# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 4.* Christ is the end of the law (Romans 10:4). Therefore, when
Christ comes, the use of the law—which has its fulfillment in him—perishes.

*Answer 1.* Christ is indeed the end and terminus of the ceremonial law, which
loses its use after Christ is manifested, as a shadow does when the body
arrives.

2. Although the moral law too, as it was then prescribed, looked to Christ or
had Christ as its goal, as Calvin says on the passage, insofar as it
demonstrated the necessity of the Mediator and compelled and impelled
miserable sinners to flee to Christ, it nevertheless did not perish
altogether after Christ's coming. For it still serves the same use. By it,
those convicted perceive clearly enough the law's impotence to justify
because of corruption (Romans 8:3), and therefore they are compelled to flee
to God's grace and to Christ set forth in the Gospel, and to trust and hope in
him. Thus Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to everyone who
believes. [Apollonius says in *On the Law of God*, pp. 216–17: *“The end of
the law is twofold. The internal end, toward which it naturally inclines and
tends of itself, is perfect righteousness and obedience, so that eternal life
may be obtained through it. Or there is the external end, which is added to
the law by the sheer determination, appointment, and intention of God in his
use of the law; and this is Christ. For whatever God reveals, prescribes,
commands, promises, or threatens in the law tends directly, by God's
appointment, to this: that from a sense of his inability to observe the law,
and from the penalty and danger of death hanging over him by the sentence of
the law, a person should be compelled to seek Christ set forth in the Gospel.
From this the apostle reproves the unbelief and stupidity of the carnal Jews,
because they did not acknowledge this use in the law, in that somewhat
obscure passage, 2 Corinthians 3:13–14: ‘Nor are we like Moses, who put a veil
over his face so that the children of Israel would not fix their eyes upon its
end, which is now removed as useless. And so their minds grew callous; for to
this very day, in the reading of the old covenant, that same veil remains
unremoved, which is taken away as having become useless in Christ,’ and so
forth. For by the veil of blindness and stupidity placed upon their hearts
(of which the veil placed upon Moses' face, concealing the glory and splendor
of his face, was a type), he indicates that they could not fix their eyes upon
the end of the law, that is, Christ, to whom the law led them by the
lawgiver's appointment.”*] Therefore, by Christ's coming,

[Printed p. 59 | IA PDF 67 | IA leaf 67 | IA decausadei02brow]

even this external end of the law is not removed, much less that internal
end, insofar as the law displays and sets forth the will of God the Lawgiver
concerning human duty.

3. We grant that the law has been abrogated insofar as eternal life is
certainly and actually to be expected or sought through obedience to it. For
that covenant of works, which promised eternal life to those who perfectly
observed this law, is now abolished. And in this respect Christ is the end of
the law for righteousness to everyone who believes, for through him alone we
obtain righteousness. He is our righteousness and was made righteousness for
us (Jeremiah 23:6; 1 Corinthians 1:30; see the final verse of 2 Corinthians 5;
Philippians 3:9). But this is not a peculiar privilege of believers under the
New Testament; it is common to everyone from Adam onward. Believers of old
obtained salvation through faith in the promised seed of the woman, just as
believers now do through faith in Christ manifested. Therefore, although this
use of the law has been removed, its primary use is no more removed now under
the Gospel than it was formerly under the Mosaic law or before that law was
given.

4. Christ is called the end of the law—that is, its *perfection, fulfillment,*
and *consummation*. Thus whoever believes in him has what the law demands, is
freed from death, and is made righteous by imputation. See Musculus on the
passage.

*Objection 5.* Believers are bound to do all things from love and willingly,
not from obligation or from having to render the obedience prescribed by the
law.

*Answer 1.* These two things do not conflict with one another. A motive and a
rule are not mutually opposed but agree and work together perfectly. The love
with which God loves us is an effectual motive for making us obedient, but it
is not therefore the rule according to which we must arrange and order our
actions. Rather, it presupposes the rule and law which it urges or moves us to
observe. The love with which a ruler regards his subjects is not the rule of
their actions but a strong reason for making them obedient to his commands
and ready and eager to carry out his orders. Yet this motive differs greatly
from the statutes of the kingdom or the laws enacted to govern the
commonwealth.

2. That love with which we regard God, or are bound to regard him, is an
internal principle in us which causes us to observe God's law devoutly. It
differs from the external law and presupposes it. The internal, impelling,
efficient, or perfecting cause of obedience ought not to be opposed to the
external rule according to which all our obedience must be ordered, for
subordinate things do not conflict.

3. This can also be confirmed by several examples. From love toward God the
angels carry out his orders, and yet they obey his commands. Adam too, in the
state of innocence, had commands set before him by God the Lawgiver which he
was obligated to obey; and yet he undoubtedly performed everything from love.
Christ himself had commands from the Father (John 10:16; 14:31; 15:10), yet
he obeyed the Father from love. Therefore, doing something from *obedience*
and from *love* are by no means in conflict.

4. Did believers under the Old Testament not obey God from love? See Psalm
116:1 and Psalm 119:97, 103, 111, 113, 127, 128, 140, 159, 162, 163, 167. Did
God not frequently command them to love him and to observe his commands from
love? See Deuteronomy 6:5; 10:12–13; 11:1, 13, 22; 30:16, 20. Therefore no
conflict appears here. They were formerly bound to obey God from love no less
than we are now; nevertheless, they had a law which

they were bound to obey. Therefore it is not inconsistent for us also to have
a law, although we are bound to serve God in all things from love.

5. That is not true love which does not impel us to render obedience to God
and his commands (John 14:15, 23).

*Reply.* It is servile to be led and moved by the law to do something, either
with a view to the reward promised in the law or because of fear of the
penalty which the law threatens against transgressors.

*Answer 1.* Even if this were true, it would nevertheless not follow that
Christians now under the New Testament ought to have no regard for the law.
For they can regard the command as a rule and be impelled by love to render
obedience.

2. Nor is it servile to look to the promised reward and to be impelled by it
to obedience. Moses looked to the bestowal of the reward and therefore
considered the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of the
Egyptians (Hebrews 11:26). Indeed, Christ himself endured the cross for the
joy set before him (Hebrews 12:2). *Love of reward* and *mercenary love*
differ greatly. [Apollonius says above on p. 231: *“The obedience of believers
contains in itself a certain relation of service toward God; hence it is that
to obey God and to serve him mean one and the same thing”* (Luke 1:74; Romans
6:16). *“And to serve God is plainly the same thing as serving obedience and
righteousness”* (Romans 6:16, 18, 22), *“because doing God's will in
subjection is serving God”* (Ephesians 6:6–7): as servants of Christ doing
from the heart what God wills, serving with goodwill as to the Lord. *“Hence
obedience with subjection to God's law indeed implies the obedience of a
servant, but not servile obedience. Just as a believer, in loving God and
performing good works, is led by affection for and attention to the reward
and prize of life, and thus indeed has love of reward but not mercenary love;
and this in no way diminishes the love and pleasure of the obedience rendered
to God, much less does regard for God's commands and the soul's obligation
under the divine law diminish its voluntary subjection joined with delight
and love in the obedience shown to God.”*]

3. Therefore, doing something from pure obedience or out of regard for a
command is not servile obedience. Rather, it shows that the soul truly fears
God and is moved by reverence and consciousness of divine authority; and this
shows grace in vigor and exercise, for this pure regard is never devoid of
true love. How often, I ask, are we commanded to fear God? And is not the fear
of God a mark of the state of grace? Are there not very many promises made to
those who fear God?
