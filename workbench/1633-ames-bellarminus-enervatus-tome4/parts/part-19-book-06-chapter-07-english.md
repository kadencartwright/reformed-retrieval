### Chapter VII

#### On the Observance of the Law

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *Heretics teach that the divine law is utterly impossible for a
man, even a righteous one. Catholics teach that God's law is absolutely
possible for righteous men through the aid of God's grace and the spirit of
faith and love infused into us in justification itself.*

Protestant reply. We do not teach that the law is utterly impossible for man:
for Adam, for example, before the fall, and afterward for Christ, nor for
those who are perfectly sanctified in Christ. But concerning others, who have
only begun sanctification, under the ordinary dispensation of grace in this
life, we say that it cannot happen that they exactly and perfectly observe
the divine law. The Pontificians, however, say that it can be done easily and
with little trouble; for these are Bellarmine's words in chapter 11.

#### Bellarmine's Arguments

2. Bellarmine. Matthew 11:29; 1 John 3:5. *My yoke is sweet and my burden is
light. His commandments are not grievous. These words indicate that God's law
is a yoke which is easily borne and that his commandments are kept with
little trouble.*

Protestant reply. Those words say or judge absolutely nothing about God's law
simply considered and its entire, perfect observance, but about the gospel,
that is, faith and new obedience. This burden does not burden but relieves,
like a wing on a bird. Nevertheless, it is not taken up and sustained so
*easily and with such little trouble* as Bellarmine imagines. But once it is
taken up and borne—whatever may be true of the flesh warring against it—it
brings the spirit sweet delight, insofar as the spirit overcomes the world
and the flesh through faith.

3. Bellarmine. *The commandments are kept by those who love,* John 14:23;
Romans 15:8; Galatians 5:14.

Protestant reply. They are kept perfectly in principle, but in act only
imperfectly. Otherwise each and every faithful man would perfectly fulfill
the law, for they all love; and thus no true believer could truly pray,
*forgive us our debts.*

Bellarmine. *We admit that love is imperfect in this life; yet we wholly deny
that it cannot nevertheless be so perfect as to suffice for fulfilling the
commandment.*

Protestant reply. According to the Pontificians, the very least thing suffices
for fulfilling God's commandments. Thus Bannes, in 2.2, question 44, article
5: *Any act of charity, however weak, suffices for fulfilling all the
commandments.* But who except a madman would affirm that imperfect observance
is the fulfillment of a perfect law?

4. Bellarmine. *Scripture teaches that some kept the commandments concerning
love with the whole heart and concerning not coveting, such as David, Josiah,
Asa, the apostles, Zechariah, Elizabeth, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. They observed the commandments in intention and effort, not
in every respect in effect; in essence, not in completion; in the quality of
sincerity, not in the quantity either of intensity or extent. The Psalms
everywhere sufficiently testify this concerning David and all the others in
him. So long ago Augustine answered the Pelagians on this matter in *Against
Pelagius and Celestius,* book 1, chapter 48: *What was said, as it seems to me,
was according to a certain conduct among men which is approved and
commendable, and which no man can justly call into an accusation and
reproachful complaint. They are accordingly reported to have possessed it in
God's sight because in it they did not deceive men by any dissimulation, but
as*

[Printed p. 371 | Google Books PA371 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*they appeared to men, so they were known to God's eyes.* The author of the
treatise *On the Fasting and Temptation of Christ,* which is among Cyprian's works,
also says: *Before Christ there had been distinguished men, prophets and
priests, but they were conceived and born in sins and lacked neither original
nor personal fault. In all of them either ignorance or insufficiency was
found; in these errors they sinned and needed the mercy of God, through which
they were taught and restored, gave thanks to God, confessed that they lacked
much for the fullness of righteousness, and, hoping in God, did not presume
to attribute any solidity to themselves.*

5. Bellarmine. *A man can do more than God commands; therefore, much more can
he fulfill a commandment.*

Protestant reply. This is as much as if Bellarmine had said: the Pontificians
can be plainly insane in one presumption; therefore, much more can they rave
in another. Yet it does not follow that if someone can do more than is
commanded with respect to the matter of a deed, he can therefore fulfill the
commandment with respect to the manner of acting and every circumstance.

6. Bellarmine. *If the commandments were impossible, they would bind no one.*

Protestant reply. I. This quibble proves no more that the law is possible for
believers than for unbelievers, for the righteous than for the unrighteous.
II. Obligation is not removed by an impossibility arising from our own fault.

7. Bellarmine. *If the law of the Lord were impossible, it would follow that
God exacts even from his friends a tribute which no one can pay, which is the
act of a cruel and foolish tyrant.*

Protestant reply. It follows that God's grace and mercy are immense, by which
he holds believers no less acceptable than if they had perfectly observed the
law, although they cannot observe it. This is the apostolic conclusion, which
stands directly opposed to Bellarmine's blasphemy.

8. Bellarmine. *If the law cannot be fulfilled, then the righteousness of the
law is never fulfilled in us, contrary to* Romans 8:4. *Nor is God's will ever
done on earth as it is in heaven, contrary to the Lord's Prayer. Nor do we
ever truly obey Christ, contrary to* Hebrews 5:9.

Protestant reply. All these labor under the disease commonly called *it does
not follow.* The righteousness, or right, of the law is fulfilled in us in
the same way that condemned sin is abolished. Cajetan, moreover, aptly
observes on Romans 8: *Sin is condemned insofar as it is judicially sentenced
to condemnation or extinction; but the extinction is not completed in this
life.* God's will is done by believers on earth as it is by the angels in
heaven—not in degree or quantity except in desire and effort (and Bellarmine
never judged himself and his peers equal to the angels in his own conscience),
but in the quality of sincerity. Not all true obedience is perfect.

[Printed p. 372 | Google Books PA372 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

9. Bellarmine. *All who are truly justified have the Holy Spirit. But the
Holy Spirit brings forth his fruits. Whoever has these fruits is not accused
by the law, since he does not transgress but fulfills it,* Galatians 5:23.
*Therefore, from first to last, he who is justified fulfills the law
entirely.*

Protestant reply. Whatever appearance the first and last parts of this
sophism possess is borrowed from a corruption of the apostle's words in
Galatians 5:23—*against such there is no law*—as if the law had nothing for
which to accuse the justified. Yet it is clear that the apostle means nothing
other than that such people are exempt and freed from the law's condemnation,
not because they entirely fulfill the law, but because, having been redeemed
in Christ, they received adoption. Galatians 4:5.

10. Bellarmine. 1 John 3:9. *Those born of God do not sin and cannot sin.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself answers this Pelagian objection in *On
the Loss of Grace,* book 1, chapter 6: 1. From Augustine, *The righteous do not
sin as sons of God, but as sons of this age they sometimes fall because of
weakness not yet wholly removed.* 2. And, more correctly, from Bernard: *The
sons of God do not sin in such a way that they remain in sin.*

#### Our Arguments

11. Protestant argument. Acts 15:10. The law of Moses is called an unbearable
yoke.

Bellarmine. 1. *Peter is speaking there only of the ceremonial law.*

Protestant reply. Let it be so; yet the argument from the lesser to the
greater holds. For the moral law is weightier than the ceremonial law.

Bellarmine. 2. *The ceremonial law should not be compared precisely with the
moral law; rather, the ceremonial law joined to the moral law should be
compared with the moral law itself after the ceremonial law is removed. For
the ceremonial law is not called an unbearable yoke because it is unbearable
by itself alone, but because, when joined to the moral law, it greatly
increases the burden and becomes an almost unbearable yoke for a man already
burdened by the moral law.*

Protestant reply. I. The first failing of this reply is that it destroys the
very thing it is brought in to support. Bellarmine had said that Peter speaks
only of the ceremonial law. To defend this he says that Peter speaks of the
ceremonial law, not precisely, but jointly or together with the moral law.
II. The second is that he does not dare deny that the moral law is weightier
than the ceremonial law, and consequently grants that the weightiest part of
the legal burden is imposed even on Christians. III. It completely overturns
Bellarmine's principal defense: *God's moral law is kept easily and with
little trouble, chapter 11.* For if the ceremonial law is not weightier than
the moral law, and the moral law is easy, then the ceremonial law joined to
the moral law cannot be a most weighty and unbearable yoke.

[Printed p. 373 | Google Books PA373 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

There is also an open contradiction between these two statements: *The moral
law is easily borne; man is burdened by the burden of the moral law.*

12. Protestant argument. Galatians 3:10. The apostle constructs an argument
of this kind: It is written, cursed is everyone who does not continue in all
the things written in the book of the law. Therefore, those who seek
justification from works of the law are cursed. But there can be no other
ground for this consequence than that no one can continue in all the things
written in the book of the law.

Bellarmine. 1. *The apostolic argument is this. Cursed is everyone who does
not keep the whole law. But no one can keep the whole law by his own strength,
without faith and grace. Therefore all those who seek to keep the whole law
by their own strength, without faith and grace, and to be justified in that
way, are cursed.*

Protestant reply. I. This is a new gloss which was unknown in the time of
Thomas Aquinas. For thus the gloss on this passage: *No one can keep the law
in the manner in which the law commands,* Deuteronomy 28. *To fulfill the
whole law is impossible,* Acts 15. *Moses commanded many things which no one
could fulfill in order to subdue the pride of the Jews, who said: The one to
fulfill is not lacking, but the one to command is lacking.* II. Bellarmine is
injurious not only to the apostle but even to the Holy Spirit himself when he
attributes to him, while correcting the Galatians, an argument which had
nothing to do with them. For it is not credible that Christians such as the
Galatians described by the apostle sought justification and salvation without
faith and grace. III. According to Bellarmine's interpretation in chapter 6,
the apostle deals in this passage with the ceremonial law, which has been
entirely abolished, not with a law to be observed with faith and grace.

Bellarmine. 2. *In the epistles to the Romans and the Galatians, Paul refutes
two errors of the Judaizers. The first was that the law of ceremonies had to
be observed. The other was that it had to be observed because such observance
was necessarily a preparation for the faith and grace of Christ, and that
therefore everyone could and should keep the law and be justified so that
they might be worthy of the gospel and Christ's promises. Against the latter
error, the apostle says that there can be no righteousness from works of the
law without faith, and so forth, speaking of the whole law, not only the
ceremonial but also the moral law.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine most futilely invents two errors where the
opinion is one, namely, that the law must be observed for justification. II.
What he imagines is still more forced and monstrous: that observance and
justification by the law were held to be a necessary preparation for the
gospel, faith, and grace. No one was ever so insane as to make justification
by the law—that is, complete innocence—a preparation for justification by the
gospel, the remission of sins. III. Least of all could this have had a place
among the Galatians, since they did not

[Printed p. 374 | Google Books PA374 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

seek preparation for the gospel, in which they had long before become
participants, but rather its completion, as the Papists do.

13. Protestant argument. Romans 7:18. To will is present with me, but I do not
find how to accomplish it.

Bellarmine. *The apostle speaks of the act of coveting without consent, which
is not sin.*

Protestant reply. It is plain from verses 22 and 23 that the apostle speaks of
the *law of God,* which he wished to fulfill but could not, because he was in
some manner captive to the opposing *law of sin.*

14. Protestant argument. James 3:2; John 1:1. No one is without sin.

Bellarmine. *Venial sins, without which we do not live, are not sins simply,
but imperfectly and in a qualified sense; nor are they against the law, but
beside the law. This is a solid reply. Those who grant that venial sin is
properly against the law, as Vega does in book 11, On the Council, chapter 20,
do not extricate themselves so easily.*

Protestant reply. Not Vega alone, but Durandus, Cajetan, Major, together with
Azorius himself in book 4, chapter 8, held this opinion; and all intelligent
people, unless they harden their minds against the truth, will readily grant
that doing what the law says, *you shall not do,* is directly against the law.
