# CHAPTER III

## The opposing arguments are examined

We have set forth our opinion on the question proposed in the preceding
chapter and confirmed it with arguments. It follows that we should briefly
examine the arguments of those who think otherwise.

*Objection 1.* Laws pertain to those to whom they are also promulgated. But the
laws delivered through Moses were not promulgated to the Gentiles, but only
to the Israelites. In Exodus 19 and 20, God the Lawgiver teaches in his own
words to whom he gave the law: namely, to that people whom he had brought out
of Egypt, with no mention made of the Gentiles.

*Answer 1.* If this argument proves anything, it will prove that only those
Israelites whom God brought out of Egypt were bound by this promulgation of
the law, and not their descendants, much less proselytes. But who will say
that the descendants of the Jews—although they were neither eyewitnesses nor
earwitnesses of the things presented to the eyes and ears of the fathers who
were present—were not bound to observe that law even insofar as it had been
promulgated by Moses? Everyone also knows that proselytes of righteousness
were bound to keep religiously that law as thus promulgated.

2. The Israelites had been brought out of Egypt insofar as they were the
Church to which the promise of redemption had been made, and not insofar as
they were such a people. The succeeding Church shares in that benefit and is,
as it were, its heir. Thus the memory of that benefit ought rightly to move
the Church in every succeeding age to gratitude and thanksgiving, and the
Church ought to acknowledge this benefit with no less grateful a spirit than
if it had been bestowed specially upon itself. See Hosea 12:4: *“and there he
spoke with us.”* The

[Printed p. 26 | IA PDF 34 | IA leaf 34 | IA decausadei02brow]

Church in the time of Hosea concluded, not without reason, that God had
addressed the Church itself when he addressed Jacob at Bethel.

3. We do not say that the Gentiles were actually and immediately bound to
observe the law delivered through Moses, but that they became bound when this
promulgation was in some manner revealed to them. For when the report of this
sanction reached their ears, they ought to have joined themselves to God's
Church and subjected themselves wholly to this law.

4. There is also a promulgation which takes place in writing, and this law was
written. See Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, I–II, question 90, article 4, answer
to objection 3, and question 91, article 1, answer to objection 2.

*Objection 2.* God exhorts the Israelites to obedience by a special benefit
bestowed upon them.

*Answer 1.* I said above that in some manner this benefit was common to the
whole Church.

2. There were also certain arguments common to everyone, such as this one:
*“I am Jehovah your God.”*

3. A general and perpetual duty may be pressed by a special argument, as can
be confirmed by many instances. This argument therefore proves nothing.

*Objection 3.* Moses was not sent to the Gentiles, but only to the Israelites.

*Answer.* He was sent to the Israelites insofar as God regarded them as his
Church, and consequently in this promulgation of the law he was sent to all
who would afterward join themselves to the Church; or he was sent to the
descendants of those Israelites.

*Objection 4.* Moses himself openly denies that the law was given to the
Gentiles, but only to the Israelites, and accordingly commands the Israelites
to acknowledge this singular benefit of God. Deuteronomy 4: *“What great
nation is there?”* etc. Thus, addressing the Israelites alone, he said, *“And
now, Israel, hear the statutes and judgments.”*

*Answer.* We concede that this law was given first and immediately to the
Israelites and that this was a singular benefit of God which they ought to
have acknowledged. Nevertheless, others who associated themselves with this
Church could be bound by this same law no less than the descendants of those
Israelites.

*Objection 5.* The Israelites alone were very often chastised with manifest
scourges for transgressing this law. The Gentiles never were, but only for
transgressing the natural law.

*Answer.* Proselytes joined to them were chastised together with them for
transgressing it. We maintain nothing more than that this promulgation bound
people other than the Israelites when they joined themselves to God's Church,
and consequently that it now binds the Christian Church. Moreover, the
Decalogue is a new edition of the natural law.

*Objection 6.* The promises added to the law pertained only to the Israelites,
not to the Gentiles; therefore the law itself did also.

*Answer 1.* The consequence does not hold, for a particular promise can be
annexed to a common law.

2. The promises added to the law also pertain in some manner to others.
Otherwise the apostle would not have said in Ephesians 6:1–3, *“Children, obey
your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and mother,
which is the first commandment with a promise, that it may be well with you
and you may be long-lived upon the earth.”* Zanchius comments on these words
as follows: [*“He had said that parents must be obeyed in the Lord,*

[Printed p. 27 | IA PDF 35 | IA leaf 35 | IA decausadei02brow]

*that is, on account of the Lord, who so established it—not only in nature,
but also (note well) by an express law. Now, therefore, he adds this command
which is contained in the Decalogue.”*] And on verse 3: [*“There are two
parts,”* he says. *“The first is general: ‘that it may be well with you.’ This
is taken from Deuteronomy 5. The second is special: ‘and you may be long-lived
upon the earth.’ This is taken from the commandment of the Decalogue itself.
But that which seemed to pertain only to the Israelites he extends to all
Gentiles. For there Moses said, ‘That you may be long-lived upon the land
which the Lord your God will give you in the land of Canaan.’ Here, however,
the apostle says simply, ‘that you may be long-lived upon the earth.’ From
this we learn incidentally how the Scriptures of the Old Testament are to be
handled: their substantial elements pertain to all believers; their
incidental elements pertain only to the Israelites.”*]

*Objection 7.* In Romans 1 and 2, the apostle convicts the Gentiles of sin, not
through the law of Moses, but through the law of nature; and he says that they
sinned without law and did not possess the law.

*Answer.* We do not say that the Gentiles were bound at once and immediately
to acknowledge the promulgation of the law through Moses, but that they were
bound as soon as it became known to them.

*Objection 8.* According to Romans 9, this was the dignity of the Jews: the
giving of the law belonged to them.

*Answer.* So it was, for the Lord Jehovah granted this dignity to no other
nation. Nevertheless, once notice was given, the obligation of this enactment
could be extended to others. It was a singular and peculiar privilege of the
Jews that they first heard the announcement of the Gospel, yet that
proclamation of reconciliation bound the Gentiles as soon as it became known
to them.

*Objection 9.* Then the Gentiles too would have been compelled by God's
command to sanctify the Sabbath; but this is not evident.

*Answer.* We saw above in Book III, chapter VII, what must be said about
Gentiles who observed the Sabbath. This much is certain: Nehemiah did not want
foreigners to profane the Sabbath.

2. This argument, if it proves anything, proves that the Gentiles were not
bound to observe the law of the Decalogue even by force of its subject matter,
and not merely by reason of the Mosaic promulgation. It therefore proves too
much, and consequently the opponent himself is bound to answer it.

*Objection 10.* The law of the Decalogue, insofar as it was the law of the Old
Testament and of Moses, has been abrogated. For Jews converted to Christ no
longer have anything to do with Moses, but only with Christ.

*Answer.* We indeed now have nothing to do with Moses insofar as he was a type
of our now-revealed mediator. Yet we still have something to do with the moral
law, which was not so much promulgated by Moses as pronounced by God himself
while Moses heard it. It has not yet been proved that the law of the
Decalogue, insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament, has been abrogated
any more than it has been proved that all the moral instructions and precepts
of the prophets have now been abrogated insofar as they were instructions and
precepts of the Old Testament. Zanchius himself proves that in the Sermon of
Matthew 5, etc., Christ delivered neither a new law nor counsels, but both
purified the old law from the false glosses of the Pharisees and explained it
according to its genuine sense. His arguments, which can fully answer this
objection, are these: [*“1. Certainly, if he delivered a new law more perfect
than the old, he therefore*

[Printed p. 28 | IA PDF 36 | IA leaf 36 | IA decausadei02brow]

*abrogated the old so that it has no relevance at all to us. But he himself
plainly says the contrary: that he did not come to dissolve the law or the
prophets. And in chapter 7, verse 12, as though briefly comprehending the sum
of the second table, he says, ‘Therefore, whatever you wish people to do to
you, do the same to them. For this is the law and the prophets’; that is,
this is the sum of the law and the prophets concerning duties toward one's
neighbor.”*] From this it appears clearly enough that the law of the Decalogue
has not been abrogated insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament. Let us
now hear his second argument: [*“2. And who does not see,”* he says, *“that in
that Sermon Christ never corrected anything in the words of the law, but only
the glosses added by the rabbis? Therefore he removes these, but does not
correct or simply abrogate the law.”*] Therefore the law of the Decalogue,
insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament, has not been abrogated. He
continues: [*“3. And what is more perfect than the law of the Decalogue, by
which we are commanded to love God with all the heart, all the soul, and all
our strength, and our neighbor as ourselves; and to covet nothing or have any
covetous desire? Therefore there was no need of a more perfect law. Nor did
Christ set before the man who asked what he must do to possess eternal life
any better law than that delivered through Moses, saying, ‘You shall love the
Lord your God,’ etc., Matthew 19.”*] Hence we may also conclude that the law
of the Decalogue, insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament, has not been
abrogated. His fourth argument follows: [*“4. Finally,”* he says, *“the
apostles always inculcate the commandments of the Decalogue, not a new law of
Christ.”*] They undoubtedly would have inculcated a new law if that law of the
Decalogue had been abrogated insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament.

*Objection 11.* The apostle to the Hebrews sets Christ over against Moses. The
law was transferred from Moses to Christ, from the hand of Moses into the
hand of Christ, the true mediator, just as the priesthood was transferred
from Aaron to Christ, the true and eternal High Priest. But, the apostle says,
*“when the priesthood is transferred, it is necessary that a transfer of the
law also take place.”* Therefore the law, as it was in the hand of Moses, has
been abrogated.

*Answer.* The apostle to the Hebrews indeed sets Christ over against Moses as
a lord over against a servant. But it does not follow from this that Christ
abolished the law of the Decalogue, since in its promulgation the Lord did not
use the ministry of Moses in the same way as he did in giving the ceremonial
and judicial law. It is true that Moses delivered the tablets of the covenant
to the people; yet God himself promulgated the law itself with a living voice,
while angels formed the voice, thunder, and so forth in the air.

2. I should like it explained to me how the moral law can be said to be
transferred from Moses to Christ, from the hand of Moses into the hand of
Christ. Scripture does not speak this way, nor did Christ intimate any such
thing when he interpreted that law.

3. When the Aaronic priesthood is transferred, it is indeed necessary that a
transfer of the law also take place—but of the ceremonial law, to which that
priesthood belonged, not of the moral law. And this change is a total
abrogation; but the opponent will not say that the moral law has been
abrogated.

*Objection 12.* The law in the hand of Moses had no power to impart, but only
gave commands. In the hand of Christ, however, it received a power which it
may impart. *“The law brought nothing to perfection,”* says the apostle, *“and
because of its weakness”*—which, namely, it had in the hand of Moses—*“it was
rejected”* and delivered into the hand of Christ. From him it becomes
effective in the elect and faithful when Christ inscribes it upon their
hearts; for in this way it becomes a new law.

*Answer.* This reasoning intimates that Christ did not inscribe his law in the
hearts of the fathers and believers in the time of the Old Testament, and
therefore that the law, although among the elect, was not effective. But

[Printed p. 29 | IA PDF 37 | IA leaf 37 | IA decausadei02brow]

if this is denied—and what orthodox person would affirm it?—the entire force
of the argument utterly vanishes. For if this law was also inscribed by Christ
upon their hearts, then the law was also delivered into Christ's hand at that
time, and consequently it must be said that believers under the Old Testament
were freed from this law of the Decalogue insofar as it was the law of Moses
and of the Old Testament.

2. In this passage, Hebrews 7:19, the apostle does not speak of the moral law
insofar as it is a rule, but under the aspect of a covenant; or rather, he
speaks of the ceremonial law. For he speaks of that law which was opposed to
Christ. But the moral law, considered as a rule of life, was not opposed to
Christ.

adopted in the Latin and reflected by “rule of life” above.]

3. The moral law does not now bind insofar as it is inscribed upon the hearts
of believers, but insofar as it was enacted by God. We know that Moses could
not inscribe this law upon anyone's heart, and that this power belongs to
Christ alone, who effects it by his Spirit. But that is not the question under
debate, for we are asking about the obligation of the law.

*Objection 13.* And this is what Christ had promised through Jeremiah chapter
31 that he would do: abrogate the old covenant and make a new covenant. How?
By writing upon the hearts of the elect the law which had been written only
upon tablets and delivered to the people through Moses. For there he does not
oppose law to law, but writing to writing. Writing upon tablets belongs to
Moses; writing upon hearts belongs to Christ. Yet it is the same law, which is
different in the hand of Christ from what it was in the hand of Moses, since
Christ administers it differently from Moses of old.

*Answer.* This objection confuses the Testament with the law of the Decalogue.
Jeremiah speaks about the covenant, not the Decalogue; he speaks about the old
dispensation of the covenant and promises a new one. But he says nothing about
this transfer of the law of the Decalogue.

2. Christ abrogated that old Testament; then, it would seem, Christ abrogated
the law of the Decalogue. But the opponent himself denies this.

3. He indeed promises that inscription of the law upon the heart, not
absolutely, but comparatively. Otherwise it would have to be said that no
believer under the Old Testament shared in this inscription. But that cannot
be said. Yet it does not follow from this that believers were freed in any
respect from the obligation of the moral law insofar as it was the law of the
Old Testament and of Moses.

4. If Jeremiah does not oppose law to law but writing to writing, he does not
oppose obligation to obligation. But we are speaking about obligation.

5. This diverse administration of the law also had a place under the Old
Testament, yet did not change the obligation. How, then, I ask, will it change
it now?

*Objection 14.* In 2 Corinthians 3:7–11 it is openly taught that the law which
was given so gloriously by Moses is now removed as useless.

*Answer.* Since we will say more about this passage later, when the objections
of the Antinomians are to be considered, it will suffice here to set forth
these few points. The apostle indeed compares the Gospel with the law, or the
evangelical administration with the legal. The latter, namely the legal
administration, he calls the ministry of the letter, of death, and of
condemnation, insofar as it was considered in itself and absolutely, abstracted
and separated from the operation of the Spirit. The former he calls the
ministry of the Spirit, of life, and of righteousness insofar as it had the
Spirit joined to it. For, as Beza rightly observes and experience confirms,
even the ministry of the Gospel, if deprived of the work of the Spirit

[Printed p. 30 | IA PDF 38 | IA leaf 38 | IA decausadei02brow]

or separated from the Spirit of God, will be a ministry of the letter, not of
the Spirit; of death, not of life; of condemnation, not of righteousness. We
concede that this obscure administration has been abolished. Yet it cannot be
denied that for believers under the Old Testament who penetrated by faith and
fixed their eyes upon the end of its manifestation, that legal ministry was a
ministry of the Spirit and of life, since the Holy Spirit worked together with
it. Thus the comparison is not absolute but comparative; see verses 17 and 18.
Therefore, just as those believers were not freed from the obligation of the
Mosaic law, neither does it follow that believers are now freed from the
obligation of the law of the Decalogue which was then promulgated. We concede
that that pedagogical administration has been abolished. Yet it does not
follow that everything which had any place in that pedagogy has been
abolished, for then the moral law will have been utterly abolished, as the
ceremonial law was, and not only insofar as it had been promulgated by Moses.
But the orthodox men with whom we contend here deny this.

Moreover, the moral law as then enacted or promulgated can be considered
either more absolutely, insofar as it contains the perfect rule of
righteousness, or more restrictively, insofar as it was pressed and set forth
in that administration. In the latter respect it more effectively expounded
the covenant of works, in which life was promised to those who perfectly
observed it and the penalty of an eternal curse was inflicted upon those who
transgressed it in the least. In this respect it is called a ministry of the
letter, not of the Spirit, and a ministry which kills and does not give life,
because it imparts no power of obedience and shows no way of remission. On the
contrary, it threatens a curse against all who do not continue in all things
written, Galatians 4:10, for this end: that they may be stirred and more
effectively moved to receive Christ. Although the law of the Decalogue,
considered in this latter way, can be said to have been abolished with respect
to degree, it has not been abolished simply. For even now this law of the
Decalogue has this use for the unregenerate: awakening their consciences so
that they flee from the wrath to come, 1 Timothy 1:9–10, and driving them to
Christ, Galatians 3:24. [*“But you will say,”* Beza states on verse 11, *“Has
the ministry of Moses been abolished in this respect? By no means. For people
must always be prepared for the Gospel by the preaching of the law. But I say
that the ministry of Moses is abolished whenever the manifest grace of the
Gospel succeeds the figurative preaching of the law.”*]

Although, I say, the law of the Decalogue can be said to have been partly
abolished in this way, it does not follow that it was in any way abolished in
the former respect. For the British Confession of Faith, chapter 19, section
5, has it: [*“The moral law perpetually binds everyone, the justified as well
as all others, to render obedience to it. It does so not only by force of the
subject matter contained in it, but also by the authority of God the Creator
who established it,”* Romans 13:8–10; Ephesians 6:2; 1 John 2:3, 4, 7, 8;
James 2:10–11.] And section 6: [*“Although true believers are not under the law
as under a covenant of works, so as to be justified or condemned by it”*
(Romans 6:14; Galatians 2:16 and 3:13 and 4:4–5; Acts 13:39; Romans 8:1),
*“it is nevertheless exceedingly useful to them no less than to others. As a
rule of life, by teaching them the divine will and their duty, it both directs
and binds them to walk accordingly”* (Romans 7:12, 22, 25; Psalm 119:4–6; 2
Corinthians 7:19; Galatians 5:14, 16, 18–23). *“It also reveals to them the
wicked defilements of their nature, heart, and life”* (Romans 7:7 and 3:20),
*“so that, examining themselves by it, they may be further convicted of sin,
humiliated for it, and inflamed with hatred of it,*

[Printed p. 31 | IA PDF 39 | IA leaf 39 | IA decausadei02brow]

*and may also perceive more clearly how wholly necessary Christ is to them and
how perfect his obedience is”* (James 1:23–25; Romans 7:9, 14, 24; Galatians
3:24; Romans 7:24–25 and 8:3–4). *“Furthermore, it is also useful to the
regenerate insofar as it restrains their corruptions by prohibiting sins”*
(James 2:11; Psalm 119:101, 104, 128). *“By solemnly threatening them, it
indicates both what even their sins have deserved and what afflictions they may
expect for them in this life, although they are freed from the curse which the
law threatens”* (Ezra 9:13–14; Psalm 89:30–34). *“Moreover, its promises show
how acceptable and approved by God their obedience is, and what blessings they
may expect upon rendering it”* (Leviticus 26:1–14 with 2 Corinthians 6:16;
Ephesians 6:2–3; Psalm 37:11 with Matthew 5:5; Psalm 19:11), *“although these
are not owed as a matter of law under the covenant of works”* (Galatians 2:16;
Luke 17:10). *“Add that if anyone performs good when invited by the law and
recoils from evil when deterred by the law, this is no argument whatever that
he is under the law and not established under grace”* (Romans 6:12, 14; 1
Peter 3:8–12 with Psalm 34:12–16; Hebrews 12:28, 39).] And section 7: [*“Nor do
the uses of the law just mentioned oppose the grace of the Gospel; rather, they
harmonize sweetly with it”* (Galatians 3:21), *“as the Spirit of Christ so
subdues and imbues the human will that it can perform spontaneously and
cheerfully the very thing which the will of God revealed in his law requires
of it”* (Ezekiel 36:27; Hebrews 8:10 with Jeremiah 31:33).]

*Objection 15.* The whole Mosaic law, including the Decalogue, was a polity
instituted to govern the political body of the Jews. Therefore, when that
polity ceases, that law also ceases.

*Answer.* A law can indeed cease when a polity ceases insofar as it was an
instrument appointed to govern that people politically, and yet not cease
insofar as it was the perfect rule of righteousness. We also know that the
judicial laws were given specially to that people as a political body and
that, as the British Confession of Faith, chapter 19, section 4, has it, they
expired together with that polity and now bind no others beyond what their
general and common equity requires. Everyone knows that the case of these
laws is one thing and that of the moral laws another.

*Objection 16.* The preface applied only to the Jews.

*Answer.* Let it be so. Yet it does not follow that the law therefore
pertained to them alone, for a common law can be commended by certain special
arguments.
