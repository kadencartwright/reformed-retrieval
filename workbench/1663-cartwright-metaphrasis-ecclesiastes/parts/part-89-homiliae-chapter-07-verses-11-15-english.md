The summary is a commendation of patience and moderation of mind, established
by a comparison with riches.

There are two parts: 1. Riches and moderation of mind agree. 2. They differ,
and moderation of mind is preferred to riches.

VERSE 11. Wisdom is good with a possession, and it is an advantage to those
who behold the Sun.

Solomon returns to the interrupted treatment of riches and joins them with
wisdom—that is, moderation of mind or patience, which he rightly distinguishes
by the name of wisdom. Proverbs 3 likewise describes patience by the title of
wisdom. For after he had exhorted his hearers in verses 10, 11, and 12 to
patience in bearing God's chastisements, in verse 13 he adds, to strengthen
this exhortation, that the person who has attained wisdom is blessed. And when
James 1 urges patience, it adds in verse 5 that whoever lacks this wisdom must
ask it from God.

Although this patience alone is not comprehended in the word “wisdom,” it is
beyond all dispute that patience is a noble member of the wisdom commended
here. In this we see that the Holy Spirit's judgment is far different from
what is commonly thought. For the Holy Spirit establishes it as proof of the
highest wisdom if someone is found to be patient and slow to anger, whereas
the world condemns this as the height of ignorance. Let us therefore use this
praise of Solomon for moderation of mind to unravel the corrupt and depraved
judgment of our flesh.

Earlier, at the beginning of the chapter, Solomon designated riches by the
name of ointment, a most precious thing; here, however, he denotes them by the
name of an inheritance, the most stable possession among all the perishable
possessions of this life. First he proposes a comparison of like things: both
wisdom and an inheritance are good, the one just as the other. This is
confirmed from the accompanying benefit they bring to mortals. The
beneficiaries are described by their activity, namely beholding the Sun—or,
rather, experiencing it; not even the blind themselves are excluded from the
scope and compass of that experience.

That riches are necessary for living this life conveniently needs no proof,
since without them neither food nor clothing is supplied for the support of
life. This is readily proved even by the industry of all people and the
painful labor they devote to acquiring riches. They regard riches just as
they would the safest fortress and refuge against every evil. Riches also
often contribute to the future life if someone uses them lawfully: through
them we lend to God, Proverbs 19; prepare friends who may receive us into the
eternal dwellings, Luke 16; and gather a treasure that no one may take from
us, Luke 18:22.

Although that very point seems capable of easy refutation, wisdom, or
moderation of mind—which Solomon likewise judges useful to mortals for
protecting this life—nevertheless lies neglected by almost everyone, as if it
were useless for preserving this life. Yet what the Holy Spirit through

pronounced always remains firm and unshaken. There will soon be an occasion to
speak about its truth and reliability in this matter. Meanwhile, we must take
care that we do not separate and tear apart from one another the things God
has joined and associated. Matthew 19.

This is chiefly what the Prophet means: the union of these two goods in one
person is of extraordinary advantage, especially when they come together in
the same person. For wisdom in a poor person is often despised, Ecclesiastes
9. When adorned by the splendor of riches, however, it is valued more highly
and possesses greater force. See Proverbs 14:24. On the other hand, riches
without wisdom often drive people to madness, so that a rich person without
wisdom is, as the proverb says, an ass carrying mysteries or a sword in a
madman's hand. This should teach us why wise people ought to seek riches.

VERSE 12. For some are in the shadow of wisdom, others in the shadow of
money; but the greater advantage of knowledge is that wisdom can preserve its
possessor alive.

Solomon illustrates this benefit by a comparison of equals, because both
money and wisdom are like a shadow beneath which one is permitted to hide
from most of this life's difficulties as from the Sun's heat. In these words
he appears to continue in the metaphor of the Sun that he used in the
preceding verse. For hunger often forces us to seek money; thirst does so at
times, cold sometimes, and diseases or the injuries of the wicked not
infrequently.

We also recline in wisdom's shadow as beneath a tree widespread with
outstretched branches. By its benefit we avoid many evils and repel others
that leap and rush upon us, evils by which foolish and incautious people are
ensnared. Thus far Solomon has taught us about the common and very similar
advantage both of riches and of wisdom. Now he proceeds to the second part,
in which he declares the superiority of the benefit wisdom provides over
riches when the two are separated in their possessors. He proves it by an
argument from the greater: wisdom provides more shade against heat, and more
sunny shelter against threatening storms and rain, than money does.

First, wisdom—that is, moderation and patience of mind—is a more certain
remedy against the very evils for which money supplies a remedy. Even when
money is present, the means by which evils are relieved cannot always be
purchased with it. And even if we obtain the things we desire, the outcome
remains dependent upon God's blessing, whereas patience and tranquillity of
mind are a present and certain remedy.

Second, most evils are of such a kind that money contains no medicine for
them. Among these are diseases incurable in their stubbornness and
oppressions by powerful people and tyrants, against which wisdom nevertheless
always has a remedy ready and at hand. Finally, the remedy sought from money
or an inheritance, because it is external and adventitious, is not always at
hand when it is needed. Wisdom, however—that is, moderation of mind—never fails
to render us its service, because we carry it about with us, planted and
inhering within us.

But let us hear Solomon himself deliver his judgment on this matter. By an
argument from effects he concludes that wisdom possesses this superiority
because it preserves the lives of those imbued with it. What is written in
Proverbs 10:2, that righteousness rescues from death, is not dissimilar. An
excellent example of this is Abigail, 1 Samuel 25, who by her modesty and the
gentle speech she opposed to David's raging anger preserved both her own life
and the lives of all her people. See also 2 Samuel 20:16–17, and so forth.
Through patience we possess both this life and the life that will last
forever.

Here someone may object that it often happens that

wealth also preserves life, something Solomon denies by a tacit antithesis.
Let this answer be given to him: a person abounding in wealth does not even
dare offer an incorrupt judge the price of redeeming his life, and upright
people in most cases are restrained by duty from giving money. Some people
also burn with such hatred for their enemies that they hold gold and silver
in contempt in comparison with their enemies' lives; the Prophet foretold
that the Chaldeans would be of this kind. Others, although they promise life
in return for receiving money, do not keep their word and promises.

There are indeed occasions when someone preserves his life by paying a price,
as happened to the ten men mentioned in Jeremiah 41:8–9. More often, however,
the rich lose their life through wealth as through a treacherous snare. Most
certain of all is that no one can be rescued from eternal death by his
riches.

Here it is worthwhile to consider the stupor of people who devote all their
mental powers to becoming rich and wealthy, yet do not stretch out even a
finger or move a foot to attain this wisdom. Racing for money as fast as they
can, they move at the swiftest pace like a dromedary; in pursuing this wisdom
they are slower than a tortoise itself. Yet it is right to labor for this
wisdom infinitely more than for wealth. What is more serious, they possess a
mind averse to this exceedingly precious wisdom and count it disgraceful to
be regarded as people of moderate and tranquil mind.

This may also console the poor, for whom access to wisdom lies as open as it
does for the rich. If God has denied them riches, they should devote that much
greater effort to acquiring wisdom.

VERSE 13. Consider the work of God himself; for who can straighten what he has
made crooked?

Solomon exhorts us to this patience and moderation of mind from the
accompanying fact of God's ineluctable power, which no one can resist. See
Isaiah 14:27 and Job 9:12–13. For it is foolish to oppose anyone without
accomplishing anything, much less God. Such opposition not only accomplishes
nothing but also brings serious harm upon oneself, Job 9:4. Who receives any
benefit from kicking against a goad and does not instead contract harm from
it? A bird caught in a snare is bound more tightly the more fiercely it
struggles to free itself.

Therefore, if someone is held entangled in God's snare, nothing is safer for
him than to commit himself wholly to God's will, especially since, together
with the supreme power with which God is equipped, he does everything justly
and everything wisely, Job 34:12. Truly, the mighty God does not condemn the
upright, and the Almighty does not pervert judgment; and in verse 10:
“Wickedness be far from the mighty God, and iniquity from the Almighty.” If it
is also added that God sends afflictions upon the pious not only justly and
wisely but mercifully, it will be easy to sustain an afflicted mind in
patience. Conversely, without a sense of divine love, it is “one of the
impossible things” to bear afflictions calmly and with a tranquil mind.

Solomon confirms this ineluctable power of God by human testimony, namely
experience that is most certain, visible to the eyes of everyone, and beyond
every objection. The same is confirmed in Job 9:4, where the Holy Spirit asks
who was ever defiant against God and escaped it unpunished. Therefore, in
order to defend and retain patience, it will be worthwhile to look upon the
examples of those who wished to shake off the yoke God placed upon them; and,
by contrast, to observe how conveniently and calmly things go with those who
commit themselves wholly to God's will in bearing evils.

VERSE 14. In a time of good, dwell in good; but in a time of evil, make use of
it. God himself also makes this over against that, for this

reason: that a person cannot discover anything of the things that lie before
him.

To arm a person better for the endurance of evils, Solomon proposes the
effective instrumental cause of this patience: namely, that if someone enjoys
prosperity and circumstances flow according to his heart's desire, he should
delight in them moderately. Refreshed by their sweetness, he will more easily
bear the bitterness and gall of affliction when it is sent. Thus in Job 1,
Job rebuked his wife's impatience because, after receiving good things from
God, she refused to account evils equally as good.

This reasoning is further strengthened from the efficient cause, God, who
weighs prosperity and adversity together as if in a balance. He does so that
people may neither become fierce and proud through excessive prosperity nor
become dejected and downcast in spirit through oppression and affliction.
Hence comes the Church of God's prayer that, in proportion to the evils she
has suffered, God would shine upon her in the enjoyment of good things, Psalm
90:15. See 1 Corinthians 10:13.

Accordingly, those who prefer to be continually amid pleasant things are to
be regarded as entirely absurd. Conversely, those who conclude that they can
never emerge or lift their head from the evils in which they find themselves
are too fainthearted. Just as night and day, summer and winter, alternate, so
adverse circumstances follow joyful ones, and joyful circumstances follow
sad ones. See Psalm 74.

The person who considers these things rightly will think in prosperous times
about an affliction that must be borne, and will prepare himself to undergo
it. Thus, while he is in the house of feasting, his mind is in the house of
mourning; when he is amid evils, however, he will console himself with hope
of a better condition. Votaries who take a cross upon themselves and bloody
themselves with scourges transgress this rule when God breathes prosperity
upon them.

Solomon notes as an adjunct of this many-varied divine governance that it
does not always fall within the grasp of human reason. In this matter there
is a most abundant harvest of patience and moderation of mind, when someone
willingly submits himself to the will of God, whose reason he cannot follow.
“I was silent, because you, Lord, did it.” Who will readily charge a king with
folly or injustice? Psalm 39:10. Job sinned in this matter, Job 34:17–18,
because, not knowing the cause of so rare and unheard-of an affliction, he
accused God of severity in sending it and repeatedly shook off the yoke of
patience.

VERSE 15. I saw all these things in the days of my vanity: there is a
righteous person who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked
person who prolongs his days in his wickedness.

Solomon proves by the testimony of his own experience the things said above.
Here he also introduces an example of an astonishing administration whose
reason no one can readily follow: namely, that a righteous person should
perish in his righteousness and a wicked person should prolong his life by
his wickedness.

The reason for this administration can be assigned from the authority of the
divine Word: from it we understand that another judgment awaits people in the
age to come, 2 Thessalonians 1, and that the pious are conformed to the image
of Christ. But if someone wishes to measure these things by the standard of
human reason, he will immediately be ready to brand God, the most righteous
judge, with the mark of injustice—especially since the righteous person not
only perishes but perishes in his just cause, while the wicked person not
only prolongs his days but sometimes attains this through his injustice.

It is asked whether ministers of God, following Solomon's example, may
reinforce the things they teach by their own experience. First, a minister
must avoid self-praise. Second, he may propose a teaching from the sense of
his own experience, provided that he confirms it by the authority of
Scripture. But no minister of our times is permitted to present his own
experience by itself as Solomon did, for Solomon wrote by divine inspiration.
