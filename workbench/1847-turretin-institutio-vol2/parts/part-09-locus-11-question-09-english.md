## QUESTION IX

### CONCERNING THE SECOND COMMANDMENT—ON THE WORSHIP OF IMAGES

Is it lawful to honor with religious worship images of God and the Most Holy
Trinity, of Christ, of the Virgin Mother of God, and of the other saints?

It is denied, against the Roman Catholics.

I. There are two principal questions concerning sacred images. First, the
question of their *worship*: are they to be worshiped and venerated? Second,
the question of their *use*: should they at least be made and placed in
churches and other sacred places? We will examine the latter next. The former
must now be treated.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

II. The question is not whether there may be images that ought to be of some
value among us, whether we consider the artist, the material, the antiquity,
or even the prototype, if it is a person of some distinction or someone dear
to us. Rather, it is whether some religious worship, whether called adoration
or veneration, ought to be rendered to images of God and the saints made by
human hands. For even the adversaries do not agree about this. Some ascribe
*adoration* to them without qualification, while others, to avoid odium,
insist only upon *veneration*. But this is an empty distinction, because,
since religious worship is one and admits no different species, it makes no
difference by what name it is designated.

III. Second, the question here is not about the *manner* of worship to be
shown to images, or *how* images ought to be adored. We need not investigate
the varying and mutually opposed opinions of the Roman Catholics, who, like
the architects of ancient Babel, are divided in language and fight fiercely
among themselves. Are images to be adored properly and in themselves, so that
adoration terminates in them, as some would have it? Or only analogically,
improperly, and by reduction to the exemplar and prototype, as others hold?
Are they to receive the same worship as the prototype, as not a few maintain,
or a different and inferior worship, as still others say? The question is
only about the *reality of the worship*: is any religious worship owed to
them? The Roman Catholics steadfastly affirm this, but we deny it.

IV. The Roman Catholic position can be gathered from no source better than
the decree of the Council of Trent. Although here, as elsewhere, it speaks
hesitantly and ambiguously so as to satisfy each dissenting party as far as
possible, it nevertheless discloses its mind clearly enough when it appeals
to the Second Council of Nicaea and approves whatever was established there
concerning the worship of images. These are its words in Session 25, in the
decree on the Veneration of Relics, the Invocation of Saints, and Images,
where bishops are commanded “to teach the invocation of saints, the honor of
relics, and the use of images, and to declare that those who teach otherwise
hold impious opinions; this has been sanctioned by the decrees of the

[Printed p. 48 | Internet Archive PDF 76 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

councils, especially of the Second Nicene Synod, against the opponents of
images.” Yet no one is unaware that this council, held in 787 and counted by
them among the ecumenical councils, expressly sanctioned the adoration of
images. Hence, in Action 7, it strikes with an anathema those who even briefly
doubt whether images are to be adored: “We judge that the images of the
glorious angels and saints are to be adored. But if anyone is not so disposed
and instead struggles and doubts concerning the adoration of venerable
images, the holy and venerable synod anathematizes him.” When, however, the
word *adoration* seemed somewhat harsh to certain persons and, so as to speak
more gently, they said that “images are to be venerated,” the synod inveighed
against them in Action 4 in this way: “All who confess that they venerate
sacred images but refuse adoration are rebuked as hypocrites by the holy
father Anastasius.” It also says that they “speak insults against the saints.”
Since, therefore, the Council of Trent approves whatever was sanctioned in
that synod, it is evident that Trent held no different position.

V. This is confirmed, second, by the daily *practice* received among them, in
which the people are known to prostrate themselves before images, kiss them,
burn incense to them, erect altars, offer prayers, make vows, institute holy
feast days, and render similar acts of adoration and worship. And lest anyone
suppose that the people sin in this practice while the principal doctors hold
something else, it is not difficult to show that their thought corresponds
to this practice. Hence Thomas, part 3, question 25, article 1, wills the
worship of latria to be shown to Christ's cross no less than to Christ
himself: “Since Christ is adored with the adoration of latria, it follows that
his image is to be adored with the adoration of latria.” *Cajetan* follows him
in his notes, as does Gabriel Biel in lecture 49 on the Canon of the Mass: “If
they are images of Christ, let them be adored with the same species of worship
as Christ, that is, with the adoration of latria.” The majority of the doctors
embrace this opinion, including *Alexander, Bonaventure, Richard, Paludanus,
Capreolus, Castro, Canisius,* and *Turrianus*, along with very many others,
whom *Vásquez* numbers up to thirty in book 2 of *On Adoration*, Disputation
8, chapter 3. Vásquez himself contends with the greatest exertion that any
inanimate things may be adored with the worship of latria—even a straw and a
ray of the sun beneath which the devil lies concealed—in book 3, Disputation
1, chapters 2 and 3.

Bellarmine, in *On Images*, chapter 21, wills images to be worshiped not only
on account of the prototype, or the thing signified, but also on account of
themselves, so that veneration terminates in the image. Pertinent here is the
fact that the cross itself is invoked by a public and solemn rite in the hymn
recited on the Friday before Easter during its adoration. For when the priest,
uncovering the cross, says, *Behold the wood of the cross*, and the choir
responds, *Come, let us adore*, a prayer is directed to the cross itself: *Hail,
O Cross, our only hope; in this Passiontide, increase righteousness in the
godly and grant pardon to the guilty.* And lest anyone suppose this is said
metonymically of the Crucified One, the cross is immediately and expressly
distinguished from him: *you alone were worthy to bear the price of the age*,
and so forth. Thus the *Roman Pontifical*, folio 205, on the entrance of a
legate, says: “The cross of the apostolic legate will be on the right, because
latria is owed to it, and the emperor's sword on the left,” and so forth.

VI. I acknowledge that this opinion does not please some Roman Catholics. They
contend that no worship ought to be rendered to images of God, but that images
should be kept only for history and the memory of past events, so that the
image itself is not adored, but only the exemplar before the image. This was
the opinion of *Gregory the Great*, who praises *Serenus*, bishop of
Marseilles, because he condemned the worship of images, but censures him for
breaking the images. Gregory says in book 3, letter 9: “We praised you
entirely for forbidding their adoration, but we reproved you for breaking
them. It is one thing to adore a picture and another to learn through the
history in a picture what ought to be adored.” He is joined by *Durandus*,
bishop of Mende, in the *Rationale*, book 4, part 2, on the fourth part of the
Canon; *Gerson*, chancellor of Paris, volume 2, *Declaration of the Defects of
the Church*, number 67; *Holcot*;

[Printed p. 49 | Internet Archive PDF 77 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*Tostatus*; and others on Deuteronomy 4. Nor is there any doubt that the more
sensible and clear-sighted Roman Catholics incline in this direction. But it
is evident that this cannot be regarded as the received position of the Roman
Church. This is clear not only from Bellarmine and others, who sharply reject
it as rash and heretical—so much so that Baronius, at the year 794, says that
those who held it were insane, while Sirmond, volume 2 of the *Councils of
Gaul*, calls it the *heresy of the Gauls*—but especially from the Council of
Trent, which expressly sanctions the worship of images.

[Marginal heading: The worship of images is rejected. 1. From the law.]

VII. The orthodox, however, oppose it as follows. First, *from the law*,
because an explicit prohibition of it exists when the Lord expressly commands
that we neither make images for the sake of religion nor adore and worship
them. Exodus 20:4 says: “You shall not make for yourself a carved image, nor
any likeness of what is in heaven above, what is on the earth beneath, or what
is in the waters under the earth; you shall not adore them or worship them.”
This is confirmed by Leviticus 26:1: “You shall not make an idol or a carved
image for yourselves, nor shall you erect a statue or figured stone in your
land, to prostrate yourselves before it”; likewise Deuteronomy 4:15, and so
forth. In these passages they are forbidden to make not only פסל, a *carved
image*, that is, a sculpted representation, but also התמונה, any *likeness*,
even a painted one, expressing or representing by any figure some form,
whether in the heavens, on the earth, or beneath the earth. Could anything
ever have been prohibited more explicitly than every image set up for
religious worship is prohibited here? Or was anything ever violated more
manifestly than this commandment is violated in the Roman communion?

VIII. To turn aside the force of this weapon, the following is objected in
vain. First, it is said that *this commandment belongs to positive law*, which
bound only the Jews, and not to natural law, by which all are bound, as
*Vásquez* takes this way of escape in Disputation 4, chapter 3, and *Ambrosius
Catharinus* does in his work *On Images*. *Grotius* joins them. The contrary,
however, is attested both by the immediate and proximate end of the
commandment, which is moral and a matter of perpetual law—namely, that the
true God be worshiped purely and incorruptly, without any idolatry—and by its
two parts. The first concerns εἰδωλοποιΐα, *idol-making*, because it is
impossible and impious to represent God by an image; the other concerns
εἰδωλολατρεία, *idolatry*, which is prohibited without qualification in
Deuteronomy 4:15. Then there is the repetition made more than once in the New
Testament, even after the ceremonial law was abrogated, in Acts 15:20, 1 John
5:21, and repeatedly in each epistle to the Corinthians. Third, the
commandment is known by nature. Hence Paul reproves the Gentiles from it in
Acts 14 and 17 and Romans 1. This is confirmed from the history of the many
nations that refused to admit images; see Plutarch, *Numa*, concerning the
Romans; Tertullian, *Apology*, chapter 25; and Tacitus, *Germany*, chapter 9.
Fourth, most of the adversaries acknowledge this with us: Bellarmine, book 3,
chapter 7; Suárez, on part 3, volume 1, question 25, article 8, Disputation 54,
section 7.

IX. Second, it is objected that the commandment treats idols of things that do
not exist, such as the false divinities of the Gentiles, and not images of
things that exist. Therefore, they judge that פסל ought to be rendered by
εἴδωλον, *idol*, but not by εἰκόνα, *image*. That this is an empty escape is
evident. First, the word פסל is used of any representation. Hence the Seventy
render it sometimes by εἴδωλον, as in Exodus; sometimes by γλυπτόν, as in
Deuteronomy 4:16 and 5:8; and sometimes by εἰκόνα, as in Isaiah 40:18, 20. The
Chaldean renders it by צלמא, *image*. Second, even if the word פסל were
narrower, the word התמונה, especially when joined with the mark of
universality, must necessarily include every image, unless we wish a likeness
not to be an image and vice versa. Third, the Vulgate version rejects this
distinction in Deuteronomy 4:16 when it renders the phrase “a carved likeness,
or the image of a male,” and in Isaiah 40:18, “what image will you set up for
him?” Fourth, a distinction between εἴδωλον and εἰκόνα is invented without
reason. For although in theological and

[Printed p. 50 | Internet Archive PDF 78 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

ecclesiastical usage *idol* is generally taken in a bad sense, while *image*
is also taken in a good sense for any representation, it is nevertheless
certain that in grammatical usage they have the same meaning and are used
indiscriminately for any representation. Hence the gloss: εἰκών, εἴδωλον.
And in Cicero *image* and *idol* differ only in that one is Greek and the other
Latin. “*Atoms*,” he says in book 1 of *On Ends*, “*are empty images, which
they call idols*.” Thus everywhere in Diodorus, Polybius, Plutarch, and other
approved authors, statues of kings, emperors, and others are called εἴδωλα.
Among ecclesiastical writers the terms are not infrequently confused, so that
εἴδωλον is used for a simple image and, conversely, *image* for an idol.

Fifth, although they may differ in that in present-day usage *idol* is taken
more narrowly and *image* more broadly, so that every idol is indeed an image
but not every image is conversely an idol—unless some relation to divinity is
added to it, whether in representation or in worship—it is certain that they
are nowhere distinguished from one another as the Roman Catholics would
have it, as though an idol were only of things that do not exist, but an image
of things that do exist. For among profane authors an idol is commonly spoken
of existing things. In Scripture, moreover, images of the true God are called
idols. Thus the golden calf is called an *idol* in Acts 7:41, and its
worshipers idolaters, although it was fashioned to represent the true God.
Likewise there were Micah's *idol* in Judges 17 and Jeroboam's *calves* in 1
Kings 12:28. Then, in the law, God prohibits not only images of false things
but also of existing things, whether in heaven, on earth, and so forth. He
does not say things that do not exist, but *things that exist*. Thus any
created things become idols when religious worship is shown to them. The
bronze serpent became an idol when the Israelites wished to burn incense to
it. Nebuchadnezzar's statue was an idol when it was set up for adoration. Most
of the Gentiles' idols were images not of false things but of things that
truly exist, whether stars, human beings, or animals. “*An idol is said to be
nothing*” in 1 Corinthians 8:4, not *subjectively*, in regard to existence, or
*objectively*, in regard to representation, but in regard to *efficacy* and
*formally*, because it is not the true God.

X. Third, it is objected that not all images are forbidden in the law, but
only those made for the adoration of latria and those *regarded as gods*.
Reply: the words of the law reject such an explanation. They forbid not only
latria but also dulia to be rendered to images, for the Hebrew expression
תעבדם means *you shall serve them*, which is δουλεύειν. Second, if only images
regarded as gods are forbidden, the commandment would deal with the *object
of worship*, not its *manner*, and thus the first commandment would be
confused with the second. Third, if the objection were valid, the Gentiles,
who often profess that they do not regard their idols as gods, could easily
be freed from the charge of idolatry. *Celsus* in Origen, book 7, page 384,
says, τίς γὰρ ἄλλος εἰ μὴ πάντα νήπιος ταῦτα ἡγεῖται θεούς; “*Who but a
complete infant thinks that these—namely, the statues—are gods?*” Arnobius,
in book 6, introduces the pagans speaking: “You err and go astray, for neither
do we consider bronze nor the materials of gold and silver to be gods in
themselves and divine beings deserving religious honor; rather, we worship
the gods through these things.” They speak in the same manner in Lactantius,
book 2, chapter 2. Augustine, on Psalm 113, reports and refutes this escape of
theirs. He says: “Those who say, ‘I worship neither the statue nor demons, but
through a bodily figure I behold the sign of that reality which I ought to
worship, and so forth. I worship not this visible thing but the divine being
who dwells there invisibly,’ seem to themselves to belong to a purer
religion.”

Nor would the Israelites have been idolaters in the golden calf, which they
did not suppose to be God. For who would think them so dull that they believed
the work of their own hands to be that God who had brought them out of Egypt?
They intended only to fashion for themselves a representation of him so that
they might worship the true God in this image, as Aaron himself testifies when
he calls the feast consecrated to this idol “*a feast to Jehovah*” in Exodus

[Printed p. 51 | Internet Archive PDF 79 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

32:5. It is no objection that they said to Aaron, “*Make gods for us*,”
because it is well known that Scripture often gives signs the names of the
things signified.

XI. From this it is evident that the adversaries have nothing by which they
may free themselves from the charge of idolatry brought against them from the
law. When they perceive this and feel themselves pressed by so explicit a
commandment, they turn their indignation against God's law itself. With
sacrilegious audacity and an act unheard of before, they expunge this
commandment from the number of the others so that it does not make trouble
for them—if not from Scripture itself, since that was not in their power, at
least from the offices, breviaries, and other forms of the divine law given to
the people. This is surely a conspicuous proof of a desperate cause.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because the worship of images is idolatrous.]

XII. Second, because the religious worship of images is never commanded or
approved by Scripture, it cannot be freed from the charge of idolatry. For,
according to the usage of Scripture and the ancient church, every image made
for the sake of religion is an idol. Therefore, the person who bows and
prostrates himself before images (Isa. 2:8–9), or who adores them in themselves
with any properly so-called religious worship—which, by whatever name it is
called, is owed to God alone—worships idols. Indeed, those who did not worship
images in themselves but worshiped the prototypes in an image are called
idolaters. Such were the Israelites in the worship of the golden calf (Exod.
32:4–5) and in the worship of the two calves erected by Jeroboam at Dan and
Bethel (1 Kings 12:28). For Ahab afterward introduced the worship of Baal and
false gods through images (1 Kings 16:32, 23), and he is therefore said to
have done evil more than all who were before him. The same is said of Micah's
idol (Judg. 17:3), which was consecrated to Jehovah and whose priest is
described as a priest of Jehovah in chapters 17 and 18. Likewise, although the
Gentiles adored the prototype before images, they are nevertheless justly
condemned as idolaters. Finally, the worship by which God is worshiped in a
manner that is not due but unlawful and forbidden cannot fail to be
idolatrous.

It is replied in vain here that the images themselves are not worshiped in
themselves, which would be idolatrous, but are worshiped only in relation to
the prototype, or that the prototype is worshiped in them. For besides the
fact that not a few of them will the image itself to be properly worshiped in
itself as something sacred, and to terminate the worship, not merely to take
the place of the exemplar, as Bellarmine teaches at length in book 2 of *On
Images*, chapters 20 and 22, the words of the law expressly forbid all worship
of images, whether absolute or relative. Where, moreover, did God institute a
relative worship of latria whose proximate object is a creature and remote
object is God? Could not the calf-worshiping Israelites of old, and even the
pagans themselves, easily have varnished their idolatry with the same
distinction? Then, if σχέσις, a *relation*, to the prototype exempted someone
from the charge of idolatry, there could never be any idolatry concerning
images of the Deity, because they cannot be worshiped as images without a
relation to the prototype they represent.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because they can be worshiped neither for themselves nor
for the exemplar.]

XIII. Third, if images could be worshiped religiously, this would be either
*for themselves* or *for the exemplar*. But neither can be said. Not *for
themselves*, because things made and preserved by human care are beneath the
dignity of man, being inanimate objects subject to the hand and imagination
of the artist. Hence the worship of statues is so often forbidden in Psalm
115:8, Isaiah 40:18, Jeremiah 10:14, and Habakkuk 2:18–19, because they are
the work of human hands. Not *for the exemplar*, whether God or the saints,
because God does not will to be worshiped in an image in which he did not put
his name, and he cannot be represented in a material image; while the saints
are to be worshiped neither in themselves nor in images.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because it was unknown in the ancient church.]

XIV. Fourth, the worship of images was unknown in the Christian church for
the first four centuries. This is evident from various testimonies of the
fathers who opposed it: *Origen*, book 3

[Printed p. 52 | Internet Archive PDF 80 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of *Against Celsus*, page 135, and book 7, page 386; *Tertullian*, *Apology*,
chapter 12, and *On Idolatry*; *Clement of Alexandria*, *Exhortation*;
*Lactantius*, book 2, chapter 1; the letter of *Epiphanius* to John of
Jerusalem in volume 2, which is reported by Jerome in letter 61 to Pammachius;
and *Augustine* on Psalm 113. Second, it is evident from the Council of
Elvira, where Canon 36 expressly decrees: “It was resolved that pictures
ought not to be in churches, lest what is worshiped or adored be depicted on
walls.” Third, the fathers everywhere bring the worship of images as an
objection against the pagans, while the Gentiles, answering Christians who
bring this reproach against them, never adduce the worship of images as
customary among Christians in order to remove, or at least mitigate, the
odium of their own superstition concerning idols. They would undoubtedly not
have omitted this if anything of the sort had already been in use among
Christians. Not only do they offer no such reply, but they charge Christians
with the absence of altars and statues as the greatest impiety. Far from
mentioning any use or worship of images among themselves in order to clear
themselves of this charge, Christians instead employ various arguments that
prove it impossible, unlawful, and useless to make images of the Deity. The
Jews, in disputes with Christians, never bring against them the worship of
images, which was supremely hateful to the Jews. Various fathers condemned
the art of painting either as absolutely unlawful or as vain and useless.
From these and other similar reasons it is invincibly concluded that the
worship of images had not yet prevailed in the primitive church, a point the
celebrated Dallaeus pursues solidly in his customary manner in books 1 and 2
of *On Images*.

Fourth, not a few Roman Catholics acknowledge this. *Cassander*, in the
heading on images in his *Consultation*, says: “It is certain that for some
time at the beginning of the preaching of the gospel, there was no use of
images among Christians, especially in churches.” Later he says: “How greatly
the ancients at the church's beginning abhorred every veneration of images is
shown by Origen alone,” and so forth. “Finally,” he says, “pictures were
admitted into the church as representations expressing the history of deeds
or conveying the true likenesses of holy men,” and so forth. Erasmus says in
the *Catechism*: “Down to the age of Jerome there were men of approved
religion who tolerated no image in churches, whether painted, carved, woven,
or even of Christ,” and so forth. In the same place he says: “For neither
human ordinance commands that images be in churches; and just as it is
easier, so it is also safer to remove all images from churches.” Lilius
Gyraldus says in the *Syntagma*, in the book *On the Gods of the Gentiles*,
page 20: “I certainly will not omit that we Christians, like the Romans at one
time, were without images in what is called the primitive church.” Polydore
Vergil, in *On the Inventors of Things*, book 6, chapter 13, testifies that
“almost all the ancient fathers condemned the worship of statues because of
the fear of idolatry, than which no crime can be more execrable.” By the
decree of the Council of Trent the Index Expurgatorius judged these words and
the following ones should be deleted. The latter teach that Moses pressed
nothing more than that the people should venerate nothing made by hand and
that the prophet said, “Let all who adore carved images be confounded.”

XV. Although after the fourth century, at the beginning of the fifth,
*Paulinus*, bishop of Nola, and *Severus*, bishop of Bourges, caused certain
historical pictures to be made in the basilicas they had constructed, this
was not done so that they would be images of that which is religiously
worshiped, nor that those pictures themselves would be religiously worshiped,
but only so that they might be historical and commemorative symbols as well
as ornaments of the basilicas, as is evident from Paulinus's letter 13 to
Severus. But when, little by little, the worship of images was introduced by
some from their use, it happened at the beginning of the sixth century that
*Serenus*, bishop of Marseilles, and the Roman *Gregory I* wished to resist
this superstition, though by different paths. The former did so by removing
the images entirely; the latter by retaining them indeed, but diligently
warning the people that they were useful only for memory

[Printed p. 53 | Internet Archive PDF 81 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and were in no way to be adored. Hence, writing “to Serenus,” he says: “We
praised you for having the zeal that nothing made by hand might be adored,
but we judge that you ought not to have broken those same images.” But since
the worship of images gradually returned from their retention and gained
strength as superstition spread little by little in both the West and the
East, various decrees for the removal of images were made by emperors: by Leo
III the Isaurian in 726; by his son Constantine VI Caballinus, called
Copronymus, who, after 338 bishops of the East had assembled at Constantinople
in the seventh ecumenical synod, caused a decree against the worship of images
to be made in 754; by the emperors Christopher and Nicephorus in 775; and by
Leo IV in 780. Although Irene, the wife of Leo IV, obtained control of affairs
in the East while her son was underage and eagerly labored to restore the
images in which she took intense delight, assembling a general synod at
Nicaea that sanctioned their worship against the Constantinopolitan synod in
787, various emperors in the East nevertheless did not cease to oppose it:
Nicephorus I and Stauracius in 810; Leo V the Armenian in 814; Michael II the
Stammerer and Theophilus in 824, 829, 830, and 832; and Michael III
Porphyrogenitus in 866. See the *Imperial Decrees on the Worship of Images*.

XVI. But especially in the West, *Charlemagne* bore the decree of the Second
Nicene Synod with such indignation that he not only opposed the worship of
images against the pseudo-synod of Nicaea with great learning in a particular
book recently reprinted under the title *Capitulary of Charlemagne*—that it
is a true and genuine production of the author, whatever Baronius and
Bellarmine bring to the contrary, is lucidly demonstrated even by the
testimony of Hincmar, archbishop of Rheims, in book 4, chapter 28, and the
lengthy letter of Pope Adrian to Charlemagne answering this argument—but he
also called the Synod of Frankfurt in 794, which condemned Nicaea and made a
severe decree against the worship of images. From this, it is so clear that
the prelates of the Western Church steadfastly repudiated every veneration of
images whatsoever that Baronius and those who edited the *Library of the
Fathers* unwillingly acknowledge that the most distinguished writers of that
age held this opinion. Among them are *Agobard*, bishop of Lyon; *Claudius of
Turin*; *Jonas of Orléans*, who indeed charges Claudius with intemperance for
removing images but otherwise agrees with him that they are by no means to be
adored, in volume 4 of the *Library of the Fathers*, book 1; and *Walafrid
Strabo*, volume 10 of the *Library of the Fathers*, page 668.

This was confirmed more forcefully at the Synod of Paris, convened in 825 by
*Louis the Pious*, Charlemagne's son, and published by P. Pithou, at which the
worship of images was again condemned. Hence Pithou, in the preface to the
history of Paul the Deacon, says: “*If we seriously want the truth, it is only
very recently that our people*”—meaning the Gauls—“*began to be fond of
images*.” The eminent Du Plessis in his *History of the Papacy* and Forbes in
*Theological Instruction* 2.7 relate at length how many and how great were the
tumults stirred up from the eighth century over the erection and worship of
images between *iconoclasts* and *iconolaters* in both East and West. Forbes
also, in chapter 12, continues the historical account of images from the time
of the Council of Frankfurt to the thirteenth century and shows the constancy
of the Gauls and Germans down to that century in rejecting images. See also
*Dallaeus on Images*.

XVII. Before the Council of Trent, not a few Roman Catholics inveighed
against the veneration of images and the notorious abuses surrounding their
use, including *William Durandus*, bishop of Mende; *John Billet*, a Parisian
theologian; *Gerson*; *Biel*; and others. Indeed, during the council,
*Sebastian*, elector of Mainz, held a provincial synod at which it was decreed
that images are not set forth in churches for adoration or for worship to be
rendered to them, but only to recall to memory the realities that ought to be
adored. See the *History of the Council of

[Printed p. 54 | Internet Archive PDF 82 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Trent*, book 3. Pertinent here is the fact that in 1561 *Catherine de'
Medici*, queen of France, sent letters to Pius IV in which, among other
things, she requested “*that the use of images, forbidden by God and condemned
by blessed Gregory, be entirely removed from the place of adoration*.” And in
1562, by her command, the bishops of Valence and Séez, Butillerius,
Espensaeus, and Picherellus, who were chosen to consult on a method of
reaching agreement with the Protestants, proposed this provision among
others: “That representations of the Most Holy Trinity be removed from
churches; that crowns and clothing not be placed on images; that vows and
offerings not be made to them; that they not be carried around in
processions; and that the sign of the cross no longer be worshiped.” Although
afterward, following the council's decree, the worship of images prevailed
everywhere in the Roman Church, there nevertheless have always been, and
there are even today, very many among them who are ashamed of such gross
idolatry. Hence so many distinctions, pretexts, and disguises are contrived
to varnish this superstition and ward off the odium of so great a crime,
although they cannot satisfy even themselves with these devices. Indeed,
Bellarmine, in book 2 of *On Images*, chapters 22 and 23, concedes that “if the
reality itself is considered, images may be worshiped improperly, or by
accident, with the same kind of worship with which the exemplar is worshiped;
but as regards the manner of speaking to the people, it ought not to be said
that any images ought to be adored with latria”—as if it were impious to say
what it is nevertheless lawful to do.

XVIII. Fifth, the Roman Catholics' disagreement and their varying, uncertain,
and even inextricable positions concerning the adoration of images
sufficiently prove the dogma false. Some contend that images are to be
worshiped with the same worship as the exemplar: namely, images of God,
Christ, and the cross with latria; images of the Blessed Virgin with
hyperdulia; and images of the saints with dulia, as Thomas, his school, and
most of the scholastics hold. Others, such as Bellarmine and his followers,
assign them a worship inferior to the worship of the exemplar. Some say they
are to be worshiped properly and in themselves; others only by accident,
analogically and by reduction to the exemplar. Since, then, it is not yet
settled among the worshipers of images whether they ought truly and properly
to be adored, or with what kind of worship they should be honored, such
worship is wrongly prescribed to the people, who are thereby brought into a
most immediate and perpetual danger of idolatry. For how many of the common
people, or even the learned, either understand such distinctions or, having
understood them, rightly apply them, so that by a mental abstraction, while
prostrating themselves before an image, they show it no proper worship, but
only relative and analogical worship, or a worship inferior to that of the
prototype itself? Who, on the contrary, does not see that in practice things
are confused which, with great exertion, the doctors attempt to distinguish
in theory in order to cloak an exceedingly dreadful image-mania? Bellarmine
certainly could not conceal this. Speaking of the distinction between
“absolute and relative latria,” he says in book 2, chapter 22: “Those who
defend the adoration of images with latria are compelled to employ the most
subtle distinctions, which they themselves scarcely understand, much less the
unlearned people.”

XIX. It is one thing *to bow oneself toward the footstool of God's feet*, that
is, toward the ark, which is so called as a symbol of God's glorious
presence; but another thing *to adore the footstool itself*. We grant the
former but not the latter. Nor is the contrary proved from Psalm 99:5, when
the psalmist commands, “השתחוו להדם רגליו,” that is, “*Adore, or bow
yourselves, toward the footstool of his feet*”; likewise in verse 9, להר
קדשו, “*toward the mountain of his holiness*.” Here the *place* of public
adoration is proposed, not its *object*, as the Chaldean renders it, “*Adore
in the house of his sanctuary*.” The Seventy likewise render the same words
of Psalm 132:7, προσκυνήσομεν εἰς τὸν τόπον, “*We will adore toward the place
in which his feet stood*.” Second, the godly are commanded to bow themselves
toward the temple in Psalm 5:7, yet all acknowledge that the temple was not
to be worshiped.

XX. The inference from the *cherubim* and the *bronze serpent* to images is
invalid. A symbol, emblem, or type of a reality—as the serpent

[Printed p. 55 | Internet Archive PDF 83 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of bronze was a type of Christ—is one thing; an image is another. Second,
neither the cherubim nor the bronze serpent was adored, as Vásquez himself
acknowledges. God indeed promises in Exodus 25:22 that he will answer Moses
from the midst of the cherubim, but nowhere commands him to adore them. Nor
were they positioned for worship, since they were above the ark in the Holy
of Holies, outside the people's sight. As for the bronze serpent, it was so
far from being due religious worship that it was even cast down and crushed
by King Hezekiah when the Jews began to worship it (2 Kings 18:4). Therefore,
the Israelites were healed not by religious worship of this serpent but by
looking at it according to God's command. Nor is it an objection that it was
placed in a high location, for the rationale of the type and the necessity of
seeing it required this. The sun and stars also have been placed on high,
surely not for worship.

XXI. The adoration of the Angel, which the patriarchs performed more than
once, does not favor the worship of images, because they adored the uncreated
Angel, who not only belonged to Jehovah but was himself Jehovah, as is clearly
gathered from the circumstances of the passages and has been shown elsewhere,
Locus VII, Question 9, Thesis 22.

XXII. When the apostle says in Galatians 3:1 that “*Christ was depicted
before the eyes of the Galatians, crucified among them*,” he is not speaking
about forming images of the crucifix, whether made by the painter's brush or
the sculptor's axe, but about the preaching of the gospel, through which
Christ is presented to us as crucified. For Paul proposes to teach nothing
other than Christ crucified (1 Cor. 2:2), and the word προεγράφη, *was
portrayed beforehand*, which the apostle uses means nothing else.

XXIII. The honor of an image does not pass to the prototype and exemplar
unless the one who is the exemplar has willed or instituted it. But if he has
forbidden an image of himself to be made or honored, he is wronged if anyone
acts contrary to his will. We contend that God has done so here. Nor can a
good intention suffice here, because the goodness of a work must be judged
not from the intention of the doer but from the command of the Lawgiver.

XXIV. The inference from the worship of *God's name*, which is prescribed in
Exodus 20:7 and Philippians 2:10, to the worship of images is invalid. In
those passages *name* is not taken precisely for syllables and letters, but
for the reality signified by the name, in which sense the Hebrews are
accustomed to call God himself שם, *the Name*.

XXV. Although Christians are read to have used the sign of the cross before
Constantine the Great, yet only ἀνυπάρκτως, *without a subsisting object*,
formed in the air by a particular motion of the fingers, as is evident from
Tertullian, *On the Soldier's Crown*, chapter 3, this was done by no law of
Scripture but by custom alone, as Tertullian acknowledges in the same place.
Nor did they employ this sign as something to be adored, or as effectual *ex
opere operato*, as Bellarmine falsely contends in the final chapter of book 1
of *On Images*, but only as a distinguishing sign and token by which
Christians recognized one another, and so that what was cast against them as
a reproach—that they worshiped a crucified man—they might claim for their
glory, as Masius testifies on Joshua 22. Hence *Minucius Felix* says in the
*Octavius*: “As for crosses, we neither worship nor desire them; but you, who
consecrate wooden gods, perhaps adore wooden crosses as parts of your gods.”
If Constantine the Great is reported to have been moved by a heavenly vision
that promised victory in the sign of the cross, with these words, Ἐν τούτῳ
νίκα, “*Conquer in this*,” so that he made the labarum in the shape of a
cross; and if his mother Helena is said to have found the wood of the cross at
Jerusalem in 326, how suspect this ought to be can be taught by Eusebius's
silence alone. Yet no worship is therefore read to have been rendered to the
cross. Indeed, Ambrose expressly refutes this in his funeral oration for
Theodosius: “Helena,” he says, “found the inscription; she adored the King,
surely not the wood, because that is the error of the Gentiles and the vanity
of the impious.” If images of the cross were afterward gradually introduced
into Christian oratories, although many godly men opposed them, they were not
yet objects of adoration but symbols of the Christian faith, until in later

[Printed p. 56 | Internet Archive PDF 84 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

times, especially around the eighth century, this worship was sanctioned
under Irene at the pseudo-Nicene synod.
