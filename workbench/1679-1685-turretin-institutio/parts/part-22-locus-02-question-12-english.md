QUESTION XII

Is the present Hebrew text so authentic and God-inspired (θεόπνευστος), both
as to the things and as to the words, that all the versions which exist must
be tested by its standard and recalled to it wherever they depart from it? Or
is it permissible to abandon the reading it supplies if that reading is
judged less suitable, to correct it either by a comparison of the ancient
interpreters or sometimes by one's own judgment and faculty of conjecture
(στοχαστικῇ), and to follow another, more suitable reading? We affirm the
former and deny the latter.

I. Because the authenticity (αὐθεντία) of the sacred text is a primary
foundation of faith, nothing ought to be of greater concern to all the godly
than that it be preserved unimpaired against the efforts of those who attempt
either to overthrow it utterly or to weaken it in any way. The preceding
controversy against the Roman Catholics was already undertaken for this
purpose. The proposed question also looks to that end. In it the opinion of
the reverend and learned Louis Cappel is brought under examination. Just as he
undertook to defend very sharply the novelty of the points as a recent
invention of the Masoretes and thus as something proceeding from human
industry and labor in *The Mystery*

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 106 | IA leaf 160 | PDF p. 160]

*of Pointing Revealed*, so in his *Sacred Criticism* he strives with great
effort to prove that we are
by no means so bound to the present reading of the Hebrew text that it is not
permissible to depart from it often, whenever by comparison with the ancient
interpreters, by the force of right reason, or by our own judgment and faculty
of conjecture we can discover another, better and more suitable reading. He
pursues this at length in book 6, chapters 4, 5, and so forth. We do not do
this with the intention of detracting in any way from the reputation of a man
who otherwise deserved well of the church of God, but only to confirm the
opinion hitherto constantly received in our churches concerning the
unimpaired authenticity (αὐθεντίᾳ) of the sacred text against those who even
today venture to adopt those “authoritative opinions” (κυρίας δόξας) and novel
hypotheses, or speak of them as though they were a trifling matter of no, or
very little, importance to faith.

II. His opinion comes down to this. 1. Because the points are a human
invention, they can be changed and others substituted when necessity so
requires and when the meaning they yield is either false or absurd. 2. There
is freedom to change the reading not only with respect to the pointing, but
also with respect to the writing itself. There is, however, greater freedom
concerning the former than the latter, because the Masoretes often made the
pointing according to their own choice and private judgment, to which we ought
not to be bound. 3. If comparison with the ancient interpreters, whether
Greek, Chaldean, or Latin, can show that the meaning of the versions is either
equally good and suitable or truer and more suitable than that of our Hebrew
codex, it is permissible to change the present reading and to follow and
substitute another. 4. This can be done not only from a comparison of the
interpreters. If we can show that a defect lies in the present reading and
that its meaning is either nonexistent or absurd and false, and thus if by the
force of right reason and the natural faculty of reasoning and arguing, or
even by divining from conjectures, we can find a better, plainer, and more
fitting meaning through another, more suitable reading, then in these cases it
is permissible to strike down the present reading and prefer another to it.
That this is his meaning can be seen from various places, and especially from
this passage in *Sacred Criticism*, book 6, chapter 5, paragraph 9: “From these
things it is clear that if any reading which differs from the present Hebrew
text—even with respect to consonants or letters, and so also with respect to
entire words and periods—produces a more suitable meaning, it is more genuine,
pure, and authentic wherever at last it may be found, whether among the
Seventy, the Chaldean paraphrasts, Aquila, Symmachus, Theodotion, or St. Jerome,
the author of the Vulgate Version, and therefore is to be followed and embraced
more than the present Masoretic reading.” He confirms this more often
elsewhere.

III. To confirm this opinion, however, he introduces another hypothesis:
namely, that the Hebrew codices used by the Seventy and the other interpreters
were different from the present codex, which he contemptuously calls the
Masoretic and Jewish codex; and that the divergences of the ancient versions
from the present Hebrew codex are so many variant readings of the Hebrew text.
He excepts perhaps those which arise from the interpreters' own mistakes when
they either did not grasp the meaning of Hebrew words or did not attend to
them sufficiently. Hence he denies that our present Hebrew Bibles constitute
the whole fountain. They are to be regarded only as a single exemplar, while
the true, genuine, original, and authentic text is finally to be constituted
and formed from a comparison of all the ancient versions. He therefore
distinguishes between the Hebrew codex in itself and the present Masoretic
codex. The latter is found in all the exemplars possessed today, whether among
Jews or Christians. The former can finally be gathered from a comparison of
the present text and the ancient versions. Those versions, in the cases
stated,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 107 | IA leaf 161 | PDF p. 161]

he not only makes equal to the present codex, but also plainly prefers to it,
for he
repeatedly determines that the meaning they yield is to be followed as more
suitable and truer than the other meaning rendered by the present codex, as
was seen above. “And not only,” he says in his *Apology against Boate*, page
54, “if the reading of the Seventy (τῶν LXX.) yields a better meaning, but even
if it yields a meaning as good and suitable as the present reading, as much
account is to be taken of the former as of the latter, because the former is
more ancient; and where reading and meaning are equally good, greater account
is taken of the more ancient codex.” And a little later: “The authority of the
codex of the Seventy (τῶν LXX.) stands in some measure above the present one,
not only in those places where its reading produces a more suitable meaning,
but also where it yields an equally good and suitable meaning, and this
because of its antiquity. The same can and ought to be said of all the codices
of the ancient interpreters.”

IV. But the received and common opinion in our churches is far different:
namely, that no codex other than the present Hebrew one is to be acknowledged
as authentic; that all versions, both ancient and more recent, must be tested
by it as by a Lydian stone and corrected wherever they depart from it; and
that it must not instead be emended from them. Our theologians do indeed judge
that various codices can and ought to be compared with one another so that
variant readings which could have arisen from the carelessness of transcribers
or copyists may be distinguished and the errors which occur in these or those
codices may be corrected. Nor do they deny that considerable benefit for
investigating the true meaning can be gained from comparison with the ancient
versions. Nevertheless, they deny that those versions are ever to be made
equal, much less preferred, to the original text, as though one might embrace
the meaning they yield because it seems more suitable to us and reject the
other meaning which flows from the reading of the present text.

V. It is clearer than the noonday light that this has always been the mind of
all Protestants from the beginning. The controversy with the Roman Catholics
themselves concerning the authentic edition, treated above, sufficiently
proves it. The reverend and learned man with whom we are dealing cannot deny
it, for at the very beginning of his *Criticism*, book 1, chapter 1, he writes:
“Each of the earliest and ancient Protestants said that everything must be
examined and emended by the Hebrew text, which they call the purest fountain.”
Sixtinus Amama confirms this in his highly praised work, *Biblical
Antibarbarian*, book 1, chapter 3. After setting forth his own opinion on this
matter, he says: “We conclude that all versions, whether ancient or recent,
with none excepted, are to be examined by that text—that is, the Hebrew
text—and so forth; that it even now is the norm, rule, and canon of all
versions.” And in chapter 4: “Consequently no version, whatever it may be, can
be made equal, much less preferred, to the Hebrew text. Thus all Protestants
think concerning all versions, ancient and recent.”

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

VI. From these things the state of this question is easily gathered. The
question is not whether versions may be compared with one another and with
the original in order to investigate the true meaning. Rather, it is whether
one may make equal or prefer to the original a reading which arises from them
and which appears more suitable, substituting it for the present reading when,
in our judgment, that reading renders either no meaning or one which is false
and absurd. The question is not whether there are various divergences between
the present text and the ancient versions. Rather, it is whether those
divergences are to be regarded as so many variant readings of the Hebrew
codex, so that no reading is to be acknowledged as authentic except that which
arises from comparing the present text and the ancient versions. Finally, the
question is not whether, in examining and comparing codices with one another,
both manuscript and printed, we may use our judgment and faculty of reasoning
to distinguish the more approved codices and to judge which reading is better
and more suitable. Rather, it is whether one may make critical conjectures in
the sacred text, no less than in profane authors, so as to change

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 108 | IA leaf 162 | PDF p. 162]

letters and points and even entire words whenever the meaning from the present
reading does not seem suitable to us. The reverend and learned man contends
for this; we deny it.

VII. The reasons are: 1. From this hypothesis it follows that there is no
authentic text in which faith can wholly rest. For it would be either the
present Hebrew codex—but on that opinion it holds the place of only one
exemplar, and a reading can be regarded as the authentic Hebrew fountain only
where there is no disagreement between the interpreters and the present codex,
as the reverend and learned man says in his *Apology against Boate*, page
17—or other codices which the ancient interpreters used. But apart from the
fact that their difference from the present codex is gratuitously imagined,
which is his first falsehood (πρῶτον ψεῦδος), as will be proved below, even if
this were granted him, they could not now establish credibility because they
are missing and no longer exist anywhere except in that version. Since that
version is human and fallible, it cannot render a codex authentic. Further,
who will be able to establish that the Seventy most faithfully followed their
Hebrew text and that the Greek text read today is precisely the same as that
which the Seventy wrote?

VIII. 2. If all the divergences of the ancient versions from the present text
were so many variant readings of Hebrew codices different from ours which the
interpreters used, why did no memory of such codices remain among the
ancients, and why are there no traces of them among the Hebrews? For many
centuries the Hebrews have been so diligent in observing and collecting even
the smallest variations, as the divergent readings of Ben Asher and Ben
Naphtali, and of the Easterners and Westerners, show. Who would believe that
these variations of lesser importance remained, while those observed today
from the ancient interpreters so completely perished that no memory of them
survives? Since there were many exemplars, it is remarkable that none came
down to us. 2. It is gratuitously assumed that there are no causes of these
divergences except a diversity of codices, although many other far more
certain causes occur. In the same way, one would wrongly infer from present
versions that their interpreters used different codices simply because
innumerable differences of translation occur, although no codex other than
the present one exists from which they were made. For who does not know that
they could often attend to the meaning rather than the words, as Jerome says
more than once about the Seventy interpreters? Again, a divergence could
sometimes arise from their ignorance or carelessness, because they did not
attend sufficiently to the words and so could often go astray among similar
letters and words even without a variant codex, as Jerome more than once
censures in them. 3. They could also take excessive license, so that although
they read one thing in their codex, they rendered another too boldly because
they did not sufficiently grasp the meaning and coherence of the Hebrew text
and, with a slight change, preferred to follow the meaning which they judged
to flow more suitably. 4. It is established that the various ancient versions
are no longer such as they were when first prepared, but have been corrupted
and changed in a remarkable manner, as is certain especially of the Septuagint
and the Vulgate. 5. It could happen through the drowsiness or ignorance of
copyists that many corruptions crept into the versions which did not therefore
arise from a diversity of codices.

IX. 3. In this way the ancient versions are made equal to the original text.
For if, in every divergence, the ancient interpreters are no more to be
examined by the text than it is by them, but both must be tested by the common
canon of the more suitable meaning, so that the reading which greater
suitability of meaning commends prevails, whether it is found in the Hebrew
text or in any one of those interpreters, then no authority above the ancient
interpreters is left to the Hebrew text except that which

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 109 | IA leaf 163 | PDF p. 163]

is sought from a greater suitability of meaning. Indeed, it is often pressed
down beneath the translations when its reading is postponed to others.

X. 4. If we are not bound to the present reading of the Hebrew text, but the
true authentic reading is to be sought partly from a comparison of the ancient
versions and partly from our own judgment and faculty of conjecture
(στοχαστικῇ), so that there is no other canon of the authentic reading than
the more suitable meaning which appears so to us, then the constitution of the
authentic reading will be a work of human choice and reason, not of the Holy
Spirit. Human reason will be placed in the citadel and held with the Socinians
as the norm and principle of faith.

XI. 5. If it is permissible to make conjectures in the sacred text even when
the Hebrew codices agree with the versions, as the reverend and learned man
says in *Criticism*, book 6, chapter 8, paragraph 17, there can no longer be
certainty of the authenticity (αὐθεντίας) of the sacred text. Instead, all
things will be rendered doubtful and uncertain, and the sacred text will be
subjected to the judgment of every private conjecturer. Any sensible person
easily recognizes whether this is not to strip it of all authority. Nor should
it be answered that conjectures are not to be admitted unless they rest upon
certain reasons and arguments and are demonstrated when the received reading
yields a meaning which is either false and absurd or doubtful and confused.
For there will be no one who does not think that he can give reasons for his
conjectures and who does not accuse of falsity and absurdity the reading he is
striving to strike down. But who will be able to be the judge of these
conjectures and determine whether they have been made rightly and truly?
Without a judge there will be perpetual disputes and contentions among
interpreters, while each fights for his own conjectures and will not allow
others to be preferred to them. And if conjectures can have a place in the
examination of various codices in order to weigh which reading is better and
more suitable, it does not follow that other conjectures, resting upon one's
own judgment but upon the authority of no approved codex, should at once be
admitted. There can be no use for these, but only the greatest danger and the
certain ruin of the Scriptures from the great and audacious presumption of
human ingenuity. Nor should the example of profane authors, upon whom
criticism can be freely and safely exercised, have weight here—as though the
principle of sacred and profane criticism were the same and there were not the
greatest difference between human writings, which are liable to error, and a
divine and God-inspired writing (θεόπνευστον), whose majesty ought to be
inviolable. It was received with such veneration, preserved with such care,
and approved with such agreement that it merited the designation of authentic
truth and of the fountain. But what will become of this sacred codex if it is
equally permissible for anyone to draw the censor's pen against it and
exercise criticism upon it as upon any profane books?

XII. 6. If the present Hebrew codex has no prerogative above the ancient
interpreters, so that no greater account is to be taken of it than of them,
and indeed their reading is not seldom to be preferred to the present reading
when it seems to yield a more suitable meaning, then the Protestants have
hitherto wrongly contended against the Roman Catholics in asserting the
authenticity of the present Hebrew text alone above any versions, whether
ancient or more recent. Nor will it any longer be possible to press against
them that all versions, and especially the Vulgate, must be tested by it and
corrected from it, since the versions are so often not only made equal but
also preferred to it.

XIII. A variant reading is one thing; a varying interpretation is another.
Interpreters could give varying interpretations, but it does not follow that
these proceeded from variant readings of various codices. Rather, they arose
from the other causes already mentioned.

XIV. It is not necessary that the scribes should have been incapable of error
(ἀναμάρτητοι) if there are no variant Hebrew codices. It is sufficient that
providence so watched over the integrity of the authentic codex that although
various errors could have been introduced into the sacred text through
ignorance or drowsiness, they nevertheless were not introduced, or were not
introduced into

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 110 | IA leaf 164 | PDF p. 164]

absolutely all the exemplars or in such a way that they could not be restored
and corrected by a comparison of various codices and of Scripture itself.

XV. Although we rightly say that Scripture is rendered uncertain by diverse
variant readings drawn from various interpreters by conjecture alone, it is
not at once rendered uncertain by diverse interpretations, because those
interpreters interpreted one and the same text in different ways. For in this
case the meaning is indeed rendered doubtful and uncertain, but not the
reading of the words and phrases. But when so many variant and uncertain
readings and conjectures are posited, it is most difficult to maintain the
text's certainty, because here a twofold uncertainty is introduced, both of
the reading and of the meaning. In the former case, a certain foundation has
been laid to which the dissenting interpreters are bound. In the latter, no
certain foundation has been laid, but everything depends upon the choice and
decision of human judgment.

XVI. For us to be bound to the present codex, it need not present to us the
very autograph (αὐτόγραφον) of Moses and the prophets without even the
smallest difference. For preserving the exact conformity of exemplars with
the archetypes, it is sufficient that the same words be in both, without which
the meaning does not stand, and also that the same letters be with the words,
without which the words do not stand and cannot be written or inscribed, even
though certain differences may occur in other minutiae and small marks.

XVII. Although the reverend and learned man often professes that all versions
must be judged and corrected from the authentic Hebrew text, and that it must
be preferred to any translations, he cannot at once be freed from the charge
laid against him: that he makes the ancient versions equal to the original
text and sometimes even prefers them to it. For by *the Hebrew text* he does
not mean the present original text which passes through the hands of all Jews
and Christians, but the Hebrew text in general. He wishes to gather this both
from the present codex and from the ancient codices which he supposes the
interpreters used—an assertion made, as was said before, without any solid
foundation. And all the theologians who have disputed up to this point about
the Hebrew text and its authenticity have understood no other text than the
common one received today.

XVIII. From these things, to add no more now, it can be sufficiently clear how
dangerous the reverend and learned man's hypotheses are and how reasonably
our theologians everywhere resisted the publication of his work, lest, as
they foresaw would happen, the cause of God suffer some harm from it and lest
they should supply the adversaries with weapons against the authenticity
(αὐθεντίαν) of the sacred text, though doubtless contrary to his intention.

XIX. If anyone desires more, he should consult the celebrated Buxtorf's
*Anti-Criticism*, which he opposed to the reverend and learned man's
*Criticism* and in which he treated this entire argument accurately and with
the greatest solidity. Other testimonies of great men could also be brought
forward, showing how greatly this opinion and writing of the reverend and
learned man displeased them. Such is the testimony of the great James Ussher,
archbishop of Armagh, who says in his letter to Boate that it “contains a most
dangerous error, which must be met early before it spreads more widely.” There
is also that of the same Arnold Boate in his letter to that venerable prelate
and in his *Vindications*. In these he speaks of this work as “a most
pernicious writing in which nothing is sound” (οὐδὲν ὑγιὲς), page 51; he calls
it “an ill-starred and truly accursed criticism, which sets forth an
utterly false opinion singularly injurious to the sacred Word of God,” page
57; and says that its author “is the most hostile enemy of the Hebrew truth,
who strips it of all certainty and authority,” page 1. Nor were there only a
few others who complained of it publicly. But for now the testimony of the
great Andrew Rivet, celebrated throughout all France and Belgium, should
suffice for us. Although, after reading the reverend and learned man's
*Mystery*, he seemed to have been drawn over to his opinion, nevertheless,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 111 | IA leaf 165 | PDF p. 165]

after reading the celebrated Buxtorf's reply, he speaks far otherwise about it
in a letter to Buxtorf sent from The Hague in 1645, which Buxtorf himself
relates in book 1 of the *Anti-Criticism*, chapter 20, page 345: “As concerns
Cappel, our most illustrious friend will arrange for his diatribe against you
to be delivered to you. I have not yet seen it, but I know it has been
published and is for sale at Amsterdam. It will provide you an occasion to
investigate more exactly the other things which, relying on dangerous reasons,
he is devising against the sacred text under the appearance of candor and
truth. In these things I see that he has imposed upon learned and weighty men
who are not sufficiently practiced in such matters. Some of them extol his
*Criticism* as a work not only polished but almost necessary. Our colleagues
who are most practiced in these studies judged otherwise here, for they could
not bring themselves to advise our printers to reproduce it with their
presses.” And in another letter sent from Breda to the same man in 1648: “In
this, among other things, I am supremely pleased that, led by genuine zeal for
the authenticity (αὐθεντίᾳ) of the divine Word, you utterly destroyed the
foundation of that most evil and profane *Criticism* which that man attempted
and handed over for the enemy to take up after it had been rejected by the
orthodox. Certainly, if it prevailed, the whole Scripture of the Old Testament
would be done for; it would be nothing other than the buskin of Theramenes, to
be fitted to every foot.” And immediately afterward: “God will restrain this
audacity so that it may not burst forth any further. I congratulate you from
my heart that not only the private cause of your blessed father (τοῦ
μακαρίτου), but also that of the public possession of the whole true church,
has roused you as a godly champion. May God bless this labor of yours and
preserve you for a long time, so that you may favor us with other similar
works.” Not a few followed this most celebrated man's judgment. Although they
had at first been more favorable to the learned man's hypotheses, after
weighing the matter more accurately and reading the writings of the most
illustrious Buxtorf and others against them, they were not ashamed to retract
their former opinion and give place to a better one.
