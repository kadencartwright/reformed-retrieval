QUESTION XXVI.

ON THE MARRIAGE OF CLERGY.

Must perpetual celibacy, by apostolic institution, necessarily be observed by
those in holy orders? Or is marriage lawful for sacred ministers? We deny the
former and affirm the latter against the Romanists.

I. Thus far we have treated the calling of ministers. We must now debate with
the Romanists about their celibacy, immunity, and privileges, since they raise
a controversy with us here concerning both subjects.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

II. To speak first about celibacy: it is well known that the Romanists
customarily designate all ecclesiastics, as distinguished from laypeople and
secular persons, by the name “clergy,” although this is contrary to the style
of Scripture, which calls the entire church the *klēros*, or inheritance, of
the Lord (1 Pet. 5:3). They impose this law upon ecclesiastics: that they
perpetually abstain from the use of marriage and live in celibacy. Their aim is
partly to commend them to the people under a greater appearance of holiness
and procure greater authority for them, and partly to subject them more closely
to the pontiff. It was as though permitting marriage to bishops were nothing
other than removing the hierarchy from the church and reducing the pontiff to
the episcopate of Rome alone, as quite a few spoke at the Council of Trent:
“For if the marriage of presbyters were permitted, it would come about that
they would all direct their affection and love toward wife and children, and
thus toward family and country. Then that close union of the clerical order
with the apostolic see would cease, etc.,” as the *History of the Council* has
it in book 6. Elsewhere the Cardinal of Carpi reasons in this way: if marriage
were permitted, it would follow “that each man, in supporting family, wives,
and children and looking to obedience to his own prince, would refuse
submission to the pontiff, so that within a short time the apostolic see would
not extend beyond the bounds of the city of Rome, etc.” Here Satan’s marvelous
deceit is exposed. Under the religious pretext of chastity and perfection, he
defiled on every side with impurity the Lord’s Levites, whom holiness ought to
have distinguished above others. And the remedy which God had supplied to all
others against the provocations of the flesh he took away by law from the
clergy alone. Thus, those who are ready in other matters to relax requirements
by dispensation are implacable in this one matter, lest they permit marriage
to priests.

III. Since the Romanists do not agree among themselves concerning this
subject, we must see what opinion is received among them. They all indeed agree
that marriage has been forbidden to the major orders and that a law of
perpetual celibacy has been established by canon 9 of session 24 of the Council
of Trent. But they do not all understand the prohibition in the same way,
either with respect to its cause or with respect to its scope. Some wish the
cause to be natural, as if the nature and use of marriage in and of themselves
conflicted with the functions of holy orders and the priesthood. Such are
those who, in order to defend the justice of the prohibition, so extravagantly
exaggerate the uncleanness and filth of marriage, regard being in the flesh and
being married as the same thing, and deny that married people can please God,
as Pope Siricius once said. Thus they judge it a far more serious sin for a
priest to have relations with his wife than to fornicate or commit adultery.
This is Coster’s most impure proposition: “If a priest fornicates or keeps a
concubine at home, although he binds himself by grave sacrilege, he nevertheless
sins more gravely if he contracts marriage.” Hosius wishes this opinion, which
is also Pighius’s, to please

[1847 vol. III printed p. 210 | IA leaf 244 | PDF p. 244]

all Catholics. Their practice sufficiently demonstrates this, since no cleric
is deposed for fornication, as the Gloss on the canon *Presbyter*, distinction
82, notes, but all are deprived of office for marriage. In this way not only
the use of marriage but marriage itself will by its very nature conflict with
the priesthood. Others, to cover this horrible doctrine with some veneer, wish
the conflict between marriage and the priesthood to be only accidental. It is
derived either from the vow of continence which, as some maintain, they wish
to be annexed to holy orders, or from the church’s prohibition, as others say.
Some in turn wish this prohibition to be a matter of divine law, such as John
Major in distinction 24 of book 4 of the *Sentences*, question 2, and Francis
Torres in *On Synods*. Others regard it as a matter of human, positive
ecclesiastical law, such as Espence, Gregory of Valencia, and Bellarmine, who
cite Thomas, Durandus, Cajetan, and others on their behalf. Again, they do not
agree among themselves about the scope of the prohibition. Some absolutely
and categorically deny that any exception can be admitted by which marriage
might be permitted to priests, and deny that any ecclesiastical authority—not
even the pope—can do anything in this matter. Such are Coster, Medina, and
others. But some contend that, since it is a matter of positive law, it can be
dispensed with, though it is nevertheless not expedient to relax it. This is
Bellarmine’s opinion in chapter 9: “We must hold that the vow of continence is
annexed to holy orders, so that after ordination it is lawful neither to marry
wives nor to have relations with wives previously married. This is indeed by
positive law, but it is most ancient and in no way expedient to relax at the
present time.”

IV. Our opinion is that marriage is not some moral good or evil which in and
of itself bears some weight toward piety or impiety, but is among the
indifferent things which a person can use or not use as he judges expedient for
himself. It was instituted by God himself at the beginning, even before sin,
so that in it man might have a useful aid in life, and after sin so that it
might be a powerful remedy for lust and the intemperance of the flesh. Hence
it should neither be imposed nor prohibited by any ecclesiastical law, but
should be left to each person’s free will. Anyone, after privately weighing his
reasons, can either use it or abstain from it according as he perceives himself
to be inwardly disposed. He can abstain if he feels himself endowed with the
gift of continence, so that he might thereby be more unencumbered in
discharging some duty, whether ecclesiastical or political. But he not only
can but also must use it if he discovers that he lacks that gift, according to
the apostle’s prescription, which commands all who cannot contain themselves
to marry. This freedom to contract marriage—and indeed this necessity and
law—is such that no mortal can or ought to hinder it. Thus the law of celibacy
has been imposed upon sacred ministers impiously and contrary to God’s order
and Christian liberty.

V. From this the state of the question readily emerges. (1) The question is
not whether all sacred ministers ought to be married, nor whether celibacy is
lawful and advantageous for those endowed with the gift of continence. None of
us asserts the former or denies the latter. Rather, the question is whether a
law of celibacy should be imposed without distinction on everyone who performs
sacred functions, so that it is altogether unlawful for them to contract
marriage. The adversaries affirm this; we deny it. (2) The question is not
whether celibacy, with respect to its end and usefulness, is more advantageous
and better in a particular situation and time, which we do not deny with the
apostle (1 Cor. 7), but whether it is superior by its own nature and intrinsic
character. This we deny. (3) The question is not whether Christians may
profess celibacy by solemn vow, which pertains to another question about vows
and monasticism, but whether the church can establish a law of celibacy for
clergy constituted in holy orders. The adversaries maintain this; we deny it.
(4) The question

[1847 vol. III printed p. 211 | IA leaf 245 | PDF p. 245]

is not simply whether those already initiated into holy orders ought to be
forbidden to contract marriage, though not before they are ordained. This is
the opinion of the eastern church and the Greeks, who distinguish a marriage
already contracted from one yet to be contracted. They permit those who aspire
to holy orders to contract marriage before receiving orders and to use it even
after receiving them, but deny that it is lawful for them to contract marriage
afterward. We do not think a marriage yet to be contracted should be
distinguished from one already contracted, because we judge that for the same
reasons both should either be permitted or prohibited. The question therefore
comes down to this: should a law of perpetual celibacy be imposed on sacred
ministers? Or should marriage and its use after receiving the ministry be left
free to them? The adversaries affirm the former and deny the latter. We do the
opposite.

[Marginalia: 1. The first argument for freedom to marry, from the honorableness
of marriage. Heb. 13:4.]

VI. The arguments are as follows. (1) From the honorableness of marriage,
because marriage is honorable among all (Heb. 13:4): “Marriage is honorable
*among all*, and the bed undefiled.” Whatever is honorable among all cannot
and ought not to be prohibited to a particular class of people; and what is
undefiled before God cannot and ought not to be forbidden as unclean. By what
right would that which the Holy Spirit declares honorable among all be
disgraceful for ministers of the church alone? It would be as if, on their own
opinion, a sacrament which sanctifies others polluted the priest who
administers the sacrament. Nor can it be said, first, that “*among all* refers
to things, not persons, and is therefore neuter rather than masculine.” Where
the law does not distinguish, it is not for us to distinguish. Next, if all
things pertaining to marriage are honorable, how can the state of marriage be
regarded as impure in some people? Third, the apostle’s aim shows that he is
speaking about persons, not only things, because he contrasts those who pursue
chaste marriage with fornicators. He testifies that God will judge the latter
all the more severely because he instituted for them a remedy against the
desire of the flesh which is honorable in all, whatever their condition.
Fourth, several of the adversaries refer the words to persons. The Louvain,
Benedictine, and Antwerp Bibles retain “among all,” as do Pagninus, Salmeron,
Cajetan, Estius, Bellarmine, and others. Paphnutius understood it this way at
the Council of Nicaea, using this passage against clerical celibacy: “Do not,”
he said, “place a heavy yoke upon the necks of those initiated into holy
orders, for marriage is honorable among all,” according to Socrates, book 1,
chapter 8. Second, it is said that “the apostle’s statement is imperative, not
assertive, so that the sense is: honor marriage, because God will judge
fornicators.” If so, Paul should have said “let it be honorable,” not simply
“marriage is honorable,” which proves that this is a mere assertion and not a
command. The following words do not have the causal “for,” as if the reason for
the command were being given, namely, because God will judge fornicators.
Rather, they have an adversative “but,” so as to contrast fornicators with
married people and testify that the honorableness of the latter is pleasing to
God while the uncleanness of the former is hateful to God and therefore to be
severely punished. The Syriac, Chrysostom, Oecumenius, Theophylact, Jerome on
Matthew 13, Augustine in *Against Pelagius and Cœlestius*, book 2, chapter 34,
Anselm on Hebrews, and others explain it assertively. Third, it is said that
“it cannot be understood universally of all persons, but only of those
lawfully joined, for otherwise marriage between father and daughter or brother
and sister would be honorable.” Marriage is honorable among all who are joined
according to God’s order. But the union of such relatives cannot be called
marriage, but incest, which God most severely prohibited as unlawful. This
cannot be said of the marriage of sacred ministers, who are nowhere excepted
from the divinely instituted order and therefore can lawfully be joined. Add
that fornication and adultery are contrasted here with marriage. Therefore,
just as the former are forbidden to all, so the latter is understood to be
commanded to all who need it.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 212 | IA leaf 246 | PDF p. 246]

[Marginalia: 2. From the apostle’s command.]

VII. 2. From the apostle’s command, which commands marriage to each and every
person who cannot contain himself (1 Cor. 7:2): “Because of fornication, let
each man have his own wife, and let each woman have her own husband.” And in
verse 9: “But if they do not contain themselves, let them contract marriage,
for it is better to marry than to burn.” Here Paul says that marriage is not
only lawful and permitted but also commanded to all who cannot contain
themselves, of whatever order and condition. If ever a distinction of persons
should have been employed, this was the appropriate place. The apostle does
not make it, but instead places the universal term “each” before the command.
He adds no exception at all except the gift of continence, no limitation for
episcopate, presbyterate, or diaconate, and no distinction either of the time
before or after priesthood or of the condition of virgins or widowed persons.
Next, the reason given is not particular but indefinite and general, namely,
fornication. Its danger threatens clergy no less than others and must therefore
be diligently guarded against, all the more because the sin is far more serious
in them. It is vainly objected that “he is speaking about married people.” Paul
says that he is speaking to the unmarried and widows and commands them to
contract marriage—“let them marry.” This sufficiently shows that it ought to
be extended further. The expression “let him have his own wife” in verse 2
ought not to be restricted to married people, but referred also to those who
are to be joined, as Lyra observes, who understands it to speak about
contracting marriage. It is no better to say that those bound by a vow of
continence are excepted, because the question is whether such a vow can
lawfully be made and such a law imposed. Paul expressed no condition except
where the gift exists. I acknowledge that vows must be fulfilled, but lawful
and just ones, not those which conflict with one’s calling, God, and
conscience.

[Marginalia: 3. From the prohibition of marriage placed among doctrines of
demons. 1 Tim. 4:1–3.]

VIII. 3. From the prohibition of marriage, which the apostle places among the
marks of the apostasy of the latter times and calls a doctrine of demons (1
Tim. 4:1–3): “The Spirit expressly says that in the latter times some will
depart from the faith, giving heed to deceiving spirits and doctrines of
demons, through the hypocrisy of liars whose conscience has been seared with a
hot iron, forbidding people to marry and commanding abstinence from foods,
etc.” Paul’s aim is to show Timothy with how much zeal he must conduct himself
in the house of God, which is the pillar and foundation of the truth, so as to
discharge his office rightly. This was not only because of the excellence of
the mystery of godliness and the sublimity of the gospel, about which he had
spoken at the end of the preceding chapter, but also because of the danger
threatening the church from seducers. Especially in the latter times they would
not only enter into apostasy and depart from the faith—that is, from the true
doctrine of the mystery of godliness—but also become entangled in various most
serious errors, giving heed to deceiving spirits and receiving their errors as
though they were divine and heavenly doctrines, when in fact they are impious
and false doctrines issuing from Satan’s workshop and proceeding from the
devil, the father of lies. Paul marks the manner of this seduction by the
“hypocrisy of liars,” that is, by fraud and falsehood covered under a
specious zeal for piety and holiness. He notes its two chief characteristics:
“the prohibition of marriage and the interdiction of foods,” because celibacy
and abstinence are the principal colors with which seducers customarily paint
their hypocrisy and impiety, not only by following both laws themselves but
also by imposing them on others. The apostle intimates this when he adds that
such liars “forbid marriage and abstain from foods.” Here the expression is
incomplete, with the contrary verb omitted. He does not mean that such
seducers forbid abstinence from foods just as they forbid marriage, which some
Romanists would absurdly wish, against the agreement of all interpreters both
ancient and modern. On the contrary, he asserts that they will prohibit
marriage

[1847 vol. III printed p. 213 | IA leaf 247 | PDF p. 247]

and the use of foods. Thus “to forbid” will either signify contrary actions
according to the nature of the matter—prohibiting with respect to marriage and
commanding with respect to abstinence—just as the Hebrew word which means “to
command” sometimes denotes forbidding (Deut. 2:37); or the contrary term is
elliptical, so that “those doing this” must be supplied, as in the Syriac, or
“those commanding,” as in Epiphanius: “forbidding marriage and commanding
abstinence from foods.” Various ellipses of this kind occur in both sacred and
profane writers, where two actions which are contrary or at least different
are understood under the same verb. Estius observes this on the present
passage from Psalm 121:6: “The sun will not burn you by day, nor the moon by
night.” It is not the nature of the moon to burn, but to chill. And James
1:9–10: “Let the lowly brother glory in his exaltation, and the rich in his
humiliation”—supply “let him be brought low,” bearing a humble mind and
considering the instability of his lot. A similar manner of speaking occurs in
1 Timothy 2:12 and 1 Corinthians 14:34, and not infrequently among profane
writers. Phaedrus says, “I do not forbid him to be released, but to be tortured
by hunger”—supply “I command.” See Eustathius on Iliad 8 at verse 506.

IX. It is objected in vain that Paul “marks those who absolutely prohibited
marriages as evil, such as the Tatiani, Encratites, Marcionites, and
Manichaeans.” (1) Accusing them cannot excuse the Romanists unless they can
prove that they do not prohibit marriage and the use of foods, which the
apostle condemns here. (2) This was the old defense of the Montanists: that
this prophecy should not be applied to them, because they did not condemn
marriages as evil but only judged that people should abstain from them under an
appearance of greater holiness; instead, it should be applied to Tatian, the
father of the Encratites, and Marcion, as Tertullian observes in chapter 25 of
*On Fasting*. Yet the ancient church condemned Montanus and the Montanists and
applied this prophecy of Paul to them, as is clear in the preface and chapters
4 and 10 of the Council of Gangra. Their case is almost the same as, and common
with, that of the Romanists. Likewise, the Manichaeans did not absolutely
condemn marriages, but prohibited them to their elect, as Augustine records in
book 2, chapter 17 of *On the Morals of the Manichaeans*. (3) This oracle has
the Romanists no less—and indeed more—in view than the ancient heretics. First,
the apostle speaks of an apostasy “of the latter times.” This designates not so
much the times of the primitive church which immediately followed the age of
the apostles as the latest times which would run toward the end of the ages,
as the Vulgate translates it. Second, Paul speaks about those who would have
authority to prohibit marriage and the use of foods. This applies less to
those heretics, who could teach but could not command or compel, than to the
Roman pontiffs, who arrogate this right to themselves. Third, he is not
speaking about those who absolutely condemned marriages, but those who in
hypocrisy—that is, under the false appearance of a certain holiness—would
forbid marriage to certain persons and impose abstinence from certain foods.
Nor was it necessary for the Spirit of God so earnestly to forewarn believers
against those blasphemous ancient heresies, whose manifest impiety would at
first sight appear hateful to almost everyone. A greater danger was to be
feared from Antichrist, who would work the mystery of iniquity under the
pretext of piety.

[Marginalia: 4. From apostolic practice.]

X. 4. From apostolic practice and the liberty granted to the apostles. The
apostles and disciples of Christ either were married or could have been.
Peter, James, and John certainly were married, from Matthew 8:14 and 1
Corinthians 9:5, as was Philip the evangelist (Acts 21:8–9). The rest had free
power to marry without prejudice to their apostolic ministry (1 Cor. 9:5):
“Do we not have the right to take along with us a wife, a sister, as do the
other apostles, the brothers of the Lord, and Cephas?” It cannot be said that
“the women who followed the apostles were not their own wives, but wealthy
women by whom they were supported after Christ’s example” (Luke 8:

[1847 vol. III printed p. 214 | IA leaf 248 | PDF p. 248]

1–2). (1) First, the word *gynē* used here commonly means “wife.” Clement in
book 3 of the *Stromata*, Eusebius, Nicephorus, Tertullian in chapter 8 of his
*Exhortation to His Wife*, Valla, Faber Stapulensis, and quite a few others
translate it this way. Reason recommends it, because Paul speaks about the
right to wives whom their husbands took with them in the manner of travelers,
not about the liberty of other women to follow the apostles. (2) The gloss
conflicts with the apostle’s aim. He testifies that he did not take a wife with
him in order not to burden the church. But if the apostles had taken these
pious women with them, the church certainly would not have been burdened but
relieved by them. (3) Paul does not speak about several women but one, who can
be none other than a wife. Otherwise, propriety did not permit them to take
such women along, whom even the Synod of Nicaea prohibited them from keeping
in their household. It is vainly added that “the apostles dismissed their
wives after their calling because they are said to have left everything to
follow Christ” (Matt. 19:27). This is invented without proof and is denied as
easily as it is affirmed. Indeed, the apostles could not have done this without
conflicting with the original law of marriage and the rule established by Paul
(1 Cor. 7:10). The abandonment of which the apostles speak in Matthew 19:27 is
not the abandonment of absolutely everything required for preserving life,
for then they would have injured themselves. It is only of the things which
hindered either their ordinary and ready ministry or their free confession of
Christ and his gospel. Lawful wives cannot be said to be of this kind, since
they could afford consolation and confirmation of faith.

[Marginalia: 5. From freedom to marry. 1 Tim. 3:2.]

XI. 5. From the freedom to marry left to ecclesiastics (1 Tim. 3:2, 4). The
apostle wills that a bishop, who must be blameless, be “the husband of one
wife.” He could not have said this if he had believed episcopacy and celibacy
to be incompatible, and if he had not acknowledged marriage to be free and
lawful for a bishop, provided that he is the husband of only one wife and not
several. We do not use this passage to prove that a bishop must necessarily be
married, which is slanderously objected against us, but to show that a bishop
can be married, provided that he is the husband of one wife. Nor can this be
“explained allegorically of the bishop of one church,” as some would wish. The
things the apostle adds conflict with this, since he mentions children and
wishes it to be judged from the governance of a family whether a man is fit to
govern the church. Nor does it speak of “one who was the husband of one wife
before ordination, not one who now is.” A verb of the present, not the past,
tense is used: “a bishop must be,” not “must have been”; Titus 1:6 says, “if
anyone is blameless”; and concerning deacons, 1 Timothy 3:12 says, “let them be
husbands of one wife.” “Husbands of one wife” must be understood in the same
way as the other attributes predicated of them—sobriety, temperance, and a
blameless life—which speak about the present, not the past, so that a man not
only was adorned with them before, but is adorned with them continually. Nor
would it help to commemorate the virtues of their wives if the wives were
either dead or separated from their husbands. Finally, they wrongly maintain
that “this ordinance of the apostle was temporary and had force only in the
primitive church because the scarcity of celibates compelled the choice of
married men, but that the circumstances of our times are different.” If in
those times, which were the times of the church’s virginity, marriage did not
conflict with episcopacy, why would it conflict today? The law established by
the apostle is not for one time but all times.

XII. Furthermore, the monogamy commanded here does not exclude digamy, or
successive polygamy. If it is lawful for a wife after her husband’s death to
marry another, provided it is in the Lord (Rom. 7:2–3; 1 Cor. 7:39), the same
must also be lawful for a man. Thus second marriages in this sense cannot be
blamed, however hostile quite a few of the ancients were to them. Rather, it
excludes simultaneous polygamy, of which there were two

[1847 vol. III printed p. 215 | IA leaf 249 | PDF p. 249]

forms. One was open and manifest, when a man supported two or more wives at
the same time, as occurred under the Old Testament. The other was more hidden,
when someone who had repudiated his wife without just cause introduced another
while the first was still alive. Since this was then customary among the Jews
and familiar to the Romans and other Gentiles, from whom various polygamists
came to the faith, it was a just and suitable reason for the apostle to
disapprove of it in one who was to be promoted to the sacred ministry. Even
though it was in some measure tolerated in others because, having already been
done, it could not be corrected despite being vicious, in a bishop it was
nevertheless intolerable and altogether forbidden.

[Marginalia: 6. From the original institution of marriage.]

XIII. 6. From the first institution of marriage. The right of marriage was
given to all without exception, both by divine law (Gen. 1:28) and natural
law. This is clear from the desire implanted in everyone to have a helper
suitable for himself, to see children, and to propagate the race in them. No
law can be given which can abrogate this right so solemnly granted. Just as no
one can hinder the natural inclination for preservation by food and drink, so
no one can be bound not to marry a wife, especially when necessity presses
marriage as a remedy for incontinence. This is all the more so because Christ
repeated, confirmed, and vindicated this original institution which was common
to all (Matt. 19:4–5; 5:27), and the apostles prescribed rules of living for
husbands and wives among Christians without distinguishing the political and
ecclesiastical orders (Eph. 5–6; 1 Pet. 3). Although the command concerning
marriage is affirmative, and therefore does not bind always and at every time
but in a case of necessity, it does not follow that it can be abrogated with
respect to some and a contrary law imposed on them. This is especially so when
necessity presses, not only for the good of propagation so that the human race
does not perish, but also to avoid fornication in those not endowed with the
gift of continence. We are nevertheless not those who think everyone must
necessarily use this right of nature, but those who contend that no one should
be barred from it. For natural right is twofold: one kind which everyone must
use, and another from which no one must be prohibited. Marriage belongs to the
latter kind.

[Marginalia: 7. From the evils accompanying celibacy.]

XIV. 7. From the evils which follow celibacy. This law was the cause of
enormous evils which filled the world with impurities and the church with
scandals. Many testify to this, not even excepting the Roman pontiffs
themselves. Pius II, previously Aeneas Sylvius, said according to Platina in
his life, “Marriage was taken away from priests for a great reason; it should
be restored for a greater.” Nor did Paul II according to Bale, Salvian,
Ulrich of Augsburg, Gratian, Bernard, Polydore Vergil, Agrippa, and others hide
this. The bishop of Pécs confessed that petty priests contaminated by lusts
and the filth of life grew old in the height of impiety. Hence complaints
about this corruption were everywhere made and frequent wishes expressed for
the relaxation and abrogation of this law. Cassander in his *Consultation* is
wholly occupied with urging that the law of celibacy be abrogated because it
is an occasion of wandering and unbridled lust. Sigismund at the Council of
Basel asked that marriages be restored to the clergy. At the Council of Trent,
the bishop of Pécs in the name of both the emperor and the Duke of Bavaria, the
French envoys, and the fathers from Lorraine petitioned for the relaxation of
this law.

[Marginalia: 8. From the difficulty of continence. Matt. 19:11; 2 Cor. 7:7.]

XV. 8. From the difficulty of continence, which is a singular gift granted to
few (Matt. 19:11): “Not all receive this saying, but those to whom it is
given.” And 1 Corinthians 7:7: “Each has his own gift from God, one in this way
and another in that.” Therefore celibacy ought not to have been imposed
indiscriminately upon all, since continence does not rest in a person’s will
but depends upon God, although a person must not neglect the means of obtaining
it. Bellarmine objects in vain:

[1847 vol. III printed p. 216 | IA leaf 250 | PDF p. 250]

“Just as the gift of continence does not belong to all, so not all ought to be
priests, but only those who appear to have this gift.” He assumes without proof
that only those endowed with the gift of continence should be chosen as
priests, when the apostle states that one who is the husband of one wife can
also be chosen. Next, appearing to have this gift is one thing, actually having
it another. Many appear to themselves and others to have this gift, but when
they come to exercise it, they acknowledge that they do not have it. Nor can
they promise it to themselves for the future, although they will and desire it
and even seek it from God in prayer. For prayers rest upon faith, and faith
upon a promise, but no promise was given to a particular class of people that
they would obtain this gift perpetually.

[Marginalia: 9. From the practice of the ancient church.]

XVI. 9. From the practice of the ancient church. The law of celibacy was not
received in the church for a long time. The Canons of the Apostles published
by Clement of Rome have the following in canon 6: “Let no bishop or presbyter
dismiss his own wife under the pretext of religion. If he has dismissed her,
let him be excommunicated; if he persists, let him be deposed.” This should not
be referred only to care for her food and clothing but also to the marriage
bed, because the apostle teaches that a lawful wife is treated with contempt
if the intercourse due to her is denied (1 Cor. 7:3–4). Hence he “does not wish
them to defraud one another.” The words of the canon themselves teach this,
because they speak about one who deserts his wife under the pretext of
religion. What pretext of religion could there be for depriving her of all
support? It is manifest, however, that separation from the marriage bed was
introduced by many under this very pretext. Thus the words of the canon must
be understood of the marriage bed. Pope Nicholas confirms this when speaking
about separation from the marriage bed in Gratian, Cause 30, question 2,
chapter 3: “Under no pretext of religion ought one spouse to dismiss the
other.” Zonaras does so as well in his comments on the words of the canon. The
same applies to canon 4 of the Synod of Gangra: “If anyone should maintain that
it is not right to participate in the offering when a presbyter who married a
wife celebrates the sacred rites, let him be anathema.” The canon’s aim is to
affirm that it is lawful for a married presbyter to perform the liturgy. It is
not speaking only about one who once had a wife but one who is still married,
as Gratian recites it in the chapter *Si quis* of distinction 28 and Balsamon
in his commentary. Eustathius, against whom that Council of Gangra was
instituted, wished marriage to be absolutely forbidden to presbyters, as
Nicephorus notes in book 9, chapter 45. But the decree of the Council of Nicaea
must especially be observed, which provided that there be no canon restricting
the use of marriage, but that it be left to each person’s freedom. When some
were attempting to establish a law of celibacy, Paphnutius, bishop of Thebes,
earnestly dissuaded them, although he himself was celibate, and cried out that
“a heavy yoke must not be imposed upon sacred men, for marriage is honorable
and nuptials undefiled.” The fathers of that council adopted his opinion and
unanimously rejected celibacy, according to Gelasius of Cyzicus in the *Acts of
that Council*, Socrates, book 1, chapter 12, Sozomen, book 1, chapter 22,
Gratian, distinction 31, and quite a few others. Canon 3 of the fifth Council
of Constantinople and canon 13 of the sixth ecumenical Council in Trullo also
pertain here. It is also well known that in the ancient church very many
sacred ministers were married: Clement of Rome, Polycarp, Polycrates, Irenaeus,
Spyridon, Gregory the father of Gregory Nazianzen, Gregory of Nyssa the brother
of Basil, Tertullian, Hilary of Poitiers, and innumerable others. Their
examples are related and vindicated by Chamier in book 16, chapter 13, Gerhard
on page 564, and Du Moulin in book 7, chapter 8 of *The Novelty of Popery*.

XVII. Whatever kind of life is better, holier, and happier properly and in
itself, and is particularly commanded to sacred ministers, must certainly be
pursued by them. But we deny that celibacy is such. This cannot be shown from
1 Corinthians 7, where the apostle indeed compares celibacy

[1847 vol. III printed p. 217 | IA leaf 251 | PDF p. 251]

with marriage and prefers the former to the latter, but not properly and in
itself. On the contrary, he expressly asserts that both are outward states of
life which do not commend us to God or render us acceptable to him (verse 18
and following), but one is preferable in a qualified sense and with respect to
a particular time. Celibacy is thus called better than marriage because it is
subject to fewer and lighter cares and troubles (verse 28) and because those
times were hostile with persecutions (verse 26), which are more easily endured
by the celibate than the married. That Paul’s expression “the good” is not to
be understood of moral and honorable goodness is taught by the institution of
marriage itself, which had a blessing joined to it, and by what God himself
says in Genesis 2:18: “It is not good for man to be alone.” Rather, it speaks
of useful goodness, as the apostle himself explains in verse 35 by
“advantageous,” meaning “convenient and beneficial,” and by “because of the
present necessity of the cross,” at whose approach it could be said, “Blessed
are the barren and the wombs which did not bear” (Luke 23:29). Finally, neither
perpetual nor temporary celibacy is commanded, but only advised. Paul denies
that he received a command of the Lord concerning it (verses 6, 25). He
testifies that he wishes to ensnare no one’s conscience with the opinion that
it is necessary or an act of worship (verse 35). He advises it not to all but
only to those endowed with the gift of continence (verses 7, 17), but commands
marriage to others (verses 2, 9). Thus Paul compares the advantages and
disadvantages of both states, not to cast a snare but to explain what is more
advisable. There are rocks and distractions on either side, but the most
dangerous comes from the provocations of lust—a burden all the heavier the
more inwardly and tenaciously it is fixed in one’s very being.

XVIII. The apostle’s requirement that a bishop be sober-minded and
self-controlled (1 Tim. 3:2; Titus 1:8) does not favor celibacy. “Sober-minded”
is contrasted either with one who is senseless and raving, in which case it
signifies one “in possession of his mind,” or with one who is dissolute, in
which case it signifies one who is “temperate” and observes moderation in
food, drink, and sexual matters. A person can be such in marriage just as much
as in celibacy. “Self-controlled” does not mean one who abstains from marital
intercourse—otherwise how could Paul command a bishop who is the husband of one
wife and has children to be self-controlled?—but one who guards against
unlawful desires and restrains himself. No pious person can number the lawful
use of marriage among such desires.

XIX. Being entangled in “the business and cares of this age”—that is,
immoderate and excessive ones which call a person away from piety, holiness,
and the discharge of his office—is one thing; being bound in marriage is
another. Paul prohibits the former to a bishop in 2 Timothy 2:4, but not the
latter. This is clear even from the fact that in the first epistle he commands
a bishop to care for his own household. Thus it is evident that he marks
business which conflicts with this sacred service, not business subordinated
to it.

XX. Although the church has power to make laws, it was not therefore lawful
for it to impose a law of celibacy upon sacred ministers. Christ sufficiently
and clearly intimates that such a law cannot and ought not to be made when he
commands in Matthew 19:12 that each person be permitted to determine what he
can receive. Neither Christ nor the apostles ever established this law. And
Paul in 1 Corinthians 7:37 wishes the “necessity” which the law of celibacy
imposes to be absent from a resolution to preserve virginity. The arguments
given above also pertain to this.

XXI. When the apostle says that spouses by consent can deprive each other for
a time so that they may devote themselves to prayer and fasting (1 Cor. 7:5),
he does not wish to impose a law of celibacy upon anyone. He teaches that there
is a time when it is advisable to abstain even from the lawful use of marriage
so that we can be better disposed for divine worship. He therefore speaks not
about just any prayers, but solemn ones, which were often appointed outside
the ordinary course together with fasting, whether because of some notable
public or domestic calamity, the choosing of ministers, or

[1847 vol. III printed p. 218 | IA leaf 252 | PDF p. 252]

some other necessity. In that situation he advises mutual abstinence, not
because marriage in itself hinders prayers, but so that after putting aside
all business and even lawful pleasures, they might devote themselves wholly to
the pursuit of piety and fervency of prayer. But what does this have to do with
a law of celibacy, since it concerns abstinence not in perpetuity but only for
a few days, not ordinary but extraordinary prayers, and is spoken not only to
clergy but also to laypeople, who are commanded to come together again lest
Satan tempt them? It is vainly added that a cleric must always abstain because
he is always bound to devote himself to divine worship. Although he is bound
always to devote himself to divine things—that is, never to be occupied with
things opposed to them, such as idolatry, and always to be directed toward
them as a settled disposition—he is not always bound to be actually occupied
with divine things so that he is never unoccupied with them. Otherwise all
duties which concern the things of this life would be forbidden to him. Divine
things must thus be cared for first and chiefly, without neglecting human
things, which have their own intervals and turns in which they must be cared
for.

XXII. The “subintroduced woman” prohibited to ministers by canon 3 of the
Council of Nicaea is not a true wife. (1) The contrary was decreed on the
advice of Paphnutius, and the use of a lawful wife was not condemned. (2) The
canon concerns a suspect woman. A wife therefore cannot be called
“subintroduced,” and Novel 123 distinguishes her from such a woman. (3) Such a
woman is prohibited “to all who are in the clergy,” while the Romanists hold
that the law of celibacy does not bind the minor orders. (4) This kind of
“subintroduced woman” was kept only by those who professed celibacy, not by vow
or law but of their own free will, as is clear from the first Council of
Carthage, chapter 3, Balsamon on Basil’s epistle, and Photius in the
*Nomocanon*, title 8, chapter 14. This is also why the canon does not except a
wife, but only mother, sister, and the rest. Thus concubinage, not marriage, is
forbidden here. Balsamon, Zonaras, and, among the adversaries, Clichtoveus,
Espence, Medina, and various others explain it this way.

XXIII. No council has power to make any law contrary to the Word of God and
natural right. Therefore, if anything concerning celibacy was decreed by some
councils, such as Neocaesarea, canon 1, Elvira, Arles II, and Carthage II and V,
this cannot make it lawful and just. Next, they object no councils except
Latin and western ones, and these not ecumenical but merely provincial, while
they omit the Greek councils, whether ecumenical or particular, as affording
them little support.

XXIV. Any uncleanness which marital intercourse brought under the Old
Testament was entirely legal and Levitical. It was abrogated when the
priesthood was transferred, no less than uncleanness from contact with a dead
body. Next, if those examples had any force, it would follow that the law of
this abstinence should extend only to the time when they are making immediate
preparation for public worship or actually engaged in it. For it was lawful
for the entire people and the priests of the Old Testament to be married,
although they were equally responsible to meditate on the law night and day.
Third, those courses, or turns of the Old Testament priests, were not
instituted because marital intercourse made the priests unworthy for the
liturgy. Those turns were instituted four hundred years after Moses, and the
high priest himself, whose duty it was to offer the continual sacrifice every
day, was married. Nor did any law exist which imposed celibacy upon priests or
commanded them to be absent from home during the time of their course. In
general, it must be observed that many things were imposed upon the Jews which
have no force among Christians. They had to abstain from pork, hare, and other
kinds of flesh, and many pollutions also had to be avoided, from which
Christians

[1847 vol. III printed p. 219 | IA leaf 253 | PDF p. 253]

have been freed as from a most grievous yoke. Thus, even if some such command
occurred, nothing could be inferred from it unless it were specifically taught
that this was continued in the gospel.

XXV. Hindering ecclesiastical duties in itself is one thing; hindering them
incidentally is another. Hindering them perpetually is one thing and hindering
them for a time another; hindering them by one’s nature is one thing and doing
so only through abuse another. The former are rightly prohibited, but not the
latter. Marriage does not hinder in itself or always, nor does it call a person
away from the duty altogether, but only to some extent. Quite a few
bishops were holy and yet married, and in the Greek church presbyters have
always been permitted to be married. Next, more evil arises from the
prohibition than from the nonperpetual distraction, because of the danger of
fornication. The danger of fornication, for whose sake the apostle made the
law, is close at hand, and the distraction will never be as great as that
danger. No disadvantage can be compared or equated with any sin, nor can any
be so great that it is not always better to marry than to burn.

XXVI. Those who “were not defiled with women” and are therefore called virgins
in Revelation 14:4 are not celibates who abstain from marriage. Marriage is
nowhere called pollution; on the contrary, it is called undefiled (Heb. 13:4).
It would also follow that only celibates, and all celibates, would then be
saved, which is absurd, since most of the patriarchs, prophets, and apostles
were married, and quite a few celibates under the papacy are fornicators.
Rather, pure worshipers of Christ are designated in this way: those who are
not polluted by idolatry, which is spiritual fornication, or by fellowship
with Antichristian churches, or by the pleasures and defilements of the world.
