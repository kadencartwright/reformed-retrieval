# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 23.* Christ rendered full obedience to the law for us and
satisfied this obligation; therefore we are freed from this obligation.

*Answer.* It is true that Christ satisfied God's law in every way for his own
and that we are justified by that righteousness of Christ imputed to us. It
rightly follows that we are freed from that obligation to this extent: we are
not bound to render obedience to the law in order thereby to be justified
before God, to be established in the state of the justified, or to satisfy
divine justice. For we ought to count all these things as refuse so that we
may gain Christ and be found in him, not having our own righteousness, which
is from the law, but that which is through faith in Christ, the righteousness
from God through faith (Philippians 3:8–9).

Nevertheless, when justification through faith without works of the law is
established, our sanctification is not taken away. Yet this is where the
Antinomians' reasoning leads. It follows from their doctrine that we ought to
shun all holiness, renewal of nature, and every good work no less than we
ought to shun all confidence in our works for acquiring righteousness before
God. For just as we are not permitted to seek righteousness in our works
because Christ has been made righteousness for us and we are constituted
righteous in him, so too we are not permitted to pursue holiness because
Christ has likewise been made sanctification for us. Thus, as they like to
say, he believed for us, mortified sin, repented, and performed all the
duties required of us in the law. It follows that we ought to shun every good
work, even one effected in us by the Holy Spirit. This is assuredly to open
the door to every kind of wantonness and profaneness.

Christ indeed kept God's law in all things for us and for our good and was
obedient unto death, so that in him we might be righteous before God, freed
from the curse of the law, and possess a right to adoption as sons and to
eternal life. He did not do so, however, that we might be exempt from the
law's obligation. For *the saving grace of God has shone upon all people,
instructing us so that, denying ungodliness and worldly desires, we may live
temperately, righteously, and godly in the present age* (Titus 2:11–12). And
*Christ gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity and
purify for himself a peculiar people, ardent in the pursuit of good works*
(verse 14). Christ gave himself up for the church *that he might sanctify it,
having purified it by the washing of water through the word, so that he might
also present it to himself as a glorious church, not having spot or wrinkle
or anything of that kind, but so that it might be holy and blameless*
(Ephesians 5:25–27).

Christ has indeed been made *sanctification* for us no less than
righteousness. This was not so that we might be exempt from the pursuit of
good works, as we are exempt from seeking within ourselves or our actions a
righteousness in which we may be found before God. Rather, it was because
*with his blood he redeemed us so that we might be holy; with his blood he
purges and washes us from all our stains and iniquities* (Zechariah 13:1; 1
John 1:7; Hebrews 9:14; 1 Peter 1:19; Revelation 1:5). By his blood and merit
he obtained the Holy Spirit, who sanctifies us, purges the heart, subdues
corruptions, puts our spiritual enemies to flight, creates in us a new heart
of flesh, arouses and moves us to good works, and effectually works those same
things

in us. Our old self was crucified with him so that the body of sin might be
abolished and we might no longer serve sin. We died with Christ and live
together with him. Hence, as the apostle teaches in Romans 6:11, we may infer
that *we are dead to sin and live to God.* Thus he is our sanctification
virtually and meritoriously, but not formally or materially. For from this
the apostle infers in Romans 6:12–13 that *we ought to devote ourselves to
holiness, so that sin may not reign in our mortal body and we obey it in the
body's desires, nor present our members to sin as weapons of unrighteousness.*

*Christ suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through
his own blood* (Hebrews 13:12). Hence the apostle infers there in verses 13,
15, and 16 that *we ought to go out to him outside the camp, bearing his
reproach, and through him continually offer to God the sacrifice of praise,
that is, the fruit of lips celebrating his name, and not forget doing good
and sharing*, and so forth. Through Christ our Savior *the Spirit of renewal
is poured out upon us* (Titus 3:5–6). *He was revealed in order to destroy the
devil's works* (1 John 3:8). Consequently, *the one who abides in him does not
sin; and the one who is born of God does not devote himself to sin, because
God's seed abides in him, nor can he sin, because he is born of God* (verses
6 and 9). Conversely, *the one who devotes himself to sin is from the devil;
and whoever does not devote himself to righteousness is not from God*
(verses 8 and 10).

The righteousness of our justification therefore inheres in Christ and is
imputed to us, whereas the righteousness of our sanctification properly
inheres in us and is produced in us by the work of the Holy Spirit. We must
carefully distinguish justification from sanctification. We must beware of
proclaiming the former in such a way that we utterly destroy the latter, and
of exalting the latter in such a way that we render the former utterly empty.
Holiness must be pursued, not so that we may make God favorable to us, but so
that we may perform the things pleasing to him; not so that we may satisfy
justice, but so that we may be holy before God and correspond in some measure
to the example set before us (1 Peter 2:21; 1 John 2:6; Philippians 2:5). We
do so not to earn anything, but to glorify God (John 15:8; Matthew 5:16), to
attest our gratitude, and to serve him in body and soul, since we belong to
him and he redeemed us (1 Corinthians 6:18–20).

The opponents' inference therefore conflicts with the whole doctrine of the
gospel and tends most certainly to the ruin of souls. Promises are made to
holiness (1 Timothy 4:8); without it there is no salvation (Hebrews 12:14;
Romans 8:13; Matthew 5:19). Our weapons are the Christian virtues (Ephesians
6:14, 16; Romans 13:12).
