VERSE 7. Though all a person's labor comes to his mouth, his desire is still
not satisfied.

VERSE 8. For what advantage does the wise person have over the fool? What is
there for the poor person to enjoy while walking before the living?

VERSE 9. You will say that the sight of the eyes is better than the wandering
of desire. This too is vanity and affliction of spirit.

VERSE 10. Whatever he may be, his name has already been declared, and it is
known that he is human and cannot contend with one stronger than himself.

VERSE 11. When goods are many, they multiply vanity; what more does the person
gain?

VERSE 12. For who knows what is good for a person in this life, during the
number of the days of his utterly vain life, which he passes like a shadow?
Who is there to tell a person what will happen after him under the Sun?

The summary is to set the vanity of riches further before our eyes, even in
those who use riches and receive fruit from them.

The parts are the vanity of riches arising from the weakness of riches
themselves, verses 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11, and from the weakness of the person who
possesses them.

VERSE 7. Though all a person's labor comes to his mouth, his desire is still
not satisfied.

Solomon censures the immoderate and insatiable desire for riches by
considering the end for which they are gathered. For there is no other end
for which people labor so intensely to acquire money except to sustain
themselves by it in this life. Here “food” is a synecdoche for the whole
maintenance of life, which turns upon these two things, food and clothing. The
Apostle confirms this in 1 Timothy 6: having food and coverings, he says, we
ought to be content with them. The Prophet therefore condemns people's
unbounded desire to heap up and turn over riches on this ground: for the sake
of so small and readily obtainable a thing, we wound ourselves so greatly and
wear ourselves out with anxious cares and untimely labors.

Christ gives clear and plain testimony to this in that notable sermon he
delivered when he came down from the mountain. Intending to set the axe to the
root of avarice, he attacks immoderate care and anxiety about food and
clothing, so that, once these fibers have been torn from the soul, it may be
plain that avarice has been strangled and crushed. In both food and bodily
care, nature is frugal and content with little. Yet our desire expands like
the grave or hell and extends its bounds, persuading itself that it can never
be satiated and filled. God made our mouth, into which we put food, extremely
narrow; but the desire to possess spreads so widely that it is as if our
mouth, like some chasm and abyss, were capable of swallowing the river Jordan
in one draught, or as if people had been given Leviathan's mouth, into which a
wagon is received together with the horses that draw it. The stomach is
scarcely a handbreadth in length and width, yet desire strains every sinew to
fill it as if it were larger than a cask. Although the stature of the back,
and indeed of the entire body, is bounded by a length of five feet, people
apply themselves hand and foot, as the saying goes, to covering it, as if
they had been given the monstrous bodies—not merely of Goliath and other
monsters—but bodies equal in size to that idol of Bel, which a tyrant erected
sixty cubits high and six cubits wide. Daniel 3.

Besides excessive grasping, another cause of this insatiable desire is the
lavish expenditure of goods upon luxury and excess. Thus people pursue not
merely satisfaction in food but Dionysian delicacies, and not merely decent
coverings in clothing but the utmost softness and ostentation. The result is
that one person devours enough to fill a hundred people and is wrapped in
clothes so soft and costly that they would be sufficient to clothe a thousand
people decently and healthfully. Hence an entire year's revenue is sometimes
spent on a feast or banquet; on a single outfit we spend not merely a year's
revenue but a good part of the inheritance—even, as the common expression
goes, the whole estate. Yet Christ makes a mockery of their folly when he
prefers the lilies' adornment and splendor of clothing to all this pomp and
elaborate refinement in dress. Matthew 6. No less could be said about certain
brute animals in their feeding: according to their nature and condition they
are fed as sumptuously and exquisitely as the greatest banqueters who gorge
themselves on every kind of delicacy.
