CHAPTER XXI.

Verse 1. Streams of waters are the heart of the King in the hand of the Lord; he bends it toward whatever matter he pleases.

In יד (*manu*, in the hand) there is a metaphor by which he indicates the power of God.

The purpose is to call Kings back to moderation, but to raise subjects—withdrawn on the one side from confidence in Princes and on the other from terror—to faith and invocation of the divine name.

Solomon’s design is to teach that it belongs to God alone to govern the minds of Kings and Princes according to his own judgment; to bend them in this direction or that; to kindle love toward these people or hatred toward those; either to confirm them in a resolution once undertaken or, when it has been changed, to bring it about that they pass to another. For this purpose he uses an argument from a comparison between equals: the heart of a prince is as ductile and flexible in the hand of the Lord as it is possible for people skilled in handling waters to drain immense bodies of water and divert them through pipes and channels. It is well known that by human art, through channels and aqueducts, the deepest waters are drained off here and there by division and diversion; that water’s nature is fluid and, once a passage has been opened, ready to follow; that it is borne headlong with great force not only in a steep and sloping place but also in level and open country; indeed, that by human art it is made to ascend, contrary to its nature, forced from the lowest parts of the earth to sloping and precipitous places for the convenience of irrigation. Sacred and profane history bears witness that the Euphrates, very broad and throughout the whole world

[1632 witness | PDF p. 456 | printed columns 896-897]

most celebrated, the river by which Babylonia was inaccessible and impregnable to peoples beyond the river as though by a wall, was drained and dried up by the Persians and Medes through diversions of its waters. How much more does God know how to drive a king’s heart, however broad and deep it may be, wherever he wishes? People indeed drain waters by great works and arduous labor; but God accomplishes this merely by a nod of his will. Moreover, people attain it only after a great interval of time, whereas God in a single moment, as in the blink of an eye, works a great change in a king’s mind.

And just as waters sometimes glide down of their own accord to lower places, while at other times, though nature resists and strives against it, they are driven by art and seek lofty and elevated places, so God sometimes changes the minds of Kings in such a way that of their own accord and eagerly they embrace a resolution different from their former one. This happened to Nebuchadnezzar in the deliverance of the three young men from death. At other times, however, even against their will and as though they had been put upon the rack, he wrests from them a resolution different from the former one. We experienced this in Pharaoh, who at first utterly opposed their departure from Egypt but was afterward compelled to let them go.

But if the king’s mind is in God’s hand, it follows that both the King’s tongue and his hand are likewise in God’s hand, so that he can neither utter any word nor perform any deed except insofar as power is granted him by God. Then, if the King’s thoughts, words, and deeds are in God’s keeping, how much more are those of others who are inferior to Kings.

We must now see what wholesome lessons and admonitions are drawn from this. First, then, Kings are taught and at the same time admonished not to lift up their horn—as commonly happens to them—and utter these proud words: “I shall do this and that,” without any qualification such as “if God wills,” and so forth. On this matter see Dan. 13.15 and John 19.10. Then God must be entreated by them to confirm them in upright counsels and drive them away from wicked ones.

Subjects are then admonished, first, to pray to God that he may suggest sound and wholesome counsels to the minds of Kings; next, not to seek the King’s favor and goodwill by flatteries and unlawful arts—much less the favor of others who serve Kings and are inferior to them—but to depend upon God. They should determine that the greatest shortcut for winning the King’s favor is to prove themselves and their services to God, who can bend the will of kings toward them. An excellent example of this appears at Neh. 1.2, where one who is about to make a petition to the King first prays to God. Likewise Est. 4.15–16, and so forth.

Therefore, even if a royal mind has hardened itself rather obstinately, we must not despair of its change for the better. For just as waters which rush forward with great force are nevertheless turned elsewhere by human art, so the King’s mind, however hardened, is easily moved by God’s hand. See an example of this in Esau, who pursued Jacob with undying hatred and had sworn his death, yet was suddenly changed and embraced him most lovingly. Likewise see it in the Egyptians, who, though they hated Israel worse than a dog and a snake, could deny them nothing when they asked.

Verse 2. Every person’s way is right in his own eyes; but the Lord is the one who weighs hearts.

On this verse see 16.2. Yet in this passage the statement seems to be illustrated somewhat by comparison and contrast with the following verse: namely, that God rejects not only those human works which people have devised from their own brain, but also those which he himself has commanded, if they are removed from love and justice toward one’s neighbor.

Verse 3. To do justice and judgment is more right before the Lord than sacrifice.

In צדקה (*justitiam*, justice) there appears to be a synecdoche for outward duty toward one’s neighbor. In זבח (*sacrificium*, sacrifice) there is a synecdoche of the part for ceremonies of every kind.

The purpose is to exhort people to the pursuit of justice and equity.

By the word *justice* he denotes the virtue by which each person renders to everyone what is his. By the term *judgment*, however, he appears to commend to us that justice which proceeds from no disturbance of mind—from love, fear, hatred, or even the prompting of others—but from sound judgment and love of justice. For a person who does what is just, yet does not do it knowingly and at the same time sincerely, will certainly not be approved by God. See Deut. 16.20.

Therefore, whether the duty of a private person or of a Magistrate toward one’s neighbor is under discussion, these two words are joined together; Gen. 18.19 and 2 Sam. 8.15. We must not, therefore, suppose that the word *justice* is to be applied to private persons and *judgment* to public persons, since each of these virtues is required equally in private and public persons.

He commends these virtues by comparison with a lesser thing, namely sacrifice, or ceremonial worship. Here the Prophet establishes a distinction between divine and human judgment. For people are generally so disposed that they place, as it were, the prow and stern of piety in the religious observance of ceremonies. God, by contrast, places duties of love toward one’s neighbor before this worship; elsewhere he places obedience before sacrifice, 1 Sam. 15; Mic. 6.

Here it is asked how Christ places the works of the first table after the duties which relate to the second. The answer may be sought from my larger catechism and indeed, unless I am mistaken, from the explanation of the sum of the first table.

See further what has been written on this argument in book 1 of *The Harmony of the Gospels*, pp. 197–198, much of which ought to be transcribed here.
