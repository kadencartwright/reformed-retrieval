II. “Now,” he says, “from eternity God has present not only the future being which contingents have
in their causes, but also the being of actual existence which they have outside their causes in each
difference of time; and in this way he can have certain knowledge of contingent futures.” Reader,
observe whether Penottus is not trying to transact everything by phrases rather than reasons. In the
immediately preceding sentence the distinction ran between a thing's future and present being. Here
he renovates it as one between a thing's future being and the being of actual existence, as though
future being were not itself the being of actual existence considered as future. Seeing how badly
this verbal device would fare, he attaches a long description—“which they have outside their causes
in each difference of time.” The entire force of the latter member's distinction from the former
rests on “have.” More fittingly it could read: from eternity God has present not only the being of
actual existence which they will have outside their causes in each difference of time, but also the
being of actual existence which they have there.

But this opinion has a pitiable outcome. The actual existence which a thing will possess in the
future is entirely the same as that which it possesses in the present, differing only in duration.
It follows that contingents' actual existence was not merely future to God from eternity but present
outside its causes from eternity—that is, produced from eternity. All contingents were therefore not
only known by God but actually coexisted with him from eternity. He could have said more briefly that
God has present from eternity both contingents' future and present being. But seeing the miserable
play on “having present the present being of things,” and the still more miserable confusion, he
varied the terms and supplied the latter member with “the being of actual existence which they have
outside their causes in each difference of time.” These are Penottus's scholastic achievements: if
he cannot satisfy the reader by true doctrine, he may do so by varied phrases—or dismiss unsatisfied
anyone refusing satisfaction of this kind.

The man of Novara continues in the same spirit: “The existence according to which things are called
present to God from eternity is their existence in the proper difference of present time,
distinguished from future time.” He could have said that the presence according to which they are
called present to God from eternity is their presence in present time. But he fled that form, though
the matter is identical, as Moses fled his staff changed into a serpent. I do not doubt that each
thing's existence in its own time is and was present to God from eternity. Why call it existence in
present time, unless, like Davus, he wished to confuse everything? Not all contingents exist in the
present; each has an appointed time in which it is, was, or will be present. Yet the existence of all
things joined in God's ordering with their appointed times

is and was present to God from eternity. The question is how it was present. Neither Dominicans nor
anyone else denies its presence. Penottus therefore makes no progress when he rests in what no one
disputes. The question is not whether each thing's existence according to the differences of time is
present to God, but how that existence was present from eternity.

To state the issue more usefully: no one denies that God knows from eternity each thing's existence
in its time. The question is whether he knew all things actually to have existed, or knew that they
would actually exist, some earlier and others later. The Dominicans say he knew them all actually to
have existed in eternity: all coexisted with God there from eternity and were so known, while he
also knew from eternity that they would exist in temporal order. We deny that contingents existed
in eternity from eternity, and deny that God knew this. We say they were all going to exist in time
and God knew this from eternity. Thus all were present to him in known and knowable being, insofar as
they were before God in willed being. This is intentional, not real, being. An adversary might think
truth wanting here, but cannot complain of wanting clarity and a lucid explanation of our mind.

Penottus refuses to rest in intentional presence and wants things really present to God before they
exist, yet rejects the Dominican fiction of coexistence in eternity. Since he will not introduce the
still more monstrous fiction that everything existed in time from eternity, his discussion collapses
into nothing: as a solution he posits what no one disputed. Note also his false assumption that
future things, while future, are wholly within their causes. They are not yet made; but since by
nature they are merely possible, could they pass from possibility to futurity without a cause? If
that transition has a cause—God's will and determination to produce them at a definite time—they
must thus far be outside their causes, for they are transferred from merely possible to future
things by divine determination. Hence from eternity they were before God in willed being and thereby
obtained knowable being within the class of futures.
