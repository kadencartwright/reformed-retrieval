# BOOK V

## ON JUSTIFYING FAITH

### Chapter I. Statement of the Case

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *The first question is what that faith is, or what is that faith,
which is required for justification.*

Protestant reply. This very thing is brought into question either ignorantly
or sophistically. For I. Many things are required for justification which do
not justify. II. The question is not so much which faith, or what faith,
justifies as what is the manner in which it is properly said to justify.

2. Bellarmine. *The Catholics dissent from the heretics in three respects. 1.
In the object of justifying faith, which the heretics restrict to the promise
of special mercy alone; the Catholics hold that it extends as widely as the
word of God extends. 2. In the faculty and power of the soul which is the seat
of faith. For they place faith in the will when they define it as trust; the
Catholics teach that faith has its seat in the intellect. 3. In the very act
of the intellect: for they define faith by knowledge, but the Catholics by
assent.*

Protestant reply. I. In the first disagreement it must be observed, first,
that we do not restrict the faith *which justifies* to the promise of mercy,
but only consider it *insofar as it justifies*; second, that we do not restrict
it to special
mercy as something antecedent, but to mercy which becomes special through
faith itself; third, that there are eminent men among the Pontificians who
agree with us. Thus Cardinal Contarini, in his treatise on justification:
*The promise of God, which the person being justified firmly believes, and
from which he therefore conceives trust, is (as St. Thomas says in 1. 2.) that
God remits sins and justifies him through the mystery of Christ.* II. In the
second disagreement it must also be observed, first, that we so place the
faith which justifies in the will that we teach that the antecedent assent of
the intellect is required for faith, and that there is a power and efficacy
of faith

[Printed p. 295 | Google Books PA295 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

consequent upon it in the intellect. For both of these reasons we commonly
describe faith as though it were partly in the intellect and partly in the
will. Second, Contarini teaches this same thing in the place cited, where
these words are found: *Faith begins from the will, which obeys God and the
Spirit, and the intellect assents without hesitation to what has been handed
down by God; it trusts in firm promises and from them conceives a firm trust
which pertains to the will, so that, as in a kind of circle, this faith begins
from the will and ends in the will.* Many of the older Scholastics teach the
same. *Alexander of Hales, part 3, question c, member 8, article 1: Faith (like
the other virtues) is in the part of the soul which is called motive, and
this is free choice. Bonaventure on 3, distinction 23: Great scholars have
taught that faith is in a certain manner in the cognitive power and in a
certain manner in the affective power. Faith, as it is a virtue and a
principle of merit, regards free choice as its subject. Durandus, in the same
place: The act of believing in God is the same as loving by believing.* Here
he indicates plainly enough (as many others do) that believing in God is an
act of the will. So Cajetan on John 6: *To believe in God is to trust by
believing and to tend toward God, which contains within itself an act of the
intellect and of the will.* III. In the third disagreement Bellarmine is
plainly ridiculous. For he attributes to us a plainly contradictory
position, as though we made faith only trust in the will and at the same time
only knowledge in the intellect. Those who describe faith in part by
knowledge mark its antecedent cause and consequent effect in order to
distinguish true faith from imaginary faith; they exclude neither trust nor
assent.

### Chapter II

#### Bellarmine's Arguments

Bellarmine. 1. *The first testimony can be taken from Hebrews 11:1. For it
cannot be denied that in this passage the apostle defines justifying faith.*

Protestant reply. Erasmus nevertheless denies this in his Annotations,
asserting that it is no more a definition than any encomium—for example, if
someone praising learning should say: wisdom is a consolation in adversity
and an ornament in prosperity. Alexander of Hales, part 3, question 68,
member 5, article 2, together with Hugh, formerly denied that the apostle here
*explains what faith is by genus and difference* (which belongs to a perfect
definition), *but only what it is.* Lombard also concedes in 3, distinction
23, that this description applies no less to hope than to faith. Finally, we
deny that these words contain a special definition of justifying faith as
such, because they do not designate the relation to Christ by which alone it
is said to justi-*

[Printed p. 296 | Google Books PA296 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*fy.

Bellarmine. 1. *The faith by which the just man lives is defined in chapter
10:38, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. The same faith is described, but not according to the
effect of justification.

Bellarmine. 2. *The apostle repeats more than once that this is the faith
without which it is impossible to please God, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. It is one and the same faith by which we please God unto
reconciliation and, once reconciled, are directed and sustained in pleasing
him in new obedience. Therefore this argument does not follow: faith is
described as that which causes us to please God; therefore it is also
described as that by which faith justifies. Let us now see what inferences
Bellarmine draws from these words.

2. Bellarmine. *Faith is called substance because it makes present those
things which otherwise are future. But representing is not an act of
the will, where trust resides, but of the intellect. For by apprehending a
thing through a species impressed upon it, the intellect makes the thing
exist before it exists.*

Protestant reply. I. This is a sophistical syllogism composed of four terms:
*making present* and *representing* are not one and the same. If this
interpretation were admitted, saving faith would no more make the future
goods which it apprehends present to us than any knowledge of past things
makes them present to the person contemplating them. *Thomas, 2. 2, question
4, article 1: The relation of the act of faith to the end, which is the object
of the will, is signified. Alexander of Hales,* in the place cited: *Being
substance is substantial to faith, but its form is in affection; it is called
substance by reason of affection.* Damascene too, in book 4, concludes from
this that hope is meant by faith in this passage. And certainly everyone sees
that trust more properly makes things hoped for present to us than either
knowledge or assent does.

3. Bellarmine. *The same is proved by the word “evidence” (argument), for
ἔλεγχος—demonstration, argument—pertains to the intellect.*

Protestant reply. I. An argument indeed pertains to the intellect insofar as
it is discovered and judged by it, but not insofar as it first exists in it.
Arguments are in the things themselves. So this argument can exist first in
the will, although, as apprehended, it pertains to the intellect. II. Faith is
called an argument by a metonymy of the effect. For, as *Alexander of Hales*
elegantly observes in the place cited, *in logic an argument produces faith,
but in theology faith produces an argument.* Trust in the will is my proper
argument for the things I hope for; it causes the intellect to assent to this
truth—those things are mine—as though it had already been convinced of it by
every rational evidence.

4. Bellarmine. *By faith we are said to understand creation, verse 3.*

Protestant reply. I. No one is justified by this faith as such. II. For this
faith to be solid there is required (according to the position of

[Printed p. 297 | Google Books PA297 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

the Scholastics) a certain pious affection of the will which moves and
inclines the intellect to assent to this testimony because of the truthfulness
of God who bears witness. III. This faith itself conduces to salvation in no
other way than because it produces or confirms trust.

5. Bellarmine. *Trust does not produce fear, but the act of faith in Noah
produced fear, verse 7. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. The proposition is false if it means the fear of caution,
not the fear of flight. II. The faith which produced fear did not justify.

6. Bellarmine. *We do not trust that God exists, but believe that God exists,
verse 6. Therefore believing is not trusting.*

Protestant reply. By the laws of the syllogism the conclusion must be
particular: therefore some believing is not trusting. So stated, however, it
does not contradict our defense.

7. Bellarmine. *In faith the thing remains obscure as it was before, but it is
believed because of the authority of the one speaking. Therefore faith,
properly speaking, is not knowledge but assent.*

Protestant reply. I. We concede the conclusion: faith, that is, insofar as it
is in the intellect, is more properly assent than knowledge. II. Although the
thing believed remains obscure insofar as it depends upon reason or causes,
it is nevertheless understood as attested by God. No one believes what he
does not in some manner understand to be credible.

8. Bellarmine. *From the same chapter it appears that justifying faith has as
its object everything God has revealed, and that historical faith, the faith
of miracles, and justifying faith are one and the same faith.*

Protestant reply. I. We concede that the faith which justifies presupposes,
includes, and implies historical faith, and in some people formerly the faith
of miracles as well. But it is manifest that historical faith and the faith
of miracles are often separated from justification. II. Although faith has
many exercises and objects, it is justifying only insofar as it regards the
mercy of God in Christ. Hence all those whose faith is praised in that chapter
aimed at *that promise* of mercy in Christ, verse 39.

Bellarmine. *Noah is said in verse 7 to have been justified by this faith
because he believed God when he said that a universal flood would occur and,
fearing by that faith, built the ark.*

Protestant reply. This is not said. Rather, Noah is said to have believed by
faith God speaking in this way, and by the ark not only to have been preserved
from temporal death but also, in that same ark as in a sacrament, to have
apprehended the righteousness sealed to him.

9. Bellarmine. *That faith is not trust is proved from the words of Romans
4:19–21.*

[Printed p. 298 | Google Books PA298 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. The contrary is proved by verse 18: “he believed under
hope,” and so forth.

Bellarmine. 1. *He is said in verse 19 not to have considered his own body.
Consideration, however, pertains to the intellect.*

Protestant reply. Nothing else follows from this except that Abraham overcame
those temptations which, through the intellect, might have weakened his
trust.

Bellarmine. 2. *He is said in verse 21 to have been πληροφορηθεὶς, most fully
persuaded, which pertains to the intellect.*

Protestant reply. I. There is also a full persuasion of hope, πληροφορία
ἐλπίδος, Hebrews 6:11. II. We are fully persuaded that Abraham's trust was
sustained by the intellect's persuasion concerning God's omnipotence. Nothing
more is taught in that phrase.

10. Bellarmine. *Hope is not the same as faith. But trust is hope. Therefore
trust is not the same as faith.*

Protestant reply. Trust, insofar as it regards future things, is hope; but
insofar as it regards present things, it is the same as faith. Thus Cardinal
Contarini, on justification: *This faith is in us insofar as we trust the
divine promise. This signification of faith as trust is joined with the
signification of hope, although hope properly regards future things, while
trust also regards present and past things; nevertheless, this trust is close
to hope. Therefore one is often taken for the other.* Damascene, book 4,
explaining the faith of Hebrews 11:1 in this way, *interprets it by hope.* In
Romans 4:19–20 the apostle opposes faith to distrust—that is, confidence.

11. Bellarmine. *2 Corinthians 10:5. Through faith the intellect is led captive
into the obedience of Christ.*

Protestant reply. This is indeed true, although nothing in that passage is
said expressly either of faith or of the intellect, for it is νόημα, not
νοῦς, which is there said to be taken captive. But it must be observed that
all thoughts and reasonings are brought into obedience, and that obedience is
the proper act of the will: for it is *the readiness of a will subject to the
one commanding. Thomas, 2. 2, question 1, article 2, reply 3.*

12. Bellarmine. *Ephesians 3:12. We have access in confidence or trust through
faith. But to say that through trust we have access to trust is absurd.*

Protestant reply. There is no absurdity in saying that we have the trust of
hope from the trust of faith, or that we have trust in our access to God
through that trust which we have placed in Christ.

13. Bellarmine. *The act of faith is believing, which in many passages of
Scripture cannot be explained by trusting.*

Protestant reply. As though we denied that an act of the intellect is often
denoted in the Scriptures by “faith” and “believing.”

14. Bellarmine. *That the object of justifying faith is not special mercy but
everything which God has revealed is proved, first, from the Gospels.*

[Printed p. 299 | Google Books PA299 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

Protestant reply. From the Gospels, then, Bellarmine will overthrow the Gospel
itself. For if the Gospel has no other place in justifying and saving faith
than the moral, ceremonial, and judicial law which God revealed, then no
reason appears why it should be called the Gospel or how it reveals the
righteousness of God from faith to faith.

15. Bellarmine. *Matthew 9. The blind men had faith in omnipotence; and in
chapter 16 Peter believed Christ's divinity. The centurion, Luke 7, believed
the same, as did Nathanael, John 1; the apostles, John 6; and Martha, John 11.
Finally, the Lord teaches and Thomas professes faith in Christ's resurrection
and that he is Lord and God.*

Protestant reply. I. It was conceded earlier that many things besides special
mercy must necessarily be believed. For no one can apprehend special mercy
unless he believes that God is in Christ reconciling the world to himself, 2
Corinthians 4:19. II. The faith of the blind men in Matthew 9 was no more
justifying than that of the lepers in Luke 17:17, of whom it is said, “Where
are the other nine?” III. Peter and those like him had already possessed
trust in special mercy through the Messiah; the determination of that trust
is explained through the confession of Christ's divinity. IV. Martha in John
11:26–27 was asked whether the one believing in Christ would live eternally.
Therefore, when she replies concerning the person of Christ, she manifestly
shows that her faith concerned eternal life through Christ. V. Thomas explains
his faith in those words full of trust: “My Lord and my God.” From these, all
similar passages can easily be explained.

16. Bellarmine. *The leper in Mark 1:40 had right faith and nevertheless
doubted the Lord's will and special mercy: Lord, if you will, you can.*

Protestant reply. There is nothing sound at all in this objection. For I. It
does not appear that this leper possessed justifying faith any more than those
in Luke 17 did. II. Doubt concerning the antecedent is not always contained
or implied in a connected proposition. III. Doubt concerning Christ's will
to confer a bodily benefit does not exclude certain trust in mercy unto
salvation. IV. Trust does not always exclude all doubt.

17. Bellarmine. *The Pharisee in Luke 18 had faith or trust in special
benevolence and yet was not justified. The publican lacked that trust and yet
was justified.*

Protestant reply. Bellarmine was at least drowsy here. For I. In this example
he confounds faith and trust, for whose difference he contends. II. He turns
the question concerning special mercy into another concerning benevolence.
III. He makes hypocritical trust and faith the measure of true faith. IV. As

[Printed p. 300 | Google Books PA300 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

an example of justifying faith he puts forward the faith of a man who was not
justified. V. He proves most absurdly that the publican did not have justifying
trust because *he lacked that trust* which the unjustified Pharisee possessed.

18. Bellarmine. *Mark 16:16. We see most plainly that the faith of miracles,
which does not regard special mercy, is one and the same with justifying and
saving faith.*

Protestant reply. We see no such thing, either plainly or obscurely. The thing
itself plainly shows the contrary, because many are justified without the
faith of miracles, and many formerly possessed the faith of miracles without
justification.

19. Bellarmine. *There are similar passages in Acts and the Epistles: Acts
2:38; 4:12; 10:43; 13:39; and 17; Romans 4 and 10; 1 Corinthians 15; 1 John 5,
in which the object is placed in the resurrection and divinity of Jesus
Christ.*

Protestant reply. These similar passages admit a similar solution. Yet two
things can be added here. I. By Bellarmine's own confession, the resurrection
and divinity of Christ pertain in a special manner to the object of
justification. II. Faith in Christ's resurrection and divinity does not
justify unless it apprehends these things as they were preached by the
apostles; but the apostles preached them with application. Romans 4:25 and
8:34; 2 Corinthians 5:19; Acts 5:31.

20. Bellarmine. *In the Apostles' Creed there is not a word about special
mercy.*

Protestant reply. Nothing indeed is said about the word “mercy,” but much is
said about mercy itself. For I. The whole creed took its origin from those
words of Christ, “Baptize them into the name of the Father, Son, and Holy
Spirit,” and it has the same meaning as those words. But the apostle teaches
in Romans 6:2 that a special application of mercy is contained in them.
Therefore “I believe in” diffuses special application through all the parts
of the creed. II. To believe in the remission of sins and eternal life from
remission is to believe special mercy.

21. Bellarmine. *Faith ought to aim at nothing but the word of God, but nowhere
in the word of God is salvation or the remission of sins announced to me or
to that man in particular, with the exception of a few people. Therefore the
rest of mankind cannot believe, as though from the word of God, that their
sins are remitted except altogether rashly.*

Protestant reply. The special promise is contained in the general one.

Bellarmine. 1. *Nowhere does Scripture say: he who believes that his sins are
remitted has eternal life or is justified. Rather, it says: he who believes
in the Son has eternal life or is justified.*

Protestant reply. He who says, “The one who believes in the Son has eternal
life or is justified,” says plainly enough that the one who be-*

[Printed p. 301 | Google Books PA301 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*lieves in the Son ought also to believe that he has eternal life or is
justified. For he affirms it for this end: that it may be believed by the one
who believes in the Son. We ask nothing more than this.

Bellarmine. 2. *From the conditional proposition, “He who believes in the Son
has eternal life,” the absolute conclusion, “Therefore I have eternal life,”
cannot be gathered except through the absolute assumption, namely, “And I
believe in the Son.” But this absolute assumption is not in the word of God
but in my opinion, which can be false.*

Protestant reply. I. Those two alternatives—being in the word and being in
opinion—are badly disjoined, as though there were no third possibility. There
is something in perception and experience which is neither in the word nor in
uncertain opinion. II. Bellarmine ought to have been ashamed to profess in
express words that neither he nor any Pontifician can say that he believes in
Christ except from *an opinion which can be false.* III. Let the Pontificians,
who gather enormous absolute conclusions from these propositions—*The
legitimate successor of Peter cannot err; a legitimate general council
approved by Peter's successor cannot err*—tell us where they find these
assumptions: *The bishop who today occupies the Roman see is the legitimate
successor of Peter; this or that council, say the Council of Trent, was
legitimate.* They certainly are not read in the word of God. Are they
therefore only in fallible opinion? Then certainly the whole Pontifical faith
is a fallible opinion. IV. Concerning the certainty of our faith we reply,
first, with Catharinus and the others who, in the Council of Trent itself (as
Medina relates in 1. 2, question 112, article 5), *maintained that the Holy
Spirit works in the hearts of God's children in a particular, personal, and
infallible manner, bearing them witness that they are children of God.* For
at the same time he bears them witness that they truly believe. Second, we
reply with Vega and Soto, *in the same place, that a man can be as certain of
his grace as he is certain that Rome exists,* namely, by evidence from
experience. Scotus in 3, distinction 23, affirms something more: *Just as I
believe God to be three and one, so I believe that I have infused faith
inclining me to it as to something true which is true in itself.* Bañez, in 2.
2, question 6, article 2, concludes that *it is very probably said that a man
can be certain that he has supernatural hope, because the man is certain that
he has faith in God the justifier and experiences himself hoping for pardon,*
and so forth.

Bellarmine. 3. *Not all those who think they believe actually believe.*

Protestant reply. Nor do all who think they know or see something know or see
it. Yet those who actually know or see can be certain that they know and see.
Because *heretics think that they believe most correctly* (as Bellarmine
objects), do Bellarmine and the other Pontificians therefore merely think, by
a fallible opin-*

[Printed p. 302 | Google Books PA302 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*ion, that their faith is right?

Bellarmine. 4. *Who is there who can affirm with certainty that he believes
with his whole heart and as he ought, and that he possesses a living faith
joined with charity?*

Protestant reply. With respect to degree, no one in this life believes “with
his whole heart, as he ought”; but with respect to truth, believers who
perceive that they have been sealed by the Holy Spirit unto eternal life can
affirm these things with certainty and glorify God on that account.

22. Bellarmine. *Justifying faith must precede justification. But faith in
special mercy follows justification. Therefore faith in special mercy is not
justifying faith.*

Protestant reply. I. Justifying faith precedes justification in the order of
nature, not in time, in the respect in which justification is its effect, not
in every respect. II. Faith in special mercy is so called in two ways. 1.
Because it apprehends Christ, or rests upon him, in order to apprehend special
mercy through him. 2. Because it apprehends special mercy as already given.
In the former sense it precedes justification and is said most properly to
justify; in the latter sense it follows justification. But because it is one
and the same faith which applies the mercy of God in Christ particularly by
apprehending it and renders certain that application already made, and because
its perfection or consolation appears in this certainty, which the enemies
of grace chiefly attack, therefore justifying faith is often described by
many through this certainty (which, nevertheless, as to the perception of it,
can be separated from faith for a time). Cardinal Contarini, even in Luther's
day, rightly understood long ago that this was the position of our theologians
and that it was true. Thus he writes in his treatise on justification: *We
receive justification through faith. Thomas in 3 calls this reception an
application, saying that the passion of Christ is like a common medicine
which each person applies to himself through faith and the sacraments of
faith. The Protestants call it apprehension, not in the signification which
pertains to the cognition of the intellect, but in that by which we are said
to apprehend that at which we arrive and which we attain after our movement.*

Bellarmine. 1. *The faith by which I believe that my sins are remitted is
false if, while I so believe, the sins are not yet remitted but are afterward
to be remitted through this very act.*

Protestant reply. When we so believe, sins are remitted. But they are properly
remitted through that act of trust by which we rest upon Christ or recline
upon God's mercy through him; by nature this precedes the act by which we
believe that our sins have now been remitted.

Bellarmine. 2. *If the object of this faith is the proposition, “My sins are*

[Printed p. 303 | Google Books PA303 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*remitted through Christ,” then certainly remission must be prior to faith,
since every act depends upon its object and the object does not, conversely,
depend upon the act.*

Protestant reply. The proper object of justifying faith as such is that which
properly justifies and in which faith rests in order to obtain justification;
that is, Christ, or the mercy of God in Christ—not a proposition or axiom. Yet
because through the same faith we immediately attain certainty of this truth,
“My sins are remitted through Christ,” some therefore speak as though this
proposition were the proper object of justifying faith, as Contarini observed
very well.

23. Bellarmine. *Faith in special mercy removes prayer, the sacraments, good
works, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. These are the imaginings of servile minds. The voice of the
children of God is that of the apostle in Galatians 2:20: *I live by that faith
of the Son of God, who loved me and gave himself for me. That love of Christ
constrains us.* 2 Corinthians 5:14.

Bellarmine. 1. *If I ought certainly and by faith to believe that my sins are
remitted through Christ, how can I ask for the remission of sins?*

Protestant reply. At least in the manner in which Christ in John 17:1, 5 asked
God to glorify him, of which he both ought to have been and was most certain.
We ask for its continuation, greater confirmation, and a particular
application of the remission granted.

Bellarmine. 2. *What need is there of baptism if through faith, which must
precede baptism, I am certain that my sins are already remitted?*

Protestant reply. No believer, according to the ordinary rule, is so certain
that he has no need of confirmation and greater certainty, which he must also
continually seek through God's ordinances.

24. Bellarmine. *Although trust for obtaining pardon, such as ought to be in a
man who is repenting, precedes justification, nevertheless the trust by which
someone trusts that his sins are remitted depends upon a good conscience and
therefore presupposes justification rather than effecting it.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine concedes in words as much as we ask when he
affirms that *trust for obtaining pardon ought to precede justification.* II.
He also concedes that *trust is that by which someone trusts that his sins are
remitted.* This cannot be hope for a future thing, and yet earlier, in chapter
4, he affirmed that *all trust is confounded with hope.* III. Although this
trust also has as a conserving cause a good conscience which arises from a
good life, as its producing cause it has only that faith by which hearts are
purged from an evil conscience—that is, from the guilt of sin. Hebrews 10:22.

25. Bellarmine. *Justifying faith produces peace and tranquility of mind,*

[Printed p. 304 | Google Books PA304 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*Romans 5:1. But does the special faith which Calvin teaches bring disturbance
and despair to minds? For it requires that a person know plainly that he
certainly belongs to the number of the predestined and that faith, once
possessed, cannot be lost.*

Protestant reply. I. The proposition plainly overthrows everything Bellarmine
has said thus far and the whole Pontifical faith, which, so far as they know,
is common to them and to those upon whom the wrath of God remains and therefore
cannot produce peace and tranquility of mind in them. II. The assumption,
together with its proof, is composed of a manifest contradiction. For when he
affirms that *special faith produces disturbance,* it must be understood in
the same way as that other statement, *justifying faith produces peace*—that
is, where it is present or possessed. But the man who affirms that certain
knowledge of election and perseverance brings disturbance and despair seems
fit to be sent to his kindred. III. It is a most false calumny that, according
to Calvin's position, certain knowledge of election and perseverance is
necessarily required for justifying faith.

### Chapter III

#### Our Arguments

Protestant argument. 1. Faith causes things hoped for to subsist, Hebrews 11:1;
therefore it is trust. This is also apparent from the use of the same word in
Hebrews 3:6, 14.

Bellarmine. 1. *The word ὑπόστασις does not signify expectation but substance
or subsistence.*

Protestant reply. This is the very thing we maintain: faith gives such
subsistence to things hoped for as to render their expectation certain.
Bellarmine will not say that faith is the subsistence of things hoped for
where hope itself has no subsistence from faith.

Bellarmine. 2. *In Hebrews 3:14, “the beginning of the substance of Christ”
does not signify the beginning of trust but the new creature, whose beginning
is faith.*

Protestant reply. We accept what Bellarmine grants and argue from it thus:
that upon perseverance in which our salvation is said in Hebrews 3 to depend
is trust which has the hope of glory joined to it, verse 6. But by Bellarmine's
confession that is faith, verse 14. Therefore faith is trust.

Bellarmine. 3. *If faith in Hebrews 11:1 signifies trust, it is nevertheless
trust in things hoped for—that is, eternal life. But trust in eternal life is
not justifying faith even according to the doctrine of our adversaries.*

Protestant reply. According to the doctrine of Scripture, which is ours, faith
is the trust by which we rest upon the mercy of God for sal-*

[Printed p. 305 | Google Books PA305 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*vation and eternal life. This justifying trust causes eternal life to
subsist for us as ours and therefore causes it to be firmly hoped for or
expected. Everything thus agrees perfectly: faith is trust in things hoped
for, and yet it is not properly the expectation of them.

2. Protestant argument. There are many passages of Scripture where the sense
cannot stand unless trust is understood by faith: Romans 4:18–20; Mark 11:24;
James 1:6; and similar passages—especially where justification is treated.

Bellarmine. 1. *In all these passages the word “faith” is taken for true
Catholic faith, by which we believe everything God reveals, not for trust.*

Protestant reply. I. Trust is Catholic just as faith is Catholic; therefore
faith is not distinguished from trust by the title “Catholic.” II. If in all
the passages the meaning is mere assent furnished to the truth of revelation,
then believing Christ justifies no more than, according to the Pontificians,
believing that Tobias had a dog which accompanied him on his journey, Tobit
5:25. Yet anyone who asserted this would rightly be considered blasphemous.
Cajetan on Romans 4, the penultimate verse, rightly observes that *faith
similar to Abraham's faith is such not with respect to just anything believed,
but with respect to Jesus Christ raised by God.* Toledo makes this observation
on Romans 4: *In order to signify that not just any faith, but faith in the
coming Christ the Redeemer, procures righteousness, Moses therefore mentions
that Abraham was justified by faith when knowledge of Christ was openly made
known,* Genesis 15, *for all the Doctors agree that in those words of Genesis
15 Abraham believed the promise concerning Christ.* The apostle teaches
plainly enough in that passage that this faith, and no other, is imputed to us
for righteousness. III. In the faith of miracles the matter is perfectly
plain, for not everyone who believes that God is omnipotent immediately
performs miracles.

Bellarmine. 2. *The faith by which miracles are obtained must be great, so
that it also produces a certain trust in obtaining what is sought.*

Protestant reply. I. Great faith, then, by Bellarmine's confession, produces
trust, and this trust is designated by the word “faith.” Let him therefore
say whether Abraham's faith was not great. Is not faith just as great required
for justification and salvation as for the cure of some illness or defect?

Bellarmine. 3. *That statement in Romans 4, “he did not waver in distrust,”
could signify that Abraham's faith was extraordinary, so that it also produced
trust.*

3. Protestant argument. In order to be justified, however, all must walk in
the footsteps of Abraham's faith, Romans 4:12. Therefore they must possess
faith which produces trust. But if trust is not necessary for justification,
then neither others nor

Abraham himself were bound to possess that extraordinary faith. A greater
degree of the same faith can produce trust, but it will not produce trust
unless having trust joined to it belongs to the nature of that kind of faith.
And this certainly is the true reason why trust is called faith: it arises
from faith and is its perfection.

Bellarmine. 4. *The statement in Mark 11:24, “Believe that you will receive,”
pertains to faith properly so called, but it is understood with the condition:
if you ask rightly and if it is expedient to be given to you.*

Protestant reply. That passage treats first and directly of the faith of
miracles, as appears from verse 23: “Whoever shall say to this mountain,” and
so forth. But Bellarmine conceded earlier that this faith *produces a certain
trust in obtaining what is sought.* Now since by the word “believing” is
signified that disposition of the soul which is required for obtaining, and
trust is singularly required for this end, it follows that trust is signified
by that word. Moreover, by the general assent which we furnish to the truth
of the promise, we obtain no more for ourselves than for all others to whom
the same promise pertains; but by trust we particularly apprehend and apply
to ourselves the force of that promise. The power of obtaining properly
consists in this particular apprehension. The reasoning in James 1:6 is
similar, where Bellarmine admits that *it is not absurd if someone says that
James speaks about the faith which produces trust and by this very fact also
excludes distrust.* If to these concessions of Bellarmine is added the rule
which has frequent use in explaining Scripture—*words of sense and knowledge,
when they carry an efficacious notion, signify the affections and motions
which follow knowledge*—then we have the entire case handed over to us by
Bellarmine.

4. Protestant argument. I. Faith is often opposed to fear in the Scriptures,
as in Matthew 8: “Why are you fearful, O you of little faith?” And believers
are said to be strong in their faith in 1 Peter 5:9.

Bellarmine. 1. *This occurs because trust arises from faith.*

Protestant reply. It is not simply because trust arises from faith, for all
the virtues have this in common with trust, but because it arises immediately
as the perfection of that faith which is in the intellect, or as that toward
which faith immediately tends and without which faith has neither efficacy
nor the nature of virtue.

Bellarmine. 2. *For the same reason fortitude is attributed to faith, since
faith is the root and origin of trust.*

Protestant reply. The faith which has its seat in the intellect is no less the
root and origin of fear than of trust, as Thomas teaches in 2. 2. 7. 1.
Therefore there must be some special reason why fear is opposed to faith in
the Scriptures and fortitude attributed to it.

be by gra-*

*ce, so that the promise might be firm.* The promise is not made firm by the
faith of the intellect; rather, the trust by which we receive the thing
promised makes the execution of the promise firm.

Bellarmine. 1. *The apostle is not speaking about the promise of remission of
sins.*

Protestant reply. The apostle speaks in express words about the inheritance of
believers—that is, about the blessing or blessedness in Galatians 3:14—which
he teaches to be situated in the remission of sins, Romans 4:6–8.

Bellarmine. 2. *By faith is not meant trust but dogmatic faith.*

Protestant reply. Dogmatic faith can coexist with the wrath of God, which the
apostle opposes to the firm execution of the promise in verse 15.

6. Protestant argument. Faith does not justify as it regards in the word of
God the histories of deeds, threats of punishments, or precepts of works to be
done, but only as it regards the promise of grace. We have no singular
relation to the promises above other things except by trust. Therefore trust
is what justifies.

Bellarmine. 1. *This reasoning is good if the office of justifying faith were
to render men secure and certain about the remission of sins and eternal
salvation.*

Protestant reply. But Bellarmine professed earlier, in chapter 10, fifth
reason, that this is the office of faith: *Justifying faith produces peace and
tranquility of mind.*

Bellarmine. 2. *The assumption is denied—namely, that faith does not justify
as it regards histories, threats, and precepts, but only as it regards the
promise.*

Protestant reply. If this is so, then faith in the history of Tobias and his
dog justifies in the same way as faith in the grace promised in Christ; faith
in the law justifies no less than faith in the Gospel; by justifying faith we
receive Judas the traitor and Simon Magus just as we receive Christ. These
doctrines are not so much to be refuted as detested. Scripture declares
expressly that Christ is our propitiation, not through just any faith but
“through faith in his blood”—that is, as Cajetan notes, *through faith which
rests upon his death.* Romans 3:25.
