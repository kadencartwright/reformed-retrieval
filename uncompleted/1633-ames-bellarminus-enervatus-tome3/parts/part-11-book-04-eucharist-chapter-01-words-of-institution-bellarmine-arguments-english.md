#### Bellarmine's arguments concerning the words of institution

19. Bellarmine. A sacrament, a testament, a compact or covenant, a law or
command, and a dogma require proper, not figurative, speech. But these words
deal with a sacrament, a testament, a compact, a command, and one of the chief
dogmas of religion. They are therefore proper and not figurative.

Protestant reply. The proposition is manifestly false in every respect. 1.
The sacraments of circumcision, Genesis 17, and the Passover, Exodus 12, are
instituted in figurative phrases, and all the others are everywhere explained
in a similar manner, 1 Corinthians 10. In Baptism we are said to put off the
body of sin, to be crucified, die, and be buried together with Christ, to be
grafted into Christ, to put on Christ, and to be sprinkled with Christ's
blood. In this very sacrament Paul says that Christ's body is broken, and the
cup is called the testament. All these statements are understood
figuratively, without controversy, by everyone. Bellarmine himself admits in
chapter 11, concerning a sacramental proposition which explains the sacrament
or its signification, that “in this kind of proposition the predicates are
always taken tropically, not properly.” 2. No formula of a testament, whether
divine or human, can be brought forward from the whole of Scripture which is
not adorned by some trope. 3. The formula of the new covenant itself in
Hebrews 8:10, “I will write my laws upon their heart,” cannot be expounded
without a trope. 4. For the most part, the precepts of the Decalogue cannot be
rightly understood unless synecdoche is admitted in them. 5. Let John 2:19 be
an example of a figurative dogma: “Destroy this temple, and within three days
I will raise it up.” To prove things that are not at all doubtful by more
examples would be excessively idle.

20. Bellarmine. When the Lord used these words, he was speaking with the
apostles, and in a place and at a time when it was especially unfitting to
speak obscurely.

Protestant reply. This consequence has no force: “He did not speak obscurely;
therefore he did not speak figuratively or tropically.” Tropes very often
illuminate speech rather than obscure it. The sacramental phrase was not
obscure to the apostles, who understood the rites of the ancient mysteries
and Christ's similar sayings.

21. Bellarmine. It is a common rule for explaining Scripture that the proper
sense of the words must ne—

[1632 witness | printed p. 75]

—ver be abandoned unless we are compelled by some other passage of Scripture,
some article of faith, or at least the common explanation of the whole
Church.

Protestant reply. We answer that in this passage we are compelled to abandon
the proper sense and admit a trope: 1. by all those passages of Scripture
which prohibit a crime such as devouring a man while he is alive; 2. by all
those articles of faith which teach that Christ's true humanity remains; and
third, by the common judgment of the whole apostolic Church for six hundred
years and more after Christ.

22. Bellarmine. The Lord was not accustomed to give thanks except when he was
about to do something great and wonderful, nor was he accustomed to bless
insensible things except when he was about to perform something wonderful in
them. But at this time Christ is said to have given thanks to God and blessed
the bread. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Protestant reply. 1. We concede the conclusion: namely, that at that time
Christ did something great and wonderful concerning the bread and wine, since
by his institution he made them effective instruments of grace. 2. The
proposition is utterly false. Although Scripture does not explain how often
Christ gave thanks to God and blessed insensible things, it is nevertheless
sufficiently evident that he fulfilled all righteousness, of which these
duties are not the least parts. He is also said to have given thanks in
Matthew 11:25 without any wonderful effect, just as he is said to have blessed
insensible things in Luke 24:30.

##### HOC (“THIS”)

23. Bellarmine. The pronoun *Hoc* does not demonstrate the bread, for it
cannot agree adjectivally with *pane* as its substantive, since *panis* is
masculine. Nor, of a thing which is seen and plainly known, can *Hoc* be used
substantivally unless it is neuter; but *panis* is masculine in Greek as well
as Latin. For when pointing to his brother no one would say, “This is my
brother,” nor, when pointing to an image of Caesar, would he say, “This is
Caesar.”

Protestant reply. 1. Although as an adjective it does not agree with *pane* as
its antecedent, it can nevertheless agree well with the consequent *corpore*.
Thus someone pointing to death might say, “This is the wages of sin.” 2. It
is taken substantivally or absolutely. Yet nothing is more commonplace in
every language than absolutely using a neuter pronoun for a thing of masculine
or feminine gender. In Zechariah 1:9, the papists' authoritative version says
of the same horses, “What are those?” and “What are these?” Arias Montanus
also observes there that the Hebrew *hema elle* means “these very things.”
Concerning the olive trees it likewise says in chapter 4, verse 4, “What are
these, my lord?” and “Do you not know what these are?”

In his examples he does not preserve a just proportion. For this phrase is
not customarily used as frequently of persons, but it is used of inanimate
things: wherever “this thing” can appropriately be used, so can “this.”

Bellarmine. When the subject is known only universally, it ought to be
expressed by a universal term. But when the subject is known particularly to
the hearers, it ought not to be expressed by a universal term. For example,
some people see something or other at a distance but do not discern what it
is—whether a tree, a stone, or a man—while I see that it is a man. I will
therefore say to the others, “That is a man,” not “He is a man.” But if they
see that it is a man yet do not discern who it is—Peter, Paul, or someone
else—I will not say, “That is Peter,” but “He is Peter.” The disciples,
however, saw bread and were not ignorant that it was bread.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine will be judged from his own mouth: “When the
subject is known only universally, it ought to be expressed by a universal
term.” But at that time the sacrament or mystery of the bread was known to the
disciples only universally. For since bread is merely the matter of the
sacrament, it stands as something universal and common; it therefore had to
be expressed by a universal term. When a hired worker sees a denarius, he is
not ignorant that it is a denarius. Yet because he does not see what or how
great his future wage will be, he is rightly told, “This is your wage.” So
when the disciples saw bread, they were not ignorant that it had been bread;
but they neither saw nor knew the future sacrament of Christ's body, or the
future body of Christ. They were therefore rightly told, “This is my body.”

24. Bellarmine. This is the strongest argument from Scripture. If *Hoc* in
the consecration of the bread demonstrated bread, it would likewise
demonstrate wine in the consecration of the wine. But Luke plainly contradicts
this in chapter 22, verse 20, where he says that the cup is poured out for us.
The cup therefore does not signify a cup of wine, but a cup of blood.

Protestant reply. 1. In the consecration either of the bread or of the wine,
since that which is consecrated is demonstrated by *Hoc*, it must necessarily
be conceded that the bread or wine is demonstrated by that *Hoc*. 2. Luke
does not contradict Matthew and Mark, in whom it is not the cup but the blood
that is said to be poured out for us. 3. Bellarmine attributes an absurd
statement to Luke: “This cup, that is, this blood, the New Testament in my
blood, which is poured out for you.” 4. The difficulty in this passage has
nothing to do with the grammatical agreement of the word *Hoc*. The
difficulty in the rest of the phrase is clearly removed in the other
Evangelists, but it does not belong to this passage.

25. Bellarmine. The position of the Catholics is therefore true: they
maintain that *Hoc* demonstrates not the bread but the thing contained under
the species of bread, which, although it had previously been bread, was then
already Christ's body.

Protestant reply. First, as for a single Catholic-pontifical position, there is

[1632 witness | printed p. 77]

none. In Part 4, question 10, member 4, article 1, section 3, Alexander of
Hales reports four positions. 1. *Hoc*, insofar as it is at least recited by
the priest, is not a demonstrative pronoun. 2. *Hoc* demonstrates Christ's body,
but not truly for the time at which it is uttered; rather, it does so for the
final instant of this utterance. 3. *Hoc* demonstrates to the senses the very
substance of the bread, not absolutely, but as converted into Christ's body.
4. *Hoc*, insofar as the proposition is significative, demonstrates the
bread, which is a sign of Christ's body, so that the demonstration is of the
thing signified through its own sign, as if it were said, “This rock is
Christ.” But the same *Hoc*, insofar as the proposition is operative,
demonstrates Christ's body, with the sign being transubstantiated into Christ's
body, so that the sense is: “The thing signified by this sign—which is bread
to be transubstantiated into my body—is my body.” In Part 4, controversy 5,
article 3 of *Theological Controversies between Thomas and Scotus*, Ioannes de
Rada of Aragon, bishop of Patti, enumerates five opinions. 1. *Hoc*
demonstrates the accidents, or species, of bread. 2. *Hoc* designates Christ's
body as it is in itself and in its proper species; thus Ockham and Major. 3.
*Hoc* designates the bread according to its substance and as it is in itself;
thus Ockham and Bonaventure. 4. *Hoc* designates Christ's body as it exists
under the accidents of bread at the end of the utterance of the words; thus
Marsilius, Gabriel, and Richard. 5. Formally and precisely, *Hoc* primarily
designates something common to the substance of bread and Christ's body,
according to the concept, or under the concept, of a thing contained, so that
the sense is: “This which is contained under these accidents is my body”; thus
Thomas, Scotus, Durandus, Capreolus, and others. 2. Bellarmine seems to approve
this last position, but he sophistically evades every opposing dispute through
that ambiguous and unusual formula, “It was then already Christ's body.” For
he did not dare to define whether it was then Christ's body when the Lord was
uttering the word *Hoc*, or only finally when he had uttered the whole
sentence. Thus, not yet resolving but rather further entangling these two
moments, upon whose distinction the whole difficulty rests, he mixes and
confuses them by “then already.”

##### EST (“IS”)

26. Bellarmine. *Est* signifies nothing except “is.”

Protestant reply. This is indeed most certain. But the being in question is
either essential or accidental. In a proposition, *est* is either of the
first adjunct, as they say, as in “This is,” or of the third adjunct, as in
“This is body.” Various things, therefore, can in some manner be signified by
this little word, although it signifies nothing besides “is.”

27. Bellarmine. *Est* has the simplest and most common signification, so that
all other verbs are resolved into it and something else. Thus “a man walks”
means “a man is walking.”

Protestant reply. 1. Although it has the simplest and most com—

[1632 witness | printed p. 78]

—mon signification, that signification can nevertheless admit some variety
of difference. This occurs when emphasis is placed upon its connotation of
present time—“Peter is,” not “was” or “will be”; when it is taken simply,
without relation—“This is”; and when it denotes a union, sometimes an
essential one, as in “Bread is body,” or an accidental and relative one, as
in “Bread is Christ's body.” 2. Not all other verbs can be resolved into
*est*. In “Socrates teaches” and “Socrates is taught,” the words “teaches” and
“is taught” cannot be resolved into *is* in such a way that the same relations
remain—namely, that of cause to effect and of subject to the act which is
occupied with it. “Bread becomes body” and “Bread is made body” plainly do
not sound one and the same.

28. Bellarmine. This verb *Est* is the copula of all propositions.

Protestant reply. 1. This point was refuted in the preceding manner. 2. Where
it has the force of a copula, it is also a part of the consequent. It is a
copula insofar as it has the force of connecting; but insofar as it signifies
or connotes something, to that extent it belongs to the consequent part of
the proposition.

29. Bellarmine. Tropes are not placed in words except by reason of some
particular signification. But *Est* signifies nothing except the conjunction
of one thing with another, or being itself, which is common to all things.
Therefore the trope is not in the verb *est*, nor is it put in place of
“signifies.”

Protestant reply. 1. The assumption of this syllogism was refuted above. 2.
As for the conclusion, those who maintain that the seat of the trope is in
the verb *Est* and those who locate it in the consequent noun *Corpus* use one
and the same explanation: “it signifies the body”; “it is a sign of the
body.” These are said in one and the same sense. There is therefore no other
question here about the matter than one pertaining to rhetoric. 3. From what
was said above, the meanings of those who affirm one or the other can easily be
reconciled. For although the proper seat of the trope is in the noun *Corpus*,
it nevertheless bears the trope not absolutely but in the relation which it
has to the antecedent part of the proposition. That relation is in some
manner denoted in the verb *Est*, insofar as it is accommodated to the
relation which the consequent term has to the antecedent. There is therefore
something in the verb that pertains to the trope.

##### CORPUS (“BODY”)

30. Bellarmine. Nowhere in Scripture is Christ's body taken for its sign or
figure.

Protestant reply. 1. This is the point in dispute. 2. Since Christ is taken
for a figure of Christ in 1 Corinthians 10, “The rock was Christ,” who can
prevent a similar sense from being admitted concerning Christ's bo—

[1632 witness | printed p. 79]

—dy? 3. Nowhere in Scripture does “This is body” signify a body which begins
to exist by the force of those words.

31. Bellarmine. There is nothing of which “a sign of the body” is predicated,
unless the true and natural body is a sign of itself.

Protestant reply. We previously gave it as proved that the *Hoc* in the
antecedent part, of which a sign of the body is predicated, is something other
than Christ's natural body.

32. Bellarmine. The body which was given for us is understood, as is evident
from the words which follow. But a body taken tropically was not given for
us. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Protestant reply. The bread is a sign of that true body which was given for
us. That true, proper, and natural body is therefore understood, but as it
were secondarily, through the rationale of the sign. Thus we say of an image
of Caesar, “This is Caesar, who conquered Pompey.”

33. Bellarmine. The words “given” and “poured out,” being placed in the
nominative case, must be joined with a substantive in the nominative, not in
an oblique case, and consequently with *Corpus*, not *Corporis*.

Protestant reply. A schoolboy in an elementary class could easily resolve
this childish objection from the difference in grammatical syntax between a
trope and its explanation. Is there not the same variation of agreement in
every common trope, as in “love is a burning fire” and “love is like a burning
fire”?

34. Bellarmine. The bread of the Eucharist is not called Christ's body
because, when it is duly received, we eat Christ's body spiritually at the
same time. For, according to Calvin's own position, believing in Christ is not
truly eating Christ; and according to him the body cannot be received in the
Supper in any other manner.

Protestant reply. Truly and vividly believing in Christ is the true spiritual
eating of him. But a distinctive manner in the act of faith, corresponding to
the distinctive nature of the sacramental means, is additionally required for
that sacramental eating which is effectual.

35. Bellarmine. The old sacraments, the word *Baptismus*, and an image of the
crucified one have the same efficacy as the bread of the Eucharist. But anyone
who said that any of them was the Lord's body would be foolish. Therefore the
one who says that the bread of the Eucharist is the Lord's body because it is
an effective sign of it is likewise foolish.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine is more than foolish in this whole objection.
For: 1. A prohibited image has no efficacy for true edification, much less
the same efficacy as a sacrament of God. 2. The old sacraments were more
obscure testaments, and therefore were not commended by attributes as clear
as those of the new sacraments. 3. The Word of God does not have the same
manner of signifying and sealing as the sacraments. 4. Concerning the water
of Baptism duly received, someone would not be foolish if he said, “It is
Christ's own blood, which cleanses us from every sin.”
