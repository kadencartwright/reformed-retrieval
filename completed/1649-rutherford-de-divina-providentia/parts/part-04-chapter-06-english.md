## Chapter VI

### The nature of permission

These remarks about God’s foreknowledge, or the first act of providence, will
suffice. The second act is that of counsel and the divine will, about which
something has already been said; this point concerning the permissive will
must also be added. One will is efficient and another permitting. For this to
be understood, divine permission must be discussed here.

Permission properly concerns sin. There are various ways of considering
divine permission. The first consideration concerns the morality involved in
permission. Hence the permission of sin is either just or unjust. Permission
is sometimes just because “an effect occurring incidentally”—as the legal
scholars teach—“which follows from an action or omission is not imputed to the
one permitting it unless he bears an obligation to take care that the one not
follow from the other.” Therefore, because God is not obligated to prevent
the existence of sin, permission is not culpably imputable to him. Permission
is unjust when someone is under a moral obligation to prevent it.

*[Marginal note: The first consideration of permission.]*

*[Marginal note: What sort of permission.]*

[PDF p. 120; printed p. 68]

Thus Eli culpably permitted his sons to transgress because he did not restrain
them as he ought to have done. 1 Samuel 3:13. Someone can contribute a physical
influx to a sin, even from a moral duty, without being morally guilty. Eli,
in giving life and food to his sons—as was morally right—is not guilty of
their fornication. Therefore God’s physical influx, by which he gives either
the power to sin or the material act, does not place upon God the mark of
imputability.

2. Sometimes, however, a person who does not physically prevent someone—even
by force—from ending his life with a noose is guilty of sin because of his
moral obligation. God, however, is innocent here, granted that he physically
promotes that crime. Hence these corollaries. 1. Neither does physical influx
as such implicate the one permitting in partnership with the crime, nor does
the denial of influx, where a moral obligation to prevent exists, absolve and
extricate him from the crime. 2. The obligation to prevent is the sole formal
ground of imputability. 3. Since law alone imposes obligation, the mark of
imputability can by no means be branded upon one who is not subject to the
law.

2. Permission is considered in a second way as it is referred to God. In this
respect permission is not at all opposed to God’s will or vigilant care, as if
he permitted sin unwillingly or in the manner of an Epicurean. 2. Nor is
permission contrary to omnipotence, as if God were unable to prevent it. 3.
Nor is it contrary to God’s knowledge. 4. Finally, it is not opposed to his
determinate counsel or intention. Indeed, God permits things while willing,
caring, acting omnipotently, knowing, and proceeding from a deliberate
intention.

*[Marginal note: The second consideration of permission, insofar as permission
includes a positive will of God.]*

3. Permission in a third way is referred to the thing permitted, and here it
regards four things. 1. Permission regards the material act of evil and is the
denial of an efficiency that would have been necessary for the act. But God
properly effects the act; he does not permit it. When, however, the act is
said to be permitted, evil is connoted, on account of which the act is said to
be permitted. 2. It is referred to the evil of the act and appears to be a
denial of grace and rectitude which, if present, would graciously qualify the
act; and the evil itself is permitted. 3. It is referred to the aggregate of
the act and its evil. In this relation the permission of sin also properly
exists. 4. It is referred to the freedom of the act and is God’s releasing or
tolerating by which he leaves the will to its connatural mode of acting. This
is improperly permission; it is rather the non-violation of the second cause.
For God’s ordinary effective concurrence with all

*[Marginal note: The third consideration.]*

[PDF p. 121; printed p. 69]

second causes, whether natural or free, is the non-violation of their
connatural motions.

4. Permission in a fourth way is referred to the action of God who permits,
whether moral or physical. Hence one permission is legal and another physical.
Legal permission exists when someone is not restrained by law from acting
wrongly except on pain of incurring guilt and punishment. This permission
does not so properly regard the act as the act’s legal morality; it is
otherwise called moral and suasory permission. For God, given the eternal law
concerning moral good and evil, it is not free but necessary, for it is
incompatible with God’s most holy nature to command evil or forbid good.
Physical permission exists when someone neither prevents another from acting
by placing an obstacle nor forcibly impels a free agent to act.

*[Marginal note: The fourth consideration. Legal or moral permission, and
physical permission.]*

But it may be objected from Acts 14:16: “God permitted the nations to walk in
their own ways,” namely by denying them the light of the Gospel, as is evident
from the text; but now “he commands everyone everywhere to repent.” Therefore
God sometimes permits certain people to sin by moral permission, namely by
denying the written law or the Gospel. I answer: moral permission is twofold.
One exists when God signifies by his revealed will that he so releases the
rational creature from the law that it can licitly sin. This is impossible.
The other is a moral permission which also includes physical permission, when
God withdraws suasory arguments possessing some power to prevent sin—sometimes
the Gospel, and sometimes, because of sins, saving knowledge. In this latter
way God freely permitted the nations to walk in evil ways, by both moral and
physical permission, denying the Gospel and the gracious operation of the
Spirit which accompanies the Gospel.

5. Physical permission is opposed either to the effecting of the evil act or
to its prevention, a distinction which must be observed carefully in this
thorny subject. By permission opposed to effecting, God permits sin not
sometimes but always, not freely but necessarily, because he never effects or
commits sin; and this permission belongs to God necessarily. But by permission
opposed to prevention, he permits sin only sometimes and freely, whenever it
has so seemed good to him. For he freely prevents or does not prevent angels
or human beings from sinning. He permitted Adam to sin, but could have not
permitted him—that is, he could have prevented rather than not prevented him.
He permits this person to resist the call; he does not permit that one to
resist, but efficaciously draws him to Christ

*[Marginal note: 5. The fifth consideration. Permission is opposed to the
effecting of an act or to its prevention.]*

[PDF p. 122; printed p. 70]

by removing impediments, because he so wills. Permission is contradictorily
opposed to effecting, and in its generality it is broader than either not
preventing or effecting, for it has a place not only in the category of action
but also in the categories of substance, quality, relation, and the rest. Thus
God permits Pharaoh to live and to be a father and king; that is, he does not
remove Pharaoh from their midst or deprive him of kingdom and children. He
similarly permits concupiscence to dwell in the regenerate—that is, he does
not remove it—and permits Job to be afflicted—that is, he neither forestalls
nor removes the evils that befall him. Permission is privatively opposed to
prevention, for it is a certain privation of an impediment. The one permitting
does not place an obstacle before an agent as such or before one inclined to
act.

In either consideration God is not properly a permitter in good acts, because
he is their effector and not merely one who does not prevent them. But God is
never an adviser in evil things; that is, he never interposes the authority of
one commanding in order to solicit someone by exhortation to sin. Yet when the
devil and human beings counsel evil, God can be the physical agent who does
not prevent the counsels of the devil and human beings from being directed to
the end that people sin.

6. If permission is referred to the people permitted, then something is
permitted either to the understanding or to the will. It is permitted to the
understanding in two ways. 1. When God denies the light by which what is being
done is known; in this way permission in evil things is a punishment, although
in this way God permits absolutely no one to sin against the law of nature,
since he engraved it upon everyone’s heart. 2. When God denies not so much the
habitual light as its use and does not give the concurrence required for the
actual weighing and consideration of the good to be done here and now, but
leaves a person to forgetfulness and interpretative ignorance. Accordingly,
God permitted Adam to sin and did not permit him. He permitted the will to
fall by not efficaciously moving it to abstain holily from desiring the
forbidden fruit. He did not permit the understanding to fall, and yet he also
permitted it. He did not permit it insofar as, by a suasory action—namely the
law’s prohibition—he forbade the first sin, nor did he withdraw the habitual
light by which Adam could have been recalled from sin. He permitted it because
he left Adam to interpretative ignorance; for God did not then grant the
concurrence by which Adam would actually weigh the evil act and its penalty.

*[Marginal note: 6. The sixth consideration. Either the understanding or the
will is permitted.]*

7. In one who is properly permitted to sin, an inclination to act is
presupposed. Stones and brute beasts are therefore not permitted to sin,

[PDF p. 123; printed p. 71]

because they have no inclination to sin. If permission is taken, as the
Arminians and Jesuits abusively take it, insofar as it is opposed to
efficiency, then concurrence is not of the essence of permission, because one
who concurs is an agent, while one who permits does not act. Perhaps they deny
that sin follows inferentially from permission in this sense because an
affirmation does not follow from a negation. But perhaps good logic would
dictate that, from the form’s being denied, a privation inferentially follows
in a capable subject at the due time, as in this inference: “God does not
effect in a person the obedience commanded here and now; therefore this person
necessarily sins here and now.” It follows perfectly well. I acknowledge that
it does not follow from every sort of non-efficiency.

If permission is taken as opposed to prevention, then the withdrawal of
concurrence is not of the essence of prevention. For to prevent is to place an
obstacle both before the act and before the inclination to act; but to withdraw
concurrence is indeed to place an obstacle before the act, though not before
the inclination to act.

Objection 1. “God’s withdrawing concurrence from the Babylonian fire is God’s
preventing the fire from burning the three young men. Therefore God’s
withdrawing concurrence is God’s preventing the will from willing.” I answer:
1. The antecedent is denied. God’s withdrawing concurrence is improperly to
prevent the act, because God did not suppress the power of burning in the
fire. He interrupted the fire’s act; he did not properly prevent it. 2.
Perhaps God miraculously imposed force upon the fire’s inclination; an
inference from a miraculous deed to ordinary prevention does not follow. 3. I
simply deny the consequence, because God is not thereby the preventer of acts
which he can procure but does not procure. For he can procure good acts in
angels and people whom he never created, but he should not be called the
preventer of such acts.

Objection 2. “When the will of a regenerate person is moved toward adultery by
an apparent good, the will is then prevented from willing an act of chastity;
and the will can be prevented by fear of punishment from willing adultery.
Therefore an act of the will can be prevented although no obstacle is placed
before the will’s inclination.” I answer: I grant that this intermediate
argument is tortuous, but I say that the word *prevention* is taken more
broadly here for procuring, in any manner, that good and evil acts do not
exist. In this way God can prevent acts of the will both physically and
morally. But if *to prevent* is taken formally for removing an act or
obstructing an act so that it does not occur, with an obstacle placed before
the physical inclination of the will, then a willing will

*[Marginal note: How God may prevent acts of the will.]*

[PDF p. 124; printed p. 72]

that is prevented acts against its physical inclination; that is, it is a
willing will that is unwilling and reluctant, which is contradictory.

Add that every action of a free will, insofar as it is free, is truly ethical
and moral, proceeding from either an apparent or real good or evil. But a
person whose will is prevented in a good act is not praiseworthy, and in an
evil act is not blameworthy. This is evident in the demons who believe from a
purely physical motion of the understanding, without any inclination of the
will toward the good, while they nevertheless tremble, James 2:19, as is
evident to anyone. But if someone were to will an act of chastity while his
inclination was impeded, with his will meanwhile led toward that act by an
ethical and moral motion, he would unwillingly will that good act, and such a
good volition would not be to his praise. This is a serious absurdity. A deed
performed unwillingly is not worthy of praise.

8. For us, theological permission is not a bare denial of God’s efficiency
but a denial of habitual and actual grace by which the creature would be
assisted toward the act. God’s will therefore permits an act because he
withdraws both general and special influence. Thus the permitting will exists
because, as the most distinguished and learned Dr Rivetus rightly says, it
comes “from God’s will to use the creature’s frailty for his own glory.” It
does not imply that God wills the act not to occur, but that God does not
approve or desire with moral complacency the act’s evil moral quality.

*[Marginal reference: Rivetus, Theological Disputation 1, On Providence,
thesis 24.]*

Accordingly, there is in God a physical complacency in the act insofar as,
according to the primordial laws of his institution, he concurs in the act as
first cause, while nevertheless hating the act’s viciousness with respect to
moral complacency. In the same way, an earthly father rejoices with physical
complacency that a son is born to him, but grieves with moral displeasure that
he is wicked.

*[Marginal note: God’s physical complacency is directed toward the act, and
his moral displeasure toward the act’s lawlessness.]*

Evil as such is permitted in two ways. 1. Insofar as habitual lawlessness is
not remedied. Here there is no abandonment of the will to its own operation,
except perhaps in an acquired habit of acting badly; rather, God permits evil
merely by not removing it through the infusion of grace. But 2. the permission
of actual lawlessness is the denial of habitual and actual grace, which
connotes the denial of gracious efficiency. God concurs with a person praying
drowsily; when grace is denied, the fervor of prayer is diminished. He also
concurs with a burning fire whose heat is extinguished by rain sent down from
heaven. Hence in the same act there are: 1. prevention, by the denial of grace,
as in §6; with grace merely being given, as in §3; and by the contrary action
of rain diminishing the fire’s heat; 2.

*[Marginal note: These five things often concur in the same act: 1. physical
prevention; 2. permission; 3. God’s concurrence; 4. moral prevention; 4. moral
suasion.]*

[PDF p. 125; printed p. 73]

permission—“to pray tepidly and drowsily is sin”; 3. concurrence; 4. moral
prevention, for drowsiness in prayer is forbidden; and 5. moral suasion, for
prayer is commanded and enjoined by God. These five things are often present
in the same act.
