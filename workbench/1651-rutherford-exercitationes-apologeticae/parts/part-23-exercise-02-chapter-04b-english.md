10. *Argument 5.* The antecedent will conflicts with God's happiness. For he
is not absolutely blessed who cannot attain the fulfillment of his desires.
Suarez replies that God is no less happy here, since he is not driven away
from his end by an external force, but from his infinite justice wills the
damnation of the disobedient. And Corvinus said above that God desires nothing
as one who lacks some thing. But I answer: 1. Once middle knowledge is posited,
which in the order of nature is preceded by the future occurrence of the
reprobates' disobedience and damnation—with the greatest external force and
fatal necessity preceding both God's knowledge and his free decree—God can no
more fulfill his natural will concerning the salvation of the damned than he
can bring it about that what now is should not be. This is simply impossible
for God and ἀδύνατον for divine omnipotence. 2. The stubborn wills of the
reprobate, which are external agents, determine God's will morally to damn
men; therefore God is driven away from his end by an external force.

[1651 logical printed p. 342 | IA leaf 360 | PDF p. 360]

3. Nothing would be taken away from God's blessedness if he did not know all
the things which actually exist. This would have been so if he had created
nothing, just as he freely could have refrained from creating. Yet God would
not be absolutely perfect if he were ignorant of beings that now actually
exist. But if there were any faculties and powers in God (so to speak) which
were not undeviatingly borne toward their objects, that would indicate a
defective happiness. 4. That God does everything he wills is a characteristic
which distinguishes him from the non-gods (Psalm 115:4–5). Therefore, to the
extent that he wills what he cannot do, he will not be God. 5. The Jesuit is
plainly raving when he says that because of his justice God cannot save all
whom he wills to save. What? Is it from justice, or rather from his absolute
will, that he grants a congruous calling to some and denies it to others? He
will say that this does not arise from justice, unless he has forgotten
himself, but from the absolute will to save some before others. Nor would he
injure his justice if he called everyone congruously. You will say: because of
stubbornness and contempt for the more common calling, some deserved that a
congruous calling should be denied to them out of justice. But this is to play
games with the reader. For that stubbornness arises either from a congruous
calling or from a non-congruous one. If from a congruous calling, then they
were not stubborn but obedient to God's motion. If from a non-congruous
calling, the question will return again: why did God deny the first congruous
motion, which the first stubbornness followed? Was it from justice or from
absolute will? Not from justice,

[1651 logical printed p. 343 | IA leaf 361 | PDF p. 361]

for there is no justice in denying to a man who is not yet stubborn a calling
which, if posited, would leave no stubbornness in him. If from absolute will,
we have won. Finally, neither would the Arminians with their Jesuits admit that
it conflicts with God's justice for him to move and effectually move everyone
to final obedience in a manner suited to the bent and disposition of free
choice. For Arminius himself admits that this is in the storehouse of divine
wisdom (which can in no way oppose justice), as we heard above.

11. *Argument 6.* It also conflicts with God's infinite wisdom for him to
intend what he foreknows he will never attain; in a man such an intention is
foolish and vain. Corvinus thinks it sufficient here that in God there is the
knowledge by which he knows that the creature whose salvation and obedience he
desires was created by him capable of both. But 1. From this I infer that God
now intends the salvation of the devils and of the damned already assigned to
hell, and by equal reason also the destruction and disobedience of the elect
angels and men who, now released from the body, enjoy eternal gladness. For he
knows that the devils and the blessed alike were created by him capable of
obedience and disobedience, salvation and destruction. If they say that God
cannot now wisely intend this because he knows that damned men and demons, and
blessed angels and men, can no longer in any way be rational objects of such
intentions, then I ask: why can they not? Because he knows that the damned
have now been placed beyond the bounds of obedience.

[1651 logical printed p. 344 | IA leaf 362 | PDF p. 362]

But what then? They are nevertheless capable of obedience, since they are
intelligent creatures. Therefore he grants that God's antecedent intention is
according to a more perfect knowledge than that by which it is known that the
creature was created capable of obedience and salvation. But if by the
knowledge by which he knows that the creature was created capable of obedience
and salvation he means the knowledge by which he knows that they have not yet
reached the goal but can reach it, then the object of antecedent love is not
the creature as such, as they teach, but a creature of such a kind—namely,
because it has not yet reached the goal of obedience or disobedience. 2. By
equal right God intends the creation of more worlds, angels, and men, since he
knows himself to be a powerful and sufficient cause for producing more worlds.
And then the question is: what would hinder God from satisfying such an
intention and making more worlds, angels, and men? For creatures do not resist
God's will here. Yet it is utterly absurd that God himself should resist it.
Does God resist God? 3. Arminius himself contends that it is Augustine's
teaching—and, he adds, perhaps also Scripture's—that by the decree of providence
God denies effectual grace and therefore life to the reprobate, and intends
their obedience with such knowledge as that by which he knows they will never
be persuaded to obey; and that he calls the elect by the decree of
predestination and with the knowledge by which he certainly knows that they
will obey. Accordingly,

[1651 logical printed p. 345 | IA leaf 363 | PDF p. 363]

perhaps it will also accord with Scripture, even by Arminius's own judgment,
that this antecedent will, which they maintain rests upon no foreknowledge, is
a mere chimerical dream. 4. Nor is God's knowledge that the creature can obey
a rational basis for a wise intention of God, when he knows that he will move
the will of those whose salvation he antecedently wills and intends in such a
way that they will never actually obey. It is as if God were to say within
himself: “I naturally, earnestly, and sincerely desire the obedience and
salvation of everyone. Yet I know that I shall call most of them in such a way
and move them in such a manner that they will never obey and be saved.” Again,
how can God decree a vindicatory justice which he can in no way manifest if
the antecedent will were fulfilled?

12. *Argument 7.* It is also foreign to God's justice for God to will that all
be saved before he considers them obedient. For such an antecedent love, by
which he embraces those who do not yet believe, is not grounded in Christ's
merits, nor does he love them in Christ. Therefore, according to Arminian
doctrines, it will be an unjust love. For by this argument they try to
undermine our decree of absolute election, by which God is said to will
eternal life to those for whom it has not yet been acquired by Christ's merits
and before he considers them as believers. I know that here they deny that the
reasoning about a natural affection and a decree is parallel. For they
maintain that the antecedent will precedes God's justice and is not formally
in itself

[1651 logical printed p. 346 | IA leaf 364 | PDF p. 364]

just or unjust, but benevolent and natural. But I answer: 1. In this way they
establish in God a nature devoid of all knowledge, wisdom, and justice, in the
same way that philosophers posit in man a sensitive nature prior to the
rational nature. Nothing is more unworthy of God. 2. They maintain that the
antecedent will flows from God's pure will and his very nature, but we do not
accept what has not been proved. From God's pure goodness he wills the
creature's good; insofar as God is good, his affection is necessarily borne
toward the good. For this is their meaning. Now I assume this: a sinful
creature's paying eternal penalties is either evil or good. If evil, justice
will be evil, the work of divine justice will be evil and unjust, and God, as
just, will be unjust, evil, and a perpetrator of evil. Hear, Arminians, where
your madness carries you! If it is good, then God's antecedent will must
necessarily be borne toward it. And thus by a natural affection God wills all
men, as sinners, to perish eternally no less than he wills them to be saved.
So let the Arminians go away with their consequences: God wills all to be
saved, therefore prayer must be made for all. In accord with their positions,
I similarly press what is contrary to the apostolic text: God by his
antecedent will wills each and every sinner to perish eternally; for this is a
good of justice; therefore prayer must be made for no one. If only they would
untie this knot for me! If they say that only the creature's good is the object
of the antecedent will, whereas the good of

[1651 logical printed p. 347 | IA leaf 365 | PDF p. 365]

justice is not the creature's good—which Corvinus seems to me to affirm—he
says: “Indeed, when after the condition is posited God wills something
absolutely, there remains in God, concerning its opposite—the thing thus willed
by the consequent will—a *velleity* (if the object is good), which is called
the antecedent will.” Then I say that it is false that the good of justice is
not naturally sought by God. For God necessarily loves his justice, and
therefore, when God wills and decrees that believers be saved, there remains
an antecedent velleity by which God wills that they perish—that is, if they had
been disobedient. What? Will Corvinus deny that this object is good and just:
that the disobedient perish? A man who denies such things scarcely deserves
the name of theologian. Is nothing good except what is good for the creature?
Why, therefore, he says “if the object is good” I cannot grasp, unless he means
that God antecedently wills something evil, which is horrible to say. 3.
Grevinchovius himself posits in God a natural affection for destroying
idolaters, an affection natural and prior to God's free decree, from which it
will necessarily follow that the antecedent will is naturally borne toward
the good of justice and thus toward the destruction of the sinful creature.
Indeed, Corvinus against Molina maintains that wisdom so moderates God's
affections that the creature's duty is loved by God more, and desired more
strongly, than its good. But because the creature's duty is a natural good in
itself no less than the creature's salvation, God must by his antecedent will
necessarily will the creature's duty and, by equal reasoning, the punishment

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 5, fol. 18.]

[Marginalia: Against Ames, p. 20.]

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 5, section 6.]

[1651 logical printed p. 348 | IA leaf 366 | PDF p. 366]

of the disobedient creature, for it is in itself a natural good.

13. Yet I would have it noted here that the Arminians incline to teach that
punishing a sinner is alien to God's nature and an entirely foreign work, a
blasphemy which Thomas Jackson manifestly asserts. “It is contrary to God's
nature,” he says, “to punish the formation of his own hand.” Thus Corvinus: “We
do not imagine that anything is done by God unwillingly or with a grief which
is a passion. We only say that it is done contrary to his natural inclination,
so that he destroys the one whom by his natural desire he would have saved;
and further, that God benefits from his intrinsic goodness, but that his
punishing” (this is the exercise of vindicatory justice) “is from us. This is
that God, compelled by men's persistent malice, punishes.” I answer: 1. If God
works all things according to the counsel of his will, as Paul teaches
(Ephesians 1:11), he does nothing contrary to his nature. Indeed, it is a
manifest contradiction that God does nothing unwillingly and yet punishes
because compelled by men's persistent malice. In that way he would do the same
thing unwillingly and not unwillingly, naturally and violently. 2. He says
that God does nothing with a grief that is a passion, by which he seems to
suggest that God destroys sinners with a grief which is not a passion; that
is, to posit passions in God which are not human but divine. Yet I greatly
desire to know what that passion is. Namely, it is that God consequently wills
and does something which by the impulse of nature he does not will; but this
very thing is a human passion, though one determined by reason. 3. He says that
God

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 5, section 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 349 | IA leaf 367 | PDF p. 367]

benefits from his intrinsic goodness. But I assume that punishing sinners is
from God's intrinsic goodness and natural justice, and that punishing is not
only to do well but to do what is best in itself, although not for the
creature. If he denies this, he must say that the work of vindicatory justice
is evil, contrary to God's goodness, and therefore unjust. 4. But when he says
that God's punishing is from us and not from God's intrinsic goodness, he
implies that justice is in God accidentally. Therefore God will be just by
accident and consequently unjust in himself. For if God's punishing is so from
us that it is not an act of justice and of God's best will—so that God punishes
contrary to his will and unwillingly—he must, perhaps he will say, punish
contingently while being essentially just. I do not hesitate to grant that.
But then, from the Corvinian antithesis, God benefits the creature
contingently; that is, he will contingently be good to the creature and a lover
of its salvation, though good in himself and essentially. Corvinus, however,
does not grant this. 4. I ask the reason why God benefits the creature from his
intrinsic goodness. The Arminians and Jackson in the cited place answer:
because God is naturally good, he therefore cannot but naturally will good to
his creature. Then I infer, therefore, that God punishes sinful creatures
because he is naturally and essentially just, by an entirely parallel
consequence. And therefore by a natural will God cannot but will evil for the

[1651 logical printed p. 350 | IA leaf 368 | PDF p. 368]

sinful creature, and consequently God punishes from intrinsic goodness no less than he
benefits. The Arminians cannot deny this unless they mean that God is not as
essentially just as he is good, which is absurd. For no essential attributes
are in God by greater or less degrees—nor even in a creature. A man is as
essentially animal as he is rational, although God does not essentially
benefit any creature. For then he would have had to create, by a necessity of
nature, a creature whom he would benefit. This is not a Christian doctrine but
rather an Aristotelian one. Therefore God must, by his antecedent will, will
all sinful men to perish, and consequently could not send Christ into the
world as the redemption of the human race. For the Arminians teach that all
men are sinners antecedently to the sending, indeed to the decree of sending,
Christ. Now I ask the Arminians for the reason why God, from the natural will
of saving men as creatures, would send the Savior, grant the gospel, call
everyone with promises and punishments set before them, and so forth, but
would not by equal reasoning, from God's natural will to destroy all men as
sinners, deny everyone Christ the Redeemer, the gospel, calling both common
and special, sufficient grace, and all the means of salvation. But I fear that
they might deny the natural will to destroy sinners, which Grevinchovius quite
sharply and fittingly for their doctrine

[1651 logical printed p. 351 | IA leaf 369 | PDF p. 369]

asserts. Indeed, Corvinus must assert it, for he speaks as follows: “God loves
man and from that love is inclined to benefit him. He also loves justice—and
indeed more than man—and therefore he does not will to benefit him unless he
practices justice. If man does not practice it, God determines to punish him.”
I shall examine these words in passing. If God loves man naturally and is
naturally inclined to benefit him, then does not God naturally hate the
disobedient man and naturally incline to destroy him? For God loves justice
more than man. Certainly the consequence is excellent. 2. If God loves man by
a necessity of nature, he does not love justice more than man but equally;
that is, he naturally loves both. For the necessary and essential attributes
in God do not admit degrees of intensity and remission. 3. If God loves justice
more than man, then he loves justice. For logicians know well enough that the
positive degree follows from the comparative. Hence I argue: God loves
vindicatory justice (for he speaks of such justice) either naturally or
contingently. If naturally, he hates sinners and thus naturally inclines to
reprobate all of them, which is the point at issue. If he contingently loves
justice, he can fail to love it, which is absurd. 4. His statement that God
does not will to benefit the creature unless it practices justice is utterly
false. For God benefits a creature before it practices justice, since to grant
the first calling and

[Marginalia: Corvinus, ibid., section 4.]

[1651 logical printed p. 352 | IA leaf 370 | PDF p. 370]

the first sufficient grace to a creature which has not yet done anything,
still less practiced justice, is to benefit the creature. Nor do the Arminians
dare deny such beneficence in God toward a creature which has not yet practiced
justice.

14. *Argument 8.* The antecedent will also conflicts with God's perfection in
many respects. For 1. It follows that disordered affections and passions are in
God. The antecedent will is God's natural wish, wholly frustrated, no
differently than a feverish patient desires wine contrary to the wise
physician's command, though it would harm his health, and the physician denies
wine to him against his will while he thirsts and longs for it. Thus God
naturally wills all devils and men to be saved, and wisely knows that a decree
has been made by him so that the glory of vindicatory justice might be
manifested in the destruction of reprobate angels and men. But this decree
will necessarily be nullified if God satisfies his antecedent natural wish in
the actual salvation of everyone; and if this decree is settled and firm, the
antecedent will must in most cases never be satisfied. Therefore there will be
two volitions in God which cannot be fulfilled without the ruin of divine
wisdom. Corvinus grants that the frustration of a wish is attributed to God,
but how? Hear the dictator's pronouncements. “Θεοπρεπῶς,” he says, “that is, in
such a way that they imply no imperfection or defect in God.” But is this not,
Master Dictator, to beg the question? By this very fact

[Marginalia: Ibid., section 2, p. 74.]

[1651 logical printed p. 353 | IA leaf 371 | PDF p. 371]

that the wishes are ineffectual, the greatest imperfection will be posited in
God. “Nor,” says Corvinus, “is it from a defect of power that God is frustrated
in his desires, but from the manner and limit which wisdom has set to the
exercise of that power.” But 1. Here he charges God's wisdom with the fault
that God is frustrated in his wishes. What sort of wisdom is this which
establishes such a determination of power that God is frustrated in his
wishes? Wisdom must therefore correct those wishes as foolish and as wishes
which cannot wisely be satisfied. I thought it more pleasing to the Arminians
that the frustration of God's wishes be caused by man's folly than by God's
wisdom! But it is rightly and deservedly asked whether that is wisdom which so
administers affairs that God's natural desires are frustrated, unless matters
have come to such a point that God's wisdom cannot arrange them otherwise than
for God's desires to become empty. Thus God's wisdom was unable so to arrange
the means by which all men and angels would undeviatingly be saved. What a
monstrous doctrine! He also adds: “And therefore it happens (since God desires
nothing from need) that God himself rests content in the ordered exercise of
his desires according to the same measure.” But what this means can scarcely
be grasped. Wisdom sets the measure of God's desires so that God desires no
further than infinite wisdom permits, but rests content. Yet this too is
utterly false. For wisdom so measures desires, as they dictate, that God does
not will the creature's salvation except insofar as the creature practices
justice, as we had

[Marginalia: Ibid., section 7, p. 76.]

[1651 logical printed p. 354 | IA leaf 372 | PDF p. 372]

Corvinus speaking above. But God does not rest content in such a measuring. For after God has
peremptorily decreed to damn the unbelieving Judas, God does not rest content;
there still remains in God a velleity to save Judas—namely, if he were not
unbelieving. Therefore I see no contentment here in God and no termination of
the natural velleity, but God's natural will to save each and every mortal
overflowing eternally, straying and roaming outside the limits and barriers of
justice and wisdom. No one with even a moderately keen nose fails to see it.
2. It follows that by natural love God wills many possible things whose actual
occurrence wisdom and God's free will oppose. This is to will many things
contrary to the dictate of reason and wisdom. This is a truly human and
disordered passion, and for it to be attributed to God θεοπρεπῶς is ἀνώμαλον.
3. It is impossible, without the mark of the greatest imperfection, to
attribute any effect to the antecedent will until the condition under which
God wills something antecedently is either fulfilled or broken. What does
Corvinus say here? He says that an effect of the antecedent will is given even
before the absolute decree. But show me the effect. It is this: “That God,” he
says, “willed to save all men under a condition.” Is that so? Therefore God
wills all to be saved not only by an inclination and first act, but also by a
second act. This act either is determined by God's justice and wisdom as to
its manner and object, as the Arminians loudly insist, or it is not. If the
former, justice and wisdom do not

[Marginalia: Ibid., section 10, p. 81.]

[1651 logical printed p. 355 | IA leaf 373 | PDF p. 373]

determine that God will that everyone be saved under a condition, but only that God will
believers to be saved and unbelievers not to be saved. For the conditional
will to save everyone does not need determination. I ask: toward what is this
will determined and restricted—toward the absolute will? But then it is not a
conditional volition, as Corvinus says. If it remains conditional, it is not
restricted. If it is not determined, they falsely say that wisdom and justice
and wisdom determine the antecedent will. For the actual volition to save
everyone if they believe is God's volition in the greatest breadth, not a
restricted and determined one.

15. *Argument 9.* If such an antecedent will, by which God naturally wills the
creature's good, belongs to God, then by the same reasoning God by an
antecedent will willed the creature's existence and from eternity willed the
world's creation by an impulse of nature. But then why did God not satisfy
that natural volition for infinite ages before the world's creation? What
resisted God here? Not the world, not men, not a lack of matter. They will say
that God's absolute will resisted his natural will. But how absurd! God freely
created the world, as Christians argue against Aristotle, Proclus, and others.
Therefore he could have refrained from creating it eternally, and such a will
would have been eternally frustrated. And by equal reasoning God eternally
wills the τὸ existence of infinite men and angels. What prevents him from
rather

[1651 logical printed p. 356 | IA leaf 374 | PDF p. 374]

satisfying the natural will by which he wills the faith and salvation of the
elect than that by which he wills infinite other things? Indeed, because the
necessary and eternal attributes in God never begin and never cease, God from
eternity naturally willed all men to be saved—and naturally indeed—and thus
created the world by a necessity of nature and could not have refrained from
creating it.

16. Nor are the Arminian arguments for this distinction of any value. For 1.
They argue as follows: God is infinitely good; therefore he cannot but
naturally will the creature's good. I answer: 1. It does not follow. For he
created the world from goodness, from goodness freely communicated, not from
necessity. Otherwise it follows that God naturally created the world. 2. God
is infinitely just; therefore he also naturally wills the destruction of all
sinners, which they would not admit. 3. God is infinitely good in himself. It
does not follow: therefore he is infinitely good toward his creatures. For if
he willed and actually communicated every good to them, he thus could not but
save each and every mortal, which experience contradicts. 4. From God's
goodness, God does everything for his own sake, not for the creature's good.
Therefore, by an intention just as primary, from the goodness by which God
does all things for his own sake, God wills the creature's destruction as
well as its salvation. For destruction is as much a means of manifesting the
glory of justice as salvation is a means of manifesting the glory of mercy,
because God is equally infinitely and equally primarily just and

[1651 logical printed p. 357 | IA leaf 375 | PDF p. 375]

wise, as well as good and merciful. Therefore the Arminians gratuitously
fabricate a primary and antecedent will of God which is borne toward the
creature's good and salvation, and secondarily and consequently toward the
creature's destruction, unless they prove that mercy and goodness belong to
God more essentially than justice—something impossible to devise.

*Objection 2.* God does not willingly punish (Lamentations 3). Therefore by an
antecedent will he wills man's obedience. I answer: 1. He is said not to punish
gladly because the whole cause of punishment and destruction called the
meritorious cause is from us—namely, sin—and not from God. Otherwise, if those
words are pressed rigidly, I ask: does God punish unwillingly, or from grief
of mind? 2. Let them tell us: because God laughs at the destruction and
stubbornness of the reprobate (Proverbs 1), does it not follow that by an
antecedent will God wills the destruction of sinners? I know that God does not
laugh; that is attributed to God ἀνθρωποπαθῶς, not properly, which I freely
grant. But is it not also θεοπρεπῶς and in the same sense in which he is said
to wish for our salvation and to wait for the vineyard to produce fruit?
Therefore it will be just as easy for me to prove that God primarily and
antecedently wills the destruction of sinners as the salvation of all, if
they say that God wills sinners' destruction consequently, not antecedently.
But I answer: if we consider God as he is in himself and intrinsically just
and inclined toward justice, a sinner's destruction is

[1651 logical printed p. 358 | IA leaf 376 | PDF p. 376]

an equally primary object of the natural divine volition by which he naturally
loves his justice, as the creature's salvation is an object of the volition
flowing from natural love toward creatures.

*Objection 3.* God would have willed by an antecedent will to establish Saul's
kingdom if he had obeyed (1 Samuel 13:13). But by a consequent will he did not
will to establish it but to overthrow it. I answer: therefore by an impulse of
nature God willed a kingdom to obedient Saul. But why not also to obedient
Samuel? Here there is the same formal object of the antecedent will. But then
it follows that by an antecedent will God wills all men to be kings if they
obey. Is this what it is to act as a weighty theologian? 2. Does the text
contain so much as a syllable about God's will or decree? It speaks only of
the external act of establishing, which is an external and temporal act. But
God's volitions and decrees are immanent and eternal acts. Since, therefore,
the prophet does not speak there about God's will, it is astonishing that
they would forge an antecedent will from it. 3. We proved above that there is
no properly so-called conditional will in God, although rewards and
punishments are conditionally proposed to us. These things differ by the
whole breadth of heaven. Eternal life is conditional, but the sacred oracles
do not know a conditional will of God to give eternal life. For there was a
will in God by which these things would be linked: the establishment of Saul's
kingdom and Saul's obedience, just as the sight of miracles and the conversion
of the Tyrians

[1651 logical printed p. 359 | IA leaf 377 | PDF p. 377]

are linked, as I mentioned above. Would the establishment of the kingdom
have followed Saul's obedience in this sense, although God both had decreed
and, through the patriarch Jacob, had long before predicted (Genesis 49:10)
that royal power would be in the tribe of Judah and that Shiloh—that is,
Christ—would arise from it? What? Would that promise have been false if Saul
had obeyed? What dreams these are! Corvinus says in the cited place that if
God had established Saul's kingdom on the condition that he obeyed, then he
would have willed to establish it. “For if,” he says, “God had done it once the
condition was posited, he certainly willed to do it before the condition was
fulfilled, under the stipulation that it should be fulfilled.” For the
conditional will includes two things: 1. “Formally and expressly, an affection
of simple complacency.” 2. “Eminently and implicitly, an effectual and absolute
will, which indeed does not actually exist, but would exist if the condition
were fulfilled. Yet insofar as it is turned into an absolute will through the
fulfillment of the condition, it is called conditional.” I answer: 1. There is
no consequence. If God had established Saul's kingdom on the hypothesis of
obedience—Corvinus fraudulently supposes this in his customary manner and does
not prove it; if only he had offered an argument—only this follows: therefore
he would have willed and decreed to establish it if he had established it,
which is entirely true and by no means denied by us. But because God sends a
Savior if men are sinners, it in no way follows: therefore he decreed to send
a Savior on the hypothesis that men are sinners. For God's eternal decree in
no way depends on a temporal condition to be fulfilled by men,

[1651 logical printed p. 360 | IA leaf 378 | PDF p. 378]

for in that case it would not be eternal. 2. If God had established Saul's
kingdom, he would have decreed to establish it; if he had decreed to establish
it, he would also have decreed to work obedience in Saul through effectual
grace, just as he accomplished it in David. Therefore, from the fulfilled
condition, a temporal and external act of God would have followed; but in no
way would an eternal and immanent act have followed from the same condition.
What? Would it be in the creature's power that there be eternal acts and
eternal volitions in God? Then it would be in the creature's power that God's
will exist and consequently that God himself exist, for God's volitions are
the same as God himself. 3. He says that the conditional will embraces an
affection of simple complacency—that is, an absolute volition. Therefore does
God absolutely love and will eternal life for Judas, and also conditionally
will eternal life for Judas? But these are contradictions. For if God simply
desired Judas's salvation, he would also effect it. Thus he still begs the
question. 4. He says that the conditional will eminently contains God's
absolute will, which does not now actually exist but would if the condition
were fulfilled. Yet from eternity God foreknows that the condition will never
be fulfilled. How, then, does the conditional will either eminently or
formally contain such an effectual will as God both foreknows and decrees will
never exist? This is a dream. 5. Is not the condition to be fulfilled such a
thing upon which God's absolute will depends? They deny this,

[1651 logical printed p. 361 | IA leaf 379 | PDF p. 379]

for the Arminians do not want the first cause to depend upon the second. 6. “Insofar as
it is turned into the absolute will,” he says, “it is called conditional.” But
change the word “is turned” into “is changed,” and then God's eternal
conditional will is changed into an absolute will. Is God's will mutable in
that way? But how is God's natural desire turned into an absolute decree? Then
the same will of God will be both natural and free—that is, free and not free.
If God has now ceased to will the salvation of believing elect people by an
antecedent will, and now absolutely wills them to be saved because they
believe, then an eternal desire of God has an end. But since he desires the
salvation of the elect because he is good toward creatures, if that desire has
now vanished, as changed into an absolute will, he ceases to be good toward
believers. 7. This absolute will, once the condition is fulfilled, follows
either necessarily or contingently. If necessarily, then God's effectual
decree concerning the salvation of believers is not free, but follows
necessarily and by a necessity of nature from a man's elective act. Yet
Arminius himself grants that election is a free decree. But if the decree
follows contingently or freely, then God decreed to decree and willed to will
the salvation of believers absolutely. How absurd that he decrees to decree
before he decrees! But this is to posit an infinite regress.

*Objection 4.* By an antecedent will God willed to gather the people of
Jerusalem, but by a consequent will he willed

[1651 logical printed p. 362 | IA leaf 380 | PDF p. 380]

their house to be overthrown after they had stoned and killed the prophets. I answer: 1.
“How often I willed” does not indicate that an act of willing in God, which is
immanent and eternal, is continued or interrupted through intervals of time,
as the adverb “how often” indicates. For what God wills from eternity, he wills
eternally. Therefore, only a ministerial invitation is signified there, by
which God approved their gathering to himself as a duty owed. 2. If by a
natural will God willed to gather the people of Jerusalem through the
continual ministry of the prophets insofar as they are God's creatures made in
his image, then by equal reasoning God ought to gather all mortals, even the
nations alien to the covenant. For they are God's creatures made in his image,
yet he did not do so with every nation (Psalm 147). But Corvinus says that God,
with liberty employed and wisdom summoned, antecedently supplies more abundant
means of salvation to these and less abundant but sufficient means to those.
But then I answer that it was not the natural will which the people of
Jerusalem resisted, but divine liberty and wisdom. Yet God's wisdom and
liberty do not require him to will good to the creature except insofar as it
practices justice, as Corvinus said above. 3. The will by which Christ willed
to gather them was free, for he could have refrained from willing to gather
them. But the antecedent will is natural, not free. 4. If from a merely natural
affection and desire he willed to gather them, then he wills it antecedently
to justice,

[1651 logical printed p. 363 | IA leaf 381 | PDF p. 381]

wisdom, foreknowledge, and a free decree. For this is their antecedent will.
But in that case the antecedent will revealed to the prophets would not have
revealed to the Jews God's wisdom, justice, and foreknowledge, but only God's
mere natural inclination which precedes all these. What could be more absurd?
The remaining arguments, being lighter than smoke, can easily be dissolved.
