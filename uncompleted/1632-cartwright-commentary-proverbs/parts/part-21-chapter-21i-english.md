Verse 21. He who pursues righteousness and kindness will find life, righteousness, and honour.

In רדף (*persequens*, pursuing) there is a metaphor from runners who quicken their pace in order to attain something.

The purpose is to kindle zeal for righteousness and kindness.

Here there are two things:

├─ An exhortation to zeal for righteousness and kindness,
└─ A reward.

Here first, by the word “righteousness,” he understands that virtue which renders to each person what is his own; but by the name “kindness” he understands that virtue by which we follow with benefits those who have deserved nothing

[1632 witness | PDF p. 476 | printed columns 936-937]

from us. From this it follows that it is not enough for the duty of a good man—as is nevertheless commonly said—to harm no one, affect no one with injury, owe nothing to anyone, and, finally, render to each person what is his own, unless he is also, through zeal for kindness, beneficent toward the undeserving, liberal toward the poor, and even practices beneficence toward enemies.

But because righteousness and kindness are said to be pursued, and to be followed as though at a quickened pace so that we may overtake them, we are thereby taught that these virtues are not to be sought by the godly languidly and coldly, but with great zeal and ardour. This will happen when we strain every nerve to exercise these virtues, apply our mind to the matter, form plans, expend labour and money, keep watch, and cut back and take something away from sleep and food, even not without loss to ourselves, Ps. 15; then when we not only devote our own labour to exercising these virtues, but also, to the best of our ability and so far as our calling permits, bring it about that others do the same. To this is added perseverance in these duties, which is comprehended in this word and is necessary for obtaining the reward.

And thus far indeed concerning the exhortation to zeal for righteousness and kindness. The accompanying reward follows, proceeding from the mere liberality of God and from no merit of our obedience. Since this reward is threefold, it is reduced to two kinds: the first is common to both virtues, righteousness and kindness; the other is proper to each.

Common to both is the promise of life prolonged in this age and never to be ended in the age to come. The promise concerning this life must be carefully noted for this reason: in executing righteousness and procuring it, many difficulties present themselves before our eyes by which danger is brought upon life, because righteousness, having found few patrons, lies exposed to the hatred and enmities of many. Next, in order to bind our hands so that no one would have them outstretched to exercise beneficence, it always enters the mind that as much is taken away from one’s life as is taken from one’s resources, by which the supports of life are procured. Although this promise of present life was fulfilled more abundantly and frequently for the Jews, nevertheless, and so forth.

To this is added immortality in heaven after this life, which one thing can and ought to keep us in our duty.

The rewards proper to each follow. And indeed the reward proper to the exercise of righteousness is that right will be rendered to him by people—with God bending and governing their hearts—who will take care that he is not affected with injury by others, Matt. 7.2; Luke 6.38. Conversely, those who practice injustice will not lack people who inflict injury and lay rapacious and violent hands upon their goods or those of their descendants. Thus, further, it will come about that God will fulfil for them his promises concerning the conveniences of this life.

But the reward of kindness is honour received not only from God but also from people, since nothing is more powerful for attaining glory than for someone to be munificent.

Verse 22. A wise man climbs the city of the mighty and causes the strength of its confidence to descend.

In this verse wisdom is taken for civil and political wisdom, just as in verse 20 it is taken for household wisdom.

The purpose is to show the excellence of wisdom over strength.

At Prov. 20.4 we are taught that war must be waged only with counsel, just as in the preceding Proverb wisdom is discerned in the right administration of a household.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 477 | printed columns 938-939]

But this also is commanded by God: that preparation for war be made, walls be erected and strengthened, and soldiers be armed. We see that at Josh. 8.1 God commanded that the whole army of Israel be led to the siege of the city of Ai, although the scouts sent to spy and explore had judged that only two or three thousand soldiers should be enrolled against the city, Josh. 7.3. And Christ the Saviour warns that peace should be sought by those who are surpassed by the enemy in great numbers, Luke 14.32. Since both things, therefore, are required from God as good and advantageous, it is asked which of them is more excellent for waging war. But Solomon teaches in explicit words, and indeed γυμνῶς, plainly, that wisdom is to be preferred to strength, Eccles. 9.18. This Proverb, however, teaches εἰκονολόγως, figuratively, that wisdom has greater use than strength in waging war. He teaches this from the subject of the mighty, who have undertaken the guardianship and defence of the city. And indeed he amplifies this argument by the accompanying number: namely, that one wise man can do more to storm a city than several mighty men can do to defend it. Next he teaches it from the subject of the city’s fortification—from its walls and other bulwarks of strength—which the wise man crosses and casts down. And just as for attacking a city, so for its defence, Solomon locates more power in wisdom than in strength, Eccles. 9 and 2 Sam. 20.15. The truth of this Proverb is discerned as in a mirror among creatures devoid of reason. For the cunning of a fox or hare is worth more for guarding itself against human snares than the strength of a bull or ass. I would also believe it can truly be said universally that the virtues of the mind are to be preferred to those of the body and to external goods.

Yet heavenly wisdom—that is, confidence in God, invocation of the divine name, and conversion to God—is more suited and fit for victory than both these things joined and bound together, so that if either of these must necessarily be done without in waging wars, it would be better to fight with the enemy naked, unarmed, and unskilled in war than while clinging to the dregs of sins. See Isa. 22.11, 12, 13, 14, how great the power in true piety is for laying enemies low. Likewise Gen. 14.

Verse 23. He who guards his mouth and his tongue guards his soul from distresses.

In פיו (*os suum*, his mouth) and לשונו (*linguam*, tongue) there is a metonymy of the efficient cause. In נפשו (*animam suam*, his soul) there is a synecdoche of the part. In מצרות (*ab angustiis*, from distresses) there is a metaphor transferred from narrow and confined places, where we can scarcely move the body according to our will, to afflictions which bring us to such a pass that, being destitute of counsel, we do not know where to turn.

The purpose is to teach how important it is to govern words and conversations rightly. Here two things are taught: the first is an exhortation to the careful governance of speech; the other is a reason to keep us in this duty.

Solomon seems in this passage to illustrate this doctrine concerning the proper governance of the tongue by a comparison with the gates or doors of Princes, and especially of cities which are besieged, over which doorkeepers and guards are appointed. Since a person’s mouth is therefore like the door of his house or like the gate of a little city, it follows that its custody ought to be diligent and continual. And just as, both in houses and in cities—especially besieged cities—doors are chiefly strengthened against the breaking in of thieves and gates are fortified with garrisons, so in governing the tongue, mouth, and lips a certain special care must be applied, so that we use them rightly and duly. But there is this difference: in guarding those doors of houses and cities, the principal care is that no one hostile to us and bearing a hostile

[1632 witness | PDF p. 478 | printed columns 940-941]

mind should enter; whereas in this guarding of the tongue the greatest caution must be applied lest anything harmful or hostile to us go out or burst forth. For within our minds there is a vast company of the worst and most destructive thoughts, which will either slip secretly away from us or, after making a weighty assault and impression upon us, rush forth unless they are restrained by the barriers and bolts of diligent and wakeful custody.

The argument which he adds agrees well with the teaching of James in chapter 3: namely, that when the tongue is rightly ordered the whole person will be governed well, no differently than either horses, into whose mouths a suitable bridle has been inserted, or a ship, which is steered by a rudder. For just as, when we make proper use of horses with the bridle inserted, we are delivered from many precipices and dangers, and as a ship with its due rudder for steering is safe against a fatal collision with rocks and waves, so he who guards his tongue, lest it belch forth anything indecent, keeps himself safe from very many of the gravest dangers both of this life and the life to come. For the broad and unrestrained use of the tongue casts a person into great straits of difficulty.

Verse 24. Proud and insolent—“scorner” is the name of him who exercises his pride in his fury.

In עברה (*furore*, fury) there is a metaphor from bodily things which leap over their bounds.

The purpose is, by the accompanying disadvantage of infamy, to deter us from proud actions committed in fury.

He who is proud generally also boils with wrath. For, bearing it most unjustly that anything should be done which does not please him in every respect, he grows hot against the person who opposes his will and resists it even in the smallest thing. But from indignation there immediately shoots forth a zeal for vengeance against the one with whom he is angry. Such a person therefore exercises and executes his pride furiously. The Prophet David confirms this at Ps. 10.2, where he expressly teaches that through pride the wicked man ardently persecutes the afflicted and the godly. Hence those proud words: “Am I to endure this, especially from him?” The clearest example of this appears in Esth. 3, where in verses 6 and 7 it is evident that Haman, inflamed with anger against Mordecai because he would not bend the knee to him, immediately plotted destruction not only against him but against the whole Jewish people. Next, at 2 Chron. 26 it is attributed to King Uzziah that through pride he was elevated to such a point that he invaded the priest’s office. When the priests resisted his attempts, we read that he immediately became indignant against them and—had he not suddenly been struck with leprosy by God—would, as is credible, have laid violent hands upon them. Conversely, in Moses, a man of the gentlest spirit, there was a mind most remote from wrath.

The accompanying penalty follows, a mark of infamy to be branded upon such a person: that he will be accounted proud, insolent, and a scorner by all people of sound judgment. For he who acts proudly, especially furiously, is deservedly to be called proud. Next, it is necessary that the proud man should also be stubborn and insolent, since, inflated by his pride, he persists in an opinion once adopted. Finally, because he is not at all corrected by words and admonitions, he can justly be accounted a scorner. Perhaps he will advertise himself as steadfast, and other ignorant people will further judge no differently concerning him; but meanwhile those who are just appraisers of things will account him, as he deserves, proud, insolent, and a scorner. Thus it will come about that he who seeks glory from people is affected with disgrace in place of glory.

Verse 25. The desire of the sluggard will kill him, for his hands refuse to do anything.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 479 | printed columns 942-943]

In תאוה (*desiderium*, desire) there is a metonymy of the subject for the deprivation of the thing sought. In מאנו ידיו (*renuunt manus ejus*, his hands refuse) there is a personification in which an aversion to the pursuit of labour is ascribed to the sluggard’s hands; see 13.4.

The purpose is to deter us from idleness. Here in the latter hemistich the sluggard is vividly described: namely, one whose hands refuse to do anything. It is as though he did not in his own mind shrink altogether from labour, but what he wishes cannot be obtained from his hands—that is, he would gladly perform a task provided it could be done without labour, trouble, and straining of the sinews. Hence it was said earlier that a sluggard scarcely moves his hand even to his mouth to feed himself, chapter 19. His mouth does not refuse to make a speech about labour and pour out many words about performing work; but, as it is in chapter 14.23, in the words of the lips there is nothing except poverty. What of the fact that he signals with his eyes, wanders here and there with his feet, and even by the movement of his fingers gives signs of his perverse disposition, Prov. 6.12, 13, yet nevertheless cannot be induced to put his hand to the task, 1 Tim. 5.13?

As the accompanying feature of this man he notes desire, the cause of which may be seen above. The same thing is also proved true in idle and pampered women, who suffer pica more often and frequently than those who are exercised in continual labour and occupation. Nor does so ardent a desire for novelties and foreign things in the remaining maintenance of life often come from any other source. See Exod. 5.8, where Pharaoh assigns idleness as the cause of zeal for novelties.

The effect of this desire, however, is torment and death itself, because they desire but do not obtain, since, exceeding every limit in their wishes and desires, they are of no account in the labour and work by which the things they desire are procured. And just as a desire which is fulfilled is a tree of life, Prov. 13.12, so in this passage desire is said to bring death when someone is deprived of the things he desires. For if a desire deferred and postponed grievously torments a person, Prov. 13.12, how much more when he is altogether deprived of it? And if desire for one thing alone torments us, how much more desire for the many things which the sluggard desires? A proof of this matter appears in those pampered women who suffer pica.

From this Proverb it is also evident that the sluggard is not only a thief but even a murderer. For when he does not have anything to eat, it comes about that he is simultaneously deprived both of food and of sleep, which he loves so greatly, by which two things the common life of a person is sustained. For when food fails, sleep also, which is induced by the exhalation of foods, must flee from the eyes.

Verse 26. All day long he continually desires desire, but the righteous man will give and will not withhold.

In היום (*diem*, day) there is a synecdoche of the part for time. Likewise in צדיק (*justus*, righteous man) there is a synecdoche of the genus.

The purpose is, by deterring us from idleness, to exhort us to labour.

The sluggard’s desire is described as so great and so intense that it is as though he were made and constructed wholly out of desires, just as it was said that idleness begets desires and multiplies them numerously. For when he is said to do this all day long and, in addition, continually to desire desire, it indicates the highest constancy in desiring. From this it also follows that he has nothing to eat or with which to be clothed. For if what he seeks were at hand, he would cease to seek and desire it. Hence it comes about that, in seeking undue and unlawful rest, he contracts greater torment and unrest than he would have contracted through due labour, however heavy and troublesome. The same thing also happens among very many other

[1632 witness | PDF p. 480 | printed columns 944-945]

sinners who, despising heavenly things and pursuing either the pleasures or the conveniences of this world, lose earth and heaven together.

He illustrates this by its opposite in the latter hemistich. Here, however, in place of one who is assiduous, diligent, or industrious, he sets the righteous man, in order to teach that every sluggard is unjust, but everyone assiduous in his calling, from conscience toward God, is righteous. For since the idle person is also a thief, it follows that he is unjust.

But he teaches that the industrious person—especially since he does not, like the idle person, luxuriate in his desires and wishes—has an abundant supply both of what suffices for himself and of what he may bestow abundantly upon others, Eph. 4.28. For the same conscience toward God by which he is incited to labour also induces him to bestow upon others according as need requires. For not everyone who is assiduous in labour is therefore pleasing and acceptable to God, since there are people who weary themselves with labours but nevertheless—no less than the sluggard—are useless both to themselves and all others, while they preserve the goods they have acquired laid away in a chest or bury them in the ground. And this is doubtless the reason why in this passage he opposes the righteous man, not the diligent man, to the idle person.

Verse 27. The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination. How much more when he offers it in wickedness?

The purpose is to shake the wicked out of security and vain confidence. See 15.8.

Since divine worship bears the palm among works, it comes about that, when worship performed by the wicked is hateful to God, all the other duties they perform, whatever appearance of piety and righteousness they display, are displeasing to him. The cause is that they themselves are displeasing and hateful to God, and by that very fact so are all the things which proceed from them. For the tree must first be acceptable before its fruits can be pleasing.

Here someone may object that Ahab’s humiliation was rewarded by God with a longer life, and likewise that Jehu’s obedience in cutting off Ahab’s posterity and destroying the worship of Baal was recompensed with royal dignity to the fourth generation. How, therefore, does God hate the things which he nevertheless follows with a reward? The answer is that God rewards these things which are good in appearance for this reason: first, that he may hold the wicked more guilty of their wickedness, since they do not aspire to true righteousness even though they see that the shadow of piety and righteousness does not lack reward; second, that he may add spurs to the godly for exercising piety and righteousness, since they see the wicked received so munificently by God, in whom only the likeness of virtue is seen. And it is plainly a shame to them if they permit themselves to be surpassed by the wicked in these works which are commanded by the law.

People have been found who honoured an enemy fighting vigorously and bravely against them, so that by this beneficence toward an enemy they might drive spurs into their own soldiers to perform military service vigorously and manfully. So too a master affects his servant, who diligently and attentively carries out his master’s commands in hope of reward, with a great reward, so that he might animate the freeborn and ingenuous, who render obedience under the guidance of love, to pursue their duties. But this liberality of God toward the wicked does not pass beyond the conveniences of this life, which are common to the good and the evil together.

But a greater difficulty arises from the example of Christ, who is said to have loved the young man who nevertheless, through zeal for riches and the conveniences of this life, refused to follow Christ when he commanded him to follow. No other cause for Christ’s love seems to be assigned except that in civil terms he had observed the first five commandments of the second table, Mark 10.19, 20. The answer is that Christ, as he was man, from whom nothing human was alien, loved him, not insofar as he is God. And it is fitting for us also to love in wicked people this civil

[1632 witness | PDF p. 481 | printed columns 946-947]

honesty, as useful to humankind and advantageous to the Church, and to love the people themselves. Nevertheless, nothing prevents God from hating people of this kind.

But in the latter part an even greater hatred for the deeds of the wicked is declared, and definite degrees of evil works are established. The sacrifice of the wicked is always hateful to God, even if it is of the kind which God instituted by law and even if it is offered so that they may obtain something good from God or people. It follows that that sacrifice and worship must be hateful to God if the sacrifice or worship is instituted contrary to the law: for example, if they offered lean or maimed victims and so forth, if victims obtained by fraud, or if those forbidden by God, as if someone should offer God a dog or pig, or mice and shrews. Next, if they should offer it to God in order to accomplish some evil deed and crime, as Balaam and the King of Moab offered frequent victims, or ἑκατώμβας, hecatombs, in order to curse the people of God.

From this it is evident what kind of worship, and how exceedingly hateful to God, is the worship of the Pontificians, which is displeasing to God not only because it proceeds from unbelievers but further because it is forbidden by the law, so that those who once offered a dog or pig sinned less than those who perform Mass. Next, while there is no Mass which is not abominable to God, this is especially true of those which they determine to be most august and holy: namely, the Masses, as they call them, of the Holy Spirit which they place before Councils, since they are offered to God for this purpose, that under his auspices they may oppose the truth and extinguish the truly catholic Church of Christ.

Evangelicals also who, while clinging to the dregs of avarice, drunkenness, and incontinence, suppose that they please God through external worship of God and exercises of piety are shamefully deceived. But those are especially hateful and abominable to God who, although they have devoted themselves utterly to sins, nevertheless, by discharging external worship of God, aim to appear to be good men. For besides the fact that their sacrifice is displeasing because they are wicked, they sin more atrociously because they perform worship in order to impose upon people, and for that very reason they are more hateful and displeasing to God.

Verse 28. A witness of falsehoods will perish, but a man who listens will speak forever.

In שומע (*auscultans*, listening) there is a synecdoche of the part for every sure means of understanding a matter, whether by the eye and so forth, or by experience. In לנצח (*in aeternum*, forever) there is a synecdoche of the whole.

The purpose is to preserve the inviolable faithfulness of testimony.

Witnesses have an outstanding use in the Church, Commonwealth, and household for ending lawsuits and controversies; and therefore great care must be applied that those who give testimony are as upright as possible and uncorrupted in their faithfulness. To this end Solomon applies two spurs in this Proverb: the first from the accompanying penalty of the false witness, the other from the accompanying reward of the faithful witness.

The penalty of one who violates faithfulness in his statements is destruction. But before I come to the arguments, it must be seen who is the false witness in this passage. To one who inquires, it will readily occur that “false witness” is taken differently in this passage than in the ninth commandment. For the sense of “false witness” here is narrower than there. In the latter, nearly all the errors of the tongue which are directed against one’s neighbour are comprehended; but here it is understood from the latter hemistich as the person whom we properly call a witness. Therefore, a false witness in this passage is not only one who tells a lie but also one who testifies to what is true but does not have certain and undoubted knowledge of it—that is, he testifies to what he neither saw, heard, nor experienced. For since a lawful witness is one who testifies to things heard, seen, and ascertained, it is evident that one who pours forth uncertainties as certainties is a false witness.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 482 | printed columns 948-949]

From this it is clear that everyone who is going to give testimony must deal first with his own mind before he looses his tongue to speak, and must examine not only with what intention he is going to speak but also by what supports the thing he is going to say is sustained. Therefore, before he appears in the presence of the judge, he ought to erect a tribunal in his own mind from which he may pronounce judgment concerning the truth of what he is going to say as testimony. Next, the words must be considered so that those are selected which are suitable for expressing the mind’s thoughts, lest they either extend more widely or are more contracted than the matter and truth require. Although everyone must do this, it must especially be carefully observed by Ministers of the word, who testify not to the words and deeds of people but to those of God. Hence that saying of Christ at John 3.11: “We testify to what we know and what we see.” See 1 Cor. 11.23 and 15.3, 4.

I come to the penalty of a false witness. Here it must be observed that by God’s civil and political law provision was made in accusatory cases that a false witness should pay the penalties which the person against whom the false testimony was given would have undergone. A false witness therefore often perishes in civil terms; but although he escapes civil judgment, he cannot avoid God’s judgment. For God is both a swift witness and a judge against false witnesses. Therefore, the Magistrate’s sword ought to be drawn if God’s glory were of any value among us. If someone attacks our name, we immediately grow hot and at once summon him to law; but if God is burdened with blasphemies and is, as it were, run through, we take no care for him.

But if a false witness who gives testimony without an oath perishes—as it is evident was done in the Commonwealth of God under the law, where an oath was required only when witnesses were lacking, and where the false witness was punished not for perjury but only with that penalty which the accused would have paid if he were held guilty of the alleged crime, Deut. 19.16–21—what will happen to our witnesses, who give testimony under oath? And if a false witness perishes, what will befall an unjust judge?

But the reward of a truth-telling witness is that he will always speak, that is, as long as he lives. For everyone will seek his testimony, and he will never be prohibited from giving testimony in court. From this another penalty to be imposed upon false witnesses is also concluded: namely, that their mouths will be stopped, lest they be admitted to give testimony.
