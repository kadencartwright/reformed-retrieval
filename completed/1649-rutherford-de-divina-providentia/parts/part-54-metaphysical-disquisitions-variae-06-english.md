Question 29. If a future contingent event necessarily occurs, whether from its
own nature, from GOD’s decree, or from any cause whatever, does it follow that
it is always future and will never be present, because what belongs to a thing
necessarily belongs to it inseparably and immutably? I answer: this is
sophistry, not argument. For the sophist who argues in this way imagines that a
future contingent has no necessity of its futurity

[Marginalia: Necessity from the decree does not belong to the decreed thing in
itself and always.]

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 598; IA PDF page 672; BSB canvas 648]

from the decree, since, if it were necessarily future, it would thus always be
future. Yet necessity from the decree comes to the decreed thing extrinsically;
it is not an intrinsic, simple, and inseparable necessity like the necessity by
which a human being must be an animal.

Second, the man who argues in this way believes that the future contingent is
merely possible, and yet he admits that the elect’s supernatural acts are
decreed from eternity. Therefore, they are necessarily future from eternity.
But according to the argument of a certain disputant in a manuscript, acts of
this kind would necessarily be future, therefore immutably and always future,
and thus would never be present.

Third, the future contingent will be indifferent from eternity to being and
nonbeing, as the Socinians maintain, and thus both future and nonfuture. Indeed,
in this way nothing is future before it is present. For this disputant says
that the causes of a past, present, and future thing are the same, and denies
that God is the cause of a future thing because in that case he would be the
cause of future sin. It follows necessarily, first, that he denies future
things have any cause. For causes determining themselves in the present, and
causes which determined themselves in the past, cannot produce from eternity
determinations which are future, since nothing temporal can cause what is from
eternity.

Second, it necessarily follows from this that nothing is future, either
necessarily or contingently. The reasoning is the same for necessary and
contingent future things, because a necessary cause acting in time can no more
be the cause of a necessary future than of a contingent future. But in that
case these sayings of Isaiah and the prophets were false and had no truth or
foundation in reality or in GOD’s decree: “A virgin will bear Immanuel”; “The
Son of GOD will be crucified”; “CHRIST will redeem the human race”; “CHRIST,
riding on an ass, will enter Jerusalem in triumph”; “The apostles will preach
the Gospel.” For the causes actually producing these things were not at all
present in the prophets’ time. Nor was “the sun will rise tomorrow” true
yesterday, because the cause operating in the present did not exist yesterday.

[Marginalia: The adversaries deny every future thing when they deny that GOD’s
decree is the cause of the future, or that it has any cause other than the
cause of a present action.]

Third, it follows that all future things occur apart from and independently of
GOD’s will and providence, and occur in the present by chance and blind fortune
relative to GOD’s will and intention. For they were not future, could not have
been predicted by God, and had no cause of their futurity.

Fourth, it is impossible that precisely the same action should be the cause

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 599; IA PDF page 673; BSB canvas 649]

of a future, present, and past thing. The practical idea in the mind and
intention of a craftsman who intends to construct a chair in the following
year, and the craftsman actually constructing it in the present, plainly are
not the same causes. Nor are the ideal chair in the artisan’s mind and decree
and the material chair actually constructed plainly the same chair. By the
same reasoning, things decreed by God and future, and things created by God, do
not have entirely the same causes, nor are they entirely the same things, as
the Disputant dreams.

The Disputant objects: *If a thing is from eternity, it has no cause, for no
thing is from eternity except GOD. And if things were future from eternity,
then they were not from eternity. The fallacy is too obvious: Homer is a poet,
therefore Homer exists. I deny that futurity was made from eternity, because
nothing was made from eternity. For where did futurity exist? Whatever exists
is either Creator or creature. But a certain future has no cause.*

I answer: the Disputant supposes that we think the futurity of things from
eternity is a real being, something created from eternity, or something
coeternal with God. We teach nothing of the kind. But even if we were utterly
unable to define what a future thing is, it would not follow that a future or
futurity is nothing other than what the Disputant defines for us. Whatever a
future thing may be, it is not mere nothing. It is a thing in GOD’s decree
which does not yet have being but will have it. Second, it is something decreed
by God. Third, it is an object of GOD’s knowledge: Isaiah 41:23, *Announce to us
the things that will be in the future, that we may know that you are gods.*
Fourth, it is something predicted by God and by GOD’s prophets: Isaiah 41:22;
Isaiah 7:7, 14–17; Revelation 1:1. Fifth, a future thing is something more than
purely possible. For when the foreknowing God predicts that something certainly
will be, he foreknows and predicts something far different from what is merely
possible, which may or may not occur. Otherwise, there would be no certainty
in GOD’s knowledge or in the prophets’ predictions, which is absurd.

[Marginalia: A future thing is not mere nothing.]

Second, this is not a parallel inference: “Homer is a poet; therefore Homer
exists.” The parallel is: “The future is from eternity; therefore the future
exists, or was made or created from eternity.” For the future is not simply,
nor does it exist, nor is it thus a creature in actuality; rather, it is
something created in potency and power, in GOD’s counsel and decree. Therefore,
the Disputant speaks most falsely:

[Marginalia: The Disputant denies future things because he does not know what a
future thing is.]

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 600; IA PDF page 674; BSB canvas 650]

“For my part, I say that every future thing will be in time, and consequently
its futurity is in time.” For it implies a contradiction that the futurity of a
thing should be in time together with the thing which is future. It would then
be future and present at the same time, which is to be future and not future at
the same time, since a future coexisting in time with the present is not
future. What, then? Is the world’s eternal futurity only in time together with
the existing and created world? But I ask you, Disputant, whether God from
eternity foreknew and decreed the world’s futurity before the world was created.
Scripture certainly says so: Proverbs 8:24–31. But either God from eternity
foreknew and decreed something made from eternity, or nothing made from
eternity.

[Marginalia: The Disputant says that a future thing is present and not future.]

Then where, I ask, did the world’s futurity exist before the world was created?
Where from eternity did the future incarnation, death, burial, and ascension
into heaven of GOD’s Son, and the future preaching of the Gospel which occurred
among the Gentiles, exist? Did not all these exist terminatively—if one may use
such a word—in GOD’s knowledge, omnipotence, and decree? Will the Disputant say
that the eternal futurity of the last judgment is in time together with the
last judgment? But the futurity of that judgment existed a thousand years ago;
on this account the last judgment itself also occurred a thousand years ago,
and therefore the last judgment is not future today.

Third, it is evident from this that the Disputant does not believe God decreed
future things before they actually exist; but this belief belongs to the
Socinian Volkelius. Accordingly, everything great and small, necessary and
contingent, which happens in time by counsel or by chance, was made in time by
no eternal counsel of GOD, no decree, and no wise providence of God, but by
reckless wandering and the blind impetus of fortune.

[Marginalia: Volkelius, *On False Religion*, chapter 23, columns 147, 176;
chapter 25, column 176.]

The Disputant objects: *If GOD decreed that there will be rain tomorrow,
tomorrow it will rain; the consequent depends upon GOD’s decree, but the
necessity of the consequence never depends upon GOD’s decree. This is both
because GOD could not have decreed otherwise—for he could not decree that what
he decreed would not occur—and because there would be an infinite regress. For
if it were said that GOD decreed that what he decreed would necessarily be
future, one could ask whether the necessity of that decree is another decree
of GOD, and so on to infinity, if indeed the necessity and eternity of every
true proposition depends upon GOD’s decree.*

I answer:

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 601; IA PDF page 675; BSB canvas 651]

it is astonishing what sort of clever objections these are. The necessity of
the consequent depends upon GOD’s decree, not the necessity of the consequence.
But if tomorrow’s rain is necessary from GOD’s decree, I do not see why the
necessity of this consequence offends the Disputant: “GOD decreed that there
will be rain tomorrow; therefore, by the necessity of GOD’s decree, it will
rain tomorrow.” But the Disputant hates every necessity of things and logical
consequences which emanates from GOD’s decree. For in his view, when GOD’s
decree has been posited, future contingents occur with no necessity; once GOD’s
decree has been posited, future contingents may finally occur actually or may
never occur, as the Socinians maintain. Yet Scripture does not reject the
necessity of things or consequences from GOD’s decree whenever it says, “It is
necessary that offenses occur,” “The Scriptures must be fulfilled,” and “Was
it not necessary that these things occur?”

[Marginalia: The Disputant resents any necessity of things arising from GOD’s
decree.]

Indeed, I ask whether Scripture does not argue from the Gospel promises and the
covenant of grace by a necessary and insuperable consequence, and from GOD’s
oath no less than from the decree, to infer that those benefits and consolations
which people receive by an act of faith that is wholly contingent will
necessarily occur. This may be seen in Psalm 89:24–29, 34–37; Hebrews 6:16–19;
Jeremiah 31:35–36; 32:39–40. What God promises and says he will perform,
interposing the solemn bond of an oath, is necessarily future. Yet no one says
that this necessity depends upon another promise and oath to infinity. Nor must
the necessity of the consequence flow from another decree to infinity, for the
necessity both of things and of the consequence emanates from the same decree
of God. Nor is every future thing a complex being and a proposition. Many
future objects of the decree and divine knowledge are incomplex; when they
become present, creation terminates in them—for example, the world, a human
being, and an angel.

Second and finally, when we ask for the cause of a future free action, the
Disputant gives us a free cause acting in the present. He distorts the state of
the question and begs the question. For the question concerns the cause of a
free action not simply, but insofar as it is future from eternity. The
Disputant, however, supplies a cause acting freely in time, when the question is
what its cause is—whether GOD’s free decree alone was not the cause why it was
future from eternity that the free will of Peter, Paul, and so forth would in
due time act and believe.

[Marginalia: The Disputant gives the cause of a present action as the cause of
a future action. What a future thing is.]

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 602; IA PDF page 676; BSB canvas 652]

Fearing the ruin of his cause, the Disputant does not dare assign GOD’s decree
as the reason this free action was future from eternity. For he answers that
the will of Peter or Paul freely believing at this hour is the one and only
cause why it was future from eternity that Peter would now believe. This is
certainly to deny, against Scripture, that the eternal decree of gratuitous
election is the cause why all who are appointed to eternal life believe: Acts
13:38; Romans 8:29; 11:1–2, 7; 1 Peter 1:2–4.

Question 30. What, then, is a future thing? I answer: it is an ideal and
objective thing in GOD’s will and decree which will at last become actually
present in its own time. Or it is a relation or respect of a thing which does
not yet actually exist outside nothing; since it is in GOD’s intention, God
himself will give it real essence and existence in its own time.

Question 31. Is it absurd to say that the futurity of sin was in God from
eternity, or that the futurity of the sin against the Holy Spirit is in God?
So says the Disputant. I answer: but I believe that the futurity of all sins is
from eternity in GOD’s permissive decree. I therefore commit these words of the
Disputant to the reader’s judgment. He says: “It is evident from this that
GOD’s decree cannot always be assigned as the cause why something is true from
eternity. Undoubtedly—even if it is, or is supposed to be, true of things that
GOD did not decree certain future things, such as Adam’s fall and someone’s sin
against the Holy Spirit—nevertheless those propositions are true from eternity,
because they are necessary on the hypothesis, since those things are supposed
both to have been future and already to have occurred.”

[Marginalia: The Disputant’s argument against the futurity of sins fights with
equal force against the futurity of good things.]

I answer: the Disputant has not yet proved that GOD’s decree cannot be assigned
as the cause why something is true from eternity, or that it is undoubtedly
true that God did not decree some future things such as Adam’s fall, while it
is nevertheless beyond doubt that God decreed all other future things, such as
the incarnation of GOD’s Son, the preserved free obedience of all the elect,
their final perseverance, and all good things which occur in time. For the
Disputant’s argument concludes that it is beyond doubt true from eternity that
God decreed no future things whatever, whether good or evil. But let the
Disputant answer his own argument: where from eternity did the futurity of good
things, rather than evil ones, exist? Second, was that futurity of good things
made from eternity

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 603; IA PDF page 677; BSB canvas 653]

or in time? Is it Creator or creature? Was it in God or outside God? If in God,
is it in GOD’s knowledge or in his will or decree? All the absurdities which
the Disputant heaps upon us because we hold that the futurity of sin is in the
permissive decree will follow from the position of those who maintain that the
futurity of good things is in the effective decree. For what is—or where from
eternity did there exist—the futurity of the world, human beings, angels,
animals, sheep, fish, reptiles, the incarnation of GOD’s Son, the calling of
the Jews and Gentiles, and the faith and obedience of the saints, since all
these things were future from eternity? Tell us, Disputant; we ask and adjure
you by your candor, for in your judgment whatever exists is either Creator or
creature. Yet the futurity of the world, human beings, angels, and so forth
cannot be the world, human beings, angels, and so forth themselves. First, in
that case the futurity of the creature would be a creature, which is absurd.
Second, in that case what is from eternity would not be in time. Nor is the
futurity of these things the Creator himself; this blasphemy is too great to
need refutation. In truth, the Disputant’s arguments conclude that nothing,
whether good or evil, was future from eternity. But I conclude that nothing,
therefore, is present in time or past.

Nor can it be said that the futurity of sins, about which the Disputant speaks,
has a far different character from the futurity of the good things which occur
in time; I will not deny that. But the futurity of good things, no more than
that of sins, is either Creator or creature from eternity. This argument of the
Disputant therefore opposes the futurity of all things, whether good or evil.

Second, the Disputant concedes that these propositions are true from eternity
and necessary on the hypothesis: “Adam’s first sin will be”; “The sin against
the Holy Spirit will be.” Yet there is no reason why the Disputant would not
say that all sins, equally with those two, are future from eternity, for the
necessity from the permissive decree is equal in them all. Nor can God be the
author of the lightest sin any more than of the sin against the Holy Spirit.
But to heap odium upon us, the Disputant is pleased to offer an instance in
those two. This is indeed a kind concession from the Disputant. Nevertheless,
if Adam’s first sin and the sin against the Holy Spirit are future from
eternity, and necessarily so by hypothetical necessity, we ask the Disputant in
turn: where did the futurity of these sins exist? Was such futurity made from
eternity or made in time? Is it Creator or creature? Solve

[Various Questions 29–31; printed page 604; IA PDF page 678; BSB canvas 654]

your own argument, Disputant. Then whence comes the hypothetical necessity—from
the first necessary being?
