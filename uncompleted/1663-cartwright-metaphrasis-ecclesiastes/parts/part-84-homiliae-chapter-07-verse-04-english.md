VERSE 4. The mind of the wise will be in the house of mourning, but the mind
of fools in the house of gladness.

From the subjects of each—both gladness and sorrow—Solomon proves that sorrow
is to be preferred to gladness, because the wise person, who readily admits
sorrow and repeatedly reflects upon it, is more excellent than the fool, who
pursues pleasure and joy. He teaches that the wise person readily admits
sorrow by saying that his mind is in the house of mourning and that he thinks
upon mourning even when he is not himself physically present in mourning.
Indeed, he does so even when he is in a house of feasting. For either he
recalls within himself the miseries and calamities of others—their hunger,
thirst, or want—or he turns his mind to considering a calamity that might
later be his own. Through this meditation he prepares himself for patience in
the midst of present gladness; and, moved by the recollection of it to
compassion, he also entreats God for those who suffer. Of this we have an
excellent example in

Job, Chapter 2:24, who declares that nothing befell him that he had not feared
beforehand amid the greatest abundance of every possession. Had he not done
this, when he was struck so severely by that storm, he would in all likelihood
have been utterly overwhelmed and crushed by it; but by this forethought and
prior meditation he lightened its burden and harshness.

Another illustrious example is Psalm 137:5, where the pious declare that in
the height and fullness of their gladness they will remember Jerusalem's
calamity; if they do not, they bind themselves under a grave curse. Hence the
Prophet in Amos 6:3–6 most sharply rebukes the Israelites because, cheered by
every kind of music at their most lavish and exquisite feasts, they drove the
day of calamity far from themselves and did not remember the calamity of their
brothers, whom he designates by the name Joseph. In this way the pious, as it
were, dilute their wine with water. Among pagan nations there survived a
shadow and faint representation of this teaching in the custom of having an
image of death carried around at their greatest feasts.

By its opposite it is shown that the fool's mind dwells in the house of joy
even when time and place summon him to mourning. Thus, whenever the Israelites
were subjected to trial by God, they immediately preferred to be in Egypt,
where they had been allowed to eat meat to satiety. Thus Zimri openly
fornicated with Cozbi in the camp while the rest of the Israelites were in
mourning and tears because of the Lord's anger over fornication with the
daughters of Moab. Numbers 36. To the same point belongs Isaiah 22:12–13,
where the Israelites, though summoned to fasting and lamentation, turned
instead to holding feasts.

We read that this folly prevailed among the kings of the Persians and Medes,
who gave themselves so completely to pleasures that no one dressed in
mourning clothes was allowed to enter the court. Esther 4:2. Would that this
folly were confined within such narrow bounds! Even among those very people
who boast in the name of Christ, many find every conversation about God and
sacred things tasteless and disagreeable whenever they celebrate a feast.
They habitually reject all such conversation on the ground that they have
gathered to make merry, and therefore regard every mention of sacred things as
untimely and intrusive because it brings sadness.

Therefore, to conclude this entire passage: just as it is fitting to apply
measure and moderation to sad circumstances by recalling both a better
condition formerly enjoyed and one that will follow hereafter, so let us also
set a measure upon joyful circumstances. Let it come to mind that afflictions
await us, through which God has decreed to exercise all his people, and that
salutary death, the end of all things, awaits us.
