To the fifth question, concerning vincible and invincible ignorance, I say:

Vincible ignorance is that which someone can overcome with study and
diligence by the ordinary means ministered by God. Such is the ignorance of
those born in the Church where the Word of the Gospel is preached, who can
know the Gospel and nevertheless remain ignorant of it. Another ignorance is
invincible, which someone cannot overcome by study and diligence. Yet if we
speak of saving knowledge of all the mysteries of the Gospel, this ignorance
is invincible to us by the powers of pure nature, because elevating the
intellect always requires saving-*

[Printed p. 39 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 92 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*grace. The theologians say that pagans' ignorance of Christ is invincible. 1.
Because Christ is not an object naturally knowable. 2. Because the obligation
to believe and know Christ arises from a positive command alone, superadded to
the law of nature. Romans 10:14: *“How will they believe in him of whom they
have not heard? And how will they hear without a preacher?”*

But these qualifications must be noted. 1. If the sound of Christ has passed
through the ears of pagans—as Paul speaks in the same place—their ignorance is
not entirely invincible. Granted that they have heard of Christ neither from
the written Gospel nor through preachers, yet they may have heard by report
and sound, just as the sound of the Creator's glory is heard in heaven and in
the work of creation, as in Psalm 19. The apostle alludes to that passage: he
does not say *“as it is written,”* as Pareus observes there, nor make the fame
of the Gospel as universal as knowledge of the Creator is, as the Arminians
prattle.

Nevertheless, I say that ignorance of Christ is not entirely invincible if
people hear by report from sailors or travelers that there is a Christ in whom
alone lost humankind is saved. 1. Because no one ought to be careless and
negligent concerning eternal salvation. 2. Because misery, natural ignorance,
and the nature of the immortal soul declare that a human being will thirst;
and if they now hear of a Savior, there would be a means of struggling out of
misery.

[Printed p. 40 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 93 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

2. It is not virtually invincible, because everyone naturally opposes and
disdains revealed knowledge of Christ.

3. Pagans do not diligently employ every means for investigating the truth of
the Gospel in proportion to the gravity of the matter. It is certain that God
requires diligence here.

It is nevertheless invincible: 1. Where there is no report of Christ. 2. Where
no suspicion arises from the external works of God's providence that the pagan
is liable to eternal condemnation unless delivered by God. 3. Where the
letter of the Gospel is altogether hidden from pagans.

Again, invincible ignorance among less educated Roman Catholics is twofold. 1.
It arises from a mere lack of reflex knowledge, whether rooted in upbringing
or in a strong opinion of their teachers' faithfulness, whom they persuade
themselves would not lie. This is in some manner invincible, because if an
uneducated Jew thought Isaiah divinely inspired, he would never examine
Isaiah's discourses. Thus less educated Roman Catholics believe the Church's
dictates to be the very oracles θεόπνευστα—breathed out by God—and, lacking all
suspicion or thought of malicious deceit by false prophets, labor under
ignorance that is in some manner invincible. Therefore, although they err in
fundamentals, if they nevertheless believed the articles of faith and ordered
their lives according to the Decalogue, even though they hold superstructures
which in reality and in themselves overthrow the founda-*

[Printed p. 41 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 94 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*tion, I would say that they labor under a speculative rather than a practical
error. In this way their ignorance is vincible, because they do not search the
written testament of Christ that is among them, although they can and ought to
do so—especially when they see the means supplied by God himself, namely the
reading of Scripture, withheld by their leaders.

2. Ignorance can be called in some manner invincible in those who hear that
their Church can err. Yet when they do not examine its teaching but know that
the way of salvation is opened to them through priests—people otherwise
profane—who perhaps do not faithfully repeat the mind of the pope and general
council, and thus, as they believe, the mind of God himself, such ignorance is
not altogether invincible and does not excuse them.

The purely and simply invincible ignorance of pagans is a *sine qua non* cause
of damnation and closes access to knowledge of Christ, but it is not the proper
cause of damnation. Abuse of the light of nature and of more commonly
available means is the cause of their damnation. Nevertheless, they err in the
most universal fundamentals of the law of nature, and their errors are truly
fundamental. 1. Because, knowing God, they do not glorify him as God. 2.
Because they do violence to the clear light of conscience, which is to despise
God's authority speaking through the dictate of natural conscience. 3.

Because they err miserably in the practical fundamentals.

To the sixth question (namely, how is knowledge of the fundamentals necessary
for salvation?), I say that a threefold necessity must be noted here. 1. There
is a necessity of means. 2. There is a necessity of precept. 3. There is a
necessity both of precept and of means.

Necessity of means exists when something is so necessary according to the
ordinary law that without it the end cannot be obtained. Thus, without Christ
being known and apprehended by faith, salvation is impossible for adults;
without the grace of God, salvation is impossible for infants. Something is
necessary by necessity of precept when it is necessary, not because the end
cannot in any way—not even ordinarily—be obtained without it, but because of
an obligation imposed on us by the command of a superior. In this way, God's
command in Colossians 3:16 makes it necessary that we abound in all wisdom and
knowledge of the Scriptures. Nevertheless, salvation is ordinarily obtained
by very many who do not abound in such knowledge; yet to be destitute of that
knowledge is a sin graciously pardoned to the elect in Christ.

Something is necessary in both ways when it both holds us bound by obligation
if we wish to be saved, and is something without which God ordinarily—as he
himself says in the Word—does not save us. Thus faith is necessary for adults
both by necessity of means and by necessity of precept.

[Printed p. 43 | Google/KBNL-UvA leaf absent; supplied from Google/BL
PA43 | GB mLpoAAAAcAAJ]

It must nevertheless be noted that, although constant and diligent observance
of God's commandments is a means appointed by God for obtaining salvation and,
in this broader use of the term, is therefore necessary by necessity of means,
it is not such in the present sense: namely, something absolutely and precisely
intended by God, without which the end—namely salvation—is not ordinarily
obtained. Hence a means is twofold: either something which is a means in such a
way that it obliges us, or something which, although it obliges us, nevertheless
does not—not even ordinarily—oblige God not to produce the end without such a
means.

Necessity of means is often the work of God's absolute good pleasure, as is the
revelation of the Gospel. But invincible ignorance often frees us from a
necessity of means, as ignorance of the Gospel frees pagans from the necessity
of believing the Gospel. Nor is it guilt or our sin that we lack something
which is formally necessary by necessity of means, insofar as this is
distinguished from what is necessary by necessity of precept. Furthermore,
from necessity of means there always arises a necessity of precept for adults
(where invincible ignorance has no place); and because this is a means, God
therefore enjoins and commands us to perform some operation concerning that
means. Thus, because the revelation of the Gospel is a means without which
salvation is not obtained, God therefore commands us to believe the Gospel.

It can be asked why everything contained in the Word-*

*of God must be believed under penalty of damnation, although not everything

by necessity of means. Reply: Because in the administration of the covenant of
the Gospel, God, as the supreme lawgiver and infinitely just judge of the
world, forbids every evil under penalty of eternal damnation, just as he
commands every good. Yet he forbids and commands in such a manner that, as a
lenient, gracious, and merciful Savior, he leaves room for free remission and
grace. By it, on account of Christ's death and obedience, he can pardon the
repentant for every sin, including doubt and unbelief, and likewise for the
omission of every means to salvation except that means which God absolutely and
precisely intends for salvation. It is right, moreover, that freer reins should
be left to divine mercy than to sin. For the way to glory is now evangelical,
through pardoning grace; but for Adam, who was to be led to glory by an
entirely legal way, whatever was necessary by necessity of means was also
necessary by necessity of precept, and conversely.

Perhaps effectual and confirming grace may be excepted, so that in the covenant
of nature the glory of freedom would belong to God, just as in the covenant of
grace the glory both of mercy and of justice belongs to God.

To the seventh question, I say that it is one thing 1. To believe fundamental
errors; 2. Another to profess them; and 3. Another to force them upon the
consciences of others.-*

[Printed p. 45 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 96 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*Many believe fundamental errors who do not know that they believe such errors,
but think that they firmly adhere to the Christian religion, as did the
Marcionites and Manichaeans. Ockham, in *Dialogue*, book 4, part 7, chapter 3,
treatises 1 and 2, calls such people unknowing heretics, not because they think
the false to be true (no one can believe this, because assent as such is a
natural act), but because they embrace as true that which is false. I know that
the Scholastics assert that the denial of one fundamental article is the denial
of all, because, as Meratius says, volume 2, treatise *On Faith*, etc.,
disputation 24, section 4, figures 7–8: *When several things are sufficiently
proposed to the intellect on account of one and the same formal reason, namely
divine authority, the intellect cannot embrace one and reject another.* And
certainly whoever spurns in one matter the dominion of authority which God
holds over the created intellect spurns it in all matters. But contempt here is
twofold. One is sometimes merely virtual, as when the disciples denied the
Lord's resurrection, John 20:9: *For they had not yet known the Scriptures,
that he must rise from the dead* (yet they believed him to be the Son of God,
Matt. 16:16–17). The seed and habit of faith lay hidden in them, but the act of
faith in this article was interrupted because the terms were not evident and
because of the force of temptations. Thus denial of one fundamental article is
not denial of all; otherwise, one act of unbelief would make a faithful person
an apostate, which must not be said.-*

*The other is formal contempt, when someone opposes a fundamental article on
the ground that he is not moved by any authority of God speaking; and thus one
who denies one fundamental article denies them all. From this it is sufficiently
clear, however, that many godly people doubt this or that fundamental article
and yet do not err in the fundamentals. Hence the Lutherans do not cease to be
a true Church, even though their errors are such as in some manner subvert the
foundation. Nor do the Arminians cease to be a Church while they retain the
foundation. But Arminianism as it has now gained strength under Vorstianism,
as it is set forth in the *Apology*, and especially as it is defended by
Curcellaeus and others, is a most grievous heresy and destroys the foundation,
since they have now mingled themselves with Arians, Socinians, Jesuits, and
Anti-Trinitarians.

To profess a fundamental error is more serious than to hold it; to champion and
defend it is still more serious. But to force it upon the consciences of others
by tyrannical violence is by far the most serious. The Church ought to tolerate
those who err or doubt concerning fundamentals until they are instructed, 2
Timothy 2:24–26. 2. It ought to restrain those who profess fundamental errors.
3. Those who teach and seduce others ought to be cast out of the Church.

To the eighth question (*To what extent ought we to separate ourselves from a
Church erring in fundamentals?*), I first ask: Is the Roman Church a Church? I
set forth my judgment in these assertions.-*

[Printed p. 47 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 98 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*Assertion 1. There is an invisible Church in the Roman Church and under the
papacy, like a woman hiding in the wilderness who, because of weakness, does
not dare to profess the pure faith. This is evident from the confession of many
who secretly admit that the orthodox faith has graciously been revealed to us.

Assertion 2. It is one thing to speak of the assembly that professes and
defends papism and presses it upon the consciences of others; and another to
speak of that same visible assembly insofar as it professes the Word of God and
the two principal sacraments of the New Testament, professes that Christ is
Θεάνθρωπον—the God-man—and died for sinners, professes and defends the
fundamental axioms separately from the conclusions, and professes that the
Holy Bible is God's Word. For these two acts of profession are found among
them as the profession of the papal assembly.

The assembly professing papism, as such, is the synagogue of Satan, the
Babylonian harlot, not the bride of Christ; and there is no question that as
such it is not the Church.

But the assembly professing papism *in concreto*—that is, the Word of God and,
together with it, those false, heterodox, and heretical superstructures—is not
a true Church in ethical truth or morally, just as an impure and adulterous wife
is not a true wife, that is, not an honorable wife. Yet such an assembly is a
true Church in physical truth, in a qualified and material sense, or, as Junius
says, with respect to God and not on the part of human beings. So he says in
his special book *On the Church*, volume 2, chapter-*

[Printed p. 48 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 99 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*17: *On one side it is still called Ammi, “my people,” and Ruhamah, “having
obtained mercy,” which most truly, on its own side, can be called Lo-ammi, “not
my people,” and Lo-ruhamah, “not having obtained mercy.” But this Church has
nothing which is not corrupted; I admit it. Yet everything divine which it has
is from God; everything corrupted which it has is from itself. Insofar as it
has everything divine, it is the Church; insofar as it has all those same
things corrupted, it is a corrupted Church. A Church is not abolished except
by total corruption, as they speak, which they call destruction. Partial
corruption does not abolish the Church, but weakens it. The Roman Church has
everything corrupted, but not altogether; this must be called not its
destruction but its partial corruption.*

Reason 1. An assembly which possesses the Word and baptism, the seal of the
covenant which is not to be repeated, is materially a covenanted people. But
the Roman Church is such. Therefore, etc.

2. An assembly in which there is active calling on God's part, passive calling
on the part of some convicted in conscience, and the marriage documents—that
is, the Word of God—as the instrument of calling, is a physically true Church.
But the Roman Church is such. Therefore, etc. *To that extent*, says Tilenus,
*Syntagma*, disputation 38, on the body of Antichrist, thesis 53, *the name of
Church is therefore left to the papal assemblies so long as κλῆσις, or God's
calling through his Word contained in Scripture, remains among them, calling
them-*

[Printed p. 49 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 100 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*to faith and repentance, and sealing this calling with his sacraments as
certain tokens and seals.* Yet insofar as the Roman Church falls away from the
foundation, to that extent it ceases to be the Church. They do not, however,
retain the foundation. For, as Pareus says in his commentary on 1 Corinthians
3, in words they indeed profess the Decalogue of the law and the Apostles'
Creed, but in fact overturn them, as he proves there.

For the assembly which performs a pastoral act is not on that account a true
Church (something of the Word is indeed there, but not faith); nor is the
assembly upon which some pastoral act is performed on that account a true
Church. For Jeremiah 51:9, *“We would have healed Babylon, but she is not
healed,”* would then prove the Chaldeans to be a true Church, because an active
calling was there on the part of the prophet Jeremiah who called them.

As there is a twofold κλῆσις, so an assembly is called the Church in two
ways: either an assembly called externally, in which sense the Roman Church is
in some manner the Church; or one called internally, that is, the assembly of
the faithful, in which sense it is to some extent the Church because of certain
elect persons externally mingled with the Antichristian crowd.

If the Church is taken passively for an assembly called by God through the Word
and Holy Spirit, holding the foundation and believing in Christ, the Roman
Church is not a true Church morally or physically, either *in concreto* or
insofar as it is papal.-*

*1. Because the foundation of salvation is not in it; rather, their teaching
overturns all the fundamentals by idolatry, superstitions, and heresies.

2. Because the Word as explained by it is not the Word of God or a marriage
document with God, but the word of human beings and Satan, contradictorily
opposed to it. Nor is it a document of marriage with Christ, but of desertion
of Christ and adultery with Antichrist.

If “Church” is taken less properly for an assembly called by the Word of God
and confirmed by certain sacraments, to that extent the Roman Church is the
Church, as is proved by the arguments which Junius adduces for this from our
writers, and Erasmus, Wicelius, Cassander, Grotius, and others who follow these
leaders among the papists or papizers—men who veneer papal faith and religion.

If, third, “Church” is taken for an invisible and hidden assembly, called and
convicted by the calling Word purely explained by Protestants and believed by
those who nevertheless, from weakness, do not dare to profess the true faith,
then even that which lies hidden at Rome is a true, though weak, Church. It can
be called Roman, although not in the sense in which “Roman” is now commonly
distinguished by theologians from the Protestant Church.

Laud, *Against Fisher*, § 20, pp. 130–131, nos. 4–5: *The Roman Church is not a
right—that is, an entire—Church; nevertheless, it is true in essential truth,

there is active and passive calling, there is an assembly of the faithful, and
therefore a Church. But maliciously to attack one fundamental article, such as
the resurrection of the dead, is, as Pareus says, to subvert the faith.

Laud falsifies the matter in *Against Fisher*, § 35, no. 1, p. 281, when he

Just as a false prophet—for example, Balaam—prophesied orthodox teaching about
the star of Jacob, that is, about the Messiah, and nevertheless remained a
ψευδοπροφήτης, a false prophet; and just as dialecticians distinguish a
logic which teaches from a logic which uses, so one Church is teaching and
another using. I do not deny that Junius acknowledges the Roman Church to be a
true Church, but certainly he speaks about the teaching Church, not the using
Church.-*

*For it is certain that the papal Church *in concreto* is a Church in the sense
that it teaches fundamentals, hands down Scripture as God's Word, teaches, and
performs true pastoral acts. But because it subverts the foundation through
heterodox superstructures, it is not a Church that uses. Therefore, although
the Roman Church teaches the orthodox faith in itself, by which many believe
in Christ and can be saved, it does not follow that it is a physically true
Church when the discussion concerns a Church using the faith.

Although we admit that the possibility of salvation exists in the Roman
Church, it is nevertheless unsafe to remain where the foundation is overturned,
even though there is a possibility of retaining the foundation and it is
actually retained by the more sincere part which lives under the papacy.

Even if all the fundamentals are conceded as true by Roman Catholics and

Both concede the necessity of good works, but one says they are necessary as
our formal righteousness, the other as effects of free justification. Both
believe Scripture to be God's Word and the norm of faith, but one party
proclaims it a partial norm and the other a perfect and total norm.-*

*Objection. But the Church has made the minister a non-minister, and he is now
as it were a layman.

Reply. He is not altogether a non-minister, because habitual ἐξουσία—authority—
remains. Its exercise is nevertheless forbidden, because he has only been
deprived until he repents; and when he repents, he is not ordained afresh by
the Church, but merely admitted to the exercise which he lost.

Objection. The Church can ministerially take away what it ministerially gave.
But the Church ministerially gave habitual ἐξουσίαν—authority. Therefore it
can take it away.

Reply 1. The major premise is not universally true whenever the Church gives
something under God. The Church confers external baptism under God, but it
cannot take it away by excommunication. For although the excommunicated person
is as a publican, or is to be regarded in the place of a Gentile and publican,
he nevertheless does not cease truly to be baptized with external baptism. 2.
The Church ministerially begets a person to God through the preaching of the
Word; nevertheless, one who is regenerate can truly and justly be
excommunicated. But the Church cannot take away the seed of God and the
spiritual birth which it ministerially conferred.

Reply 2. I deny the minor premise. In conferring office the Church does not
give habitual ἐξουσία—authority; God alone gives it. The Church designates
someone for exercise and by its power causes the person whom God has endowed
with both ἐξουσία—authority—and δύναμις—power—to be able rightly to
discharge and actually exercise the ministry divinely given to him.
