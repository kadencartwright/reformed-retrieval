QUESTION XXVI.

Has the forensic law been abrogated in the New Testament? We distinguish.

[Marginal heading: The ends of the forensic law.]

I. The *forensic* or judicial law concerned the civil government of the people
of God in the Old Testament and contained a system of precepts concerning the
form of that political government. Its ends were various: 1. Εὐταξία, *good
order*, and the lawful constitution of the Jewish polity, which had to be a
true θεοκρατία, *theocracy*, as Josephus calls it. 2. The separation of that
commonwealth and nation from all other peoples and commonwealths, and the
establishment of that polity as the seat of the church and the place of God’s
revelations. 3. The vindication of the moral and ceremonial law from contempt,
and consequently the enforcement of obedience and obligation toward both. 4.
The foreshadowing of Christ’s spiritual kingdom.

[Marginal heading: Three opinions concerning its abrogation.]

II. There are three opinions concerning its abrogation. The first errs by
*defect*. It is the opinion of the *Anabaptists* and *Antinomians*, who judge
it to have been absolutely and simply abrogated in every respect. Therefore,
whenever arguments are drawn against them from the Old Testament for the right
of the magistrate, the lawfulness of war, the division of inheritances, and
similar matters, they are accustomed to dispose of them with this one answer:
those matters are forensic and pertain to the Israelite people and the Old
Testament, but have now been abrogated under the New Testament.

The second errs by *excess*. It is the opinion of those who judge that this law
still remains in force and must be retained, and that Christian commonwealths
must be governed according to the Jewish norm. This was the opinion of
*Carolostadius* and *Castalio*, whom the Lutheran *Brochmannus* joins. Both
opinions stray from the truth. On the former view, many moral precepts
contained in the forensic laws would be abrogated. On the latter, many
typological precepts utterly foreign to the circumstances of our times would
have to be observed. The third opinion is that of the *orthodox*, who take the
middle course and resolve the matter by a distinction: the law has been
abrogated in one respect but is still retained in another.

III. Various ends must be distinguished in this law. Insofar as it was the
*separation of the Jewish commonwealth from the Gentiles* and a *type of
Christ’s kingdom*, it has been simply abrogated, because in Christ there is no
longer a distinction between Jews and Gentiles (Gal. 3:28; Eph. 2:14). Just as
the Jewish commonwealth and polity have been destroyed, so also there is no
need of a type to foreshadow Christ’s future kingdom, since it has now been
manifested.

But with respect to the *εὐταξίαν*, *good order*, or *form of government* of
the Israelite people, the law cannot be said to have been abrogated except in
a certain respect. Namely, those things in the law which belonged to
*particular law* and regarded the Jews peculiarly according to the condition
of the place, time, and Jewish nation—such as the law concerning levirate
marriage, the bill of divorce, gleaning, and so forth—must be carefully
distinguished from those which belonged to *common and universal law* and are
founded upon the law of nature common to all. Such are the laws concerning
judgments and the punishments of crimes, widows, orphans, strangers, and
similar persons, which belong to moral and common law.

With respect to the former, the law is rightly said to have been abrogated,
because once the Jewish polity was removed, all the laws that peculiarly
regarded it necessarily ceased as well. But with respect to the latter, it
still remains, because it enters into the nature of the moral and perpetual
law and was prescribed to the Jews not simply as Jews, but as human beings
subject to the law of nature along with all others.

To distinguish those things which belong to common law from those which belong
to particular law, a threefold criterion can be employed. 1. Those laws which
obtain not only among the Jews but also among Gentiles who follow the light of
right reason belong to common law. Thus the Greeks, Romans, and others had
their own laws in which many things agree with the divine laws, as even the
comparison of Mosaic and Roman law undertaken by various authors teaches. 2.
Those laws which are found to conform to the precepts of the Decalogue and
serve to explain and confirm it belong to common law. This is readily gathered
by attending either to the object and matter of the laws or to the reasons for
sanctioning them. 3. Those laws which are repeated in the New Testament in
such a way that their observance is commended to Christians belong to common
law.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IV. In laws founded upon common law, or the law of nature, the substance of
the precept must be distinguished from its circumstances. Some belong to
common law both in their substance and in their circumstances. Others belong
to common law in their substance, but to particular law in their
circumstances. The former are perpetual in every respect; the latter only in a
certain respect. Thus, in laws concerning the punishments of crimes, the
substance of the punishment belongs to natural law, but the manner and degree
of the punishment belong to particular law and are therefore changeable.

V. Whatever forensic laws are intermingled with types are by their nature
changeable and are therefore abrogated *de jure*, because their causes and
foundations are temporary, not perpetual. Such are the laws concerning the
right of the firstborn (Deut. 21:17), the cities of refuge (Deut. 19:2), the
Jubilee, not sowing a field with different kinds of seed, and not wearing a
garment made of wool and linen, and similar matters. Although these laws could
also have a political end, nevertheless, because they were typological, by
that very fact they cease to bind.

VI. Forensic laws which were accommodated to the character and constitution
of the Jewish polity have not only become useless to Christians living under
another polity, but neither can nor should they be observed any longer. Such
were the law of levirate marriage, the law concerning the drink of jealousy,
the law concerning the sale of a son (Exod. 21), the law concerning the
sabbatical rest of fields, the law concerning the division of the land of
Canaan among the tribes, and similar laws. These have a peculiar σχέσιν,
*relation*, to the Israelite people and their government; once that government
was removed, they can no longer have any use.

VII. Once a polity has been abolished, the laws upon which that polity rests
must be abolished if they belong to positive law and regard the Jewish
commonwealth simply as such. But other laws which are founded upon natural law
and are appendices of the Decalogue are not immediately abolished. Therefore,
the forensic law has not been abrogated with respect to the general
determinations which

rest upon the moral law, but it has been abrogated with respect to the special
determinations which regarded the commonwealth of the Jews.

VIII. The forensic law is considered either *formally*, insofar as it was
given to the Jews, and so it has been abrogated; or *materially*, insofar as it
agrees with the law of nature and is founded upon it, and so it still remains.

IX. Although the laws sanctioned by God were the best and wisest for the state
of that people, it does not therefore follow that they must be perpetual. By
positive and free right, God could give them at a definite time and for
definite reasons to one nation without their being valid with respect to
others. What is good for one is not immediately good for another.

X. What is better than other things in every way—both in the abstract and in
the concrete, and both in thesis and in hypothesis—is to be preferred to other
things. But the forensic law is better than other laws not in thesis but in
hypothesis, because it was determined for definite circumstances which no
longer obtain. Further, it is better than human laws insofar as they are simply
human, but not insofar as those laws are founded upon the law of nature, which
is from God. Therefore, when Roman laws are preferred to Mosaic laws, they are
not preferred insofar as they were simply enacted by human beings, but insofar
as, being drawn from natural and common law, they can be more suitable to
places, times, and persons.
