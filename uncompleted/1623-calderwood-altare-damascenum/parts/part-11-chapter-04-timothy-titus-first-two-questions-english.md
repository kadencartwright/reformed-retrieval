Tilenus, as he learned it from Saravia, proves the episcopate of Timothy and
Titus from the Scriptures as follows. *I have observed that Sosthenes,
Silvanus, Timothy, Titus, Tychicus, Crescens, Luke, John Mark, and any others
of that kind were called by Paul directly to the ministry of the church and
were placed both over flocks and over several pastors of flocks, and that
they exercised that very episcopate which the English retained from that
genuine antiquity, and that the authority received from Paul was not only
employed for a time but was also transmitted to their successors. Thus, just
as the ministry of the Word and sacraments is necessary to the church, so in
this ministry that right and authority to appoint ministers differing from
one another no less in rank than in gifts is perpetual: those superior in
rank and set over several churches are to judge those below them, who feed
only one flock; they are either to admit or reject accusations instituted
against them; openly rebuke those lawfully convicted; lay hands hastily on no
one; and administer everything in a holy and religious manner, doing nothing
from partisan zeal or `κατὰ πρόσκλισιν`, “out of partiality,” but all things
`χωρὶς προκρίματος`, “without prejudgment,” 1 Timothy 5:19–22. Whoever holds
that these functions in the church are temporary thinks that the church
itself is at last to be overturned and utterly ruined by the vicissitudes of
the times.* Likewise in chapter 5 of the *Paraenesis*: *Therefore, if Timothy
was ordained by the presbyters of the church at Lystra, as Beza supposes, and
was set over several churches of Asia, to each of which he gave its own
pastor, just as Titus did to the Cretans according to Paul's command, do we
still doubt that one pastor, furnished with suitable gifts, may lawfully be
set over not only several flocks but also several pastors? Shall we persist
in willfully shutting our eyes to that visible model of ecclesiastical
government, as Paul himself drew it for us with his own brush and expressed
it by his example? Will those canons prescribed in the epistles to Timothy
and Titus be extraordinary and temporary for us? Why, then, do we measure the
ordinations of our fathers by this standard?* But Downham presses the argument
more forcefully. *It is apparent from Paul's epistles to Timothy and Titus,*
he says, *that he ordained Timothy bishop at Ephesus and Titus in Crete, since
the epistles themselves are true patterns and `πρότυπα` of the episcopal
function. For just as the apostle had entrusted them with episcopal authority
in respect both of ordination and of jurisdiction—which is presupposed in the
epistles—so in these epistles he instructs them, and in them all bishops, how
they should exercise their function: first, in respect of ordination (as in
Titus 1:5, “I left you in Crete so that you might appoint presbyters town by
town, as I directed you”; and 1 Timothy 5:22, “Do not lay hands hastily on
anyone, nor share in the sins of others.” To this end he describes what sort
of presbyters and deacons those whom they ordained ought to be, 1 Timothy 3:2,
8; Titus 1:6–9). Second, in respect of jurisdiction not only over the people
but also over presbyters, enjoining them to be at once leaders and censors of
their doctrine (as in 1 Timothy 1, “I urged you to remain at Ephesus so that
you might charge certain persons not to teach*

*a different doctrine or attend to myths,” and so forth; 2 Timothy 2, “Restrain
profane clamors about empty things”; Titus 1, “There are many deceivers `οὓς
δεῖ ἐπιστομίζειν`, ‘whose mouths must be stopped,’ who subvert whole
households, teaching for shameful gain things which they ought not; for this
reason rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith”; and Titus
3, “Restrain foolish questions, genealogies, contentions, and fights about
the law”). At the same time he made them judges both of their persons and of
their conduct, as in 1 Timothy 5: “Do not receive an accusation against a
presbyter except on the evidence of two or three witnesses. Rebuke those who
sin before everyone, so that the rest may fear. I charge you in the sight of
God and the Lord Jesus Christ and the elect angels to observe these things
without prejudgment, doing nothing with an inclination to either side.” Titus
3: “Reject a heretical man after a first and second admonition.”* Three things
must be discussed here. First, whether all the precepts enumerated here
necessarily imply the authority of a judge, or jurisdiction. Second, whether
their execution was entrusted to Timothy and Titus alone. Third, whether it
was entrusted to them as evangelists or as bishops.

As to the first: there is no doubt that laying on hands and appointing
presbyters belong to the authority of ordination. Charging certain persons
not to teach a different doctrine can be done outside a consistory as well as
in one, just as can charging those who are rich in this age not to become
haughty, 1 Timothy 6:17. Moreover, as Theodore Beza observed, *`τὸ
παραγγέλλειν`, “to give a charge,” is properly said of the kind of mandates
which is handed on and received through hands, so that the actual author of
the mandate is said `διαγγέλλειν`, that is, to signify something through
another, while one who announces something in another's name, as though
received through a hand, is said `παραγγέλλειν`.* Thus this charge is not so
much Timothy's as that of the apostle who sent him. Or, *that you might
announce,* that is, *that you might be God's messenger,* as Anselm of
Canterbury interprets it at this place. And what else is charging men not to
teach a different doctrine than what the same Paul commands Timothy in 2
Timothy 2:14: *Remind them of these things, charging them in the sight of the
Lord not to fight about words, which brings no benefit*? “Charging” means
calling upon the Lord also as witness, or rather as judge. To restrain profane
clamors about empty things—literally, to *stand around* them—is nothing other
than to watch and besiege them so that they do not creep into the church, as
Beza interprets it. And what is that but to keep watch, which the apostle
commands all the presbyters of the Ephesian church to do in Acts 20:28–29? If
you instead render the Greek word *Avoid,* it will signify still less. To stop
the mouths of deceivers in Titus 1:11 is nothing other than *to refute them,
overcome them by reasoning from the Scriptures, and impose silence on them
by the weight of testimonies,* says Anselm. For he had said previously in
verse 9 that it is required of a pastor that he be able to convince those who
contradict. Yet any pastor can do this, even outside a consistory. And

[1623 printed p. 147 | IA leaf 159 | PDF p. 159]

in verse 13, repeating the same command in other words, he says, *For this
reason rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith.* “Rebuke
them sharply” means explicitly and without any circumlocutions, so that no
avenues of escape lie open. Rebuking those who sin before everyone can be
done openly in a sermon. Not receiving an accusation against a presbyter
except under two or three witnesses, and rejecting a heretic after a first or
second admonition, do indeed seem to imply a judicial process. But grant that
all the things which Downham said belong to jurisdiction are consistorial;
nevertheless, it does not follow that they were in the authority of Timothy
and Titus alone. *Those who think that either Timothy at Ephesus or Titus in
Crete exercised royal power, each arranging everything according to his own
judgment, are mistaken. They presided only so that by good and salutary
counsels they might lead the people in appointing ministers, not so that they
alone, to the exclusion of all others, might do whatever pleased them,* says
Calvin. It is not credible that Timothy and Titus did what the apostles
themselves did not do. The apostles did not excommunicate alone where a
church had been constituted. Paul does not claim for himself alone the
excommunication of the incestuous man, nor does he alone lay hands on
Timothy, but does so together with the presbytery. And when Paul commands
Timothy not to lay hands hastily on anyone, he warns him to keep himself pure
and not to share in the sins of others. *It can be seen,* says Piscator, *that
these words contain the second part of the precept, in which the apostle
forbids Timothy to agree with other presbyters when they choose for the
church's ministry someone who is not worthy.* Must the command about
supporting widows be restricted to Timothy alone because the apostle says,
*Honor widows*? The apostles themselves did not arrogate this authority to
themselves alone in Acts 6. *To receive an accusation is to bring a charge
before the church, to bring the accused to judgment, and to rebuke him
openly,* says Whitaker, *which not only superiors can do, but equals and even
inferiors. In the Roman republic, the equites passed judgment not only on
members of the people but also on senators and patricians. And Timothy
certainly does not seem to have had a consistory or tribunal such as was
afterward established for bishops in the church. Likewise, the nature of
this authority can be understood from what follows: `ἔλεγχε`, “rebuke,”
those who sin before everyone—which equals also can do.* The bishop of Ely,
in the *Torture of Tortus*, admits that the jurisdiction of the external
forum was instituted in Matthew 18 with these words: *Tell the church,* and
so forth. But to tell one prelate is not to tell the church. Therefore, the
things written to Timothy and Titus were not written so that they alone
should exercise the jurisdiction of the external forum, but so that they
might perform their parts while the right of the churches remained intact.
We shall show later that the things commanded here to Timothy and Titus are
also commanded elsewhere to presbyters. You will say, then, why are the
epistles addressed to Timothy and Titus? I said above that one cannot rightly
infer the singular authority of those to whom epistles are addressed merely
from their inscriptions. It is enough that they are presidents and moderators
of the ecclesiastical proceedings to whom the epistles are directed,

because they are expressly addressed. *For this is how Roman historians
often speak,* says Calvin: *the consul who held the assembly is said to have
created the new magistrates, for no other reason than that he received the
votes and guided the people in the election. It is certainly not credible
that Paul granted more to Timothy and Titus than he assumed for himself. Yet
we see that he customarily created bishops by the people's votes. The
passages above must therefore be understood in such a way that they take
nothing away from the church's common right and liberty.* We do not deny that
Timothy and Titus retained the presidency as long as they remained there.
Besides the ordinary presidency of their order, certain peculiar things must
also be considered in Timothy and Titus. First, their function was of a more
eminent rank. Ordinary presidents have the same function as their colleagues;
Timothy and Titus had a higher and loftier one. Second, they were the
apostles' fellow workers, sent by the apostles to those churches with
apostolic mandates: they were to see that things were completed by the
churches in the same order in which the apostle himself would have completed
them had he been present. It is not without reason that the apostle wrote in
2 Timothy 4:22, *Grace be with you all,* and in Titus 3:15, *May the grace of
God be with you all.* Third, not all the churches in Asia and Crete had been
constituted. The situation of churches being constituted is one thing; that
of churches already constituted is another. It is no wonder if Titus alone
appointed presbyters town by town where there were no presbyters, or if
Timothy did so in Asia. For they relate that Timothy was bishop not only of
the Ephesians but also of the Asians. But we will show later that in
constituted churches not even the apostles themselves exercised ordinary
jurisdiction alone.
