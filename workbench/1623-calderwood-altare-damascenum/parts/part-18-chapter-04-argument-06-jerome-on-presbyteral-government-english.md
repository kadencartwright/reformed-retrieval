Gratian, citing Jerome on the Epistle to Titus in the *Decretum*, has these
words: *Formerly a presbyter was the same as a bishop. And before, at the
devil’s prompting, parties arose in religion and it was said among the
peoples, “I am of Paul, I of Apollos, and I of Cephas,” churches were governed
by the common counsel of presbyters. But after each man thought that those
whom he had baptized were his own and not Christ’s, it was decreed throughout
the whole world that one man elected from the presbyters should be placed
over the rest, to whom the entire care of the church should belong, and that
the seeds of schisms should be removed.* And a little afterward: *Therefore,
just as presbyters know that by the custom of the church they are subject to
the man placed over them, so bishops should know that they are greater than
presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance, and that
they ought to govern the church in common.* Jerome has similar words in the
letter to Evagrius cited above on p. 204, which the reader may consult. Jerome
distinguishes three periods. *The first, in which churches were governed by
the common counsel of presbyters. The second, in which parties arose in
religion and it was said among the peoples, and not at Corinth alone, etc.
For when those words were first being said at Corinth, the churches were
still governed by the common counsel of presbyters, as is plain from 1
Corinthians 5 and 2 Corinthians 1. The third, finally, in which one man
elected from the presbyters was placed over the rest. And each of these
periods had its own breadth, as the common expression has it.* Concerning the
first period Dounamus says three things. First, he says that Jerome’s position
is false with respect to the church of Jerusalem, and he proves it from
Jerome himself, who says in the *Catalogue of Writers* that immediately after
the Lord’s passion James was ordained bishop of Jerusalem by the apostles.
But in Bellarmine’s own judgment that *Catalogue of Writers* is not Jerome’s,
but the work of some more recent author, as we observed above on pp. 140–142.
As for the testimony itself and James’s episcopate, see what we brought
forward above; here we are treating Jerome’s mind and testimony. Second, he
says that for a time presbyters governed the churches in a private manner,
attending to them jointly and governing them as it were in the forum of
conscience, and feeding them with the sacraments and the nourishment of the
divine word. But that government was in no way changed, for they retain to
this very day the power of governing in the internal forum. Jerome is
speaking of that form of government which was afterward

changed—that is, of external jurisdiction in the public forum. Third, he
says that for a time presbyters governed the churches by common counsel, but
as it were under the apostles, who reserved to themselves the episcopal power
of ordination, and indeed of external and public jurisdiction. The name
“bishop” was confused with “presbyter,” but the offices of bishop and
presbyter were not confused. Rather, the office and authority of the bishop
still resided in the apostles, while the presbyters under the apostles were
then such as they afterward were under bishops. He likewise says throughout
that presbyters never possessed the power of ordination and public
jurisdiction; under bishops they were merely counselors, not judges; and
bishops were not bound to their counsel. He illustrates his position as
follows: *Suppose,* he says, *that the commonwealth—the province of the
Jews—was governed under the Roman emperor for a time by the Sanhedrin or by
the common counsel of the elders, until the emperor placed a king over them,
as he placed Herod. It can be said that for a time that commonwealth was
governed by the common counsel of the elders, but as it were under the
emperor, who retained royal authority in his own power. It cannot be inferred
from this that the offices of the senators and the king were confused. For
the supreme authority resided in the emperor, and the senators under the king
were such as they had formerly been under the emperor.* *Jerome’s
presbyters,* says Saravia, *performed all the duties in Christ’s church which
bishops perform, with only the power of jurisdiction and ordination
excepted.* Bilson has similar statements throughout. Tilenus writes: *As to
the objection from the same Jerome that in the apostles’ time there were no
men who in their own right presided over the churches’ presbyteries, but that
each church was customarily governed by the common counsel of presbyters, we
indeed grant this without reluctance—namely, concerning those times in which
the apostles survived and, no vicars or successors yet having been appointed,
themselves visited the churches from time to time as the general and catholic
bishops of each and all.* I answer, first, that if the apostles employed the
counsel of presbyters in external government, the presbyters did not govern
merely in a private manner and as it were in the forum of conscience. For if
presbyters under the apostles were like the elders of the Sanhedrin under the
emperor before a king had been appointed, they governed not only as it were
in the forum of conscience but also in the public forum, although the
apostles were their superiors. Conversely, if you except ordination and
public jurisdiction, what will remain but the individual and private care and
oversight of each pastor? What sort of government, I ask, must be imagined in
which presbyters under the apostles governed churches by common counsel?
Jerome says that this government belonged to the presbyters without a bishop,
and that its care and solicitude were afterward conferred upon the bishop.
But it was the solicitude and care for external government in the public
forum, for ordination, and for public jurisdiction that were afterward
conferred upon the bishop. Therefore Jerome holds that before a bishop was
placed over them, presbyters governed the church by common counsel on their
own, including in respect of ordination and public jurisdiction. Jerome does
not distinguish,

but embraces all ecclesiastical government in his words when he says,
*churches were governed by the common counsel of presbyters,* as though he
had said: at the beginning all government of the church was in the hands of
presbyters. That he is speaking of public government is also plain from the
passages of Scripture, Acts 20 and Hebrews 13, from which he shows in that
same place that among the ancients presbyters and bishops were the same.
Although Bellarmine denies this concerning ordination, he admits that Jerome
held it concerning jurisdiction: *The blessed Jerome appears to have been of
the opinion that, if jurisdiction is in question, bishops are indeed greater
than presbyters, but by ecclesiastical law, not divine law; that opinion is
false.* We have already proved Jerome’s opinion true; here the question is
only the meaning of Jerome’s words. Bellarmine admits jurisdiction but
elsewhere excepts ordination because Jerome says in his letter to Evagrius:
*What does a bishop do, except ordination, which a presbyter does not do?*
But as we said above, Jerome is speaking of his own age. Others therefore
come closer. In book 2 of *On the Church*, Ioannes Antonius Delphinus teaches
that *in Jerome’s opinion, at the beginning of the newborn church all
presbyters were bishops. Not because the episcopate and presbyterate are the
same, but because in that first period each dignity was given to all priests.
Afterward, however, in order to avoid schisms, only some presbyters began to
ordain, and in this way it came about by the custom of the church that bishops
were greater than presbyters. Some say,* writes Durandus, *that at that time
both powers were conferred upon all who were placed over congregations, so
that the congregations would have ready at hand an ecclesiastical prince who
could administer all the sacraments and appoint ministers.* These men are
sounder than the English hierarchists. *But this solution does not fully
satisfy* Bellarmine, *for St. Jerome tries to uphold the dignity that
presbyters possessed in his own time against the insolence of deacons, as is
plain from the letter to Evagrius. From this he then tries to show that the
dignity of presbyters is the greatest, because it is the same as the
episcopal. But he would accomplish nothing toward proving this if he taught
only that the first presbyters were equal to bishops because they too
possessed episcopal power and were not mere presbyters. Then, if the first
presbyters were equal to bishops because they themselves were also true
bishops, the distinction which, according to Jerome’s opinion, arose
afterward would have come about because some men later received the mere
presbyterate in ordination. But St. Jerome signifies the contrary, for he
teaches that the episcopate is new and arises from the custom of the church,
not the mere presbyterate. For he himself says: “Churches were governed by the
common counsel of presbyters. But after each man,” etc.* The genuine sense of
Jerome’s words, therefore, is that the presbyterate was the episcopate, one
and the same office, and not two different offices compatible in one subject;
and that by virtue of their order and of the keys entrusted to them,
presbyters could do the same things that were afterward reserved to the
bishop. *But it is more surprising,* says Durandus, *that by the words which

could accumulate here many testimonies from those who interpret Jerome’s
position as teaching one and the same office, but I aim at brevity. Yet our
hierarchists are not ashamed to say that Jerome must be understood as holding
that bishop and presbyter, though differing in their names, are the same.
Their new and unwarlike defender, our Tilenus, writes as follows with no less
ignorance than shamelessness: *Those who perceive that little protection lies
in the indiscriminate use of the terms “bishop” and “presbyter” which we see
in Scripture, where the names “apostles” and “deacons” are also sometimes
used ἀδιακρίτως, indiscriminately—although in that sharing of names no one has
suspected equality of ministries or confusion of ranks—call Jerome’s
patronage to their aid. He says in the letter to Titus that bishops are
greater than presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord’s
ordinance. But what if in the same matter—that is, from the same sharing of
names—an occasion for error was also presented to Jerome?* We proved above
on pp. 204–206 from many testimonies that Jerome did not err in proving a
bishop and a presbyter to be the same both in fact and in name. For present
purposes it is enough that Tilenus does not openly deny that Jerome held a
bishop and a presbyter to be the same, since it is now Jerome’s position that
is under discussion. *Some try to interpret St. Jerome’s words piously, as
does St. Thomas, who writes at 2.2, question 184, article 6, that Jerome speaks
only of the names “bishop” and “presbyter,” not of the thing itself. But
certainly those words already cited, “churches were governed by the common
counsel of presbyters,” and these, “greater than presbyters more by custom
than by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance,” etc., signify something other
than the mere sharing of terms,* says Bellarmine. In the letter to Evagrius,
Jerome attempts to uphold the dignity of presbyters against the insolence of
deacons. Jerome would have argued childishly and ineptly if his argument had
been drawn merely from the sharing of names and not of office. For the
deacons could have retorted that in Scripture the names “apostles” and
“deacons” are also used ἀδιακρίτως, as Tilenus said above. Jerome says that
one man was placed over the rest as a remedy for schism, set in a higher
rank, and named bishop. If, afterward, a distinction of ranks was devised
together with the distinction of names, it follows that previously the
sharing of names was also a sharing of ranks; that is, a bishop and a
presbyter were one and the same in function and office. If it was not only a
distinction of names that was devised as a remedy for schism, but also one of
ranks, then the mere sharing of names was not the cause of schisms. The
things conferred upon bishops as a remedy for schism show that before the
remedy they were common to presbyters. Bishops are called greater than
presbyters and placed over presbyters; before the remedy, therefore, they were
neither greater nor placed over them. Moreover, Jerome says

not only that before schisms and their remedies presbyters governed churches
by common counsel without a bishop, but also that even now they ought to
govern the church, although bishops are greater than presbyters by the custom
of the church. The second period of which Jerome speaks is the period of the
schisms: *When it was said among the peoples, “I am of Paul,” etc.* The
hierarchists say that Paul testifies in 1 Corinthians 1 that this occurred in
his own time, and that Jerome held that the primacy of bishops arose from
that time. Jerome had in view not so much the Corinthian church as many other
churches, and therefore did not say *at Corinth* but *among the peoples,* and
*after each man thought those whom he had baptized,* etc. *He recounts that
the evil arose not only at Corinth but was the evil of the churches, born and
vaingloriously proclaimed among the peoples, before the whole world came to that
decision. Paul himself denies it. Although he reproves the evil of the
Corinthian church, he says not one word in that epistle about placing a bishop
over it, nor one in the second epistle. What Paul says, he says only of the
church of the Corinthians; but Jerome speaks very differently when he says,
“among the peoples,” and again, “it was decreed throughout the whole world.”
The periods are therefore different, although in speaking of an evil diffused
everywhere Jerome alluded to the particular evil which Paul reproved among
the Corinthians.* The time during which Jerome supposes that schisms
prevailed had its own breadth. It was not said among the peoples, “I am of
Paul,” etc., in one and the same year. This evil raged among peoples, and not
in one particular church, before it was decreed throughout the whole world
that one man should be placed over the rest. For it would have been
exceedingly unjust to change a divinely instituted order in all the other
churches because of the schisms of one church. The third period follows, when
a bishop had been placed over the rest. *For Jerome does not say that it
began from the time when those words were said among the peoples, but after
that time: “But after each man thought those whom he had baptized were his own
and not Christ’s, it was decreed throughout the whole world that one man
elected from the presbyters should be placed over the rest.” Was this decree
made by the apostles? Let Jerome himself answer: “Just as presbyters,”* he
says, *“know that by the custom of the church they are subject to the bishop
placed over them.” “By the custom of the church,”* says Jerome, *not by a
decree of the apostles. Then he adds, “so bishops should know that they are
greater than presbyters more by custom than by the truth of the Lord’s
ordinance.” But if the apostles had changed that order, placed bishops over
presbyteries, and forbidden churches to be governed by the common counsel of
presbyters, this would surely have been the Lord’s ordinance, since it
proceeded from Christ’s apostles—unless, perhaps, the things which the
apostles decreed are to be ascribed to custom and not to the Lord’s
ordinance. But while the apostles lived nothing in that order was changed.
For the Epistle to the Corinthians was written when Paul was in Macedonia;
but after this time he left Titus in Crete to appoint presbyters town by town.
If the apostle had thought that the order should be changed, he would not
have commanded presbyters to be appointed in every town; nor would Jerome
have brought forward testimonies from Paul, Peter, Acts, and*

[Marginalia: Iunius *de clericis* c. 15. not. 16.; *De pontific.* lib. 1. cap. 8. not. 24.; Iunius *de clericis* c. 15. not. 17.; Whitak. *de pontific.* qu. 1. ca. 3. sect. 29.]

[1623 printed p. 257 | IA leaf 269 | PDF p. 269]

*John by which he demonstrated that a presbyter is the same as a bishop. Paul
wrote his epistles to the Philippians, Timothy, and Titus, Peter wrote his,
and John wrote his, after that schism arose at Corinth; and Luke also writes
that after that schism the Ephesian presbyters were summoned by Paul to
Miletus. Since Jerome contends chiefly from these passages—Philippians 1:1; 1
Timothy 3:2; Titus 1:5, 7; 1 Peter 5:1; Acts 20:17—that a presbyter is in every
respect equal to a bishop, he could not have been so forgetful of himself as
to think that this arrangement was changed by the apostles. Similarly,
elsewhere, after he brought forward testimonies of Scripture by which he
proved that bishop and presbyter do not differ, he adds that afterward one
man was elected and placed over the others. If one man who was superior to
the presbyters was elected afterward, it was therefore not the apostles, but
some ecclesiastical custom or ordinance, that introduced that difference.
Indeed, at the end of the letter he indicates that the apostolic tradition is
that they are the same. Therefore the apostles did not decree the contrary.*
But the very words *it was decreed throughout the whole world* do not imply a
decree of some council, whether of the apostles or after the apostles’ time.
We read of no such council held while the apostles lived, either in Scripture
or outside it; and after the apostles’ time there was no council that can be
called a council of the whole world before the Council of Nicaea. Nor are *it
was decreed throughout the whole world* and *it was decreed by the council of
the whole world* the same. For a decree of a council of the whole world is
not properly called a decree made throughout the whole world. The meaning of
the words, therefore, is that when it was said among the peoples, a decree was
made throughout the whole world—that is, among the peoples, in particular
churches or provincial councils. “Throughout the whole world” is to be taken
distributively, meaning in the parts of the whole world, not
representatively, in an ecumenical council of the whole world. Who does not
see that the words *it was decreed throughout the whole world* signify the
same as *a decree was made through the whole world,* which must be understood
distributively? Moreover, the decree of a general council representative of
the whole world is made at one time and in one act; but Jerome says that *all
the solicitude was gradually conferred upon one man,* and that the bishop was
placed over the presbyters *by the custom of the church.* These words do not
suit the decree of a general council but the decrees of particular churches
or parts of the world, as some churches followed the examples of others and
decreed the same thing they had decreed. Hence Jerome infers that bishops are
greater than presbyters more by the custom of the church than by the truth of
the Lord’s ordinance—that is, more by a custom which crept into the church
than by the authority of the Scriptures or the institution of the Lord,
either through himself or through the apostles. For Jerome had said earlier:
*Does anyone suppose it to be our position, and not the position of the
Scriptures, that bishop and presbyter are one?* Then he adduces testimonies
of Scripture; finally he infers, greater by the custom of the church than by
the truth of the Lord’s ordinance—that is, than by *the position of the
Scriptures,* as he said above. Nor does he say that according to the position
of the Scriptures bishop and presbyter were one, as in the beginnings, but
that they are one,

as though he had said that according to the position of the Scriptures they
had always been one. Those, therefore, who pretend that by “the custom of the
church” Jerome meant an institution of the apostles, and by “the Lord’s
ordinance” an institution of Christ himself, not only pretend that Jerome
spoke ineptly by calling an institution of the apostles a custom of the
church, but themselves attach many absurdities to him. For, with the
necessary changes, Jerome’s words would have to be understood as follows
according to their interpretation: *Therefore, just as presbyters should know
that by a decree of the apostles they are subject to the bishop placed over
them, so bishops should know that by a decree of the apostles they are
greater than presbyters more than by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance, and
that they ought to govern the church in common*—as if schisms had arisen
under the apostles and the apostles themselves had changed the Lord’s
ordinance in order to avoid schism. *It appears to me utterly absurd to say
this,* says Saravia. *Our Lord, who is the wisdom of God, knew far better than
the apostles what was useful and necessary for preventing schisms. Just as
they did not seek to be or appear wiser than him in this matter, neither was
it their part to change the Lord’s institution because of the sin of one
church. Likewise, it is not probable that Paul changed the Lord’s institution
or, had he wished it, that he could have done so. The other apostles, who
were dispersed through various nations, did not know what was happening at
Corinth, so that they might be believed to have added their votes to Paul’s
decree. But even if we grant that the Corinthian schism came to their notice,
and that by some miracle they met together from different and exceedingly
remote parts of the world, who will be persuaded that for the sake of
avoiding schism they consented to a change in the Lord’s institution?* And
elsewhere: *Far be it from us to judge the apostles so fickle that they
changed a divinely instituted form of government, or that they themselves
observed one form and handed down another to be observed.* He rightly draws
these absurdities from that interpretation, but he is mistaken in thinking
that it was Jerome’s meaning. As we showed above, the apostles neither took
away nor could take away the power of the presbyteral order; neither could
they take away the jurisdiction which flows from the order and is exercised
even in the public forum. Let the correct interpretation of Jerome’s words
therefore stand: by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance—that is, by the
authority of the Scriptures—bishops are not greater than presbyters. *For
“truth,”* says Bilson, *among the ancient fathers is not seldom understood as
written truth. See Tertullian, On the Soldier’s Crown; Cyprian against
Stephen; and the Council of Carthage, On Baptizing Heretics.* Above, Jerome
called this truth the position of the Scriptures. They therefore devise
another interpretation, but one sufficiently foreign to Jerome’s mind, which
Tilenus expresses as follows: *But he—that is, Jerome—did not err if he meant
only this: that the names which by the truth of the Lord’s ordinance were
common to both offices were finally distinguished by ecclesiastical custom.
It should be known, however, that in the age when Jerome wrote this a
presbyter was permitted neither to preach nor to baptize or administer the
Lord’s Supper in the presence of the bishop without*

*his license and permission, as Tertullian testifies in On Baptism, Jerome
himself in Against the Luciferians, and Possidius in the Life of Augustine;
this custom rested solely upon the practice of the church, with no command of
Christ.* From the purpose and context of the words we have proved that Jerome
did not mean only that names which had been common by the truth of the Lord’s
ordinance were afterward distinguished by the custom of the church. As for
that subjection introduced in the course of time, it is true that it is not
according to the truth of the Lord’s ordinance; but Jerome attributes
absolutely all the subjection of presbyters to the custom of the church and
their equality to the Lord’s ordinance. This is plain from the arguments by
which, from the position of the Scriptures, he proves that bishop and
presbyter are one and the same, and that a bishop had not yet in any way or
even in the smallest matter been placed over a presbyter. Finally, because
they see that no other sense can be wrested out by a corrupted
interpretation, they say that Jerome is inconsistent and contradicts himself
elsewhere, as when he says that James was appointed bishop of Jerusalem by
the apostles, and Mark bishop of Alexandria in the *Catalogue*, and likewise
that it is an apostolic tradition that bishops be placed over presbyters. We
treated James, Mark, and the apostolic tradition above on pp. 140, 125,
189–190, and showed that Bellarmine denies that the *Catalogue* is Jerome’s.
Jerome rightly says on Matthew 23: *What has no authority from the Scriptures
is rejected with the same ease with which it is proved.* And on Haggai 1:
*The sword of God strikes the things which they spontaneously discover and
invent without the authority and testimonies of the Scriptures, as though by
apostolic tradition.* I conclude with the words of Spalatensis: *There are
some who wish to drag Jerome, even unwilling, into the correct position. Yet
when he ascribes to ecclesiastical custom alone and to a human decree of the
church what was (falsely) practiced by the apostles by divine law, he
certainly deviated and strayed somewhat; and on this point he can neither be
sufficiently excused nor be drawn into a different, contrary sense. His words
in the Epistle to Titus, which admit no other position, defense, or excuse,
are these: “It was decreed throughout the whole world,” etc.* And in the
words of Tilenus himself: *We judge—not only with Jerome, but also with
Lombard, Gratian, Cardinal Cusanus, and others—that the placing of one man
from the college of these pastors or presbyters over the rest and his being
specially adorned with the title of bishop arose from a human institution,
not from a divine precept. See Jerome on Titus 1 and to Evagrius; Lombard,
book 4, distinction 24; Gratian, distinction 93, chapter “We read,” and
distinction 95, chapter “Formerly,” and the following chapter; Cusanus, On
Catholic Concord, chapter 13. And in the Mirror of Antichrist, disclosing the
rise and increase of Antichrist, he cites Jerome on Titus 1, aphorism 87. And
in aphorism 88 he says: “Not only Jerome on 1 Timothy 3, but also Scripture
plainly teaches in Titus 1, Acts 20, and Philippians 1 that bishops and
presbyters were the same in fact and function; consequently that distinction
of functions under different names is of human institution, or of positive
law as they call it.”* And thus far concerning Jerome’s position.
