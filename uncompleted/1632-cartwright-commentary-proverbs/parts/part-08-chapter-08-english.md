COMMENTARY

Who does not readily perceive that it is easier to enter a brothel than the school of Christ—and, to sum up everything briefly, to join one’s mind to the devil rather than to God?

Moreover, just as they differ from one another by a long distance, so their effects are likewise entirely opposed. For the harlot and the other teachers of corruptions whisper their plagues, mutter them, and, as it were, lie in wait to whisper them into the ear; and, hesitantly, they set forth their thoughts. But wisdom cries aloud and speaks boldly, confident in her cause.

So evident is it that she is the ὁδηγός, the guide, of the whole journey from beginning to end, and like the pillar of cloud and fire that led the Israelites through the wilderness. Therefore, if anyone wishes to stray from the straight path, he deserves to perish, since wisdom is openly displayed everywhere to point out the truth to him. For if we do not ask it of passers-by, and especially of wisdom, which knows the road and all its turnings perfectly—or rather, if we do not accept the direction freely offered—who can deny that we ourselves are bringing destruction upon ourselves?

V. 4. You, O distinguished men, I call; and my voice is addressed to the sons of men.

V. 5. Understand prudence, you foolish; and you simple ones, pay attention.

V. 6. Hear, for I shall speak excellent things; and the opening of my lips shall utter what is right.

Up to this point the speech was Solomon’s; what follows is attributed to Christ, and for that reason he speaks here. The former says something while leaving much unsaid; wisdom pours out her entire spirit into the bosom of her own people, and devotes almost her whole voice and tongue to instructing them. So also Isaiah 58:1. Here too we are taught with what zeal God’s word must be presented—not coldly or weakly.

There is another distinction arising from the location. First, while the harlot and the other teachers of impiety seek hiding places and speak as though from the earth or from a pit, wisdom climbs the lofty mountains, so that she may be heard from there. For when she cries out from an elevated place, she can easily be heard; hence the pulpit is raised above the people sitting in their seats. From this we learn that the doctrine of the Gospel must be presented so that it may be understood most clearly by everyone—first, by the convenience of the place. Thus, in Acts 1, Peter preached in the midst of the disciples. This is directed against the papists, whose priests withdraw far from the people in the congregation and turn their backs on them.

Next, it must be presented in a familiar language; without this, an elevated pulpit will no more help than if someone were speaking from a cave or den. Finally, it must use a manner of speech and form of expression familiar to everyone. And just as all these things are to be supplied by the Minister, so they are to be approved and desired by the people.

Nor does it contradict this that, in a time of persecution, the Church seeks the night and secret meetings. For it seeks places that are public and well known; yet it prefers hidden places, where the truth may be gathered in completely, since these contain matters of greater excellence and difficulty. See 1 Corinthians 7. “This I say, not the Lord”; and, “this not the Lord, but I.”

Furthermore, he teaches that wisdom chooses the summit of a mountain that is near the road—the road by which people go and return, and along which travelers come and pass—so that she may direct them on their journey. For this reason she also stands at crossroads, where the broad road divides into various paths, a situation that may deceive the passing traveler unless there is a guide to show him the way. Next, she stands at the city gate, so that she may point out to the person entering the street that leads directly to the place he has proposed to visit; finally, she stands at the doorway of the house where he must rest.

In this part Christ addresses the persons whom wisdom addresses, verse 4.

The attention is divided according to the matters about which he speaks and the persons addressed.

The attention is gathered in two ways from the persons:

- By the distribution of people according to inward qualities.
- By the distribution of people according to outward qualities.

According to inward qualities, people are:

- Noble.
- Ignoble.

With respect to outward qualities, they are:

- Obscure.
- Distinguished.

Here he also understands the rich and the poor, the learned and the unlearned; for those are called noble not only by descent, but also by riches and learning. In contrast, the rich are poor and unlearned.

Here we first see that God is not content to address all people without discrimination or distinction, unless he also calls them more particularly. Thus Psalm 49:2: he did not consider it sufficient to proclaim, “Hear this, all peoples; give ear, all inhabitants of the world,” unless he also added in the following verse, “Both those born of the common people and those born of an eminent man, together the rich and the needy.”

The reason why he does this is especially worthy of observation: whatever is said to everyone in general is thought to have been said to no one. Yet, if some benefit of this life were granted to all the citizens of a city, no one would be so foolish or bashful that he would not understand that he ought to share in it and would not ask to be received into its fellowship.

Therefore the Holy Spirit designates each person almost by name. For unless wisdom had addressed the noble specifically, they would object that they are occupied with the greatest and most serious affairs, so that they cannot give time to hearing wisdom; and that they themselves are so filled with that wisdom, those writings, and such knowledge of things that they have no need to seek anything further. See Psalm 62:11.

But they are especially hindered by their own greatness and pride of spirit from submitting themselves to discipline and taking up Christ’s yoke. See the excellent passage Jeremiah 5:4. Hence it is so difficult for a rich man to enter the kingdom of heaven; and hence there are few noble and wise, etc. 1 Corinthians 1.

Therefore they must be pressed all the more earnestly to draw in and restrain their desires.

Next he addresses the common people and the weaker condition of humanity, who, through a kind of despair arising from their own lack of learning, reject the receiving of the doctrine of truth, as though it belonged only to the learned, academics, or ministers. An outstanding testimony to this is Jeremiah 5:4 and John 7:49.

For they think they were born to remain fixed to the earth and cling to it perpetually, although Scripture calls everyone and professes that even the most stupid are suitable hearers of this divine philosophy; and this is done at the beginning of this book, which was written for the very ignorant and unlearned as much as for those who are most learned.

Then they refer the whole matter to the noble, who have leisure to learn through their resources, while they themselves, barely seeking a livelihood by manual labor, say that they cannot accomplish it. Thus they cling to the earth when they are called to possess heaven; thus they occupy themselves with a rented farm for a term of years while meanwhile rejecting the inheritance! This strikes at the Roman Catholics, who forbid the common people the reading and hearing of Scripture, so that they may stray from the knowledge of God.

COMMENTARY ON PROVERBS

Those who have been imbued with those things are drawn away from them only with difficulty; from which evils the former seems the lesser.

Here one might have expected that, just as previously he divided all people into noble and ignoble, so now he would divide them into foolish and wise. But the ignorance naturally attached to human beings prevented this, since from the highest to the lowest—from the king to the woodcutter, from the academic doctor to the most unlearned idiot—of themselves they understand nothing at all concerning God in a saving manner. For since not even the nobles of this age, amid such education, choice society, and the sight, hearing, and experience of great things, know anything of the mystery of salvation, or are even able to know it (1 Corinthians 2:8–9), much less does the ignorant common crowd, who know nothing beyond fields and oxen.

Moreover, human wisdom and learning themselves do not make a man truly wise to such a degree that they may not sometimes provide a more disgraceful occasion for wandering in religion. Thus it happened to the Egyptians and Chaldeans. See also Acts 6:9 and 17. Hence we understand what is the madness of certain men who, although they have never trained their minds in the discipline of theology, nevertheless consider themselves sufficiently skilled in it. Who has ever been proficient in humane letters without attending a school or academy and applying himself to learning them? Yet we shall be able to understand those subjects more easily without a teacher than the doctrine of God. Therefore it was said of Christ with wonder: “How does this man know letters, since he has never learned them?”

- Such were the persons in whom eminence was simply sought: the things proposed for comparison now follow.

First, therefore, when Wisdom praises its doctrines to men, it does not imitate merchants, who praise their wares in order to sell them better and obtain greater profit—for if they are wise, they are wise for themselves, and their goods do not reach God—but rather a physician eager for a cure, who praises the medicine in order to draw the sick person to receive it.

The first argument is from an accompanying circumstance, verse 6: that they are principal axioms, worthy of princes in dignity, in which there is nothing futile, nothing idle, nothing childish. For even the first elements taught to children in this discipline are of exceptional excellence. See 2 Timothy 3:16; Psalm 119:130; Ecclesiastes 12:12; Proverbs 22:20. We see how eagerly and desirously Solomon’s words were received by the queen of Sheba; much more, then, ought the words of God himself to be received. And if the words of the Son of God were so excellent even under the Law, how much more under the Gospel! 1 Timothy 3:16.

Let the word נגידים be understood: these words of God excel the words of others as much as princes excel the common people. Therefore let all who are generous-minded and marked by true nobility seek them.

Next he commends them from the accompanying circumstance of rectitude, or uprightness. Thus Ecclesiastes 12:12 affirms that those writings were composed with uprightness. The reason is that they lead us by a straight line to eternal happiness, without any turning, winding, or deviation to the right or left, whereas all other writings not rarely lead—or at least can lead—along a crooked path. Therefore those are all the more intolerable who add human inventions to the words of God as though they were necessary for salvation. From this it is also clear that those who do not embrace Christ’s words are strangers to the pursuit and love of what is right.

Next, this also must be noted: every lie that is contrary to truth is wickedness. Therefore even so-called joking and “polite” lies are unlawful, whatever façade of piety or justice may cover them. See also whether the statement in the Psalm—that justice and truth kiss one another—does not fit this argument well.

Verse 8. All my sayings are just; there is nothing twisted or perverse in them.

The second accompanying quality of his word signifies, namely, justice, which he illustrates by contrast with crookedness and perversity. Two things are attributed to the judgments of the Lord: that they are true and that they are just.

Here it is appropriate to speak of the certainty of God’s promises and threats, which is chiefly the subject of these words. Here we see that all who despise or neglect God’s words are liars and addicted to lying. We also see how great is the perversity of our nature: we readily accept the testimony offered or presented to everyone except the word of God, to which the greatest faith ought to be given.

He illustrates this argument by contrast, declaring every lie to be wickedness, since his lips abhor wickedness. Here it must first be noted that true piety consists not merely in speaking truth, but in doing so from love and devotion to truth; and not merely in avoiding falsehood, but in doing so from hatred and abhorrence of falsehood. For there are many who speak truth and avoid falsehood, yet do so without love of the one or hatred of the other; they may instead act from desire for profit, fear, or honor. Nevertheless, such things are wrongly and perversely spoken.

For whoever blurts out a secret entrusted to him, or loudly exposes his brother’s hidden sin, does indeed speak the truth, but his speech is unjust, since he has uttered what ought to have remained silent. Thus the responses of soothsayers and diviners are sometimes true, but they are not just.

This quality of the divine word also stops the mouths of noisy objectors who condemn certain things in Holy Scripture as wicked and unjust—for example, the repayment of good for evil, and so forth. Just as everyone’s mouth must be closed lest they accuse God’s creature, since everything he created is affirmed to be good, so people must be restrained with a kind of double bridle lest they rage against God’s word. From this it also follows that those who despise God’s word deserve to be marked with the stain of injustice; likewise, those act wrongly who think that something contained in God’s word is of no use or does not concern them.

Verse 9. They are all plain to the prudent, and right to those who attain knowledge.
[1632 witness | PDF p. 86 | printed columns 146–147]

The final addition is that these things are set forth and made accessible, both in themselves and in their evident fairness. This is what is meant by their being light and a lamp; by their being presented to children, and indeed even to infants; whereas in the other disciplines by far the greatest part is obscure, and the more important each matter is, the more it is entangled in difficulties. Hence that saying also arose: no one has ever been found, not even among the intimate disciples of the greatest philosophers, who had grasped the secrets and mysteries of his teacher. So much more detestable is the error of the Romanists, who accuse the Scriptures of obscurity in order to keep the common people from reading them. Although it were to be wished that this error belonged to those people alone, and that many were not also found among the Evangelicals who, being of a more abject spirit, are driven away from the study of Holy Scripture by a certain despair of understanding it.

Yet, when he says that Scripture is set forth, but that it is so for the intelligent, and that it gives sound knowledge to the one who finds it, he seems to confirm precisely what the Romanists maintain. This also concerns the statement that there are certain things in Paul’s Epistles which the unlearned distort.

I answer that this understanding and knowledge is not implanted by nature, nor acquired by industry and the humane letters, but sent down from heaven and taught by the Holy Spirit; and that the unlearned are just as capable of this understanding and knowledge as the learned. Nor does that saying of Peter help them: for all the Scriptures, both the easy and the difficult, can be distorted. Therefore, if anyone here declares himself incapable of understanding Scripture, at the same moment he declares himself destitute of the Holy Spirit. See 1 Corinthians 2:14–15.

Verse 10. Receive my instruction, and not money; and choose knowledge rather than the finest refined gold.

Verse 11. For wisdom is better than carbuncles, and all desirable things cannot be compared with it.

Thus far concerning the first part, namely, the simple praise of the Word of God. Praise by contrast and comparison follows. He illustrates the Word of God by comparing it with lesser things: silver, gold, carbuncles, and finally all desirable things compacted together, as it were, into one bundle and body. Thus, in Psalm 19, after setting forth the outstanding qualities of God’s Word, he compares it first with gold and silver, but in sweetness with honey and the honeycomb.

Let us therefore examine ourselves here: in what honor and at what price do we hold the Word of God? With what zeal and love do we embrace it? If anyone says — as surely everyone will profess — that he values the Word of God more highly than all the most desirable things, Wisdom, or the Son of God, will immediately put this profession to the test, if he listens to the exhortation which he has given here: “Receive instruction, not silver,” that is, instruction rather than silver. How, then, will those give proof of this, who wish to profane the Sabbath for the small profit of two or three denarii; who, for the sake of a single feast or drinking party, wish to withdraw themselves from hearing the divine Word; who, for the sake of the smallest ceremonies — as they themselves profess them to be — wish to deprive themselves of its ministry?

In the first part, verse 1 occurs, where he says that astuteness dwells together with wisdom—that is, with the Son of God—and is perfectly implanted and innate in him, not received or drawn from elsewhere. Hence that statement in John 1, that Christ was filled with wisdom, although he had not learned letters; likewise what he himself professes: that he speaks only what he learned from the Father—that is, what, being consubstantial with the Father, he knows from all eternity.

It is well known, however, that people pursue the fountain itself rather than the streams, and willingly seek every good thing from the place where it naturally grows and arises. If that is not possible, they seek such things, in the second place, from those who have approached the fountain; and therefore they prefer to obtain and buy merchandise from merchants rather than from shopkeepers, and from the first hand, as they say, rather than from the second. Therefore let us pursue wisdom as it is committed to the Scriptures, as its genuine and authentic seat; since all human and worldly wisdom, transplanted from its native soil, does not grow successfully or flourish happily. Here water is sought as from a fountain, whereas elsewhere it is drawn from cisterns; here from a river, whereas elsewhere it is dipped from wells. Thus we cannot sufficiently marvel at human folly, when people, abandoning divine wisdom, fly over to human traditions and inventions.

The word “astuteness” is derived from a word signifying “to be naked,” by analogy with people who have stripped off their garments, so that they may bend and turn themselves more easily and nimbly in every direction. Therefore astuteness is taken here in a good sense—not for fraud or deceit, or fox-like craft and trickery, but for that wisdom which instructs a person how to enter and depart, and what path to take throughout the whole course of his calling; where to proceed, where to stand firm; where to yield, where to resist; where to speak, where to be silent, and so forth.

Thus the servant of God is said to have been made cautious by the divine law, Psalm 101. And the Savior Christ commends serpent-like astuteness to us, Matthew 10. Yet many think that piety is nothing other than stupidity, and that all godly people are stupid and incautious; they think one cannot devote oneself to piety without immediately bidding farewell to prudence and caution. Solomon, however, maintains the contrary: that true knowledge of God has joined to it a certain astuteness and cleverness. Thus David is described as a very godly man who conducted himself prudently in all things, to such an extent that Saul himself confirms that he was astute.

It is indeed true that Christ commands dove-like simplicity, which is as far as possible from fraud and deceit; nevertheless, he also commands prudence and circumspection in the conduct of affairs.

He adds that the knowledge of ingenious devices, or the most ingenious knowledge, is in wisdom's possession. If there is any difference between this and the preceding term, it seems to be this: that the former astuteness is the wisdom of God by which we avoid dangers prepared for us and traps that have been laid; whereas the knowledge of clever devices is that wisdom of God which supplies clever and acute thoughts, either for bringing some good to us or for inflicting evil upon our enemies.

Verse 13. The reverence of Jehovah is to hate evil; I hate pride and loftiness, the evil way, and finally a mouth devoted to perversities.

Here someone may perhaps object: the wisdom of which it was previously said that it is hidden,

to be obscure, subtle, and full of speculation; to which he replies that this wisdom is not so much contemplative as practical. For its greatest part, indeed almost the whole of it, or at least something like a compendium of the whole, is contained in the fear and reverence of God; concerning which see above. He defines this by synecdoche as hatred of evil, thereby including also love of good. Here it must be noted who are truly godly: namely, those who do not merely avoid evil and do good, but who flee evil out of hatred for evil and pursue good out of love for good. For you often see people who do good or avoid evil because of their habits or upbringing, moved by the rewards or punishments of the laws, by the disgrace or honor set before them, or drawn on by the reputation or popular favor they seek; but those who are truly godly do both, so that—

Here also it must be noted that the sum of godliness consists in this: that a person should hate and love those things which God pursues with hatred and love. Hence also that statement: “You have this, that you hate the works of the Nicolaitans, which I also hate.” Revelation 2. For just as among human beings friendship is recognized by their common avoidance and love, so it is fitting that our union with God should likewise be proved in the same way.

**Verse 14.** Mine is counsel; whatever is, mine is prudence; mine is strength.

- It would be little to possess very sharp thoughts and inventions, unless these same things are strengthened and established by counsel. Thus Solomon says: “Thoughts without counsel are scattered; but those established by an abundance of counsellors shall stand,” Proverbs 15:22.
- Now Christ professes this counsel as his own and proper counsel, since he has been appointed by the Father as the wonderful Counsellor, Isaiah 9. Therefore, if anyone wishes to receive sound and well-considered counsel, let him come to Christ, who reveals himself in his word.
- For just as the counsels of God are the firmest of all and attain their desired outcome, while the counsels of men are utterly broken and scattered, Psalm 33:10, so that counsel which Christ gave us in his word, as proceeding from him, comes most nearly to firmness.
- Hence the statement: “He prospers in his way, and all that he undertakes,” Psalm 1. Excellent examples of this are found in Joseph, Jacob, David, and other eminent servants of God.
- On the contrary, Scripture testifies that the wicked are held fast in their own crafty counsels.
- Therefore the counsel provided by Christ is called solid and, so to speak, substantial counsel.
- Hence David confirms that God’s doctrine had been his counsel and support, Psalm 119.
- For Christ, the Son of God, is not otherwise given to us by the Father as Counsellor except as he is given to us in his word—

The evil which he first stated in general he now explains by its kinds and parts. First, by pride and arrogance he appears to designate the hidden thoughts of the mind. He appropriately and suitably mentions inward pride and arrogance rather than the other corruptions of the mind, because arrogance and pride are the root, or source, and mother of the other vices. Paul agrees with this in Ephesians 4, where, intending to raise up the structure and edifice of good works, he lays humility as the foundation beneath them all. To these thoughts he adds the way of evil, that is, wicked deeds. Finally, he adds perversity of the mouth, or wicked speech, especially speech that is hypocritical, when the mind thinks one thing while the tongue speaks another.

let us lend an ear to the counselor. Therefore, whether in turning aside evils brought upon us from elsewhere, or in pursuing good things, the wisdom of God in his word must be employed by us in our counsels.

He adds the same prudence. By which saying, just as above he signifies wisdom in forming counsels, so here he signifies prudence in carrying them out and administering them. For however wisely counsels may have been undertaken, yet unless they are managed with a certain prudent ἀκριβείᾳ and dispensation, they become empty and ineffectual. Christ also diligently supplies this wisdom to his own by his words.

He adds that strength and powers, and the sinews by which counsels are brought to their outcome and result, are held with him. For although counsel has more weight for carrying out affairs than strength, nevertheless nothing can be accomplished or brought to completion without words, Isa. 39. v. 5.

and if we wish to become sharers in these things, to whom but to Christ, the same fountain, should we betake ourselves? Yet to him we cannot come except in the wisdom of his word. For he is the God of hosts; his are the chariots and horsemen; his are silver and gold; he is the one who supplied David with the strength by which the bow of steel was broken by his arms; who supplied that swiftness by which he could leap over a wall; and who made his feet swift as the feet of a deer.

Finally, he is the one by whose benefit five put five hundred, a hundred a thousand, indeed one a thousand, and finally a few put many to flight. Therefore, if we seek noble thoughts, sound counsels, prudent administration of counsels, and finally strength, either we obtain these things in Christ, through devotion to and love of his word, or there will be no place where we may seek them.

V. 15. Through me kings reign, and rulers decree justice.

V. 16. Through me princes exercise princely rule, and all the noble judges of the earth.

All these things, namely, shrewdness, sharp thoughts, counsel, prudence, and finally strength, he teaches can abundantly be obtained by any godly person in Christ. The argument from the greater: because kings, senators, and all noble judges receive from him that by which they may successfully administer governments, kingdoms, and commonwealths; so that we ought not to doubt that we shall be able to conduct and manage our private affairs and matters of lesser importance, provided that in conducting them we employ God in our counsels. The whole sacred history is an abundant witness of this, teaching that those commonwealths were successfully administered which were governed according to the prescription of the word by godly kings, lords, and other magistrates. If, therefore, large commonwealths have been rightly and successfully administered, like the ships of the Argonauts, much more will towns, families, and finally the individual heads of men, by his rule and guidance, like little boats, bring their affairs to a desired outcome. And thus far concerning the first part; the second part follows, concerning wisdom communicated for blessedness.

V. 17. I find those who love me in spirit, and those who diligently seek me.

He indicates that the source of the communication of all these good things, and therefore of perfect and accumulated blessedness, is the love of God, which, as it were, is first the foundation and root: and of our election before the foundations of the world were laid: much less of the things which God communicates with us in this life. This love indeed rests on none of our works, but on the good pleasure of God alone; and thus the Apostle judges that the love of God alone is the cause why he gave himself for him: Gal. 2, 20, in whom and for whose sake all things are communicated to us. Yet this may appear foreign to it.
[1632 witness | PDF p. 90 | printed columns 154–155]

It may appear that he subjects himself to those who love him, loving them; as though the first love of God toward us arose from our love toward him: whereas it is quite otherwise: since, if he loved Jacob before he had done anything good or evil; so also all the elect. Hence that saying: in this consists the love of God, not that we loved him; but that he loved us, and sent his Son as a propitiation for our sins. 1 John 4:10. Rom. 5:6, 7, 8, 11. What he therefore says, “those who love God,” that he loves them, is set forth for the singular consolation of the godly: so that he may make them more certain of God’s fatherly love. For if God loved us when we were his enemies, and shared his good things with us; much more will he do this for us who have been reconciled and adopted as sons. Rom. 5:9, 10.

From this it is evident that this magnificence of God is not open to all: but has been set forth by the elders only to the godly. And if it should be objected that no one is so lost and of such abandoned life that he does not love God: he immediately replies that the love of God is proved by its effect, namely, seeking him, and that earnestly and promptly; for in this way the upright are easily known from the wicked, the godly from the ungodly. Ps. 27. | Seek my face: your face I will seek, O Lord. | For when people either do not seek God, or seek him coldly, while they eagerly pursue the world and the pleasures of the world; they openly show that they are driven more by love of the world than of God. Thus Ps. 97. He distinguishes the godly by another effect of the love of God, when he says: [you who love God, hate evil.]

In this duty he confirms us by adding the promise that we shall find him. Just as Christ also teaches, seek and you shall find, etc. Mar. This indeed was not said as though we should anticipate God and find him before he finds us, since in Is. chap. 65. v. 1. he teaches that God was found by those who did not seek him: but in order to remove a scruple from the minds of the godly, and to make them certain that God comes to meet those who seek him, since he has offered himself to meet those fleeing from him: as is clear in Adam, Paul, and very many others.

V. 18. Riches and honor are with me, enduring substance and righteousness.

By another argument he encourages and confirms us in his love, and in seeking him, from the accompanying promise of riches and honors, which are with him. Thus Christ: seek first the kingdom of God, and then all these things shall be added to you, Matt. 6. Likewise, he who did not spare his only-begotten Son, but gave him for us; how will he not also give all things with him? Rom. 8. Here, therefore, let us note the perversity of human beings, who first seek riches and honors; then God: first the advantages of this life; then the kingdom of heaven: first freedom from evils; then the Son of God, whereas the opposite ought to be done, thereby imitating the loves of prostitutes, who are more attracted by the loves, and other gifts and pledges, of their lovers, than by the lovers themselves. Moreover, he also imposes this upon them, that they suppose the followers of godliness to be excluded from riches and honors: whereas the right to and possession of these belong to the godly, as to the true worshipers of God, whose alone it is to bestow honors and riches. For who is so mad as to think that the royal treasuries ought to be more open to enemies and rebels than to his own friends and citizens? Further, by a third argument he stirs us up to the love and seeking of God, from the accompanying promise of righteousness, which he first magnifies by comparison with durable and lasting riches. As Paul teaches godliness to be great riches, 1 Tim. 6: likewise Christ, Luke 12:21. So is everyone who gathers treasures for himself and is not rich toward God.

In worldly works, however, not production alone is to be considered, but also arrangement and adornment. For although production is attributed to these sublunary things, and fitting adornment is applied to the heavens, both nevertheless belong to all God’s works. Along with these we are also admonished to observe that continuity which, as it is conspicuous in all creatures, so in the position of the earth, the suspension of the clouds, and the sea’s vast cohesion, so firmly maintained, indicates a constant providence—one especially wondrous to behold, Job 26; Jeremiah 5:22.

The use of all these works is that we may learn to reverence and worship God because of his power; and this we ought to do all the more when we contemplate God himself, in whom there is far more power than he has ever yet exercised. Nor ought we merely to reverence God, but also to love him because of his infinite wisdom, which shines forth in these same works. Having first said that he existed in the ways of God, he then declares this specifically through all the kinds of works in which God’s way is displayed.

All these things, therefore, confirm one point and tend toward it: that they may demonstrate the eternity of the divine Word—particularly that effective power by which it leads either to salvation or to destruction. This Word is immortal seed, 1 Peter 1:23, and therefore makes those who receive it participants in immortal glory; while to those by whom it is rejected it also brings immortal corruption. Thus, in Hebrews, the Apostle, having first declared Christ’s excellence in the opening chapter, infers at the beginning of the second chapter that we ought to attend all the more to the things we have heard, lest at any time we should drift away.

From that mutual delight which existed between the Father and the Son from eternity, we can receive divine blessedness—and also our own happiness, since we have Christ acting and interceding for us, in whom the Father has delighted and rested from eternity.

The exhortation is that we obey God in his word. For when God requires that he be heard, he does not require merely the bare hearing of the word, but obedience joined with hearing. Therefore here he uses that construction of the verb which signifies obedience; and in the other half-verse, by what he had previously said, “Hear me,” he calls it the observance of his ways. But he uses the verb “to hear” for this reason: that through hearing one proceeds to obedience. Thus in Psalm 40, “the piercing of the ear,” or, as the Apostle speaks, the preparation of Christ, signifies obedience set in opposition to the legal sacrifices. Therefore ἠθέλησας, “you desired,” is added in the same Psalm: Christ delights in obedience to the divine will, and God’s doctrine is, as it were, engraved in the midst of his inward parts. For just as a harvest comes through tilling, offspring through intercourse, a tree through planting, and nourishment through eating food, so obedience comes through hearing the word.

Accordingly, it is equally absurd for anyone to expect obedience from one who is not occupied with hearing the divine word, as to expect a plentiful harvest from that soil which has neither received seed into its bosom nor drunk in the dew of heaven. Hence in the Papacy, where works are so loudly proclaimed, especially almsgiving, there is no obedience and no obedience of faith; nor is there any among those who boast of being Evangelicals while they are held by no zeal or concern for hearing the word.

A twofold reason is attached. The first is drawn from the relation of sons: the title is not attributed to them unconditionally, but on this condition, that they obey God. For just as the Apostle says that God presents himself as a father to those who do not despise his correction, Hebrews 12; and likewise Proverbs 3:11 and 1 John 3:1, so here he offers himself as a father to those who give themselves over to his obedience. Therefore, since the dignity of God’s children is exceedingly great and honorable, let us strive by obedience arising from hearing the word to preserve this dignity. Otherwise we shall indeed be called sons, but, as in Isaiah 1:2, “rebellious and transgressing sons,” and so forth.

The second reason is drawn from the happiness which comes to those who obey; so Luke 11:21, 28. Many have devoted great effort to seeking happiness: everyone desires happiness, and they are ready to obtain it by great journeys, labors, and expenses. What perversity, then, in human beings, who reject what they desire so eagerly when it is offered in the observance of these ways! Yet we will not therefore say that our obedience is the cause of happiness, although those who obey are happy: for they are accompanied by salvation, which is not the cause of salvation; just as dryness accompanies the heat and burning of fire, and coldness accompanies the moisture of water, and so forth. This is expressly noted afterward, verse 35, lest anyone should ascribe it to the merit of works.

**Verse 33.** First he expounds obedience, and argues for it from its chief and most difficult aspect, as though, after comparing the greater matter, he were drawing the conclusion. It is as if he reasoned in this way: Whoever accepts and admits correction, and does not withdraw himself from obedience to God because of chastisement, will be obedient to God in every duty. Since, therefore, it is an infinite matter to travel through all God’s ways and paths, in which he ought to show himself faithful and obedient, let him take the experience of obedience especially in this one thing: whether he remains constant and steadfast when suffering hardships for the truth.

Proverbs 24 teaches that the strength of the man who has shown himself cowardly and dissolute in the day of distress is small; and, conversely, that the one who perseveres in the day of affliction possesses unconquerable virtue. 2 Timothy 2:11; James 1:12. Likewise Romans 5: patience produces proof, that is, it shows a man to have been approved. In Matthew 13, the good soil is that which does not dry up in the heat of persecution, especially if anyone does not withdraw himself, Hebrews 10:38.

Then he commends this patience and long-suffering from the wisdom joined to it, or rather from what he calls wisdom itself. See James 1:5. See also whether Proverbs 3:13 may be referred here. In this way he confronts the corrupt opinion of the common people, who attribute folly and stupidity to anyone who patiently endures injuries.

**Verse 34.** He notes another part of obedient hearing in extended and earnest attention to, and investigation of, the divine will. He illustrates this by the likeness of those who diligently watch the doors of advocates or judges morning and evening, and keep watch at them. See Matthew 7: Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and you shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you. Likewise 1 Samuel 2, concerning the women keeping watch at the door of the tabernacle. An excellent example of this exists in Cornelius, Acts 10. See also Ezekiel 46:10, where the king is commanded to be present at the temple at the beginning of the liturgy and to remain until the end. To this may be added what he taught earlier in verse 17 of this chapter, where he illustrates this doctrine with another comparison: with those who rise early in the morning to obtain something worth their labor.

**Verse 35.** The second part follows, concerning the happiness proceeding from obedience. This happiness is argued from the eternal life joined to it, which those who obey and attain true wisdom receive. Everyone desires life, and especially a long life, which they would buy at any price, although, because of the many miseries mixed with it, this life is more like death and rather the shadow of life than life itself—especially the life that exists outside Christ. “The widow who lives for pleasure is dead while she lives,” 1 Timothy 5. And, “Let the dead bury their dead,” Matthew 9.

**Verse 36.** Another argument is taken from the contrary: the unhappiness and death of those who oppose this wisdom of God and sin against it. Here they are called αὐτοκτόνοι, self-killers, and haters of themselves. Whoever preserves his soul will lose it; and whoever loves it beyond his duty hates it. Thus he teaches what is said in Proverbs 9:12: if you are wise, you are wise for yourself; but if you act wickedly, you alone shall bear it—understand, the loss.
