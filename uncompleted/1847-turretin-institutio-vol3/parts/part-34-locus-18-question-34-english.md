QUESTION XXXIV.

ON THE CIVIL GOVERNMENT OF THE CHURCH.

What right does the Christian magistrate possess concerning sacred things, and
does care and cognizance of religion pertain to him in any respect? We affirm.

[Marginalia: On the civil government of the church.]

I. Since we have spoken of the church’s ecclesiastical government, something
must be added concerning its civil government. A weighty question is raised
about this, in whose examination and decision errors have been committed in
different ways, both by excess and by defect.

[Marginalia: Some err by excess.]

II. Those err by excess who claim all ecclesiastical power for the magistrate.
By suppressing the liberty of the ministry, they put the censer into Uzziah’s
hands and hold that no power belongs to pastors except what is derived from the
magistrate.

[Marginalia: Others by defect.]

Those err by defect who remove him from every care of ecclesiastical matters,
so that he takes no concern at all for what anyone worships and permits
everyone unrestricted power to do and say anything whatever in the cause of
religion. So do those who, although they assign to him the care of nurturing
and defending the church so that he may foster her kindly and protect her
powerfully, nevertheless leave him no cognizance and no judgment concerning
religion, but execution alone. They rest upon the premise that this cognizance
and judgment concerning matters of faith belong peculiarly to the
ecclesiastical order, whose decrees the magistrate is bound to accept and
execute. This is the doctrine of the papists, which Bellarmine sets forth in
book 3, *On the Clergy*, chapter 17.

[Marginalia: The orthodox hold the middle.]

III. Holding the middle between the two extremes, the orthodox maintain that a
pious and faithful magistrate neither can nor ought to be excluded from every
care of religion and sacred things, which God has entrusted to him. Yet this
right must be circumscribed by definite limits, lest the duties of the
ecclesiastical and civil orders be confused, but the parts properly belonging
to each be left to it. We embrace this in two propositions.

[Marginalia: First proposition: that a right concerning sacred things belongs
to the magistrate. It is proved: 1. From the divine command. Deuteronomy
17:18.]

IV. First proposition: “A manifold right concerning sacred things belongs to
the Christian magistrate.” It is proved, 1. From the divine command. Since the
guardianship of the divine law has been entrusted to him, he ought therefore
to care no less for piety and the worship of God, which are commanded by the
first table, than for justice and charity, which are commanded by the second,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 269 | IA leaf 303 | PDF p. 303]

table. Deuteronomy 17:18 says: “After the king has sat upon the throne of his
kingdom, he shall write for himself a scroll of this law, taking a copy from
the priests; and he shall have it with him and read it all the days of his
life, so that he may learn to fear the Lord his God and keep the words of this
law.” This is repeated in Joshua 1:8, and at Joash’s solemn inauguration the
book of the law is given to him by Jehoiada so that he may understand that its
guardianship has been committed to him (2 Kings 11:12). The same command is
confirmed from other passages where kings and princes are commanded to be
instructed, to serve the Lord with fear, and to kiss the Son (Ps. 2:10); where
they are said to be coming to Christ and bowing down before him (Ps.
72:10–11); and where they are said to be appointed so that we may lead a quiet
life in all piety and honesty (1 Tim. 2:2), which cannot be obtained without
special care for religion.

[Marginalia: 2. From the titles given to the magistrate.]

V. 2. It is proved from the titles and designations given to the magistrate in
Scripture. (1) They are called the church’s nursing fathers: “Kings shall be
your nursing fathers, and queens your nurses” (Isa. 49:23; see also Isa.
60:10). This pertains not only to procuring temporal goods for her, but also
spiritual and heavenly goods, which are far more necessary. (2) They are
called “gods” (Ps. 82:6), because they bear the mark of God’s power and rule
the peoples in his name and authority. They ought therefore to conduct
themselves as God’s deputies by promoting his glory before all things and
taking care that their subjects pay him the tribute and tax owed in lawful and
pure worship. (3) They are called “shepherds” (Isa. 44:28), not because they
feed the belly alone, but because they ought to be solicitous for the people’s
salvation by taking care that their subjects are led to the saving pastures of
God’s Word. (4) They are called “fathers” (1 Sam. 24:12), who ought to care for
their people so that provision is made for them not only in food and clothing,
but especially in spiritual nourishment and instruction in the fear of the
Lord.

[Marginalia: 3. From reasons.]

VI. 3. It is proved from reasons. (1) The welfare of the commonwealth and all
things pertaining to it have been committed to the magistrate, and among these
the care of religion and sacred things is preeminent. Hence the Philosopher,
in book 7 of the *Politics*, chapter 8, assigns the foremost place among the
things without which a city cannot subsist to “care concerning the divine.”
Plato, in book 2 of the *Republic*, says that “the principal care in the
commonwealth must be undertaken concerning religion”; and in book 6 he says
that it is “the bond of every society and the support of rightly sanctioned
laws.” Cicero, in book 2 of *On the Nature of the Gods*, calls religion “the
foundation of human society.” (2) The magistrate ought to procure every good
for his subjects, for whose sake he was appointed, and to render an account of
it to God; but religion and the things pertaining to it are unquestionably
their chief good. (3) Commonwealths are the church’s lodging places; the
magistrate therefore ought to take care that it goes well with her.

[Marginalia: 4. From the examples of kings.]

VII. 4. It is proved from the approved examples of kings and magistrates in
both the Old and New Testaments. Moses, exercising the office of king among the
people (Deut. 33:4), established religion by divine authority, promulgated the
law, inaugurated Aaron, and prescribed laws to the whole people and the order
of priests. Joshua commanded the Levites, ordered the ark to be carried
around, saw to the people’s circumcision, and renewed the covenant. David saw
to the ark’s return from the house of Obed-edom to his own, appointed singers,
distributed the Levites’ duties through fixed rotations (1 Chron. 15:16;
23:6), and prepared everything necessary for the temple’s construction (1
Chron. 22:2). Solomon removed Abiathar from office, substituted Zadok (1 Kings
2:27), built the temple (1 Kings 6), brought the ark into it with solemn pomp,
and consecrated it with prayers and sacrifices (1 Kings 8). Asa overturned the
altars of foreign worship and cut down the images and groves (1 Kings 15:12,
15; 2 Chron. 14:4). Jehoshaphat procured a reformation and established an
ecclesiastical and civil council (2 Chron. 19). Joash restored the temple (2
Kings 12:4). Hezekiah destroyed the high places and broke in pieces

[1847 vol. III printed p. 270 | IA leaf 304 | PDF p. 304]

the images and the bronze serpent to which the Israelites burned incense (2
Kings 18). He opened and restored the doors of God’s house, instituted a
reformation of worship and purification of the temple, entered into a covenant
with the Lord (2 Chron. 29), and brought the people back to the true worship of
God (chapter 30). Josiah published the book of the law found by Hilkiah the
priest and saw that it was read before the whole people (2 Kings 23; 2 Chron.
34), celebrated Passover, and exhorted the priests to their duty (chapter 35).
To these can be added the examples of pious emperors—Constantine the Great,
both Theodosiuses, Arcadius and Honorius, Valentinian, Manian, Justinian, and
others—who everywhere gave evidence of their piety in caring for religion, as
is clear from ecclesiastical history and the *Novels*. Hence these sayings of
Constantine, worthy to be preserved in cedar, in a letter written to the
churches after the Nicene Synod, recorded by Eusebius in book 3 of his *Life*:
“I judged that before all things I must strive for this: that one faith among
the most blessed people, sincere charity, and religion toward God, the author
of all, be observed without disagreement anywhere in the church.” Honorius
says, “Among the greatest concerns of our empire, reverence for the catholic
law is either the first or the only one; for we undertake nothing else in the
labors of war or arrange anything else in the counsels of peace, except that
the devout people of our world preserve the true worship of God.” Arcadius
says to Flavian, “No matters hold us equally solicitous as the care of the holy
churches and the firmest foundation of faith in Christ, through which we are
saved and barbarian nations are subjected to us. For we do not accomplish
these things by our own strength or by the power of horses and chariots of war,
but, strengthened by the power of God, all things turn out prosperously for
us.” Ambrose commends Theodosius in this respect, because “when near death he
took greater care for the church than for the empire.” Justinian says in
*Novel* 3, “Our care for those things which benefit the most holy churches is
no less than our care for the soul itself.”

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. It is one thing rashly to usurp another’s calling and another thing
lawfully to administer the parts of an office entrusted to oneself. Uzzah and
Uzziah rashly invaded another’s calling. But when princes care for religion,
they administer the parts of the office entrusted to them.

IX. Although care for religion belongs to the magistrate, civil and
ecclesiastical power are not therefore confused, because they are occupied
with it in different ways and the parts belonging to the one differ from those
belonging to the other. The former is occupied only with outward matters, with
respect to the external disposition and order of things pertaining to God’s
worship; the latter is occupied with inward matters, namely the administration
of things pertaining to God’s worship. A person does not exercise the ministry
of the Word when he admonishes and corrects a minister who errs in doctrine or
offends in life and removes him if he is obstinate or scandalous. A person does
not baptize when he prohibits baptism from being profaned and takes care that
holy things are done in a holy manner.

X. Just as bishops may not wield the sword, so princes and civil rulers may
not put their hand to the censer with respect to the preaching of the Word,
the administration of the sacraments, and other things which formally belong
to pastors and are essential to their office. But it does not follow from this
that no other parts concerning the church’s government belong to a faithful
magistrate. For although bishops may not handle civil matters or pronounce law
in court, they nevertheless may and ought to admonish and exhort the magistrate
concerning his duty, to reprove him wherever he fails in it, and to denounce
God’s judgment. In turn, although the preaching of the Word does not belong to
magistrates, they may nevertheless admonish and reprove bishops and pastors who
neglect or stray from their duty. Indeed, they may bring those who exceed
bounds back into order and take care that the ministry is not corrupted and
religion suffers no loss.

XI. Although Christ entrusted his church not to Tiberius but to Peter, he

[1847 vol. III printed p. 271 | IA leaf 305 | PDF p. 305]

does not exclude princes from caring for religion, for he called them nursing
fathers; nor did the one who said “Kiss the Son” reject kings as such. The
ministry of the Word was entrusted to pastors, but care of the commonwealth was
no less entrusted to the magistrate. If the church dwells within that
commonwealth, why should the pious magistrate as such not provide lodging for
the church and drive away the wolves who ravage the flock under the name of
pastors? Otherwise, by the same argument, I will deny that the defense of
religion belongs to the magistrate, because Christ gave Tiberius no commands
concerning religion.

[Marginalia: Second proposition: the magistrate possesses no absolute right
over sacred things, but a limited one. In what it does not consist, by way of
removal.]

XII. Second proposition: “Although a right concerning sacred things belongs
to the magistrate, it is not absolute but limited, circumscribed by definite
boundaries, and far different from the right of pastors.” This can be shown
(1) negatively, by way of removal, in what it does not consist, and (2)
positively, by way of position, in what it consists. With respect to the
former, (1) he cannot frame new articles of faith or institute and command new
forms of worship, because this is self-chosen worship condemned by God’s Word
(Isa. 29:13; Matt. 15:9; Col. 2, final verse), subjected to anathema (Gal.
1:8–9), and punished by various examples of divine judgment in Jeroboam, Ahab,
and Nebuchadnezzar. (2) It does not pertain to him to preach the Word and
administer the sacraments, because these are the formal, proper, and essential
acts of the ministry (Matt. 28:19–20), which no one ought to exercise without
a calling (Heb. 5:4; 1 Cor. 7:20).

XIII. (3) He cannot exercise ecclesiastical discipline by the authority of the
ecclesiastical keys, closing or opening the kingdom of heaven, because those
keys were given to the church or presbytery (Matt. 16 and 18:18), not to the
magistrate, whose weapons are not spiritual but carnal. (4) He cannot
prescribe to ministers the form of preaching or administering the sacraments,
because the pastor has that immediate authority from Christ, not from the
magistrate. Nor can he command them to do anything contrary to the rules and
ordinances of the ministry, any more than he can command this or that person
to act contrary to his calling, or command a physician to give hellebore
instead of rhubarb to someone suffering from fever. Such things belong to the
office of the ministry, which is of divine right. (5) In judging and
constituting ecclesiastical matters, he neither can nor ought to command
anything without consulting and hearing the pastors, any more than if he were
to prescribe something pertaining to the medical art concerning a college of
physicians and the shops of apothecaries without hearing them. The same must
be maintained concerning controversies of religion, which he ought not to
recall to his judgment alone and determine by his authority, although by
reason of his calling he can and ought to take cognizance of religion and
distinguish true religion from false.

[Marginalia: In what it consists, by way of position.]

XIV. Positively, by way of position, many things concerning sacred matters
belong to the magistrate. (1) According to the prescription of God’s Word, he
ought to establish sacred doctrine and the pure worship of God in the
commonwealth, faithfully preserve it once established, or restore and reform
it if it has collapsed, as is clear from the passages cited above concerning
Asa, Jehoshaphat, Josiah, Joash, and Hezekiah. Hence one cannot but praise the
purpose of pious princes and Christian magistrates by which they applied their
hand to the Reformation, which was expected in vain from the Roman curia, and
devoted all their endeavors to fostering and sustaining it. (2) To the best
of his ability he ought to protect the church, restrain heretics and disturbers
of ecclesiastical peace, promote God’s glory, defend and propagate true
religion, and prevent a confusion of religions. (3) Where the ministry of the
Word and sacraments rightly administered according to God’s Word does not
exist, he ought to procure it; where it exists, he ought to regard it with
reverence and support and protect it honorably. He ought also to open and
foster schools as nurseries of the commonwealth and the church, in which the
young may be instructed and formed. (4) With all diligence he ought to provide
that every minister performs his duty, to confirm the diligent, stir up the
sluggish, and, according to ecclesiastical canons or civil laws,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 272 | IA leaf 306 | PDF p. 306]

take action against offenders. (5) He ought to bring about the lawful
sanctioning of solemn formulas and
ecclesiastical constitutions which define the church’s doctrine and government
according to the rule of Scripture, and to preserve them inviolate once
sanctioned. (6) He ought authoritatively to establish the church’s ordinary
assemblies, in which ecclesiastical business concerning doctrine, discipline,
and the church’s order is transacted from God’s Word. To preserve purity of
doctrine and holiness of discipline and to judge controversies of religion,
he ought, in addition to ordinary assemblies, to convene provincial and
national synods where necessary, moderate their proceedings and judgments by
his counsel, and further confirm and protect them by his authority.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XV. One kind of ecclesiastical power is internal, direct, and formal and is
occupied with the administration and exercise of sacred things, such as the
preaching of the Word, administration of the sacraments, and dispensation of
the keys. Another kind is external, indirect, and only objectively
ecclesiastical; it is occupied with sacred things with respect to their
procurement and disposition, so that all things may be done decently and in
order in God’s house. The first belongs to pastors alone, to whom Christ
entrusted his church and gave the keys of the kingdom of heaven. The latter
belongs to magistrates and Christian princes insofar as they ought to be
guardians of both tables. Thus in a well-ordered household the father disposes
and arranges all things whose execution and administration belong to members
of the household. To this pertains the distinction between “a bishop outside”
and “a bishop inside,” which Emperor Constantine employed between himself and
the church’s pastors: “You are bishops of those within the church, while I am
a bishop of those outside, appointed by God”—as recorded by Eusebius, book 4
of the *Life of Constantine*, chapter 24.

XVI. Although in ancient times the same people were kings and priests, not
only among the pagans but also sometimes among God’s people, and although all
the firstborn accordingly exercised among their people a certain appearance
of kingship and priesthood, this type of Christ—whom alone the church
acknowledges as both her King and High Priest—was abrogated along with the
other ceremonies. Consequently, it is not lawful to join those two
administrations in the same person. The example of Moses which is brought
forward is extraordinary; after his brother Aaron was inaugurated and
established as high priest, Moses did not touch the priest’s duties. Agreement
and coordination between these administrations does not bring it about that
both must be entrusted to the same person, who is scarcely fit enough for
either one. Nor is it expedient for the public that they be mixed and sacred
things managed in a human manner.

XVII. Although civil power, as external and objective concerning sacred
things, presupposes formal, proper, and intrinsic ecclesiastical power and its
exercise, it is nevertheless always first and antecedent in its own kind.
Indeed, if we wish to speak accurately, it is the only such power, because no
other public, supreme, and architectonic power precedes, accompanies, or
follows it. It is followed and accompanied only by the submission and
obedience of all citizens and the subordinate and ministerial power of civil
officials. Nor, if the magistrate is subjected to Christ the Head of the
church and to his Word, is he thereby properly subjected to Christ’s minister
who proclaims that Word. A prince is subordinate to the king, but not to the
herald or messenger through whom the king’s commands are conveyed to him. It
is one thing to obey and be subjected to someone as lord, and another to hear
someone and obey his admonitions. An unlearned father obeys his son who is
skilled in law, medicine, or theology, especially if he is furnished with a
doctoral faculty. A prince obeys a counselor and a college of judges when a
cause is decided against him in favor of some citizen; nevertheless, he always
remains the supreme lord.

XVIII. Civil power is occupied with something either directly and immediately
or indirectly, mediately, and consequently. In the former way it is occupied
absolutely with the outward person and the matters of daily life without any
distinction between faith and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 273 | IA leaf 307 | PDF p. 307]

unbelief and with all civil affairs. In the latter way it is occupied with
sacred and spiritual things, not with respect to their spiritual and
ecclesiastical character as such—which is not the immediate object of civil
power as such—but with respect to some external adjunct of place, time,
persons, or other circumstances which are in themselves the object of civil
power.

XIX. Although in our doctrine the magistrate does not have supreme and
unlimited authority concerning sacred things, it does not follow that our
doctrine does not differ from the papists’ doctrine. (1) According to us, the
magistrate protects the church as a lord—that is, as one furnished by God with
ruling power—but according to the papists, only as the pope’s minister. (2)
According to us, the Christian magistrate has the right to take cognizance of
and judge matters of faith, at least by a discriminating and approving
judgment: ought he to confirm the church’s judgments by his authority and
command their execution? But according to the papists, the church alone judges
with a supreme, definitive, and infallible judgment which the magistrate, no
less than private persons, is bound simply and with implicit faith to embrace
and command to be executed, without any preceding judgment of discretion upon
the church’s judgment. He ought to draw the sword at the priest’s nod and be a
blind executor of the church’s decrees whenever the church judges it
expedient, whether or not the circumstances and interests of the commonwealth
permit it. (3) According to us, if the magistrate abuses his power in his
judgment concerning ecclesiastical matters, he does not cease to enjoy the
title of that power by human and divine right. The church as such cannot
resist him or repudiate his authority, much less abolish his title and power.
Her weapons are prayers and tears, and she opposes him only by suffering. But
according to the papists, the church has power to bring a magistrate who, in
her judgment, abuses his power back into order, either through his own
subjects, whom she absolves from their oath of allegiance, or through
neighboring princes, after exposing his territories to plunder or
extermination.

XX. The name “Head of the Church,” which is given to the king of England, is
not understood of intrinsic, formal, and spiritual ecclesiastical power, but
of power external, objective, or defensive concerning ecclesiastical matters.
This is clear from the articles of religion approved first by the London Synod
in 1562 and then by the public consent of the queen and estates of the
kingdom. Article 37 reads: “When we attribute supreme government to the royal
majesty—a title by which we understand the minds of certain slanderers to be
offended—we do not give our kings the administration either of God’s Word or
of the sacraments, as the injunctions recently issued by our Queen Elizabeth
also testify most clearly. We give them only that prerogative which in the
Holy Scriptures we see always attributed by God himself to all pious princes:
namely, that they keep in their duty all estates and orders entrusted to their
faith by God, whether ecclesiastical or civil, and restrain the obstinate and
delinquent by the civil sword.” This is confirmed from Lancelot Andrewes,
bishop of Winchester, in *Tortura Torti*, page 379, where in explaining the
royal primacy he responds to an adversary who objects Calvin: “Just as Calvin
did not approve the pope as king, neither did he approve the king as pope; nor
do we approve in the king what we detest in the pope. Both he with us and we
with him hold that King James has the same duties in the Christian church as
Josiah had in the Jewish church, nor do we aspire for anything further to be
done, etc.” On page 382 he says: “If you prefer an example from Christians,
the king requests to be a bishop of those outside, as Constantine was; to be a
governor of religion, as not only Charlemagne but also Louis the Pious was.”
In the same way, James Ussher, archbishop of Armagh, in an address delivered in
the Dublin chamber on November 22, 1622, published with his polemical work
against the Irish Jesuit, defends the oath of allegiance which declared the
king the one supreme governor in the kingdom, etc. In order to distinguish, he
says, “Two distinct powers were established by God in these lands: one of the
keys, entrusted to the church; the other of the sword, entrusted to the civil
magistrate. The former was ordained to operate upon the inward person, having
an immediate relation to the forgiveness and retention of sins; the latter was
ordained to operate upon the outward person, providing protection for the
obedient and inflicting external penalties upon rebels.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 274 | IA leaf 308 | PDF p. 308]

He explains and proves this in the same place. Afterward he says: “Although in
this respect we make the prince and priest guardians of both tables, and
although the matter around which they exercise their office can be the same,
the form and manner of governing it are nevertheless altogether distinct. One
extends only to the outward person, the other to the inward; one looses or
binds the soul, the other attends to the body and matters pertaining to it;
one has a special respect to the judgment of the age to come, the other
regards the present retention or deprivation of certain comforts of this life,
etc.” Finally, after many other things, he concludes that “according to the
common doctrine and public authority of the Anglican Church, the king’s
supreme government is understood to be civil and the power of the sword, and
cannot in any way extend to a government which is purely of another kind.”
Whoever wishes to read more on this argument should consult the celebrated
Voetius, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 1, tractate 2, chapter 3.

[Marginalia: Can the magistrate compel subjects to the faith?]

XXI. A question commonly raised here must not be omitted: ought the
magistrate’s care concerning sacred things to be extended so far that he can or
ought to compel his subjects to religion and faith? This indeed is the papists’
doctrine, which they have more than sufficiently confirmed by their deeds
until now and continue to confirm daily, both by the cruel practice of the
Spanish Inquisition and by the dreadful and deadly persecutions they exercise
against those unwilling to embrace the Roman faith and submit their necks to
the pontifical yoke. Indeed, they commonly place this policy among the means of
conversion. For as on the right hand they endeavor to entice people to
themselves with the hope of rewards and worldly advantages, so on the left
hand they strive by fear of punishments and evils to turn them from the faith
once delivered.

[Marginalia: The negative is proved.]

XXII. But the Holy Spirit’s doctrine is far different. Just as he teaches that
people are to be drawn to faith by no hope other than that of heavenly goods
and eternal life, so he wills them to be stirred to piety by no fear other than
that of God’s judgments and hell. “Knowing the terror of the Lord, we induce
people to faith” (2 Cor. 5:11). Every other way ought deservedly to be suspect
to us as proceeding not from Christ, the prince of life, but from Satan, the
prince of death. Just as Satan displayed the kingdoms of the world and their
glory to Christ to stir his hope, so as a roaring lion and murderer he commonly
rages among people with fear of punishment and death to call them away from
the faith. But Christ’s disciples, following their Master and Teacher, hold
that no one ought to be compelled to faith and religion. The weapons employed
here ought to be spiritual, not carnal; they should powerfully persuade the
mind by the evidence and demonstration of truth, not violently compel the
person by blows and torments; not the sword of the flesh but the sword of the
Spirit; not bonds like iron fetters, but the gentle bonds of love and charity.

[Marginalia: Reasons. 1. From Christ’s command.]

XXIII. The reasons are these. (1) Christ’s command and example. Nothing of
this kind was ever either commanded by Christ or approved by his example.
Christ indeed commands the apostles to teach and baptize, but nowhere commands
them to kill those unwilling to hear or drive them to belief by the authority
of the secular arm. Nor does Luke 14:23 prove the contrary, where the servant
is commanded to compel the guests invited to the heavenly householder’s
banquet: “Compel them to enter.” This compulsion must be understood according
to the subject matter,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 275 | IA leaf 309 | PDF p. 309]

not as physical compulsion which forces an unwilling person, but moral
compulsion which bends and persuades the will. Thus it excellently designates
the soul-bending and most powerful force of the Word and Spirit which God uses
in converting people to work in them both to will and to accomplish (Phil.
2:13) and to draw them to Christ—gently indeed, but powerfully; by a gracious
invitation, but by most effectual persuasion. It makes the unwilling willing,
tears them from the world by invincible power, draws them to Christ, and
introduces them into his communion. This sweet compulsion and persuasive
necessity are treated in Jeremiah 20:7, John 6:44, and Acts 9.

[Marginalia: 2. From 2 Corinthians 10:4.]

XXIV. (2) From the apostle’s words in 2 Corinthians 10:4–5: “The weapons of
our warfare are not carnal but mighty through God for the destruction of
fortresses, destroying reasonings and every high thing, and taking captive
every thought to the obedience of Christ.” Thus the apostle notes that the
means or instruments which we use and by which we serve Christ as soldiers in
propagating the gospel are not carnal, such as kings and princes of the world
commonly employ to extend their empire’s borders—namely, steel and violence.
Rather, they are “mighty through God,” that is, spiritual and most powerful for
the destruction of fortresses, meaning the desires by which people fortify
themselves in sins, just as Jericho’s fortifications were cast down at the
sound of the trumpet. They overthrow the reasonings of human wisdom and every
high thing of the flesh and take every thought captive into Christ’s service.
This certainly cannot be obtained by bodily weapons, which can bring the body
into order but have no power over the soul. It is accomplished only by the
efficacy of God’s omnipotent Word and the Holy Spirit, which so affects and
moves human hearts that, conquered by the truth, they are brought into
obedience to Christ and freely take up his yoke.

[Marginalia: 3. Because no one has dominion over the conscience.]

XXV. (3) Because religion and faith pertain to the human conscience, but no
one except God can assume dominion over the conscience. “We do not have
dominion,” says Paul, “over your faith” (2 Cor. 1:24). Maximilian II also used
to say well that “there is no more intolerable tyranny than wishing to rule
consciences,” because God reserved that dominion for himself alone. Seneca was
not ignorant that the conscience can be subjected to no mortal. In book 3 of
*On Benefits*, chapter 20, he says: “Bodies are liable and assigned to masters,
but the mind belongs to itself. It is so free and unconstrained that it cannot
be confined even by this prison in which it is enclosed, but uses its own
impetus and goes forth into infinity as a companion of heavenly beings.”

[Marginalia: 4. Because this is attempted in vain.]

XXVI. (4) Because the outcome of this endeavor is ineffectual. The desired
effect can never be obtained, because faith resides in the soul, which cannot
be compelled. “Who will impose on me the necessity,” says Lactantius in book 5
of the *Institutes*, chapter 20, “either of believing what I do not will or of
not believing what I will? Nothing is so voluntary as religion; if the mind of
the one sacrificing is turned away, religion is already removed and is
nothing.” What is compelled cannot be sincere, and nothing is pleasing to
Christ unless it is voluntary. These violent means accomplish nothing. Either
people’s minds are further alienated and irritated, or they become hypocrites
who suppress one thing in their heart and utter another with their mouth; and
while outwardly professing this or that faith, they are inwardly elsewhere in
their mind. Especially relevant here is what Philo, in the book *Every Good Man
Is Free*, relates concerning Calanus, the most ancient philosopher of the
Indians. He is said to have written to Alexander the Great to this effect:
“Your friends advise you to compel the Indian philosophers, though they have
not observed our affairs and business even in a dream. You will move bodies
from place to place, but you will inflict no force upon unwilling minds, no
more than if you compel bricks and wood to give forth a voice, etc. No king,
no prince can cause us to do anything contrary to the mind’s judgment.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 276 | IA leaf 310 | PDF p. 310]

[Marginalia: 5. From the testimonies of the ancients.]

XXVII. (5) From the testimonies of ancient and more recent writers. Tertullian,
*To Scapula*, chapter 2: “It does not belong to religion to compel to religion,
which ought to be undertaken voluntarily, not by force.” Hilary, *To Emperor
Constantius*: “It is neither fitting nor right that the unwilling and resisting
be forced and compelled.” Lactantius, book 5 of the *Institutes*, chapter 20:
“There is no need for violence and injury, because religion cannot be
compelled. The matter must be carried on by words rather than blows so that
there may be willingness, etc.” And afterward: “Torture and piety are far
different. Piety cannot be joined with violence, nor justice with cruelty.
Religion must be defended not by killing but by dying; not by savagery but by
patience; not by crime but by faith.” Athanasius, in his *Letter to Those Who
Lead the Solitary Life*, testifies that it belongs to heretics to propagate
their religion by killing. Speaking of the Arians, he says: “They compelled
the unwilling to change their opinion, which least of all belongs to people
confident in their cause. For truth is proclaimed not by swords, spears, or
military force, but by persuasion and counsel. Yet what freedom of persuasion
or reason for counsel is there when one who contradicts receives exile or
death as his reward? In the same place this new and execrable heresy, when it
is overwhelmed by arguments and collapses in shame before truth itself,
endeavors to draw to itself by force, blows, and prisons those whom it could
not induce by words. Thus it manifests that it is neither pious nor a
worshiper of God. For it belongs to pious religion, as I said, not to compel
but to persuade, etc.” Hilary, *Address to Constantius*: “God,” he says,
“taught rather than exacted knowledge of himself, and by the wonder of heavenly
works conciliated authority for his precepts; he despised a will compelled to
confess him.” In the same work against the Arians and Auxentius: “The church
terrifies with exiles and prisons and compels herself to be believed—she who
was believed amid exiles and prisons. She depends upon the favor of her
communicants—she who was consecrated by the terror of her persecutors. She
drives out priests—she who was propagated by banished priests, etc.” Gregory
to John of Constantinople: “We were made shepherds, not strikers; and the
excellent preacher says, ‘Reprove, entreat, rebuke with all patience and
teaching.’ But this preaching which exacts faith by blows is new and unheard
of.” This is incorporated into canon law, chapter *Quid autem*, Distinction
45. Bernard, Sermon 66 on the Song of Songs: “Faith must be persuaded, not
imposed.” The sounder men among our adversaries cannot deny this. When the king
of France promulgated a severe decree to exterminate the Reformed in 1553, the
Parlement of Paris, wishing to dissuade him from his purpose, wrote very
weightily among other things: “It seems equitable that the footsteps of the
ancient church should rather be followed. She accomplished the establishment
and propagation of religion not by steel and flames, but by the example of the
purer doctrine and honorable life of her bishops.” De Thou, book 16. To this
belong his golden words in the Preface: “Experience,” he says, “has sufficiently
taught us that steel, flames, exiles, and proscriptions have irritated rather
than healed the disease clinging to the mind. To cure it we need not those
things which penetrate only the body, but doctrine and diligent instruction,
which descends gently instilled into the mind. All other things are sanctioned
according to the civil magistrate’s decision; religion alone is not commanded,
but is infused into well-prepared minds from a prescribed opinion of truth
with the grace of the divine power added. Tortures have no force toward it,
because they harden minds rather than break and persuade them.”

[Marginalia: 6. From the practice of the ancient church.]

XXVIII. (6) From the practice of the ancient church. The church suffered
persecution but did not incite it. She conquered paganism as paganism had
conquered her, but never repaid it in kind. She did not use the authority of
the Constantines and Theodosiuses to compel unbelievers to the faith or to
drench the temples of false gods with the blood of their worshipers, as the
pagans had used the swords of the Neros, Maximins, Deciuses, and Diocletians to
stain the earth with the blood of Christians. One must be altogether

[1847 vol. III printed p. 277 | IA leaf 311 | PDF p. 311]

a stranger and foreigner in ecclesiastical history not to know that in the
conflicts the
catholic and orthodox church had with Arians, Eutychians, and other heretics,
she commonly used no weapons against them other than exhortations, arguments,
and councils. Heretics, on the contrary, brought steel and fire into the
living temples of the Holy Spirit and propagated their deadly errors chiefly
by terror of the death and torments which they inflicted upon the faithful
rather than by other means.

XXIX. Although the magistrate ought to be solicitous for his subjects’
salvation and omit nothing within his power to procure it, it does not follow
that this ought to be done by violent and unjust means which conflict with
divine and natural law, Christ’s example, the spirit of Christianity—which
breathes pure charity and gentleness—and the practice of the apostles and the
ancient church. Teachers, not torturers, must be employed here. More is
accomplished by admonishing than by threatening, and by teaching than by
beating.

XXX. Although faith ought not to be commanded but persuaded, and no one ought
to be compelled to abjure a heresy, it does not therefore follow that a
confusion of religions and Samaritanism must be introduced into the
commonwealth, or that any and every sect must be tolerated—especially those
which tear down the principal doctrines of Christianity, such as the Socinian
and similar sects. This would be openly injurious to God’s glory and supremely
harmful to the commonwealth’s welfare. Nor does it follow that every heretic
erring concerning the faith must simply be tolerated and restrained by no
penalties, which the Socinians and other sectarians of the same bran urge in
order to attend to their own affairs. Yet because the papists tend toward the
other extreme and hold that as many as are judged to have fallen into heresy
must be persecuted with steel and fire and punished by capital penalties—a
heresy which they measure by dissent from their own faith, so that they may
incite princes all the more fiercely to massacres of Protestants and the
Reformed—we must consider briefly how the middle can rightly be held between
these two extremes.

[Marginalia: Are heretics to be punished? The state of the question.]

XXXI. Here before all things we hold that a pious and Christian magistrate
ought to perform his duty concerning heretics: both to heal them by whatever
gentle means he can if they are capable of healing, and to check and restrain
them if they are incorrigible, so that the spreading corruption may be kept
away from the churches. But manifold caution must be employed, lest he sin
either by untimely severity or excessive leniency. An accurate distinction
must be drawn between the seduced and seducers, those who are “deceiving and
being deceived” (2 Tim. 3:13); between those who err more lightly and those who
err more gravely and obstinately—the former are to be instructed gently, the
latter restrained more sharply; between those who attack one or another
article of faith and those who overthrow the principal foundations of
Christianity; and between those who sin from ignorance and privately keep
their errors to themselves, and those who “teach a different doctrine” (1 Tim.
6:3) and infect as many as they can with the contagion of their errors. Among
the latter are especially heresiarchs, blasphemers, factional people, and
disturbers of public peace, who not only obstinately defend the capital errors
they cherish but continue spreading them whenever occasion is given, contrary
to repeated admonitions and prohibitions, polluting heaven and earth with
unspeakable blasphemies, and who under this pretext stir up disturbances and
seditions in the commonwealth. No one can doubt that these must be treated
more severely. Again, a distinction must be made between the multitude of a
nation infected by some error and particular individuals who seek to lead a
school and make a sect that will survive them. Those with whom the magistrate
has not yet entered any covenant and whom it accordingly lies in his judgment
to tolerate or reject must be distinguished from those who live under the
public pledge of covenants and edicts previously granted and repeatedly
confirmed to them. Nothing is owed to the former, but covenants once
sanctioned must be observed toward the latter, since they bind the magistrate
no less than

[1847 vol. III printed p. 278 | IA leaf 312 | PDF p. 312]

the people, unless the subjects make themselves unworthy of such a benefit by
some crime.
Finally, a great distinction must be made between heretics who are judged such
by the consensus of all Christians and have been lawfully convicted from
God’s Word—such as atheists, deists, Epicureans, Socinians, and the like—and
those regarded as heretics by some out of their own prejudiced opinions
because of dissent from their faith, although they were never lawfully
convicted of their errors and, with respect to positive articles, teach
nothing which is not received by others.

[Marginalia: First proposition. Heretics can be restrained.]

XXXII. Having posited these distinctions, we explain our doctrine in three
propositions. The first is: “Magistrates may restrain obstinate and stubborn
heretics who cannot be healed and who tear apart the church’s peace by their
factions, and may punish them even with some penalty.” For since, as was said
before, magistrates are guardians of both tables and care of religion pertains
to them, they ought to see that religion suffers no loss and prudently oppose
advancing evil, lest this gangrene spread more widely and diffuse itself
through the whole body. They cannot preserve religion unless they restrain its
obstinate and factional despisers. Both God’s glory, whose avengers they are,
and the welfare of the commonwealth, whose guardians they are, demand this. If
lesser evils are restrained by severe punishments, the greatest evil—which
injures God’s truth, blasphemes his name, tears the church, corrupts the faith,
and brings the salvation of the faithful into danger—ought not to be left
without remedy. Indeed, sometimes a remedy is needed all the more promptly and
powerfully as the destruction threatening the whole body from it is greater,
unless provision is made against it in time.

XXXIII. To this pertain the laws given by Moses against apostates,
blasphemers, false prophets, soothsayers, and idolaters, concerning which see
Deuteronomy 13:5 and 17:12 and Leviticus 24:16. To this also pertain the
examples of Moses and the pious kings in the Old Testament, who did not
hesitate to purify religion, restrain false prophets, heretics, and idolaters,
and inflict various penalties upon them. Likewise, Christian princes under the
New Testament passed various laws against heretics, by which they were
punished not only with imprisonment and exile but restrained by other, graver
penalties. See Law 3.1, Code *On the Supreme Trinity and Catholic Faith*; Law
*Manichaeos*, *On Heretics and Manichaeans*; Law *Quicunque* 8, *On Heretics*.

XXXIV. Nor is this doctrine opposed by the parable of the tares. As was said
before in Question XXXII, section 23, this does not pertain to the civil
magistrate or ecclesiastical ministry in such a way as to deny that any guilty
people should be punished by civil judgments or restrained by ecclesiastical
discipline, which would be utterly absurd. Rather, it teaches us that offenses
will never be lacking in the church until the consummation of the age and that
evils which cannot be corrected without destruction must be tolerated. Nor is
it opposed by Christ’s example, who does not quarrel and contend (Isa.
42:2–3) or break the bruised reed, etc., but gently calls people to himself.
For gentleness is attributed to him which sustains the weak, not which further
hardens robust malice. He is gentle so as not to break the bruised reed, but
not so as to foster the obstinacy of those who break the weak; instead, he is
armed with an iron scepter by which he crushes his enemies’ heads. Nor is it
opposed by Christian gentleness, because this does not take away the
magistrate’s right to bear the sword and ought not to cause him to refrain from
drawing the avenging sword against criminals. It does indeed require that
gentle remedies have their place and that all things be attempted before
proceeding to extremes. But this mild and humane method of healing differs
greatly from a lax softness which is nothing but the nourishment of evil. It
is a cruel mercy which exposes the sheep as prey so that the wolf may be
spared, and which permits Christ’s body to be torn and souls to be killed by
the poison of corrupt doctrines so that the stench of one putrid member may

[1847 vol. III printed p. 279 | IA leaf 313 | PDF p. 313]

remain untouched. Nor is it opposed by the fact that the civil magistrate has
no power over the soul in which heresy lies hidden. For although he has no
right over the soul, he has a right over the tongue as he has over the hands.
He can punish a heretic who teaches a different doctrine no less than a thief
who steals another’s goods or a robber who kills a person, because the heretic
infects the society for which the magistrate ought to care. Coercive force
therefore does not regard inward faith but is occupied with outward acts over
which the magistrate has a right. For although the mind’s opinion is not to be
punished, a pestilential and impious profession is by no means to be tolerated.

[Marginalia: Second proposition. Not every heretic may be punished by capital
punishment.]

XXXV. The second proposition is: “It is not lawful to punish every person who
errs and every heretic with capital punishment.” This is against the papists,
who hold that all heretics must be persecuted with steel and fire, whether
they are seduced or seducers, whether they sin from simple error and weakness
or maliciously and stubbornly, whether they truly err concerning the faith by
the common consensus of Christians or are regarded as such in the papists’ own
judgment because they dissent from the Roman church. They have more than
sufficiently confirmed this by their practice: the most accursed tribunal of
the Inquisition, the torture chamber of consciences, and the horrible massacres
exercised both in past ages and our own against pious worshipers of God who
were most falsely defamed by the hateful name of heretics. They exercised
these not only against one or another individual but against whole peoples and
nations, against myriads of people of every kind, sex, age, and condition, as
is clear from the martyrologies.

[Marginalia: It is proved. 1. Because this conflicts with the spirit of
Christianity.]

XXXVI. The reasons for our doctrine are these. (1) This barbarity and cruelty
conflicts with the spirit of Christianity and the purpose of the gospel, which
is to save and not destroy; to draw people to faith by the Word, not compel
them by steel; to kill errors and vices, but to spare persons as far as
possible. It belongs to Muhammad to advance by slaughter and blood and
establish his empire by savagery and torments. But Christ reigns in us by the
Spirit of grace and love; he thirsts for people’s salvation, not their blood.
Hence when the apostles asked that fire be called down from heaven to destroy
the Samaritans, Christ said, “You do not know by what spirit you are led, for
the Son of Man did not come to destroy souls but to save them” (Luke 11:54),
indicating that such vengeance does not agree with the evangelical spirit and
the end of his advent. Even Maimbourg cannot deny this in book 1 of his
*History of Calvinism* when he maintains that “Calvinism sufficiently shows
itself to be false and never to have proceeded from Christ, who is the God of
peace, by the violent and anti-evangelical manner in which it wishes to be
established.”

XXXVII. (2) Because this closes the way to people’s conversion and salvation,
which ought to be the principal end of religion and ministry. For while those
who err enjoy the use of life, there is always room for repentance, which is
snatched away by death because after death it is sought in vain. (3) Because
this is a mark of Antichrist, who was to be conspicuous for persecutions and
blood. The “beast which ascends from the abyss” is said to wage war against
the saints and overcome them (Rev. 11:7); and in chapter 13:15 it “will cause
all who did not worship the image of the beast to be killed.” To the same
point is what is said concerning the Babylonian harlot: that “she is drunk
with the blood of the martyrs of Jesus” (chapter 17:6), and that “in her was
found the blood of prophets, saints, and all who were slaughtered in her.”

XXXVIII. (4) The fathers acknowledge this, as is clear from the passages cited
above. Augustine agrees in book 3 *Against Cresconius*, chapter 50: “No good
person in the catholic church approves that anyone, though a heretic, be
treated violently even to death.” And in Letter 127 to Donatus the proconsul
concerning the Donatists: “We desire them to be corrected, not killed; we do
not wish discipline concerning them to be neglected, nor that they be
subjected to the punishments they deserve. Therefore restrain their sins in
such a way that they repent of having sinned.” And in book 1 *Against the
Letter of Petilian*, chapter 29: “Love the people, kill the errors; contend for
truth without savagery.” See Chrysostom, Homily 47 on

[1847 vol. III printed p. 280 | IA leaf 314 | PDF p. 314]

Matthew 13 and Homily 67 to the people of Antioch: “The Lord does not prohibit
dispersing the assemblies of heretics, stopping their mouths, and cutting off
their freedom to speak; but he prohibits killing and slaughtering.” Arnobius,
book 1 *Against the Nations*: “By Christ’s teaching and laws we have received
that we should pour out our own blood rather than pollute our hands and
conscience with another’s blood.” Nor do we read that the practice of the
ancient church was different, as was just said. And if, after the emperors
became Christians, certain penalties were decreed against heretics to restrain
them, nevertheless their blood was spared. Or if stricter laws concerning
capital penalties were passed against certain people, such as the Manichaeans,
Donatists, Circumcellions, and others, these regarded them not so much as
heretics as factional people, disturbers of the church, and seditious people
who had arrived at the lowest depth of criminal wickedness. They killed those
who dissented from them, subjected them to exquisite torments, and filled all
things with frenzies, fires, and robberies, as Augustine observes in various
places: Letter 68 to Januarius; book 1 *Against the Letter of Parmenian*,
chapter 7; and Letter 166 against the Donatists.

XXXIX. Although heretics can lawfully be excommunicated, they cannot therefore
lawfully be killed. Otherwise, fornicators, gluttons, contentious people, and
the like could lawfully be killed because they can be excommunicated. The
rationale of a civil penalty differs from that of an ecclesiastical penalty.
Whoever deserves a greater penalty in the same genus also deserves the lesser,
but the inference does not hold from one species to another, from a civil to
an ecclesiastical penalty. Add that in this respect excommunication cannot be
called a greater penalty than death, because it always leaves room for
conversion and salvation, which capital punishment does not do.

XL. Although the magistrate has the right of the sword against criminals and
the impious, it does not follow that it must be drawn indiscriminately against
every heretic, any more than against every sinner. Only this is rightly
inferred: the magistrate can restrain and punish heretics and, in accordance
with the offense, even inflict capital punishment if they are blasphemous,
factional, and rebellious, as will be stated afterward. But this does not apply
to simple and seduced heretics who sin from ignorance and weakness, who are
worthy of compassion rather than punishment and therefore must be instructed
and healed, if possible, not punished.

XLI. A common rule cannot be drawn from extraordinary, singular, and heroic
examples. Hence this question does not concern Elijah’s deed in seeing that
four hundred prophets of Baal were killed; Peter’s deed in consigning Ananias
and Sapphira to death (Acts 5); or Paul’s deed in blinding Elymas the magician.
These were altogether extraordinary, intended to conciliate authority for the
Lord’s prophet and apostles, not to prescribe any rule to the magistrate.

XLII. The rationale of murderers, poisoners, and counterfeiters differs from
that of simple heretics. The former sin against society and the commonwealth;
therefore they are justly killed both as punishment for the offense and as an
example. But heretics who privately keep their errors to themselves harm only
themselves.

XLIII. Those among us who take the affirmative side in the question whether
heretics ought to be punished with capital punishment do not speak of any and
every heretic, of the simple and seduced who sin from weakness and ignorance
and harm only themselves, but of factional, obstinate, seditious, and
blasphemous disturbers of the public peace. Beza repeatedly warns of this. On
page 95 he says: “Far be it from us to arm the magistrate against those who sin
from simplicity rather than wickedness, without the destruction of others and
notable blasphemy. We are not so foolish, nor have we put off every feeling of
humanity. Indeed, even in contagious and capital diseases, we will that every
gentler remedy first be attempted and directed to God’s glory and love of our
neighbor.” On page 118 he says: “I say this: we are not discussing those who
are weak in the faith. We have shown by the matter itself that these are to be
taught, not punished, yet so that their weakness is not fostered.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 281 | IA leaf 315 | PDF p. 315]

Rather, we are discussing self-condemned heretics—that is, stubborn and
factional enemies within the church.” On page 143 he says: “We have shown that
those are finally called heretics who, while claiming the name of the
faithful, have been lawfully convicted from God’s Word, yet follow their own
judgment and so obstinately and stubbornly defend certain false doctrines in
religion against the church that they do not hesitate to tear her peace and
agreement by their factions, etc.” Calvin had already warned of this in his
*Refutation of the Errors of Servetus*, page 823: “For when we appoint
magistrates as guardians for the protection of religion, we do not sharpen
their swords so that, about to exact punishment for any error whatever, they
immediately rush to blood. We know that there are three degrees of errors. We
admit that pardon must be given to some and that moderate chastisement
suffices for others, so that only manifest impiety is punished with capital
punishment.” He afterward explains: “Where religion is torn from its
foundations, detestable blasphemies against God are uttered, souls are swept to
destruction by impious and pestilential doctrines, and finally, where open
defection from the one God and pure doctrine is attempted, it is necessary to
descend to that final remedy, lest such poison spread further, etc.” And on
page 821: “There is no doubt among us how exact both prudence and gentleness
must be preserved, and that a calm and religious cognizance of doctrine ought
to precede judgment. But this is no obstacle at all to its being the
magistrate’s duty to restrain by sword and penalties those who, being
themselves apostates from the right faith, solicit others to defection and,
being contemptuous toward God, ensnare wretched souls by their fallacies,
disturb the church’s peace, and split and tear the agreement of piety.”
Bullinger, Decade 2, Sermon 8, says: “There is great diversity among persons.
There are standard-bearers and stubborn leaders in errors who are audacious,
hypocritical, and loquacious, and therefore most capable of seducing others.
They themselves rush without correction to their own destruction and draw
others with them. These must be restrained in every way as pests of the
church, lest their contagion spread more widely like a cancer. Then there are
certain people seduced and rendered senseless by others, who err but not from
malice or obstinacy. These must not be condemned immediately, but we must pray
to the Lord and instruct those who err in the spirit of kindness and
gentleness until they return to a better mind.” And afterward: “Now there is
also a notable distinction among punishments and penalties. Those who
obstinately err and contend to lead others with them into error and retain
them in errors—blasphemers and disturbers, indeed overthrowers of
churches—can lawfully be killed. Yet not everyone who errs is therefore to be
punished immediately with capital punishment. Those who can be healed by
threats and rebukes ought not to be purged and punished by harsher means;
moderation is best in everything, etc.”

[Marginalia: Third proposition. Blasphemous heresiarchs may be killed.]

XLIV. The third proposition is: “We hold that incurable, factional, and
blasphemous heresiarchs can be punished with death when they do not cease to
spread their poison contrary to repeated prohibitions and their pledged faith,
and disturb the commonwealth and the church.” Yet matters ought not to proceed
to this point unless every other gentle means has been tried in vain to heal
them and recall them to a better mind. When it is clear that such remedies not
only fail to cure the evil but rather exasperate and increase it, then at last,
though reluctantly and compelled by the necessity of his office, the
magistrate will consider this. He will be like physicians who commonly employ
extreme remedies for extreme and desperate evils, so that what cannot be
corrected and healed is cut away by knife and cautery, lest the sound part be
dragged with it. Meanwhile, to perform their duty rightly in this matter,
magistrates ought to take care that they remain within the boundaries of their
power and do not presume to judge heresy alone, since they are parts of the
church no less than magistrates. Then they ought to take care that judgments
of errors are exercised in a holy and uncorrupted manner, not from any human
authority, whoever they may finally be, or from any prejudice of any time or
custom, but from the pure and sincere declaration of God’s Word,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 282 | IA leaf 316 | PDF p. 316]

lest through error they present themselves not as avengers of God’s truth but
as servants of another’s cruelty. Finally, after an accurate examination and
diligent consideration of every circumstance, they ought to decree the
penalty by which proper honor may be asserted for God’s majesty and provision
made for the church’s peace and tranquility.

[Marginalia: It is proved. 1. From the atrocity of the crime.]

XLV. The reasons why we so maintain are various and were indicated by us above
in section 32 and following. To them we add the atrocity of the crime. For if
the punishment ought to increase according to the gravity of the crime, no one
can doubt that blasphemy and impiety, by which God’s majesty is directly
attacked, are the greatest of all crimes and therefore ought to be punished by
the greatest penalty. This is especially so if there is added to them an
obstinate and stubborn contempt for civil and ecclesiastical order, perjury,
and a mad fury to infect others with the same poison. Such human monsters must
be regarded as public pests and cancers, as disturbers of the church and
commonwealth whose removal from the midst is of the greatest importance,
whether for avenging the glory of the injured supreme Deity or for preserving
human society.

[Marginalia: 2. From God’s authority.]

XLVI. The second reason is God’s authority. He enacted various capital laws
against apostates, false prophets, idolaters, blasphemers, and the like (Deut.
13:1–2; 17:12; Ex. 22:20; Lev. 24:15), discussed above, for no other reason
than to show the magnitude of these crimes and the justice of the vengeance to
be taken upon them. Although we do not deny that these had something peculiar
in accordance with the more rigid Mosaic polity which does not pertain to us
who live under a gentler economy, it is nevertheless certain that in their
general character they still have their use among us. Two things can rightly
be gathered from them. First, since defection from the religion constituted by
God’s Word and solicitation to defection are punished by the civil magistrate,
the same crimes ought not to remain unpunished but ought still to be punished
now. Second, since no reason can be offered why God’s majesty and the welfare
of the church and commonwealth ought to possess less weight among us than they
once did among the Jews—indeed, they ought to possess more because the Father
revealed himself more clearly through the Son than through the prophets—
Christians can be less excused if they despise true religion or defend it with
less zeal than the Jews. Various examples of severity exercised against the
impious, idolaters, and blasphemers can also be brought forward here: Moses
against the idolaters (Ex. 32), Josiah slaughtering the priests of the high
places upon their altars (2 Kings 23:20), Jehu killing the priests of Baal (2
Kings 10), and similar examples. Although it cannot be denied that something
in them is extraordinary, it is nevertheless gathered from them that such
crimes deserve this punishment and that it could be justly decreed by God.

XLVII. To the same end pertained the various laws discussed above which the
emperors passed against the Manichaeans, Donatists, Apollinarians, and
Eutychians. Some condemned them to exile and deportation, infamy and
deprivation of goods, while others condemned them to capital punishment.
Marcian in Law *Quicunque* of the Code, *On Heretics and Manichaeans*, wills
“that those who attempt to teach unlawful things be restrained by the final
punishment.” Law *Ariani* 5, speaking of those who return to Manichaeism,
wills them “to be handed over to the final punishment.” And Law *Quisquis*
“wills the Encratites to be visited with capital punishment.”

XLVIII. Lest this be called the doctrine of only some among us, Zanchi notes
in volume 2 of the *Miscellanies*, chapter *On the Magistrate*, that “almost
all our theologians hold this doctrine, that heretics are to be punished by
the sword.” Bucanus, in *Institutes of Theology*, locus 49, proposes the
question, “May the magistrate proceed against heretics with the civil sword?”
He answers affirmatively. In addition to Calvin and Beza, Bullinger; Aretius in
his *History of Valentino Gentile*; Junius in his second *Defense of the
Trinity*; Danaeus in *Christian Ethics*, book 2, chapter

13; and others assert the same. Gerhard himself, although he sharply opposes
the capital punishment of all heretics, admits on page 1047 that it can justly
be inflicted upon some: “The question does not concern seditious,
blasphemous heretics who, in addition to propagating false doctrine, further
stir up seditions, incite subjects against the magistrate, and spew forth
direct and open blasphemies against God. We will not greatly object that these
can be visited with capital punishment for seditions and manifest blasphemies,
etc.” And on page 1004: “No one among us denies that stubborn heretics can be
excommunicated; no one prevents seditious heretics who disturb the public
peace from being punished by the sword.” Thus Antonio de Dominis, book 7,
chapter 8, although he proves solidly by various arguments that no one is to be
driven to the faith, nevertheless acknowledges that Christian princes and
magistrates can and ought to rise up by laws and penalties, sometimes even
capital ones, against true heretics and schismatics. (1) So that they may have
a peaceful commonwealth, which is commonly greatly disturbed by heresies and
controversies of religion. (2) From the proper obligation and duty by which
Christian princes are bound to preserve pure and intact religion. (3) So that
they may avert blasphemies against Christ and God. (4) So that, terrified by
the hardships of laws and the scourges of punishments, they may at least be
inclined to embrace the truth, even if they are not compelled.

XLIX. It ought not to be said here that weapons are thus extended to the
papists and swords sharpened for them to persecute us with steel and fire. No
one can fail to see how greatly we differ from them in this matter, nor can our
doctrine be advanced as an excuse for their cruelty except most unjustly. Who
does not acknowledge that it is one thing to will that blasphemous and
factional heresiarchs who disturb the church be punished, and another to draw
the sword indiscriminately against everyone who errs, whether seducers or
seduced, weak or obstinate, ignorant or sinning from malice? It is one thing
to proceed justly, but most rarely, against one or another openly impious and
blasphemous heretic, and another to perpetrate horrible massacres against
whole nations and myriads of erring people who are otherwise upright and
peaceful, of every sex, age, and condition, so that they are destroyed to
extermination and lawfully punished by capital penalties. It is one thing to
decree the penalty of death against those who, having been lawfully convicted
of their deadly error, show themselves incorrigible after every gentler means
has been employed in vain and do not cease, as far as they can, to spread
their poison contrary to admonitions and prohibitions. But it is another thing
to rage mercilessly against pious worshipers of God who were never convicted
of their heresy and cannot be charged with any error, except that they are
unwilling to subject themselves to the pontifical yoke; acknowledge no Word of
God other than the written Word, no other redeemers and no other Head of the
church than Christ, no other sacrifice than his death, and no other religious
worship than that which is due to God alone. Let any pious person judge
whether such people ought to be branded by the hateful name of heretics.
Certainly the royal edicts in France do not permit it, for they have repeatedly
prohibited this. Yet these are the harmless people whom the papists have
persecuted and continue daily to persecute with steel, flames, and every kind
of cruelty, at the mere thought of which the mind shudders. Sorrowful
experience has more than sufficiently taught this, and it is related at length
by the martyrologies and all who wrote the history of the persecutions and
inhuman massacres perpetrated at various times in Spain, France, Ireland,
Bohemia, Belgium, Piedmont, and elsewhere. Most recently the celebrated author
of the *Response to the History of Calvinism*, part 3, in the second charge,
chapters 1–4, describes in chapter 3, from Bartolomé de las Casas, the horrible
cruelties the Spaniards exercised upon the Indians under the pretext of
conversion. From all these things, as also from the execrable tribunal of the
Inquisition, he proves most certainly that no sect is more cruel than the
papacy.

L. Those who denounce the punishment inflicted upon the most impure Servetus
as unjust and cruel, in order to heap odium upon the most distinguished
Genevan magistrate and especially upon CALVIN, the great man of God, have never
sufficiently weighed the atrocity of his crime.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 284 | IA leaf 318 | PDF p. 318]

(1) It was not a simple heresy lying hidden in the heart concerning one or
another article of faith, but a compound and most foul heresy bursting forth
outwardly concerning the principal doctrines of Christianity, and especially
the adorable mystery of the Trinity. That wicked man did not blush to call the
Trinity, with a blasphemous mouth, “a three-headed Cerberus”—I shudder to
relate it—along with many other unspeakable blasphemies. (2) He did not cease
to disseminate this deadly poison of his merely once and in passing, but
repeatedly and for a long time, namely thirty years; not finally in one place,
but in several parts of Europe; not only by his slanderous mouth, but also by
most virulent writings, contrary to repeated admonitions and prohibitions. (3)
Matters proceeded to this point only with reluctance and after every other
means had been tried in vain to break his stubbornness and recall him to
repentance; and he could have avoided the penalty if he had wished. Calvin
says in his *Refutation of the Errors of Servetus*: “No danger of a graver
penalty threatened if he had been curable in any way. It would have been lawful
to buy off the penalty by modesty alone.” (4) Nothing here was done rashly and
precipitately by the magistrate, but only after every circumstance had been
maturely weighed in the fear of God and after consultation with even the most
distinguished leaders of Reformed Switzerland, who acknowledged the judgment’s
equity and approved it by their vote. (5) Bucer judged him “worthy to be torn
apart after his entrails were ripped out.” Melanchthon affirms that “the
Genevan magistrates acted rightly in killing the blasphemous man after the
matter was judicially adjudicated.” But Grotius, when he endeavors to transfer
the whole odium of that punishment to Calvin by maliciously calling him “the
burner of Servetus,” a name fabricated as an insult, strenuously slanders him
contrary to the credibility of the whole history and the testimony of all the
writers. They assert that Calvin performed what belonged to his office so that
Servetus might be convicted of monstrous and inhuman heresy and return from
his pestilential error to a better condition. Yet they never say, nor is it
clear from any argument, that he counseled the magistrate to burn Servetus.
Indeed, it is evident that along with the college of pastors he advised against
this kind of punishment, but that the magistrate recoiled from so many
blasphemies and was unwilling to treat him more gently. Yet who would marvel
that the punishment of that condemned man, who is deservedly numbered among
the standard-bearers of the impious sect, displeased a Socinian?

LI. We conclude that the Christian magistrate can proceed with capital
punishment against similar pests and human monsters, but in such a way that
extreme remedies are employed only for extreme evils and that even in these
such moderation is preserved that charity and clemency worthy of a Christian
are never violated.
