The second part of the assertion, namely, that the right and power to ordain
belong only to Ecclesiastical Officers, is demonstrated as follows.

1. This right does not belong to the people, as has been proved. Therefore it
belongs to the Rulers, for no third party is given—unless we say that this
right belongs to the Magistrate, which those with whom we are contending here
will deny along with us.

2. This right and this power to ordain and authoritatively send pastors and
Ecclesiastical Officers is expressly entrusted to presbyters and overseers of
the Church. Thus, (1.) 1 Timothy 5:22: “Do not lay hands hastily upon anyone.”
The right and power to ordain according to a certain manner is granted to the
person to whom that manner of ordaining is prescribed. But the manner of
ordaining is prescribed here to Timothy: namely, that he should not act
hastily, rashly, or inconsiderately, without weighing and examining everything.
Therefore this right and power is given to Timothy. *The imposition of hands*
signifies Ordination—that is, the sign is taken for the thing itself. Calvin
says on this passage: “For he forbids him to admit too readily anyone not yet
sufficiently proved. For there are some who, from an eagerness for novelty,
would desire the promotion even of someone wholly unknown as soon as he has
given one or two samples which please them. A grave and prudent Bishop ought
to resist this importunate desire, just as Paul here commands Timothy to do.”
(2.) 2 Timothy 2:2: “And the things which you heard from me among many
witnesses, entrust these to faithful men who will be competent to teach others
also.” He says, “entrust these things”: “commend and entrust these things.”
*To entrust* means to commend something to another’s care and protection,
Luke 12:48; 1 Timothy 1:18; 2 Timothy 1:14. And in the New Testament it nearly
always means either to appoint a Ruler or to give him greater power, Matthew
24:45, 47; Luke 12:14, 42, 44; Acts 7:10; Matthew 25:21; Acts 6:3.

[Volume II; printed p. 187; ONB canvas 195]

Paul therefore intended Timothy to admit no one but a faithful man to the
ministry. He therefore prescribes to Timothy what those who are to be admitted
to the ministry ought to be like: first, faithful men who wish to teach true
doctrine; then, men competent to teach—that is, furnished with the gifts and
qualifications required for discharging that office. Therefore, when he gives
Timothy these commands, it is beyond dispute that he also gives Timothy the
right and power to ordain competent and faithful pastors, or authoritatively
to entrust the ministry to them. (3.) Titus 1:5: “For this reason I left you
in Crete: that you should correct what is lacking and appoint presbyters town
by town”—“and appoint presbyters in every town.” The text itself teaches
plainly enough that Paul is speaking here about the ordination of pastors. See
Calvin on the passage and Beza.

The adversaries *reply* that this word sometimes means *to establish*, or to
make firm those who had previously been constituted in office, and not always
to create or appoint new ministers, just as the Septuagint translators use
that word in Psalm
2:6. *Reply.* 1. Although this verb is sometimes used with that meaning, it
does not necessarily follow that it must be understood that way here. Indeed,
the contrary is clear. For if Paul intended nothing other than that Titus
should confirm, console, or make firm those who had already been constituted
presbyters, he had no need to add this. For this can be included in the
preceding words, “that you should correct what is lacking.” Then why would he
say, “that you should appoint presbyters town by town”? Must it be supposed
that before this time several presbyters had been constituted in every city
throughout the whole island of Crete? Finally, why would he add the
characteristics and qualifications of a true and lawful presbyter? 2. It is
not safe to interpret this passage of Titus by that corrupt
translation which the Septuagint translators made of another passage, a
translation which Ri-

[Volume II; printed p. 188; ONB canvas 196]

vet and Musculus show in their comments on the passage to be exceedingly
corrupt. 3. It is better to take the word in its ordinary meaning, since
nothing appears in the text which compels us to depart from it,
and we showed above that this word ordinarily means *to appoint an officer* or
to give him greater authority. It is so used in the ancient Writers; see
Council of Nicaea, canon 4; Ancyra, canons 10 and 18; Antioch, canon 23;
Laodicea, canons 11 and 16; Sardica, canons 4 and 6. So also in Clement’s
Epistle to the Corinthians, “they were appointing their firstfruits as bishops
and deacons”; and by the Septuagint translators in Psalm 8:6 and Daniel 1:11.
4. The expression in the second Psalm can also denote God’s eternal decree
concerning Christ’s incarnation, or it can be understood of Christ’s eternal
generation. But whichever way it is understood, it does not prove the meaning
of this verb which the adversary fabricates.

3. The same thing is confirmed from the examples of the Apostolic Church, in
which it is clear that the power of sending and ordaining pastors was reserved
for the Overseers of the Church and was never handed over to the common people.
We always see other Ministers ordained by the Apostles and other Ministers of
the Church. (1.) Acts 6:3, 6: “Therefore, brothers, select from among you seven
men of good reputation, full of the Holy Spirit and wisdom, whom we may appoint
over this task”; “they set them before the Apostles, who prayed and laid hands
on them.” Here it is evident that, although the multitude elected these men at
the Apostles’ direction, it was nevertheless the Apostles who ordained them as
Deacons. See Calvin on the passage, and Beza, Gualter, the Belgian Annotations,
and others. (2.) Acts 13:1–4: “Now at Antioch, in the Church which was there,
there were certain Prophets and Teachers”; “and the Holy Spirit said to
them, ‘Separate for me Barnabas and Saul for the work to which I have called
them.’ Therefore, when they had fasted and prayed and laid hands on them, they
sent them away.” However these words are understood—whether as denoting the
admission of Paul and Barnabas into the number of the Apostles, as if they had
not been Apostles before this, as

[Volume II; printed p. 189; ONB canvas 197]

some maintain; or only their calling to a special part of their office, as
others maintain;
see the Belgian Annotations—it is perfectly clear that they were separated not
by the people but by Ecclesiastical Officers, the Prophets and Teachers who
were in the Antiochene Church, and that this was done at the command of the
Holy Spirit as well. (3.) Acts 14:23: “And when they had appointed presbyters
by vote in each of the Churches and had prayed with fastings,” and so forth.
Now those who appointed presbyters by vote were Paul and Barnabas. And although
the word “appointed by vote” includes the people’s consent and votes, the
Apostles’ official votes, so to speak, are not thereby excluded. Indeed, as
was said above, this word can be applied to the Apostles alone. What some say
against this—that it is absurd to speak of two men deciding by votes—has no
force. For Grotius testifies on the passage that “*to appoint by vote* is
commonly used of any election, even one made by one or a few.” Hence the
expression “those appointed beforehand by God alone”; and the verb was used of
the person who presided over an action. See Calvin,
*Institutes*, book 4, chapter 3, section 15, and Ames, *Bellarmine Enervated*,
volume 2, book 3, chapter 2, section 31. And it properly means *to raise* or
*extend the hands*. The Apostles, however, did not raise other people’s hands,
but their own. Finally, it must be observed that Stephanus says in the
*Thesaurus of the Greek Language* that this verb does not mean *to cast a
vote*, but *to appoint* and *to ordain* when it governs the accusative, as it
does here. And ecclesiastical Writers use this word for the imposition of
hands, which belongs only to Ecclesiastical Officers. See Calvin on the
passage; see also Ignatius’s Epistle to Polycarp and his Epistle to the
Philadelphians. (4.) 1 Timothy 4:14: “Do not neglect the gift which is in you,
which was given to you through prophecy with the imposition of the hands of the
presbytery.” That Ordination is to be understood by this imposition of hands is
clear from the fact that this phrase denotes it everywhere. Some wish
*presbytery* here to be understood not as a body of presbyters but as the
office of a pres-

[Volume II; printed p. 190; ONB canvas 198]

byter, or the presbyterate. Calvin seems to incline to this
opinion in his comments on the passage. Nevertheless, he says that both senses
fit reasonably well and by no means rejects the other, for he says, “Those who
think that the collective noun here is used for the college of presbyters are,
in my judgment, correct.” Indeed, that it cannot be understood otherwise is
demonstrated by the following. 1. That collective noun, *presbytery*, is always
used in this way in the New Testament—for example, Luke 22:66 and Acts 20:5—and
never in another sense. 2. There are other words denoting this office, namely,
πρεσβεία and πρεσβείον. 3. Does an office have hands to impose? How, then, can
it be called “with the imposition of the hands of the presbyterate”? 4. The
imposition of hands is always that of a person. See 2 Timothy 1:6, “through the
imposition of my hands”; Acts 6:6 and 13:3. 5. To evade the force of the
argument drawn from this passage to prove that the imposition of hands also
belongs to presbyters, Bellarmine says the same thing: namely, that “by the
word *presbytery* is understood the office itself entrusted to Timothy, so
that the meaning is, ‘through the imposition of the hands of the presbytery,’
that is, ‘by which you received the presbyterate.’” Gerhard says in section
235 of his work on the ministry: “But this exposition is forced and strained,
for we compare the parallel passage, 2 Timothy 1:6. Just as the Apostle teaches
there that he laid his own hands upon Timothy in Ordination, so also by the
same phrase in 1 Timothy 4:14 he teaches that the presbyters laid hands upon
Timothy together with him.” 6. Yet in *On Clerics*, book 1, chapter 15,
Bellarmine regards the other exposition as truer, saying: “The exposition of
the Greeks seems truer to us: by the word *presbytery* they understand a choir
or body of presbyters, Bishops, who lay hands upon a new Bishop. For this is how
Chrysostom, Theodoret, Theophylact, and Oecumenius expound it.” Other Roman
Catholics—Mariana, Salmeron, and Cardinal Hugh—admit the same. 7. Although
certain Latin Writers—Jerome, Ambrose, Primasius, Haimo, and Lyranus—say that
*presbytery* here is the *dignity or office of the presbyterate*, greater
weight must nevertheless be given

[Volume II; printed p. 191; ONB canvas 199]

to the opinion of the Greeks, who better understood the force of Greek words.
Moreover, in *Apology for the Opinion of
Jerome*, section 3, Blondel demonstrates that Greek Writers used *presbytery*
for a meeting of the Overseers of the Church. He does so from the testimony of
Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 6; Origen in Eusebius, book 6, chapters 20
and 25; and others whom he cites there, namely, Basil and Epiphanius. See also
what John Cameron has on Matthew 18:15. Finally, let Didoclavius be heard in
*The Altar of Damascus*, page 158: “Nowhere in the New Testament,” he says,
“is this word *presbytery* used for an office, but collectively for the body
and assembly of presbyters. But certainly the syntax and sequence of words
cannot bear that sense concerning the office of the presbyterate. For what
would the meaning be if someone rendered the words in this way: ‘Do not
neglect the gift which is in you, which was given to you through the imposition
of the hands of the presbyterate’? Add similar constructions: ‘through the
imposition of the hands of the episcopate, the diaconate,’ and so forth.
Neither in Scripture nor in approved authors will you find *imposition of
hands* construed with the office to which someone is called, but with the
persons imposing them. If you were to relate the word *presbytery* to *gift*,
there would not only be an inelegant hyperbaton: ‘Do not neglect the gift
(which is in you, which was given to you through prophecy with the imposition
of hands) of the presbytery.’ But the phrase itself—‘the gift of the presbytery
or presbyterate which is in you’—would also be harsher. Elsewhere the Apostle
illuminates the sense of this passage, in 2 Timothy 1:6. The imposition of hands
there is that of a person, not an office. The event itself and the history of
what was done also reject that sense.” There is no reason for me to stir here
the controversy among Presbyterians, Roman Catholics, and Hierarchists over
whether this was a body of Bishops or of Presbyters, since both sides grant
what I am seeking here: that this Ordination was performed by Officers of the
Church and not by the people.

But some *object* that the gift given here to Timothy was something
extraordinary, because prophecy is mentioned. *Reply.* We need not anxiously
inquire what that gift was;

[Volume II; printed p. 192; ONB canvas 200]

whether it was something extraordinary or ordinary makes no difference, for
it does not undo the presbytery’s action. Whatever gifts Timothy possessed and
however they were conferred upon him, he was ordained by the presbytery, or a
body of Presbyters laid hands upon him. Moreover, it must be noted that Paul
does not say *through* the imposition of hands (*dia*), as he says *through*
prophecies (*dia*), but *with* it (*meta*). Therefore, although the gifts
conferred in this way—*through* prophecy or *through* the imposition of the
Apostle’s hands, 2 Timothy 1:6—were extraordinary, what was given *with* the
imposition of hands could be, and was, ordinary.

They further *object* that Timothy, as an Evangelist, had no need to be
ordained by presbyters, who were inferior to an Evangelist. *Reply.* 1. There
are some, even among the Hierarchists, who say that Timothy was ordained twice:
once as an Evangelist, and then as a Presbyter—namely, as an Evangelist by the
Apostle, 2 Timothy 1:6, and as a presbyter by the presbytery, 1 Timothy 4:14.
2. It cannot be proved that he was appointed an Evangelist by the presbytery;
nor can it be proved that he was an Evangelist before he was a presbyter. 3.
There is no absurdity even if we say that he was ordained an Evangelist by the
presbytery, because, although an Evangelist was superior to any individual
presbyter, he was not superior to a body of presbyters. And in Acts 13:1–3 we
see Prophets and Teachers laying hands upon Apostles. Nor can the adversaries
say that this is absurd, since they wish mere private persons to ordain
presbyters.

4. All antiquity casts its vote for our position. There is no reason for me to
produce instances. Consult the Writings of the Hierarchists and Presbyterians
(especially Blondel, *Apology for the Opinion of Jerome*, section 3, chapter
1, on ordinations) as they contend against each other concerning the power of
ordaining—that is, whether it

belongs to presbyters or to Bishops. For all the testimonies which each side
brings forward prove that this right was attributed not to the people but to
the Officers of the Church. And thus this Assertion of ours is sufficiently
confirmed. The next one follows.
