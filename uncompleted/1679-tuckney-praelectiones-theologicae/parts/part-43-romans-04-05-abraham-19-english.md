Bellarmine rises against this in his seventh argument against justification
by the imputation of Christ's righteousness, in book 2, chapter 7, *On
Justification*. His argument runs thus: “If through Christ's righteousness
credited to us we can truly be called righteous and children of God, then
Christ can also truly be called a sinner through our unrighteousness
credited to him.” Bellarmine says that Illyricus admits this, namely, that
Christ is most truly called a sinner through the Father's most powerful
imputation and his voluntary reception of sins upon himself. But Bellarmine
calls this blasphemous because Christ is everywhere proclaimed in Scripture
to be holy, innocent, spotless, and most righteous. Therefore, if Christ was
and was called not a sinner but righteous on account of the righteousness
inhering in him, notwithstanding the imputation of sins, then similarly,
notwithstanding the imputation of Christ's righteousness to us, we will be
and be called unrighteous on account of the unrighteousness still inhering
in us, and therefore not truly justified.

I answer generally that this controversy concerns not inherent
righteousness but justification in its forensic sense. If this argument had
any force against us, it should have been framed thus: “If we are justified
by Christ's righteousness credited to us, then Christ, by our sins credited
to him, was treated as guilty and condemned.” We freely and gratefully
confess both, with Scripture's agreement and express assertion, as we will
soon see. This is precisely what we say and acknowledge: Christ can be
called unrighteous or a sinner in the same manner in which we are called
righteous in justification strictly considered. As to sanctification, which
is always joined to justification, we are indeed made inherently righteous,
truly though only initially. But that is not considered in the formal nature
of justification. In that sense justification frees us only from guilt and
punishment through the forgiveness of sins and the imputation of Christ's
righteousness. By imputation it thereby reckons us righteous and God deals
with us as though we were truly, properly, entirely, and inherently right-

eous. Similarly, through and because of our sins credited to Christ, Christ
does not become unrighteous in every respect—that is, properly and
inherently, as this argument requires—but imputatively only, so that he was
dealt with as though he had truly been unrighteous. Let that suffice as a
general answer. But, if desired, let us proceed more particularly and
minutely.

1. First, I deny the consequence: “If we are made righteous through the
imputation of Christ's righteousness, then Christ is similarly made
unrighteous through the imputation of our sins.” The cases are not the same.
For:

1. Christ's grace in the imputation of his righteousness to us is far more
efficacious and abundant than our guilt credited to him, as the Apostle
teaches in Romans 5.

2. Our unrighteousness is credited to Christ in order to be conquered and
removed; his righteousness is credited to us in order to remain over us in
its own force and efficacy.

3. Our unrighteousness is not credited to Christ in order to change his
state absolutely. His righteousness is credited to us in order to change
our state.

4. In taking our sins upon himself, Christ did not take all their
consequences upon himself. By this imputation he took upon himself the
punishment and curse, which served our salvation, but not the defilement.
Defilement neither ought nor could be transferred to him, and had it been
transferred, it would have made him unfit to redeem us.

5. Finally, Christ admitted the imputation of our sins on the condition that
we should be constituted righteous by his righteousness. But our sins were
not credited to him on any corresponding condition that he himself should
be constituted unrighteous by them. For these and other reasons, therefore,
Bellarmine's argument contains no valid consequence: “If we are made
righteous by the imputation of Christ's righteousness, then he would be
constituted unrighteous and a sinner by the imputation of our sins.”

2. Second, his entire argument suffers from equivocation, since it consists
of four terms. He argues: “If we are truly righteous by Christ's
righteousness credited to us, then Christ is truly unrighteous or a sinner
by our sins credited to him; but the latter is blasphemous, therefore the
former is false.” Being truly righteous or unrighteous has two senses:
according to imputation or according to actual inherence. To say that Christ
is truly and inherently unrighteous and a sinner is indeed blasphemous. But
to say it according to imputation alone, in the same sense that we contend
we are made righteous or justified in justification by his righteousness
credited to us, is not blasphemous at all—unless we accuse the Spirit
himself of blasphemy in Scripture. Scripture plainly pronounces that Christ
was made “a curse” for us in Galatians 3:13 and “sin” in 2 Corinthians 5:21.
This is not only because he was made a sacrifice for sin insofar as he paid
the penalty for our sins—which is a true but incomplete explanation of the
passage—but because our very sins were credited to him; otherwise he could
not justly have paid their penalty. Therefore, because the Apostle says that
he was made “a curse,” he can reverently be called “accursed” in that sense;
similarly, because the same Apostle says that he was made “sin,” he can no
less properly be called a “sinner,” imputatively speaking. In this sense
Luther says that Christ was the greatest of all sinners—perhaps too

[1679 printed p. 252 | IA leaf 260]

boldly. Yet the Romanists should not therefore accuse him of blasphemy,
since one of their own, Ruard Tapper, explains the Apostle's statement from
Chrysostom, Theophylact, and Oecumenius by saying that Christ was made a
notable sinner for us. For the sake of Christ's honor, I would gladly
abstain from such forms of speech. Yet their intended assertion is most true
and contains nothing false or blasphemous: Christ not only bore the
penalties of our sins, but our sins themselves were credited to him so that
he might justly do so.
