CHAPTER XX.

Verse 1. Wine is a scoffer, and intoxicating drink a brawler; therefore he who strays in it will not be wise.

In לץ (*derisor*, scoffer) and המה (*tumultuator*, brawler) there is a metonymy of the effect.

The purpose is to deter people from excess in intoxicating drink.

Since wine is a great support and comfort of human life, and has been granted to human beings by God’s extraordinary

[1632 witness | PDF p. 426 | printed columns 826-827]

beneficence, it follows that its abuse is exceedingly hateful to God. As for what the use of wine may be, see Judg. 9.12, namely, that it should gladden human beings and God. For although wine is no longer poured out to God, nevertheless, since there is a special use of wine in the Lord’s Supper, and it is therefore suitable for gladdening human minds, so that by its moderate use they may be more eager in singing God’s praises, it follows that the use of wine in divine worship is no less today than it was formerly under the law. Therefore it is a grave wrong when wine is not accommodated to these ends, but it is a more atrocious wrong for it to be applied to contrary uses. This happens whenever people exceed due measure in the use of wine, a point which is especially confirmed by this Proverb of Solomon. For observe, I pray, how many and how rotten are the fruits which proceed from drunkenness.

First, by the morals he has defiled through an excess of wine, the drunkard exposes himself to the mockery and derision of everyone, and even of the members of his own immediate family. This is clear in the example of Noah, who was mocked and held in derision by his son Ham. Lot, moreover, contracted from his drunkenness that mark of ignominy which, although it may have been blotted out before God, will nevertheless cling among human beings both to his own person and especially to all the posterity of Am. and Moab. Since, therefore, it is incumbent upon each person to protect not only his neighbour’s honourable reputation but also his own, it follows that the person who exceeds due measure in the use of wine brands himself with a mark of infamy.

The second fruit is that drunkenness renders a person a mocker. For drunkards are proud. Hence at Isa. 28.1 the drunken men of Ephraim are also called proud. For drunkenness induces forgetfulness both of another’s virtue and excellence and of one’s own weakness and deficiency. Hence at Hos. 7.5 the King, overcome by wine, is said to have stretched out his hand with mockers. Hence it is expressly said at 1 Sam. 25 that Abigail was unwilling to admonish Nabal of his folly until he had slept off his drunkenness. For she prudently considered that he would turn every reproof into an object of mockery.

The third fruit is that drunkenness stirs up tumults and disturbances. This is confirmed at Prov. 23.29: “Who has woe?” and so forth. See also Hos. 4.18.

The final effect is that the person devoted to wine will not be wise. For it is an obstacle to anyone’s imbibing wisdom, since the abundant exhalation of wine dulls the understanding. Indeed, it reduces even those endowed with wisdom to folly or madness, Hos. 4.11. What is celebrated in our proverb agrees with this very well: “When wine enters, understanding departs,” as among us, “when the wine is in, the wit is out.” This convicts of error those who once a month drink themselves to drunkenness in order to sharpen their understanding. And even if understanding might be gained from it, it ought nevertheless not to have been purchased at so great a price of offence against God.

Although an excess of wine is harmful to every kind of person, it is harmful to none more than Magistrates and Ministers, to whom judgment in divine and human affairs is entrusted. See Prov. 31 and Lev. 10. See Isa. 28 concerning the Prophets, and so forth. There it should especially be noted that he says not so much that they have swallowed wine as that they have been swallowed by wine. For a person destitute of wisdom is, as it were, nobody. Concerning Kings, see 1 Kings 16 concerning Baasha.

Verse 2. The King’s terror is like the roaring of a young lion; whoever provokes him to fury sins against his own soul.

The purpose is to instruct people in lawful obedience to the King and Princes.

The former part of this Proverb is the same as

[1632 witness | PDF p. 427 | printed columns 828-829]

19.12, except that in place of the indignation which occurs there, *terror* is substituted here—the effect, namely, for the cause. For terror follows from the King’s indignation. From it comes the contraction of his brow, from it the King’s threats, from it the preparation for death, all of which strike terror into even the bravest person. We experience this in Joab, a most warlike man, who, as soon as he had perceived and recognized the King’s indignation, immediately ran to the altar. Therefore, so that this terror may be met, insofar as it is granted and permitted by God’s law, diligent and zealous obedience must be rendered to him in all things. But if obedience toward God and religion hinder us from obeying his whim, an extraordinary protection against terror has been placed in the justice of our cause and our innocence. For the just man is confident as a young lion, Prov. 28.1. Therefore the King’s indignation will not have so much power to produce terror as being conscious of no evil will have to produce confidence and peace and tranquillity of mind—especially when the goodness of our cause has flowed from justifying faith, which renders consciences peaceful, and without which no innocence of ours would render us secure from the King’s terror.

In the second part of the Proverb there is a great affinity with 16.14 and the former half of that Proverb, where the Prophet affirms that the King’s blazing anger is a certain messenger of death. But this is the difference: here he seems to have employed a word which signifies, as it were, a higher degree of anger. For in that Proverb blazing anger, or seething anger, is presented. Although it is more kindled and intense than simple anger, it is nevertheless surpassed in severity by fury, which is presented here, or by anger exceeding every measure. From this we gather that we ought not to rush headlong into the indignation of the King and Prince if through imprudence and ignorance we have fallen into offence against him. For there are thoughtless and rash people who, when they have once stirred up the Prince’s anger against themselves, thereafter do not take much care to avoid adding and accumulating offence upon offence, just as though their affairs could not be in a worse condition. In this they are very greatly mistaken. For Kings, when angry, do not immediately rush upon their subjects. Even if they have grown hot and seethed, nevertheless, as is said at 16.14, a wise man knows how to appease that seething anger. We experience this in Saul the tyrant, who was reconciled to David by Jonathan’s prudent speech, as was said above. See 1 Sam. 19.4–6. But if the King is driven into fury by repeated and frequently reiterated defections, then at last he grows hot and is inflamed to such a degree that not even any gentle water of prudent speech can be found to extinguish the fire of his anger.

When he says that such a person sins against his own soul, this may suitably be referred not only to the destruction of the present life but also to that of the future life. For since the word נפש signifies both life and soul, it follows that by defection from the King—to whom obedience must be rendered for the sake of conscience toward God, Rom. 13.5—not only the King’s fury but God’s is provoked.

Here it must also be learned in how great a danger we are placed if we provoke God, the King of Kings and Lord of lords, not only to anger and blazing wrath but even to fury. See Amos 1, verses 3, 6, 9, 11, 13, and 2.1, 4.

Verse 3. It is an honour for a person to cease from strife; but every fool will mingle himself in it.

In איש (*viro*, man) there is a synecdoche of the part for a human being, whether man or woman. Likewise, in יתגלע (*immiscebit se*, will mingle himself) there is a metaphor drawn from liquids which are unsuitable for mixing, transferred to people wickedly committed against one another in strife.

The purpose is to exhort us to pursue peace by avoiding contention.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 428 | printed columns 830-831]

In verse 11 of the preceding chapter, the latter half contains a maxim very similar to the former half of the present Proverb. There it was said that it is an ornament to a person to pass over an offence. But this is the distinction: in that Proverb a person is praised if he overlooks an injury inflicted upon him and passes it over in silence, of which an excellent and illustrious example exists in Saul, 1 Sam. 10.27. By the present Proverb, however, we are taught to withdraw from a dispute already undertaken and debated on both sides. Thus there are very many who, induced by zeal for their own quiet or even by religion, will pass over an injury inflicted upon them, but who nevertheless, once they have begun a dispute, will not withdraw from it. Christ alludes to this disposition of human beings at Matt. 5.25. For they suppose that something is detracted from either their power and strength, or that the opinion of their wealth among human beings is diminished, or finally that their reputation for wisdom and prudence or for constancy is injured. In short, they suppose that all these things are undermined unless they pursue to the very end whatever dispute they have entered. The Prophet opposes to their foolish thoughts this maxim, by which—to say nothing of its utility—he teaches that it is an honour to a person if he desists from carrying on a dispute. For it belongs to some greater virtue to cease from a dispute already undertaken than to pass it by. Just as it is more difficult to gather back waters which have been released than to hold gathered waters enclosed within their receptacles, so it is more difficult to cease from a dispute than not to enter it. See Prov. 17.14.

An extraordinary example of this matter exists at Jer. 28.11, where the Prophet, while disputing with the false prophet, yielded place to that importunate man. This rebukes the perversity which has prevailed among us today, among whom it is customary to boast of casting the last word in a dispute and inflicting the last blow or slap in a fight. What is still more grievous, they will not hesitate to profess this openly and confidently.

Very closely akin to the latter half of this Proverb is that statement at 18.6: “The lips of a fool enter into strife, and his mouth summons beatings.” ] The only difference is that there the wantonness of the tongue of a person stirring up strife is spoken of more particularly, while here every manner of mixing a dispute is declared more generally. Concerning this folly of mixing disputes, see the example of Nabal, 1 Sam. 25, who, though greeted with the most flattering and honourable words, began a dispute with David. See likewise King Hanun, concerning whom it is written at 2 Sam. 10.

Verse 4. The sluggard will not plough before winter; he will beg at harvest, when there will be nothing.

In יחרש (*arabit*, will plough) there is a double synecdoche of a particular part for the class: that is, ploughing stands for the whole system of agriculture, and agriculture for every honourable craft, because agriculture is the most liberal among the non-liberal arts. Likewise, חרף (*hyeme*, winter) stands for every hard and difficult time.

The purpose is to deter people from sloth and idleness by exhorting them to honourable labour.

The sluggard is described in two ways: first by the effect, and second by the accompanying punishment.

In the former part, which concerns the effect that is denied, it is affirmed that he does not exercise himself in honourable occupations, but either lies drowsily in bed or leans idly upon his elbow. Or, if there is anything to which he directs his mind and applies himself, it consists either in perpetrating crimes or in games—partly unlawful ones, such as dice and playing cards, and partly ones which are undertaken merely for recreation, so that people may be more eager to undertake labour. For in this way Scripture brands such people with the mark of fleeing honourable labour, while it affirms that they are accustomed in the meantime to be ἀέργους, idle, and πολυπράγμονας, busybodies, Prov. 13.14; 1 Tim. 5.13.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 429 | printed columns 832-833]

He assigns the cause of this effect: the sluggard is unable to bear the cold. By that one thing he embraces within its scope everything which brings pain or trouble. For by the same reasoning by which he flees labour because of excessively intense cold, he will shrink from labour in summer and autumn because of frequent rains. He will reject this craft because it is entirely sedentary; another because it requires constant standing; a third because it demands bending the body, so that, unless he can practise some craft while lying on his back or reclining upon his side, he admits no work. He will find fault with one craft because it will keep him shut up at home like women, and another because it will detain him continually in the field like wild beasts.

There follows the addition of the punishment, which is twofold: the former is that he will beg, and the latter that by begging he will obtain nothing.

There is ignominy and shame in begging, as is clear from the common judgment of all human beings and from that saying of the unjust steward, Luke 16. Thus we see the slothful and idle man branding himself with a mark of infamy by his inactivity. For either he is reduced to the beggar’s wallet, or, driven to theft, he is led to the cross.

Yet here someone may ask how this happens, since in the Commonwealth of God, especially when it was well ordered—as it was under Solomon and the rule of other godly Kings—there was no place for beggars, Deut. 15.4; Acts 4.34. It may be answered that, although he is not permitted by the laws to profess begging openly and to seek alms from door to door, nevertheless he will be able to seek alms secretly here and there. Next, Solomon foresaw that in very many places and ages this most just law of God would be neglected. Finally, he declares what happens in all matters, profane as well as others.

This begging is amplified by the accompanying circumstance of time: he will beg at harvest, when all things are most abundantly available to human beings, to say nothing of winter, when granaries and wine cellars are nearly exhausted. It follows that the person who was unwilling to endure the cold while labouring will be compelled to undergo it while begging.

The second punishment is that by this so ignominious begging he will obtain nothing. First, this is because it has been provided by wholesome laws that the person who is unwilling to labour should not eat, 2 Thess. 3.10. Next, it is because by the just judgment of God the inward affections of all human beings—even of those inclined to compassion—are closed and, as it were, contracted against the idle. Here he also adds the weight of the accompanying circumstance of time: not even at harvest itself, when because of the utmost abundance people are accustomed to make more liberal provision than during the rest of the year even for the beasts that carry sheaves and bundles into the barns in wagons. At that time provision is also made by the law that something from the crops, vineyards, and olive groves should be left for the poor.

This Proverb can suitably be transferred to spiritual idleness and cessation from good works. The person who does not plough here through good works or sow seed will in the age to come reap not even a grain of consolation with which to defend himself against the destruction of soul and body. Then, like the rich man in the Gospel and the foolish virgins of Matt. 25, he will beg, but in vain—especially since this world, if you regard the godly, may deservedly be likened to winter, and the future age and day of judgment to harvest, as happens more than once in the Scriptures, Matt. 13; Song 2. For no one can exercise himself in good works except with some difficulty and labour, as though with cold. Therefore, not unsuitably let those be referred to this class who because of either excessive cold or heat, or because of sleep, withdraw themselves from the Church’s sacred assemblies, or who are present at them while yawning.
