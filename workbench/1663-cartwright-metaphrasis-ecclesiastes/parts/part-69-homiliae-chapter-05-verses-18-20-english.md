VERSE 18. Behold what I have seen: that it is good and beautiful to eat and
drink, and to enjoy good from all his labor with which he labors under the Sun
during the number of the days of his life that God himself gives him, for this
is his portion.

VERSE 19. Indeed, whenever God himself has given a person riches and
resources, and has granted him the ability to eat from them, receive his
portion, and rejoice in his labor, this is the gift of God.

VERSE 20. But if he does not have much, he will nevertheless remember the days
of his life, for God himself responds to the gladness of his mind.

The summary concerns the lawful use of riches.

The parts are: what the lawful use of riches is, verse 18, and from whom it
comes, verses 19–20.

In the former two things are set out: 1. The beneficial use of riches. 2. The
reasons upon which it rests.

Lest Solomon appear in the preceding and following discourse to condemn every
use of money and riches, he inserts certain things about their right and
lawful use in human life. The use of money is to eat and drink. Because this
is done by everyone, even the greatest misers, who otherwise could not even
live, he adds what distinguishes lawful from unlawful use. A person eats,
drinks, and dresses in such a way that he fares well and comfortably, enjoys
the good, and is refreshed in spirit, as is clear from the following verse.
This is not done by those who eat more sparingly and ungenerously than their
condition permits, or by those who, although amply fed with food and drink, do
so in darkness, entangled in cares and anxieties. Yet neither do those who
stuff themselves with feasts and wine to the point of utter satiety enjoy the
good. A heavy cold, sickness, or nocturnal restlessness prevents their faring
comfortably from these things, however much, for a

moment of time, their senses are tickled by some sweetness. Thus to eat, drink,
and dress so that thereby you both preserve your health and are cheered in
mind is the lawful use of drink and bodily care.

Next, the reasons by which this doctrine is confirmed must be weighed. The
first is taken from the testimony of Solomon himself, who discovered by his
own inquiry and investigation that this was true. Although his testimony is
weighty and ample, when we consider that Solomon's testimony in this passage
is the testimony of the Holy Spirit himself, it follows that it is beyond
every objection. We will set this testimony of the Spirit against the
judgments and reasonings of a thousand people. For example, famine, old age,
and sickness can come. Just as thinking about them ought to restrain us from
luxury, so it ought not to deter us from the honorable and moderate use of
riches. Then, after Solomon's example, let us take care to seek out moderation
and the proper use of things, without which it would be better to lack and be
deprived of the things themselves. For one who knows how to use poison
correctly, the poison itself is preferable to an antidote in the possession
of one who does not know how to use it. If failure to investigate the right
use of things is blamed, how much more is it a fault not to use that right use
properly when others have investigated and discovered it?

Another reason by which the enjoyment of good from the possession of riches
is confirmed is taken from the goodness and beauty joined to it: it is good
and beautiful. I relate *good* to the person using it and *beautiful* to the
judgment of others who behold this lawful use. This is the same as if Solomon
said that it is both healthful and honorable. There is no question about its
healthfulness, since by a moderate and cheerful use of food and clothing we
preserve health and prolong life. That it conduces to an honorable reputation
(to which we ought to attend insofar as it is consistent with obedience to
God) is sufficiently declared by the contempt and low opinion held of the
miser who defrauds his own natural appetites, an opinion attested by the many
reproaches and jests directed against him. In the same way, everyone speaks
ill of the man who dissipates and bankrupts his fortune by continually sipping
and licking up delicacies. If someone who does not pursue what is good is
rightly condemned, even though it may for the most part be hated and
unwelcome to people—as are many things that Christ's gospel requires from us,
such as the prohibition of vengeance, patience, and so forth—with how much
heavier a penalty will those be punished who violate those precepts of God
whose observance is pleasing and acceptable to God and people alike? Such is
moderation in wine and strong drink. It is healthful to the person who
practices it, pleasing to God, and approved by the vote of all people. Thus
drunkards must be hated by God and people and at last become hateful to
themselves.

The third reason is drawn from the labor and weariness joined to it, which a
person undergoes by the common law of our calling. So that we may sustain it
all the more cheerfully, God wills that we be comforted and refreshed by the
things our labor has acquired. For since God wills that fodder and forage be
supplied and furnished to beasts of burden in proportion to the burdens and
labors they sustain, and for that very reason forbids a farmer to bind the
mouth of an ox that treads out the grain, how much more does he will this to
be observed by people, for whose sake this is commanded? It follows from this
that one who refuses to labor, as the Apostle likewise testifies in 2
Thessalonians 3:10, ought not to enjoy this good, whatever his condition or
rank may be.

The fourth reason is drawn from the time joined to it, namely, the brevity of
the life we live here on earth. Hence life is spoken of in days rather than
years, just as Jacob professed before Pharaoh, king of Egypt, that although he
had reached his 130th year, he had lived few days. To this belong the frequent
comparisons of human life with grass, which flowers in the morning and,
having been cut, withers in the evening. The comparisons of a person's life
to a watch in the night, to a span, and even to a thought

come to the same thing. The fact that people invent and dream of a long and
enduring life makes them more sparing both toward themselves and toward others,
and they fear that they
will not have enough to sustain life to their very last breath. But let what
is taught here come to mind: the days of our life are few, and therefore the
fewer days remain for us to live, the less grasping and fixated upon property
we ought to be. The statement that we should enjoy good throughout all the
days of our life also refutes those who postpone the benefits of their life
until old age, which they do not know whether they will ever reach.

The final reason is drawn from the portion joined to the person who possesses
riches: this honorable and generous taking and sharing is all that he himself
can receive and enjoy from the things gained by his labor. For, as has been
said, he can carry nothing away from here; rather, just as he came naked into
the world, so he will depart the world naked. This fact will have an effect
full of danger as well as distress: he will bear his departure from life with
pain and grief when he has received no fruit from the things gained and
accumulated by his labor and anxiety. Even if he should live to his thousandth
year, he would nevertheless not be full of days, a phrase used once and again
of the patriarchs. For since he endured so many and such great labors with the
purpose of at last cheering and comforting himself by their means, once the
hope of enjoying them is taken from him, he cannot meet his day of death with
a peaceful and tranquil mind. If the godly die much more peacefully when they
actually attain what they desired in their minds, as we see happen in Jacob's
experience—after seeing Joseph, whom he had thought dead, Genesis 46:30, he
embraced death more readily—how great is the unrest in the minds of misers
when they meet their day of death, because they are deprived of the fruit they
desired and for which they labored so profusely? Thus far concerning the
former part.

It remains to discuss from whom this honorable and lawful use proceeds. The
Prophet identifies its giver as God himself, who gave riches and money. Here
two things are taught. God is the one who gives riches and resources, and
without his gift labor spent acquiring them is in vain. Likewise, it is beyond
controversy that there is some lawful use of riches, since they are God's
gift. Once people have been made masters of riches, who does not determine
that it lies within his own discretion to use them as he pleases? Solomon
refutes this foolish and rash audacity of human beings when he claims this
power for God alone. This gift, like the free bestowal of riches, is indeed of
the kind conferred to some extent upon some wicked people, just as it is upon
all the truly godly in due proportion to their piety and trust in God. Yet the
fact that the wicked share it with the godly does not prevent its being
reckoned among God's free gifts. Let this teach us not so much to pursue the
study of riches greedily and thirstily as to contend with God in prayer that
he not grant us an abundance of them unless, together with their possession,
he also graciously gives us their lawful use. Otherwise it is just as if
someone were to put a sword into a madman's hand so that he might kill
himself. At the same time, let us also learn to pursue virtue and piety
ardently, since no one can abuse them to his own ruin and they are always
useful to their owner and possessor.

When it is said to be God's gift that someone takes his portion from his
riches, Solomon thereby teaches that it is our duty to use our portion for our
own good. For if we are unwilling to be deprived of what is bequeathed to us
by our parents, much less should we allow what God has granted us to be
snatched away through avarice and to slip from our hand.

But one thing must be noted: God has granted only a portion to us and for our
use. See 1 Timothy 4:3. For God claims a portion, Proverbs 3:9–10 and Isaiah
23:18; the

poor claim a portion, Isaiah 23:18; and a wife and children claim a portion, 2
Corinthians 12:14. Thus only this one use of riches is designated here—and it
is not the most excellent of them all—lest anyone suppose, under the pretext
of this doctrine, that he is permitted to devour everything himself.

VERSE 20. But if he does not have much, he will nevertheless remember the days
of his life, for God himself responds to the gladness of his mind.

This verse is an anticipation, whose objection is drawn from disparate
circumstances: someone can indeed be cheered, provided he has many things at
hand by which a richer table and finer clothing may be maintained, but this
gladness and delight of mind cannot belong to him, nor offer itself as a
companion to one whose means are narrow and whose household goods are scanty.
The reply is drawn from contrary circumstances. Although one's resources are
slender, it should nevertheless always come to mind and be kept in memory
that God so compensates for that slenderness with joy of mind that gladness is
not lacking, a gladness supplied by God himself and not by an abundance and
delicacy of food. This is confirmed by Christ's saying in Luke 12: a person's
life does not depend upon possessing much.

Since, therefore, the poor prolong their lives just as the rich do, and the
fruit of a long life is a tranquil and joyful mind, God thus compensates for
slender resources with joy of mind. He does so not only in the poor
themselves but also in their children, who are no less comely than those who
have been delicately fed. The greatest and weightiest testimony to this fact
can be sought from Daniel and his companions, Daniel 1. And since God is said
to instill gladness in people's minds together with food and drink, and it is
agreed that this has been supplied to poor and rich alike, it follows that
this gladness of mind is not peculiar to the rich. It is also relevant that
people of slender fortune are not distracted by such great cares as the rich,
nor are they so exposed to the envy of others.
