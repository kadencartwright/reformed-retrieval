We now proceed to the second question: is Christ's righteousness truly
credited to us as righteousness, or for our justification?

The Roman writers all cry with one voice that God never said this and that
it is found nowhere in the sacred text. Therefore, if we are to devote
ourselves properly to interpreting Scripture, they say that we should not
waste our labor extracting a sense which occurs nowhere in Scripture.

[1679 printed p. 222 | IA leaf 230]

We also acknowledge that the precise words “Christ's righteousness is
credited to us as righteousness” do not occur explicitly and verbatim in
Scripture. Neither do the phrases “Trinity of Persons,” “baptism of infants,”
“institution of the Lord's Day,” and several others. Yet these teachings
are most true and firmly founded in themselves and do not lie outside
Scripture's sense or the interpreter's proper task, provided that equivalent
words occur there or that a clear and necessary consequence follows from
what Scripture states expressly. That is the situation here.

For in the very terms at issue we read of “credited righteousness” in Romans
4:6: “God credits righteousness apart from works.” Again, verse 11 says,
“so that righteousness might also be reckoned to them.” Here is credited
righteousness word for word in Scripture—the very thing the Roman writers
blasphemously call a merely “putative” righteousness and spit upon as
ridiculous, simultaneously insulting the Holy Spirit who speaks this way in
Scripture. They have no basis for accusing us of either a new faith or new
terms to express it. This terminology of credited righteousness is neither
new nor ours. We received it long ago inscribed in the sacred canon,
consecrated and employed by the Holy Spirit for our greatest comfort.

Someone may reply that these passages indeed say righteousness is credited,
but do not say that Christ's righteousness is credited to us for
justification. “To credit righteousness to someone” means only to regard
and judge him righteous, just as “to credit sin” in verse 8 of the same
chapter means nothing but not to forgive it, as in verse 7, or to hold the
person guilty.

We readily grant that this is what the expressions mean, but it confirms
our position. To credit sin is to hold someone guilty of sin, yet there
must be some sin which is credited to him. Likewise, when Paul says more
than once that righteousness is credited to us, his phrase indeed means
that we are regarded as righteous, but there must be some righteousness by
which we are so justified. This is not our righteousness, as Paul has
sufficiently proved in the preceding chapters. It is therefore another's
righteousness; and if another's, then Christ's alone. If it is Christ's, it
becomes ours by crediting. The point we seek thus plainly follows: Christ's
righteousness is credited to us for justification. Paul twice says that
righteousness is credited; and since this righteousness belongs to no one
else, it necessarily follows that Christ's righteousness is credited to us
as righteousness.

They may answer that we are indeed justified by Christ's righteousness,
but by an infused and inherent righteousness. This is most truly and
properly called Christ's, because his death merited it and his Spirit
infused it. I reply that infused or inherent righteousness may rightly be
called Christ's for both those reasons. Yet insofar as it inheres in us, it
may also be called ours, as indeed Scripture calls it in Titus 3:5. This,
however, does not address our present proposition, which concerns the word
“credited.” Paul says that the righteousness in the passage under
discussion is credited, not infused. Crediting righteousness and infusing
it are quite distinct, as Romans 4 itself proves in at least two ways.

[1679 printed p. 223 | IA leaf 231]

First, verse 6 says that God credits righteousness “apart from works.”
Infused righteousness is not apart from works but includes them, and does
so as the formal constituent of justification according to the Roman
position, which asserts and contends that a person is justified by works.
We have already demolished their fiction that the works Paul discusses are
only works done before or without faith, and not works proceeding from
infused grace or righteousness.

Second, Paul treats crediting righteousness in verse 6 and not crediting sin
in verse 8 as equivalent. But forgiveness, or the non-crediting of sins, is
far different from the infusion of righteousness. The infusion and the
crediting of righteousness, which Paul treats here, must consequently be
equally distinct.

This, then, was our first proof. The Roman writers and even some of our own
hiss at credited righteousness as an unheard-of monstrosity and some
chimera of our invention. Yet we find it written expressly in Scripture;
and since this righteousness belongs to Christ alone, we have proved quite
plainly that Christ's righteousness is credited to us for justification.
