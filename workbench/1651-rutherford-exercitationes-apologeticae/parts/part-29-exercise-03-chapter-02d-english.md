17. Before I proceed to vindicate other passages, I shall show here that the
Arminians can and must be regarded as enemies of divine grace no less than the
Pelagians. For they teach that the act of faith and of free conversion to God
is a free, contingent, resistible act produced by the persuasive action of the
Word. *Thus Grevinchovius speaks: The form* (he says) *of faith is assent, and
assent is from the free will. Thus Corvinus: For just as* (he says) *the will
is free by its nature, so faith is an act of obedience, whose nature also
includes being free.* Therefore the act of faith and conversion is considered
either as

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 544.]

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 32, fol. 27, p. 530.]

[1651 logical printed p. 451 | IA leaf 469 | PDF p. 469]

supernatural or as free. In the former manner, namely, insofar as it is
supernatural, it is not a free act of obedience commanded by God and worthy
of praise or reward. For according to them, what is not free is not obedience,
is not worthy of praise or blame, and does not fall under a command. Therefore
the act of faith, insofar as it is free—that is, insofar as it comes from
choice alone, as from a free active principle—is worthy of praise and is the
sort of thing to which a crown of glory, a reward, and a prize are due. And
what grosser teaching is offered by Pelagius, Caelestius, and the heretics most
hostile to God's grace than that which moves God to choose this man and not
that one, Peter and not Judas? For the act of conversion, insofar as it is
supernatural, is in no way from free choice and consequently is in no way
obedience; nor is the supernatural character of the act in any way from the
influence of created choice, but from the influence of God's grace. It is a
later quality superadded to the act insofar as it is free or contingent and
commanded by God. Thus it is from free choice that there is assent and
conversion rather than dissent and non-conversion, and from grace that the
assent is supernatural and gracious. Grevinchovius proves this as follows:
*the law of God immediately commands and forbids that which it is in man's
power to do or not to do. But the goodness and badness of an act are not in
man's power except by reason of the act.* He therefore means that the act
properly falls under an affirmative or negative command, while the gracious
or evil character of the act does so not properly but only secondarily. Hence
the act insofar as it is a free act is in our power, but not the act

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 388.]

[1651 logical printed p. 452 | IA leaf 470 | PDF p. 470]

insofar as it is supernatural. It follows from this that all the praise, and
the entire praise, for our conversion is due wholly to human choice, pure and
simple, and in no way to God, because something in man is worthy of praise
insofar as it is free. And thus, because conversion is essentially a free act
and an act of obedience, it will be worthy of praise in that respect; and
something is not worthy of praise insofar as it is not free. Therefore
conversion insofar as it is supernatural—since in that respect it is not a
free act, but comes from a principle that is not free, namely God's grace,
which acts determinately and not indifferently—is not worthy of praise in us.
This man will therefore boast in himself, not in God.

18. From this let the reader see how falsely Corvinus asserts that free choice,
not simply but assisted by grace, believes and converts itself, since neither
prevenient nor concomitant grace contributes anything to the act of conversion
insofar as it is free, an act of obedience, worthy of praise, and commanded by
God. For the influence of grace, as they teach it, does not terminate in the
will insofar as the will is the active principle. For in that case God would
command what he himself effected, which the Arminians openly mock in their
Confession. If they say that the moral and persuasive influence of grace
terminates in the will, which it awakens from stupor, I recognize both
Pelagian phrasing and Pelagian style—as though a man paid the debts with which
I was overwhelmed because he persuaded me to pay them myself, or as though a
man freed and released me when bound in iron chains because he persuaded me to
free myself. What sane man would imagine, even in his dreams, that to persuade
is

[Marginalia: *Against Molina*, ch. 32, fol. 27.]

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Confession*, ch. 17, thesis 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 453 | IA leaf 471 | PDF p. 471]

to assist by adding strength? But the influence of prevenient grace terminates not in
the will but in conversion, and not in conversion simply but in its
supernatural character, which is what Grevinchovius says. *The manner of
acting* (freely) *belongs to choice; but the manner of acting thus is the work
of piety.* Yet concomitant grace contributes nothing to the free act either.
For to be causative is to be either efficient, material, formal, or final;
otherwise it is not a cause. But if grace contributes being at all, it must
contribute the being that belongs to grace, not the being free, because grace
is a cause acting in the manner of nature and is not a vital cause. It
therefore does not confer being formally free. For being formally free flows
from a cause that is free, elective, and vital. If concomitant grace
efficiently confers being free, it must efficiently determine. For efficiently
to confer being free is efficiently to effect willing, and thus to remove
indifference toward willing and unwilling. Yet they teach that for grace
efficiently to determine the will is to overthrow freedom. But they will say
that grace determines the will by a partial and collateral efficiency, not a
total and prior one. Let that collateral efficiency of grace be granted: it is
either prior to the collateral efficiency of the will, simultaneous with it,
or later. If it is prior, grace will determine the will by a prior motion,
which they will deny. If simultaneous, there will be no reason why the will
wills unless grace simultaneously contributes its influence; and if grace
does not contribute

[Marginalia: *Against Ames*, pp. 201–202.]

[1651 logical printed p. 454 | IA leaf 472 | PDF p. 472]

its influence, the failure to act will not be imputed to the human will. For
so they argue against our physical predetermination. And if grace
simultaneously contributes its influence, the will will be necessitated to
accommodate its own simultaneous influence; otherwise there would be an
action of the first cause without an action of the second cause, which is
absurd. But if the influence of concomitant grace is later than the influence
of the will, then the will will determine cooperating grace, and cooperating
grace will not determine the will. For that which determines, in its act of
determining, cannot be later than what is determined, but must be prior.

19. But I know how Corvinus will answer here. He means that grace and free
choice are partial causes of a good work, not by a partiality of the effect—as
though one part of the effect were from grace and the other from free
choice—but by a partiality of the cause, such that the whole effect is at once
from grace and free choice. This is how he expounds Grevinchovius, but he
rather distorts him. For Grevinchovius understands a partiality of the effect:
he says that the manner of acting belongs to choice and the manner of acting
thus to piety. What could be clearer? 2. According to Arminian belief, freedom
and grace cannot be combined by a partiality of the cause, because freedom is
a free, indifferent, elective cause, but grace is a cause determined to
produce one effect. And for them these causes are distinguished as the
contingent and the necessary, which according to Arminius stand infinitely
apart in opposition and contradiction, and

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 32, fol. 27, p. 530.]

[Marginalia: p. 201.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, reply to article 31, arguments 58, 148.]

[1651 logical printed p. 455 | IA leaf 473 | PDF p. 473]

because they divide the whole range of being, they cannot be joined together
to produce one contingent effect, such as conversion. For then the effect
would be partly necessary and partly contingent, which will be a grave
absurdity of the great Arminius's new metaphysics. If only he could extricate
himself here!

20. Nor is it from God's grace that conversion rather than non-conversion is
in a man. *For that an act of the will* (says Arminius) *has this essence rather
than that one, or is this act rather than another—so that it is a forbidden
act rather than one not forbidden* (that is, conversion rather than
non-conversion, or the reverse), *against this command rather than that one,
depends on the freedom of the will with respect to the species of the act.*
Therefore the fact that this man is converted and that one is not comes from
the will alone, by which the converted man distinguishes himself from the
unconverted. Yet Corvinus is not ashamed to attribute all activity in
spiritual things to grace. What could be more contradictory to Arminius? Nor
can the use of grace be said to be from grace. For Grevinchovius says:
*efficacious aid does not precede the efficiency of the will in the order of
causality, and therefore does not act upon the will before it influences the
act or action of the will, but together with the will* (once prevenient grace
has already awakened it) *effects the act.* From this I prove that the use of
grace is not principally from grace but principally from free choice, contrary
to what Corvinus says: the use of grace is the actual application of grace to
the act; but

it is not grace but free choice that actually applies grace to the act.
Therefore the use of grace is principally from free choice, not from grace.
The major premise is clear from the definition of the use of grace. Proof of
the minor: neither prevenient nor cooperating grace applies grace to the work.
Therefore free choice alone performs this. Proof of the antecedent: because
prevenient grace is a certain potentiality, it needs another grace to actuate
it. Therefore it cannot apply grace to the work. For no potentiality as such
actuates itself; in that case it would be more excellent than itself, since
everything that actuates is more excellent than what can be actuated. Nor
does cooperating grace apply grace to the work, because if cooperating grace
does not apply the will to the gracious act and does not precede the efficiency
of the will in the order of causality, it cannot apply grace to the work. But
the former is true. Therefore the latter is also true. On this point Tilenus
is more candid than the other Arminians, for he admits plainly that the will
uses grace. He therefore compares grace to a sword that an idle soldier allows
to be consumed by rust and dulled in its sheath, but which a vigorous soldier
uses in battle. So too the Jesuit Molina: *It comes from the free choice's own*
(he says) *industry and greater or lesser effort that each person gains more or
less from the talents given to him.* But it is clear to everyone that Corvinus
and the Arminians do not depart from the Jesuits' doctrine on this point by so
much as a trace or footprint, if you except congruous calling. Indeed, both

the aid of grace.* So also Cassian, as Prosper says: *For since the nations

says) *in expectation we consider not only that knowledge but also the highest

21. Indeed, in this whole dispute over the efficacy of grace, nothing else is
now being asked than what was formerly asked between Augustine and the
Pelagians. Thus Prosper, *Against the Conference*: *The great question is
whether God has mercy on us because we have supplied the beginning of a good
will, or whether we obtain the beginning of a good will because God has mercy.*
Thus Prosper and Hilary contended with the Massilians, and Augustine says:
*The issue between us is whether this grace precedes or follows man's will;
that is, to put it more plainly, whether it is given to us because we will, or
whether through it God brings about that we will.* And *Hypognostica* 3: *In

22. I proceed to other passages. *Philippians 1:29. It has been given to you
on behalf of Christ not only to believe in him, but also to suffer for him.
Philippians 2:13. For God brings about in you willing and accomplishing
according to his good pleasure.* Hence, he who grants belief and effects
willing itself predetermines the will through grace to believe, suffer, will,
and accomplish. The efficacy of grace will thus be referred back to the God
who wills and determines, and not to free choice.

Reply 1. Corvinus says that *God grants that we believe* because he grants the
power of believing and because he concurs with the act of believing by
supernatural aid. And the Remonstrants say that God concurs by both a moral and
a physical influence; and at the Hague Conference: *Although actual resistance
is removed, the power of resisting remains.* In this way they maintain that

God effects willing in us. Answer 1. Although he grants the power of willing and concurs with the act by
a collateral and indifferent concurrence, in this manner he no more grants
belief and effects willing than he grants unbelief and effects unwilling. 2.
In this manner he grants it on the hypothesis that we will—that is, after we
have effected willing in ourselves, he effects willing. Is one the cause of a
thing's being when he grants being after the thing already has being? A
wondrous mystery! 3. If God removes actually resisting, the dispute is ended.
They freely concede that the power of being unwilling remains in those in whom
God effects willing, but less candidly. For they maintain that actually
resisting is removed in a manner consistent with the nature of freedom—that
is, so that indifference remains—and thus actually resisting is no more
removed than actually not resisting. 4. If God effects willing and
accomplishing because he effects the power of willing, and actually effects it
if we will, then God effects willing and accomplishing in us not according to
his own good pleasure but according to man's good pleasure, which Cassian and
the Massilians taught. Reply 2. Suárez says that [*God's working that we will*]
is nothing other than his working something in us by which we are disposed or
inclined to will, and doing so with the purpose and intention that we will.
Answer. By that disposition it either comes about that we will undeviatingly,
and thus we have established our point; or that we will deviably, and thus he
no more effects willing than not willing. For since God's intention is a
purely immanent act, it does not by a transitive act place willing or not
willing in us. If

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On the Aids of Grace*, bk. 3, ch. 34, fol. 6.]

[1651 logical printed p. 460 | IA leaf 478 | PDF p. 478]

he means, however, that God effects willing in us by a congruous motion because
he foreknows that willing will follow from this motion and not from that one,
administered in such a way—namely, after we have willed and he foreknows by
middle knowledge that we will will—then he effects willing. A tale certainly
most worthy of the Jesuits!

Reply 3 of the Remonstrants. 1. *The subject here* (they say) *is not the
efficacy by which God works faith in our mind and will, but the efficacy that
God uses in us who already believe. For he is called “the one working,” not
“the one who has worked.”* Answer 1. But what is that efficacy which he uses in
believers? Is it not willing, accomplishing, believing, persevering, and
working out salvation with trembling? These are free acts of precisely the
same nature as the first free act of our conversion. For the freedom of nature
remains in each case, and therefore in each case God effects willing. 2. They
say that the subject is not that efficacy by which God works faith. This is a
wretched conjecture from the word *working*, as though there were no people
among the Philippians who were yet to be converted. A brazen dream. Reply 2 in
the same place: *He does not say, “God brings about that you will well and
rightly,” but “that you will.” He presupposes that there is already a good
will, because they were already believers; but he says that God, lest that good
will grow weak and as it were lie idle, awakens, kindles, and inflames it on
his part, so that, insofar as it lies with him, he keeps it in perpetual
vigor.* Answer 1. God effects willing undeviatingly; therefore he effects
willing well and rightly. For the reasoning is equal in each case. 2. Because
they were already believers, they wish to presuppose a good will in the

Reply 4. *What could be more inept than “Work out your salvation with
trembling,” and immediately to add this reason: “Because God effects in you
that you work out your salvation with fear and trembling, with an efficacy by
which it is impossible that you should not work out your salvation with fear
and trembling”?* Answer. This appears inept to the scale of human prudence when
it weighs God's wisdom. For because our faith grows weak and we fear lest God
desert us in temptations, faith must be confirmed in the course of obedience
by hope and the promise of divine strength and omnipotence, by which he
invincibly upholds and strengthens his own lest they fail. And because we are
here moral agents, it is no small consolation that by invincible power God
effects in us the very thing that, from weakness of faith, we repeatedly fear
might not happen. So Isaiah 38:21. The command, *Let a lump of figs be applied
to Hezekiah's ulcer*, is wholly the same and parallel; yet God had promised to
cure the king's disease with an efficacy by which he could not fail to extend
his life another fifteen years. Christ could not fail to lay down his life for
his sheep, and yet he received from the Father the command to lay down his
life, John 10:10. By invincible omnipotence God had promised that he would
drive out the Canaanites, and in such a way that they could not fail to be
driven out, Deuteronomy 3:26–28. And yet God exhorts Joshua to be strong and of
good courage, Joshua 1:5–6, and to gird himself for battle. By his omnipotence
God effects that the earth

to bring about its outcome undeviatingly, yet the casting of the lot was

principle, namely, that a contingent means cannot be subordinated to God's
irresistible action and intention, which is utterly false. 2. It was necessary
for them to work out salvation with fear, because unless they did this they
would trust and hope in vain that God would effect willing and accomplishing

were mere trunks and stumps that behaved merely passively; rather, God
efficaciously effects willing and accomplishing in us as we will and as we
accomplish through God's efficacious grace. But the exhortation would be
ridiculous if we did nothing, as the sophists suppose. For then God would
effect willing and accomplishing undeviatingly even while we were asleep and
unconscious, which is absurd; and thus he would exhort stumps while he himself
irresistibly did everything. But for God to exhort us freely to

do what is our duty, when he himself efficaciously determines us as we act most freely, is not only

supernatural works would by no means belong to God. Reply 5. *It is absurd
that God should effect in them, through predetermining grace, the very
solicitude and care for working out salvation to which he was exhorting them.*
Answer. For them to effect that solicitude through free choice apart from
determining grace is dishonorable to God's grace. For thus it is false that
care and solicitude are required through such grace, and our men have answered
this a thousand times. For I ask whether God decreed that we should be holy,
and whether he decreed it absolutely or conditionally. If conditionally, I
wish to know when the Arminians supplied the condition; to this day they have
never supplied it. If absolutely, then solicitude does not conflict with an
invincible decree, and for the same reason neither does it conflict with the
irresistible efficacy of grace. Reply 6. *The exhortation is addressed to all
