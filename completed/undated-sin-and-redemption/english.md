# On Sin and Redemption

*An undated, doubtfully attributed consilium; a fresh translation from CO X/1 156–157.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 156]

The first of all human beings was created by God with an immortal soul but a mortal body. God himself adorned him with his own likeness and thus made him wholly pure from every crime. He commanded him to feed on all the things in his most delightful garden, with the sole exception of the fruit of that tree in which all life lay hidden. God willed him to keep his hands from it on this condition: on the very day he first touched its fruit, he would die. But he touched it. Consequently he died at once and ceased to be like God. This, then, was the first origin of death.

That this is true is proved by these words: “Whenever you eat, you shall surely die.” The immediately following passage testifies that he ate after being deceived, as does that wise Hebrew in these words: “Through the devil's envy death entered the world” (Wisdom 2:24). Paul agrees with this in several passages.

That man was therefore driven into exile with all his descendants so that there, having lost the horn of plenty, he might in utter misery experience every labor and every evil, and in that way seek his livelihood—now sweating, now suffering cold, often hungry, often thirsty, always wretched.

At length, however, God took pity on this man, so afflicted and unhappy in every way. God ordered the sentence which he himself had passed to remain in force, but he gave that only Son, and therefore the Son most beloved by him, as an expiation and victim for so great a crime. By this truly marvelous and unprecedented exercise of mercy, he commended his own love toward us far more than if he had rescinded his sentence.

And so Christ, the Son of God, conceived under the overshadowing of the Holy Spirit and born of a virgin, was finally raised upon

### [CO X/1, p. 157]

the cross and by his own death delivered the human race from everlasting death. Hence comes the first resurrection unto life. John did not hesitate to call its participants blessed in the Apocalypse (20:6), and in chapter 5 of his Gospel he says that the same people have passed from death to life (verse 24).

Therefore, whoever places every hope of his salvation in Christ's cross will be free from the second death, since he participates in the first resurrection. But whoever despises so great a benefit of God will have Christ as judge of such impious and insulting contempt, not as redeemer from the common destruction. That judgment of Christ will have the force of the second death, not because of that common crime, but because of each person's own private crime. This, therefore, will be the origin of the second death.

John Calvin.

## Translator's notes

1. The work's attribution is genuinely uncertain. The only manuscript known to the CO editors is not in Calvin's hand, and they explicitly declined to offer their own judgment about its authenticity. The closing name is transmitted by that copy, not verified here as an autograph signature.
2. The description of the forbidden tree as one “in which all life lay hidden” is preserved as printed. It sits awkwardly beside Genesis's distinction between the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and should not be silently harmonized.
3. *Quoties comederitis morte moriemini* is not a verbatim Vulgate citation and unexpectedly uses a plural verb. The translation conveys its sense without correcting the Latin witness.
4. “That wise Hebrew” introduces Wisdom 2:24. The wording is translated from the Latin printed here, not copied from a modern English Bible.
5. *Copiae cornu* is the classical “horn of plenty,” used here for the abundance forfeited by exile from Eden.
6. “Because of that common crime ... because of each person's own private crime” makes explicit the ellipsis in *non publici facinoris illius sed privati cuiusque*. The contrast appears to be between Adam's common or universal offense and each contemner's personal offense.
