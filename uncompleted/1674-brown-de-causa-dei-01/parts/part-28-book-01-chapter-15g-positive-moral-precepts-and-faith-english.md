From what has been said thus far it is sufficiently evident that these
statements of our adversary do not prevent some positive precept from being
called moral. Indeed, when “moral” denotes nothing other than perpetually
binding, no sane person will deny that some positive precepts can be such. For
beyond doubt the same legislator who, by his absolute dominion and not merely
on account of some temporal or typical relation, bound people in this age to
do or omit something of this kind could, if it had seemed good to him, have
bound others in another age in the same way—indeed, everyone to the very end
of the world. But you will say that such a precept cannot be called moral, but
must always be called positive. I reply that those who explain “moral” in this
way are under no law requiring them to take the term “moral” in that sense,
for the force of the word suggests nothing of the kind. Moreover, this better
serves the hypothesis of those who maintain that the Sabbath precept still
binds us, and that it should therefore be called moral in order to distinguish
it from purely positive precepts which have now been abrogated. And if the
adversaries conceded that this command still bound

[1674 logical printed p. 218 | IA leaf 236 | PDF p. 236]

us, the dispute over the word would quickly cease, and it would make no
difference whether it were called moral, perpetually positive, or perpetually
binding.

Since, however, the adversaries wish every command which depends upon God’s
free choice, and does not flow necessarily from the nature and attributes of
God and of man, to be positive, let us see whether there is any such command
which is nevertheless moral.

First, the precept concerning faith in Christ and evangelical repentance does
not flow necessarily from God’s nature and attributes. For without any injury
to his nature or attributes he could have determined to deliver no fallen
human being from the sentence of death, any more than he delivered the angels,
or to prescribe no means for that end. Nor does this flow necessarily from the
nature or attributes of man, for people eternally condemned would still have
remained human beings. Yet this precept is moral, as the adversary himself
concedes. Therefore there is such a thing as a moral-positive precept.

What does the adversary answer? “What do I hear?” he says on pages 30–31. “Is
the precept of faith a dogma? Is it so because it was God’s εὐδοκία, good
pleasure, to hand Christ over for us? On the contrary, it is an eternal truth:
once a mediator was given, it was just and necessary that the sinner be saved
through faith in him, and God could command nothing else. In the same way,
once man is posited, it is necessary that he love God.” Reply: 1. We know that
“the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ has blessed us with every
spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ; that he chose us in him
— and predestined us for adoption as sons to himself through Jesus Christ,
according to the benevolent purpose of his will; and that in that beloved one
we have redemption — from the riches of his grace, which he caused to abound
toward us in all wisdom and prudence; and that he made known to us the mystery
of his will according to his gracious good pleasure,” and so forth (Ephesians
1). Consequently, we know that from his mere good pleasure he sent a redeemer,
and that through faith in him he saved, and continues to save, some of those
human beings. Hence, whether the precept of faith is called a δόγμα, dogma, or
not, this much is certain: neither God’s nature nor human nature necessarily
demanded it. 2. Although it was an eternal truth, and necessary, that once a
mediator was given the sinner should be saved through faith in him, it was not
an eternal truth, or absolutely necessary in itself, either that man should
fall or that fallen man should be restored through a mediator. Therefore it
was not necessary, whether with respect to God’s nature or man’s, that man be
bound to faith in a mediator. Consequently, faith in a mediator is not a moral
duty—that is, one which necessarily flows from the nature and attributes of
God or man—but flowed from God’s entirely free good pleasure. 3. Who will say
that once man is posited it is necessary for him to love God? What he meant to
say was that it is necessary for man to be bound to love God. But once man was
posited, was it necessary for him to be bound to faith in Christ?

“Our author,” he goes on to say, “appears not to resist the Remonstrants, who
say that Christ died so that God might, at his pleasure, enter into a covenant
with us on any possible condition—as Socinianism consists in thinking that
God can, at his pleasure, save man in any manner whatever—as though, once
Christ was given, we could be righteous otherwise than through faith in him.
He confuses things necessary in the first and second orders, which we discussed
above in axioms 3 and 4 and which depend upon the hypothesis of the established
order of things.” Reply: 1. It is false that his colleague does not resist the
Remonstrants. Nor, when we say that the command of faith flows not from God’s
nature but from his free good pleasure, is it necessary that we have anything
in common with the Remonstrants. 2. It is false that Socinianism consists in
thinking

[1674 logical printed p. 219 | IA leaf 237 | PDF p. 237]

that God can, at his pleasure, save man in any manner whatever. For there are
those who know how to overthrow Socinianism and nevertheless maintain that by
his absolute power God could have saved man otherwise than through Christ’s
redemption. 3. Although, once Christ is given, we cannot be saved or be
righteous otherwise than through faith in him, this notwithstanding, the
precept of faith can be said to flow from the divine pleasure. For once the
Sabbath precept is posited, people cannot fail to be bound to observe the
Sabbath, yet he will concede that this obligation flows not from God’s nature
but from his good pleasure. 4. In axiom 3 he called things necessary in the
second order “mediated moral duties founded upon human nature”: given human
nature, its creation in the image of God, the established nature of things,
and their order, they could not have happened otherwise and could not have
failed to happen. But faith in Christ has no place among these. For given human
nature, its creation in the image of God, and the established nature of all
the things first created, man could have remained unfallen, in which case
faith in Christ would not have been necessary. Fallen man could also have
remained unredeemed—here each of us abstracts from the divine decree.
Therefore this faith does not follow necessarily from the creation of human
nature in the image of God.

He next replies, second: “Do we have life from faith as a matter of mere
pleasure? Does faith here contribute through its material act, or only through
its formal aspect and correlate, as they say?” Reply: Whether the matter stands
so or otherwise—for there is no reason to be carried beyond the limits of our
present dispute, although on this point we agree with him—it makes no
difference to the subject now under discussion. For the precept of faith in
Christ the Mediator, however faith is taken, always flows from God’s free good
pleasure.

Third: “He does not distinguish τὸ ‘living by one’s deeds’ from ‘living by
faith,’ as in Habakkuk 2:4. The person who lives by faith in Christ will live
on account of the righteousness of him in whom he believes, and believes that
he is justified not as ἐργαζομένῳ, ‘one who works,’ but believes in δικαιοῦντι
τὸν ἀσεβῆ, ‘him who justifies the ungodly’ (Romans 4:5). This is to confuse the
law with the Gospel and to contradict the Apostle (Galatians 3:11–12).” Reply:
How will he prove that in this way we confuse the law with the Gospel, or fail
to distinguish τὸ “living by deeds” from “living by faith”? We are speaking of
faith taken in its evangelical sense, and yet we say that the precept of this
faith flows neither from God’s nature nor from man’s nature, and therefore is
positive according to the adversary. And because it is moral, by his own
admission, we conclude that there is such a thing as a moral-positive precept.
