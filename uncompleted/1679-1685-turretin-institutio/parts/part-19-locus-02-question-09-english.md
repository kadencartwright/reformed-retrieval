QUESTION IX

ON THE APOCRYPHAL BOOKS

Are Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, the first two books of Maccabees,
Baruch, and the additions to Esther and Daniel rightly included in the number
of canonical books?—No, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: Whence the apocryphal books are so called.]

I. The books are called *apocryphal* not because their authors are unknown,
for there are both canonical books whose author is unknown and some
apocryphal books whose author is known; nor because they were read only
privately and not publicly, for various ones were also read publicly. Rather,
it is either because they were removed “from the hiding place” (ἀπὸ τῆς
κρύπτης), from that sacred chest in which the sacred books were stored, as
Epiphanius and Augustine hold; or because their authority was hidden and
suspect, and consequently their use was also hidden, since the church was not
accustomed to read them to confirm the authority of ecclesiastical doctrines,
as Jerome has it in his preface to the Proverbs of Solomon; or, what is more
accurate, because they are of uncertain and obscure origin. Their obscure
origin was not made clear to those through whose testimony the authority of
the Scriptures has come down to us, as Augustine says in *The City of God*,
book 5, chapter 24.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question does not concern the books of the Old and New Testaments
which we regard as canonical, for the Roman Catholics admit these as canonical
along with us. Nor does it concern all the apocryphal books, for there are some
which the Roman Catholics reject no less than we do, such as 3 and 4 Esdras, 3
and 4 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, and so forth. Rather, it concerns only
Tobit, Judith, Baruch, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, 1 and 2 Maccabees, and the
additions to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 94 | IA leaf 148 | PDF p. 148]

Esther and Daniel, which the Roman Catholics include in the number of the
canonical books. We exclude them from that number, not because they do not
contain many true and pious things, but because they do not possess the marks
of canonical books.

[Marginal heading: Why the apocryphal books are excluded from the canon.]

III. There are various reasons. 1. The Jewish church, to which the oracles of
God were entrusted, Romans 3:2, never regarded them as canonical but retained
the same canon as we do, as Josephus testifies in book 1 of *Against Apion*,
and as Becanus admits in *Manual of Controversies*, book 1, chapter 1, and
Stapleton, volume 1, book 3, controversy 5, chapter 14. This could not have
happened without grave guilt if those books had been entrusted to the Jews no
less than the others; yet this was never charged against them either by Christ
or by the apostles. Nor should the canon of the Jews here be distinguished
from the canon of Christians, because Christians neither can nor ought to
accept as canonical any other books of the Old Testament than those they
received from the Jews, their “book-bearers,” as Augustine calls them in Psalm
40, “who carry the codices for us who study.” 2. They are nowhere cited by
Christ and the apostles as canonical in the same way as the others. Indeed,
when Christ divides all the books of the Old Testament into three classes—the
law, the Psalms, and the prophets—in Luke 24:44, he clearly approves the canon
of the Jews and excludes from the canon those books which are not contained in
that division. 3. For four hundred years the Christian church acknowledged
the same canonical books as we do, and no others. This is evident from the
Council of Laodicea, canon 59; Melito, bishop of Sardis, who lived in A.D. 16,
in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, book 4, chapter 25; Epiphanius in the
heresy of the Epicureans; Jerome in the *Helmeted Prologue*; Athanasius in his
*Synopsis*; and so forth. 4. Their authors were neither prophets nor
“God-inspired” men (θεόπνευστοι), since the books were written after Malachi,
the last of the prophets; nor were they composed in the Hebrew language, as
the books of the Old Testament were, but in Greek. Hence Josephus in the
passage cited acknowledges that the writings produced among his people after
the time of Artaxerxes do not possess faith and authority equal to the earlier
writings, “because the exact succession of the prophets did not occur” (διὰ τὸ
μὴ γενέσθαι τὴν τῶν προφητῶν ἀκριβῆ διαδοχήν).

IV. The style and subject matter of the books proclaim them to be human, not
divine, so that a person would have to possess exceedingly little discernment
not to perceive that they proceeded from human effort, although some are more
excellent than others. For apart from the fact that their style does not savor
of the majesty
and simplicity of the divine style, but reeks of the faults and weaknesses of
human ingenuity—in the vanity, flattery, curiosity, affectation, and untimely
display of learning and eloquence which occur not infrequently—so many things
appear in them which are not only frivolous and absurd but also false,
superstitious, and contradictory that it is readily apparent that these are
human and not divine writings. We give a few examples from among their many
errors. Tobit attributes a lie to the angel who in chapter 5, verse 15, says
that he is Azariah the son of Ananias, and in chapter 12, verse 15, that he is
Raphael, an angel of the Lord. In chapter 6 the same book gives magical counsel
for driving away a demon with the smoke of a fish’s liver, contrary to
Christ’s statement in Matthew 17:21. It attributes to Raphael the offering of
prayers, which belongs to Christ alone, chapter 12, verse 12. The book of
Judith celebrates the deed of Simeon, chapter 9, verse 2, which Jacob strikes
with an anathema in Genesis 49; it praises the fraud and lies of Judith,
chapter 11, which accord poorly with piety; worse still, it says that she asks
God’s blessing upon her frauds and lies, chapter 9, verse 13. There is no
mention of the city of Bethulia in the Scriptures, nor does any trace occur,
either in Josephus or Philo, who wrote about Jewish affairs, of the deliverance
recorded there. The author of Wisdom falsely asserts that he was king in
Israel, chapter 9, verses 7–8, so that he might be taken for Solomon, although
in chapter 4, verse 2, he alludes to athletic contests which were not yet in
use among the Greeks in Solomon’s time. Add that in chapter 8, verses 19–20,
he maintains the Pythagorean “transmigration of souls” (μετεμψύχωσιν), and in
chapter 14, verses 15–16, he tells falsehoods about the origin of idols. In
chapter 46, verse 20, Sirach attributes to Samuel what was done by an evil
spirit summoned by wicked arts, 1 Samuel 28:11; he falsely

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 95 | IA leaf 149 | PDF p. 149]

relates that Elijah will return bodily, chapter 48, verse 10; and in the
prologue he excuses his own mistakes.

V. There are so many contradictions and absurdities in the additions to
Esther and Daniel that Sixtus of Siena rejects them outright. Baruch says that
in the fifth year after Jerusalem’s destruction he read his book to Jeconiah
and all the people in Babylon, although Jeconiah was in prison and Baruch had
been taken with Jeconiah into Egypt after the murder of Gedaliah, Jeremiah
43:10. He mentions the altar of the Lord, chapter 1, verse 10, although none
existed when the temple had been destroyed. The books of Maccabees disagree
with each other more than once; compare 1 Maccabees 1:16 with 9:5, 28, and book
1, chapter 10. The “suicide” (Αὐτοχειρία) of Razis is praised in 2 Maccabees
14:42. “Will-worship” (Ἐθελοθρησκεία) is commended in book 2, chapter 12, verse
42, when Judas offers a sacrifice for the dead contrary to the law. The author
excuses his own immaturity and weakness, and complains about the laborious
trouble of stitching together his patchwork from the five books of Jason of
Cyrene, chapters 2:24 and 15:39. If anyone desires more concerning these books,
he should consult Rainolds, Chamier, Du Moulin, Spanheim, and others who have
treated this argument extensively and soundly.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VI. The canon of faith is one thing; the canon of ecclesiastical reading is
another. The question here does not concern the canon in the latter sense. For
it is certain that these apocryphal books were at times also read publicly in
the church, but only for the edification of the people, not for confirming the
authority of doctrines, as Jerome has it in his preface to the books of
Solomon. Thus the *legends*, which take their name from being read and which
contained the sufferings of the martyrs, were read publicly in the church,
although they were not regarded as canonical. Rather, the question here
concerns the canon of faith.

VII. The word *canon* is used in two ways among the fathers, either broadly or
strictly. In the first sense it embraces not only the canon of faith but also
the canon of ecclesiastical reading. In this way the Third Council of Carthage,
canon 47, must be understood when it calls these books canonical—if indeed that
canon is not “surreptitiously introduced” (παρείσακτος), because it mentions
Pope Boniface, who was not yet pope at that time; hence the monk Surius assigns
this canon not to the Third but to the Seventh Council of Carthage, volume 1,
*Councils*, page 546. It speaks not strictly and properly of the canon of faith,
but broadly of the canon of reading, as the synod expressly says, since it also
wills the sufferings of the martyrs to be read. Augustine must likewise be
understood in this way when he calls them canonical. For he makes two orders
of canonical books: first, those which are admitted by all the churches and
about which there has never been any doubt; second, those which were admitted
by only some and were customarily read from the pulpit. He judges that these
are not to be valued as highly as the former and that their authority is far
unequal to theirs, *Against the Manichaeans*, book 2, chapter 5. But those
apocryphal books are adulterated, false, and wicked writings; in *The City of
God*, book 5, chapter 24, he calls them “fables of the Scriptures, which are
called apocryphal.” The canon in the strict sense, however, is taken for that
which possesses divine and infallible authority for proving doctrines of
faith. Jerome takes the word *canon* in this way when he excludes them from the
canon. Thus Augustine understands the word *canonical* more broadly than
Jerome, while Jerome understands the word *apocryphal* more broadly than
Augustine—not only for openly false and fabulous books, but also for those
which, although read in the church, were not employed to prove doctrines of
faith. Thus the statements of these fathers, which seem to clash with one
another in this matter, are easily reconciled. Cajetan, in his commentary on
Esther near the end, explains the fathers’ statements in this way, saying that
both the words of councils and those of doctors are to be brought back to
Jerome’s standard: these books are not canonical, that is, regulative for
confirming matters of faith, although they can be called canonical, that is,
regulative for the edification of the faithful, inasmuch as they were received
into the biblical canon for this purpose, and so forth. Denis the Carthusian
agrees with this in the proem to the book of Tobit.

VIII. The Roman Catholics distinguish in vain between the canon of the Jews
and the canon of Christians. For although our canon, taken as a whole for all
the books of the Old and New Testaments in which it consists adequately, is
not admitted equally by the Jews, who reject the New Testament, nevertheless,
if it is taken partially with respect to the Old Testament, in which sense we
speak of it here, it is certain that ours does not differ from the canon of the
Jews, because they admitted into the canon no other books than those we
receive.

IX. If the fathers sometimes mention deuterocanonical books, they do not mean
books which are truly and univocally canonical with respect to faith, but only
those which were included in the canon of reading because of the many pious
and useful things which can contribute to edification.

X. The citation of a passage does not immediately prove a book to be
canonical; otherwise Aratus, Menander, and Epimenides, cited by Paul in Acts
17:28; 1 Corinthians 15:33; and Titus 1:12, would be canonical. 2. The things
alleged by the adversaries as citations from the apocryphal books occur in
other canonical books, from which the apostles could therefore have cited
them rather than from the apocryphal books.

XI. If they are joined with the canonical books, it does not follow that they
possess equal authority, but only that they are useful for shaping morals and
learning the history of the times, not for establishing faith.

XII. Although some of the apocryphal books are better and more polished than
the others and contain various ethical teachings of good quality which can
have their use, such as the book of Wisdom and Sirach, nevertheless, because
they contain not a few other things which are false and absurd, they are
rightly removed from the canon of faith.

XIII. Although some doubted the authenticity of certain books of the New
Testament, such as the epistle of James, the second epistle of Peter, the
second and third epistles of John, and Revelation, which were afterward
regarded as canonical by the church, it does not follow that the same could
have happened with respect to the apocryphal books, because the circumstances
of the books of the Old and New Testaments differ in this matter. For the books
of the Old Testament were not given successively in the course of time or to
separate parts of the Christian church. Rather, at one and the same time the
church received from the Jews all the books belonging to it, written in one
codex, after they
had possessed undoubted authority confirmed by Christ himself and the
apostles. But the books of the New Testament were published in parts at
various times and places and were gradually collected into one body. Hence it
happened that some later books, which came into people’s hands more slowly,
were regarded as doubtful by some churches, especially in places remote from
their origin, until their authenticity gradually became known. 2. Although
some epistles and the book of Revelation were disputed in certain churches,
there were nevertheless always far more who received them than who disputed
them. But the apocryphal books were not disputed, because they were always
rejected by the Jewish church.
