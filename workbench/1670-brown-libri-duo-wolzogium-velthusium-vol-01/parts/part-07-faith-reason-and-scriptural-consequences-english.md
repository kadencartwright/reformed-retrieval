XII. In point 2 on pages 29–30 he says that *we rely no more certainly even
upon reason than upon the sayings of God,* and therefore that whatever it
tells us about the nature of things, whether heavenly or earthly, ought to
have the greatest credit with us. *Indeed*, he says, *we believe before we
know, and so forth; provided it is established that God has spoken, we do not
wait until we are confirmed by the evidence of reason.* These statements
would not be badly made—apart from the first sentence, of which more shortly—
nor would the statement he has on page 32: *For who understands the venerable
mysteries handed down concerning the Trinity or the Incarnation? And yet who
does not understand that they are handed down in the Scriptures? Here faith
alone acts and is aided in nothing by Reason.* This would be so, had he not
rendered these statements useless by what he said before, when he affirmed
that Reason is the *rule for interpreting the Scriptures*, and when he later
says that it is the *guide* and calls everything to examination. For if those
statements are true, what he says here will be false; and if what he says here
is true, those statements will be false. For if I believe before I know, as
he says here, I must believe before I have examined or judged what I believe
by the rule of reason. But how shall I know that God said these things unless

[Page 73 | ONB canvas 127 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000127]

I first grasp the meaning of the words, or have examined the words by that
rule and interpreted them rightly? Therefore either what he said before and
is to say afterward must be withdrawn, or it must be acknowledged that the
things said here were said to make sport of his Readers, and that he in fact
thinks very differently.

But what is the meaning of this, *so that we rely no more certainly even upon
Reason than upon his sayings?* And shortly afterward: *Because he who says
these things can no more deceive or be deceived than reason itself?* Does he
not here betray the disposition of a Traitor, who again denies what he
asserts, or at least in some fashion withdraws it? Tell me, *Wolzogen*, does
Reason—as it now exists, and as that Reason of which you speak here exists,
namely the Reason upon which we rely—have all forms of truth enclosed within
itself? Is it the very oracle of truth? If not, how, I ask, can we rely upon
it just as certainly as upon divine sayings? On page 58 of the *Censure of the
Censure* you say that God *has such authority that whatever certainty we seek
from reason in human writings, we ascribe in Scripture to God alone as he
speaks; nor can we employ reason more certainly in human matters than God's
authority in divine matters.* Answer: True, but does God not have greater
authority in divine matters than our corrupted and vitiated Reason has even
in human matters? Is the certainty we have in divine matters from God's
authority not greater than that which flows from reason in human affairs? Who
does not discern *Wolzogen's* disposition here? And when you say that *God can
no more deceive than Reason itself*, do you not believe reason to be just as
infallible as God himself? Your elder *Velthuysen* sings the same tune in *On
the Use of Reason*, and so forth, pages 4–5, when he says that both Philosophy
and Theology, considered objectively, are perfect, complete, certain, and
infallible in their own kinds; that neither in this sense is inferior to the
other; and that both, considered subjectively too, are of equal dignity,

[Page 74 | ONB canvas 128 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000128]

since the truths of each are from God, perfect, certain, and founded upon
infallible foundations. And on pages 24–25 he has the following: *There is no
reason for anyone to be overly concerned about the class from which he seeks
his arguments; rather, let him offer those which appear clearest to him. For
in some matters Scripture is obscure where reason is clear; in others reason
is obscure where Scripture is plain. Nor should people who have truth at
heart and wish to occupy their diligence only with useful matters labor
greatly over which conclusion and argument proceeds from Reason and which
from Scripture, and consequently what is held by faith and what by reason,
since, if we weigh the matter rightly, no argumentation about matters of faith
is given which does not in its own way rest upon reason, because some natural
knowledge is always presupposed.* Let us now see how much *Wolzogen*, together
with his *Velthuysen*, attributes to Reason. Indeed, it is just as much as to
God himself. For *Wolzogen* himself says on page 59 of the *Censure of the
Censure*: *Not only do I attribute as much to God as to Reason—which, note
well, I nevertheless do—but, since we have nothing more certain than reason,
I transfer to God in divine matters the likeness of the highest certainty.*
What could have been said more openly or clearly? Who does not see that he
makes Reason equal to God, although he himself denies this shortly afterward?
But the words which follow in the same place must be noted. *Even if*, he
says, *it is true in some sense that reason is as infallible as God, because
reason is from God and is in nature as it were a voice of God, nevertheless,
because reason becomes the voice of God only with nature as an interpreter,
whereas he himself speaks without an interpreter in Scripture, the author's
own voice is by its nature to be preferred to the voice of an interpreter,
even though both are certain.* Answer: In what sense, I ask, is it true, or
can it be true, that Reason is as infallible as God? How subtle is the
argument by which he tries to prove it? Many things are no less from God than
reason is, yet they must by no means be said to be as infalli-*

[Page 75 | ONB canvas 129 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000129]

*-ble as God himself. Are our Senses not also from God? Yet who will therefore
say that they are infallible? Where now is Wolzogen's reasoning? It certainly
is most fallible. Who would not judge it harsh to say that Reason not yet
corrupted was as infallible as God himself, when even *Wolzogen* himself on
page 33 calls this pure Reason *imperfect*, and likewise on page 66 of the
*Censure of the Censure* calls it *imperfect because finite*? Who does not
know that infallibility is an attribute of God and proper to him, and
therefore must not be attributed absolutely to any creature? Was *Adam's*
Reason, not yet corrupted, fallible? Lamentable experience bore witness that
it was. How absurd, then, to say these things even about corrupted Reason? But
in the same place he replies that *he is not treating corrupted reason, but
reason which has been purified by the aid of Philosophy in natural matters
and, in heavenly matters, by a certain singular power of the Holy Spirit.*
Answer: 1. He is speaking about the Reason by which we now judge things, and
this is no other than that which now exists, namely corrupted and vitiated.
2. Is Reason purified in natural matters by the aid of Philosophy alone? Is
it not also purified by the aid of the divine word insofar as it speaks about
these matters, since on his own testimony the highest credit must be given to
that word on whatever it speaks about? 3. Can Philosophy so purify reason that
it becomes at last as pure as it was before the fall, or can it even render
reason purer? If neither can be said, how can *Wolzogen* imagine that it can
be so purified as to become at last as infallible in natural matters as God
himself? 4. *Wolzogen* also appears to maintain that Reason, once purified by
a certain power of the Holy Spirit, is infallible even in heavenly matters—
indeed, just as infallible as God himself. Therefore with respect to these
heavenly matters our reason will be perfect even in this dark valley of
miseries, perfectly sanctified and enlightened, indeed far more perfect than
it was in the state of integrity. What then—will *Wolzogen* be a
*Perfectionist*? If not, let him extricate himself, if he can, along with all
his infallible Reason, purified as it is by the aid of Philosophy in natural
matters and by a certain power

[Page 76 | ONB canvas 130 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000130]

of some Spirit in spiritual matters; and let him show that here he defended
the orthodox cause candidly and without deception or wicked disguise. But in
the same place he says: *Reason which deceives or is deceived must by no means
be called reason. Therefore I understand that certain and immovable reason
which is placed in the evidence of demonstration, not that which is observed
in Aristotle or Descartes or any mortal, all of whom can be deceived when
they do not use reason rightly, but that which has proceeded from God.*
Answer: Then *Wolzogen* possesses or is endowed with no reason, because all
his reason either deceives or can be deceived. Then absolutely no reason now
exists in the nature of things, ever has existed, or ever will exist, because
all reason which has existed until now was fallible and could be deceived. 2.
He plays games when he speaks of such reason, because it is merely a fictional
and imaginary thing; for from that imaginary, infallible reason we can learn
little about how infallible God's veracity is. 3. Since every Philosophy,
Cartesian no less than Aristotelian, can deceive and be deceived, I would have
*Wolzogen* tell us which Philosophy will give us Reason so purified that it
can judge infallibly. 4. He says that he understands Reason placed in the
evidence of demonstration. But that evidence of demonstration is considered
either abstractly, in itself or objectively, or subjectively and with respect
to us. If it is considered in the latter way, it will not be infallible,
because we can be deceived in apprehending it. We can also regard as the
evidence of demonstration what is not actually so, and sometimes we can fail
to perceive the evidence of a demonstration. If he means the former,
*Wolzogen* talks nonsense, for when he says this he says nothing to the point;
and the words that follow indicate well enough the sense in which he takes
Reason. Even the Reason which had just proceeded from God, or had been created
pure in *Adam*, was fallible and was false. 6. They are deceived, he says,
when they do not use reason rightly. But everyone affirms that he uses reason
in the most correct way and

[Page 77 | ONB canvas 131 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000131]

knows no other reason than that which he uses; yet it is established that it
is fallible and deceives. He will say in vain that it does not deceive so
long as people use it rightly, for this is the same as if I were to say that
it does not deceive so long as it does not deceive.

XIII. On page 30, from what has been said he infers a difference *between
faith and knowledge, revelation and reason*: the former depends upon the
authority of the speaker alone, the latter upon the light of truth itself;
the former also believes doctrines, while the latter wills narrated events
to be proved. The former, he says, reigns throughout Scripture; the latter
holds sway in examining human records. So far, so good. But he adds: *This,
however, must not be understood as if faith excluded knowledge, or faith were
trampled underfoot by knowledge. For Scripture has many things which are also
illuminated by the light of reason, and reason itself in turn often borrows
light from faith.* Answer: We certainly do not say that faith excludes
knowledge or that faith is trampled underfoot by knowledge. We also admit
that there are certain things in the Scriptures which are illuminated by the
light of reason. Nevertheless, faith remains firm, and these things are
believed because they are revealed. Our placing faith in them does not depend
upon reason, but upon the authority of God who reveals them to us in the
Scriptures; otherwise he spoke wrongly in saying that faith reigns throughout
Scripture. I would have him reconcile these words with those of his elder
*Velthuysen*, for on page 71 of *On the Use of Reason*, and so forth, the
latter places not only *Scripture* but also *right reason* as a *foundation of
faith*. I would then have him explain to me how reason itself borrows light
from faith, so that in this way he might make it appear that he thinks
orthodoxly and agrees perfectly with himself. For when, as we shall see, he
subjects everything contained in the Scriptures, with no matter excepted, to
the judgment and examination of Reason before it is believed, I do not see
how faith can serve Reason or what Reason borrows from faith. He appears to
explain this in what follows, for he says: *There is a twofold school which
instr-*

[Page 78 | ONB canvas 132 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000132]

*-ucts us about God and many divine matters, the one of nature and the other
of grace; they render mutual services to one another and have considerable
commerce with one another.* It is most true that by the light of nature many
things about God and divine matters become known to us. But *Wolzogen* has not
yet shown us what he undertook to demonstrate, for he does not show how grace
comes to the aid of nature, or Scripture of philosophy—or, what is the same,
how Reason borrows light from faith. Perhaps he will do this in what follows.
He divides those matters of Scripture into three classes. The first consists
of those hidden things which reason can neither discover nor, once they are
discovered, attain. The second consists of those which reason cannot indeed
discover but which, once discovered, right reason understands. The third
consists of those which reason both discovers and understands promptly and
clearly. Yet in explaining these things, especially those of the first and
second orders, he neither shows nor can show what faith borrows from Reason,
because with regard to matters of the first order he himself says on page 32
that faith is aided in nothing by Reason, and with regard to matters of the
second order he says on page 35 that reason only approves them once they have
been discovered and cannot invent anything more agreeable to itself. Thus it
is only in matters of the last class that reason does something. But nowhere
does he show when, or in what matters, reason borrows anything from faith—no
doubt lest he openly offend *Velthuysen* and others dear to him. Here is that
defender of truth and peerless resolver of all controversies!

XIV. Although we do not disapprove this distinction among things revealed in
the Scriptures, there are nevertheless certain statements which *Wolzogen*
makes in explaining that distinction, especially when he speaks of matters of
the first order, which are by no means pleasing. They are as follows.

1. When he speaks of those mysteries of religion which our mind can neither
discover nor, once they have been discovered, grasp, he does not distinguish
between corrupted Reason and uncorrupted reason as it was in *Adam*,

[Page 79 | ONB canvas 133 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000133]

or as it will be in glory. It is certain that *Adam* could grasp more than we
can, and that we can understand far less about those mysteries than the
glorified can.

2. He assumes it as certain that even in the state of integrity *Adam* could
not have known, without special revelation, the Divinity of the persons in
God.

3. If, as is probable, he speaks only of reason as it now exists in us, he
must be faulted for not ascribing this inability to grasp and understand
these mysteries in any degree to the fall, but wholly to the constitution and
nature of our mind. For on page 32 he speaks as follows: *This is the nature
of our mind, that it cannot raise its feeble eyes to such subtlety.* And a few
words later: *For we who know God know that God is of infinite perfection; if
we have any knowledge of his nature, we also know that it is finite. But
because it is demonstrated to us by natural light that the infinite cannot be
grasped in its entirety by the finite, I believe it follows by necessary
conclusion that the God whom we judge infinite cannot be comprehended by us.*
And so our mind is no more incapable of grasping these mysteries than it was
before the fall or will be in glory, for it was and always will be finite, a
finite mind. It can never comprehend the infinite God.

4. He does not distinguish between grasping and comprehending perfectly.
What? Since our Mind or Reason will be finite even in glory and therefore
will not be able to comprehend God perfectly, does it follow correctly that
the glorified do not grasp those mysteries?

5. He does not guard sufficiently against the Antitrinitarians when he speaks
as follows on page 33: *For he who made Gods would not be God, nor would those
whom he made Gods themselves be Gods; for neither would possess all
perfections, since it is an imperfection to receive perfections, and he who
shares all perfections with another

[Page 80 | ONB canvas 134 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000134]

must lose those which cannot be divided.* For on their face these words sound
badly and appear to favor the *Socinians*, who strive to overthrow the Trinity
with these and similar arguments. But on page 61 of the *Censure of the
Censure* *Wolzogen* says that *he makes no mention of the Trinity.* Answer: No
one affirms that he does. But although he makes no mention of the Trinity, he
can nevertheless say things which by consequence will overthrow or assail
the mystery of the Trinity and will be most welcome to the *Socinians*. He
says further that he is not speaking *of that communication by which the
numerically same singular essence is communicated to several persons, but of
that by which the essence is multiplied as many times as the person is
multiplied.* Answer: He speaks of a communication of perfections without
employing any distinction at all, and thereby bears witness that he spoke
with too little caution. But on page 62 of the same work he adds that *the
Censor could not have been ignorant that I was treating the latter, both
because I am treating human beings—and because I speak of making Gods, which
can never be said in divine matters.* Answer: Although you were treating
human beings, you could still have spoken with too little caution. And
although you said *making Gods*, which can never be said of divine matters,
the Antitrinitarians could and still can seize upon your other propositions
while setting this one aside, and from your sayings assail that mystery. Nor
do they shrink from that phraseology, namely, *God made*.

6. Likewise, he appears even in what follows to favor the *Socinians* when he
says: *Yet because finite reason does not attain many things, they must not on
that account be believed contrary to reason. The one destroys reason; the
other shows it to be imperfect. The latter we can, and indeed must, maintain;
the former would charge God himself with imperfection, by whose kindness we
received reason. When reason perceives clearly and distinctly, if anything
could be contrary to it and yet be true, I do not see how the Most Holy Deity
could be absolved of all deception.* For the *Socinians* will say these same
things and argue from them against the mysteries

[Page 81 | ONB canvas 135 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000135]

of Religion which are clearly and distinctly revealed to us in sacred
literature. They affirm that they perceive many things clearly and distinctly
enough, to which those things that we believe and defend are opposed and
contrary; and therefore they reject them as false, as we saw above. But on
pages 67–68 of the *Censure of the Censure* *Wolzogen* says that *none of the
Reformed dares to deny this proposition: no truth can be contrary to reason
when it perceives something clearly and distinctly.* Answer: Reason clearly
and distinctly perceives a golden mountain and imagined things which involve
no contradiction—goat-stags, Centaurs, and many similar things, as well as
other things which do not exist and perhaps never will exist. Yet truth is
contrary to this reasoning. But *Wolzogen* continues: *For it perceives a
thing clearly and distinctly when it perceives it as it is in itself. But if
it is true in itself, it cannot be destroyed by another truth.* Answer: It is
most true that a thing is perceived clearly and distinctly when it is
perceived as it is. That is, it is perceived clearly and distinctly when it
is perceived clearly and distinctly. But then there will be no clear and
distinct perception except one which comprehends and knows everything in the
object, or which is perfectly adequate to or agrees with the object. Yet when
shall we know that our perceptions are clear and distinct—that is, adequate
to those things? Further, the originators of this maxim wish to prove from a
clear and distinct perception that the object is as it is conceived to be,
because it is perceived clearly and distinctly. They do not, contrariwise,
prove that the perception is clear and distinct because it agrees adequately
with the object perceived. And just as other Cartesians take this maxim in
that way, *Wolzogen* must take it in that way here; otherwise he talks utter
nonsense. For he does not dare to deny that many things must be believed
contrary to reason when, that is, reason does not perceive the thing as it
is. But *Wolzogen's* own words indicate clearly and distinctly enough that he
takes this maxim in the sense in which it is commonly taken by *Des-*

[Page 82 | ONB canvas 136 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000136]

*-cartes* and his followers. For the meaning of *Wolzogen's* words here is
that nothing which is true can be contrary to our reason when it perceives
something clearly and distinctly, because truth cannot be contrary to truth.
And unless what our reason clearly and distinctly perceives is true, the Most
Holy Deity cannot be absolved of all deception. Therefore whatever Reason
clearly and distinctly perceives is true; and so the argument proceeds from a
clear and distinct perception to the clarity of the object. But if he
understood nothing else by clear and distinct perception than the perception
of an object as it is in itself, this statement of *Wolzogen's* would be
ridiculous. For it would have this meaning: things must not be believed
contrary to reason when reason perceives the thing as it is, because this
would destroy reason; and when reason perceives a thing as it is in itself,
if anything could be contrary to it and yet be true, God, by whose kindness
we received reason, could not be absolved of all deception. But because
*Wolzogen* would be ridiculous if he spoke in this way and would be saying
nothing in support of the reason about which he is speaking, but only in
support of the *true thing* which reason perceives, to which another truth
cannot be contrary, he takes this maxim in the Cartesian sense, whatever he
may say in the *Censure*. And thus he greatly favors the *Socinians*, for they
would gladly hear that nothing must be believed contrary to reason because
this would destroy reason and charge God himself with imperfection, by whose
kindness we received reason; and that when reason perceives clearly and
distinctly, if anything could be contrary to it and yet be true, the Most
Holy Deity could not be absolved of all deception. For they assert that they
perceive many things clearly and distinctly enough, to which those things
that we affirm to be most true and firmly believe are contrary. They therefore
reject those things, because truth cannot be contrary to truth; and they
suppose that what they clearly and distinctly perceive cannot be false,
otherwise they believe that the Most Holy Deity cannot be

[Page 83 | ONB canvas 137 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000137]

freed from all deception. But does *Wolzogen* not acknowledge that our Reason
is imperfect and can err, since it is corrupted? He says much about this on
pages 66–67 of the *Censure of the Censure*. Its sum is that our reason is not
absolutely perfect, because it is finite, so that it could have happened that
it knew far more, cast a more diffused light, and penetrated the knowledge of
things more directly and without roundabout paths. Yet it has a restricted
perfection enclosed within its own nature, by which it can perceive truth;
and if this perfection is absent, Reason will be destroyed, since it cannot
be understood if truth is foreign to its search. But if it can attain truth,
that truth cannot be contrary to another truth. Yet, 1., here he ascribes no
other imperfection to our Reason than can be ascribed to reason not yet
vitiated by sin, indeed to Angels and blessed souls as well. All of them are
finite creatures, and it could have happened that they knew more. 2. He does
not allow that restricted perfection which reason had from the Creator to be
vitiated by sin or diminished in any fashion. Thus our reason today is as
perfect as it ever was. 3. Our Reason is not destroyed but always remains the
same in its substance, although it is now more corrupted than it was—indeed,
blind in spiritual matters. 4. No one denies that even corrupted reason can
perceive some truth; but it is false that it can perceive every part of truth,
or that everything it perceives is necessarily true. For it can also perceive
what is false and suppose that it apprehends truth when it apprehends
falsehood. 5. Therefore what it apprehends as true and supposes to be true can
be contrary to another truth. 6. From all this it is sufficiently clear that
*Wolzogen* in this business favors the *Socinians* not a little. For if reason
can attain nothing but truth, and nothing can be contrary to that truth, the
*Socinians* have everything they desire.

XV. After this, on pages 37–39 and 38–40, he has certain things

[Page 84 | ONB canvas 138 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000138]

about consequences deduced from sacred literature. Since the occasion arose,
we noted some things about these above, so it will suffice to add a few here.
1. He says: *Those things which indeed have their source in the Scriptures,
because they conduct their streams through the channel, as it were, of
reason, are regarded as cast in some fashion outside Scripture.* Answer:
Although they are regarded as outside Scripture, they are not outside
Scripture. For those things which are evidently deduced from the Scriptures
must also be regarded as Scripture, since they are a part of Scripture—all
our writers speak this way, as I could easily show if it were necessary. But
here he appears to attribute more to reason than an instrumental use, as we
also noted above, which is evident from what follows. For in explaining this
he employs the following comparison: *For just as*, he says, *the elements of
letters are very few but have a varied and infinite combination, from which
arise as many words as suffice for that greatest diversity of languages and
the infinite modes of speech in the same language, which in turn are combined
in such diverse ways by the different people who employ them that so great a
difference of discourse and style arises from them, and so forth.*

But who does not know that when People compose discourses and use a diverse
and peculiar style, they do not extract them from those very few elements,
but rather, with the knowledge of the elements presupposed, properly produce
them and are their causes? Will *Wolzogen* say this about consequences deduced
from Scripture? Must they be ascribed to people in the same way as discourses,
utterances, or diverse modes of speech? Or does he suppose that as much must
be attributed to people in drawing out those consequences as must be
attributed to them in composing a discourse or fashioning some utterance? He
appears to assert this when, on page 69 of the *Censure of the Censure*, he
affirms in the following words that this example, in its many points of
agreement, is very similar to the matter at hand: *The elements of letters
are few if compared with the number of words; primary doctrines are likewise
few in comparison with the others. Words are composed from elements,

[Page 85 | ONB canvas 139 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000139]

consequences from doctrines; usage joins the former, reason the latter. And
just as an entire language rises from the alphabet as its root, yet spreads
through various branches beyond the simple brevity of the alphabet, so a
consequence resting upon the foundation of Scripture is, by its breadth and
force of consequence, regarded as cast beyond Scripture.* According to
*Wolzogen*, therefore, a person does as much in deducing consequences from
Scripture as in composing a Discourse from the elements of letters. And those
consequences are no more contained in Scripture than Discourses and utterances
are contained in the elements of letters. As if God does nothing else in
giving us the sacred Scriptures than anyone who, in the process of forming a
language, devises the elements of letters! I had supposed that speech was
given first, and that from speech, by human diligence and art, certain
elements were derived by whose aid speech could more easily be written and,
once written, understood. Thus this example appears entirely dissimilar. But
*Wolzogen* continues: *Christian doctrine*, he says, *has certain elements of
its own which, although they are often spread out in Scripture itself
according to the analogy of faith, nevertheless leave much to human industry.
By drawing out certain successions from the individual heads of doctrine, or
by comparing the heads of doctrine themselves with one another—few, many, or
all of them at pleasure—and by drawing little streams from that proportion as
from a spring, this industry produces all that fruitfulness of the Christian
Religion which we so greatly admire.* Answer: I would have *Wolzogen* explain
how those particular elements are *often* in Scripture and not always, or
*often* according to the analogy of faith and not always. Then let him tell me
whether all that fruitfulness of Religion must be ascribed to God, since it
has been revealed to us in sacred literature, or only to our Reason. If he
says the latter, he undoubtedly clings to error and rejects the doctrine of
all the Reformed. If he does not, then the comparison he offered will be
entirely dissimilar, because that fruitfulness of discourse which we so
great-*

[Page 86 | ONB canvas 140 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000140]

*-ly admire in every language must not be ascribed to the master of the
alphabet or the inventor of its alphabet. But a little later, on page 39, he
speaks more plainly when he says that *reason teaches these emanations, just
as the former things are inscribed in Scripture alone.* Answer: Then these
consequences or emanations must be ascribed to Reason alone, just as the
sources are ascribed to Scripture. Thus Scripture does not teach us these
outflows; only Reason does. Consequently they rest not upon Scripture but
upon reason, as *Velthuysen* says on page 23 of *On the Use of Reason*: *We say
that these inferences rest upon Reason, because I know them by reason, which
Scripture presupposes.* How, then, will these emanations possess divine
authority if they rest upon reason alone? *Because*, *Wolzogen* says in the
same place, *the sources themselves are always ascribed to God as their
author.* Answer: Then, by equal right, all Discourses and all the varied
styles of discourses must be ascribed to the author of the alphabet, because
the elementary sources of absolutely every discourse must be ascribed to him.
But who does not see how ridiculous this is? Must those outflows then be
ascribed to God in no other way than the discourses of Teachers are ascribed
to the master of the alphabet? Is this what our writers say? Do the Reformed
attribute so much to reason in joining consequences? But he further
illustrates the matter in another way on page 39: *All this resembles what is
observed in many games, but especially in the one called latrunculi. The first
inventor of this harmless war established certain rules and laws of this war,
by which both armies are bound in inviolable fidelity. Yet while those laws
are observed, there are various arts and infinite stratagems by which the
superior player emerges as victor, and so forth.* Answer: This comparison too
confirms what we have already said. For even if those general laws are from
the inventor of the game, the ambushes and stratagems employed must be
ascribed not to that author but to the ingenuity and judgment of the player.
In the same way, according to *Wolzogen*, those outflows must be ascribed not
to Scripture

or its Author, but to Reason. But who does not see how dissimilar even this
comparison is, since those arts, ambushes, tricks, or stratagems are not
contained in those general laws in the way that consequences are contained
in the Scriptures? The author of that war does not teach those arts or
stratagems, but Scripture teaches these consequences which we derive from it.
Hence it is clear that *Wolzogen* attributes far too much to Reason in this
matter. And when he says that reason teaches them, without adding any
qualification, he will be reproved even by *Velthuysen* himself. For on page
22 of *On the Use of Reason* the latter says that *there are certain
inferences which proceed from Scripture, such as those in* Hebrews 12:27 and
Galatians 3:11 and 9, *because we know the consequence only from Scripture.*
But let *Wolzogen* see what *Velthuysen* says besides this. For on page 23 the
latter maintains that even those inferences which we do not make, but which
Christ himself and the other Sacred Writers made and which are revealed to us
in the Scriptures, rest upon reason.
