Thesis II of the Württemberg Doctors

That he not only foreknew those who would be saved, but also chose them from eternity and predestined them to eternal life.

Antithesis II, Theodore Beza

We agree, provided that the word *choosing* is taken for predestination—that is, for the decree of election which would be executed in its own time. And since one who chooses does not take everything, we say it necessarily follows that the rest are passed over by the same decree.

1. We employ the word *choosing* in our thesis with its customary meaning in Holy Scripture. In that meaning it does not signify God’s decree absolutely, but as limited and described in Christ. Ephesians 1: “He chose us in Christ Jesus before the foundations of the world were laid.” Likewise, “He loved us in the beloved,” as our third thesis teaches.

Thesis III of the Württemberg Doctors

That election was made in Christ before the foundations of the world were laid; that is, so that they might be saved through Christ.

Antithesis III, Theodore Beza

We agree.

Theodore Beza’s response to Dr. Andreae’s observation

Nothing could have been answered less suitably to our antithesis. For first—

—we wished it to be observed that the word *election* is considered in two ways. In the first, it is taken for the eternal decree of choosing itself, which, as regards the order of things, precedes every intermediate cause for executing that decree. In this meaning the Son of God, no less than the Father and the Holy Spirit, determined to have mercy upon certain persons who would be born in their own time and chosen from the ruined mass. In the second, regard is had for the execution of the decree itself. In this latter sense Christ as Mediator is subordinated to the decree’s execution, for the disease precedes its remedy in order. For this reason Augustine too, in *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 15, says that Christ himself—insofar as he is man, assumed by the Word coeternal with the Father into the unity of person—“alone was predestined to be our head, and we his members.”

Augustine does not write this rashly, since 1 Peter 1:20 says that he was “foreknown” before the foundations of the world were laid, and Acts 2:22 says that Christ was “delivered up” by God’s definite counsel and foreknowledge. By the same reasoning we are said to have been “chosen in him before the foundations of the world were laid,” and Paul writes that “grace was given to us in Christ”—namely, by the appointment for executing our election in Christ—“before the ages began.”

Failure to observe this distinction is the cause of the greatest errors in this subject. It also most clearly refutes the opposing doctrine: if it is maintained that God thought about man only after foreseeing his fall, it follows either that in the order of things he created man before he determined why he would create him, or that he was deceived in his counsel—to pass over other things which I will discuss in their place, and which cannot even be thought about God without impiety.

I therefore wished Andreae to be reproved also on this point: in that thesis he acknowledges no purpose of God concerning those upon whom God did not determine to have mercy. I refuted that error with this invincible argument: since one who chooses cannot be said to take everything, and one who takes EVERYONE cannot select some, the matter itself cries out that no eternal decree of future election can be granted unless the opposite decree concerning future reprobation is established in precisely the same order. But what does Dr. Andreae say to this? Not even a grunt.
