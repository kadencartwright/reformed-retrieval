# CHAPTER XV

## The Decalogue is not the formula of the covenant of grace

We have now seen what kind of covenant the Lord made with the Israelites and
what kind it was not. We must now see what should be said specifically about
the Decalogue and what relation it bore to that covenant.

Our opponents Cocceius, Burmann, and others openly say that the Decalogue, or
the ten words, is the formula of the covenant of grace and contains commands
to faith and repentance. Thus Dr. Burmann speaks in *Synopsis*, book 4,
chapter 6, sections 6 and 13. And in his *Defense of*

[Printed p. 161 | IA PDF 169 | IA leaf 169 | IA decausadei02brow]

*the Vindication of the Inquiry into the Morality of the Weekly Sabbath*,
pages 56–57, he says that *the Decalogue was the formula or stipulation of the
covenant of grace which God made with the Israelites.* Cocceius likewise says
in his *Investigation of the Sabbath* that *the Decalogue contains nothing
but the covenant of grace*, and elsewhere that it is *the sum of the
evangelical covenant*.

We, however, will first show that the Decalogue is not the formula or
stipulation of the covenant of grace; next, that it does not contain the pure
and simple covenant of grace and nothing else; and finally, we will call our
opponents' arguments to examination.

For everything to be clear, the following points must first be noted.

1. We must distinguish the precepts of the Decalogue from the other things
found in the Decalogue, such as the preface and promises. For here we consider
the Decalogue insofar as it contains the law—that is, the ten precepts or ten
words.

2. We must distinguish the Decalogue considered *simply and abstractly* from
the same Decalogue considered *in its whole setting*, or *relatively*—namely,
insofar as it had a place in that covenantal administration. Considered
*simply and in itself*, the Decalogue contains the covenant of works, or is
the formula of the covenant of works, without any relation to Christ or to
the covenant of grace. Considered *relatively*, insofar as it was proposed in
that administration, the Decalogue had a certain relation to the covenant of
grace. It was promulgated for this end: not that they might be justified by
the law or the works of the law and expect or seek life, but that the
Israelites, despairing of themselves and of obtaining life by works, might
apply themselves to obtaining the righteousness of faith, or Christ's
righteousness, through faith, as we made clear in the preceding chapter.

3. The end for which the Decalogue was proposed to the Israelites is either
*nearer* or *more remote*. The *nearer* end was that they might see their
natural condition under the covenant of works and what they were to expect
from the works of the law. In this respect the Decalogue is proposed as the
formula *of the covenant of works*. The *more remote* end was that, convicted
of their sins and perceiving their inability to fulfill the law or to satisfy
divine justice for their falls and transgressions, they might devote
themselves all the more diligently to embracing the remedy offered in the
covenant of grace—namely, Christ, the sole Mediator of the covenant. In this
respect the Decalogue looks to *the covenant of grace*.

4. The covenant of grace can be considered in two ways: either with respect
to *its internal administration and foundational constitution*, or with
respect to *its external administration*, that is, *its proposal to people*.
With respect to its *internal* administration, it was made with Christ, the
*second Adam*, and in him with all the elect as his seed. Thus it embraces
all the promises, not only conditional but also absolute: the promises of a
new heart, namely of faith, repentance, and so forth. With respect to its
*external* administration, it is made with all who are called, both
unregenerate and regenerate, who by outward profession accept the conditions
and promise that they will fulfill them. In this administration the
conditional promises must especially be regarded: namely, justification and
the remission of sins, and so forth, under the condition of faith and
repentance. Some of these professors and called people are in the covenant of
grace *in a special manner*—namely, all who have truly fulfilled the
stipulated condition of faith. These are all the regenerate, into whom God
has infused and implanted saving faith by the effective operation of the Holy
Spirit. Others are in it only outwardly and in the letter, not in the Spirit:
namely, those who have not yet been regenerated or actually

[Printed p. 162 | IA PDF 170 | IA leaf 170 | IA decausadei02brow]

believed in Christ, but have merely accepted the condition of faith in Christ
and profess it outwardly.

5. We must distinguish between *the condition of the covenant* and *the
condition of the covenanters*. The *former* is that whose performance makes
and establishes the covenant. With respect to the covenant's external
administration, it is a profession of faith—in adults, that is. With respect
to its more special and internal mode, it is true faith, which receives
Christ for righteousness and eternal life. The *latter*, however, embraces
all those duties which are required of the faithful, or God's covenanters.
The Decalogue does contain the sum of this condition of the covenanters,
because it is the rule of conduct and life and prescribes moral duties to all
Christians.

With these points premised, we say that the Decalogue is not the formula or
stipulation of the covenant of grace. This can be proved as follows.

1. That which is the formula or stipulation of the covenant of works is not
the formula or stipulation of the covenant of grace. But the Decalogue is the
formula or stipulation of the covenant of works. Therefore, and so forth. The
connection of the major premise is plain and evident in itself. The minor
premise is established by the fact that the Decalogue contains the law given
to Adam in the state of integrity and according to which God made the
covenant with him. [Cocceius himself says in *On the Covenant*, section 13:
*Nothing else is contained in the tablets of the covenant and the book of the
law than what the law of nature commanded man in his integrity, at least with
respect to the substance of worship.*] Dr. Burmann says in *Synopsis*, book
4, chapter 2, section 3, that [the moral law, or Decalogue, belongs to natural
law]. He thereby acknowledges that the law of the Decalogue is the same as
the moral or natural law which God implanted in man at his first creation,
together with his image. See the same author in chapter 3, section 1, of the
same book. In chapter 5, section 1, he says that *the Decalogue is a compendium
of the moral law and of all holiness and virtue.* Moreover, that the
Decalogue is the formula of the covenant of works is proved from the awakened
consciences of the heathen themselves. They have no law other than this one,
which has not yet been completely erased, and their conscience dictates that
those who violate this law deserve death (Romans 1:32) and that those who
observe it should be rewarded. It is also proved from the natural effort of
other people's consciences to acquit a person as sufficiently righteous if he
has performed certain prescribed works.

2. That which contains the precepts by which the person who does them will
live—that is, the precepts of the covenant of works—is not the formula of the
covenant of grace. This is plain, for the covenant of works and covenant of
grace do not have the same stipulations but very different ones. But the
Decalogue, or ten words, contains the precepts by which the person who does
them lives—that is, the precepts of the covenant of works—as Dr. Burmann
himself testifies in the place cited above, chapter 2, section 4. Therefore,
and so forth.

3. That which had a place in the state of innocence cannot be the formula of
the covenant of grace. But the Decalogue, or the obedience to be rendered to
the law of the Decalogue, had a place in the state of innocence. Therefore,
and so forth. The major premise is certain, because the covenant of grace did
not and could not have a place in the state of innocence: it presupposes
fallen man who must be redeemed by grace. The minor premise is conceded, as
we have seen.

4. That which would have had a place even if Adam had not fallen cannot be
the formula of the covenant of grace. But the law of the Decalogue would have
had a place even if Adam had not fallen. Therefore, and so forth. The major
premise is certain because the covenant of grace presupposes the fall. The
minor is evident.

[Printed p. 163 | IA PDF 171 | IA leaf 171 | IA decausadei02brow]

5. That which would have had a place even if God had never instituted the
covenant of grace cannot be the formula of the covenant of grace, as is
plain. But the law of the Decalogue would have had a place even if God had
never instituted the covenant of grace, for transgression of the law did not
exempt man from obedience to the law. Therefore, and so forth.

6. That which makes no mention of Christ the Mediator is not the formula of
the covenant of grace. But the Decalogue makes no mention of Christ the
Mediator. Therefore, and so forth. The major premise is certain, for how can
the formula of the covenant of grace, which is founded on the Mediator, be
conceived without the Mediator, since faith in the Mediator is the very
condition of the covenant of grace? The minor premise is evident. The moral
law is always the same, and this moral law made no mention of the Mediator
either before or after the fall. Unless God had revealed to Adam that the
woman's seed would crush the serpent's head, Adam would never have learned
this from the Decalogue or from the law inscribed on his heart. Now, after the
gospel has been revealed, we can detect some such thing from the mercy
mentioned in the second commandment and from the preface. But we are speaking
of the precepts of the Decalogue, as was noted above.

7. If the Decalogue, or law of the Decalogue, is the formula of the covenant
of grace, then the formula of the covenant of grace is in force among
barbarians and Indians, because the law of the Decalogue is in force among
them—that is, they are bound to observe it. But they are not under the
covenant of grace, for otherwise the gospel would not be hidden from the
Gentiles or be such a mystery as the Apostle proclaims it to be in Romans
16:25 and Ephesians 3:3, 6. How, then, could the Apostle's statement in
Ephesians 2:12 have been true: namely, that before the revelation of the
gospel the Ephesians were strangers with respect to the covenants and without
hope? For they had the moral law, and consequently they had the formula of
the covenant of grace. Indeed, they were under the covenant of grace with
respect to its external administration, which is nevertheless false. And
what would these words in Romans 10:14–15 mean: *How, then, will they call on
him in whom they have not believed? And how will they believe him of whom
they have not heard? And how will they hear without someone preaching? And
how will they preach unless they have been sent?*, and so forth. Does a new
mode of promulgation so change the nature of the law that the law now becomes
the formula of the covenant of grace when it was not so before? Who will
believe this, since the law remains the same in whatever manner it is
promulgated?

8. Burmann himself says in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 6, section 7, that
precepts of such a kind stand in the Decalogue that, if it had pleased God,
they could have been converted into the covenant of works. But if this is
true, how can the Decalogue contain the formula of the covenant of grace? Can
the formula of the covenant of works be converted into the formula of the
covenant of grace, or vice versa? Can the condition of faith in Christ be
converted into the condition of our personal works, or this condition into
that one? Or can the covenant of grace be converted into the covenant of
works, or vice versa?

9. Indeed, in the same place he asserts that by God's just judgment the
Decalogue passed into a certain form of the covenant of works. This proves
abundantly that the Decalogue was never the formula of the covenant of grace,
for the formula of the covenant of grace says that life and righteousness
must be sought in Christ alone, whereas the formula of the covenant of works
says that life and righteousness must be sought from our own merits and
works. These are so diametrically opposed to each other that one cannot be
changed into the other.

10. Moreover, Dr. Burmann says in the same place, section 9, that the
Decalogue, armed with those two declarations—*The person who does these things
will live by them*, and *Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all
things*—

[Printed p. 164 | IA PDF 172 | IA leaf 172 | IA decausadei02brow]

exhibited a certain form of the covenant of works and was a reminder of the
violated covenant of works and a daily reproach for faithlessness. Thus the
Israelites were to recognize their sin and curse and the impossibility of
legal righteousness; driven away from that hope, they were to thirst for the
righteousness of the Redeemer and be led by the hand to Christ. All of this
shows most fully that the Decalogue considered in itself by no means contains
the formula of the covenant of grace. For leading people by the hand to
Christ is not something incidental and accidental to the formula of the
covenant of grace, since that formula cannot be conceived without Christ.

11. If the law is the formula of the covenant of grace, the Apostle was wrong
to oppose it to faith so often. For the formula of the covenant of grace is
not opposed to faith but most properly requires faith in Christ, as is plain.

12. If the law was the formula of the covenant of grace, the Apostle would
never have said that *if those who are of the law are heirs, faith has been
made vain and the promise rendered void*, as he says expressly in Romans
4:14. For if the law were the formula of the covenant of grace, those who
were of the law would have been heirs, and yet faith would not have been made
vain or the promise rendered void. Law and faith, and law and promise, would
then agree perfectly. The inheritance would be both from the law and from the
promise and faith, because the inheritance is according to the formula of the
covenant of grace and the law is said to be the formula of the covenant of
grace, while it is conceded that faith and the promise belong to the formula
of the covenant of grace.

13. In verse 15 of the same chapter the Apostle says that *the law produces
wrath, for where there is no law, there is no transgression.* This cannot be
said of the formula of the covenant of grace, which does not announce death
to the sinner but opens the way of life to him.

14. That which most properly reveals sin is not the formula of the covenant
of grace. But revealing sin is proper to the law, as Paul testifies in Romans
7:7: *Indeed, I would not have known sin except through the law*, and so
forth.

15. Indeed, in Romans 5:20 the law is said to have entered so that the offense
might increase. [Calvin says on the passage: *He does not set forth the whole
office and use of the law, but touches on only the one part which suited the
present subject. He teaches that, for a place to be made for God's grace,
people had to have their ruin more clearly disclosed to them. They were
indeed shipwrecked before the law, yet because in the midst of their ruin they
seemed to themselves to be swimming, they were plunged into the deep so that
their deliverance might be more illustrious when they emerge from it beyond
human expectation. Here an increase simply in knowledge and obstinacy is
indicated, for sin is set before a person's eyes by the law so that he must
observe it continually whether he wishes to or not, and thus sin takes
possession of the conscience.*] Therefore, the law which produces this effect
cannot be the formula of the covenant of grace.

16. If the law is the formula of the covenant of grace, the Spirit could
have been supplied and miracles performed by works of the law, contrary to
what the Apostle implies in Galatians 3:5. The reason is that by performing
the covenant's stipulation the covenanters become participants in the goods
promised.

17. The Apostle's statement in Galatians 3:10 abundantly proves our thesis,
namely, that the law is not the formula of the covenant of grace. Those who
are under the formula or stipulation of the covenant of grace are not under a
curse. But as many as are of the works of the law are under a curse.
Therefore, the law is not the formula of the covenant of grace. The
stipulation of the covenant of grace does not say that everyone who does not
continue in all the things written in the book of the law, to do them, is
accursed.

[Printed p. 165 | IA PDF 173 | IA leaf 173 | IA decausadei02brow]

18. The formula of the covenant of grace is, *The righteous person will live
by faith.* But this is opposed to the law. For the Apostle says in Galatians
3:11–12: *It is evident that no one is justified by the law before God,
because the righteous person will live by faith. But the law is not of faith;
rather, whoever does these things will live by them.* If the law had been the
formula of the covenant of grace, the law would indeed have been of faith,
and justification by faith and justification by the law would not be so
contrary as the Apostle affirms.

19. The inheritance is not from the law, but from the promise, as the Apostle
testifies in Galatians 3:18. Therefore, the law is not the formula of the
covenant of grace, for in the covenant of grace the inheritance is always
through the stipulation or stipulated condition. The inheritance would
therefore have been through the law, and not through the promise, if the law
were the formula of the covenant of grace.

20. The law is said to have been added because of transgressions (Galatians
3:19). But this cannot be said of the formula of the covenant of grace.

21. If the law was the formula of the covenant of grace, the Apostle would
never have denied that a law could be given which was able to give life, or
that righteousness was from the law, as he denies in Galatians 3:21.

22. If the law was the formula of the covenant of grace, it would not have
been a pedagogue leading to Christ, because we have now been freed from that
pedagogue, but we have not been freed from the stipulation of the covenant of
grace. The law, however, is called a pedagogue leading the Jews to Christ in
Galatians 3:24, while we have been freed from this pedagogue according to
verse 25 of the same chapter.

23. If the law was once the formula of the covenant of grace, it remains so
today, because the stipulation of the covenant of grace is always
substantially the same. But now we are sons of God not through the works of
the law but through faith in Jesus Christ (Galatians 3:26).

24. That which was the ministry of death and condemnation was not the
stipulation of the covenant of grace. But the Decalogue was such a ministry:
that is, the law set down in letters or formed on stones (2 Corinthians
3:6–7). The major premise is evident.

25. It was the covenant of works itself which, according to Dr. Burmann in
the place cited above, book 2, chapter 12, section 11, prepared a way under
the Old Testament for the Christ who was to come. It did so, 1. because it
threatened a curse against all who sought their own righteousness, whereas
righteousness was disclosed only in the promised seed and was to be sought
solely in him; and 2. because meanwhile it daily accused sin as not yet
removed and, by that severity—which was considerably increased by the old
yoke and bondage—made the faithful sigh for Christ. Therefore the law, which
was the matter of this covenant, was not the formula of the covenant of
grace.

26. It is certain that no mortal can ever observe and fulfill the law
perfectly, and consequently that no one can fulfill this stipulation and
condition of the covenant of grace. But by God's grace the faithful fathers
formerly fulfilled the condition of the covenant of grace, and the faithful,
or regenerate, also fulfill it now by believing in Christ. Therefore, the law
of the Decalogue is not the stipulation of the covenant of grace.

27. Nowhere in the Decalogue do we read these or similar words: *If you
believe in the Messiah, you will be saved.* How, then, will it appear that the
Decalogue is the stipulation of the covenant of grace? All the orthodox know
that faith is the condition of the covenant of grace and that the sum of the
covenant of grace, as it is externally proposed and administered, is this:
If you believe in Christ, you will be justified and saved.

28. If the law is the formula of the covenant of grace, how, I ask, are law
and gospel distinguished?

29. The law requires perfect obedience and condemns all imperfect obedience,

[Printed p. 166 | IA PDF 174 | IA leaf 174 | IA decausadei02brow]

but the covenant of grace accepts true and sincere faith, although imperfect
and joined with no small measure of unbelief. Therefore, the law is not the
stipulation or formula of the covenant of grace.

30. If the law was the formula or stipulation of the covenant of works, what
sense would Paul's words in Romans 7:9 have: *For without the law sin was
dead, but I was once alive without the law; when that commandment came, sin
revived, but I died*? Can someone be alive before the condition of the
covenant of grace is known and, once it is known, be regarded as dead? And
what sense would the words that follow in verses 10–11 have: *And that
commandment which was for life was found to result in death for me. For sin,
taking an opportunity through that commandment, deceived me and killed me
through it*?

31. If the law was the formula of the covenant of grace, in what sense could
Paul say in Romans 8:3 that *the law was powerless and deprived of strength
through the flesh*? Was the law powerful to save according to the power
appropriate to a condition or stipulation?

33. If so, that objection would have no plausibility at all: *Do we, then,
render the law useless through faith?* (Romans 3:31). For if the law were the
formula of the covenant of grace, who could even dream that it could in any
way be abolished by the doctrine of faith?

Therefore this opinion, which thoroughly overturns and destroys the gospel,
must be cast out and rejected with abhorrence. But Dr. de Raadt seeks a way
of escape in his *Defense*, pages 200–202, saying that the fourth precept of
the Decalogue does not belong to the covenant of works; that it is not absurd
for the same precept to have a place both in the covenant of works and the
covenant of grace; that no one says the precept *You shall have no other
gods* is a precept of the covenant of grace if these words are taken
absolutely, and neither are the remaining precepts, except the fourth,
precepts of that covenant, since when considered absolutely they belong to
the covenant of works; that besides the law of
the covenant of works the Decalogue contains all the promises of the covenant
of grace, because in the fourth precept sanctification through the Messiah,
prefigured by manna, is promised; and that with respect to Israel there is
also a doctrine of grace in the Decalogue (Exodus 24:12). The precepts of the
law were given to the Israelites only so that they might learn what kind of
obedience was required for God to become their God in the emphatic sense, and
so that they might have a standard according to which they should strive to
be holy as God is holy.

I respond: 1. We proved above in book 3, chapters 8–13, that the fourth precept
belonged to the covenant of works, since it was commanded in the state of
innocence. 2. We grant in this sense that one and the same precept can belong
to both covenants, of works and of grace: both covenanters can be bound to one
and the same duty, and the same command can retain its binding force under
both covenants. If others mean nothing more when they say that the Decalogue
is the formula of the covenant of grace than that it contains the duties of
the covenanters and shows what God still requires of them, no orthodox person
will contradict them. I proved above in chapter 5 of this book that the
Decalogue is the perpetual rule of conduct. But those who commonly speak in
this way mean something very different: namely, that the Decalogue contains
the condition of the covenant of grace itself. At the beginning of this
chapter we indicated the difference between *the condition of the
covenanters* and *the condition of the covenant*. 3. We deny that the
Decalogue contains all the promises of the covenant of grace in addition to
the law of the covenant of works,

[Printed p. 167 | IA PDF 175 | IA leaf 175 | IA decausadei02brow]

and this will never be proved. 4. As for the fourth precept, we deny that
sanctification through the Messiah is promised there. This escape therefore
rests on foundations overturned above in book 3 and to be overturned still
further hereafter in this same book. 5. It is remarkable that he confines the
doctrine of grace which he wishes the Decalogue to contain to the fourth
precept, since he himself explains at least the other precepts of the first
table in such a way that it can be evident to everyone that they express the
doctrine of grace and the gospel itself. See pages 155–156 and 161 of his
*Defense*.
