The other power follows, namely that of jurisdiction. Dounamus divides
jurisdiction into internal and external. He grants the internal or spiritual
power of jurisdiction to parish presbyters, and he says that it is the power
*of governing in a private manner and as it were in the forum of conscience.*
By this power they govern not only in the public ministry, but also by private
care. As to the public ministry, they are, he says, the guides and directors
of the people in public worship: they preach the word by admonishing,
rebuking, and consoling, and administer the sacraments. He says that even a
presbyter who is not a parish minister can, by the power of his Order, not
only baptize, celebrate the liturgy, and preach—which are common to
presbyters and deacons (when they have been

instructed by authority from the bishop)—but also administer the holy Supper
and remit and retain sins. *This power,* he says, *of remitting and retaining
sins is called the key of Order, and according to the doctrine of the papists
it is referred to the conferral of the sacrament of penance.* You see here
that Dounamus establishes only two acts of the presbyteral Order: namely, the
celebration of the Eucharist, and the power of remitting and retaining sins
separately from the preaching of the word and administration of the
sacraments, and this in the inner forum, of which we shall speak presently.
Thus the papists say that there are two powers in a presbyter, one of
consecrating the body and blood of Christ and the other of remitting sins.
Like him, they also assign to deacons the right to administer baptism and to
preach. He refers this power of remitting sins to the power of the presbyteral
Order because, he says, the presbyter receives it in ordination, and it
belongs to him simply insofar as he is a presbyter, apart from jurisdiction
or a relation to a flock or the care of souls. The power of external
jurisdiction consists not only in binding and loosing sins, but also in
calling ministers, discussing and adjudicating doctrinal controversies, and
making laws concerning external polity. We spoke above about the calling of
ministers—that is, election, ordination, and mission. There remain dogmatic
power, δογματικὴ, which is exercised in adjudicating controversies; ordering
power, διατακτικὴ, which is exercised in establishing external polity by
canons and rules in external and indifferent matters, in order to preserve
what is fitting, τὸ πρέπον, and good order, εὐταξίαν; and critical or judicial
power, κριτικὴ, which is exercised in restraining offenses. That dogmatic
power belongs to presbyters is plain from Acts 15: *The apostles and
presbyters therefore assembled to consider this matter,* verse 6, namely,
the observance of the law; and from chapter 16:4, where the decrees of that
council are called δόγματα, ordinances or decisions of the apostles and
presbyters: τὰ δόγματα τὰ κεκριμένα ὑπὸ τῶν Ἀποστόλων καὶ πρεσβυτέρων. If
the decisions are decrees made by the apostles and presbyters, what Bellarmine
says is false: that *the apostles attended that council as judges to make the
determination, but the presbyters to give counsel.* Nor is it surprising that
the presbyters, together with the apostles, had a deciding vote. For although
the apostles stood above the rest of the church in a singular respect, they
nonetheless shared with the other ordinary officers in what was ordinary and
common. *Indeed, they employed ordinary means where churches had already
been established, unless the matter was of a kind that had to be declared by
an extraordinary divine revelation or conducted by divine impulse.
Consultation,* says Junius, *has no place in extraordinary revelation. But
here the apostles are said to have employed consultation.* And although the
apostles were sufficiently equipped to decide by themselves, they
nevertheless refused to issue a decision without the presbyters, thereby
showing that the first place in councils belongs to presbyters as the
successors of those who

were then first in an extraordinary order. If in synods and extraordinary
assemblies they cast votes in adjudicating controversies even when the
apostles were present, much more may they do so in a presbytery. Paul exhorts
the Ephesian presbyters to attend to the flock and watch those who speak
perverse things in order to draw disciples away after themselves, Acts
20:28–29. The same apostle also says in 1 Corinthians 14:32, *The spirits of
the prophets are subject to the prophets.* Paul wishes such presbyters to be
appointed in Titus 1:9 as are able τοὺς ἀντιλέγοντας ἐλέγχειν, to convict
those who contradict. If a presbyter ought to convict opponents by arguments,
what prevents him from casting a deciding vote in adjudicating controversies?
Ordering power, διατακτικὴ, also belongs to presbyters, as is plain from the
said chapter, Acts 15, where the apostles and presbyters decreed that
converted Gentiles *should abstain from things offered to idols, from blood,
and from what had been strangled,* Acts 15:29. And Acts 21:25: *We*—James
and the presbyters who were present, all of whom were present according to
verse 18—*wrote and decreed that they should observe no such thing, except
that they should beware of things offered to idols, blood, things strangled,
and fornication.* The Jerusalem presbytery also advises Paul to take as
companions four men who were under a vow and to sanctify himself with them,
in order to care for the weaker brethren and the peace of the church, Acts
20, 21, 22, and 24. When Paul commands the Corinthians that all things be
done decently and κατὰ τάξιν, according to order, in 1 Corinthians 14:40,
does he not acknowledge the power of establishing good order in their
particular church? The common critical power, κριτικὴ, to which all the
members of the church are subject—namely, the power of binding in the
external forum as well as of loosing—belongs to presbyters. *Censure is
twofold,* says the bishop of Chichester, *that of the publican and that of the
heathen, the lesser and the greater. The lesser merely excludes from the
sacraments. But concerning the other, the greater, by which the church
itself bars a man and makes him just like the heathen, who were not permitted
to enter the temple though publicans were: scarcely anyone fails to admit
that it was instituted by Christ in Matthew 18 through the words “Tell the
church,” and so forth. The external forum is in view there; the jurisdiction
of the external forum is founded in that passage and nowhere else.*
Bellarmine admits that the external forum is treated here: *And although this
passage appears chiefly to treat the power of jurisdiction by which sinners
are excommunicated, nevertheless the fathers already named explain it of
both powers, namely, of Order and of jurisdiction.* He says that the keys of
Order and jurisdiction were not given but promised to the apostles, and that
they were given in John 20:21—the key of Order when he said, *Receive the
Holy Spirit; whose sins you remit,* and the key of jurisdiction when he said,
*As the Father sent me, so I send you.* But in these latter words there is no
mention of the keys; it is in the former, where the acts of the keys are
expressed as remitting and retaining sins—that is, binding and loosing, as he
said in Matthew 18. Therefore, by these words the same keys are delivered as
were promised in Matthew 16 and 18, and consequently the keys of external
jurisdiction, whose noblest act is that greater excommunication discussed

above. To the man who is granted the acts of the keys is also given the power
itself on which such acts depend. But the acts of the keys are granted to
presbyters, namely, to remit and retain, which properly speaking is to open
and close heaven. For in the ordination of a presbyter the bishop says,
“Receive the Holy Spirit: whose sins you remit,” and so forth. The papists say
that by these words only the keys of Order are signified, that is, the acts of
binding and loosing in the private forum of penance. Dounamus, as we saw
above, although he appears to reject the sacrament of penance, nevertheless
appears to embrace the secret and private penitential forum of the penitent
and his confessor. For he grants to the presbyter a power of remitting and
retaining sins apart from the preaching of the word and celebration of the
sacraments, and he grants it only in the private forum. But since the same
keys are promised in Matthew 16 and 18 and delivered by these words, the keys
of external jurisdiction in the public forum are delivered. Therefore, when
the power of remitting and retaining sins is delivered to presbyters, the
power of excommunicating is delivered. It is a futile invention that the
power of Order for remitting and retaining sins in the forum of penance is
given to the presbyter, but jurisdiction is given to him only in the internal
forum. For in that way the power of Order and the power of jurisdiction are
confounded. Indeed, there was no private penitential forum in the purer ages.
*At first, external ecclesiastical penance was always public, not only when
someone was brought to penance after being accused and convicted of a public
and notorious crime, but also when someone voluntarily wished to obtain
ecclesiastical absolution for secret offenses as well. For he publicly
confessed his particular crimes, and thus was bound and excommunicated by a
penitential excommunication and was placed among the public penitents; then,
when the penance had been completed, he was reconciled,* says Spalatensis.
When grave sins are manifest and cause scandal, the sinner is rightly bound
by the keys until he comes to his senses and publicly professes repentance.
But when satisfactions and penal works were imposed upon offenders, the
superstitious then thought that they were not sufficiently secure through
inner repentance and could not worthily receive the Eucharist unless they had
first voluntarily confessed their secret crimes, so that they might first be
bound and afterward loosed. The fathers increased that scruple by exhorting
people to external penance for more serious crimes even when secret. *And
because the same promise of Christ is similarly certain and infallible—so
that, when the church grants release from the ecclesiastical bond, provided
the key does not err, the bond by which the sinner bound himself before God,
and to which the church had added its bond of excommunication, is immediately,
certainly, and infallibly loosed in heaven also through the certain and
infallible remission of that sin—the fathers therefore always earnestly
exhorted faithful sinners defiled by crimes after the laver of baptism that,
for the sake of greater security, they should not be content to apply to
themselves the remedy of inner repentance alone, although it is sufficient in
itself, but should voluntarily, through their own confession of a grave sin,
even if it was secret,

before at least a minister of the church, take care to have themselves bound
by that ecclesiastical penitential excommunication, so that, when release
from this bond had been obtained by reconciliation, they might be wholly
certain that the sin had also been remitted to them in heaven,* says
Spalatensis. External penance, therefore, was first public for manifest
crimes, and then public also for secret ones, where the disclosure of the sin
was public and the sinner wished to enjoy the benefit of the keys. *Afterward,
however, this penance was indeed undertaken and performed publicly, but a
grave sin which was not in itself public and notorious was disclosed secretly
in the church to the bishop or penitentiary priest. In proportion to the
offense, the penitent underwent a measure of penance which he nevertheless
performed publicly, abstaining from sacred things, remaining with the public
penitents in a special place designated for this purpose in the church, and
practicing acts of humility, until at last he obtained reconciliation and,
consequently, absolution on earth and in heaven.* He indicates from Sozomen
the reason for the change. *At first it seemed odious, as may be believed, to
the priests to announce offenses as if in a theater while the multitude of
the church listened. They therefore appointed to this office a presbyter
especially notable for integrity of life, able to keep secrets, and wise.
Those who had sinned approached him and confessed the acts of their life. He,
after declaring what each person ought to do or suffer for his particular
offense, absolved them, so that they might exact from themselves the penalties
for the things they had committed.* Spalatensis says that when the office of
penitentiary priest was abolished in the time of Theodosius the Elder, public
penance was abolished. *Afterward it came about that public penance was
entirely reduced to secret penance, not only as to the disclosure of the sin,
but also as to the performance of the penance itself. Thus the sinner,
accusing himself to the minister of the church appointed for this purpose,
was secretly bound by him for secret sins, was punished by penitential
excommunication—namely, exclusion from participation in the Eucharist
alone—and was obliged secretly to perform the external ecclesiastical penance
imposed upon him. When this had duly been completed, he was reconciled and
admitted to communion in the holy Supper. This, therefore,* says
Spalatensis, *is the whole introduction of secret penance for hidden sins. It
is precisely that public penance and no other, merely reduced to secrecy in
the manner explained: namely, those burdened by secret crimes which were not
public and notorious, and who wished to obtain remission by the benefit of
the keys, secretly disclosed the secret sin to a minister of the church by
their own confession, and thus were subjected and bound to ecclesiastical
penance, though one to be performed secretly; at last they were reconciled
and loosed by the minister through the power of the keys.* And elsewhere:
*At first, as we said, they confessed publicly in everyone's hearing. Later
the confession was secret, but the penance was still public; finally both
confession and penance became secret. And certainly ecclesiastical penance
itself is always wholly one and the same in itself, in its power and efficacy,
and in the performance of the whole penance from beginning to end. It has
varied only as to being public or secret.* But even the most

secret penance is in itself wholly the same as that which was most *public,
which our modern writers call solemn.* Since this private forum of secret
penance was introduced later, I conclude from what has been said that it is
foolish and childish to imagine that the keys of Order, rather than those of
jurisdiction in the external forum, were delivered when Christ said in John
20, “Whose sins you remit,” and so forth. For remitting and retaining sins is
the same as binding and loosing, and the keys delivered here are the same as
those previously promised by Christ. But he promised the power of binding
even the recalcitrant man who refuses to hear the church. This is done in the
external forum. In that inner forum of penance a priest will bind only a
willing man who consents of his own accord. It is plain, therefore, from what
has been said that presbyters can bind and loose in the external forum,
because they can remit and retain sins, as our opponents themselves admit;
and they can do so in the public forum, as our opponents call it, because
Christ treats the public forum in Matthew 18, where the power of binding and
loosing is discussed, while that secret kind of penance was introduced into
the church later. Spalatensis says that the proper and adequate effect is
only to bind and loose sins; so too does the bishop of Chichester in *Tortura
Torti*. But Field also places binding by laws among the acts of the keys.
The adversaries themselves do not deny that presbyters can establish canons
in councils. Let us therefore proceed to the practice of the other act. In
the Corinthian church, presbyters had the leading role in excommunication.
They were able to excommunicate the incestuous man without consulting the
apostle, as we proved above. Much more can they do so without consulting the
bishop. No bishop had yet been appointed at Corinth, as was shown in chapter

feasts and of an ordinary matter. Bilson says that *the apostles designated

everything necessary for the pastoral office those whom they had found

work and worthy of the Holy Spirit both to indicate to Paul the men upon whom
he should lay hands and to enrich those men with the aids of his marvelous
grace, so that he might render them prophets and pastors useful and suitable
for the benefit of the church.* Is it really plausible that the apostle,
endowed with such abundance of the Spirit, equipped no one with the things

church abounded in many presbyters, and names the Corinthian church among

been given one bishop who stood above those presbyters, and I too agree with
him.* But leaving this little digression, I proceed. What may be said about
the presbyters of the Corinthian church may also be said about the presbyters

the first epistle with chapter 3, verse 14 of the second. Judgment proper to

because savage wolves were about to arise and men would speak perverse
things. To what end were they to watch, unless to keep the wolves away? The
hierarchists themselves interpret this text as referring to government, but
in this they attempt deception: they understand by the name “presbyters”
their own bishops, because Paul calls the same men ἐπισκόπους, bishops. But
according to their own principles there were not many bishops of one church;
and Bilson numbers the Ephesian church among the churches which abounded in
many presbyters laboring in the word. *But the men whom Paul addressed in
that discourse were presbyters of the Ephesian church whom he had summoned

bishop.* Peter speaks of the external government of presbyters in general

says that a pastoral act is not merely to provide food, but also to lead,
bring back, protect, preside, govern, and chastise. Hence kings are
metaphorically called pastors, and to govern is called shepherding, as in
Matthew 2:6 and Revelation 2:27; 12:5; and 19:15. Saravia acknowledges that
in both profane and sacred writers shepherding signifies governing. Dounamus
concedes that shepherding signifies governing when it is attributed to civil
pastors, that is, kings and magistrates, but when attributed to spiritual
pastors, it signifies only providing food. Thus by his own authority he
diminishes the proper force of the metaphor. For in a metaphor the whole
force of the proper meaning is transferred. And although civil and spiritual
pastors do not govern in the same manner, each nonetheless governs in his own
manner, as the analogy of the metaphor suits the one or the other. Pastors of
the church do not govern in an imperial or royal manner, nor with an iron
rod, but with the staff and crook of a kindly shepherd. *There is no doubt
that by shepherding the office of a pastor is meant. And because this is said
metaphorically of ministers, they ought to perform these pastoral acts: to
provide food, to lead, bring back, go before by word and life, protect from
wolves and other wild beasts, preside in spiritual matters, govern not
τυραννικῶς, as masters, but as guardians—not by command but by the true law
of the flock—and chastise by reproofs, admonitions, excommunications, and the rest
of discipline,* says Whitaker. Chrysostom more than once calls the pastoral
office προστασίαν, presidency. A pastor's duty is not only to play the pipe,
but also to govern by the crook of ecclesiastical discipline and drive away
wolves. Bishops claim the crook for themselves alone and leave presbyters
only the pipe—and not even that except as they themselves please. Presbyters
