OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. If Scripture is sufficient without another tradition, then either
the whole canon taken together is sufficient, or the individual books are
sufficient by themselves. But neither can be said: not the latter, because
the individual books do not contain everything, and if one book contained
everything there would be no need for the others; not the latter, contrary to
what our adversaries urge.[^1]

Response 1. There is an equivocation in *sufficiency*, for it is twofold: the
sufficiency of the parts or of the whole. The whole Scripture is sufficient
with a total and essential sufficiency diffused through each and every book,
but the individual books have the sufficiency of parts, toward which they are
ordered within the whole. Thus a man has his essential sufficiency throughout
the whole and through the individual parts, permeating them in a manner
appropriate to them; and the individual parts of a man also have within the
whole their own particular sufficiency, each being fit for the uses to which
it is ordered. The individual books are not sufficient with the essential
sufficiency of the whole, but they are sufficient with their own particular
sufficiency, toward which they have been ordered by God.

2. There is an error in the proof of the first member. Even if one book
contained everything, there would nevertheless be need of the others for the
well-being of faith. Thus what is in the New Testament is contained in the
Old, yet no one would therefore say that there is no need of the New
Testament.

3. It is false that the whole canon does not contain everything. Indeed, we
say that the canon which existed in the time of Moses and afterward was then
sufficient for those people, while the canon now is most fully sufficient
without traditions.

Instance 1. This is how he proves that the whole canon is not sufficient. Many
books that were truly sacred and canonical have perished. Therefore, we
neither possess nor for many years have possessed suffi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 137 | BSB frame 00083]

—cient doctrine, if it is all in the Scriptures. This is proved from
Chrysostom, who says in Homily 9 on Matthew that the Jews lost some things by
negligence and burned others, so that they were not restored by Ezra and do
not exist even now. Such are the words in Matthew 2, “He shall be called a
Nazarene,” and the words, “Out of Egypt I called my son,” which are not found
in the Old Testament.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied, because no sacred books have perished.
2. The proof of the antecedent proves nothing, because Chrysostom, being
ignorant of the Hebrew language, followed the translators’ version, in which
these statements are not found. But according to Jerome in his book *On the
Best Kind of Interpretation*, both statements are found in the Hebrew text,
the former in Judges 13:5 and Isaiah 11:1, and the latter in Hosea 11:1.
Indeed, both exist in the Vulgate version. Finally, it was shown above that the
Jews preserved the Scriptures so religiously that no book perished through
negligence or malice, and Bellarmine himself proves this in book 1 of *On the
Word of God*.

Instance 2. Ezra did not restore those books which perished, which are
mentioned in the last chapter of 1 Chronicles, 2 Chronicles 9:29, and 1 Kings
4:32. And from the New Testament it is certain that Paul’s Epistle to the
Laodiceans, mentioned in Colossians 4:16, has perished, as has another to the
Corinthians, mentioned in 1 Corinthians 5:9; and it can easily be the case
that some other writing has perished.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied—namely, that these passages mention books
which have perished. For, first, the books mentioned in 1 and 2 Chronicles
were those of Nathan and Gad, namely, the history of 1 and 2 Samuel. For only
the first book of Samuel was written by Samuel, and that only in part: he died
before the history contained near the end of the first book. Hence these books
are called books 1 and 2 of Kings in the Vulgate edition. The book of Ahijah
the Shilonite and the book of Iddo are 1 Kings according to us, or 3 Kings
according to the papists. 2. The proverbs and songs of Solomon mentioned in
1 Kings 4 either exist in his cano—

[1620 Tome I | col. 138 | BSB frame 00083]

—nical books, if he is speaking of those which pertain to the canon, or they
were not canonical writings about faith and religion, but concerned political,
physical, and medical matters. The question, however, concerns canonical
writings. For not everything a notary writes is authentic, but only what he
writes in his capacity as a notary. Thus David’s letter to Joab was written by
a prophet, but not in his capacity as a prophet. 3. Colossians makes no mention
of an epistle written to the Laodiceans, for it does not say πρὸς Λαοδικεῖς,
but ἐκ Λαοδικείας, as Chrysostom and Luther note on this passage, as does the
papist Catharinus in his commentaries. Thus it signifies either that the
Laodiceans wrote some epistle to Paul, as other churches wrote to Paul—for
example, 1 Corinthians 7:1—or another epistle written by Paul from Laodicea,
namely, 1 Timothy, which was written from Laodicea, as is evident from its
subscription at the end. 4. First Corinthians 5 does not speak of another,
different epistle, but of 1 Corinthians itself, for it says ἔγραψα ὑμῖν ἐν τῇ
ἐπιστολῇ, namely, ταύτῃ, alluding to chapter 5, verse 2, as Chrysostom, Ponti.,
and others interpret it. Finally, in saying that other books could have
perished, Bellarmine wrongs God, the holy Fathers, and the Church.

Instance 3. Numbers 21:14 mentions the Book of the Wars of the Lord, and Joshua
10:13 and 2 Samuel 1:18 mention the Book of the Upright. But these books have
perished. Therefore.

Response 1. This does not conclude what is in question, because the question
is not whether any books have perished, but whether any canonical books have.
The proposition should therefore have added that these books were canonical,
which we deny. For according to Augustine, chapter 38 of his book *The City of
God*, there were two kinds of books: some were canonical, dedicated by divine
inspiration to authority in religion; others were composed by human diligence
for a fuller knowledge of military and political affairs, as these books were.
2. If the Book of the Wars of the Lord, mentioned in Joshua 10, was canonical,
it nevertheless does not

[1620 Tome I | col. 139 | BSB frame 00084]

follow that it perished, because *the history of Moses*, taking its name from
its subject, signifies the history, and because by a Hebrew idiom any
historical account is called a book; see Genesis 5:1, Nehemiah 7:5, Matthew
1:1, and elsewhere. 3. The Book of the Upright, or of the righteous, was a
book of public annals. Although it perished along with others mentioned in the
books of Ezra and Esther, it does not follow that the canon is imperfect.
Finally, the Chaldean paraphrase renders the passage in Joshua 10, “in the book
of the law,” namely, the Mosaic law. Others understand this very book and this
chapter of Joshua.

Instance 4. Jude 14 cites the prophecy of Enoch and the history of the dispute
over the body of Moses, but these writings also no longer exist. Therefore.

Response 1. It is not said that these things had been written in canonical
Scripture, and so they conclude nothing against us. 2. The cited passages do
not prove the use of unwritten traditions, because they do not cite things
which had once been written for the edification of the Church. Finally, some
concede that canonical books have perished, yet that the canon remained
sufficient and perfect because it contains everything necessary.

Objection 2. From the difference between the apostles’ preaching and writing.
If Christ and the apostles had intended to restrict the Word of God to
Scripture, Christ would have explicitly commanded a matter of such importance,
and the apostles would somewhere testify that they wrote by command, just as
they taught by command. But we nowhere read this, and the apostles preached
spontaneously but wrote only when compelled. Likewise, if they had written by
design, they would have written a catechism or some other such book, or all
together would have published one book before they separated, just as they
composed the creed, as some Fathers testify. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because Scri—

[1620 Tome I | col. 140 | BSB frame 00084]

—pture is θεόπνευστος, “God-breathed,” according to 2 Timothy 3:16, and those
moved by the Spirit are said to have written in 2 Peter 1:21. God is also said
to have written what the prophets wrote in Hosea 8:12, and Moses is said to
have written all God’s words in Exodus 24:4. Likewise, μαθητεύσατε in Matthew
28:19 signifies teaching both in writing and by the living voice. For the
apostles had to teach for a time by the living voice, but they also had to
leave behind the true doctrine which would endure to the end of the world, as
Chrysostom teaches in Homily 75 on Matthew. Likewise, in Revelation the command
“Write” occurs twelve times. Thus Augustine, book 1, last chapter of *The
Harmony of the Evangelists*, says that God commanded them to write. Athanasius,
in his letter to Liberius, says that Christ composed the Old and New
Testaments. And if no one dares write letters in the name of a prince unless
the prince commands it, much less would the apostles do so unless Christ
commanded it.

2. The proof errs in denying that the apostles wrote a catechism, for the
epistles are nothing other than so many catecheses. And in Hebrews 6 the
apostle expressly enumerates the heads of catechesis. Nor does this follow:
they did not write a catechesis or another book jointly; therefore, they wrote
without a command. Likewise, the Apostles’ Creed is a catechesis containing
the sum of faith. But, as they wish, it was composed by the apostles, and
according to Augustine in book 1 of *On the Creed*, it is found scattered
through the apostles’ books.

3. It is false that the individual apostles had their own provinces, because
each of them had all provinces according to the command, “Teach all nations.”
Finally, the fact that they did not write a common book contributed more to
the spread of the gospel: namely, so that they might not seem to foist
fabricated tales upon the world by concerted design, and so that the divinity
of the gospel might become better known from the fact that so many dispersed
men preached exactly the same thing.

Objection 6.[^4] There are many things which cannot be unknown, yet in the
Scriptures

[1620 Tome I | col. 141 | BSB frame 00085]

they are not contained. Therefore Scripture is not sufficient without
traditions.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because everything which it is fitting to
know concerning faith and morals is contained in the Scriptures.

Instance, by which he proves the antecedent. In the time of the Old Testament,
women no less than men had a remedy by which they might be cleansed from
original sin, and yet circumcision, according to many, was instituted for
males. But Scripture nowhere states what there was for females.

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because Christ’s blood cleanses all
people under both the Old and the New Testaments: Isaiah 53:5; 1; Revelation
13:8; Romans 5:19; Ephesians 2:16; Hebrews 9:26; 10:14; and 13:8; 1 John 2:2.

Response. The proposition must be distinguished. That circumcision was
instituted for males alone is false. That it was instituted for them and for
women inclusively, but not exclusively, is true. Women, however, were
consecrated in the circumcision of the men, because married women belonged to
their husbands and unmarried women to their parents.

Likewise, circumcision was not only sacramentally—a remedy against original
sin, though not *ex opere operato*, as the papists maintain—but also against
actual sins, as baptism is; and it was also a sign of the transmission of
sins. But sin is transmitted especially through men, and therefore this sign
was rightly impressed upon them.

Finally, Scripture teaches that under the Old Testament various remedies were
instituted for women so that they might be sanctified: baptism in the sea and
the cloud, manna, the water flowing from the rock, the Passover, sacrifices,
and offerings. Indeed, it teaches that circumcision itself extended to women
by virtue of the covenant, although they were incapable of receiving the
external sign; see Genesis 34:34, Acts 2:39, and Luke 13:16.

Instance 2. It is not likely that under the Old Testament there was no remedy
for males dying before the eigh—

[1620 Tome I | col. 142 | BSB frame 00085]

—th day, the only day on which they could be circumcised. But nothing about
this exists in sacred Scripture. Therefore.

Response 1. This, like the preceding instance, rests on a false foundation:
namely, that salvation depends by necessity upon the sacrament and its
performed work.

2. The assumption is denied, because those who died before the eighth day, if
elect, were sanctified by the blood and Spirit of Christ. Isaiah 53:5 and 10
speaks there about the souls of adults as well as infants. The covenant also
extended to them both before and after circumcision was instituted or
received.

Finally, this does not follow: they lacked the external sacrament; therefore,
they lacked the remedy. For God could bestow grace apart from this one
instrument.

Instance 3. Under the Old Testament many Gentiles were saved and belonged to
the Church, but nothing about their justification from sins is contained in
Scripture. Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because they were justified through
faith; otherwise they were not saved. Hebrews 11:6 and 31:2. Concerning the
external means by which Gentiles were saved, a law is contained in Numbers
9:14; 15 and 29; and 35:15.

Instance 4. It is necessary to know that some divine books exist. But this can
in no way be known from Scripture. Therefore. The assumption is proved: even
if Scripture says that the books of the prophets and apostles are divine, I
will nevertheless not believe this with certainty unless I first believe that
the Scripture which says this is divine. For in the Quran we read that it is
from heaven and from God, and yet we do not believe it.

Therefore, that some writing is Scripture cannot be known sufficiently from
Scripture alone. Consequently, since faith rests upon the Word of God, unless
we have an unwritten Word of God, we will have no faith.

[1620 Tome I | col. 143 | BSB frame 00086]

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because explicit testimonies exist which
testify that all the books of Scripture are divine: 2 Timothy 3:16 and 2 Peter
1:21.

Likewise, the apostles and Christ himself testify that the prophetic books
are divine.

Peter also testifies the same thing about Paul’s epistles in 2 Peter 3:15.

Finally, the very doctrine of the individual books confirms itself.

2. The proof of the assumption is impious in many ways. 1. Bellarmine says
that he would not certainly believe the books of the prophets and apostles to
be divine even though Scripture says so; in this he ascribes greater authority
to the pope than to Peter or Scripture. 2. He equates Scripture’s assertion
with the Quran’s declaration.

3. He wishes our faith to rest upon human testimony rather than upon the Holy
Spirit speaking in Scripture, although faith arises only from the Scriptures
rightly understood and rests upon them. Thus Augustine rightly says in book
19, chapter 18 of *The City of God*: “The City of God believes the Scriptures
which we call canonical, from which faith itself is conceived, by which the
just shall live.”

Instance 5. It is not enough to know that Scripture is divine; it is necessary
to know which Scripture it is. But this cannot be known from the Scriptures.
Therefore.

The assumption is proved: how will we gather from Scripture that the Gospels
of Luke and Mark are true, while those of Thomas and Bartholomew are false,
when reason dictates that a book bearing an apostle’s title should be believed
rather than one not bearing an apostle’s title? And how shall I gather that
the Epistle to the Romans is truly Paul’s, but the Epistle to the Laodiceans is
not Paul’s, when Colossians 4 says that he wrote to the Laodiceans, but nowhere
says that he wrote to the Romans? Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is distinguished. It is indeed necessary to know
this, but not

[1620 Tome I | col. 144 | BSB frame 00086]

simply for salvation and in the same manner for everyone. For many in former
times doubted various canonical books, at least for a time, and these people
will not therefore be excluded from salvation.

Furthermore, those who possess the Holy Spirit, through whom we recognize the
canonical books, do not possess him in the same measure. The same must be held
concerning the Spirit’s other operations.

2. The assumption is denied, because prophetic Scripture is confirmed by the
testimonies of Christ and the apostles, while the apostle teaches in Ephesians
2:20 that both are divine, since the Church’s foundation can only be divine.

3. The Gospels of Luke and Mark are known to be divine from the books
themselves. In the same way, those of Thomas and Bartholomew are known not to
be divine, because they contain many things which conflict with sacred
doctrine, according to Augustine in book 1, chapter 14 of *The Harmony of the
Gospels*. It is indeed true that the Gospels of Mark and Luke were received on
the authority of the Church, but only as a secondary authority; and the
Gospels of Thomas and Bartholomew were rejected by the Church, but for no
other reason than that they contained many things conflicting with the analogy
of faith.

5. From the canonical title of the Epistle to the Romans, chapter 1, verse 1,
it is evident that it is Paul’s; and Bellarmine says against Scripture that
Paul nowhere said that he wrote to the Romans—Romans 15:15. 6. There is no
Epistle to the Laodiceans written by Paul. The one which now exists under
Paul’s name, according to the Second Council of Nicaea, was always rejected by
everyone. Finally, the claim that the Epistle to the Colossians establishes
that Paul wrote to the Laodiceans was refuted in the response to Objection 1.

Instance 6. It is necessary not only to know which books are sacred, but also
to know in particular that those in our hands are those very books—for
example, that the Gospel of Mark now read is the true and uncorrupted Gospel
which Mark wrote. But this cannot be known from Scripture,

[1620 Tome I | col. 145 | BSB frame 00087]

because it cannot be known from Scripture whether this Gospel is spurious or
corrupted. Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because the Church has always held this
particular Gospel of Mark to be canonical in no other way than from the Gospel
itself: namely, from its canonical title and from the things discussed above,
which all the faithful recognize by faith in Mark’s Gospel. Therefore, it
rests upon that Word of God.

2. If the argument were sound, all human and divine writings would have to be
doubted.

3. One must not dispute with a person who denies first principles. Therefore,
those who deny that Mark’s Gospel is Mark’s, although Scripture says it is,
must be rejected with the Manichees, Marcionites, Ebionites, and Cerdonians,
who urged this very thing along with the papists.

4. The proposition is not altogether necessary, because it is not necessary
for salvation that I know in particular that this or that book was written by
this or that definite author, provided that I receive the writing itself as
divine. For there are many books of the Old and New Testaments whose author is
not certainly known.

Instance 7. It is necessary not only to read Scripture but also to understand
it. But Scripture is very often ambiguous and perplexing, so that it cannot be
understood unless it is explained by someone who cannot err. Indeed,
Bellarmine says, there are two things in Scripture, the words and the sense. Of
these, the first is possessed by everyone who knows letters. 2. The second is
not, because in very many passages we cannot be certain of the sense of
Scripture unless tradition is added. Such are the equality of the persons, the
procession of the Holy Spirit, original sin, and Christ’s descent into hell,
which cannot easily be settled from Scripture. Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied if someone besides the Holy Spirit is
meant,

[1620 Tome I | col. 146 | BSB frame 00087]

for the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture is his own interpreter and cannot
err.

Hence the Bereans are commended in Acts 17:11 because they searched in
Scripture for the sense of the Scriptures cited by Paul, who said nothing
beyond Moses and the prophets, Acts 26:22.

2. In the assumption Bellarmine falsely asserts that Scripture is very often
perplexing and ambiguous. For in itself it is entirely foreign to all
ambiguity; if there is any ambiguity, it belongs to those who either do not
search or do not attain the true sense of Scripture.

3. In the assumption there is a fallacy of qualification, because when he
asserts that Scripture is very often ambiguous, he admits that it is often not
ambiguous, or that it is ambiguous with respect to us. But things which are
ambiguous with respect to us can be explained through those which are not
ambiguous, as was proved above where the clarity of Scripture was treated.
There is also a fallacy from the equivocation in an interpreter who cannot
err. For they mean either the Holy Spirit, or the prophets and apostles who,
moved by the Spirit, spoke immediately and extraordinarily; we concede that
these cannot err. But their interpretation is not found outside Scripture.
Alternatively, they mean ordinary pastors and teachers; but these can err to
the extent and in the manner that they depart from the analogy of faith.

5. When he concedes that these doctrines cannot easily be deduced from
Scripture, he concedes that they can be deduced, and thus disagrees with other
papists, who think they can be proved from unwritten tradition alone, as
Stapleton does in chapter 9 of book 12.

6. The Fathers of old proved these doctrines from Scripture itself. Thus
Tertullian in his book *Against Praxeas* and in Sermon 60 of his letters,
Ambrose in chapters 4 and 5 of his book *On Faith against the Arians*, and
Augustine in book 3, chapter 14 of *Against Maximinus the Arian* proved the
equality of the persons and their consubstantiality from Scripture. The Church
has always truly held from John 15:26 the procession of the Holy Spirit from
the Father and the Son according to Scripture, and Thomas proves this at
greater length in the tenth ques—

[1620 Tome I | col. 147 | BSB frame 00088]

—tion of his disputed questions. Before Thomas, Augustine did so in Tractate
99 on John. Original sin is manifestly proved from Romans 5:12 even though the
term itself does not occur in Scripture, and Augustine proves it from many
Scriptures in his book against the Pelagians. As for the descent into hell in
the manner in which the papists hold it, we concede with Andradius in book 2
of *The Defense of the Council of Trent*, against Bellarmine and others who
attempt to prove it from Psalm 16:10, Acts 2, and 1 Peter 3:19, that it
cannot be proved at all.

Finally, Bellarmine ineptly says that if matters are to be conducted by the
Scriptures alone, disputes with the insolent can never be ended. For even after
the declarations of Christ and the apostles, disputes over religion came to
an end. Therefore, according to the apostle, one should contend with
contentious people by silence instead; see 1 Corinthians 11:16.

Instance 8. Against Helvidius, it must be believed that the blessed Mary was
always a virgin, as the whole Church has always believed. But there is no
testimony about this in Scripture. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, for she is called a virgin before and
after giving birth: Isaiah 7:14, Matthew 1:23, and Luke 1:34.

Furthermore, we nowhere read in Scripture that Mary was known by Joseph.
Indeed, Christ commended his mother not to Joseph but to John, John 19:27.

Therefore, Mary then had neither children nor husband.

Finally, Jerome proves this by many testimonies of Scripture against
Helvidius, and Basil gathers the same from Matthew 1:25 in his sermon on the
Lord’s nativity, although he maintains that one should not dispute curiously
about the matter.

Instance 9. It must be believed that in the New Testament Easter is to be
celebrated on the Lord’s Day, because the Quartodecimans were regarded as
heretics by the Church. But nothing about this is contained in Scripture.
Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is denied. It is indeed true

[1620 Tome I | col. 148 | BSB frame 00088]

that there was a most serious and useless dispute about this between the
Romans and Greeks. For Pope Victor threatened to excommunicate those who
celebrated Easter on a day other than the Lord’s Day. But Irenaeus and many
other bishops reproved the pope on this account, according to Eusebius in book
5, chapter 25 of his *History* and Sozomen in book 7, chapter 19 of his
*History*. This controversy was settled by Victor and Polycarp, the disciple
of John: namely, each should preserve his own custom. It could also be that
John tolerated the Jewish practice in this respect for a time, as Paul did
circumcision and other Jewish ceremonies. But it does not follow from this
that Easter should be celebrated with the Jews or celebrated perpetually,
because the observance of Jewish days and other ceremonies was abolished in
Christ: Romans 14:4 and 6; Galatians 4:9 and 11; Colossians 2:16. The Passover
was also a type and ceremony. Therefore, it ceased. Indeed, Socrates in book
5, chapter 22 teaches that the apostles established nothing about Jewish
feasts. 2. The Quartodecimans were rejected as heretics chiefly because they
did not observe the day of Christ’s resurrection, but precisely the
fourteenth day of the first month.

Finally, the assumption is not simply true, because, once it is granted that
Easter should be observed, some gather from these passages that it should be
observed on the Lord’s Day: John 21:1 and 26; 1 Corinthians 26:2.[^2]

Instance 10. Infant baptism must be believed. But this cannot be proved from
the words of Scripture. Therefore.

Response. This does not conclude what is in question, for the question is not
whether it can be proved from Scripture alone. Indeed, the argument should be
reversed, for he concedes that it can be proved from Scripture, and that it is
in fact proved from Scripture will be said in its proper place.

Instance 12. All heretics believe that there is no Word of God except the
written Word. But this is nowhere found in Scripture—not even in Deuteronomy
4, the passage they cite, because Moses there mentions not the written Word,
but the Word which he spoke. Therefore.[^3]

[1620 Tome I | col. 149 | BSB frame 00089]

Response 1. The proposition is denied if it is taken simply. For the orthodox
believe that there is the eternal Word of God, John 1:1; the word of God’s
power, Deuteronomy 8:4, Matthew 4:4, and Hebrews 1:3; and the Word implanted
through the Holy Spirit, James 1:12. This is in reality the same as the written
Word, differing only in its mode of relation. But if it is understood κατὰ τι,
in a certain respect—namely, that there is no Word except the written Word from
which doctrines of faith and morals are to be sought—we concede it. For it is
said of this Word that it makes men wise for salvation and perfect for every
good work, and Christ directs us to it; nothing of the sort is said of an
unwritten Word. Indeed, we say that Christ and the apostles spoke many things
which are not contained καθ’ ἕν, individually, in the Scriptures, and that
there was no need for this, but that it is sufficient for salvation that all
the individual heads of doctrine are contained there.

2. Response. The assumption is denied, because wherever the written Word is
commended to us and its perfection is praised, there it is taught that there
is no unwritten Word of the kind the papists invent.

3. Response. What Bellarmine says about the passage in Moses is false, for
Moses speaks about the written Word in Deuteronomy 4:8–9, where he says that
this Word is the whole law which he commands fathers to teach their sons. But
not all parents could have known an unwritten law, since they wish it to have
been mystical and hidden from the common people. Finally, Moses himself later
divides this law into moral, ceremonial, and judicial law, but all these laws
are found in writing. Consequently, the things Bellarmine produces here are
either in Scripture or are not necessary for salvation.

Instance 11. There is a purgatory, but it cannot be proved from Scripture, as
Luther maintains. Therefore.

[1620 Tome I | col. 150 | BSB frame 00089]

Response. The proposition is denied. Luther therefore rightly asserted that
purgatory cannot be proved from the Scriptures. Luther did, however, at first
believe that purgatory existed, but only as a probable matter, as is evident
from Luther’s article 17.

Objection 4. If Scripture does not contain everything, and an unwritten Word
handed down is necessary, it will follow that there is some unwritten Word
handed down; otherwise God would not have provided well for the Church. But
the former is true, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. Both members of the connected antecedent are false, because
Scripture contains everything necessary for salvation; it is also false that
some unwritten Word handed down is necessary. Consequently, God has provided
for the Church through Scripture in the best and wisest manner.

Objection 5, from John 16:12: “I have many things to say to you, but you cannot
bear them now.” But what he promised to say here, he undoubtedly said after
the resurrection, while those things do not exist in writing. Therefore.

Response 1. There is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification, because
those many things were not simply different from those which he had previously
taught, but were the same things which had to be repeated and inculcated so
that the disciples might perceive them, recall them to memory, and preach them
to others, as is said in John 14:26. For Christ revealed everything received
from the Father, John 15:15. That the apostles did not perceive everything
before receiving the Holy Spirit is evident from John 2:22 and countless other
passages. Therefore he sent upon the apostles in Acts 2 the Holy Spirit
promised in John 15 and 16. Hence the papist Jansen rightly says on these
words that these many things were not different from those which he had taught
thus far, but were a clearer explanation of them; and he appropriately cites
in this sense what is contained in 1 Corinthians 3:1.

2. Response. The assumption is denied—namely, that those many things do not
exist in writing. For without doubt Christ himself taught the apostles these
many things, indeed all things,

[1620 Tome I | col. 151 | BSB frame 00090]

before and after the resurrection, Acts 1:3, and through the Holy Spirit, Acts
2. And the apostles taught and wrote those same things. Nor does this follow:
Christ then had many things to say; therefore, they were not all written
afterward.

3. Christ withheld many things; therefore, the things he withheld are papist
unwritten traditions. This does not follow. Augustine therefore rightly says
in Tractate 96 on John: “Since the Lord himself was silent, which of us may say
that they are these things or those? Who is so vain or rash as to affirm,
without any divine testimony, that they are the things which the Lord was
unwilling to say at that time?” Thus those futile traditions about rites,

4. In this argument the papists imitate the ancient heretics, according to
Augustine in Tractate 77 on John: “For all the most foolish heretics who wish
to be called Christians take occasion from this gospel statement to attempt
more boldly to give their inventions a specious coloring, where the Lord says,
‘I still have many things to say.’”

Finally, if the apostles, who afterward heard these many things from Christ
and received them from the Holy Spirit, did not write them for the use of the
Church, then either they were unwilling because they were envious, or they
were unable because they became forgetful after receiving the Holy Spirit.
But neither can be said. Indeed, they explained and wrote everything in the
Gospels and epistles. Augustine therefore rightly says in Tractate 49 on John:
“Those things were selected to be written which seemed sufficient for the
salvation of believers.”
