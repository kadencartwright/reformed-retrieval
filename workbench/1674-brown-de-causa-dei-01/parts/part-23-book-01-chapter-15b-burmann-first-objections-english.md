Let us first hear his arguments. “For if this were so,” he says, “either all
positive laws bind us, since they are supposed to be moral, or an explanation
must be given why one positive law binds us more than another. If it is said
that one positive law is moral and another is not, the question returns: What
is the mark of this distinction? If it is answered that one positive law rests
upon natural reason and another does not, by that very fact the positive law
is

[1674 logical printed p. 199 | IA leaf 217 | PDF p. 217]

transformed into a moral and natural law. For whatever rests upon natural
reason is moral and therefore immutable,” and so forth.

Reply: 1. We do not say that all positive laws bind us, but only those which
are moral. 2. We do not say that the reason for this distinction is that the
positive law which we call moral rests upon natural reason. Rather: (1) this
law concerns the conduct of human beings as such, while the others do not; (2)
it is proposed for the purpose of binding perpetually in every condition of
the Church, while the others are not; (3) what is commanded by this positive
law has an antecedent congruity with human nature, while the others do not;
(4) it is prescribed in the law of creation, while the others are not; (5) it
possesses an intrinsic moral ground, while the others do not; (6) it is founded
upon something moral, while the others are not; (7) it has never been abrogated
with respect to its substance, while the others have been abrogated or ceased;
(8) it is substantially useful to all human beings, while the others are not;
(9) it is contained in the Decalogue, while the others are not; and (10) it was
engraved upon Adam’s heart in innocence, while the others were not. What next?

“Nor does it avail to say that those positive precepts seem always to involve
moral precepts and are therefore moral as well. For external obedience to
positive precepts does not please God unless internal obedience of the mind is
added—that is, unless it is also joined with faith. Otherwise that obedience
is abominable to God. This is indeed true, but it still does not follow that
those positive precepts are at the same time moral. It follows only that they
presuppose those moral things which, as it were, clothe them and which must be
joined together with obedience to them, yet in such a way that they remain
distinct. Thus in every positive and ceremonial precept attention, love, fear,
obedience to God, and the desire to glorify him through that precept, and so
forth, were required. But those virtues were not commanded in the positive
precept which enjoined that external obedience and that act alone—the outward
work of sacrificing, washing, and so forth. Those prior virtues were
presupposed and commanded in other, namely moral, precepts. Consequently,
although those external acts separated from internal obedience and a spiritual
movement of the mind were not pleasing to God, nevertheless, such as they
were, provided the external rites were duly observed, they formed part of the
external and carnal worship and of the ‘ordinances of the flesh’ (δικαιωμάτων
σαρκός; Hebrews 9:1, 4, 10). In this way the external and carnal Jew satisfied
the ceremonial law, although he transgressed the moral law. Thus there was
always an evident difference between moral and positive law, even when the
observance of both was conjoined in practice.”

Reply: 1. We do not say that all positive precepts are moral, with the word
taken strictly. 2. We do not say that any positive precepts are properly moral
because they seem to involve moral precepts in this manner, as is clear from
what has now been said. 3. It is not surprising that by this argument he thinks
he firmly proves that positive precepts are not moral, since above he attempted
by this same argument to prove that no external work—indeed, no internal work
unless joined with love and obedience to God—is moral, and consequently that
no command which enjoins such works is moral. Does external obedience to moral
precepts please God without internal obedience of the mind or without faith?
Shall we therefore say that the fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, and ninth
precepts of the Decalogue are not moral, but merely presuppose the moral things
which clothe them, as it were? 4. We know that attention, love, fear, and
obedience to God were required in every positive and ceremonial precept, and
that these were not formally required in those positive precepts. But can the
same not also be said about certain moral precepts of both the first and second
tables?

[1674 logical printed p. 200 | IA leaf 218 | PDF p. 218 | ends before Pergit Pag. 10. § 13.]

Love, fear, and the desire to glorify God are not required formally and
immediately in the second, third, and following precepts, but, as he himself
says, in the first precept. Shall we therefore say that none of these are moral
precepts? 5. Above, strictly speaking, he wished only love of God and neighbor
to be called moral, because without them no external action could be called
moral or an act of virtue. Here he adds faith. 6. Here he numbers fear among
the virtues presupposed in every act pleasing to God, yet above he said that
even fearing God is not moral in itself unless joined with love and obedience
to God. 7. I cannot sufficiently wonder that he became so angry with his
venerable colleague for saying that obedience rendered to ceremonial precepts
was a morally good work, since he himself here acknowledges—what indeed cannot
be denied—that those external acts, even when separated from internal
obedience and a spiritual movement of the mind, formed part of external
worship, provided the external rites were duly observed. For what forms part
of the external worship of God is undoubtedly a morally good action, much more
so if internal obedience and a spiritual movement of the mind are present,
without which we concede that such an act is by no means pleasing to God. 8.
We concede that there is an evident difference between moral and positive—that
is, ceremonial and similar—law. But nothing appears here from which it can be
inferred with any appearance of probability that no positive law, or law which
does not flow from the nature of God or the human being, can in any respect be
called moral.
