### QUESTION X

Can the Creator inflict an injury upon a creature, and what right does GOD have
over his creatures?

I answer, first: “Right” is taken, first, for a certain constancy and
immutability in acting. Thus, on the supposition that God produces a creature
which is to endure for a time, by right of creating he cannot fail to preserve
it. And if he promises something to the creature, he makes himself a debtor
rather to his own constant and immutable nature—so that he immutably does what
he freely decreed and promised to do—than he creates any right in the creature
itself. For, strictly speaking, by the decree to create God did not from
eternity give a nonbeing a right by which he would be bound to create every
creature out of nonbeing and nothing. Rather, he was “voluntarized”—if one may
coin a word (ὀνοματοποιεῖν)—that is, through immutability he was most freely
necessitated toward a free creation.

[Marginal thesis: “Right” is taken in three ways, and what right God has over
creatures.]

2. “Right” is taken for a debt of justice. In this sense no one can do either
right or injury to a thing that is simply his own. For otherwise, first, the
creature to be founded would have had a right against God that he not fashion
it for everlasting dishonor, and the vessel could retort to the potter, “Why
did you form me this way?”, contrary to Romans 9. Second, the Creator's right
and dominion over the creature is infinite, for the whole creature and
everything belonging to it is GOD's in every way. If a servant belonged to his
master in every way, Paul would not say, “Masters, render to your servants what
is just and equal” (Colossians 3). Thus a servant has a certain right not to be
afflicted by his master according to the latter's absolute and independent
choice. I therefore admit that a created master owes his servants some right.
Because of the dominion he has over a servant by either the right of war or of
purchase, a master owes a servant less right than one equal owes another, or
than the servant owes his master. But because GOD's dominion over human beings
and creatures is infinite, GOD cannot, strictly speaking, do either right or
injury to the creature by afflicting it with eternal torments.

3. If God cannot inflict eternal torments upon an undeserving creature, then
the creature has a right against GOD that God not inflict eternal torments
upon it—and by the same reasoning, not even torments for an hour. The Socinian
Johann Völkel says: *Thus no one can do injury to his own thing insofar as it is
his. Yet it can happen that a person who belongs to another's jurisdiction is
certainly his own, not another's, free and not a servant, with respect to a
thing or its use; to that extent he is capable of right and injury. Our freedom
and right indeed rest upon a divine benefit, but—*

[Marginal reference: Johann Völkel, *On True Religion*, book 1, chapter 25,
page 739.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 578; IA PDF page 652; BSB
canvas 628]

*—once it has been given, we cannot be deprived of it except by our own
voluntary fault.*

I answer, first: if by creating things God would do injury when he took away
what he had given, he could not annihilate created things except because of
their fault, which is absurd. Second, all created things, including the freest
creatures—I mean human beings—are GOD's just as much and indeed infinitely
more than clay belongs to the created potter. Freedom does not confer on us a
right against GOD, for Paul says concerning free creatures in Romans 9: “Who
are you who answer GOD? Would the vessel say to the potter, ‘Why did you form me
thus?’” And that vessel is free. Third, Völkel assumes that through freedom we
are more our own and less GOD's, although freedom as freedom is absolutely a
creature of GOD; and through freedom we by no means cease to be GOD's
creatures. Rather, the freer we are by nature, the more we are GOD's servants
by right of creation. It is as though creatures without freedom, because they
are servile and not free, were more GOD's servants than rational human beings,
and as though we human beings, because we enjoy freedom, belonged less to GOD.
But created freedom and free acts are subject to GOD's dominion just as much as
any frog or fly. The right of all other creatures rests upon GOD's benefit no
less than the right of a free creature does; and GOD's freely bestowed benefit
establishes for God the foundation of a right over every creature.

[Marginal thesis: Freedom of choice subjects us more to GOD's dominion by a
right of obligation because of so noble a workmanship; it in no way frees us
from GOD's dominion, as Völkel dreams with the Socinians.]

Third, “right” is taken for moral fittingness and equity arising from the
relation of master and servant, father and son, Creator and creature, first
cause and effect. In this way, because God produces things outside himself, it
is fitting that the servant be subject to his master, the son to his father,
and the creature to the Creator. Because of God's primacy and the creature's
baseness and inferiority in relation to the Creator, it is fitting and just
that in addition to physical subjection the creature should also be morally
subject to the Creator. In this way the Creator, because he is Creator, has a
right over rational creatures even if we posited that there were no decrees in
God; but rational creatures have no right over God.

Nor do we cultivate a syncretism with the Socinians in this opinion. First,
the Socinians deny that GOD's dominion can extend to creating a creature for
eternal torments. With Paul in Romans 9, we assert that GOD's dominion extends
to forming vessels for everlasting dishonor according to GOD's absolute good
pleasure and without foreseeing sin; see Völkel. Second, the Socinians maintain
that GOD's eternal dominion exists only *a parte post*, “for as long as the
thing subject to him endures”—so Völkel—but we defend GOD's dominion

[Marginal thesis: We do not cultivate syncretism with the Socinians.]

[Marginal reference: Völkel, *On True Religion*, book 1, chapter 23, column
165; ibid., column 169.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 579; IA PDF page 653; BSB
canvas 629]

from eternity *a parte ante*, for God exercised dominion over nonbeings before
creation, as was said above. Second, we teach that dominion consisting in
having and possessing created things remains in act as long as the subject
thing endures, although, properly speaking, nothing is taken away from or added
to GOD's dominion in God. Third, we also teach that GOD's dominion extends to
the annihilation of things and to holding them, as it were, within the bounds
of nonbeing. Third, the Socinians suppose that GOD's right and dominion extend
to GOD's own usefulness—so Völkel; we deny that a human being can be useful to
his Creator. Fourth, the Socinians say that GOD's dominion over rational
creatures is limited and diminished by the creatures' freedom—so Völkel. We
assert that GOD's dominion over created free wills is full and independent.
Hence, fifth, what the Socinians say is blasphemous: that human beings have
independent dominion over their own hearts—see Völkel; second, that from
eternity GOD did not define by his decree all free acts and the circumstances
of things—so Völkel; third, Völkel's words are: *From a change in the vessel's
conduct there follows a certain change in GOD's special decree.* Sixth, the
Socinians teach that many things are illicit antecedently to GOD's free good
pleasure: for example, they teach that GOD's reprobating human beings because
God wills to reprobate them would be tyrannical.

[Marginal references: Völkel, ibid., column 107; *On True Religion*, chapter
13, column 176; chapter 25, columns 238, 239, 240; chapter 22, column 172;
chapter 23, columns 174, 176; chapter 25, columns 175, 179.]

I do not deny that if God created other rational creatures specifically
distinct from those which now exist, there would necessarily arise from the
natures of Creator, creature, and creation an essential relation between
effects and cause, servants and supreme Lord, father and children. And since
the Creator is the cause and author of the created thing, it is necessary that
the dependence, both physical and moral, of the creature upon the Creator—of a
first cause and its effect—should arise from this very fact, according to the
nature of a creature capable or incapable of moral obedience. For it is a
contradiction for something to be a created being and not be subject to its
Creator. I do not know whether a created being could be created that resisted
the Creator's decree, because on account of GOD's primacy and independence and
the creature's inferiority and dependence, it would be a created and an
uncreated being, dependent and independent. Indeed, because of GOD's absolute
dominion it would be subject and not subject to GOD's natural dominion if it
resisted his infinite dominion and absolute decree. Yet the question would
remain concerning a thing which materially is moral subjection: whether loving
the first lovable one (for believing that GOD is not God is, as was said, a
very different matter) is a moral subjection essentially owed by necessity of
nature by every creatable rational creature. Is

[Marginal thesis: The very fact that something is created establishes a
relation between Creator and creature, lord and servant, and a certain
dependence together with obedience owed.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 580; IA PDF page 654; BSB
canvas 630]

there an essential connection between a thing materially capable of being
created—whether it is moral or physical—and the Creator? Permit me, I ask, to
suspend judgment (τὸ ἐπέχειν) and to have room for doubt. Is not a creatable
moral thing, considered according to its moral being, no less of this or that
essence and of these degrees of perfection than a physical thing considered in
the same respect, because it pleased God by his free good pleasure to fashion
the degrees of things thus, so great as four, five, six, one hundred, or one
thousand?

Nor do our theologians posit an absolute necessity in the mode of the
satisfaction offered by CHRIST. Pareus, in his commentary on Hebrews, chapter
2, number 10, says: *If we consider GOD's absolute wisdom and power, GOD could
perhaps have pursued another way; but if we compare human sin with GOD's
justice and threatening, without doubt no more fitting means could have been
devised than this very one.*

[Marginal thesis: Our theologians do not posit an absolute necessity in the
satisfaction offered by CHRIST.]

But the Socinians, such as Völkel, deny that future contingents are decreed or
determinately known by God, and that GOD assigned his Son to death by either an
absolute necessity or the necessity of a decree. Völkel says: *When the
characters of the human beings to whom those decrees pertained changed, the
decrees themselves were also changed—or, if you prefer, the effects of those
decrees did not follow.* In the same place he says that God today chooses and
ordains a human being to eternal life if he believes, and tomorrow reprobates
and does not decree him if he does not believe; and in column 349: *GOD decrees
certain things which he had not previously decreed, and indeed things whose
contraries he had previously decreed.*

[Marginal reference: Völkel, *On True Religion*, chapter 32, column 331.]

[Marginal thesis: The Socinians deny the immutability of GOD's decrees.]

But Socinus himself enters upon a path plainly more blasphemous than all
blasphemy, for he says that, given this order of things, *GOD could by right
have forgiven us without accepting any true satisfaction for our sins.*
Likewise: *In either punishing or absolving human beings, GOD should not be
regarded as some judge who deals with another's right and is not permitted to
depart from the prescription of laws, but as Lord and Prince whose sole will*
(changed at every hour as the creature's will changes), *since only his own
right is in question, is the law and most perfect standard of all things.*

[Marginal reference: Socinus, *On Jesus Christ the Savior*, part 3, chapter 1.]

I answer: he is never the judge of the world if he is not judge in punishing or
absolving human beings, for the just Judge of the world can do nothing unjust,
neither absolve the guilty nor condemn the innocent (Romans 1:18; Hebrews 9:22;
Deuteronomy 27:26; Exodus 34:3). Here the norm of judgment is not the absolute
will acting from mere absolute dominion, but the just and determinate will. We,
however, teach that, if we consider his absolute dominion, God can absolve

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 581; IA PDF page 655; BSB
canvas 631]

or not absolve, punish or not punish the sinner, and that as absolute Lord he
does not do what he does under compulsion by a necessity of nature.

Socinus in the same place says: *GOD could therefore by right have forgiven us
our sins without any satisfaction for them accepted from anyone. Indeed, he
could so completely have done this by right that in a certain way he ought to
have done it—that is, he could not have done otherwise. Who would not* (says
the blasphemous man) *call that king tenacious and avaricious who, although he
could generously forgive his debtor subjects the money they owed him, instead
demanded it from one who owed him nothing—especially if he were so rich that
he had no need of that sum of money, while meanwhile GOD boasted that he had
remitted the whole debt to his debtors?*

[Marginal reference: Socinus, ibid., part 3, chapter 2.]

I answer: that blasphemer will therefore accuse the most just GOD of tenacity
and avarice. Though God needed nothing, he nevertheless willed to lay upon
CHRIST, the most innocent one, the chastisement of our peace through the most
dreadful and atrocious torments which he suffered—merely under the designation
of martyrdom, as Socinus saw it—and to appoint him as sin and an exemplary
sacrifice for us. Let Socinus himself answer for God in accordance with his
own opinion.

Second, we say that GOD's supreme dominion consisted in this: that he
transferred the penalties owed by us to his innocent Son, to whom no penalty
was owed. And although by reason of absolute right we believe that God simply
could have freely forgiven human beings' sins without any ransom intervening,
for God gave his Son to be crucified for us not by necessity of nature but by
a free and gracious decree, as the entire Gospel proclaims, nevertheless
Socinus is impious when he maintains that the Son could not even have been
given as a ransom without imputing necessity and avarice to God. For it would
be a mark of greater tenacity and cruelty—away with the blasphemy—to give the
innocent Son of GOD, who was free from every sin, whether imputed or his own,
merely as an example and proclamation, as Socinus supposes.

[Marginal thesis: By his doctrine Socinus brands GOD with the blasphemy of
tenacity and avarice, which he falsely imputes to us.]

Third, this right of GOD does not absolutely demand punishments from the Son of
GOD; nor does God act naturally in punishing the Son. Rather, once GOD's Gospel
decree had been established—whose unsearchable wisdom, shining mercy, and exact
justice we admire—we maintain that necessity lies with God. For once the decree
had been posited, by the necessity of immutable justice God could not fail to
exact punishments from the guarantor who had now been made under the law and
stood at the altar as a sacrifice subject to the curse. But by his absolute
dominion God could either appoint or not appoint him as guarantor.

Whether, and to what extent, God could command hatred of himself is another
question. I would not easily raise a question that sounds so ill to pious ears,
nor would I do so unless compelled. But it resolves to this: whether it implies
a contradiction for GOD to have created the rational creature that now exists
and for GOD to command the creature thus created to hate God. It is certain
that God is God, and that for him not to be the best and first lovable one
implies a contradiction. God therefore cannot command a rational creature to
believe that he is not the first lovable one. For that GOD could command such
a thing—that is, approve and will that contradictories be simultaneously
true—is impossible. Hence it seems to follow that God cannot command the
rational appetite to reject the first lovable one as something adverse and
harmful to itself, because by that very command God would order the intellect
to judge that the first lovable one is not the first lovable one. I neither
dispute what God could command by absolute power if GOD created rational
creatures differing from human beings and angels not in degree but in nature
and species (for surely by omnipotence he can create everything that does not
involve a contradiction), nor do I readily believe that it is lawful to
investigate it.

[Marginal thesis: To what extent God can command hatred of himself.]

We reject the opinion of the Socinians, who contend that *justice insofar as it
is opposed to mercy does not truly reside in GOD and is not a quality or
property of GOD, but only an effect of his will; and that anger or mercy are
certain affects, as it were, and effects of free will, by which GOD is simply
moved to punish sins.* On the contrary, justice inheres in GOD not in the
manner of a passing act, but in the manner of an abiding attribute and
property, as Scripture teaches: Exodus 34:7, 3, 5, 5, 6; Habakkuk 1:13; Nahum
1:2; Romans 1:19; Numbers 14:18; Exodus 20:5; Lamentations 1:18. The same must
be said of mercy: Exodus 34:6; 1 Chronicles 16:34; Psalm 103:8; Exodus 22:27;
Luke 6:36; 2 Corinthians 1:3; Psalm 86:5, 15. Nor should we invent anger,
severity, or anything else existing in GOD which simply moves him to punish
sins but does not abide in him perpetually. When God now punishes and now does
not punish, the change is in the outward object, not in God himself.

[Marginal references: So Völkel, *On True Religion*, book 1, chapter 23, page
177; Socinus, *On the Savior*, book 1, chapter 1; Crellius against Grotius, book
1, chapter 78.]

Those who say the following do not satisfy: *Punitive justice is not therefore
non-natural even though God did not always exercise it in act, because GOD was
naturally omnipotent, wise, and upright from eternity, before the world was
founded, prior to exercising those properties.* For the reasoning is not
parallel,

[Marginal thesis: The evasion of some who say that relative justice is natural
in God even if he does not always exercise it.]

[Witness note: The printed citation sequence is difficult and partly anomalous;

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 583; IA PDF page 657; BSB

because omnipotence or wisdom is not the adequate principle of things created
outside God unless the determination of GOD's free will is added, so that here
and now he exercises those properties. But relative punitive justice, which
stands in relation to creatures, is nothing other than GOD's will as efficacious
in second act, here and now punishing according to the worthiness of the sin,
or, so to speak, the will actually repaying. If God punished by a necessity of
natural justice, once a punishable object was posited he would always punish,
without any longsuffering or slowness to anger.

Nor do we accept this statement of Socinus: *Just as GOD could, and indeed by
right, have refrained from condemning sinners to eternal death, so after he has
condemned them to that death by his edict, by his will alone he can again
exempt them from his command, and indeed by right.* First, given the decree to
punish sinners with infernal penalties, God could not fail to punish either the
sinner in himself or in his guarantor—as Sibrandus most aptly says—with an
infernal penalty as to its substance or essence, on account of the justice of
truthfulness and immutability. And although, considered in terms of absolute
power, by his will alone, unrestricted by the decree of justice, he could again
exempt from eternal death, nevertheless he who said the following does not seem
to answer correctly: *This is the voice not of an equitable judge but of a
tyrant: “Thus I will; thus I command; let my will stand for reason.” When GOD's
penal judgment is under discussion, one should consider not absolute dominion,
but the judge who regards worthiness.*

[Marginal reference: Sibrandus Lubbertus, *On the Savior against Faustus
Socinus*, book 1, chapter 1.]

For GOD's absolute dominion is not a bare will to dominate apart from eternal
reason or infinite wisdom, as it is in this world's earthly tyrants, whose bare
lust or lustful will apart from every reason is the law and formal reason for
commanding. But infinite reason and wisdom reign in GOD's absolute dominion, as
appears by comparing Romans 9:20 with 11:33–34. Furthermore, neither the
worthiness nor the corresponding worthiness of the outward thing so determines
GOD's will as to exempt it from all absolute dominion; nor is God so determined
to punish by the intrinsic unworthiness of sin, because by reason of his
supreme dominion God is simply greater than every intrinsic consideration in
the object. This is what Sibrandus affirms: *It must be asked* (he says)
*whether there is some right to which, as to a rule, that equity which GOD
observes in correction is to be referred. For if civil law is the rule to which
the equity employed in civil corrections is referred, there is likewise some
right by which the equity employed in divine corrections is defined. No
one—*

[Marginal thesis: Another reason underlies GOD's absolute dominion than GOD's
bare will alone, no less than it underlies GOD's strict justice. See Andreas
Essenius, *On the Satisfaction of Jesus Christ*, book 1, section 2, chapter 2,
pages 9, 17.]

[Marginal reference: Sibrandus, *On the Savior*, book 1, chapter 1, page 9,
against Socinus.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question X; printed page 584; IA PDF page 658; BSB

*—one will have any material for evasion. This right is GOD's will, which, just
as it is the rule of all justice, also defines and determines this equity. The
word of GOD, therefore, is the written law by which this justice or equity
employed in the observance of this justice is defined.* Together with the
testimonies of Calvin and Pareus cited above, and to which I add this statement
of the reverend and most learned Beza—*If you consider GOD's decree, evil
itself has the character of good, even though it is evil in itself*—this
testimony, along with those of others who are thought not to have favored the
impure Socinus in any respect, proves that GOD's most free will is the origin
of all relative justice.

[Marginal thesis: Our theologians establish GOD's will as the first rule of his
outward justice. Beza, in a question and answer.]

Hence the question concerns the rule of that justice which God commands to
creatures. It must be said that GOD's free will is not the adequate rule of all
the justice which God can will and command outwardly; rather, GOD's nature and
his natural fittingness in acting, as I warned above, sometimes concur in this
rule. Second, Sibrandus speaks more plainly in the same place: *Both these
tables, namely of the law and the Gospel—that is, GOD's will revealed in the
Law and Gospel—are that right to which, as an exact standard and rule, GOD will
refer the equity that he employs in correcting the human race. Both are
immutable right, for they are GOD's own will, which it is impossible for
anything to change.* Third, hence the origin of justice relative to creatures
(I except natural and intrinsic justice, by which it is intrinsically equitable
for the Creator to love himself, for God to be and to be just, and for the
creature in general to be subject to the Creator) is GOD's free will.
