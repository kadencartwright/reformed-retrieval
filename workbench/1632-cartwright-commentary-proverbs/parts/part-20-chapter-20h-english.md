Verse 25. It is a snare to a man who swallows a sacred thing; and after vows to make inquiry [is a snare].

In מוקש (*tendicula*, snare) there is a metaphor from birds or wild animals which are captured by snares through baiting.

The purpose is to deter people from sacrilege.

First one must see what it is that he calls sacred. That is sacred which is assigned and devoted to sacred uses either by God’s institution and command or by the voluntary and spontaneous liberality of people. Here, if you ask what sacred uses are, the answer is readily at hand: whatever is contributed for the use of divine worship, schools, and the relief and support of the poor. Of this kind under the law in former times were firstfruits, tithes, sacrifices every third year, gleanings, the gathering of remaining grape-clusters, vows, and other things of that kind. Now under the Gospel, although firstfruits and tithes are not necessary, nevertheless whatever has been established by law or contributed through human liberality for these uses must all be regarded as sacred. For this reason either the removal or even the diminution of these things is condemned as the crime of sacrilege; Deut. 23.21–23.

Although this corruption has prevailed in every age, in all memory it has never raged more than in this most unhappy age of ours. In it very many not only give nothing, but even, so far as they can, take pains to cut something away from and gnaw at that which was contributed by their ancestors for the worship of God and the support of schools and the poor. Their ancestors used the greatest liberality—or rather lavishness—for the worship of idols and even of the Devil himself, which they falsely believed to be the worship of the true God. They could never satisfy themselves in liberality toward their seducers and guides to eternal damnation; and they were exceedingly generous toward their priests, though unlearned and even avaricious, who demanded payment from every Baptism and from other fictitious sacraments, burials, funeral rites, and extraordinary masses. They went so far that from gain and commerce which fled the conscience of everyone else and of which no one besides themselves was aware, they contributed tithes. But this age, which God has adorned with faithful and learned Ministers

[1632 witness | PDF p. 449 | printed columns 872-873]

even by his own testimony, is intent upon always stealing and taking something away from the lawful Ministry. As though it were not enough for them, imbued with a false opinion, to regard true Ministers as impostors, they treat them so unworthily and restrictively even while regarding them as true.

This crime is aggravated in the Prophet at Mal. 3.8–9, who complains that this injury has been done not so much to people as to God himself. Hence also in the Law it is said that the tribe of Levi did not have an inheritance among its brothers, but that God was its portion, because what fell to them was devoted and consecrated to God; afterward, by a new donation of God, it devolved upon the Priests and Levites. This was done for this reason: that the people should not think the Ministers beholden to them because of these contributions.

But since sacrilege is so much more grave and atrocious than theft, robbery, and embezzlement, in proportion as it is more criminal to despoil God than people, there are also degrees in sacrilege. For the gravest sacrilege is that committed against the ministers of the word. This is indeed so much the more atrocious because those who take away the support of Ministers and Schools are guilty not only of their own destruction but also of the ruin of infinite souls, which in succeeding ages will perish through want of the divine word, by the very fact that just stipends are lacking for the support of Ministers.

Next to this is that which is committed against schools, the seminaries of officers of the Church and Commonwealth. Last is that which consists in intercepting relief for the poor.

Now let us see what the Prophet pronounces against those leeches. Here

1. he compares the things sacred to God to the bait with which a hook is covered or which is set upon a trap and snare, so that the fish or bird which gnaws at it is immediately caught by either the hook or the snare and strangled.

Therefore the Prophet teaches that this transfer of sacred things to one’s own uses carries before it the appearance of a singular good, by which foolish and unwary people, who savour only earthly things, are enticed. He teaches that these things have a sweet and pleasing taste while they are in the mouth, but afterward sting and bite more severely than those who have been struck by scorpions, vipers, and other most venomous serpents; and that the same thing happens to them as to unwary fish and birds which have exchanged their life for a single bite of desired food, so that their condition is no better than that of the profane Esau who sold his inheritance for a meagre mess. For a time will come when they will repent of having even touched what they so greedily devoured and gorged themselves upon.

But if it is deadly and destructive to those who enjoy these goods, what will become of those who were merely servants of other people’s avarice, who themselves perceived no fruit from it, and who were drawn into this sacrilege only by the hope of obtaining plunder afterward?

Then in the latter half it is taught that the very attempt and desire to revoke and withdraw sacred things once given to God is like a snare by which people are enticed, even though they do not attain what they desire—much more if they obtain their wicked wishes. And if it is so grave to take away from God what they themselves gave him, of how great a crime will those be guilty who, though they themselves contributed nothing, attempt to snatch away what others contributed?

This, however, must not be understood as though no vows of any kind whatsoever are to be rescinded, since these things must be understood of lawful vows made according to the prescription of the word.

Verse 26. A wise King winnows the wicked when he turns a wheel over them.

In מזרה (*ventilat*, he winnows) there is a metaphor drawn from farmers, who separate chaff and refuse from the grain with a winnowing fan. In מלך (*rex*, king) there is a synecdoche of the species for every Magistrate.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 450 | printed columns 874-875]

In אופן (*rotam*, wheel) there is another metaphor, likewise drawn from farmers, who in hot regions used to thresh the grain by the turning of a wheel attached to an ox or horse.

The purpose is that by the due execution of justice the Commonwealth should be cleansed as though it were a threshing floor.

From this Proverb it appears in what place and estimation Scripture holds the wicked: namely, that of chaff, tares, and refuse. This nevertheless seems to conflict with what is written elsewhere, at Psalm 37, where they are compared to a laurel flourishing throughout the whole year and spreading and extending its branches on every side; at Isa. 2, to cedars and oaks; and likewise at Psalm 144.5, to mountains. But it will be easy to reconcile these with one another if we say that by the former comparison the Scriptures describe the wicked as they truly are and are in God’s judgment, which judges everything according to truth, while by the latter comparison they describe them as they are in their own vain opinion and our false one. We esteem something greater and more magnificent concerning them than the fact and truth bear.

Therefore we must correct our error and recall our opinion of the wicked to God’s incorrupt judgment, lest we fear them, since the greatest and most powerful tyrants in their most ample glory and splendour are nothing other than chaff, a speck of dust, husk, and so forth.

Then let us consider how great a difference there is between the godly and the ungodly: namely, as great as between grain and chaff, and between the purest gold and the most ignoble metal. Thus we should not envy them or be led by a desire for their prosperity.

But especially—and this is the point proper to this passage—it is foreign to the duty of a good farmer to permit grain to be mixed with chaff, since because of the multitude of chaff it is even completely buried by it. For thus the grain will be of no use for sustaining human life. It is no less harmful to permit the wicked to be mixed with the godly on the threshing floor of the Commonwealth. Indeed, it is much more dangerous for the wicked to be confounded with the godly than for chaff to be mixed with grain, because through association and dealings with the wicked the godly are corrupted and, when the wicked abound, are utterly exterminated.

Although this must be accomplished in every Commonwealth, it must be done so much the more in a Christian one, because the wickedness which is committed against the light of the divine word is greater, and at the same time more obscuring of divine glory for this reason: whatever is committed is attributed to the Gospel of God. Hence at Psalm 101 it appears that David stirred himself to the execution of justice with this spur, so that he might purge the city of God by the destruction of the ungodly.

But here it is objected again that the state of the Church in this world is set forth by Christ in such a way that it will always have the wicked confounded with the godly, no less than tares are mixed with grain in a field, and fish with mud and stones in a net. I answer that the teaching of this parable does not prevent the Magistrate from straining every sinew to scatter and uproot the wicked, because even amid the greatest effort and utmost attempt to uproot them, some nevertheless will remain. Then Christ speaks about those wicked people who, while feigning piety, restrain their wickedness within their minds; but Solomon speaks about lawbreakers whose wickedness bursts forth before everyone’s eyes.

From this it is concluded that the Magistrate who is drowsy and delays in his duty—or, as Solomon would have it, is foolish—is one under whom wicked and shameful people, adulterers, fornicators, drunkards, and those haunting public places spring up. For it is most certainly established by Solomon’s Proverb that when punishment is taken against evildoers, they are routed; just as, on the contrary, they stream together in troops and overflow when they are not restrained by punishments.

But if the Magistrate’s winnowing fan should cease, by the winnowing fan of divine judgment

[1632 witness | PDF p. 451 | printed columns 876-877]

they are nevertheless scattered, as is said everywhere in the Scriptures. This is the more grievous because it is inflicted late and slowly, so that it would have been far preferable for the Magistrate’s wheel to torture the wicked than for them to experience God’s most severe judgment.

Verse 27. The spirit of a man is Jehovah’s lamp, searching all the inward parts of the belly.

In נר (*lucerna*, lamp) there is a metaphor of light for knowledge, by which hidden and secret things are uncovered no less than light plunged into darkness uncovers them. In בטן (*ventris*, of the belly) there is likewise a metaphor for hidden things, because just as the intestines enfolded and entwined with one another in the belly are not seen, so also the secret counsels of a person which the human spirit searches are not seen.

The purpose is to kindle people to the investigation of truth, so that, once the frauds of the wicked have been detected, they themselves may pay due penalties and the upright may pass their life in peace. And it seems to be a reply to a tacit objection that could be raised from the preceding Proverb: How would the King winnow, and so forth, since the wicked lie hidden and wickedness has many hiding places and recesses in which it conceals itself?

In Job 28 the Prophet, in order to amplify by a comparison from the lesser the unsearchable knowledge of God, celebrates human wisdom, by which a person searches out and investigates the most difficult things—specifically, metallic substances hidden in the innermost chambers and recesses of the earth: gold, silver, and sapphires. He celebrates how, once veins of metal have been discovered, a person dries up waters that break forth, even though they are so deep that they cannot be crossed or passed through, and stops them so that they do not drip even a drop; how he restrains flames in digging those mines; makes the very mountains sink by driving tunnels; moves huge rocks; and finally accomplishes other marvellous things by human art and ingenuity.

At 2 Sam. 14 David is compared to an Angel of God because of his supreme acuteness in investigating all civil matters; and Solomon at Eccles. 2.13 and following teaches that the difference between this worldly wisdom and folly is as great as that between light and darkness, and the distinction between a wise person and a fool as great as that between a blind person and one who can see.

From this it is understood that this light shining in those shadows must be fostered by study, diligent reflection upon the works of God, and experiments. For this very reason idleness, by which it is corrupted as though by rust, and drunkenness, by which the sharpness of the intellect is blunted and finally buried—or, as the Prophet says, swallowed up—must be vehemently avoided as reefs of this wisdom.

One must take care, moreover, that through confidence in our ἀγχινοίας (*quickness of mind*) we do not drowsily pass over inquiry into matters and solicitous investigation. This is habitual and customary for certain people who, trusting in their own sagacity and being impatient to discuss a disputed matter more fully, often pronounce a false or rash judgment.

Just as this teaching has a use in every part of this life, so its singular use especially exists in civil judgments. There the difficulty of the matter brought into judgment ought not to deter judges from employing labour and diligence to examine what in each matter is true or false, just or unjust. See the example in Solomon, 1 Kings 3, and Job 29.16.

Then, because he says that this lamp was kindled by Jehovah, we are taught that it must be employed especially in those matters which concern him and his worship, so that those evil deeds by which the worship of God is especially violated may be disclosed by this light of reason. Of this kind are idolatry, superstition, enchantments, and profanations of the Sabbath, so that when they have been uncovered they may receive due penalties.

Finally, since this light belongs to Jehovah,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 452 | printed columns 878-879]

we are taught not to be puffed up by esteem for it or to become proud and, as though our horn were raised, utterly to despise others who are less discerning. 1 Cor. 4.7.
