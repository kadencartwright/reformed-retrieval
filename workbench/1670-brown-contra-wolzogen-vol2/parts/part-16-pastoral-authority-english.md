ASSERTION XIII.

Those Pastors who have thus been called, sent, and ordained possess a distinct
authority and power; that is, they are bound to discharge with authority the
office imposed upon them. Because Velthuysen denies this, we will attempt to
prove it. This must first be stated: we are not speaking here of that special
Ecclesiastical authority and power commonly called the power of jurisdiction,
for we will speak of that later. Here we speak only of authority in general,
or specifically of authority in announcing the Gospel, and we understand an
inferior and conditional ministerial authority. Our thesis is proved as
follows.

1. Paul says in 2 Corinthians 10:8, “Even if I should boast somewhat more of
our power which the Lord gave,” and so forth. Ecclesiastical Officers therefore
have power granted by Christ. For although the Apostle speaks here specifically
of the authority of his Apostleship, it can nevertheless be proved very well
from this that all Ministers of the Word are also endowed with power. See
Calvin on the passage: “For what preaching of doctrine,” he says, “is without
power?”

2. Titus 2, final verse: “Speak these things, and exhort and reprove with all
command. Let no one despise you.” The Apostle wills him to add the weight of
his command to his doctrine, as Calvin says on the passage. “With the highest
authority,” Beza says on the passage, “as God’s ambassador, doing everything
not in his own name but in God’s.” The Belgic Annotations say the same. The
Greek word in Scripture signifies command, precept, and injunction; see 1
Timothy 1:1; Romans 16:26; 1 Corinthians 7:6, 25; 2 Corinthians 2:8. As some
note, it is a military word denoting the command which a Commander has over
his soldiers.

[Volume II; printed p. 225; ONB canvas 233]

3. 2 Corinthians 13:10: “Furthermore, I write these things while absent, lest
when present I should employ cutting severity, according to the authority
which the Lord gave me for building up and not for destruction.” Here, Calvin
says on the passage, “he first fortifies that rigor by God’s command, lest it
seem to be thunder without a thunderbolt or an impulsive attack rashly stirred
up.”

4. 1 Timothy 4:11, 12: “Charge and teach these things; let no one despise your
youth.” The word signifies “I command and charge.” It is a forensic word,
denoting the edicts of magistrates, Acts 5:28; Luke 5:14. It is so used by the
Seventy Interpreters in Joshua 1:1; 1 Kings 10:17; 15:4; 22:14. “Sometimes,”
says Beza on 1 Thessalonians 4:2, “it is taken generally for commanding and
forbidding. But, as the very derivation of the word shows, it is properly
spoken of that kind of commands which is handed over and received through
another’s hands by someone who announces something in another’s name, as if
received through his hands.” It is so explained in 1 Corinthians 11:17. See
also 2 Thessalonians 3:4, 6, 12; 1 Timothy 5:7; 6:13, 17; 1:3. “The word
*charging*,” Calvin says on the passage, “signifies power. For Paul wills to
furnish him with power to restrain others.” See also Acts 17:30. Hence
*parangelia* is a command and a charge which, received from a Superior power,
is announced to subjects through a messenger or ambassador. 1 Thessalonians
4:2: “For you know what precepts we gave you through the Lord Jesus.” See Acts
16:24; 1 Timothy 1:18.

5. What else is conveyed by those words in 1 Thessalonians 5:27, “I adjure you
by the Lord”? The Belgic Annotations direct us to compare them with Matthew
26:63. The verb *horkizō* properly signifies requiring an oath from someone so
that he gives you assurance. Hence it is often taken for commanding something
with the authority of the divine name interposed; see Acts 19:13.

6. Those words in 1 Timothy 5:21 also denote authority: “I testify solemnly
before God—that you observe these things.” See Acts 18:5, where Paul, impelled
by a new power from God, did not simply assert the truth, but proved it as
though by interposing a solemn testimony.

[Volume II; printed p. 226; ONB canvas 234]

See also 2 Timothy 4:1; consult the Belgic Annotations on the passage.

7. We read of “commands” in Colossians 4:10: “concerning whom you received
commands.”

8. Our Assertion is also confirmed from those passages of Scripture in which
mention is made of the obedience, subjection, honor, and reverence to be
rendered to Ecclesiastical Officers by the people or flock, Hebrews 13:17. Yet
we do not will them to lord it over the Flock, for this is expressly forbidden
in 1 Peter 5:3, but to exercise paternal authority and administer Christ’s
institutions with the gravity and authority which is fitting.

9. When they are despised, the very authority of God and Christ is by
implication despised, Numbers 16:3, 11; Luke 10:16. “It is,” says Calvin on the
passage, “a remarkable commendation of the external ministry when Christ
declares that whatever honor and reverence is rendered to human preaching,
provided it is faithful, God acknowledges as rendered to himself.” And a
little later: “Conversely, to deter us from contempt for the Gospel, he adds a
severe denunciation: those who are reluctant to hear Ministers, however
contemptible, are insulting not men but Christ himself and God the Father.”
See Matthew 10:40; John 13:20.

10. The Assertion is further confirmed from those titles indicating authority
with which they are distinguished, such as (1.) *proestōtes*, “those who rule,”
1 Timothy 5:17, compared with 1 Timothy 3:4, 5, 12; (2.) *proistamenoi*, “those
who preside,” 1 Thessalonians 5:12; (3.) *hēgoumenoi*, “leaders,” Hebrews 13:7,
17. “He sought a title,” Beza says on the passage, “wholly removed from all
ambition, yet one of the greatest authority.” It is a military and political
word and is spoken of a public person who presides either in a city or in a
camp. It corresponds to the Hebrew word *nagid*—that is, prince, commander,
prelate, or overseer—by which title a prince of the people is signified. (4.)
They are also called Bishops, Acts 20:28; 1 Timothy 3:1. The Athenians gave
this title to those who were set over cities.

[Volume II; printed p. 227; ONB canvas 235]

(5.) They are called *oikonomoi*, “stewards,” 1 Corinthians 4:1; Titus 1:7;
Luke 12:42. They therefore have some authority, for a steward is the governor
of everything his master possesses. See Luke 16:1; Galatians 4:2, where the
word signifies governors or guardians. (6.) They are called *kērykes*,
“heralds,” 1 Timothy 2:7; 2 Timothy 1:11; 2 Peter 2:5—that is, public heralds
or envoys who faithfully and authoritatively publish an edict or announce
something. Hence *kērygma* signifies an edict delivered by a herald’s voice.

11. This is also confirmed from certain metaphorical titles denoting authority,
such as the titles Fathers and schoolmasters in 1 Corinthians 4:15.

12. Our Assertion is especially confirmed from 2 Corinthians 5:20 and
Ephesians 6:20, where they are said to discharge an embassy. They therefore
discharge that office authoritatively and by virtue of a special delegated
office.

I know that the furious author of *Grallæ* ridicules this and receives it with
his customary mockery. On page 125 and following he also labors to prove—but
with a fruitless attempt—that no one should now be called Christ’s ambassador.
Why, I ask? Because no one now has been sent by God to discharge an embassy in
God’s name or furnished with the requisite gifts—that is, no one is now called
immediately by Christ or endowed with the gift of infallibility.

But 1. This reasoning would prove with equal force that no one can now preach
the word or administer the sacraments. For the Apostle was called immediately
to preach the word and endowed with the gift of infallibility to discharge
that office.

2. Paul calls himself Christ’s ambassador not for those reasons which that
author of *Grallæ* recalls, but for reasons common to all duly constituted
pastors who duly discharge their office. For in 2 Corinthians 5:18, 19 he gives
this reason: “He gave us the ministry of reconciliation and placed in us that
word of reconciliation. Therefore,” he concludes in verse 20, “we discharge
an em-

[Volume II; printed p. 228; ONB canvas 236]

bassy in Christ’s name.” From this it is clear that all persons today to whom
that ministry of reconciliation has been given must be called Christ’s
ambassadors, or be said to discharge an embassy in Christ’s name. Therefore it
must either be said that there is now no one in whom that word of reconciliation
has been placed and to whom this ministry has been given, or that all those to
whom it has been committed must be said to discharge an embassy in Christ’s
name. Similarly, in Ephesians 6:20 he says, “for whose sake I discharge an
embassy.” For whose sake, I ask? See verse 19: “that I may make known the
mystery of the Gospel.” He discharged an embassy in this, namely, in making
the mystery of the Gospel known. This was the principal matter of the embassy.
It is therefore evident either that there is now no one who makes or can make
known this mystery of the Gospel, or that faithful, duly called and constituted
Pastors who make this mystery known must be called Christ’s ambassadors.

3. By this reasoning the ministry will be utterly destroyed. And this indeed,
it seems, is the aim of that unhinged author and of all who follow in his
footsteps, for the gift of infallibility is granted to no one now, nor will he
affirm that anyone is now called immediately by Christ in the way that the
Apostles and Prophets were separated to this office. Or at least the ministry
will be of less use and benefit. For experience teaches that when the ministry
has once been despised, depraved persons devoted to pleasures make light of
whatever Pastors say in God’s name. But however much these people applaud
themselves in this, they will finally find that saying of Moses in Deuteronomy
33:11 most true: “Break the loins of those rising against him”—that is, Levi—
“and of those who hate him.” And whatever they mutter, that saying of Christ
is most true: “He who hears you hears me, and he who despises you despises me.”

4. On pages 98–99 he asserts that the Prophets and Apostles alone were such
messengers and ambassadors. But 1. Neither Prophets nor Apostles were always,
everywhere, and in all

[Volume II; printed p. 229; ONB canvas 237]

things infallible, as is evident from 1 Chronicles 17:2; Galatians 2:11–14.
He himself also admits this in the *Frisian Cautery*, page 120. 2. Then the
Priests of old should not be called God’s ambassadors, because they were not
Prophets. But Scripture testifies the contrary in Malachi 2:7, where the
Priest is called an Angel of “Jehovah of hosts.” He himself does not dare to
deny that both Priests and Levites were such, for on page 126 he speaks as
follows: “For he wills his ambassadors to possess such authority that they be
obeyed absolutely and no one turn either to the right or to the left from
their command, under penalty of death, as may be seen in Moses, Deuteronomy
17.” But the ambassadors of whom Moses speaks in Deuteronomy 17 were Priests
and Levites, as verse 9 demonstrates. 3. Then neither should the Evangelists be
called Christ’s messengers and ambassadors, for they were not Apostles or
Prophets but were distinguished from them, 1 Corinthians 12:28, 29; Ephesians
4:11. What he has in the *Cautery*, page 77, is ridiculous—namely, that Timothy
and Titus were immediate ambassadors of the Apostles, but mediate ambassadors
of Christ. For then they should have preached the Gospel immediately in the
name of the Apostles, and they would have been endowed with the gift of
infallibility only by reason of the Apostles and not of Christ. Although
nothing more absurd can be imagined, he seems to intimate just as much on
pages 80–81 of the *Cautery*. 4. The Pastors of the Churches of Asia Minor are
called Angels in Revelation 2 and 3. But an Angel is the same as a Messenger or
Ambassador. Yet these were neither Prophets nor Apostles called immediately or
endowed with the gift of infallibility. 5. Christ sent other Ministers besides
Apostles or Prophets, as may be seen in Ephesians 4:11. Yet those Pastors and
Teachers were not called immediately by Christ or endowed with the gift of
infallibility. Nevertheless, they were Christ’s Ambassadors, and those who
heard them heard Christ himself. Otherwise it must be said that those sent and
given by Christ to build up the Church should not be regarded as Christ’s
ambassadors. What could be more absurd, since

[Volume II; printed p. 230; ONB canvas 238]

he himself does not dare to affirm that anyone was sent by Christ without
being furnished with gifts, or that one whom Christ sends is not fortified by
a valid and authentic commission? 6. On page 126 of *Grallæ* he himself does
not dare deny that all stewards of God’s mysteries are Christ’s ambassadors.
But persons other than Apostles and Prophets were such stewards of God’s
mysteries, 1 Corinthians 4:1. 7. I think he will not deny that Magistrates, in
discharging the magistrate’s office, bear God’s name and administer justice in
his name, place, and authority. Yet he does not dare affirm that all Magistrates
are infallible in discharging this office. But if he does not dare say this,
the reasoning he advances perishes and vanishes into smoke. 8. By what
argument, I ask, will he prove that no one can now discharge an embassy on
behalf of Christ or can or ought to exhort in turn unless he is endowed with
the gift of infallibility?

“We do not deny,” says the very celebrated Hoornbeeck in his *Apology for the
Christian Church, Not Apostate*, page 107, “that among those who act in God’s
name there are some who act infallibly. But they do not have this from the fact
that they act in God’s name, but from the fact that they act thus and
infallibly in God’s name by a peculiar grace and order. Namely, there is a
twofold order of Ministers sent and commissioned by God. The first, supreme,
extraordinary, and immediate, is that of infallible Teachers, such as the
Prophets and Apostles, Ephesians 2:20. The second, inferior, common, and mediate,
is the order of fallible Ministers and Teachers from God through the Church’s
mediate calling, Ephesians 4:11, 12; Romans 12:6–8; 1 Corinthians 12:4–8. The
former cannot err, not because they speak in God’s name, place, and authority—
which in its own manner is common to both—but because they belong to that order
and are peculiarly endowed with the gift of infallibility. The latter indeed
can err, even though speaking fallibly by virtue of office in God’s name. Nor
does the mere fact that someone acts and speaks in an-

[Volume II; printed p. 231; ONB canvas 239]

other’s name make him unable to err. Thus an ambassador represents the name
and person of his Prince, but only insofar as he conducts himself according to
the instructions given him, from which he certainly can also deviate shamefully.
Thus Ministers in the Church represent God’s name and person when they teach
by virtue of office, although those who belong to the first and immediate order
do so more nearly, more significantly, and more illustriously, ‘from men nor
through man,’ and represent God’s place more nearly. Nevertheless, all
Ministers of the Church, even the common and ordinary, are considered to act
in God’s name and authority—namely, when and so long as they speak according
to the instructions committed to them from the written documents, do not
evangelize beyond the Gospel, and preach what they received, Galatians 1:8, 9.
Thus anyone who acts in another’s name is understood to act in that name so
long as he observes the trust given him, from which he can also depart.”

Thus on page 110: “Suppose,” says the same celebrated Author, “we sin in this,
that we hold our sermons in the name or authority of God. Can it be denied that,
even if they are not held in God’s name in the sense which he desires, this
can nevertheless be admitted in another sense—namely, the one which we
previously gave from Scripture: that they are said to be performed by God’s
command and help and for his honor and worship? And does this not also carry
with it some authority, namely, that which we described?” And a little later,
page 111: “VI. So far is it from seeming that sermons should be avoided because
they are held in God’s name, that they ought not to be held otherwise, by force
of that universal rule in Colossians 3:17: ‘Whatever you do, whether in word
or deed, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus’; 2 Thessalonians 3:6:
‘We announce to you, brothers, in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, that you
withdraw,’” and so forth.

I need not add more to confirm the Assertion, since no one can say with any
appearance of reason that there is no difference between a Pastor who publicly
announces the Gospel by virtue of his office

[Volume II; printed p. 232; ONB canvas 240]

and a private person who pronounces the same things which the Pastor said, or
similar things. Who does not know that what a public herald promulgates in the
King’s name and authority has a very different force and authority from what
some private person relates? But Pastors are public heralds, envoys, and
messengers sent by Christ to promulgate the Gospel. Therefore they possess
greater authority than some private person. Indeed, if there were no difference,
that office would be rendered utterly empty. For to what purpose is that
office instituted if one who does not discharge it can do the same thing in
precisely the same manner as one who has been solemnly called to that office?
If any private person can publish or promulgate the King’s will and laws with
equal authority and convenience, to what purpose would that public office of
heralds or envoys be instituted? Would not the greatest confusion arise in
commonwealths from this, and would the things done by private persons and those
done by persons discharging a public office have altogether equal authority?
What, shall we think that Christ did not institute order in his Church or did
not will to remove all confusion from it? Far be it from us to think so. Paul
“spoke freely in the name of Jesus” at Damascus, Acts 9:27. What, does this
not denote authority? When a public herald pronounces or promulgates something
in the King’s name, does he not do it with authority?

Ministers are God’s ministers, 2 Corinthians 6:4; 1 Thessalonians 3:2. And they
are Christ’s ministers, 2 Corinthians 11:23; Colossians 1:7; 1 Timothy 4:6,
and they announce the Gospel in his name and authority. Hence Christ says,
“He who hears you hears me.” Christ established authority for himself from the
fact that he came in the Father’s name, John 5:43, and performed in the
Father’s name the works which he had done. Therefore, since Pastors discharge
their office in the name of Christ, by whom they have been sent and whose
Angels and Messengers they are, they necessarily possess authority.

[Volume II; printed p. 233; ONB canvas 241]

Finally, it will be worth considering where the contrary opinion tends. For if
it prevails, the ministry is finished, and consequently the Church and our
salvation are finished. If a private person possesses the same authority as a
public Pastor, who will not readily conclude from this that the ministry is
neither necessary nor very useful? Perhaps our adversaries aim at this one
goal: that it be utterly abolished—a thing which Satan uniquely desires and
would purchase at a great price. For once the ministry is removed, he will
dominate, the Kingdom of darkness will increase daily, atheism will reign, and
every impiety will run rampant. If Pastors possess no authority, who will not
readily hold them in contempt, make light of them, and set any value upon what
they say? “We are no more bound,” they will say, “to obey them than any other
private persons.” But let them read 2 Chronicles 36:15, 16: “Jehovah, the God
of their fathers, continually sent to them by his messengers from early dawn,
because he compassionately embraced his people and his dwelling. They mocked
God’s messengers, despised his words, and said they were being led about by his
prophecies, until the heat of Jehovah’s wrath was kindled against his people,
so that there was no remedy.”

This is what the Quakers and others promoting Satan’s Kingdom press, intend,
and attempt: that the ministry be removed. And what else is conveyed by those
words of Velthuysen, that the entire congregation is now holy? Does he not fear
Jude 11: “and they perished in Korah’s contradiction”? It is well known for
what end these words were first spoken by Korah and his accomplices in Numbers
16: “They assembled against Moses and Aaron, saying, ‘Enough from you! For this
whole assembly—all of them—are holy, and Jehovah is among them. Why then do
you exalt yourselves above Jehovah’s congregation?’” verse 3. They willed the
order instituted by God to be abolished, especially the priestly ministry, so
that it might be lawful for each and every person, equally with

[Volume II; printed p. 234; ONB canvas 242]

the Priests, to perform sacred rites, because all were no less holy than Aaron.
The sacred text indicates what a tragic and miserable end and outcome this
rebellion had. Nevertheless, we desire repentance for our adversaries in this
question, lest they perish with those despisers and violators of the Divine
order. We also ask them to learn to speak magnificently and honorably of
Ministers. For they are God’s assistants—*synergoi*, 1 Corinthians 3:9, and
*synergountes*, 2 Corinthians 6:1—in spiritual husbandry. The gathering and
building up of the Church is a kind of husbandry, and the supreme *geōrgos*,
husbandman, is the heavenly Father himself, John 15:1. Ministers are fellow
workers and assistants in this work; they plant and water, 1 Corinthians 3:6.
They are Ministers of the new covenant, 2 Corinthians 3:6. How beautiful ought
the feet of those evangelizing peace and good things to be? Isaiah 52:7; Nahum
1:15; Romans 10:15. They are Christ’s sweet fragrance, 2 Corinthians 2:15.
They are Christ’s Ministers and stewards of God’s mysteries, 1 Corinthians
4:1; Titus 1:7; Luke 12:42. They are stewards who, with the keys given by the
master of the house, open the treasures which God deposited in the Church’s
house—indeed, treasures of mysteries, and of God’s mysteries concerning
eternal salvation. They are Fathers begetting persons in Christ Jesus through
the Gospel, 1 Corinthians 4:15, and they labor in birth until Christ is formed
in their hearers, Galatians 4:19. Instrumentally through the word they generate
faith in their hearers, and thus, by converting the faithful to Christ, beget
or regenerate them. Hence they are also said to save, 1 Timothy 4:16. They
cherish like nurses, 1 Thessalonians 2:7. They are men of God, 1 Timothy 6:11;
2 Timothy 3:17. They are heralds proclaiming the Gospel of peace, 1 Timothy
2:7; 2 Timothy 1:11. They are witnesses, Luke 24:48; John 15:27; Acts 1:8;
10:41; Revelation 2:13; 11:3, bearing testimony concerning God’s will from his
own word. Let people also learn “to obey and submit to” those Teachers, Hebrews
13:17, to remember them, verse 7, and to acknowledge those who labor among
them, preside over them in the Lord, and admonish them, and to esteem them very

highly in love because of their work, 1 Thessalonians 5:12, 13—*kai hēgeisthai
autous hyper ekperissou en agapē*, with an infinite overflowing, more than
overflowingly, as Beza says on Ephesians 3:20. For it is surely doubtful
whether those who do not esteem them dearly and regard them as fathers and
begetters have yet been converted to God or regenerated. Daily experience
teaches that this institution of God is held in the highest value by all who
have been converted to Christ through it. Let them remember that Ministers
discharge an embassy in Christ’s name, 2 Corinthians 5:20, and have the word
of reconciliation, verses 18, 19. See also Ephesians 6:20.

It remains now to call for examination the things in Velthuysen’s little book
which seem to militate directly or indirectly against this Assertion. But I
will first set down certain words of that distinguished and most noble
Commentator Robert Bodius on Ephesians 6:20, page 1112 in my copy: “From this
same title and designation of Ambassador, these three things are principally
gathered. First, the dignity and excellence of this ministry. For first, you
know that an Ambassador is not just any messenger or courier, even of the
lowest order, or a worthless little man, but an honored and illustrious
messenger customarily chosen from the highest, or certainly the noble and
equestrian, order of men. Nor is he customarily sent or commissioned by just
anyone, but by some King or Prince; for it does not belong to everyone to send
Ambassadors, but to Kings and Princes or illustrious Commonwealths which
possess supreme authority over their subjects. Hence it happens that the
greater and more exalted the King, Monarch, or Emperor by whom Ambassadors are
sent; the greater and more illustrious the business to which they are sent;
and finally, the lower and inferior in dignity those are to whom it is decided
that an embassy should be sent, the more honor and reverence is customarily
shown to the Ambassadors themselves. Now if anyone wishes to apply all these
things

[Volume II; printed p. 236; ONB canvas 244]

to Christ’s ambassadors, who does not see at first glance how great in every
way is the superiority and eminence here, and how great according to every
respect mentioned above is the dignity and excellence of the spiritual
Ambassador? For he is sent by the supreme Monarch of heaven and earth to
unworthy and miserable mortals, with the power of remitting or retaining sin
and of binding or loosing hearts and consciences. He is sent to perform the
greatest and most desirable of all tasks: reconciling people to God, as was
said; converting them from sin and darkness to light and righteousness, from
Satan to God and Christ; transferring them from a state of death and
condemnation to grace and eternal life; and for this purpose he is furnished
and sealed with the most abundant gifts of the Holy Spirit as with the seal of
divine power.”

Because some wish to apply all these things to the Apostles alone, Bodius there
indeed acknowledges that primarily and absolutely only the Apostles, chosen
immediately by Christ and furnished with extraordinary gifts, can be called
Christ’s ambassadors. But he further says: “Secondarily and less principally,
just as the function itself, so also the name and title of Ambassador can and
ought to be extended to all true Pastors of the Church and Interpreters of the
Word and divine Will. For in their own place and order they also expound God’s
will to people, announce the word of reconciliation, and exhort people in
Christ’s name and place to be reconciled to God—that is, gratefully and humbly
to embrace the peace and grace which God offers them in Christ. We have already
said that this is the work and duty of this embassy, although they do not do it
with the same authority and independence as the Apostles, or with, so to speak,
the same indifference as to places and persons. Rather, their calling is more
restricted in this respect and bound to certain places and persons.” And on
page 1113, column B: “Third, he proposes God’s

[Volume II; printed p. 237; ONB canvas 245]

utterances as God’s utterances in discharging his embassy—that is, with the
gravity, sincerity, and authority suited to Christ and his Holy and Sacred
Word.” And on page 1116, column C: “Now from this verse so expounded, besides
the things which were copiously deduced from the preceding related clause, let
us see what gleaning of observations occurs. First indeed, since this title of
Ambassador with which the Holy Spirit sometimes distinguishes the heralds of
the Gospel must not be restricted to the Apostles alone, although they were
foremost in gifts and authority, but must be extended to all Pastors of the
Churches, called by Christ though through human ministry to indicate his will
to people and exhort them in Christ’s name to be reconciled to God, as we
taught above: you have heard that the dignity of this ministry and its duties
or parts deduced from it also pertain to all of us in our own place and rank,
as many as have succeeded the Apostles themselves in the pastoral office.”

And after some things, column 2, E: “Now if anyone asks the use of this
observation, this consideration of spiritual dignity and excellence serves
first to vindicate our ministry from the contempt of profane people, or
certainly to strike shame and horror into them at once, because although they
profess Christ’s name and faith, many nevertheless dare to carry their contempt
for Christ’s ambassadors so far as to regard them as the lowest and most
worthless of men.” He has similar things on page 1120, column 1, B: “Next, let
profane and impious people who, although professing that they believe in Christ

what they ought to judge of themselves or what opinion to conceive of
themselves. Whoever you are who loathe and disdain Christ’s faithful servants,
perhaps because their doctrine and discipline oppose your conduct and
corruptions,

you profess remarkable devotion and zeal. For so formerly the Scribes and
Pharisees, building the tombs of the Prophets and adorning the monuments of the
righteous, said, ‘If we had been in the days,’ and so forth, Matthew 23:30.
They said this, as Christ reproached them, while they pursued Christ himself,
the Head and Lord of the Prophets, with implacable hatred. Therefore, whoever
you are, neither condemn your predecessors nor exempt yourself from the number

He begins to spew out his poison in this way on page 273. Among other things
which he says savor of superstition, he does not omit this one but pursues it
at greater length: namely, that “some think it in itself more pleasing to God
to hear the Truth from the mouth of a Preacher than from another’s mouth, and
place greater religion in hearing a sermon than in reading the same or in
hearing a precentor read a chapter.” *Reply.* I fear that while you do not
wish to appear superstitious, you appear wholly profane, and that, like fools,
while avoiding one vice you rush into its opposite. Do you regard obeying God
and worshiping him as he himself has prescribed as superstition? Did God not
command the faithful to acknowledge, hear, and follow their Pastor and obey
him in the Lord? Did Christ not institute this office of preaching for the
building up of the Church, for converting the elect,

[Volume II; printed p. 239; ONB canvas 247]

and so forth, as we too will make clear later? Do you therefore will those to
be superstitious who prefer to hear those whom God commanded to be heard and
whom Christ sent for this end, rather than others whom they do not know and do
not understand by what Spirit they are led, but whom they know well enough to
be endowed with no Ecclesiastical office and not sent by Christ? What? Is it
not more pleasing to God to obey him than to despise his commands? Next,
attendance at public sermons is part of instituted worship; but reading a
sermon is only a private action to which Christians are bound by no special
command. Therefore no comparison should be instituted between these. Further,
by what argument will you prove that those precentors or Readers were
instituted by Christ and that the word must necessarily be read in public
assemblies by those unable to explain it? I indeed will God’s word to be read
publicly, but by those who are bound by virtue of their office and furnished
with gifts to explain it and resolve knots and difficulties—that is, by
Pastors called to this by Christ. What? Do you wish to institute a comparison
between God’s word merely read aloud by a private person and the same word
expounded and suitably applied by a Pastor?

What next? “They hold the word announced by a Teacher to be of greater
efficacy,” he says. And why not, since, as was proved, they possess authority?
You are a remarkable detector of superstition, since you regard this as
superstition. But you seem to correct what was said in the following words:
“I am not speaking of those who hear sermons so that they may grow in knowledge
and be stirred to religion, and who feel holy motions aroused in themselves
through them—which indeed are truly religious acts. But I speak of those who
place religion in the very hearing of the sermon, just as for the Jews the very
rest of the Sabbath day was a part of religion,

[Volume II; printed p. 240; ONB canvas 248]

although they admit that rest not sanctified by works of religion and mercy
was not pleasing to God. Likewise, participation in baptism and the supper is
a part of religion, although it is by no means acceptable to God when done by
the unprepared. So some will the hearing of a Preacher to be a part of religion,
although by no means pleasing to God when they attend without the requisite and
proper motions of the heart.” *Reply.* 1. Can a good intention so change the
nature of an action that the same action which in itself is no worship or part
of worship becomes true worship? I had thought that all instituted worship and
every part of it was instituted by God alone and depended upon him, not upon
human beings. 2. Do you think that the very rest of the Sabbath day was a part
of worship only for the Jews and not also for us? If so, you are mistaken.
Although it is true that such rest is by no means very pleasing to God when we
press no further and neglect or postpone that for which the rest was instituted
as a means, nevertheless it can, despite this, be just as truly a part of
religion for us as for the Jews. 3. It is well that you seem to admit the
celebration of Baptism and the Lord’s Supper to be in itself a part of worship
or religious act. For how then can you say, as we heard you say, that these can
be administered by anyone? I do not sufficiently understand, since just as
Christ instituted these parts of worship, so he expressly indicated through
whom he willed them to be administered. 4. If the administration of the
Sacraments is a religious act, why is the administration of the word not also,
since both are included in the same commission, Matthew 28:18? 5. Therefore it
will no more be superstitious to say that attendance at sermons is a part of
God’s worship than to participate in the Sacraments.

But he continues on page 275: “From this they conclude that just as in the
legiti-

mate use of the sacraments God is accustomed to dispense grace in a special
manner which he does not grant outside the administration of the Sacraments,
so he dispenses grace in a special manner through the words of the Preacher,
although they differ in no way from those which another person not constituted
in the office speaks and teaches, or from those which the Preacher himself
writes in a little book.” O how great is the profanity when people dare openly
to profess and declare to the whole world that they have never perceived any
good either from the administration of the sacraments or from the
administration of the word, and thus testify that the announced word has never
yet been to them a savor of life unto life but always has been, and still is,
a savor of death unto death; and that until now the Gospel has been veiled to
them, as it is veiled to those who perish, in whom the god of this age has
blinded the minds of unbelievers lest the illumination of the Gospel of the
glory of Christ, who is God’s image, should shine upon them, 2 Corinthians
4:3, 4. Therefore, Velthuysen, if you are ignorant of these irradiations of
the Holy Spirit and efficacious motions which sometimes accompany the faithful
administration of the word—by which persons truly convicted of sin and taught
the nature of the Gospel are drawn to Christ by God’s powerful right hand,
rest upon him, place all their hope in him, and remain, cling, live, and work
in him, and he in them—if, I say, you are ignorant of these, you would have
served your reputation by remaining silent. For now you openly pronounce that
you are utterly ignorant even of the rudiments of salvation and of the manner
in which God ordinarily converts the Elect to himself; indeed, ignorant of
things which I do not know whether any member of a well-constituted Church
ought to be ignorant. Therefore you must be numbered among those “who revile
whatever they do not know,” of whom the Apostle speaks in Jude 10. What? Do
you dare attack this conclusion which all the faithful in every age have
experienced to be most true? O how

great and what kind of impudence this is! Although this action may be
superstition to you who ridicule these things, you must concede that it is not
superstition to others who know, feel, and experience the contrary. For this
is the force of your argument.

What next? “That Teachers teach with special authority and power and have a
special divine character, even without considering that their office is from
God, who wills people to be instructed in Religion and aroused to virtue by
exhortations; and that, for the sake of order and decorum, this be done by
those fit for it, to whom this burden and care has specifically been committed
and who undertake it.” *Reply.* 1. We have now confirmed that Pastors possess
a special authority and ought to announce the Gospel with authority; this can
therefore be said without any danger of superstition. 2. That anyone says this
without respect to the office instituted by God is merely your dream and
invention. For it is a contradiction in terms to say that God’s Servants have
authority without any respect to that office which is from God. 3. You cause
the matter to be disjointed by speaking here of that office, but elsewhere a
more suitable occasion will be given to demonstrate what your opinion is. In
what else do you blame them?

That “they regard hearing the Preacher as a special work of Religion apart
from consideration of the exercise of the communion of Saints and of good
order, according to which they ought not to despise an exhortation made to the
assembly by a suitable person; nor do they consider that they ought to praise
God in the congregation.” *Reply.* Do you regard that communion or gathering
of Saints as an act of Religion in which there is no exercise of piety? Or do
you think this gathering, considered by itself and

[Volume II; printed p. 243; ONB canvas 251]

abstracted from sermons, prayers, thanksgivings, and similar things, to be a
part of Religion? Indeed, you will far surpass in superstition those whom you
wish to censure here. Know that this gathering is only a means to exercise
public and solemn worship, in the consideration of which it is included. 2. If
that order is merely human, as you seem elsewhere to affirm, it is no wonder
if they do not regard it as Religion. But if it is divine, they cannot fail to
consider it when they regard hearing a Pastor exhorting, admonishing,
promulgating God’s law, and signifying God’s mind—and doing this in God’s
name—as a part of worship. 3. Praising God is indeed a religious act, but one
act does not destroy another.

He continues on pages 276–77: “Thus scarcely any necessity can occur to which
the hearing of a sermon ought not to yield. Neither decency nor shame, which
prevents someone among a refined people from appearing in public, has any
force here. Hence Preachers and sermons are multiplied. Hence the common
people think they profane the Sabbath when, without any necessity restraining
them, they do not hear sermons from early morning until evening; and they
think that reading and praying at home is not so pious an exercise as hearing
a Preacher.” *Reply.* Now, O Velthuysen, we have a specimen of your piety and
of how highly you value that great institution of Christ. 1. Can you name
anyone who thinks or says that no necessity can rightfully detain him from
hearing a sermon? Or do you will those to be superstitious who do not invent
or create for themselves a necessity by which they can be drawn away? What you
see is hidden from me, but anyone who does not see that even at Utrecht not a
few—indeed, very many—without any necessity whatever compelling them, hide at
home, wander through the streets, run here and there, or openly profane the
Sabbath by attending to their business, whether privately or publicly, is
blind.

[Volume II; printed p. 244; ONB canvas 252]

This evil increases daily, especially since someone publicly taught and
asserted in writing that the Fourth Commandment concerning observance of the
Sabbath is not moral, and thus loosened the bridle for every profane, wanton,
atheistic mind and despiser of religion, and undertook the patronage of every
impious person. 2. This is your piety: to value civil respectability—which is
rather vanity—so highly and cast away all care for the soul. What? Does God’s
Kingdom consist in vain, worldly adornment of the body? Do these lofty things
of the world render anyone more pleasing to God? Away with it! Those willing
to appear in public without any shame to see some spectacle, without
ornaments, curls, gold, pearls, costly clothing, or the covering of cloaks, can
also attend divine sermons without them. And if they knew what it was to
hunger, or like newborn infants to desire that pure milk of the word so that
they might grow through it, they would not be restrained or held back so
readily. Or if they had awakened consciences terrified by a sense of divine
wrath because the law had been violated, forgetful of adornments they would
run as swiftly as possible to the announcement of the Gospel and overcome very
great difficulties. 3. It displeases you that there are so many Pastors and
public sermons. Perhaps one sermon a week, or each month, or perhaps each year,
would be enough for you. Perhaps you will also think that one Pastor can feed
the entire flock of Utrecht. From this it is clear where Velthuysenian piety
tends and by what Spirit it is driven. 4. Perhaps you think that those
sufficiently sanctify the Sabbath who, to sleep off their drunkenness, lie in
bed far into the day and, after perhaps hearing an afternoon sermon, spend the
rest of the day in sleep, seek their own pleasure, and wander through streets,
gardens, the ball court, or along the walls, and so forth. 5. And who besides
you, who seem to have cast away all

[Volume II; printed p. 245; ONB canvas 253]

piety, would say that attending public sermons and publicly worshiping God
according to his prescription is not better than reading or praying at home?
The pious know well enough from what Spirit such comparisons proceed. I will
add no more.

We have now heard about the people; what does he say about Pastors? “And the
Pastors,” he says on page 278, “also believe the same, or it seems not to
displease them that the common people believe so. They address the assembly at
the beginning of the sermon as if they were those in whose mouth God had
specially deposited his word. They will everyone to believe that their
prayers, doctrine, blessings, free declarations concerning curse and
damnation, consolations, and so forth, are holier and rendered more efficacious
by God than when the same things are done by some other pious private person.”
1. Why ought Pastors not believe what is most true? And why ought what pleases
God not to be most pleasing to them? 2. Why ought Pastors not to go forth as
God’s ambassadors and prepare and move the people by serious exhortation to
receive and imbibe the truth with the greatest reverence, faith, attention,
fear, and trembling? Whatever seems good vexes, distresses, torments, and
angers you. You do not will Pastors to stand in Jehovah’s counsel and see or
hear his word, but rather that each steal God’s words from his friend or be
prophets of the deceit of their own heart. But if they have God’s word, hear
what God says through Jeremiah 23:28: “Let the Prophet who has a dream relate
the dream, and let him who has my word speak my word truly. What has straw in
common with wheat? says Jehovah.” 3. That stale comparison, set before us so
often, has long since begun to stink. If you advanced any argument to prove
what you repeat here to the point of nausea, we would hear and examine it. But
things stated nakedly and crudely and repeated again and again—I know not how
many times—we can readily dismiss.

What

[Volume II; printed p. 246; ONB canvas 254]

next? “Showing that they are God’s messengers, not simply because according to
God’s command they are elected by those to whom this right belongs to propose
to the people what, according to their best wisdom and knowledge of the
doctrine of salvation brought from wherever each can bring it, they believe
and judge ought to be received and done; but that they are such messengers as
are specially instructed by God in what God wills to be made known for us to
do or avoid, and who by reason of the pastoral office have a special
commission or letters of trust or credence, as they are called. Hence they
will their words, threats, and promises to be received as if God had spoken
through them. Hence they also apply to themselves, ‘He who despises you
despises me.’” *Reply.* 1. We proved and vindicated above that Pastors are
God’s messengers, and you seem to concede it in some manner, at least in some
respect. But in reality you will them to be messengers who are not messengers.
For 1. you will them to be God’s messengers in this respect, that they are
elected by those to whom this right belongs, but not because they are sent,
instituted, and ordained by God. 2. Who are those to whom this right of
election belongs? Neither here nor anywhere in this entire little book,
although you frequently mention this election, do you indicate it. I suspect
what you will: namely, that this right belongs uniquely to Magistrates, and
thus that it belongs to the Magistrate to elect and constitute them in the
same way that they elect and constitute their servants, lictors, officers of
the court, bailiffs, attendants, fire wardens, assistant teachers, captains or
commanders, and the like. But tell me: can all these, by reason of this calling,
be called God’s Messengers? By what right, therefore, can Pastors by reason of
this calling be called God’s Messengers? 3. To what end are they elected? So
that they may propose to the people what they believe the people ought to do
and believe; therefore not to expound God’s will to the people, explain God’s
mind,

[Volume II; printed p. 247; ONB canvas 255]

and make manifest what God requires of them. These are the men whom you will
to be regarded as God’s Messengers; or if there are any Messengers of God, you
will them to be called Messengers for this reason. 4. But from where do they
have or draw the knowledge through which they teach others? From God? According
to you, this is not necessarily required, for it is all the same to you from
wherever they have it. Is that so? Then it is all the same whether they have it
from God or the Devil, or from their own brain and dreams. O what kind of
Messengers of God these will be! If indeed they have their instructions from
the Devil, they will be messengers of the Devil, not of God. And unless they
have everything from God, they will be Prophets of the deceit of their own
heart, not God’s ambassadors. 5. Is it not right that God’s ambassadors be
taught and instructed by God? And does it displease you that Pastors are taught
by God, or that they say they have been instructed by God? But what if it is
true? I have heard of some who studied by praying and, through prayers, had
from God everything which they proposed to the people. And indeed all Pastors
ought to be on the mountain with God and have from him not only the matter but
all other requisites, so that they may discharge their office with greater
fruit. But because you do not grasp these things, you seem to ridicule and
receive them with mockery. Yet take care that you do not continue to scoff,
lest your bonds be strengthened, Isaiah 28:22. 6. You seem to deny that Pastors
by reason of their office have a commission and, as it were, letters of trust
from God. But by what reasoning or argument, I ask, will you show that the
matter is not so? Yet you seem to think that you have sufficiently discharged
your office if you deny the most certain and evident things without annexing
even the appearance of a reason, so that you appear all the more absurd and
irrational. 7. If Christ said to his Apostles, “He who despises you despises
me,” the Successors of the Apostles in preaching Christ and announcing Christ’s
Gospel can apply this to themselves without any sacrilege. But the former is
true; therefore. Yet everyone says that these things are true insofar as they
teach true things,

[Volume II; printed p. 248; ONB canvas 256]

which he himself admits in the words immediately following on page 279. But
from this he infers that their authority depends upon the truth of their
doctrine, not upon their office. *Reply.* He is no less ridiculous here than
elsewhere, as if all the authority of a Magistrate depended upon the equity of
the judgments he renders and in no way upon the office he discharges. And
because those who despise a public herald duly and faithfully executing his
Prince’s commands also despise the Prince himself, it would follow that this
Herald possesses no authority by reason of his office. It also displeases him
there that Pastors, in prayers both before and after sermons, call the words of
their discourse God’s word. But whose word, I ask, ought they to propose to the
people? Their own? Then they will not be Christ’s Servants. The Devil’s? Far
be it. Ought they not to announce the Gospel? But whose word is this? Ought
they not to reveal God’s will? But whose word, I ask, is this? What? They are
God’s Messengers, yet ought not to propose God’s word? They do not say that
they cannot err, but will everything they utter to be examined by the
touchstone of the divine word. Yet those things which they know to conform to
God’s word and which actually do conform to it, they can rightfully propose as
such.

But from this he infers on page 281 that “any private person can exhort,
console, and so forth, his neighbor with as much authority as a Pastor can, or
that both do it without any authority.” *Reply.* By equal right, because
Magistrates are not infallible, their commands, judgments, and decrees will
have no greater authority than the same words spoken by a private person. Thus
a fellow pupil will have the same authority as a teacher and schoolmaster; a
brother as a father; and a neighbor as a husband. But let us hear the reason:
“For if the formal reason why the Teacher’s word ought to be received is the
authority of God’s word, and if hear-

[Volume II; printed p. 249; ONB canvas 257]

ers ought to judge as far as the direction of their consciences is concerned,
then Teachers have no authority whatever as to doctrine.” *Reply.* This is a
futile argument and proves only that the Minister does not have the authority
of the Prince; it does not prove that he lacks the ministerial authority of
which we speak. When the Magistrate commands Subjects to worship God duly, the
formal reason why the subjects worship God in this way is not the Magistrate’s
bare authority, but the authority of God’s word, which commands God to be
worshiped thus. And everyone is bound to examine that very command of the
Prince or Magistrate by the touchstone of God’s word. Yet who is so senseless
as to say that the Magistrate has no authority? When the Magistrate executes
his commands through a bailiff or officer of the court, the formal reason why
the subjects obey the command explained by that officer and set forth in the
Magistrate’s name is the authority of the Prince or commanding Magistrate.
Nevertheless, that bailiff, in promulgating the Magistrate’s judgment, has
ministerial authority greater than that of any other private person. Thus one
who despises, makes light of, or wrongs him as he duly discharges his office
despises, makes light of, and wrongs the Magistrate and Prince himself. One who
holds in contempt some other person not furnished with the Prince’s authority,
but doing such a thing by his own authority—that is, none—does not do this.
Who besides Velthuysen, who does not see the sun shining at midday itself, is
ignorant of these things?

But he continues on page 282: “Everyone is bound to embrace the truth taught
by nature or God’s word because of the truth itself and God’s authority. But
that truth borrows no authority or binding force from anyone’s mouth, but has
all of it from God its author.” *Reply.* From what has been said it is evident
that there is no lime in this trap. When a Magistrate, Parent, or Teacher

[Volume II; printed p. 250; ONB canvas 258]

commands a Subject, Son, or Pupil to worship God, sanctify the Lord’s Day, and
the like, that subject, son, and pupil is bound to obey, not only by force of
the subject matter because it is God’s command, but also because of the force
of the Fifth Commandment, which commands Inferiors to obey their Superiors in
the Lord. If they refuse to obey, they must be punished not only because they
did not perform the commanded thing, but also because they despised the
authority of their Superiors. 2. Truth in itself borrows no authority from
human beings, but a person who proposes it can nevertheless have authority
with respect to us, whose authority in preaching the truth we are bound to
acknowledge.

What next? “It is absurd for them to assume the quality of God’s Ambassador
because they have been elected by those who possess the right of election to
spend time—with God’s help, who must therefore be asked—in investigating what
God’s will is and what people must do, since no one is bound to embrace their
declaration of God’s will according to their letters of trust merely upon
their word, but must consult their Master. Who has ever heard of such
Ambassadors and letters of trust?” *Reply.* It is certain that the Apostle
Paul says in 2 Corinthians 5:20 that he and others discharge an embassy in
Christ’s name. Does Velthuysen dare deny this? What then will he say? Where
will he turn? Even those to whom they announced God’s will examined their
doctrine and consulted their Master, and were commended for it, Acts 17:11:
“Those at Thessalonica—namely, the Bereans—were more noble, since they received
the word with all eagerness, daily examining the Scriptures to see whether
these things were so.” What will Velthuysen say of Christ himself? Was he
God’s ambassador?

[Volume II; printed p. 251; ONB canvas 259]

Did he have authority and nevertheless command his hearers to search the
Scriptures, John 5:39? You therefore see that these things can be fully
consistent: that someone discharges an embassy in Christ’s place, and that
Christ himself is consulted concerning what that person says. 2. Above, you
willed them to be called ambassadors because of such an election. We do not
say this, but assert that they are Christ’s ambassadors because of Christ’s
mission; nor because they are elected to explain God’s mind, but because they
are sent in Christ’s name, with Christ’s authority, to announce the Gospel,
promulgate God’s laws, put Christ’s commands into execution, and so forth. Let
Calvin be heard on the passage: “This contributes no little to establishing
confidence in the embassy; indeed, it is wholly necessary. For who would rest
upon a person’s testimony concerning his eternal salvation? The matter is
greater than that we can be content with human promises, unless it is certain
that they have been ordained by God and that God speaks to us through them.
And to this pertain the commendations with which Christ himself distinguished
the Apostles: ‘He who hears you hears me,’ and so forth; ‘Whatever you loose
on earth will be loosed in heaven.’”

We must now see what Velthuysen says elsewhere. On page 331 he has similar
things and says it is absurd to say that Ministers have authority. What is his
reason? “First,” he says, “who are those Preachers who teach with authority?
The sound ones, evidently. But who are they, and who designated them? Must we
judge authority from doctrine? Does the person who faithfully announces his
master’s mind have the character of God’s ambassador before we know his
doctrine rather than his authority?” *Reply.* The Ministers who teach with
authority are those sent, ordained, and instituted by Christ, to whom he gives
the word of reconciliation to announce in his name. And if perhaps they teach
some false things contrary to the commission given them, or the sacred
documents, in

[Volume II; printed p. 252; ONB canvas 260]

that they have no authority from Christ, because all their authority is for
building up and promulgating the truth. Thus from the doctrine taught by one
who says that he has been sent by Christ, we judge—at least in part—concerning
his authority and mission. And what, I ask, is absurd here? A herald has
authority to promulgate the King’s will; but if he perhaps promulgates the
dreams of his own head as the King’s decrees, in this he is destitute of all
authority. And by an authentic copy of the King’s decree the subjects can
judge that herald’s authority in that business. Does it follow from this, I
ask, that when the herald faithfully discharges his office he has no
ministerial authority?

He continues: “Are they only Ministers who have that authority in the true
Church? This is the same as saying that the wisest man is the Master of all
others, though he is designated by no one. What benefit is there in saying
that such power is from God, when anyone is free to regard this or that man as
the wisest? Then this power was not inherent in him before our judgment, but
is attributed to him through our judgment. If they say that this right belongs
to the wisest man whether we regard him as such or not, what kind of right, I
ask, is that which no one is bound to acknowledge before being convinced? The
authority of Ministers contributes nothing to this conviction, but follows
it.” *Reply.* Velthuysen advances many words here but says nothing. Accumulating
words without sinews, he toys with the reader and trifles. We therefore say
briefly that the person whom Christ sent has authority. But who that person is
is evident from the Scriptures, for there we are taught whom we ought to regard
as a true or lawful Pastor: namely, one placed in that office by the ordinary
manner prescribed by Christ, furnished with the requisite qualifications, and
faithfully discharging the office entrusted to him,

[Volume II; printed p. 253; ONB canvas 261]

leading the people to Christ and not away from Christ. His commission or
letters of trust are declared to us in Holy Scripture; therefore we must “test
the spirits to see whether they are from God,” 1 John 4:1. The Ephesian Church
also tested those who claimed to be Apostles and were not, and found them to
be liars, Revelation 2:2. Therefore we have the marks and characters of a true
ambassador of Christ described, and thus have Christ’s ambassadors clearly
designated. Nor does this right belong to anyone because we think or judge so,
or fail to belong because we believe so. Our judgment is a Lesbian rule and
can err. Some did not regard Paul as an Apostle; nevertheless he was a most
true Apostle and proved by infallible marks that he was an Apostle. There were
also pseudo-apostles whom others regarded as true Apostles, yet they were not
true Apostles. Finally, one who is a true ambassador of Christ must be
received. Nor is it enough to say that certain persons are not yet persuaded
in their consciences that he is a true ambassador of Christ, because an erring
conscience does not bind. We are bound to know the marks of a true ambassador
of Christ and, after making a suitable examination, to know that these exist
in an ambassador sent by Christ. Therefore the ambassador’s authority does
not in itself follow our knowledge or persuasion, but only in some measure
with respect to us. For he has authority whether this is known to us or not;
but it is not established for us before we know it.

He continues on pages 332–33: “It can be asked in how many points of doctrine
he must be judged by us to be orthodox before we ought to regard him as a
Teacher sent by Christ. And once he is regarded as such with respect to these,
it can be asked how far his authority and commission extends with respect to
other controversies of faith.” *Reply.* Since it is certain that the
Magistrate has no power or authority from God to exercise injustice and is not
from God insofar as he exercises tyranny and injustice, the same questions can
also be raised concerning him—namely, how many acts or kinds of justice he
must first perform before we regard him as a lawful Magistrate,

[Volume II; printed p. 254; ONB canvas 262]

and how far his authority extends with respect to other controversies. Let
Velthuysen answer for the Magistrate and he will answer for Ministers at the
same time. If he says that he cannot, then let all Magistrates know that he is
no less injurious to them than to Ministers. But the answer is easy: authority
differs from doctrine and neither properly flows from it nor depends upon it,
but has been granted by God for promulgating doctrine. Yet from the doctrine
we can know *a posteriori* whether a person faithfully discharges his office
and whether he has truly been sent by God. Although every error does not
testify that he is not Christ’s ambassador, he has no authority from Christ to
propose or defend any error. Every defect does not abolish the office itself.
But if he obstinately errs in fundamentals or in things which properly touch
fundamentals and are of great moment and consequence; if through his doctrine
he leads the people away from Christ; or if in life he is criminal or

Finally, the commission of Christ’s Ambassador extends to everything revealed
in God’s word, for he must declare the whole will of God.

He proceeds: “It is a remarkable kind of embassy in which those who transact
some business with that ambassador do not found his authority upon the letters
of trust which he has, but upon their belief that he does everything according
to the instructions he received.” *Reply.* It is sufficiently clear from what
has been said above what must be answered here. For we say that the Pastor has
his authority from Christ and that it is founded upon the fact that he has
been sent by Christ,

[Volume II; printed p. 255; ONB canvas 263]

not upon the fact that we think or believe he faithfully administers the
office. Yet from bad administration we can judge that to that extent—or
altogether—he does not have authority, examining and testing his doctrine by
the touchstone of God’s word and a certain balance. Thus the things he has at
the end of that paragraph are also resolved.

He further asks on page 334: “Do Pastors have this authority when they write
books, or when they exhort in a carriage or boat, and so forth, or propose
consolations or threats to the sick?” *Reply.* The same can be asked concerning
Magistrates: do they have authority when they write books, or when in carriages
or boats they pronounce what is equitable, and so forth? 2. Not all a Pastor’s
actions are pastoral. 3. In discharging the pastoral office, whether publicly
or privately, they are Pastors and enjoy pastoral authority; therefore they
must be acknowledged as such.

He next asks on page 337: “Does one have authority who has been elected only
by the people, not confirmed or ordained through the imposition of hands? And
does he have the power of the keys? If he does, it would follow,” he says,
“that the people possessed those keys; and if the people possessed them, the
right of the keys will be nothing other than a private right, according to
which any private person judges another so to err in faith or morals or both
that he has no part in God’s Kingdom and therefore must not be regarded as a
brother.” *Reply.* Velthuysen errs here, ignorant of Scripture. All pastoral
power and authority is from Christ, but it is given only ministerially by
people who observe Christ’s laws. A husband’s power over his wife is from God
alone, not from the Pastor or Magistrate who celebrates the marriage. Thus
pastoral power is from God and from God alone; no other cause which creates or
effects that power is given, although instruments or means are given

[Volume II; printed p. 256; ONB canvas 264]

through which God grants or gives it. What should be said of one elected only
by the people but not ordained, and who because of insuperable impediments
cannot be ordained according to Christ’s rules, is irrelevant. For in such a
case he can truly be a Pastor and truly sent by Christ. But if anyone,
despising ordination which Christ instituted and which at least customarily
and ordinarily is necessary by necessity of command and as a necessary means
to order and well-being, as some say, assumes this office for himself from the
calling of the people alone, I cannot regard him as lawfully called. Yet even
if such an election sufficed, it would not follow, as Velthuysen imagines, that
the people had the keys of the Kingdom of heaven and gave them to that Pastor.
For, as was said, all ministerial power is from Christ and is given immediately
by him. I say “immediately” to exclude all intermediate efficient causes, not
to exclude the instruments or means through which the power is applied to
this person. The things which follow on page 338 are partly beside the point
and partly refuted elsewhere.

But on page 339 he uses this argument: “It is no less irrational to say that
God imposed this burden upon Christians, that they learn from Ministers whose
sermons God renders efficacious and whose manner of teaching they hold in
reverence, even if he teaches nothing but things of lesser importance and the
hearer can teach the Preacher himself, than to say that a pupil is bound to go
to school daily to read and write, although he can surpass the teacher
himself.” Thus far we have seen cartloads of insults hurled against God’s
ordinance and institution, but no arguments. And here, so that the last things
may answer the first, we hear nothing but mockery. There was recently in
England a kind of people who, rejecting all God’s institutions, affirmed that
they were above them. Shall we regard Velthuysen as such? Has he advanced so
far in piety that he can rightly be regarded as surpassing ordinary Christians
by many degrees and parasangs?

[Volume II; printed p. 257; ONB canvas 265]

Would that he could be numbered among Christians of the lowest bench! For he
seems to me a most bitter hater of all true piety. Christ commands me to judge
the tree by its fruits. For hear, Reader, how contemptuously he speaks here of
Christ’s institution. I do not approve if Pastors treat trifling matters in
their sermons; but if they treat necessary things, although common;
fundamentals, although easy; the word of reconciliation, although known—if
they treat them seriously, diligently, gravely, and spiritually—no one,
although far more learned than the Pastor, ought to despise them. For one can
know these and many more things literally, notionally, and even scholastically,
yet be wholly ignorant of what it is to hold them savingly. And through the
preaching of the word, as we will immediately make clear, God ordinarily
brings his own to salvation. Therefore that learned man must submit himself to
God’s institution, though untaught; that scholastic, though unknowing; and one
skilled in many things, though ignorantly. As for the faithful, they have been
taught to hold all Christ’s institutions in their proper place, and know well
enough how seriously to distinguish between the institution and its
administrator. They have also found by experience that the same truth which
they knew before, when uttered in a sermon with God efficaciously concurring,
has sometimes penetrated their hearts incredibly and affected them in an
ineffable manner, so that it seemed to them almost a new truth. To institute
such comparisons as we see here is a most certain indication of a profane
disposition.

But he continues to institute his comparisons and further says there: “A
reason must be given why we are more bound to hear the sermons of Pastors than
the lectures of Professors of Theology.” And who, besides one who has deprived
himself of his eyes, does not see the reason? Professors of Theology, as such,
are not Ecclesiastical Ministers instituted by Christ; as such they are not
set over the Church, do not have the word of reconciliation entrusted to them,
and are not Christ’s ambassadors. Nor in their lec-

[Volume II; printed p. 258; ONB canvas 266 (upper portion)]

tures do they proximately and immediately intend the conversion of their
hearers. Rather, they are instituted by human beings to instruct the young in
Theology and for that reason are set over schools, so that they may prepare
and arrange the material and render it fit, as it were, to receive the form of
the ministry. Enough now concerning this Assertion. Let us proceed to another,
so that another occasion may be given to examine Velthuysen’s paradoxes.
