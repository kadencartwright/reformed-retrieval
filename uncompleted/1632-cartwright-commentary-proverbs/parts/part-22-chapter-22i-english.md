Verse 17. Incline your ear and hear the words of the wise; and apply your heart, that you may know me.

Verse 18. For it will be pleasant when you preserve them in your belly; together they shall be arranged upon your lips.

Verse 19. So that your confidence may be in the Lord, I have made them known to you today; you also shall have done it.

Verse 20. Have I not written for you all the chief things set in counsels and knowledge?

[1632 witness | PDF p. 503 | printed columns 990-991]

Verse 21. To make known to you the rectitude of the discourse of truth, that you may report words of truth to those who send to you.

The purpose is, by setting before the minds of the godly the benefit that will come back to them from it, to arouse and awaken them to serious and continual attention to the things that are to be said.

Just as in war, when it has come to hand-to-hand combat, even the most courageous soldiers need the drum and trumpet, by which their flagging spirits may become more eager for battle, so in this spiritual warfare of attentively hearing the word, where we must join battle with Satan and the corruptions of our flesh, it is exceedingly necessary to arouse from time to time, by an exhortation, spirits that have grown slack and relaxed. For while sleep readily creeps upon every somewhat more attentive thought, it does so in none more than in the study and meditation of the divine word—such is the depravity of our nature—where a certain lethargy is accustomed not so much to insinuate itself secretly as to thrust itself forward importunately. Therefore, just as in the first nine whole chapters he prepared the hearer's mind for attention, so here, taking a new beginning, he comes down to this exhortation. For here he begins a new commencement of discourse, because he starts a manner of teaching somewhat fuller than the preceding one. For whereas the preceding lessons are each contained in almost a single sentence, these for the most part extend over several verses and periods.

In the exhortation he first requires the ear, and an attentive ear at that, as is indicated by the word *inclining*. For one who listens attentively is accustomed to bring his ear nearer to the speaker lest perhaps anything said should escape him. This is said, therefore, so that a distinction may be established between those who are heavy and uncircumcised in their ears and those who listen promptly and eagerly, Mark 4.23, 24.

He exhorts to this attention of the ear from the subject of hearing, namely, the words of the wise, because anyone who refuses to listen must plainly be foolish and unwise. For if it was counted to the praise of the Queen of Sheba that she undertook so long a journey merely to hear Solomon's civil wisdom, how greatly will it be accounted to our reproach if we refuse to hear his sacrosanct wisdom when it is offered to our very mouth!

But it is asked why things that appear to belong to Solomon alone are called *the words of the wise*. The answer is that Agur and Bathsheba added certain proverbs of their own at the end of this book. Next, it will not be inappropriate to call the Proverbs of Solomon alone the words of the wise, because all wise people will employ in all their discourse about sacred matters, if not the words of these Proverbs, certainly their sense.

The other thing he urgently demands is that we summon the mind and the internal ear to perceive wisdom, without which all external hearing will be empty, indeed harmful. And by calling the mind forth to the study of wisdom, he requires the will and the affections at the same time.

He exhorts to that, however, from the adjunct of pleasantness. For although, as soon as it is heard, the discourse may not affect us with pleasure,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 504 | printed columns 992-993]

nevertheless, when preserved in the mind, considered again, meditated upon, and ruminated, it will delight. If people are heard attentively, they readily rest content in this attention of the ears; or if they advance further, they can obtain no experience beyond an understanding of the things that have been said. But since we have to do with God, the knower of hearts and reins, or the searcher of the most hidden thoughts, we must not rest content unless all the faculties of the whole soul are consecrated to his word.

To persuade us to this application of the heart which he requires, he draws another argument from the subject, since these Proverbs contain the knowledge and acquaintance of God himself. What greater thing can be spoken, or what more excellent thing imagined? For it must be the most desirable of all things that are, have been, or will be to know the highest good of all, by knowledge of which alone you are truly to be called blessed and in ignorance of which—however accurately you understand all other things—you will be unhappy and wretched. Therefore, although the fact that God's discourses proceeded from wise people can wondrously warm our love for them, nevertheless this fact ought to inflame that same love: that they set the knowledge of God before our eyes.

Verse 18. Thus far we have received the two members of the exhortation, together with the reasons separately fitted to each; now two others follow which are applied jointly to the whole exhortation. The former of these is sought from the adjunct of pleasantness in verses 18 and 19, which is declared by its subjects, the belly and the lips. For since people so greatly desire and pursue what is pleasant, there is a singular spur to investigating the knowledge of God, because it suffuses the person who attains it with pleasure. See Prov. 2.10 and 3.17. But how pleasant it is appears abundantly from verse 18, where it is likened to the tree of life. At Ps. 19.10 it is compared to honey and the honeycomb; it is drink to the thirsty, food to the hungry, and cool water to a deer burning with heat. Therefore, let it be a cause of shame for those who seek pleasantness so greatly in other things to neglect drowsily the knowledge of God, which is the most pleasant of all things. For while all other things immediately vanish like smoke, this alone brings solid and eternal pleasure and is the surest mark of the children of God.

The first subject is the belly, by which synecdoche are signified the innermost chambers of the heart which are contained in the belly, and thus the human mind itself. There is therefore the greatest difference between the body's pleasant things and this pleasant thing of the mind, between the pleasures of the ungodly and of the godly. The delights of the former are perceived only by taste and the mouth, but those of the latter are thoroughly digested and assimilated by the mind. The former only sample sweetness, as it were, with the edges of their lips; but it is proper to the latter to be suffused with sweetness in the innermost recesses of the mind. Contrary to this teaching appears what is written at Rev. 10.10, where the little book given to the Prophet to eat is declared to have been sweet in his mouth but bitter in his belly and mind. But that difficulty is readily removed, because this is said not of every Christian, but only of ministers of the word, for whom it is disagreeable to keep shut up within themselves what must be proclaimed, but agreeable and pleasant, according to the nature of their calling, to spread it among the people.

The other subject of pleasantness is the lip, or tongue. For it is pleasant to the godly to begin discourse about this heavenly wisdom. Thus David says at Ps. 119.103, “How sweet is your discourse to my palate! It is sweeter than honey to my mouth.” What is said about ministers at Rev. 10.10 also sometimes has a place in all the godly. For there are times when the discourse of God enclosed and restrained in the mind ought to be bitter to any Christian, indeed like burning coals; just as, conversely, it is pleasant in the mouth if brought forth at the proper time.

Verse 19. He draws another argument by which the study

[1632 witness | PDF p. 505 | printed columns 994-995]

we direct toward this heavenly wisdom may be kindled, from its end: first, that we may place confidence in God; second, that we may attest our confidence by good works. For there are many Proverbs here upon which our faith rests, and still more which look to integrity of life. Therefore, if anyone wishes to abound in faith and good works, he must be devoted to the study of this heavenly wisdom. For from it, as from seed, they spring; and by it, as by food, they are nourished and confirmed. And for that reason one who has little experience in it must be judged to possess little faith and no great abundance of good works.

Moreover, since Solomon joins all these things together in such a way that each preceding one either produces the one that follows or at least increases and confirms it, it is evident that the person who does not rejoice in the conversation commanded in verse 18 and in discourse about this wisdom abounds neither in faith nor in works. From this it follows that the discourse of those who proclaim themselves doers of the law is empty, although they never hold discourse about the word of God.

Verse 20. He brings another argument by which he may impel us to love this wisdom, from the excellence attached to these Proverbs. He sets forth this argument ἐρωτηματικῶς (*interrogatively*), declaring by that very fact that their excellence is wholly beyond the hazard of controversy, and therefore that one who does not understand it must be very foolish, or one who denies it shamelessly impudent. Their worth and excellence, moreover, are illustrated by frequent comparisons in the Scriptures. At Ps. 19.11 it is compared to much purified gold; at Prov. 3.14, 15, to silver, gold, pearls, jewels, and finally all the most desirable things, all of which are placed below this Wisdom.

It is a familiar saying that the person who has mingled the useful with the sweet has carried every point. For although things which afford delight are sought even if they are not of great worth, those which have excellence joined with delight are most rightfully to be sought. Hence it is evident how intolerable is the distaste for this heavenly treasure from which most people nevertheless suffer.

He amplifies this singular excellence from its end: that they conduce to counsel and knowledge, or to communicating the wisest counsels to others. For just as in the preceding verses he set forth the fruit which pertains to ourselves, so in this and the following verse he set forth that which is accustomed to come back to others. He indicated this use in chapter 1, verse 2.

Verse 21. He commends these counsels further in verse 21 when he presents them as certain and by no means ambiguous, as were the oracles of Apollo λοξίου (*Loxias*); and then as so full of truth that they contain nothing false. These two things—certainty, I say, and truth perfect in all its parts—render counsels supremely salutary. And David abundantly confirms at Ps. 119 that knowledge of the divine word furnishes this capacity for giving counsel.
