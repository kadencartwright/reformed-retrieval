On the Presiding Bishop

While all things were conducted by the common counsel of the presbyters in
that golden age of the apostles, nevertheless, for the sake of order, someone
always presided. He had no power over his colleagues, with this one exception:
in the assembly he was first as moderator of its proceedings, to propose
matters, collect votes, and perform the other things that necessarily belong
to the president of any body whatsoever. After the age of the apostles had
passed, this presidency, which had formerly been temporary, changeable, and
rotating, was changed into προστασίαν ἀδιάδοχον, a non-rotating, or
permanent, presidency. Perpetual presidency had its beginning in the
Alexandrian church, if the things Jerome reported are true. There the
presbyters placed one man elected from among themselves in a higher rank and
called him bishop. In Africa something new is always born—some monstrosity.
Nevertheless, the Alexandrians’ institution did not immediately prevail
everywhere, but only step by step and little by little, as other things
introduced not by law but by custom have done. The ancients did not foresee
the evils that would arise from it, and so were more readily inclined toward
this arrangement. In every aristocratic government the perpetual presidency
of a president is dangerous, and one must always fear that the primacy of
order may gradually be changed into a primacy of power. For every presidency,
even a temporary one, necessarily has some power annexed to it: to summon
meetings outside the regular order; at all meetings, both regular ones and
those summoned outside the regular order, to report on business, solicit
opinions, collect votes, and pronounce the decision; and, in the name and by
the authority of the presbytery, to administer and attend to many other
things. It is therefore not expedient for this presidency to be perpetual and
fixed. The outcome has shown that from perpetual presidency arose

an episcopate of power, archbishops, patriarchs, and Antichrist himself—the
Babylonian pontiff, the ecumenical bishop. Taught by the experience of former
centuries, we can assert with the venerable Beza *that the foul stream drawn
from the gradually corrupted human episcopate can scarcely be dried up entirely
in any other way than by once blocking the spring of that episcopal rank,
since most of those who will be ordained are likely to be scarcely less
ambitious than those ordained in former centuries.* This most distinguished
man also says candidly elsewhere: *Unless those words of Jerome are taken in
this way, it is enough for me to say, without injury to the Fathers, that
little by little, although they were thinking of nothing less, a humanly
devised entrance was thus opened to that episcopal rank. Ambition soon entered
through it and raised that rank first into an oligarchy and finally into that
dreadful ecumenical Antichristian tyranny, which I hardly know whether it will
ever be abolished unless, once the steps by which it ascended there have been
removed, it is brought back into the order of divine institution.* The Fathers
erred in identifying Antichrist because he had not yet been recognized and
revealed; therefore, in their ignorance, they corrupted discipline and
prepared the way for his tyranny. From a small seed a tall tree often arises.
If discipline had not been corrupted as well as doctrine, the church would
never have seen that great Antichrist. Heresies and errors in the doctrine of
faith would never have introduced him unless the Fathers had also erred
shamefully in government. For the impious monarchy of Antichrist is part of
ecclesiastical government—but of corrupt ecclesiastical government—and the
summit of the hierarchy whose foundations they laid. Nor should anyone object
to us the authority of the primitive church. For we recognize no church as
purely primitive except in the age of the apostles. She remained a virgin for
that long; after their departure the mystery of iniquity, which was being
carried on in their own age, began to raise its head higher. The book of the
Apocalypse had been sealed with seven seals. Jerome Lombardellus the
Franciscan said, *The authority of the church of this present time is no less
than that of the primitive church.* This is true if one understands it with
the exception of that first church, which, because of the college of the
apostles, was of greater grace and authority, as Driedo speaks in *On
Various Dogmas*, book 4, chapter 4. If, therefore, we detest Antichrist now
revealed, let us also detest the egg from which he was born. We showed above
that those ancient and first bishops had been only presidents and, in actual
fact, archpresbyters, although they were bishops in name.

On the Presbyter-Bishop
