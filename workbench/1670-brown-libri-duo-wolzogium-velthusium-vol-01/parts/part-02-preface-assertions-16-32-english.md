16. We see that even those who do not display signs of a regenerate condition
are adorned by the Holy Spirit with titles equivalent to the title *member of
the Church*. Such titles are these or similar ones: (1) They are called
*brothers*. For those who

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 36 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000036]

defraud and wrong brothers are also regarded as brothers, 1 Corinthians 6:8,
for brothers' brothers are brothers. (2) They are said to *believe*, as Simon
Magus did, Acts 8. (3) They are *sanctified*, even those who trample the Son of
God underfoot, Hebrews 10:29. (4) They are in some sense *redeemed*, 2 Peter
2:1. (5) They are *regenerated*—by water, namely, in baptism—John 3:5. (6)
They are called *sons*, Galatians 4:6, 9. (7) They are *God's people*, Isaiah
1:3; Jeremiah 7:12. (8) They are said to *bear sons to God*, Ezekiel 16:20.
(9) They are *called*, of whom there are more than the elect. (10) They are
*sons of God*, Romans 9:4. (11) They are called *sons of the covenant*, Acts
3:25; Romans 9:4. (12) They are also *sons of the kingdom*, Matthew 8:12. (13)
They are thus said to be in Christ's field and granary. (14) They are said
simply to be in Christ, John 15:2. (15) They are *disciples*, John 6:66. (16)
They are *of the house of Israel*, Luke 15:6; Romans 9:6. (17) They are
*inside*, and not outside the Church, 1 Corinthians 5. (18) Finally, they are
called *vessels* in *God's house*, 2 Timothy 2:20.

17. Hence the visible Church is commonly defined without such a relation to
saving grace. Thus the Leiden professors say in *Synopsis*, disputation 40,
thesis 32: *The visible Church is the assembly of those who coalesce into one
body through the outward Word and the use of the sacraments and ecclesiastical
discipline.* Trelcatius the younger, on page 201 of his *Institutes*, calls the
visible Church *an assembly of people called out to worship God's glory by an
external calling, or the preaching of the Word, and by participation in the
sacraments.* Apollonius writes thus in *Consideration of the Question*: *The
visible Church is the society of those who profess the true faith in order to
exercise ecclesiastical communion and fellowship among themselves.*

It is certainly no wonder that they define the visible Church in this way,
since not only true believers but also other, unregenerate people are called;
and the Church, according to Ames at the place cited above, is an assembly of
called people. Trelcatius, at the place cited above, says openly that *the form
constituting the visible Church is the external calling, which God effects
mediately.* But not all the called are elect. Therefore some of them will
nev-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 37 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000037]

*er be regenerated. Nevertheless, they are truly called out of the world and,
by responding to this calling, embrace the true religion. Although this
calling is not saving, it is nevertheless proper to the Church and real,
since it is effected by God, and it produces real effects, though not saving
ones.

Moreover, not only the truly godly but also others have a certain relation to
Christ as the head of the visible Church. For they have gifts from him, are
truly baptized, truly discharge ministry, and are objects of ecclesiastical
discipline. They are therefore members of the Church, or citizens and subjects
of Christ.

18. A true member of the visible Church is one who seriously professes the
Christian religion, or the true faith, and subjects himself to Christ's
institutions. I say *seriously*—that is, not in pretense, as an actor, for
show, or with open hypocrisy, although gracious sincerity or conversion of
heart may be absent. Therefore it is not lawful to reject or regard as a
nonmember someone who makes such a profession, even if he is not regenerate.
Nor may anyone be regarded as a nonmember under this sole formal rationale,
consideration, or reduplication: because, or insofar as, he is unregenerate.

The reasons are: 1. God wills that even the unregenerate be received into the
Church so that they may become regenerate, for the visible Church is, as it
were, Christ's workshop. 2. Pastors, insofar as they are pastors in the
visible Church, instrumentally convert and regenerate, Romans 10:14–15;
Galatians 3:2; 1 Corinthians 3:9 and 4:15; Galatians 4:19; John 2:29; 2
Corinthians 11:2–4, 2:6, and 5:20; Acts 26:18. 3. In former times proselytes
were admitted to the Jewish Church upon making such a profession. 4. Such a
profession makes a disciple. 6. In the time of the apostles such a profession
was sufficient, as is evident from Acts 2 and 8.

19. The settled and fixed preaching of the divine Word and its public
reception or profession, together with the sacraments attached to it, is
commonly regarded by the orthodox as a sure mark of the visible Church,
although the Arminians think otherwise in their *Apology*. See also
Episcopius, disputation 8, thesis 10, and the

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 38 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000038]

Socinians. See Socinus himself, *On the Church*; the Racovian Catechism; and
Theophilus Nicolaides, *Defense of the Treatise on the Church*, pages 54–55.
Trelcatius rightly says at the place cited above, pages 209–210:

*Just as knowledge of any particular thing is ordinarily twofold—one kind
designating its essence through its true and immediate causes, and the other
derived from its accidents, and so forth—so the one proper and essential mark
of the visible Church, regarding its essence immediately and properly and
flowing most nearly from its form, is truth: namely, the truth of God's Word
revealed and communicated. The truth of the sacraments is joined to this as an
inseparable connection*, Hebrews 4:12; John 10:27; Matthew 28:10; Romans 4:11.
*For the truth of each is so much a proper and essential mark of the Church
that this truth and the Church are adequately convertible. Accidental marks
are of two kinds. Some are derived from the Church's inseparable properties,
which are also properties of its first mark: the pure preaching of the Word
and the lawful administration of the sacraments*, John 5:39; Acts 17:11;
Matthew 26:26; 1 Corinthians 10:17. *These two are necessary and proper
adjuncts and attributes of the Church.*

So also Walaeus in *Synopsis on the Church*, theses 45–48:

*Hence it is also sufficiently clear*, he says, *that the true and essential
marks of this pure and visible Church are pure preaching and a reception
sealed by the lawful use of the sacraments. For if an impure and false Church
is known from the impurity and falsity of its doctrine, it is necessary that a
pure and true Church be recognized from the purity and truth of its doctrine.
This is also evident from the fact that the communication and acceptance of
the tablets of the covenant are a sure sign of a covenant people. For God
makes a covenant with no one to whom he does not communicate the tablets of
the covenant. Hence the apostle says that the Gentiles, alienated from the
Church of Israel, were without covenants*, Ephesians 2:12; *and, contrariwise,
that the people of Israel were God's Church until Christ's advent. From this
the apostle demonstrates that the covenants belonged to them*, Romans 9:4.
*No one doubts, moreover, that God's Word is those tablets

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 39 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000039]

of God's covenant. Hence it also happens that when God erects his Church among
a people or restores it after it has collapsed, he accomplishes this in no
other way than by the Word and sacraments. Thus we see that through Moses'
ministry he erected the Church among the Israelites when he gave them his Word
and the seals of the Word; and that through his apostles Christ propagated
churches throughout the whole world with this command: Go, and so forth*,
Matthew 28:19–20.

It is true that the Word is a means for gathering the Church, Romans 10:14; 1
Corinthians 1:23 and 3:5; Acts 16:14, and in this respect is not a mark of the
Church. But we are speaking of the Word publicly proclaimed at fixed and
appointed times and publicly received. In this respect the lampstand is fixed;
and wherever this lampstand is fixed, there is the visible Church and the
ministerial Church. By this mark the Church is distinguished from other
societies, Deuteronomy 4:6; Psalm 147:19–20; Deuteronomy 12:29–32; Isaiah
2:2–3 and the last verse of chapter 59.

The Church was also distinguished by this mark under the New Testament, Acts
2:42: namely, because *it continued in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship,
the breaking of bread, and prayers*. Hence the Church is called the pillar of
truth, 1 Timothy 3:15, because by teaching and openly professing the doctrine
of truth, it preserves it.

Moreover, by this mark it is best distinguished from its opposite, namely the
world, for among the Gentiles this proclaimed Word is not found. It is chiefly
for this profession that the Church is harassed and oppressed by its enemies.
When God, as it were, excommunicates the Church and reduces it to no Church, he
takes away the tablets of the covenant—namely, the Word and sacraments,
Revelation 2:5 and 3:16. We know that this has also happened today as regards
those churches of Asia.

20. Therefore those who locate the essential mark of the true Church in the
conduct of life do badly. For although upright conduct is required by God of
all the Church's members in order to attain eternal

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 40 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000040]

life and is necessary in this respect, it is nevertheless not the one mark and
essential characteristic of members of the Church, much less of the Church
itself. I admit that because of corrupt conduct God can *by right* remove the
lampstand, as God himself threatens in Revelation 2 and 3. Thus this corruption
of conduct can render a Church no Church by way of demerit. Nevertheless, as
long as purity of doctrine and sacraments remains, a pure Church remains,
notwithstanding this depravity of conduct, which does not formally and
effectively reduce the Church to nothing.

It is sufficiently well known that many pagans surpass many Christians with
respect to outward actions; nevertheless, the Church is not among them. An
inward and effectual reception of the proclaimed Word, and true faith attested
and declared by outward uprightness of conduct, is a mark of the invisible
Church rather than the visible, and a characteristic of the members of the
former Church, not the latter. It therefore cannot with any reason be regarded
as a mark of the visible Church. For if it were a mark of the visible Church,
some society of Socinians who overturn the foundation might perhaps be a purer
Church than one of Christians who profess sound doctrine. Hence the orthodox
number this uprightness of conduct only among the accidental and separable
marks of the Church. See Trelcatius at the place cited above.

21. As regards ecclesiastical discipline, some wish it to be absolutely
necessary to the Church's *being* and number it among the Church's essential
marks. See Walaeus at the place cited above and Pareus, *Explanation of the
Catechism*, question 59, article 6. Others, such as Junius in *Theological
Disputations* 44, theses 41–42, say that it pertains to the Church's good
order. Others do not number it among the marks, such as the Augsburg
Confession; Calvin, *Institutes* 4.1.9; Beza, *Confession*, article 7; and
similarly Whitaker, *On the Church*, controversy 2, question 5, chapter 17.
Trelcatius, at the place cited above, places it among the accidental and
separable marks. Our stout rhetorician settles the matter by distinguishing

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 41 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000041]

as follows. 1. Between the power of discipline, which has been granted to true
churches, and care to exercise it, which can sometimes be absent while the
Church's essence remains intact. 2. Between the Church's *being* and its
*well-being*. Discipline and its exercise are indeed required for the latter,
as Parker proves very well in book 1 of *On Ecclesiastical Polity*, but not for
the former, as the Anabaptists and Separatists suppose. 3. Between the *right*
and its *exercise*. The former is indeed an essential property of the Church,
while the latter can be absent without the Church's destruction. 4. Between a
complete Church, perfect in every respect, and a Church in which there are
certain defects. The exercise of discipline is necessarily required for the
former, and where it is absent, that Church is imperfect to that extent.

22. Particular churches—as is stated in our British Confession, chapter 25,
theses 4 and 5—are more or less pure according to the degree of purity with
which the doctrine of the gospel is taught and received in them, the divine
ordinances administered, and public worship celebrated, Revelation 2 and 3; 1
Corinthians 5:6–7. The purest churches of all those on earth are subject both
to mixture and to error, 1 Corinthians 13:12; Revelation 2 and 3; Matthew
13:24–30, 47.

23. To the visible catholic Church—which he intended first and primarily—
Christ gave the ministry, or order of ministers, and God's oracles and
ordinances, in order to gather and perfect the saints until the end of the
world, 1 Corinthians 12:28; Ephesians 4:11–13; Matthew 28:19–20. All the
members of this Church are bound to enter into and preserve holy fellowship
and communion, both in divine worship and by performing other spiritual duties
that can contribute to their mutual edification, Hebrews 10:24–25; Acts
2:46–47; Isaiah 2:3; 1 Corinthians 11:20. But because not all

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 42 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000042]

the members of this Church can assemble together to worship God, smaller or
larger particular churches have been instituted as convenience and usefulness
could permit. Therefore, whoever have communion with one another in the
celebration of God's worship in those particular churches also have, and
profess, communion in some manner with the whole catholic visible Church. For,
as we said above, there is only one Church of Christ, just as there is only one
King and one head of the Church.

All particular churches are members of this catholic Church; and all members
of particular churches are also members of this Church. Consequently, although
the members of a particular assembly have a nearer communion with one another
in their distinct, particular assemblies, they nevertheless have a potential
and more remote communion with all the members of the visible Church. In the
same way, all those invited to a banquet must be said to have table fellowship
with one another and are regarded and called fellow guests, although they
cannot all recline at the same feast, but more conveniently sit at separate
tables laid for them and perhaps in different, widely separated places.

Hence, when pastors are ordained, they enter into a relation not only to the
particular churches over which they are appointed. First in the order of
nature, they also enter into a relation to the catholic Church; and in *first
act* they become pastors of the catholic visible Church. Hence also, when
members are solemnly received through baptism, they are admitted not only as
members of that particular church but also as members of the universal Church,
of which they are members first in the order of nature and primarily.
Otherwise, all who migrate from one particular church to another would have to
be rebaptized. Therefore all are called *brothers*—not only those who, joined
together, form

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 43 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000043]

a particular church, but also those who are members of distinct churches, as
can be seen throughout the *Acts of the Apostles* and the epistles, and thus in
Matthew 18:15–22, 25. Moreover, all participate in one and the same general
calling; all are received into one and the same external covenant, Acts 2:39.
One gospel is proclaimed to all without any distinction, Matthew 20:14; Mark
14:9; Romans 10:18; Colossians 1:6; Titus 2:11. Hence also the members of one
particular church can commune, or partake of the Lord's Supper, in another
congregation. Finally, those who are rightfully excommunicated in one church
must be regarded as excommunicated in all the others.

24. This Christian communion flourishes in particular churches and is
manifested and exercised in the settled, fixed, and ordinary hearing of the
divine Word, as it is in the other public exercises of the Christian religion.
A distinction must be made here between regular and settled hearers and those
who are passing through, as it were, and hear while present incidentally. I am
speaking here of the former, not the latter. I say that in this act and
exercise the former cultivate ecclesiastical communion with one another.

The reasons are: 1. This is public and solemn worship of God. Therefore those
who participate in it regularly and as settled hearers, even if not
constantly, have communion with one another. 2. This is a public and
ecclesiastical action instituted for the Church's edification, 1 Corinthians
14:4–5, 12, 24, 29. Therefore there is ecclesiastical communion in its
celebration. 3. The same thing that is sealed in the sacred Supper is publicly
offered in the proclamation of the Word. Therefore, just as those who partake
together of the Supper have ecclesiastical communion with one another, so
those who regularly hear this Word together cultivate communion with one
another. 4. Who would say that there is no greater communion here than exists
between Christians and pagans? 5. If a Christian went to the temples of idols
and worshiped an idol at appointed times together with idola-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 44 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000044]

*ters, would he have communion with those idolaters? Why then do Christians
not also have communion with one another when they worship the true God
together at appointed times? 6. If this is no communion, there would be no
ecclesiastical communion of members at all except in celebrating the Supper.
7. Consequently, the members of the Old Testament Church would have had no
ecclesiastical communion with one another except in celebrating the Passover;
and not even then, because each family ate the Passover separately at home,
and not all together in the temple.

25. The sins of others who publicly worship God with me, whether in hearing
the Word or celebrating the Supper, neither release me from my duty nor deprive
me of my privilege. As was said, it is the duty of all the Church's members to
worship God, for to this end he instituted the Church. See Bucanus, *On the
Church*. It is also a privilege to be admitted to hear the Word and celebrate
the sacraments, as everyone concedes. For God grants these things not to all
nations, but to his Church, which he pursues with singular love. Therefore, if
someone does not worship God rightly in the celebration of the Word or
sacraments, just as this is not imputed to me—unless by consent,
participation, or approval I become a partaker in his offense—so there is no
reason for me not to celebrate God's worship or to be deprived of my
privileges.

26. Although profanity may be said, *by way of demerit* and *by right*, to
excommunicate the profane person, or deprive him of certain ecclesiastical
privileges, it nevertheless does not *actually, properly, formally,* and
*effectively* exclude him from the Church's communion. It follows that, in the
human forum and as regards us, a person, however profane, must not be regarded
as excommunicated before the Church has rendered a sentence against him. For
he remains a member of the Church in fact, because no one

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 45 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000045]

is bound to punish himself, nor should an ecclesiastical penalty be inflicted
by any but superiors.

27. Even someone excommunicated by the greater sentence of excommunication—
not the greatest, which is commonly called *anathema maranatha*—remains to
some extent a member of the Church, for he is not completely cut off. This is
not the place to indicate what should be said about one excommunicated by the
greatest anathema, for the Church rarely inflicts that sentence, and many think
that it should be rendered only against those guilty of the sin against the
Holy Spirit. Nor are we speaking here about those excommunicated for total
apostasy, but only about those who are to be regarded as pagans and tax
collectors because of scandals or contumacy. I say that these are not totally
cut off by excommunication, for the following reasons:

1. There would otherwise be no greater degree of excommunication. 2. Second
Thessalonians 3:14–15 says, *If anyone does not heed our word through this
letter, mark him and have no dealings with him, so that he may be ashamed. Yet
do not regard him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.* That the apostle
speaks here about excommunication and an excommunicated person is attested by
the commentators Calvin, Beza, Bullinger, Marloratus, and others at this
passage. 3. He is not to be regarded as a pagan, Matthew 18, but *as a pagan*—
ὥσπερ ὁ ἐθνικός. A similitude as such is not identity. Therefore an
excommunicated person is distinguished from a pagan. 4. Excommunication is
ecclesiastical medicine applied so that the sinner's spirit may be saved, 1
Corinthians 5:5; that he may learn not to blaspheme, 1 Timothy 1:12; and that
he may be won, Matthew 18:15. 5. He must not be excluded from hearing the Word.
6. His habitual communion with the Church remains, and therefore he is not
rebaptized when readmitted. 7. His actual communion with the Church also
remains in some respects, for he must be admonished as a brother. 8. In former
times one excommunicated

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 46 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000046]

by *niddui*, to which the sentence about which we are speaking corresponds,
was permitted to enter the temple and, according to some, the synagogue, in
order to hear. They also prove this from the fact that one excommunicated by
*cherem* could neither teach nor be taught.

28. Separation, or schism, according to Ames's definition in *Cases of
Conscience* 4.12, *is the severing, disjunction, or dissolution of that union
which ought to be observed among Christians.* It is either *in the Church* or
*from the Church*; that is, either *particular* or *total*. The former occurs
when someone separates himself from some practice or doctrine received in
that church. The latter occurs when someone separates himself from all
communion with that church.

The latter is in turn either *from one church* or *from several or all
particular churches*, and it is done either *formally* or *by implication*.
Someone can withdraw absolutely and renounce all communion with one or several
particular churches, yet cultivate communion with certain other churches,
when the cause of separation is not common to the other churches and to those
from which he now separates himself. But when someone separates himself from
any church for a cause common to all, then, although he does not actually and
formally separate himself from all churches, he nevertheless does so virtually
and by implication.

To apply these points, we must say: 1. In a true Church, it is always lawful to
separate from every communion that is unlawful and in which we cannot avoid
sinning—for example, when worship is corrupted and becomes idolatrous, or
when some false doctrine is proposed. To that extent it is not lawful to
cultivate communion with such a Church. Certain arguments of the Separatists,
drawn from Colossians 2:20–23; 2 John verse 10–11; Hosea 4:16; Amos 5:5;
Ephesians 5:11; 1 Corinthians 5:6; Galatians 5:9; 2 Corinthians 6:17; Psalm
16:4; and Jude verse 23, prove this, but nothing else. 2. Separation is lawful
from a Church corrupted in fundamentals and where it is not lawful to

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 47 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000047]

participate in worship without sin, especially if believers are compelled by
penalties to participate in such worship. We also concede this to the
Separatists, and their arguments drawn from Revelation 18:4 and similar
passages prove nothing more. 3. It is not lawful to separate from any true
church because of certain defects of less importance, whether in worship or
conduct, since then one would have to separate from every particular church.
For the purest Church is not perfect, but every Church is subject to some error
and defect. The Separatists' arguments will never establish the contrary. If
they establish anything, they will confirm the Anabaptist opinion and prove
that one must not remain in a Church in which there are even hypocrites.

29. One separation is called *negative*, another *positive*. The former occurs
when someone does not actually participate or join others in some part of
divine worship, but quietly withdraws, abstains from communion, or does not
embrace some received dogma. The latter occurs when a new and distinct Church
is erected. The former can be lawful in many cases in which the latter is
utterly unlawful. The former can also be tolerated and excused to some extent,
even when it is irregular. The latter, however, is not merely rash but
unlawful, unless the causes are most serious, because it supposes that all
communion with that Church is utterly unlawful.

30. Corruption of the Church is either public or private. It is public when it
is present in public worship, public proceedings, and public doctrine; that
is, when some corruption is not only publicly tolerated but also commonly
received, approved, and even defended. It is either among the pastors or among
the flock. It can be in the flock when conduct is corrupted in part. It can be
among the pastors when they acquire office by indirect and not entirely lawful
means,

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 48 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000048]

such as simony or some other discreditable thing, or when they do not discharge
their office with sufficient faithfulness. Finally, these corruptions among
pastors either destroy the essence of the ministry, or they are merely
personal and do not destroy the ministry or office itself. Finally, these
corruptions are either more or fewer.

Now, even if we were to grant that it is lawful to separate from a true Church
when the corruptions are public—which, as was said, we cannot grant in every
case—it nevertheless does not follow that separation is necessary when the
corruptions are only private and peculiar to the flock, or are also common to
the ministers but do not destroy the essence of the ministry itself.

31. A distinction must be made between a Church which is guilty of defection,
goes backward, and retreats from the degree of reformation to which it attained
by God's grace; and one which, although it does not progress or advance in the
reformation it began, nevertheless does not return to its vomit, contemplate
defection, approve what it previously condemned, or condemn what it approved.

Now, in the former case, all may indeed be obliged to defend the reformation
and the degree of reformation they attained; to patronize no corruption, even
the least, once rejected or abjured; not to approve it by practice and
participation; but to abhor and separate themselves from everything of that
kind. In this case, however, they are not Separatists. Rather, those others who
have defected from received, approved, confirmed, and sanctioned truth are the
Separatists, because they separate themselves from the reformed Church and
contemplate a return to Egypt, while the believers merely defend the
reformation and do not associate themselves with those dogs lapping up their
vomit again.

In the latter case, however, there does not appear to be the same cause for
separation, or even for nonunion, unless, as was said, that Church is so cor-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 49 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000049]

*rupt that it is not lawful to remain in its communion without participating
in sins. A further distinction must be made between separation from the
Church, divine worship, the ministry, and divine ordinances, and separation
only from scandalous, wicked, and faithless ministers of that kind. Whenever
separation is made from the tents and administrations of such Korahs, it is
not therefore always a separation from the Church itself or from the
congregation of Israel.

32. A distinction must also be made between a corrupt Church in which private
persons are permitted to preserve a good conscience by bearing witness and
protesting, or even by presenting a petition, to remove those enormities and
corruptions—whether more or less public and whether in the flock or among the
pastors—and a Church in which private persons, whether conjointly or
separately, are not permitted to complain about evils or relieve their
consciences by public testimony or protest.

Whatever must be said about separation in this latter case—especially when
the evils are serious and public, publicly received and approved, and
conformity is urged under severe penalties—in the former case at least
positive and total separation does not appear so lawful, unless, as was said,
the corruption is so great that no one may remain any longer in that Church's
communion.

I need not indicate how these general theses can be applied to the present
state of affairs. What conclusions condemning the separation already begun can
be inferred from them is obvious to everyone. Nor does the narrowness of the
page now permit me to add here those corollaries which I appended in the
writing named above.

But I now see that, if Schluter's book—which I was given the opportunity to
see after I had delivered these things to the compositor—is approved by them
all, I have labored in vain. For it is now evident that they embrace the
Anabaptist position and lay the foundations of antipaedobaptism, whereas I had
previously suspected that they favored only Independency. For the present,
however, make use of these things, and farewell.

A. D.
