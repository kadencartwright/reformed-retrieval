CHAPTER II

Whether the Arminians candidly acknowledge the eternity and immutability of
the divine decrees.

I first offer a few preliminary points about divine freedom. First note: free
acts in God are not merely extrinsic denominations—as some dream—as though
God's willing meant nothing other than that an outward change occurs in the
thing willed by God. For since God is a living and intelligent spirit, there
are vital acts in God, and

[1651 logical printed p. 19 | IA leaf 37 | PDF p. 37]

Scripture attributes to God that he understands, wills, and loves. Thus, second
note: understanding, willing, and loving in respect of the entitative
perfection they include are necessarily present in God. God could not be God
if he were destitute of them. But in respect of their termination upon a
created object, they are necessary only on the hypothesis, for he could have
refrained from creating the world.

Third note: freedom does not essentially imply dependence of appetite upon an
object. The object is therefore in itself that which terminates, not that which
perfects, appetite; hence God's freedom neither depends upon its object nor is
perfected by it.

Fourth, God's freedom is not in itself undetermined, but by the eminence of its
act is determined from eternity, as Scotus rightly says. Hence the privation of
second act found in created freedom does not fall within God's freedom; and
for the same reason, God's freedom is not in either active or passive potency.
Therefore, as Alvarez rightly teaches, that indifference which is opposed to
free determination and implies a certain potentiality and suspension of second
act does not belong to the essence of freedom, for it is not found in God's
freedom. Accordingly, when we say that God can refuse or will, the word “can”
does not imply an actualizable potency in God, but only an indifference of the
act as to whether it is terminated upon this object or is not terminated upon
it—which is remarkable. Thus Francis Suarez rightly observes.

Fifth, a free act in God is a simple act, and a simple act is the simple
essence. But God's essence cannot be, or have been, otherwise

[Marginalia: Scotus, book 2, distinction 27, question 1.]

[Marginalia: Alvarez, *On the Aids of Divine Grace*, book 3, disputation 22,
number 35.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, *Metaphysics*, volume 2, disputation 30, section 42.]

[1651 logical printed p. 20 | IA leaf 38 | PDF p. 38]

than it is without mutability of the divine nature. In reality, the act of
willing which is terminated upon various things without any change is
altogether the same act; and that same act, insofar as it is terminated upon
this object, is both volition and hatred. Nor are there in God, as there are in
us, any genuinely contrary acts that add something real. Consequently, the
acts of God's decrees would have been the same acts they now are, and God's
essence would have stood in the same way in which it now stands, if those who
are now reprobate had been elect and those who are now elect had been
reprobate. For in creatures freedom regards indifferent acts, but in God it
regards only one or another object outside himself. From this the Arminians can
see how God's immutability is consistent with absolute freedom.

2. We leave the dispute even to the more careful Jesuits and Scholastics. Do
the Arminians teach things worthy of the three-times-dreadful and immutable
nature of the divine majesty, when, to curry favor with the heretic Vorstius
and their own genius, they maintain that there are many free acts in God which
would never have been in him unless certain human and angelic volitions had
preceded? But to the matter itself: I reduce the present inquiry to two
sources, the Arminian doctrines concerning predestination and justification.
The reader will pardon one writing exercises if, “passing over into another
kind,” I attempt incidentally to vindicate our doctrine of justification.

3. First, that forensic distinction of election into a peremptory, complete,
and irrevocable election and a non-peremptory, incomplete, and

[1651 logical printed p. 21 | IA leaf 39 | PDF p. 39]

revocable election quite openly establishes that God's volitions are mutable and
temporal and arise together with every step of our slippery and inconstant
choice. For as often as a person believes, there is a decree of election in
God; as often as, in the same hour, he distrusts the God who promises, he makes
himself the formal object of reprobation and a new decree of reprobation arises
in God. Again the same person believes, later does not believe, sins gravely,
then repents again and believes. See! Like the Euripus, God's decrees must be
made and altered daily, more changeable than the revolutions of human choice
or the little wheels of a cart.

And who does not know that in the same prayer to God the church in Psalm 77,
and the saints—David in Psalm 6 and Job in chapter 2—both celebrate sacred
triumphs and also remonstrate with God out of the distrust of prevailing
flesh, even charging God with forgetfulness, inconstancy, and a changed
purpose, not far from sprinkling him with blasphemies? Is the church, even in
the same hour and amid its prayer, enrolled in the choir of the elect, and then
again counted with the reprobate and the angels of darkness in God's utterly
simple will?

What do the Arminians say here? “We acknowledge,” they say, “a peremptory
election and reprobation, from the foreknowledge of the final perseverance in
faith or unbelief of everyone living in the church.” Reply: to what purpose,
then, is that much-vaunted distinction which the Arminians loudly claim to
find in Scripture? Why would God adjudge to life someone whom he foreknows,
according to his final unbelief, will die in sin against the Holy Spirit with
horrendous obstinacy? Would not

[1651 logical printed p. 22 | IA leaf 40 | PDF p. 40]

that obstinacy, objectively as present to the God who knows everything as a
particular act of faith, have had to prevent God from destining to the reward
and recompense of eternal glory one whom he foreknows will be worthy of the
recompense of eternal torments and will unworthily reject the prize of glory?

Second, I earnestly desire the words of the Remonstrants in their *Apology
against the Censure*, chapter 17, p. 187, to be considered. “For who,” they say,
“threatens punishment to someone whom by a peremptory decree he wills to be
immune from punishment and upon whom he wills to bestow a grace which, once
posited, makes it impossible that he incur punishment?” and so forth. I add the
minor: but the Arminians hold that God threatens punishment to one whom he
foreknows will die in faith and upon whom he wills to bestow Arminian
efficacious grace, which, once posited, makes it impossible that he incur
punishment. Thus Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 665: for he calls them elect, he
says, “by the decree of predestination distinguished from the decree of
providence, with that grace posited by which he certainly knows they will
believe to the end and escape eternal punishment”; and yet he threatens
eternal punishment. But in the cited place the Remonstrants say this is
unbecoming to divine wisdom and indeed altogether foreign to every right
reason.

But because the Arminians believe that no one has been peremptorily destined
to glory from eternity, they therefore wish threats to be made to everyone,
since it is possible that everyone may incur eternal punishment. For either
peremptory election is not eternal but temporal and mutable according to the
acts of human choice, or God, contrary to his wisdom and every right reason,

[1651 logical printed p. 23 | IA leaf 41 | PDF p. 41]

peremptorily threatens punishment to the elect. Yet this is the mystery: no
one is peremptorily elect from eternity, but is at last peremptorily elected in
time when he dies in faith. Hence there begins in God a new temporal and
peremptory election, which is a genuine change, and even new knowledge, from
which God's eternal foreknowledge will perish.

4. But another refuge is open here to the Arminians, a thoroughly lukewarm
one. “God,” one says, “elects believers for the time and reprobates
unbelievers; and God absolutely and immutably decreed from eternity to elect
believing persons and reprobate unbelieving persons, from foreknowledge of
faith or unbelief. God's decrees are therefore still eternal and immutable.”

But I reply, first: divine changes do not cease to be changes because they
were immutably decreed. By the same reasoning, if someone immutably and by a
most constant decree resolved within himself to change his faith with places,
persons, king, court, fortunes of war, and adverse or favorable winds of human
affairs—and in accordance with that unmoved decree always conformed himself to
every religion, evangelical, papist, and Mohammedan—would he therefore be
called immutable? I ask, will God be immutable because from eternity he
immutably resolved to change his decrees hour by hour along with the new
goddess Contingency and human freedom? Away with theology that should be
banished to hell! In like manner, God decreed from eternity that man should be
mutable; will man be immutable because of such a decree?

[1651 logical printed p. 24 | IA leaf 42 | PDF p. 42]

Second, a decree is an enactment of good pleasure, an edict issued after
wisdom has been consulted. But what wisdom and what determination is it to
decree and establish today what must be rescinded tomorrow? Is this fitting
even for a wise man?

5. Second, the Arminians’ words prove the same thing. For the Remonstrants say
in their *Apology*: “We conclude, therefore, that it is false—although most
people have hitherto held it—that election is anywhere in Scripture said to
have been made from eternity, or that election—except when it is taken for a
decree—is presented in Scripture as an internal or immanent rationale in God
which existed in him from eternity. On the contrary, it is most true that
election is presented in Scripture as an external and transient action of God
which began in time, contrary to what has been almost universally believed
hitherto.”

[Marginalia: *Apology against the Censure*, chapter 19, pp. 190–191.]

But first, who does not see this hourly faith of Jacob Arminius, who defines
reprobation as “the decree by which from eternity he determined to condemn
unbelievers,” and so forth, and elsewhere calls predestination election in
thesis 3? He concludes in the same way in thesis 6: “We ascribe eternity to
this decree because God does nothing in time which he did not decree from
eternity to do.” And thesis 10: “This decree is according to election, as the
Apostle says.”

[Marginalia: Arminius, public disputation 15, disputation thesis 11.]

Therefore Arminius himself truly judges that eternal predestination is made
according to eternal election. For if election is temporal, it cannot be the
rule according to which eternal predestination is made, as Arminius says:
“Election is God's decree by which he determined within himself from
eternity,” and so forth. Second, we do not deny that election sometimes
signifies calling in

[Marginalia: *Antiperkins*, p. 658.]

[1651 logical printed p. 25 | IA leaf 43 | PDF p. 43]

time, 1 Corinthians 1:26–27. But this is not the destination of human beings
to eternal life spoken of in Romans 9 and Ephesians 1, which in Scripture is
from eternity. Ephesians 1: “Before the children had done anything good or
evil.” Nor do the Arminians take election for calling. For the election they
defend in the cited place of the *Apology* is the election of those who truly
believe, as is stated in their *Confession*, chapter 18, thesis 2. Indeed,
those “who have now been converted and truly believe are separated from the
profane multitude of those who perish,” and so forth. But calling belongs to
those not yet converted, but to be converted. Indeed, according to those same
doctors in their *Confession*, chapter 17, thesis 2, calling belongs to those
who do not believe; “indeed it is possible,” they say, “that those called may
never actually believe and be saved.” They therefore believe election and
destination to glory to be temporal and to arise from the choice of human
beings who can believe or not believe, and consequently to be mutable.

Third, this temporal election of believers to glory is either the actual
bestowal of glory and thus glorification—but who calls glorification a
destination to glory? Neither Scripture nor an ecclesiastical writer—or it is
God's destination in mind and will, by which he willed to give glory. But this
is an immanent and eternal action, for no divine volition is temporal or
transient; what God wills he has willed from eternity. The Arminians cannot
supply a third alternative. Either it is an act of divine omnipotence, in
which case it must be glorification, which they would not say; or it is an act
of intellect and will, in which case it will be an immanent and eternal act.

[1651 logical printed p. 26 | IA leaf 44 | PDF p. 44]

Here they will be at an impasse.

6. Third, Corvinus maintains in *Do This and You Shall Live* that there was a
legal decree concerning the final end of rational creatures. But since the law
has been abrogated, as they themselves acknowledge, this legal decree must
have ceased to exist in God many years ago, namely, immediately after Adam's
fall. They will say that the decree was not abrogated because, with respect to
human beings, it was not peremptory but conditional. But what then? Is there
now, I ask, a decree in God to save human beings if they fulfill the law? Yet
they themselves teach that there is no salvation through the law. Therefore,
God either rescinded and rejected that decree, or he says this within himself:
“Although I have determined within myself to save no one through works of the
law but only through faith, nevertheless I still decree to save everyone if
they keep the law.” Here there will be in God a foolish and empty legal decree,
and there will be two plainly contradictory decrees in God and consequently
contrary wills—which is blasphemous.

Second, it lay with Adam's will either to fulfill the law, in which case there
would have been no evangelical decree in God, or not to fulfill it, in which
case there was an evangelical decree in God. Thus God's evangelical decree is
caused by a temporal human volition and is consequently not eternal. “But,”
they say, “God, foreknowing Adam's fall from eternity, made the evangelical
decree from eternity.” Yet here let the Arminians see what kind of God they
fashion for themselves, decreeing within himself from eternity as follows:

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 12, p. 187.]

[1651 logical printed p. 27 | IA leaf 45 | PDF p. 45]

“I decree that Adam and his posterity shall be saved if they fulfill the law;
or if they do not, that they shall be saved if they believe; or, if none
believe, that they shall be condemned.” Here God was wavering, suspended, and
variable in establishing decrees, his eternal decrees depending entirely upon
the temporal acts of human beings.

Third, he made the evangelical decree either foreknowing that some would
believe or not foreknowing it. If the former, then the decree concerning the
salvation of certain particular persons will precede the general decree
concerning the salvation of believers, which reverses the Arminian order of
the decrees. If the latter, God established the whole gospel with no
providence, because with no knowledge, and in that decree he did not know
whether he would attain the manifestation of the glory of mercy or of justice.

Fourth, why did God make the legal decree non-peremptory? Certainly either
because he foreknew that Adam would fall—and then why did he make at all what
he foresaw would be abrogated and would be made in vain without attaining its
end? For it is not the part of a wise man to do something when he foreknows its
end will be futile. Or he made it non-peremptory because he did not foreknow
but conjectured that it was possible that man, being a mutable creature, might
sin. In that case there was no need in that conceptual instant of the legal
decree for him to decree anything whose rescission he knows.

7. Fourth, Arminius says: “It is certain that God wills some things which he
would not will unless some human volition preceded.” But all human volitions
are temporal, and elsewhere he maintains that no one is actually reprobate
unless he actually exists. Thus

[Marginalia: *Antiperkins*, p. 750, Bertius edition.]

[1651 logical printed p. 28 | IA leaf 46 | PDF p. 46]

reprobation will be a temporal decree. He also holds that human beings are not
elected except in the crucified Christ; but Christ died in time. Therefore,
human beings were elected in time. The fiction of foreknowledge will not help
them one whit. For they hold election and the destination of a human being to
glory to be an act of justice just as much as the actual bestowal of glory is;
but according to the order of justice the latter is impossible unless the
ransom has been paid. Therefore, I say, the payment of the ransom in
foreknowledge does not satisfy justice in God's election; rather, it must
actually be paid before God can actually choose human beings for eternal life,
just as it must actually be paid before God can actually communicate life.
This consequence rests upon the Arminian doctrines, for according to them,
destining to glory is an act of justice just as giving glory is.

8. Fifth, Corvinus says: “It is absurd, when God wills the creature's
obedience, that he should propose a law according to such foreknowledge by
which he knows the creature will not transgress it. The reason is that by that
very fact he would propose a law which, by his absolute will, he would not
permit the person to whom it is given to transgress; and although the person's
freedom would remain in appearance, in reality it would be determined by that
decree to one kind of act.”

From this I argue: God gave the law either according to that foreknowledge by
which he knew Adam would sin, or according to no foreknowledge. If the former,
God's absolute will precedes freedom and destroys it—for this is Corvinus's
consequence. If according to no foreknowledge, then afterward, when God

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 6, section 6, p. 207.]

[1651 logical printed p. 29 | IA leaf 47 | PDF p. 47]

foreknew, there began in God new knowledge of a thing he had previously not
known, and also a new temporal permissive decree. But let it be noted in
passing that God's foreknowledge of a future free thing, no less than a
determining will, overthrows freedom, and consequently that the Arminians in
fact deny that God has foreknowledge of free acts.

Second, it follows that God intended the glory of justice in rewarding Adam's
obedience when he gave him the law, and yet did not know whether or not he
would attain that end. But what wise man would resolve to ransom captives with
gold from a particular mine while ignorant whether he would find gold there or
not? Is this to pronounce things worthy of God's infinite wisdom?

Third, after the law has been violated, there begins in God a new volition of
proposing the gospel and a new intention of saving human beings, while he does
not know whether they will obey or not. Most do not obey: a new volition to
condemn arises, contrary to the intention to save. Again they obey: a volition
to save arises once more. Indeed, it is possible for someone to believe
throughout his whole life and cease believing at the final moment of his last
breath; from that one act of unbelief there arises in God a peremptory,
irrevocable, and fixed volition to condemn him eternally. And someone who was
utterly wicked throughout his life, neither believing nor obeying the gospel,
believes at his last gasp, and there arises in God a new peremptory volition to
save him eternally. Who does not see here that the unhappy fortune of
contingency draws temporal

[1651 logical printed p. 30 | IA leaf 48 | PDF p. 48]

and mutable volitions in God after it? I say nothing of the signal injury done
to God's justice by rewarding with eternal destruction or ineffable glory one
act of faith or unbelief which perhaps arose even contrary to divine
providence and will.

9. Sixth, Corvinus defends these words of Vorstius: “I say that only a
particular outcome depending upon a contingent secondary cause is sometimes
changed, with respect to which God's decree, then considered separately in
parts, can be said to be changeable to some extent.” And what could be clearer
than what Arminius himself says? “A person can by his unbelief deserve that God
should change the effect by which he offered his Son as Redeemer into wrath by
which he wills to punish him.” Nothing is plainer here than that God's decrees
and effects change according to the change of free effects. But no one, I
think, will be ignorant that the human will changes hour by hour.

[Marginalia: *Duplication to Piscator*, chapter 1, section 6.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 746.]

Nor does it help the Arminians that God's decrees are immutably adjusted to
human conduct and that consequently the will by which God wills believing
Peter to be saved and later wills the same Peter, now unbelieving, to be
condemned is not contrary, because believing Peter and unbelieving Peter are
not the same object. Thus the same immutable will can will both. But I answer
as follows. First, it is not the same will when there is first a willing of
salvation and afterward a new non-willing of salvation. For if an earthly
judge resolves to acquit this man while innocent and later to condemn the same
man while guilty, everyone will say that the judge's will is physically
changed,

[1651 logical printed p. 31 | IA leaf 49 | PDF p. 49]

even though the change arose from the condemned person's good or evil conduct
and not from the judge's inconstancy. For it is equal blasphemy whether we say
that God's decrees are mutable from internal inconstancy or from an external
object. Neither in this way nor in that way can Jehovah, who does not change,
be mutable. Malachi 3. In this way human beings by refusing and willing whirl
God about from unwilling to willing and again from willing to unwilling. Water
which passes from cold to hot and then goes back from hot to cold does not
thereby cease to be mutable because these changes happen to it from external
agents such as air or sun.

Second, Arminius says in express words that God changes a benevolent affection
into wrath. Therefore, after human beings stubbornly reject Christ, the decree
in God to save such persons, or even the antecedent will, ceases and is changed
into a decree to condemn and into hatred. But the Remonstrants, to avoid the
force of this argument, forsake Jacob, their master. For this is what they say
in their *Apology*: “There is not even an appearance of mutability here. For
God does not change the intention to save”—although Vorstius and Arminius teach
that he does change it—“because he had only a hypothetical intention. If the
person himself is unwilling freely to be, or remain, rebellious or ungrateful,
that same hypothetical intention remains; but if the hypothesis does not
exist, God's constant will and his already-disclosed intention to punish the
ungrateful and rebellious person remains.” A plausible but fallacious escape.

[Marginalia: *Apology*, chapter 17, p. 161.]

First, if he does not intend or decree to save except hypothetically,

[1651 logical printed p. 32 | IA leaf 50 | PDF p. 50]

then antecedently God no more wills the salvation of all than the destruction
of all. Will not their common love, which they imagine in God toward all
mortals, be counterfeit? Second, when one act of believing is posited, the
condition is posited on the person's part; God therefore elects believers for
the time. They fall back into unbelief, and the condition is removed; then a
contrary decree to condemn is posited once more. Blind is the person who does
not see here that something is placed in being in God which was not previously
in him, and that something which previously was in him is not posited but
removed—a thing which can happen a thousand times with one person. Let the
reader judge whether this is not a real change.

Third, here they make the most unworthy claims about God. Namely, after Judas
has been thrust down into hell, God's hypothetical intention to save him
remains. Be of good cheer, then, you damned and reprobate angels! The Arminians
announce to you the same gospel that is preached to the living: namely, there
still remains in God toward you a constant and deliberated will to save you,
provided that you believe. And to those now dead in the Lord who have attained
the crown of glory, the same gospel must be preached: namely, eternal
destruction awaits them if they will to be ungrateful and rebellious. But the
Arminians complain against us—most falsely—about God's love of saving those
whom, they loudly claim, it is impossible to save because of the obstacle of a
fateful decree. Yet with better right and good reason we charge them with
burdening God with a will to save, albeit hypothetically,

[1651 logical printed p. 33 | IA leaf 51 | PDF p. 51]

the damned themselves, whom it is absolutely impossible to save.

Fourth, if God does not change his intention to save the damned, God's wrath
and hatred cannot succeed a benevolent affection, as Arminius says. Hence
there is no hatred in God toward rebels unless they acknowledge that there can
coexist in God toward one and the same person the benevolent affection of a
father, mother, and most loving hen—most merciful, very slow to anger—arising
from a true desire to save and an ardent love, together with hatred and a true
and just desire for eternal destruction. They themselves will not admit this.

Fifth, they wish the hypothetical intention to save the damned to remain so
that no new volition begins in God and another ceases. But this is in vain. For
either God hates Peter while he denies the Lord and then loves him for eternal
life when he repents, so that volitions and non-volitions necessarily begin
and cease in God; or God's will remains suspended toward everyone who in this
life cultivates justice and piety and everyone who gives himself to impiety
and wicked practices. That is utterly absurd, for Scripture says the opposite.

10. Seventh, their principal doctrine is that God calls everyone with the mind
and intention that they should obey and be saved. But among those called there
are many who from eternity are hated and rejected by God peremptorily and
irrevocably, from his foreknowledge of their final impenitence. Yet a serious
and natural intention to save is diametrically

[1651 logical printed p. 34 | IA leaf 52 | PDF p. 52]

opposed to a peremptory intention to condemn. They think they have admirably
discharged their obligation by telling us that God by an antecedent will wills
life to those for whom by a consequent will he wills eternal death. But this is
irrelevant. For we say it would be absurd in an earthly magistrate, after he had
passed an irrevocable sentence of death upon a thoroughly wicked traitor, to
invite that same person to life by any will, with the hope of rewards set before
him, and with this plan and intention: that everyone should freely enjoy courtly
honors and every pleasure. Yet such is the Arminian God they fashion for us.

12. Nothing can help the Arminians here unless they flee to that assertion of
Vasquez: although God's free will concerning things to be produced adds nothing
beyond his essence except a conceptual relation, and by this relation the will is
called free to produce creatures—so that if God had not decreed from eternity to
produce creatures, he would lack nothing except a conceptual relation—nevertheless
the will cannot be changed in time, nor can that conceptual relation. But if such
a relation ought to be in God, it ought to be from eternity, as we said concerning
knowledge. This is not because any intrinsic change in God, or even in his
knowledge itself, would follow if the relation could change in time, but because
future things are future from eternity and nothing can begin in time to be future.
It follows that the relation to things to be produced cannot begin in time.

*[Marginalia: Vasquez, on the First Part, disputation 63, question 19, section
39.]*

Hence some Arminians infer: just as the act of creating, which was not from
eternity but begins to be in God in time, posits no change in God, so

[1651 logical printed p. 35 | IA leaf 53 | PDF p. 53]

neither does a new volition beginning in God in time posit any change in God.
But I reply: First, creation and volition do not stand on equal footing. Creation
is a mere extrinsic denomination by which things outside God begin to exist
through God's eternal wisdom, will, and omnipotence. But volition is not a mere
extrinsic denomination; it is also a vital act, which cannot begin anew without
change in God. Creation is God's transient action and therefore cannot fail to be
eternal, since the created being is that upon which God's action terminates. But
volition is an immanent act and therefore cannot fail to be eternal, for it is the
same as God's essence and identified with it. Anyone who thinks God's essence
mutable or non-eternal thinks God not to be God. Thus Suarez too; see his
*Metaphysics*.

*[Marginalia: Suarez, Metaphysics, chapter 1, disputation 20, section 6, number
24.]*

The mode of loving by which God decreed from eternity to establish the world—as
Petrus Fonseca says in his *Metaphysics*—had to be terminated upon the world at
the time when he established it. Therefore, the efficient causality by which he
produced it was eternal in itself, because it was in reality the same as God's
eternal decree and thus identified with the divine essence; but its causality was
temporal terminatively and virtually.

*[Marginalia: Fonseca, Metaphysics, book 7, chapter 8, question 4, article 5,
§.]*

Second, Vasquez thus opposes them: “If that relation to willed things ought to be
in God, it ought to be from eternity, and consequently such a relation cannot
begin in time. But creation, although it flows from a vital act of God, is not
formally a vital act, but a relative action terminated in

[1651 logical printed p. 36 | IA leaf 54 | PDF p. 54]

time upon created things.” Third, future things cannot fail to have been future
from eternity; and if God in time wills something to be future which he did not
will from eternity, there begins in God a new vital action, which is absurd.

*[Marginalia: *On the Divine Essence*, section 2, chapter 10, pages 121–122.]*

point 12. The double-negative statement “cannot fail to be eternal” (`non
potest non esse æterna`) in the description of creation on p. 35 is also
retained exactly as printed.]*
