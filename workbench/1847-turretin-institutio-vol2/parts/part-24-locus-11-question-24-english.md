QUESTION XXIV.

THE CEREMONIAL LAW.

What was the end and use of the ceremonial law under the Old Testament?

[Marginal heading: The Mosaic law is threefold: moral, ceremonial, and
forensic.]

I. The law given through Moses is customarily distinguished into three
kinds: the *moral law*, which treats conduct or perpetual duties toward God
and neighbor; the *ceremonial law*, which treats ceremonies or rites to be
observed in sacred matters under the Old Testament; and the *forensic law*,
which constituted the civil government of the Israelite people. The first is
the foundation upon which the obligation of the others rests. The others are
its appendices and determinations: the ceremonial law with respect to the
first table, by determining its circumstances, especially regarding external
worship; and the forensic law with respect to the second table in judicial
matters, although it also establishes penalties for crimes committed against
the first table.

[Marginal heading: The basis of the distinction.]

II. The truth of this distinction is evident not only from the diversity of
the names by which the laws are designated in Scripture. For the moral law is
usually expressed by מצות, *precepts*; the ceremonial law by חקים,
*statutes*; and the judicial law by משפטים, *judgments*, which the
Septuagint renders by ἐντολὰς, *commands*, δικαιώματα, *ordinances*, and
κρίματα, *judgments*. Thus Deuteronomy 5:31 says, “I will declare to you all
the precepts, statutes, and judgments which you shall teach them”; so also
6:1, 20; 7:11; and Leviticus 26:46. Nevertheless, these words are sometimes
synonymous and used interchangeably (Ezek. 5:6; 20:11, 16, 18).

The distinction is especially evident from the nature of the matter and the
office of law, which is to constitute the order according to which a human
being is joined to God and neighbor. A human being is joined to God, first,
through a certain internal and external likeness in love, justice, holiness,
and truth, whose norm the moral law delivers. He is then joined through the
external signification and attestation of these acts of divine worship, with
tokens and symbols employed, whose character the ceremonial law prescribes.

[Printed p. 129 | Internet Archive PDF 157 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Next, the forensic law, applied more distinctly to the Israelites, explains
what one human being owes another by duty. The moral law regards the Israelite
people insofar as they are *human beings*; the ceremonial law, as the *Church
of the Old Testament* awaiting the promised Messiah; and the forensic law, as
a *peculiar people* who had to possess in the land of Canaan a commonwealth
suited to their character and disposition.

[Marginal heading: The difference of the moral law from the others.]

III. Hence arises a manifold difference between the moral law and the others.
They differ, first, in *origin*, because the moral law is founded upon natural
right and is therefore known by nature, while the others are founded upon
positive right and are therefore from free revelation. They differ in
*duration*: the former is immutable and eternal, the latter changeable and
temporary. They differ with respect to their *object*: the former is
universal and regards everyone, while the latter are particular and regard
only the Jews. The forensic law regarded them as a distinct commonwealth
dedicated to God, while the ceremonial law regarded their ecclesiastical
state and their state of minority and infancy. Finally, they differ with
respect to *use*, because the moral law is the end of the others, while the
others serve the moral law. So much for the moral law; we must now discuss the
ceremonial law.

[Marginal heading: What the ceremonial law is.]

IV. The *ceremonial law* is the system of God’s positive precepts concerning
external worship in sacred matters, prescribed for the ancient Church for
the sake either of order or of signification. It is so called from the
*object* with which it is concerned, namely, the ceremonies or sacred rites
formerly to be observed in the external worship of God.

The word *ceremonies* may be derived from the Etruscan word *cerus*, which
signifies *holy*, as *sanctimony* comes from *holy*, as *Joseph Scaliger* and
*Dionysius Gothofredus* hold in their notes on Festus. Or it may be derived
from Caere, a town of Etruria. When the Gauls occupied Rome, its people
preserved the eternal fire and the Vestal Virgins, and for this reason it was
called the shrine of the Roman people and the repository of Roman sacred
objects. Thus, in recompense, the Romans called sacred rites *ceremonies*
after Caere, as *Valerius Maximus* (1.1) and *Florus* (1.13) maintain. Or,
what is more probable, it comes from the Hebrew חרם, which signifies a
sacred thing devoted to God.

V. It is distinguished by various names: sometimes חקים, *statutes*, just as
משפטים denotes the judicial law and מצוה the moral law;
sometimes νόμον τῶν ἐντολῶν ἐν δόγμασι, *the law of commandments in decrees*
(Eph. 2:15), because its precepts are δεδογμένα, *decreed*, by the will of
God, and possess no goodness except from the divine
decision; sometimes ἐντολὴν σαρκικὴν, *a carnal commandment* (Heb. 7:16),
and δικαιώματα σαρκός, *ordinances of the flesh* (Heb. 9:10), because it is
occupied with prescribing external and corruptible things, which come under
the name *flesh* in Scripture; and sometimes στοιχεῖα ἀσθενῆ καὶ πτωχὰ τοῦ
κόσμου, *the weak and beggarly elements of the world* (Gal. 4:9), because
they were the first rudiments and, as it were, the alphabet of the Jewish
Church, in which it had to be instructed during its infancy. In themselves
they could accomplish nothing either toward justification or toward
salvation.

[Marginal heading: The origin of ceremonies.]

VI. Ceremonies are external rites and sacred adjuncts to the worship of God.
They are not in themselves the worship of God, but are adjuncts which
accompany and assist it by signification, exercise, work, and decorum. Their
origin and foundation are sought from the nature and condition of the human
being. Since a human being is a *ceremonious animal*, as Melanchthon says, he
adheres and is attached to ceremonies and external rites. Since he consists
of soul and body and ought to glorify God in each, he requires external rites
by which he may order the movements of his body to the worship of God and be
raised from sensible and earthly things to spiritual and heavenly things.

Hence even in the state of integrity certain external symbols and ceremonial
rites were not lacking. Such were the tree of life, appointed above the
others as a sacrament of the covenant and promises; the tree of the knowledge
of good and evil, which alone out of the many was set apart as a symbol of
trial; and the sanctification of the Sabbath. But after sin the necessity of
these rites increased, not only for εὐταξίαν, *good order*, and as a

[Printed p. 130 | Internet Archive PDF 158 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

bond of holy society and pious communion under God, for the sealing of the
covenant both with respect to God’s presence and benefits and with respect to
the testimonies of our gratitude and the tokens of our duty toward God, but
especially to foreshadow the person and offices of the Mediator, without whom
there is no access to God.

VII. From this arose the institution of the ceremonial law. Besides the most
wise will of God, in which we must rest content, several very weighty reasons
for it are given.

1. The *condition of the Church*. While still established in its childhood
and infancy, on account of the small measure of the Holy Spirit given to it
(Gal. 4:1), it had to be instructed through figures of this kind, since it
was not ordinarily capable of greater light.

2. The *nature of the Israelite people*. Since they were a stiff-necked
people and strongly inclined toward idolatry and the profane worship of the
Gentiles, they had to be guarded by ceremonies of this kind and restrained,
as it were, by certain barriers, lest they desert to other forms of worship.

3. The *foulness and gravity of sin*, which had to be shown so that a human
being, convicted of it, might be stirred to seek a remedy. This was done
through purifications and sacrifices.

4. The *necessity and efficacy of grace*, which had to be foreshadowed by
various types.

[Marginal heading: The general end of the ceremonial law: εὐταξία, good
order. Its special end with respect to the moral law.]

VIII. Therefore, the *end*, no less than the *use, of the ceremonial law* can
be considered as manifold.

1. Its *general* end was εὐταξία, *good order*, so that the people might
have in it an external form of public worship and religion which would be
approved by God.

2. Its *special* end was again diverse according to its diverse σχέσιν,
*relation*, whether to the moral law, the Israelite people, or grace. With
respect to the *moral law*, its end was to serve the first table by
prescribing legitimate external worship and rejecting all foreign forms of
worship and ἰδιοθρησκειῶν, *self-made religions*, and to assist the moral law
in convicting human beings of their *impurity*, *inability*, and *guilt*.

[Marginal heading: With respect to the people.]

3. With respect to the *people*, it had to serve in *distinguishing* them
from the other peoples and binding them more firmly together. It is therefore
called the μεσότοιχον, *dividing wall* (Eph. 2:14), by which the Israelites
were separated from the other nations. For this reason those nations had no
intercourse with them, and indeed they exercised hatred and hostilities
against one another on this account. Hence in the same passage the apostle
calls it ἔχθρα, *hostility*, that is, an occasion of the hatred and discord
which existed between them.

Thus it served as a kind of pedagogy which would lead the people away from
communion with the Gentiles and from their idolatry and impurity, and would
lead them to God and bind them more closely, so that they might worship God
particularly as their own God. It would so keep them occupied in this
laborious worship that no time or hour remained free in which they might
either invent new forms of worship themselves or adopt those practiced by
others.

[Marginal heading: With respect to grace.]

IX. With respect to the *covenant of grace*, the use of the law was to show
its *necessity* through the demonstration of sin and human misery; to
demonstrate its *truth and excellence* through the foreshadowing of Christ
and his offices and benefits; to *seal* his manifold *grace* through its
figures and sacraments; to keep the people in desire and expectation of him
through that laborious worship and to compel them by the severity of its
discipline to seek him; and to exhibit the righteousness and image of the
*spiritual worship* required by him in that covenant.

Three things especially must always be impressed upon a human being: 1. his
*misery*, 2. *God’s mercy*, and 3. the *duty of gratitude*—what he is by
nature, what he has received through grace, and what he owes through
obedience. The ceremonial law placed these three things before the eyes of
the Israelites. The ceremonies included these three relations most of all:
the first insofar as they were appendices of the law, and the other two as
sacraments of evangelical grace. 1. They were *confessions of sins*, *human
misery*, and the guilt contracted through sin (Col. 2:14; Heb. 10:1–3). 2.
They were *symbols and*

[Printed p. 131 | Internet Archive PDF 159 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*shadows of God’s mercy* and of the *grace* to be conferred through Christ
(Col. 2:17; Heb. 9:13–14). 3. They were *images and pictures of duty* and of
the worship to be exhibited to God as a testimony of a grateful spirit (Rom.
12:1). Misery produced humility in their spirits, mercy produced comfort,
and the duty of gratitude produced sanctification.

These three things were expressly designated in the sacrifices. As the
*handwriting* from God’s side (Col. 2:14), they represented the debt
contracted by sin. They were also *shadows of the λύτρου, ransom*, to be paid
by Christ (Col. 2:17; Heb. 10:5, 10), and *pictures of the λατρείας λογικῆς,
rational worship*, the rational and evangelical worship to be exhibited to
God by believers (Rom. 12:1; 1 Pet. 2:5).

X. It is nevertheless certain that the first and foremost σχέσιν,
*relation*, of this law regarded Christ. As he was the goal and foundation of
all the promises and oracles (2 Cor. 1:20), so he was the body and truth of
all the types and figures (Col. 2:17). These were σκιὰ τῶν μελλόντων, τὸ δὲ
σῶμα τοῦ Χριστοῦ, *a shadow of things to come, but the body is Christ*. They
are called *shadows*, first, *physically*, as a shadow is opposed to a body.
Just as a shadow is the sign of a body, so the ceremonies were signs of
Christ who was to be exhibited. As a body is the cause of the shadow and not
the reverse, so Christ was the cause of the institution of these rites. As a
shadow has no force or power of itself, but the efficacy belongs to the body,
so all efficacy comes from Christ, while the ceremonies in themselves are
empty and dead.

They are also called shadows *artistically*, as an obscure and rough outline
of a thing, in opposition to an εἰκόνα, *perfect image* of the thing, in which
sense the apostle uses the word in Hebrews 10:1. God most wisely arranged it
in this way. Since for the weightiest reasons he willed to defer the advent
of the Messiah for many ages, the Messiah was not only promised and foretold
in the oracles but also represented in types until the time of διορθώσεως,
*reformation*, should come and the Sun of righteousness himself
should dawn. Thus the ancient people would sustain themselves by these
shadows and oracles in the hope of the coming Messiah and under these images
would possess some specimen of the heavenly grace to be exhibited in Christ.
Thus also believers of the New Testament would recognize the Messiah who had
been exhibited more easily and certainly from these marks, and their faith
would be more firmly confirmed as they beheld the fulfillment of the shadows
in the Body and of the figures in the truth.

[Marginal heading: Whether the ceremonial law was given from wrath to
wrath.]

XI. From this it can easily be gathered what must be determined in the
question raised today by some: whether the ceremonial law, commanded after
the νομοθεσίαν, *giving of the law*, had “the use of sealing the guilt and
curse which still bound believers, and thus was given from wrath to wrath and
to aggravate and seal the bondage of the fathers under the guilt of wrath and
the curse,” as some would have it; or whether it principally regarded Christ
and was given as a type of good things to come in order to lift up the spirits
of believers, as others think.

The law had a twofold σχέσις, *relation*, which must be carefully attended.
The one was *legal* and regarded the covenant of works and its violated law.
The other was *evangelical*, by which it was referred to the covenant of grace
and its Mediator. We must therefore reason differently about it according to
this twofold σχέσιν, *relation*.

With respect to the former, there is no doubt that it had the use of revealing
sin, sealing its guilt, and declaring the wrath and curse of God impending on
account of sin. In this sense Paul says that it was a handwriting against us
and that in it an ἀνάμνησιν, *remembrance*, of sins was made. But when the
latter relation is considered, it is certain that it also contained symbols
and seals of God’s grace in Christ, by which believers were raised to the hope
of good things to come and strengthened against the sense of sin and God’s
wrath.

XII. These σχέσεις, *relations*, are so connected to one another that anyone
who wishes
rightly to grasp the nature of the Old Testament economy and the use of the
ceremonial law must always join them. They cannot be separated from one
another without grave loss of truth. For just as one who attended only to the
evangelical σχέσιν, *relation*,

[Printed p. 132 | Internet Archive PDF 160 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

while setting aside the rigor of the legal pedagogy, would fail to attain the
character of the Old Testament which chiefly consists in that rigor, so those
who press the legal relation too far, while neglecting and insufficiently
weighing the evangelical relation, appear greatly to obscure the grace of God
which displayed itself under that dispensation and excessively to depress and
diminish the faith and comfort of the fathers.

The safest way here is therefore the middle way, always to keep both these
σχέσιν, *relations*, before our eyes. Yet the legal relation is inferior,
serving the
evangelical and regarding the less principal end of that whole dispensation.
The evangelical relation is primary, since it contains God’s chief and
ultimate end in that economy.

XIII. Hence it cannot be said absolutely that the ceremonial law was given
from wrath to wrath to seal and aggravate the bondage of the fathers under
guilt and the curse; that sacrifices for sin were not a remedy but a
handwriting; and similar statements by which the rigor of the legal pedagogy
is expounded. These statements may indeed be made in a sound sense with
respect to the legal σχέσιν, *relation*, considered precisely in itself, and
we would judge that no dispute should be raised with brethren over them if
this is their meaning. But they cannot be received absolutely and simply if
the ceremonies are regarded in relation to the evangelical σχέσιν, from
which they must never be separated.

For if it is true, as cannot be denied, that under them God willed to
foreshadow and seal to us the evangelical mysteries and the saving grace of
Christ—a fact which the apostle so often and expressly inculcates in the
Epistle to the Hebrews and which the nature of types and sacraments
necessarily requires—how can it be said that this law was given from wrath to
wrath and to seal and aggravate the guilt and bondage of the fathers? Must
that which had so pleasing and sweet a use be judged to have been given for so
harsh and burdensome an end? And if the sacrifices sealed the future
atonement of Christ unto a deliverance already efficacious at that time, how
are they said to have sealed the guilt incumbent upon the fathers with a
distressing reproach of their consciences?

XIV. Nor can the contrary opinion be established from the fact that the law
is called an ἀβάστακτον, *unbearable*, yoke (Acts 15:10). This refers to the
law considered in itself in opposition to the gospel, in which sense it is
the ministry of death and condemnation which burdened consciences with
laborious observances. It does not refer to the law subordinated to the
gospel, in which sense it was a pedagogue to Christ (Gal. 3:24).

Nor can it be established because the law is described as the weak and
beggarly elements of the world (Gal. 4:9), to which the apostle ascribes τὸ
ἀσθενὲς καὶ ἀνωφελές, *weakness and unprofitableness* (Heb. 7:18). Paul does
not speak of the ceremonies in their legitimate use from God’s institution
under the Old Testament, but of them as already abrogated and rendered dead
through Christ, or considered in themselves apart from the typological
relation—that is, separated from Christ, as the Judaizers regarded them.

Nor can it be established because the law is said to have had a shadow of
good things to come, not the image itself of the things (Heb. 10:1). It is
certain that the apostle speaks of the law considered simply in itself, apart
from Christ the Mediator. No one denies that in this sense it had only the
shadow of future things and could neither expiate sins nor sanctify the
conscience. But this does not prevent it from being referred to Christ to
foreshadow his grace. Indeed, the apostle’s words manifestly lead us there.

XV. No better inference is drawn from Colossians 2:14, where the law is called
a handwriting against us, which seems to denote the sealing and reproach of
the guilt still incumbent upon believers. This is said only with respect to
the legal σχέσεως, *relation*, which must not be pressed so far that the
evangelical is effaced. Otherwise Paul would have wrongly said that
*circumcision was a seal of the righteousness of faith* (Rom. 4:11). Under
the former relation it can properly be called a handwriting because in it
there was a confession of sin and guilt, which was not abolished by legal
victims, as was evident from their repetition, but was to be abolished by
Christ’s death alone. But

[Printed p. 133 | Internet Archive PDF 161 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

under the latter relation it was a figure of the substitution of the Mediator
in our place and of guilt transferred to him, and thus of the deliverance to
be received through him. Indeed, it figured remission already received by
virtue of the future sacrifice.

Hence Paul does not say simply that the handwriting is ἐναντίον,
*contrary*, to us, but ὑπεναντίον, *subcontrary*, that is, contrary in some
respect. For while it was a handwriting of sin, of guilt contracted on its
account, and of deserved punishment, and thus was against the fathers under
that legal σχέσει, *relation*, and insofar as they were sinners, yet insofar
as they were believers, the ceremonies contained favorable symbols and seals
of grace already dispensed to them at that time according to the evangelical
σχέσιν, *relation*. This was the principal and ultimate relation, through
which the former was softened for the comfort of believers, lest they be
regarded as still under guilt, which had been transferred to Christ by virtue
of the promise made and the future payment.

XVI. Furthermore, the multitude of ceremonies and figures was an argument
for the imperfection under which they labored. At the same time it pressed
upon their minds and eyes the supreme perfection of Christ, whose
foreshadowing required so great a variety. What could not be fully represented
by one small picture was exhibited through several which presented the same
object considered under a different σχέσει, *relation*.

The same Christ was foreshadowed in kings, prophets, and priests with respect
to his threefold office; in the priest offering, with respect to the eternal
Spirit or divinity; in the offered victim, with respect to his human nature;
in the altar of burnt offerings, with respect to satisfaction; and in the
altar of incense, with respect to intercession, and so forth. The same Spirit
was signified by the water of purification, the oil of anointing, and the
sacred fire with respect to his various operations. Hence the things divided
among the types are joined in the antitype. Judgment concerning the antitype
must be made not from one or another type separately, but from all of them
considered together, because there is no adequate type which fully represents
the antitype. All are partial and inadequate.

XVII. Just as the same mystery is often represented under various figures
and ceremonies, so the same figure is often employed to foreshadow different
mysteries, especially when those mysteries are subordinate and have a
σχέσιν, *relation*, to one another. Thus the *tabernacle* was a type of
*Christ* (John 1:14), of the *Church* and believers (Eph. 2:20), and of
*heaven* (Rev. 11:1–2). The *sacrifices* were figures of Christ’s atoning
sacrifice (Heb. 10:5–6) and of the spiritual and eucharistic sacrifices of
believers (1 Pet. 2:5). The priests represented Christ, the high priest of
the New Testament (Heb. 9–10); ministers of the gospel, who ought λειτουργεῖν,
*to minister as priests*, to God (Rom. 15:16); and believers, who have been
made a royal priesthood (1 Pet. 2:5; Rev. 1:6). The Passover was not only a
memorial of the angel’s passage through Egypt and of deliverance from Egypt,
but also a symbol of the future redemption through Christ from bondage to the
devil and sin, and a figure of the passage of believers from bondage to
liberty and from death to life (1 Cor. 5:7).

[Marginal heading: The inability of the ceremonies to expiate sin.]

XVIII. From this it is clearly gathered against the Jews that the use and end
of the ceremonial law was not the justification and sanctification of sinful
human beings. For “the law ἐτελείωσεν, *perfected and consummated*, nothing,
but [there was] the introduction of a better hope through which we draw near
to God” (Heb. 7:19). Since it was occupied only with a carnal command and
earthly and bodily things, these *cannot τελειῶσαι, perfect, the conscience*
(Heb. 9:9), but extended no further than the *purity of the flesh* (v. 13),
that is, external and ceremonial purity, as the apostle demonstrates fully in
Hebrews 10:1–4.

This is easily proved, first, generally from the *nature of that law, which
had a shadow of things to come* (v. 1) but not the express image of the
things, and therefore could not perfect those who approached. Second, it is
proved particularly from the imperfection of the ceremonies, which had no
proportion either to *sins*, which, as moral faults,

[Printed p. 134 | Internet Archive PDF 162 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

cannot be expiated and purged except morally by a victim who acts morally,
that is, who offers himself to God from the highest love and in the most
perfect obedience, such as Christ alone is; or to *God*, to whom satisfaction
must be made and who, as Spirit, also requires a spiritual and rational victim
to satisfy him. Nor would his wisdom or justice allow sins to be removed by
brute and material blood. They required that a rational victim wash away the
guilt committed by a rational creature and that infinite Majesty, having
been injured, be appeased by an infinite price and satisfaction. Nor, finally,
did the ceremonies have any proportion to the *human being* for whom
atonement had to be made, because sin had to be punished in the same nature
in which it was committed.

Hence the apostle expressly asserts that “it was impossible for the blood of
bulls and goats to take away sins” (Heb. 10:4), not only from God’s
ordination but from the nature of the thing, since in the perpetually repeated
sacrifices there was instead an annual ἀνάμνησις, *remembrance*, of sins
(vv. 2–3).

XIX. Nor, if expiation is frequently attributed to these sacrifices, does it
follow that they expiated truly and properly. This must be referred only to
typological and *ceremonial* expiation, by which the impurity of the flesh was
removed, that is, sin committed against that law, not to real and spiritual
expiation from the guilt and stain of sin properly so called, which brought
purity of conscience, as the apostle says in Hebrews 9:14.

A twofold effect of that law must therefore be considered. The former was
external and *carnal*, namely, the abolition of carnal impurity, by which
someone was barred from ecclesiastical assemblies. Such impurity was
contracted by contact with things prohibited by the ceremonial law. The
latter was *sacramental*, namely, the signification and sealing of grace and
of all the benefits to be obtained from the Mediator. On this see the
Disputation on the Satisfaction of Christ, Part VI, Thesis 14.

[Marginal heading: The ceremonies are considered either with respect to
persons or in other respects, and so forth.]

XX. These ceremonies can be distributed into various classes: 1. with
respect to the *persons* by whom the sacred acts had to be performed; 2. with
respect to the *sacred acts themselves* which had to be performed; 3. with
respect to the *places* in which they had to be celebrated; and 4. with
respect to the *times* in which they had to be discharged.

*Sacred persons* were of two kinds: some are called sacred in a common
respect and others in a singular respect. The former kind includes all who
were initiated by the sacrament of circumcision, by which token the people
dedicated to God were distinguished and recognized apart from the profane
nations. Its rite figured the abolition of our native corruption, the renewal
of God’s image in us, our engrafting into Christ, and the sealing of the
divine covenant.

XXI. The latter kind includes those whose ministry God used for the
government of his Church. They were ordinary and extraordinary ministers.
The latter were immediately raised up either to found the Church and first
constitute order in it or, when it had fallen, to restore it and foretell
future things. Such were the *prophets*. Thus they were instructed to
prophesy through visions, dreams, and the internal inspiration of the Holy
Spirit.

The ordinary ministers were called mediately and were bound to care for the
order constituted in the Church by performing sacred rites. Such were the
*priests* and *Levites*.

[Marginal heading: Priests.]

The priests descended in perpetual succession from
Aaron’s family, exercised their office in the tabernacle, laid their hands
upon the people, and blessed them by invoking God’s name. Among them the high
priest was eminent. His singular consecration, singular dress, and singular
office constituted a peculiar type of Christ, which it is not our purpose now
to pursue in detail.

[Marginal heading: Levites.]

Those were particularly called Levites who, employed in
sacred service in place of the firstborn (Num. 18), served the priests in
cleansing the sacred vessels, slaughtering and washing the animals, setting
out the loaves, and other duties of this kind.

[Marginal heading: Nazirites.]

XXII. The *Nazirites* also belong to the class of sacred persons, although
they did not handle sacred things. Some were *Nazirites by vow*, concerning
whom Numbers 6 speaks; others were born and divinely commanded to be such,
including Samson, Samuel, and John the Baptist. Their peculiar holiness had
to foreshadow the perfect purity of Christ, who was the true נזיר,
*Nazirite*, of God, such by preeminence, ἀφωρισμένος, *set apart*, from
others. In him is truly found in the mystery what occurred literally in the
Nazirites. He abstained from every defilement which could stain his most
perfect holiness, did not share in the dead works of sin, and his life was a
perpetual exemplar of sobriety, temperance, and every other virtue. He applied
himself to his office with such zeal that he preferred it to every duty of
kinship.

[Marginal heading: Sacred things. Gifts.]

XXIII. *Sacred things* were things exempted from profane uses, sanctified by
God’s Word, and appointed for sacred uses. They were *gifts* or *sacrifices*.
The former were offerings of inanimate things produced from the earth which
supplied a human being with either nourishment for life, seasoning for
gladness, or medicine for health. From the first kind bread and wine were
offered; from the second, oil and salt; and from the third, incense. By these
were designated both the benefits of Christ toward us and the gifts of the
Spirit, as well as our duty toward God and the attestation of a grateful
spirit (Mal. 1:11).

[Marginal heading: Sacrifices.]

XXIV. *Sacrifices* especially belong here, since they constitute a great part
of the ceremonial law. This does not mean that they were previously of free
use, so that they could be offered or not offered without neglect of the
worship that was due, as some would maintain. That they were prescribed by
God is gathered more than sufficiently from the fact that *Abel is said to
have offered by faith* (Heb. 11:4), while faith rests upon God’s Word, and
that *God is said to have borne testimony* to these sacrifices, thus
testifying that they were pleasing to him. But in the matter of religion
nothing pleases him except what he himself has commanded, and every
ἐθελοθρησκεία, *self-made religion*, is condemned (Col. 2:23). Their
institution, however, was renewed more clearly and solemnly through Moses.

*Sacrifices* were victims consisting of animate things, presented by a
believer and offered to God through a priest according to the rites
prescribed by God himself. Their integrity and ὁλοκληρία, *wholeness*,
signified both Christ the unblemished Lamb and the goal of perfection at
which we must aim in this life. Their offering designated first the sacrifice
of Christ offered for us and then also our λογικὴν λατρείαν, *rational
worship*.

There were two principal kinds. Some were *hilastic* or *propitiatory* for
sins committed. They foreshadowed the atoning sacrifice of Christ, offered
for us on the cross εἰς ὀσμὴν εὐωδίας, *as a fragrant aroma*, for the
expiation of our sins (Eph. 5:2), and the consecration of both our body and
spirit made to the Father in Christ (Rom. 12:1). Others were *eucharistic*
for benefits either hoped for or received. They were types of the spiritual
sacrifices of prayers and praises to be offered to God daily and of the
beneficence to be exercised toward our neighbor (Heb. 13:15–16).

XXV. *Sacrifice* is sometimes taken *broadly* for any religious action
instituted by God, in which we offer our things to him for his glory and are
joined more closely to him in holy fellowship. In this sense it could have a
place even before sin. Its end was to attest, seal, preserve, and increase
communion with God as the human being gave thanks, acknowledged all things as
received, and presented and set all his things before God as a testimony of a
grateful spirit and in order to obtain the benefits which he still needed.

But *strictly*, it denotes the offering and slaughter of a victim for the
expiation of sin through its αἱματεκχυσίαν, *shedding of blood*, and death.
In this sense it could have a place only after sin. For since the evils of
guilt and punishment introduced through sin had to be removed, and the good
things from which the human being had fallen had to be procured through
satisfaction of divine justice and appeasement of his wrath, the shedding of
blood and the death of a victim were necessarily required in

sacrifices to foreshadow more effectively the sacrifice of Christ through
his accursed death and the shedding of his most precious blood.

[Marginal heading: Sacred places.]

XXVI. Third, since *place and time* accompany sacred things, they also belong
to the ceremonial law. A *sacred place* is one in which God exhibited his
presence by some visible symbol and commanded himself to be publicly
worshiped there. For this reason, even before the tabernacle and temple were
built, such a place is called “the face of Jehovah, the house of God, and the
gate of heaven” (Gen. 16:13; 28:17).

Just as the law commands sacred assemblies, so places for assemblies had to
be selected in which the things for whose sake the assemblies were held could
be performed conveniently and suitably. This distinction depended not upon
the choice of human beings, who often invent divinity where no evidence of it
is given, but upon God. He selects and sets apart those places both by his
manifestation, by which, as though a lightning flash shone forth, he who is in
himself the most luminous of all—indeed, light itself—turns every eye toward
himself, and by the declaration of his will.

This local attachment was made necessary under the Old Testament, when
external and ceremonial worship also required a definite place to which it
was bound. But now under the gospel, when the knowledge of God is diffused
throughout the whole world, it has been made accidental and indifferent,
because our worship is bound to no place, neither Mount Gerizim nor Jerusalem,
provided that it occurs in spirit and truth (John 4:23). In every place one
may lift pure hands to God (1 Tim. 2:8).

[Marginal heading: The tabernacle and temple.]

XXVII. This place was revealed to Moses by God’s ordination: first, the
movable tabernacle which he was commanded to build for that end, and which was
therefore commonly called the *tabernacle of meeting and testimony*; and
later, in Solomon’s time, it was changed into the fixed and immovable temple.
It was a figure of:

1. *Christ*, in whom the πλήρωμα, *fullness*, of deity dwells σωματικῶς,
*bodily* (Col. 2:9), and in whom alone God can be rightly known and worshiped.
Through his humanity he ἐσκήνωσεν, *dwelt as in a tabernacle*, among us (John
1:14; Heb. 10:20; John 2:19).

2. The *Church*, outside which there is no salvation (Rev. 11:2), which is
the tabernacle of God in the Spirit (Eph. 2:22) and the house of the living
God (1 Tim. 3:15).

3. *Believers*, who carry about the earthly and handmade tabernacle of the
body which is gradually dissolved (2 Cor. 5:1; 2 Pet. 1:14), and are
themselves God’s temple (1 Cor. 3:16; 6:19), in whom Christ dwells through
faith (Eph. 3:17).

4. *Heaven and glory* (Rev. 11:19; 21:3, 22).

XXVIII. Its *unity* signifies the unity both of Christ the Head and of the
mystical Body, or the Church. Its distinction into the sanctuary, the holy
place, and the twofold court could foreshadow in *Christ* the *body*, which
corresponds to the court; the *soul*, which corresponds to the holy place;
and the *deity*, which corresponds to the sanctuary.

In the *Church* it could foreshadow its three orders: the *visible Church*,
which hypocrites and profane people can enter and which corresponds to the
court; the *invisible Church* of the elect, which still dwells upon earth and
into which only believers and the elect are received like the legal
priesthood in order to render λογικὴν λατρείαν, *rational worship*, to God,
where they still need the light of the Word to direct them, the food of the
sacraments to nourish them, and the incense of prayers; and the *Church of
the blessed*, who, admitted to heaven as God’s sanctuary, enjoy intimate
communion with God (Heb. 9:24). Or it could foreshadow the states of nature,
grace, and glory.

In a *human being* it could foreshadow the three parts: the *external senses*,
which apprehend τὰ αἰσθητά, *sensible things*, which are in the court; the
*intellect* and mind, which know τὰ νοητά, *intelligible things*, which are
in the holy place; and the *heart* or conscience, which embraces τὰ πιστά,
*things believed*, which are in the most holy place, things which the eye has
not seen nor the ear heard.

In *God’s revelation* it could foreshadow the three degrees: through *works*,
which correspond to the court because they lie open to everyone
indiscriminately; through the *Word*, which corresponds to the holy place
because it is appointed for the Church alone; and through *face-to-face
vision*, which corresponds to the sanctuary and is given only to the blessed.

XXIX. The tabernacle’s harmonious joining together by its joints and
connections figured the Body of the Church, συναρμολογούμενον, *being joined
together*, and συμβιβαζόμενον, *being knit together*, from Christ the Head
and his faithful members (Eph. 4:16).

Its change into the temple designated the changeable form of the Jewish
polity, which was first movable and itinerant in the wilderness and afterward
obtained rest under Solomon. The temple designated that rest. It was indeed
analogous to the tabernacle in its parts and instruments but was far more
august in stability, splendor, and majesty. Thus an image both of the ancient
Church and of the changeable state of all human life is properly exhibited in
the tabernacle, while an image both of the Christian Church and of the
triumphant Church is exhibited in the temple. Therefore only the Jews worked
on the former, while Gentiles, Tyrians, and Sidonians also labored on the
latter.

[Marginal heading: The ark of the covenant.]

XXX. The *ark of the covenant* was contained in the tabernacle and temple. In
it the tables of the law were stored, and the golden covering of the mercy
seat, covered by cherubim, was placed upon it. It signified:

1. *Christ*, upon whom God erected the throne of grace (Heb. 4:16), in whom
he shows himself propitious to us, and who was given to us as a propitiatory
in his blood (Rom. 3:25). In him are hidden the treasures of wisdom and
understanding (Col. 2:3) and the tables of the law are deposited, since God’s
law was inscribed within him (Ps. 40:8). In him the λόγιον, *oracle*, of God
is found, because as the λόγος, *Word*, of the Father he utters his oracles
and reveals his will to us. Angels minister to him, and the cherubim
παρακύπτουσι, *stoop down*, and lower themselves to contemplate his glory
and mysteries more deeply (1 Pet. 1:12).

2. The *Church*, in which God erected his throne and willed the sacred tables
to be stored; where he exhibits himself as present in grace and appeased in
Christ, the true propitiatory; where he utters oracles through the Word; where
angels as ministering spirits perpetually stand beside believers and watch
over their salvation; and where the *rod* of discipline and the cross, by
which he exercises us, is joined with the *manna* of consolation and life, by
which he sustains us.

[Marginal heading: The twofold altar.]

XXXI. The *twofold altar, of burnt offering and of incense*, represented two
parts of Christ’s priestly office, which had to be fulfilled in his twofold
state: *satisfaction*, in the offering of his body and the shedding of blood
in the state of humiliation; and *intercession* in heaven in the state of
exaltation through the living and continual representation of his sacrifice,
which is foreshadowed by the golden censer in which incense is burned upon the
altar (Rev. 8:3). “We have an altar,” says the apostle, “from which those who
serve the tabernacle have no right to eat” (Heb. 13:10).

The passage was made from one altar to the other. The sacrifice first had to
be offered upon the bronze altar before the incense was offered. So
satisfaction had to precede intercession, because without satisfaction all
prayers are useless. Christ is *our altar*: on the *cross* through
immolation, in *heaven* through intercession, and at the *table* through
commemoration.

[Marginal heading: The table. The lampstand. The altar of incense.]

XXXII. Three principal things are observed in the holy place: the *table*,
upon which the bread of the Presence was set; the *lampstand*, upon which the
lamps were lit; and the *altar of incense*, where incense was burned. To these
correspond in the Church the *light of the Word*, by which he illuminates us;
the *bread of life*, by which he feeds us; and the *sacred worship* which he
requires from us through the offering of prayers and other spiritual
sacrifices. The *light of faith* shines in the lampstand, the *life of hope*
is nourished at the table, and the *fire of love* is fostered at the altar.

[Marginal heading: The bronze basin.]

XXXIII. The *bronze basin*, in which the priests were bound to wash their
hands and feet before they approached the altar and performed the sacred
rites, was a sign of the singular purification required in those who approach
God, whether they are furnished with the peculiar calling of God’s ministers
or the common calling of believers. Thus it was evident that no one could

approach to worship God rightly unless through faith in Christ he had been
fully cleansed from the guilt of sins and at least partially from their stain
(John 13:8; Heb. 10:22; Titus 1:15–16).

[Marginal heading: Sacred times. The continual sacrifice.]

XXXIV. Finally, *sacred times* are those in which God’s benefits, whether
past or future, are publicly proclaimed and celebrated. To these belongs,
first, the *continual sacrifice* customarily offered every morning and
evening with the accompanying gift. It signified both the eternal power and
efficacy of Christ’s sacrifice and the daily prayers of the pious and the
worship made pleasing to God through Christ.

[Marginal heading: Feasts.]

XXXV. Second are the *feasts*, which were of three kinds: weekly, monthly,
and annual. The *weekly feast* was the festival of the seventh day, called
the Sabbath, which reminds us both of the creation of the world and of Christ
redeeming and sanctifying us and beginning his rest in us. The *monthly
feasts* were called new moons and were celebrated on the first day of every
month, though without any cessation from external labors. They taught that
all months and their seasons are sanctified for us through Christ.

XXXVI. The *annual feasts* either recurred every year or only after a certain
number of years. Among the yearly festivals, the most solemn were the three
at which every male was commanded to appear in the Lord’s presence, not
without a gift: namely, Passover, Pentecost, and the Feast of Tabernacles.

Although they were instituted for the recollection of past benefits—Passover
in commemoration of deliverance from Egypt, Pentecost to recall the benefit
of the giving of the law, and the Feast of Tabernacles in memory of divine
protection in the wilderness—they nevertheless had joined with them the
foreshadowing and promise of spiritual benefits to be exhibited in Christ.
Passover foreshadowed deliverance from Egypt, deliverance from the power of
hell, and Christ’s immolation (1 Cor. 5:7; 1 Pet. 1:18–19). Pentecost
foreshadowed the sending of the Holy Spirit and the inscription of the law
upon the tables of the heart through that same Spirit (2 Cor. 3:2–3; Jer.
31:33). The Feast of Tabernacles foreshadowed the pilgrimage of the pious
human being through the wilderness of this world to the heavenly homeland
(Heb. 13:14).

XXXVII. Feasts recurring after intervals of a certain number of years were
the *Sabbatical Year*, returning every seventh year, in which cultivation
ceased, and the *Jubilee*, in the fiftieth year, in which there was in
addition the manumission of slaves, remission of debts, and restitution of
sold property. The latter was a symbol of the evangelical jubilee proclaimed
through Christ’s gospel, which is truly the acceptable year of the Lord (Isa.
61:1–3). In it all spiritual debts, namely sins, are remitted to us through
Christ (Rom. 8:1; Col. 2:14); liberty from bondage to sin, the devil, and
death is granted to us (Luke 1:74; Rom. 6:13); and the good things lost
through Adam are restored. In a word, complete deliverance from every evil
and perfect and everlasting rest under Christ are granted.

XXXVIII. Let these things suffice as a passing and concise account of the
ceremonies and their use, especially their typological and figurative use.
To pursue these matters at greater length and examine the mysteries of every
ceremony individually would be the subject not of one or two small pages but
of a full treatise. That is not our present purpose and belongs to didactic
rather than elenctic theology.
