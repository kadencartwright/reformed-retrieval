QUESTION VII.

THE TWOFOLD ADMINISTRATION OF THE COVENANT OF GRACE.

*Why did God will to dispense the one covenant of grace in different ways;
and how manyfold and of what kind was its administration?*

I. The *substantial unity* of the covenant of grace was asserted before; now
its *administrative diversity* must be examined. For just as the πολυποίκιλος,
*manifold*, wisdom of God shines wonderfully in the works of nature, in their
manifold variety, arrangement, and diverse government, so it is no less—indeed,
more clearly—conspicuous in the works of grace, and especially in the varied
dispensation of the new covenant. It had various forms and, as it were, faces
on account of the varied administration of the mystery of Christ, who is its
foundation. God willed to administer it in such a way that he first proposed
it more obscurely and afterward more clearly, first in *promise* and then in
*fulfillment*.

Hence its twofold administration was instituted. One is *old*, commonly
called the Old Testament, which promised and foretold Christ who was to come.
The other is *new*, called the New Testament, which announces Christ as
exhibited, incarnate, and dead. In the former God spoke to the fathers
πολυμερῶς καὶ πολυτρόπως, *in many portions and in many ways*, through many
occasions and modes. In the latter, however, he spoke to us through the Son
(Heb. 1:2).

[Marginal heading: Why did God dispense the covenant of grace in different
ways? 1. God’s manner of acting required it.]

II. If it is asked why God willed to institute such a dispensation of the
covenant, God’s will alone could abundantly suffice to restrain curiosity.
Nevertheless, various reasons for that most wise counsel are not lacking. 1.
God’s *manner of acting* seemed to require this. He does not complete great
things all at once, though he could do so most easily, but successively and
through various degrees, so that his wisdom and power may be perceived more
clearly. Thus God willed gradually to perfect and promulgate the covenant of
grace, which is rightly called his greatest work and worthy of the highest
admiration, so that it would have its beginnings and increases before its full
consummation followed.

Nor was it fitting to God’s wisdom that so great a Light as there is in the
gospel should be poured out all at once and, as it were, in one rush, but
gradually and through narrow openings, like the sun, which ascends to midday
not in one moment but through degrees. The apostle teaches this in a few but
excellent words in Heb. 1:1, when he says that *God spoke to the fathers of
old πολυτρόπως and πολυμερῶς, in many ways and in many portions*. The former
denotes the *various modes of revelation* which obtained in the Old Testament,
but the latter its different *degrees* through which he disclosed himself to
the ancients.

[Marginal heading: 2. The condition of the church.]

III. 2. The *condition of the church* required this. Just as God willed to
lead it through various ages, so he determined to deal with it in different
ways. Therefore, while it was in infancy and had not yet reached mature age,
it had to be treated as an infant (Gal. 4:1). Thus God, as though lisping with
it, granted it the smallest measure of revelation and kept it under first
elements and rudimentary figures. These afterward had to give way to a fuller
revelation when the church was more advanced and reached adult age.

[Marginal heading: 3. The dignity of the Messiah.]

IV. 3. The *dignity of the Messiah* and the *excellence of the work* to be
performed by him. Just as for the weightiest reasons he had to be exhibited
not immediately at the beginning but only in the fullness of time, it was
fitting first to propose his image, awaken desire, and hold minds in desire
and expectation of him, lest through a longer delay they

[Printed p. 193 | Internet Archive PDF 221 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

grow weary. It was also right to reserve the open declaration of so great a
mystery for Christ, the supreme Prophet, so that the distinction between Moses
the servant and Christ the Lord would appear more clearly: between a symbolic
teacher who taught first elements and the heavenly Teacher who revealed every
secret. Then man’s impotence had first to be proved, so that the necessity of
grace would be known more clearly and desire for the Savior through whom it
was to be brought would be kindled more ardently.

[Marginal heading: 4. The nature of the things.]

V. 4. The *nature of the things*. Since they were still future and prophecies
before fulfillment are usually more obscure, at least concerning the manner
of fulfillment, it is not surprising that they were first proposed more
obscurely and afterward more clearly as the time of fulfillment drew nearer.
Thus the light increased more and more. Revelation advances: one prophet
hands the lamp of the gospel to another prophet, who takes it up so that the
church may be illuminated, and adds a new degree of light. The same promises
are repeated and explained, and the nearer the time of fulfillment presses,
the clearer and fuller they are.

Before the law was given through Moses, the promise was more obscure; from
the giving of the law to the times of the prophets, less obscure; from the
times of the prophets to John the Baptist, clearer; after John’s preaching,
open; and after Christ’s advent, clearest. Thus at the beginning the Messiah
was promised only as the Seed of the woman (Gen. 3:15), afterward as the Son
of Abraham (Gen. 12), as one arising from Judah (Gen. 49), and within the
tribe of Judah from the family of David (2 Sam. 7). Then various circumstances
were added: concerning the condition of his mother, that she would be a virgin
(Isa. 7); the place of his birth (Mic. 5:2); its time (Dan. 9); his twofold
state of sufferings and glory (Isa. 53); the Forerunner (Mal. 3:1); and other
adjuncts which explained the promise more and more.

VI. Nor was it without weighty cause that this mystery was proposed more
obscurely the farther the times were removed from Christ’s advent. 1. It was
fitting that the farther the times appeared from the rising of the Sun of
righteousness, the more obscure they would be. 2. The nearer Christ’s advent
pressed, the more sharply human minds had to be aroused in expectation of him.
3. The nearer the church was to its beginnings, the more rudimentary it was,
and therefore it had to be instructed in a more rudimentary form.

[Marginal heading: The Old Testament extends from Adam to Christ.]

VII. Furthermore, the diversity of this administration is commonly
comprehended in two parts, ordinarily designated by the names *Old and New
Testament*, as was said before. The former extends from Adam to Christ; the
latter from Christ to the end of the age. Whatever in the dispensation of
this covenant was typical and connoted imperfection and the confession that
the λύτρου, *ransom*, had not yet been paid pertains to the Old Testament.
Whatever connotes redemption actually accomplished through Christ is proper
to the New Testament.

I know that a τριχοτομίαν, *threefold division*, is more pleasing to some, so
that the administration of the covenant of grace is considered, 1. *Under the
Promise* before the law, from the first gospel to Moses. They contend this was
a promise of pure grace and liberty without any yoke or burden of the accusing
law. 2. *Under the Law*, from Moses to Christ, where they maintain that the
Old Testament, whose inheritance is the land of Canaan, first begins. 3.
*Under the Gospel*, where the New begins. Yet we do not think the received
position should be abandoned. It retains the διχοτομίαν, *twofold division*,
and commonly designates the first administration before Christ by the notion
of the Old Testament.

For according to the usage of Scripture, the *Promise* is referred not only
to the time before the law but is extended to every following age under the
law as far as Christ, as is gathered from Acts 2:39; 7:17; 13:32; Luke 1:72;
Rom. 9:4; and Eph. 2:12. And the designation *Old Testament* is not restricted
to the Mosaic administration but extends to

[Printed p. 194 | Internet Archive PDF 222 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the whole dispensation which preceded Christ from man’s fall itself and the
promise given to him (2 Cor. 3:14). Hence Christ is called the Mediator in
Heb. 9:15, “appointed for the expiation of the transgressions committed under
the former Testament,” certainly not only under the law but from the beginning
of the world. And in chapter 8:13, whatever is to be made obsolete is called
the Old Testament: not only the ceremonies first enacted by Moses, but also
sacrifices and circumcision, which already obtained before the written law.

VIII. Nor, if the compact of the Old Testament is sometimes attributed in a
peculiar manner to the Mosaic administration, does it follow that the Old
Testament began only then. The same religion obtained in each period, both
before and after the law: sacrifices, circumcision, and many other things
which constitute the Old Testament. Rather, this attribution is made by
reason of a more illustrious confirmation. At that time the confirmation of
the old rites together with the new was formed into a fixed order of sacred
things, and that period was so enriched by a solemn renewal of the covenant
and many additions that it was far more perfect and seemed, as it were, to
absorb all the preceding periods. In this sense circumcision, which existed
long before Moses, is said to have been given by Moses (John 7:22). Thus in
Scripture a thing is often said to be made when it is renewed and proposed in
a more illustrious manner, as was said before concerning the new covenant to
be contracted in the last days.

This easily explains Deut. 5:2, 3; Jer. 31:32; and Gal. 4:24, in which the
beginning of the covenant or Old Testament seems to be derived from Moses and
from the covenant contracted by him with the Israelites in Horeb. The subject
there is not the thing itself, but only its mode. It does not deny that God
contracted a covenant with the fathers, which is so often asserted elsewhere.
Indeed, whatever God did for Israel, he did by virtue of the covenant made
with the fathers (Exod. 2:24). Rather, it intimates only that it had not yet
been made in that mode and form and with such apparatus: addressing them from
the midst of thunders and lightning, giving them the law inscribed by his own
hand, and adding so many rites.

IX. When Paul says in Heb. 9:18, 20 that the *Old Testament was inaugurated
with blood*, he does not intend that its origin must be referred only to the
αἱματεκχυσίαν, *shedding of blood*, in Exod. 24:6, as though it were still
recent. Besides the fact that this consecration could already have occurred
through the αἱματεκχυσίαν made in the patriarchs’ sacrifices, if it is
referred to Exod. 24, it will denote only a solemn dedication, not the first
institution. Thus a thing solemnly dedicated anew can also be said
ἐγκαινίζεσθαι, *to be inaugurated*, although the thing itself existed long
before. Therefore, Ἐγκαίνια, *dedications*, can be understood of any
dedication, whether first or restored (1 Macc. 6; 4:43; John 10:22; Heb.
10:20).

X. If theologians say that the covenant of grace contained under the bare
promise made to Adam received a more covenantal σχέσιν, *relation*, in the
time of Abraham, and in the time of Moses a more perfect testamentary
character, this does not exclude the fathers before the law from the scope of
the Old Testament or restrict the Old Testament only to the typical promise
of the land of Canaan. Rather, it marks the progress of revelation of the
covenant of grace and distinguishes the infancy and rudiments of this
administration before the law from its more perfect constitution. Although in
one period of the Old Testament the inheritance of the land of Canaan was
given to Israel, this does not exhaust the whole nature of the Old Testament,
which comprehends whatever was typical and ceremonial in that dispensation
from the very beginning of the world.

[Marginal heading: The first age of the church, from Adam to Abraham.]

XI. Now, to speak distinctly about this twofold administration, the former,
which is that of the Old Testament, can be regarded under three periods or
courses: from Adam to Abraham; from Abraham to Moses; and from Moses to Christ.
The first

[Printed p. 195 | Internet Archive PDF 223 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

course ran from Adam to Abraham. During this time the sum of the covenant of
grace was contained in the very brief but most luminous words of the first
gospel, or *first Promise*, in Gen. 3:15: *The Seed of the woman will crush the
serpent’s head*. By these words the faith of our first parents was nourished.
The substance of this oracle was that someone would one day arise who would
rescue from destruction those with whom God had determined to enter into
covenant, and would do so by crushing the one through whose fraud they had
fallen into this calamity. God’s wonderful mercy shines in this: he willed
immediately to propose to Adam the remedy of grace to heal the wound received
from Satan, so that where the poison of sin increased, the ἀλεξιφάρμακον,
*antidote*, of grace would meet it. Natural philosophers note that this is
often observed in nature, especially in thora and its antidote anthora, placed
next to each other.

XII. The thing itself cries out that this oracle regarded Christ. We do not
pause over the fact that the Vulgate put *she* in place of *he*, so that by a
manifest corruption it might be referred to the blessed Virgin, as though
היא had to be read in place of הוא. Guido Fabricius Boderianus attempted with
malicious deceit to corrupt the passage in this way in the Antwerp Polyglot’s
interlinear Bible. The sounder Roman Catholics themselves, compelled by the
force of the truth, have acknowledged it, including Pagninus, Arias Montanus,
Vatablus, Isidorus Clarius, Lucas Brugensis, and innumerable others.

It is agreed that this is contrary to the testimony of all approved codices;
to the most ancient versions of the Chaldean Paraphrast and the Septuagint;
to grammatical analogy, which cannot allow a feminine relative to be
attributed to the masculine noun זרע and the verb תשופנו of the same gender;
and to the nature of the thing attributed to this subject. The crushing of
the serpent’s head—that is, the destruction of Satan’s dominion—is such that
it simply surpasses human powers and is everywhere claimed for Christ in
Scripture.

XIII. Therefore, it is gathered that by the *Seed of the woman* Christ must
be understood antonomastically and principally. 1. No other seed is opposed
to him as before, but one singular antagonist, namely, the serpent, whose
head had to be crushed by this Seed, intimating a certain singular combat. 2.
The manner of the struggle and victory is such that it can belong to none but
God, namely, victory over the devil, who could be conquered neither by man,
already defeated, nor by an equal angel, but by God alone. 3. Scripture refers
the same promise of the blessed Seed made to Abraham to Christ (Gal. 3:16):
“It does not say, to seeds, as though there were many, but to the Seed, which
is Christ.” 4. The fulfillment of this oracle is everywhere claimed for
Christ in the New Testament (John 12:31; 14:30; Luke 10:18; Col. 2:15; Heb.
2:14, 15; Rom. 16:20; 1 John 3:8; Rev. 12:8, 9).

XIV. Christ is said to be the one who will crush the *serpent’s head* insofar
as he had to abolish all the devil’s tyranny and power given to him through
sin, which is designated by the serpent’s head. For a serpent’s poison is
chiefly in its head, and when this is wounded and crushed, it can no longer
harm. A symbol of this crushing was the marvelous power granted to Christ’s
disciples in Luke 10:19: “Behold, I give you power to tread upon serpents and
scorpions and over all the power of the enemy, and nothing will harm you.” It
is also attributed to the godly in Ps. 91:13: “You will walk upon the asp and
the basilisk,” and so forth.

Christ accomplishes this not all at once but in various degrees: first *in
himself for us*, then *in us and through us* by his power. *In himself*: 1.
Through his *incarnation and death*, by which he most fully satisfied God’s
justice and expiated our sins, so that he took away from Satan every further
right to accuse human beings: “Through death he destroyed the one who had the
power of death” (Heb. 2:14, 15); “He openly led principalities and powers in
triumph on the cross” (Col. 2:15). 2. Through the *resurrection and ascension*,
in which, victor over death

[Printed p. 196 | Internet Archive PDF 224 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and the grave, “he led captivity captive” (Eph. 4:8). 3. Through the
*preaching of the gospel*, concerning which he says in Luke 10:18, 19: “I saw
Satan fall from heaven like lightning,” and so forth. 4. Through his *final
advent*, when Satan and the false prophet will be cast into the lake of fire
and sulfur (Rev. 20:10).

*In us*, through the operation of the Spirit, by which believers are said to
*overcome the evil one* (1 John 2:14; Rev. 12:11): 1. In *calling*, through
which they are turned from Satan to God and from the power of darkness to the
kingdom of light (Acts 26:17, 18; Col. 1:13). 2. In *justification* and
*sanctification*, which free us from the guilt and dominion of sin through
which Satan exercised his tyranny over us (Rom. 8:1, 31, 32, 33; 1 John
5:18). 3. In the *final resurrection* and last judgment, in which Satan will
be crushed beneath our feet (Rom. 16:20) and cast headlong into eternal fire
(1 Cor. 15:54, 55; Rev. 20:10, 14; Matt. 25).

XV. This victory to be gained by Christ over Satan could not be bloodless and
without conflict, for his heel had to be crushed by the serpent: *You will
crush his heel*. There had to be a struggle on each side, but a very dissimilar
one. Christ seeks and crushes the serpent’s head, that is, he overthrows
Satan’s kingdom. But the serpent can only reach and bite the heel, that is,
afflict Christ’s humanity and flesh, by which he walked the earth, through
temptations, persecutions, and death. Yet he cannot destroy him, because
Christ immediately rose again and came forth victorious. Thus the sufferings
here must necessarily be joined with the glory to follow.

The same is also discerned in believers. God indeed permits that ancient
serpent to bite believers’ heel, that is, to trouble them in their bodies and
bodily goods through temptations, when he sifts them (Luke 22:31); through
afflictions and crosses (Rev. 2:10; 12:13, 14, 15); and through death, since he
is a murderer from the beginning. But he cannot strike the heart and head,
because he has no power over the soul and salvation.

XVI. Although it cannot be doubted that *Christ* is to be understood here
primarily and κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν, *preeminently*, by the *Seed of the woman*, the name
“Seed” is collective, commonly comprehending not one individual but many, and
must be taken in this way when the *serpent’s seed* is discussed. Therefore,
the position of those who maintain that this word must be taken collectively
must not be rejected, so that it extends to all believers who are Christ’s and
have a share in that promise. Yet it must be understood so that in that
comparison one person stands out as the author of so great a work.

Thus the whole church, Head with members, is included here, but with this
distinction: it is attributed to Christ primarily and principally, but to the
others secondarily and dependently; to him originally and eminently, but to
the others participatively and in a lower degree; to him as Victor and Leader,
by whose power all things are accomplished, but to the others as soldiers who
serve beneath his banner and whom he calls to receive the fruit of the
victory. This was the position of our Calvin with many others. He is most
unfairly reproached by the Lutherans as though he Judaized in this matter and
overthrew this first evangelical promise because he takes the woman’s Seed
collectively, not individually. He expressly intimates that “it is necessary
to come to one Head, so that we may discover to whom the victory pertains,”
which he confirms more than once in the *Institutes* (book 1, chapter 14,
section 18; book 2, chapter 13, section 2).

XVII. From these things it is easily gathered that this primeval promise given
to our first parents, although more obscurely, contained as though in a
summary the chief parts of the covenant of grace and the gospel. Although the
believers’ response or reciprocal stipulation is not expressly proposed in
those words, they nevertheless manifest the obligation of worship and
obedience to be rendered by them to God. It is clear that this was rendered
when the name of the Lord is said to have been invoked (Gen. 4:26). Add that
the promise on God’s part necessarily demands faith on man’s

[Printed p. 197 | Internet Archive PDF 225 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

part. Hence the faith of Abel, Enoch, and Noah is celebrated by Paul (Heb.
11:4, 5, 6, 7). Nor did the ἱλαστικὰ, *propitiatory sacrifices*, offered to
God by the godly in those first times—not without a divine command, since they
were offered through faith, which rests on God’s Word—aim at anything other
than sustaining them in the hope of future redemption.

[Marginal heading: The second age, from Abraham to Moses.]

XVIII. The *second age* extends from Abraham to Moses, the liberator of the
people and promulgator of the law. Since the memory of the divine promise had
almost faded from human minds and the contagion of idolatry had contaminated
even the holy race of Shem and the family of Terah (Josh. 24:2), God chose and
called Abraham out of it and renewed the covenant with him and his seed. He
pledged that the Messiah would be born from his posterity and in turn demanded
faith and obedience from him (Gen. 13:18; 17:1, 2).

XIX. The promise concerned not only temporal and earthly benefits—the
multiplication of his seed, the renown of his name, and possession of the land
of Canaan—but especially *spiritual and heavenly* ones. These are included in
the blessing given to Abraham: *In you all the families of the earth will be
blessed* (Gen. 12:3); *in you*, that is, *in your seed*, as it is explained in
chapters 22:18 and 26:4. This *blessing* comprehends every kind of saving good
(Eph. 1:3), just as the *curse* opposed to it included every kind of evil. The
nature of the thing itself proves that it is evangelical, as does the
apostle’s word when he refers it, 1. To justification in Gal. 3:7, 8:
“Scripture,” he says, “foreseeing that God justifies the nations by faith,
preached the gospel beforehand to Abraham, saying, In you all the nations
will be blessed”; and in verses 13, 14: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of
the law, having become a curse for us, so that the blessing of Abraham might
come upon all believers through Christ.”

2. To adoption in Gal. 3:29: “If we are Christ’s, we are Abraham’s seed and
heirs according to the promise”; and chapter 4:28: “We are children of promise
after the likeness of Isaac.” 3. To conversion in Acts 3:25: “You are the
children of the prophets and of the covenant God entered into with the
fathers, saying to Abraham, In your seed all the families of the earth will
be blessed. To you first God sent Christ, having raised him up, to bless you
by converting and turning each one from his sins.” 4. To the promise of the
Spirit (Gal. 3:14). 5. To glorification. Hence the nations are said to recline
with Abraham in the kingdom of heaven (Matt. 8:11; Luke 16:23), and the elect
are called the Father’s *blessed* (Matt. 25).

XX. The *Seed* in whom this blessing must be conferred is not merely natural,
but mystical and blessed, namely, *Christ*, according to the apostle’s
interpretation in Gal. 3:16: “The promises were spoken to Abraham and his
Seed; it does not say, to seeds, as though there were many, but as of one, to
your Seed, which is Christ.” The thing itself necessarily requires this,
because the Seed in whom all families of the earth were to be blessed and
through whom Abraham was to become the father of many nations cannot be Isaac,
but only Christ. It is a Seed whose *faith had to be imputed for
righteousness* (Gen. 15:6), that is, confer justification, which is fulfilled
only in Christ, *in whom all the promises are Yes and Amen* (2 Cor. 1:20).

Therefore, the nations were to be blessed in Christ, both *through and on
account of him*, who acquired this blessing by his merit, and *in him and with
him*, that is, by virtue of the mystical union which believers have with him.
Thus the unity of the seed designates not precisely one person but a certain
mystical Body united through faith beneath Christ the Head, from whom the
other members are named.

XXI. Finally, the covenant *promise* in which all the others are contained is
that “God would be his God and the God of his seed after him” (Gen. 17), of
whose sense and extent we spoke above. Furthermore, God

[Printed p. 198 | Internet Archive PDF 226 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

was not content to express this promise in words but willed to confirm it in
many ways so that its immutability might be denoted (Gal. 3:15, 17). Its
κύρωσις, *ratification*, was threefold. The first was through the solemn rites
of covenant makers in Gen. 15: the dividing of animals and passage through
their midst, which Jer. 34:18 teaches was customary in contracting covenants.
The second was through the sacrament of circumcision added to the promise
(Gen. 17). The third was through an oath (Gen. 22; Heb. 6:17, 18): “In this
God, willing further to show the heirs of promise the immutability of his
counsel, intervened with an oath,” and so forth.

XXII. The *conditions* of the covenant are also proposed. First, *faith*, by
which he is said to have *believed God* (Gen. 15:6). Second, *conversion and
the pursuit of sanctification* in Gen. 17:1: *Walk before me and be upright*.
Each is sealed and commanded in the sacrament of circumcision, and each
displayed itself wonderfully in the father of believers.

1. *Faith*: when Abraham embraced the promise concerning the Seed (Gen. 12:4,
5, 6), he expected every good from him, not only temporal things, but
especially spiritual and eternal things. His faith was therefore justifying.
Its excellence is increased by the circumstances: he believed things so
difficult and plainly incredible—the possession of a land held by a most
powerful nation when he was alone; offspring when he and Sarah were already
worn out; and the blessing of all nations in his Seed, whom he never saw.
Paul graphically describes the firmness of this faith in Rom. 4:18, 19, 20,
21.

2. His *obedience* also shone in two things. 1. In his *departure from his
father’s house* and country, uncertain where he was going (Heb. 11:8). It is
the highest glory of obedience to follow God as he calls and leads, to go
where you do not know and where you are ignorant whether it will go well or
badly for you. 2. In the *offering of his son* (Heb. 11:17; Gen. 22:2), which
was the greatest and hardest trial of all: not only to lose an innocent,
beloved, only son, but to kill him and slaughter him with his own hands, so
that the one who had been a *father* in giving life would become a *murderer*
in taking it away. And he was to do this promptly and without delay, drawing
the blade through each limb, cutting him apart like a sacrifice, and offering
him as a burnt offering so that he would be wholly consumed. He was to do it
after forethought, having spent three whole days amid these thoughts.

This seemed sacrilege rather than a sacred act: against every divine and
human law; against nature’s own light and impulse; against the promise given
concerning the blessed and blessing Seed; and thus against the eternal faith
and salvation of Abraham and all the covenant people. Yet he obeyed, and from
faith in the resurrection of the dead was prepared to offer even such a son
(Heb. 11:19). This singular obedience obtained for him the name *friend of
God* (Isa. 41:8; James 2:23).

XXIII. There was a manifold distinction between the administration of this
age and the preceding one. The former is more properly called the
*promise*, but the latter the *covenant*, because here the condition of
obedience is proposed clearly and expressly, while there it was contained
only implicitly. The former indeed had the rite of sacrifices, but the latter
was sealed with the sacrament of circumcision as a peculiar seal. The former
was proposed commonly and indefinitely to the human race; the latter was
particularly restricted to Abram and his posterity. In the former the
promises do not appear so clearly, the calling of the nations is not mentioned
so plainly, and no one is proposed as the father of the covenant people. For
this reason Abraham is called heir of the world (Rom. 4:16, 17), because he
regarded the world—that is, all the nations to which the blessing was to
come—as his children.

[Marginal heading: The third age, from Moses to Christ.]

XXIV. The *third age* of the church and the administration of the Old
Testament extends from Moses to Christ, or from the promulgation of the law in
the wilderness to the exhibition of the Messiah. In this interval of time the
face of things was far different. Then occurred

[Printed p. 199 | Internet Archive PDF 227 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the erection of the Israelite commonwealth through Moses. By its benefit the
covenant of grace had not only the character of a promise and covenant, but a
more perfect and more testamentary form, being sanctioned in the blood of
victims (Exod. 24) as a symbol of Christ’s blood (Matt. 26). Before this, the
covenant had been restricted to certain persons and families. But through
Moses it was expanded objectively, since it was sanctioned with a whole and
numerous nation, and subjectively on the part of the covenant itself through
many solemnities by which it was established and enlarged: not only Passover
and other sacraments, but very many other things, especially sacrifices as
figures of Christ’s death, while new prophecies concerning Christ to be
revealed in his own time were continually added.

Thus after Abraham’s Seed had grown into a whole nation, God most clearly
disclosed himself to it through deliverance from Egypt, not only as אל שדי,
*El Shaddai* (Gen. 17:1), that is, omnipotent and entirely sufficient in
himself, lest anyone doubt the fulfillment of the promises, but also as יהוה,
*Jehovah* (Exod. 6:2, 3), who along with the eternity and immutability of his
essence had constancy and fidelity in fulfilling the promise concerning the
people’s deliverance from Egyptian captivity, which subsisted with its very
fulfillment.

[Marginal heading: The Author of the law’s promulgation.]

XXV. In this interval the *promulgation of the law* occurred. Its Author was
God, the one Legislator who can save and destroy (James 4:12), who has the
right of command over consciences and, by taking Israel as his people,
demonstrated in a singular manner that he was their God. Indeed, the Author
was the whole Holy Trinity, whose equal majesty and glory in the one Deity we
are bound to acknowledge and worship, but the Son in a peculiar manner as the
people’s Leader and Ruler. For the same Angel who appeared to Moses in the
bush afterward gave Moses the law (Acts 7:38).

It is solidly gathered that he was not a created angel from the fact that he
speaks in the person of Jehovah, which belongs to no angel or delegate, when
he calls himself the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Things are attributed
to him which cannot be said of a created angel, such as that he must not be
provoked because he will not pardon, since God’s name is in him (Exod. 23:20,
21). Hence elsewhere he is called the *Angel of the presence κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν,
preeminently* (Isa. 63:9), and the *Angel of the covenant* (Mal. 3:1). For
this reason the Israelites are said to have tempted Christ (1 Cor. 10:9),
namely, that Angel in the pillar of cloud and fire.

[Marginal heading: The ministering angels.]

XXVI. The less principal or instrumental causes were the created *angels*:
as *attendants*, who stood beside the supreme Legislator for his greater
glory; as *witnesses* of everything being done; and as *ministers*, through
whose intervention not only the thunders, lightning, and those terrible signs
which accompanied the promulgation of the law were produced, but also the
voice of God speaking was intelligibly formed. In this sense the law is
called λόγος λαληθεὶς δι᾽ Ἀγγέλων, *the word spoken through angels*, in Heb.
2:2. And in Gal. 3:19 it is said to have been διαταγεὶς δι᾽ Ἀγγέλων ἐν χειρὶ
μεσίτου, *ordained through angels in the hand of a Mediator*, that is,
commanded, arranged, enacted, and promulgated through the ministry of angels.

To this belong Stephen’s words in Acts 7:53, where he asserts that the
Israelites received the law εἰς διαταγὰς Ἀγγέλων, *in dispositions of angels*.
Either διαταγὰς, *dispositions*, are understood with some as troops of angels
drawn up in military order attending their Commander, which seems to be
intimated in Deut. 33:2 when “Jehovah is said to have come forth from Sinai
and to have come from myriads of holy ones,” and so forth; or, as others
maintain, διαταγὴ, *disposition*, is put here for *command, ordinance, and
sanction*, as in Rom. 13:2. The sense is then that the law was received
according to the ordinances of the angels to whom the Son of God had given it
to be promulgated, as to ministers and heralds.

To these was added the external and visible minister, *Moses*, who with
respect to the external dispensation could sustain the character of a
Mediator, but only one who

[Printed p. 200 | Internet Archive PDF 228 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

was typical, as he was a typical λυτρωτής, *redeemer* (Acts 7:35), as a
servant in God’s house (Heb. 3:2, 5), and a mere intermediary between God and
the people (Deut. 5:31).

[Marginal heading: The manner of legislation.]

XXVII. The *manner of legislation* was august and terrible, not only because
boundaries were placed around the mountain so that no one would have free
access, with the penalty of death pronounced upon one who dared to ascend the
mountain or touch any part of it (Exod. 19:13), but also from the horrible
signs and wonders which accompanied God’s presence on the mountain (Exod. 19;
Heb. 12). Not only the people but Moses himself trembled all over and openly
professed that he was ἔκφοβον καὶ ἔντρομον, *terrified and trembling* (Heb.
12:21). These things pertained to nothing other than indicating the
Legislator’s dreadful majesty and designating the nature of the law, through
which no access to God can be given to a sinner; indeed, in itself it is
nothing other than a ministry of death and condemnation.

[Marginal heading: Whether the Decalogue is the covenant of grace.]

XXVIII. From this it is gathered without obscurity what must be held about the
question raised here by some: *Did the Decalogue promulgated on Mount Sinai
contain nothing but the covenant of grace and its pure stipulation?* Since it
is agreed from what has just been said that the manner of this νομοθεσίας,
*legislation*, was terrifying, striking fear into consciences and removing
them from God’s sight by the rigor of its threats, everyone sees that this is
not the σχῆμα, *form*, or genius of the covenant of grace. That covenant
exhibits God as appeased toward us and calls sinners back to him by the
sweetness of the promises.

Add that the law contained in the Decalogue belongs to natural right and is
founded upon God’s justice. But the covenant of grace belongs to positive and
free right and is founded upon his εὐδοκίᾳ, *good pleasure*, and mercy. The
latter exhibits a Surety and promises the remission of sins and salvation in
his satisfaction; it not only demands obedience but also effects it. Yet in
the Decalogue there is no mention of a Surety or promise of salvation to be
given to sinners, but only the promise of life to those who do and the threat
of death to transgressors. Hence the law of works found in the Decalogue is
everywhere distinguished by Paul from the law of faith and the promise of
grace (Rom. 3:27; Gal. 3:17, 18). Just as the law is not from faith (Gal.
3:12), neither is faith from the law. There is so great a contrariety between
these two means that they are plainly ἀσύστατα, *incompatible*.

XXIX. We do not say this as though we wished to deny that the covenant of
grace was then contracted with the Israelites, which is expressly taught in
Deut. 5:2 and easily gathered both from its sanction in Exod. 19:5 and from
its solemn confirmation and ratification in Exod. 24, where mention is made
of the sprinkling of the victims’ blood and the elders’ access to God. Nor do
we suppose that the Decalogue had nothing in common with the covenant of
grace and was nothing but the covenant of works itself, renewed for the end
that it might call the people back to that covenant to seek life from it.

Since after sin the law was made weak through the flesh (Rom. 8:3), the way
to life through it was rendered wholly impossible and impassable for man.
Hence Paul testifies that the law was not given so that it could give life
(Gal. 3:21), or to abrogate the promise first given (v. 17), but “on account
of transgressions,” so that when sin was uncovered through it, the necessity
of grace might appear more clearly.

XXX. Thus the Decalogue is rightly said to have pertained to the covenant of
grace and, indeed, in a certain measure to have flowed from it, insofar as it
was promulgated by God’s counsel to serve that covenant: antecedently, like a
schoolmaster, by conviction of sin leading to it human beings struck with the
fear of death and despairing of themselves; consequently, like a norm, by
prescribing the rule of obedience and holiness which God required in the
covenant of grace. Hence it also

[Printed p. 201 | Internet Archive PDF 229 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

happened that God willed various rays of the covenant of grace to be in the
Decalogue, which would call the people back to it lest they wholly despair of
God’s grace from knowledge of their impotence and the terrible denunciation
of death. This is taught plainly enough both by its preface, where he
professes himself to be *the God of his people* who brought them out of
Egyptian slavery, and by the promises annexed to the second and fifth
commandments.

XXXI. Meanwhile, because God was pleased to administer the covenant of grace
in this age under a rigorous legal administration, both on account of the
condition of a people still in infancy and on account of the delay of
Christ’s advent and the satisfaction to be rendered through him, a twofold
σχέσις, *relation*, always had to obtain in this dispensation. The *first was
Legal* and more severe. Through a new promulgation of the law and the covenant
of works, with the intolerable yoke of ceremonies, God willed to express what
human beings owed and what they had to expect because their duty was not
performed. In this respect the law is called the killing Letter (2 Cor. 3:6)
and the handwriting against us (Col. 2:14), because through it human beings
professed themselves debtors and children of death by a declaration written
in their own blood in circumcision and in the blood of victims.

The *other was Evangelical* and gentler, insofar as “the law was a
schoolmaster to Christ” (Gal. 3:24) and contained “the shadow of things to
come” (Heb. 10:1), whose body and express image is in Christ. Hence as much
trouble and vexation as that administration brought through its former
σχέσιν, *relation*, so much consolation and joy it brought through the latter
to godly human beings who attended to it and sought beneath that husk and veil
the spiritual and evangelical truth which the Holy Spirit taught them through
revelation continually made clearer. God furnished more and greater aids
according to the time, not only through promises and oracles repeatedly
reiterated, but also through more definite types, shadows, and more perfect
figures. In these they had a more exact delineation of the thing itself and a
more precise πρόγραμμα, *outline*, of Christ, as though crucified before their
eyes in the types.

[Marginal heading: The twofold administration of the covenant under the Old
Testament. 1. The external, and its matter.]

XXXII. According to this twofold σχέσιν, *relation*, this administration can
be regarded either with respect to the *external administration* of legal
pedagogy or with respect to the *internal truth* of the evangelical promise
lying hidden beneath it. The *matter* of this external administration was the
threefold law—moral, ceremonial, and forensic. The first is fundamental; the
others are its appendages.

[Marginal heading: The form.]

The *form* was the compact added to that external dispensation. On God’s part
it was the promise of the land of Canaan and of rest and happiness in it and,
beneath the image of each, of heaven and the σαββατισμοῦ, *Sabbath rest*, in
it (Heb. 4:3, 9), or of eternal life according to the clause, *Do this and you
shall live*. On the people’s part it was the stipulation of obedience toward
the whole law, both *righteousness with perfection* (Deut. 27; Gal. 3) and
*personal* righteousness, with justification through it (Rom. 2). But this
stipulation in the Israelite covenant was only accidental, being added only
so that through its ἀδυναμίαν, *inability*, man might be led to renounce his
own righteousness and embrace the alien righteousness lying hidden beneath
the law.

[Marginal heading: The end.]

XXXIII. The *end* of this administration κατ᾽ ἄρσιν, *by negation*,
was not man’s *justification*. With respect to the moral law they could easily
know this from their *sins*, to which they were liable (Rom. 3:20); from the
*provocation* of their desires (Rom. 7); from the *impossibility* of fulfilling
the moral law (Rom. 8:3); and from the law’s *curse*, which it pronounced upon
the smallest transgression (Gal. 3:10). With respect to the ceremonial law
they could know it, 1. From the *nature of the ceremonies*, which was wholly
worldly and carnal and had nothing in common with the conscience. 2. From
their *repetition* and reiteration, which justification consisting in the
non-remembrance of sins does not admit (Heb. 8). 3. From their *condition*,
both because they were *mutable*, which would not have had a place if

[Printed p. 202 | Internet Archive PDF 230 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

a sinner could have been perfected through them, and because they functioned
as figures and ὑποδείγματα, *patterns*, of future goods (Heb. 10:1; Col.
2:17).

But its end κατὰ θέσιν, *by appointment*, was in itself, 1. That through it
the people might have an external form of religion approved by God. 2. That
it might serve the covenant of grace and Moses might serve Christ (Rom. 10:5,
6), so that through that administration the spiritual administration which
had to have its place alone in the New Testament might be represented. Here
God acts in the manner of excellent artists who, before applying their hand to
a work, commonly delineate the idea conceived in their mind with a rough
brush, ὡς ἐν τύπῳ, *as in an outline*, through σκιαγραφίαν, *shadow painting*.
3. That by its burdensomeness it might render the gospel, or Christ’s yoke,
sweeter (Matt. 11:28, 29).

[Marginal heading: Characteristics and effects. 1. The promise of the coming
Messiah.]

XXXIV. The *characteristics and effects* of this external administration were,
1. The *promise of the coming Messiah* and the delay of the satisfaction to
be rendered for us. Although Christ was continually promised in oracles and
graphically foreshadowed in various types, he was not to be incarnated during
that whole time, nor was the λύτρον, *ransom*, required for our redemption to
be paid. In this sense Peter says in 1 Pet. 1:10, 11, 12 that it was revealed
to the prophets that they administered not to themselves but to us the things
announced to us concerning Christ’s sufferings and the glory to follow.

Hence it is rightly said that the righteousness of the ages had not yet been
brought in (Dan. 9:24), and that sins remained without true expiation until
they were blotted out through Christ’s satisfaction. It was not that they
still rested upon believers, from whom they had been transferred to Christ
the Surety, but that they existed in the Surety’s accounts, resting upon him
to be discharged, and were not canceled until payment was rendered.

[Marginal heading: 2. Obscurity.]

XXXV. 2. *Obscurity*. Since the Sun of righteousness had not yet risen, only
the prophetic Word was given, like a lamp shining in a dark place (2 Pet.
1:19). The time of the Old Testament was the time of night, but that of the
New is the day which has shone upon us. Hence the ceremonies were like a veil
by which the mysteries were covered. The apostle teaches that this was
foreshadowed by the veil upon Moses’ face (2 Cor. 3:13), because Moses there
allegorically denotes the whole administration of the Old Testament. The veil
of the tabernacle and temple, barring the Israelites from entrance into and
sight of the sanctuary, tended to the same end.

[Marginal heading: 3. Servitude.]

XXXVI. 3. *Servitude*, or the servile condition through which the ancient
church was subjected to the law, which was a covenant of servants (Gal. 4).
With respect to those who sought salvation in the law, this servitude was
absolute and *total*, but with respect to those who looked to Christ in it,
only *relative*. Thus in Egypt the Israelites who did not think about
deliverance were slaves totally, but those who received it through faith were
indeed slaves in body, but not in mind. Hence Paul says in Gal. 4:1 that “an
infant does not differ from a slave,” and in Matt. 11:28 Christ calls them
πεφορτισμένους καὶ κοπιῶντας, *burdened and laboring*. These words intimate
labor and a servile yoke such as the Israelites once had in Egypt.

This servitude was all the more grievous both because, no differently than
Pharaoh’s taskmasters, it demanded fulfillment but supplied no powers for
fulfilling, and because it threatened penalties and even the divine curse for
the smallest transgression. This servitude was confirmed through the στοιχεῖα
τοῦ κόσμου, *elements of the world*, that is, ceremonies so called because of
their rudimentary character and imperfection, under which the Israelites were
kept. Because of that immense multitude of rites which they had to perform
under a severe threat, these ceremonies burdened their worshipers. In this
respect they are called the *yoke of servitude* (Gal. 5:1) and an ἀβάστακτον,
*unbearable*, burden (Acts 15:10).

[Marginal heading: And the spirit of servitude, Rom. 8:15.]

XXXVII. This servitude was accompanied by the *Spirit of servitude* adapted to
the servile administration (Rom. 8:15). Through the law and its threats daily
resounding in their ears, it ordinarily produced *servile fear* of God the
Judge, dread of punishment, and from this

[Printed p. 203 | Internet Archive PDF 231 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

a kind of continual anxiety and care more than it produced eagerness through
the doctrine of grace, which was preached more sparingly and obscurely. This
was not because believers were absolutely destitute of the Spirit of adoption,
as is stated afterward, but because he aroused motions suited to that
condition in which an infant heir differed little from a servant.

[Marginal heading: 4. Rigor and severity.]

XXXVIII. Hence arose, 4. *Rigor and severity* on account of legal sermons
continually intervening and promises of grace repeated more rarely and
obscurely. There was also *coercion* by which they were driven to duty more by
fear of punishment than by love of God and righteousness, as Moses continually,
like a harsh master, not so much persuaded obedience with his rod as extorted
it. To this belongs the terrible apparatus under which the law was enacted,
by which not only the people but Moses himself is also said to have been
terrified (Heb. 12:21). Nor was that rigor and terror without reason. The
stubbornness of Israel, to which Moses and the other prophets so often
reproached a stiff neck and adamantine heart, could scarcely be subdued
otherwise.

[Marginal heading: 5. Purity of the flesh.]

XXXIX. 5. To this administration belonged *external purity of the flesh*, or
immunity from carnal guilt. Those cleansed by the law’s institution could
freely participate in public assemblies and perform public worship together
with others. Such purity was obtained through the observation of ceremonies
(Heb. 9:13), and to it are opposed *purity of conscience* and immunity from
the spiritual guilt of sin.

To this is added *immunity from temporal punishments in the earthly forum*.
With respect to sins not committed with a high hand, it was granted through
sacrifices, which, without regard either to repentance or faith, restored
human beings to their rank and rendered them immune from every forensic
penalty. But as for *spiritual punishment in the heavenly forum*, immunity
from it could not be obtained from animal sacrifices as such, because then
there would have been no proportion between moral guilt and satisfaction. It
could be obtained from them only as figures and types.

[Marginal heading: Adjuncts. 1. Weakness.]

XL. Finally, there were various *adjuncts*. First, *weakness and imperfection*.
This administration did not have the true λύτρον, *ransom*, for sin, but only
its type, nor the accompanying power of the Spirit to subdue the flesh and
supply powers to fulfill the law. Thus through it considered as such, no one
could be causally saved—that is, either justified (Acts 13; Heb. 10:1, 2),
sanctified (Heb. 9:14, 15), or obtain life—because it was a ministry of
condemnation and death (2 Cor. 3:6, 7; Heb. 7:18). For this reason Paul
testifies that this administration was not ἄμεμπτον, *faultless* (Heb. 8:7),
namely, διὰ τὸ αὐτῆς ἀσθενὲς καὶ ἀνωφελές, *because of its weakness and
uselessness* (Heb. 7:18).

[Marginal heading: 2. Mutability.]

XLI. Second, *mutability and abrogation*. It comprehended earthly, carnal,
transitory things, corruptible by their own nature, which consisted only in
food, drink, various washings, and δικαιώμασι σαρκὸς, *carnal ordinances*.
They could not sanctify the conscience and were not to remain in force
forever, but were imposed μέχρι καιροῦ διορθώσεως, *until the time of
reformation* (Heb. 9:9, 10). Hence an ἀθέτησις, *abrogation*, had to occur on
account of its imperfection (Heb. 7:18), especially when a more perfect
administration succeeded it, just as when sunlight arrives there can no
longer be a place for candles or burning torches.

[Marginal heading: 3. Glory and splendor.]

XLII. Third, the *glory and splendor* with which this administration was
instituted. It shone especially in the magnificent apparatus and illustrious
miracles with which God willed the law’s promulgation to be accompanied.
Hence Paul calls this ministry glorious (2 Cor. 3:7, 9). But this glory was
both *terrifying*, striking fear

[Printed p. 204 | Internet Archive PDF 232 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

not only into the people but into Moses himself, who for this reason is said
to have been ἔντρομος, *trembling* (Heb. 12:21), and *mutable* and transitory,
being καταργουμένη, *done away with*, and made obsolete (2 Cor. 3:11). This
appeared in the splendor of Moses’ face, which had to fade (v. 7).

[Marginal heading: 2. The internal dispensation. It contained Christ;]

XLIII. Such was the external dispensation of the Old Testament. But the
*internal* dispensation lying hidden beneath it pertains to the substance of
the covenant of grace and the evangelical promise. It too was administered in
different ways and through various degrees. On God’s part it contained
*Christ* himself, the promised foundation of the covenant in the oracles and
lying hidden beneath the types of the ceremonies, together with his
sufferings, crucifixion, death, and glory, though more obscurely and under
cover. Hence the gospel is said to have been of old a hidden mystery (Rom.
16:25), because it was obscurely proposed beneath various veils of figures,
yet in such a way that it could be seen through them, as Moses did (Heb.
11:26).

[Marginal heading: And temporal and spiritual promises.]

It also contained *promises*, both temporal and spiritual, which the covenant
formula, *I am your God*, and so forth, includes in itself. The temporal
promises were the inheritance of the land of Canaan and earthly blessings
recounted at length in Deut. 28, which were promised there to faithful
worshipers of God less principally and secondarily because of the disposition
of the Israelite people, still in infancy and attached more to carnal things
than spiritual ones. But the spiritual promises were primary and principal,
as absolutely necessary for salvation.

XLIV. Although this *revelation* of grace was more obscure and sparing with
respect to its *mode*, since it was wholly *enigmatic* and *specular*, such
that Christ could be seen only beneath a veil, like Moses of old (2 Cor. 3:7),
it was nevertheless sufficient with respect to its *substance*. Hence the
knowledge and faith of the ancients rested upon it. Although it differed with
respect to circumstances and mode and can be called implicit and confused in
this sense—a thing is known through it, but not so as to be known according
to everything by which it is distinguished from other things—it was not
different with respect to substance.

We intend this to be understood concerning the *state* of the Old Testament
church, not concerning *this or that individual*: Abraham, Moses, David, and
others who were endowed with a richer knowledge of divine mysteries. Their
faith saved them no less than faith under the New Testament saves us, because
Christ saves not insofar as he is known perfectly and according to all his
circumstances, but insofar as he is truly and seriously apprehended.

[Marginal heading: Of what kind it was. 1. Remission of sins.]

XLV. To these spiritual promises belong, 1. *Remission of sins and
justification*. Just as justification considered causally and in itself is
denied to the law, so it is attributed to faith in Christ (Acts 13:39; Rom.
4:7) and claimed for the fathers, such as Abraham, David, and others (Ps. 32;
Rom. 4), although it was felt less on account of the intervening cloud of the
law and the payment not yet actually rendered.

[Marginal heading: 2. Adoption.]

2. *Adoption*, which is attributed to the Israelites not only externally
(Rom. 9:4), but also *internally*, without which the former would be useless
(Gal. 4:2). Yet it was joined with a servile condition on account of the
minority of the children. They were children who could and ought to invoke
God as Father (Job 34:36; Isa. 63:16), but minor children subjected by the
Father to the rod and stripes of schoolmasters (Gal. 4:2, 3), who were not
admitted to such familiarity with the Father that they might penetrate the
inmost things of his counsel.

[Marginal heading: 3. Sanctification.]

3. *Sanctification*, which is everywhere commanded and promised under the Old
Testament (Lev. 26; Isa. 1, and so forth).

[Marginal heading: 4. The gift of the Spirit. Ezek. 36; Jer. 31; Isa. 43.]

4. The *gift of the Spirit*, which was included in the promise made to
Abraham (Gal. 3:13, 14) and is promised more than once elsewhere (Isa. 44;
Ezek. 36; Zech. 12:10). Yet, speaking generally about the state of the whole

[Printed p. 205 | Internet Archive PDF 233 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

church, the Spirit did not have the same character then as under the New
Testament, whether *intensively* with respect to *degree* or *extensively*
with respect to *gifts*, either *ordinary* or *extraordinary*, which with
respect to the church are proper to the New Testament. For the outpouring of
the Spirit was owed to the glory of Christ after he had entered heaven (John
7:39). Such as the character of the covenant was, such was the Spirit.

He was, namely, 1. The *Spirit of illumination*, but not illumination as clear
and distinct as in the New Testament. It was therefore joined with ignorance
of various circumstances and of the mode, as appears in believers and the
apostles themselves in Christ’s time before the outpouring of the Holy Spirit.
2. The *Spirit of adoption* or *sonship*. Otherwise, Old Testament believers
could not have known infallibly that they were children of God, which they
nevertheless knew when they invoked God as their Father (Isa. 63). But their
adoption was not purely such; it was mixed with servitude and thus was not an
adoption of full emancipation. For this reason he is called the *Spirit of
servitude* in Rom. 8:15, not absolutely, but relatively, insofar as through
him believers were rendered certain of their adoption, though not fully with
respect to sense and use. 3. The *Spirit of consolation and hope*, but in the
same manner, insofar as both were joined with some fear and terror of death
and of the curse threatened by the law.

[Marginal heading: 5. Resurrection. 6. Eternal life.]

5. The *resurrection of the dead*, which Christ deduces from the covenant
formula. 6. *Eternal life*, which was also included in that formula, as the
apostle gathers it from the formula in Heb. 11:10, not to recount the various
prophetic oracles in which it is promised, discussed above in the Question on
the unity of the covenant.

[Marginal heading: Of what kind the New dispensation is.]

XLVI. Thus far the old dispensation. The new succeeds it, which is the
administration of the covenant without the law and ceremonies after Christ
has been exhibited. It is called new not with respect to the covenant’s
substance, which is the same in each, but, 1. With respect to circumstances
and mode, since it is manifested without the *veil and law*. In this manner
the covenant appeared as new, or renewed. It also proposes Christ not as one
to be exhibited but as exhibited. In this manner it can be called new both
*intensively* with respect to the degree of light and *extensively* with
respect to its breadth, which extends indiscriminately to all nations. 2.
With respect to the excellence and glory of this dispensation, which is far
greater than the old (2 Cor. 3), just as elsewhere “new” is taken for
excellent and outstanding (Rev. 5; Ps. 33). 3. With respect to its perpetual
duration, through which it remains, as it were, always new, since things which
must end are called old.

[Marginal heading: It consists, 1. In the incarnation of the Messiah. 2. In
the abrogation of the ceremonial law. 3. In the outpouring of the Spirit. 4.
And so forth.]

XLVII. It consists, 1. In the *advent of the Messiah* and his manifestation in
the flesh, and through him the fulfillment of the whole law: namely, its
ceremonies and prophecies and thus all the righteousness prescribed by God in
the law. 2. In the abrogation of all ceremonies and the whole legal
dispensation by which the covenant of grace was veiled. 3. In the *outpouring
of the Holy Spirit*, both *visible* with respect to extraordinary gifts and
*invisible* and perpetual with respect to the ordinary gifts necessary for
each believer’s salvation. This is placed as a benefit proper to the New
Testament (Joel 2): first with respect to the extraordinary outpouring, which,
although it had a place in certain persons in the Old Testament, did not
pertain to the church because such persons were very few; then with respect
to the ordinary outpouring, which is both *intensively* greater with respect
to the degree of light, confidence, and consolation, and *extensively* with
respect to its *parts*, because it teaches all things (John 16:13; 1 John
2:20), and with respect to its *object*, because it teaches everyone from the
greatest to the least (Heb. 8:11).

XLVIII. 4. In the *calling of all nations*, which is proper to the New
Testament with respect to the *full knowledge* of the circumstances and mode.
This was not perceived by the ancients, who thought the nations would come to

5. In *adoption*, which in the New Testament is such not only with respect to
the *thing*, as it is in the Old Testament, but also with respect to its *mode,
condition, and effects*. 1. In the New Testament it is proposed as acquired
through Christ (John 1:12). 2. It is *purely such*, apart from pedagogy,
servitude, and fear, and therefore belongs not to infancy but to adult age and
emancipation, joined with the highest παῤῥησίᾳ, *boldness*, and confidence
(Rom. 8:15).

6. In *liberty*, not only *spiritual*, which also existed in the Old
Testament, but also *external*, through which we are free from the legal
ceremonies (Col. 2:20, 21, 22) and therefore are servants of Christ alone, not
of human beings (Gal. 5:1).

7. In *sanctification*, which is greater in the New Testament with respect to
the greater *illumination* of the intellect; its *mode*, which is not external
and ceremonial and therefore common even to hypocrites, but internal; its
*efficacy*, which is greater on account of the greater measure and power of
the Spirit; and its *motives*, which are far stronger, being drawn especially
from Christ’s example.

8. In *eternal life*, which belongs to the New Testament, 1. With respect to
its *actual acquisition* made through Christ’s blood, which in the Old
Testament existed only *morally*. 2. With respect to Christ’s *actual entrance
as man into heaven* as the true sanctuary, into which he entered as forerunner,
not only for himself but also for us (Heb. 6:20). 3. With respect to its far
more illustrious *manifestation* (2 Tim. 1:10), which was more obscure in the
Old Testament (Heb. 9:8). Hence confidence and hope were far greater, all fear
of death having been removed, now penetrating within the veil (Heb. 6:19). 4.
With respect to the *more frequent promises* which regard eternal life alone,
the promises which regard this life and its longevity having been removed;
the latter were far more frequent in the Old Testament.

perfection. 3. Perpetuity.]

XLIX. The adjuncts of this dispensation are, 1. *Posteriority in time*, not
with respect to substance, which was the same from the beginning, but with
respect to circumstances and mode. 2. *Dignity and perfection*, through which
it is far more dignified and perfect in comparison with the old, and of a
kind befitting mature age. 3. *Perpetuity*, because this administration is
the final one until Christ’s last advent, in which it will be changed into the
immediate and direct communication of God through which God will be all in
all.
