ON THE TRINITY

Thus far we have treated the divine essence as it is considered in its unity.
We must now treat the Trinity of persons. These have been the errors
concerning the Trinity.

In the apostles’ time Ebion and Cerinthus denied Christ’s divinity and said
that Christ was a mere man born of Mary and Joseph. According to Eusebius,
book 3, chapter 27 of the *Ecclesiastical History*, John wrote his Gospel
against them. Carpocra—

[1620 Tome I | col. 209 | BSB frame 00121]

—tes, from whom the Carpocratians took their name, revived the same heresy in
the year of Christ 137. He maintained that Christ was born from both sexes and
was a mere man. See Augustine, *On Heresies*, chapter 7, who asserts that this
heresy belonged to Artemon around the year of Christ 200.

Arius, an Alexandrian presbyter under Constantine the Great, taught that God
had not always been Father, but that the Son was created from nothing before
all created things, and that, just as the Son was created by the Father, so the
Holy Spirit was created by the Son. See Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*.

The Eunomians—named after Eunomius, bishop of Cyzicus, around the year of Christ
360—taught that Christ was not only *heteroousios*, of a different substance
from the Father, but *anomoios*, unlike him in every respect; they also taught
that the Holy Spirit was created by the Son and unlike him in every respect.
They were contrary to the Arians, for the Arians said that Christ was
*homoios*, like the Father.

The Samosatenians—so called after Samosatenus, bishop of Antioch—taught around
the year of Christ 270 that there was only a human nature in Christ and that
he was *psilon anthropon*, a mere man. They taught that “Word” in John 1 did
not signify a distinct person, but only the Father’s deliberation or decree
concerning creation, redemption, and so forth; that it was a spoken and
vanishing word; and that the Holy Spirit was nothing other than God’s
non-subsistent energy. See book 7, chapters 27 and 30 of his *Ecclesiastical
History*, and Augustine, *On Heresies*, chapter 44.

The Sabellians, Noetians, Praxeans, and Hermogenians wrongly abolished the
distinction of the persons and said that the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit were
the same person. According to various dispensations and offices, this person
is called now Father, now Son, and now Holy Spirit: Father in heaven, Son on
earth, and Holy Spirit in the powers of creatures. They said that the same one
was visible and invisible, mortal and immortal, passible and impassible. Hence
they were called Patri—

[1620 Tome I | col. 210 | BSB frame 00121]

—passians, because they said that God the Father suffered. See Augustine,
*On Heresies*, chapter 41.

Photinus, bishop of Cyrene,[^p23-1] renewed the heresy of Samosatenus around
the year of Christ 336. He taught that Christ was born only from Mary and was
a mere man. See Socrates, book 2, chapter 18 of the *Ecclesiastical History*.

Macedonius, bishop of Constantinople, who forcibly occupied the see after
driving out his predecessor Paul, taught around the year of Christ 345 that
the Son was like the Father in every respect, but that the Holy Spirit had
nothing in common with the Father and Son and was not Lord but servant, not
God but a creature. His followers were called *pneumatomachoi*, fighters
against the Spirit. See Augustine, *On Heresies*, chapter 52.

In order to avoid Sabellius’s heresy, the Tritheites said that the Father, Son,
and Holy Spirit were three Gods distinct in essence.

The Muslims confess that there is one God, but deny that there are three
persons in the divine essence. They say that Christ is a great prophet, not
God or the Son of God.

Around the year of Christ 1530, Servetus renewed and enlarged the heresy of
Samosatenus.

Since these heresies therefore seem to abolish either the plurality of the
persons or their unity, two things must be treated:

1. The distinction of the persons.

2. The *homoousia*, or consubstantiality, of the persons.

The names “person” and “Trinity” do not occur syllable for syllable in
Scripture, but the realities themselves do. The following disputation will
show that the reality of person occurs in Scripture. Hebrews 1:3 also contains
a name signifying the same reality, namely *hypostasis*. The Fathers used this
word in disputation against Arius and other heretics until serious disputes
arose about its interpretation, some construing it as essence and others as
person. The Latins therefore used the name *persona* in its place and the
Greeks the name *prosopon*. The reason

[1620 Tome I | col. 211 | BSB frame 00122]

they chose these terms rather than *hypostasis* was that the word *hypostasis*
has a wider scope than the name “person” and is also said of
things not endowed with reason.

The following disputation will likewise show that the reality of the Trinity
occurs in Scripture. First John 5:8 says that there are three. Where there are
three, there is a trinity, just as where there is one, there is unity, and
where there is God, there is deity.

A person in the Godhead is a subsistence in the divine essence, possessing in
himself by nature that entire divine essence, and distinguished from the other
persons to whom he is related by an incommunicable property. He is called a
subsistence because he is not an accident, thought, decree, vanishing sound,
quality, or created motion. He is said to be distinguished by an
incommunicable property so that person may be distinguished from essence,
which is common to several. Or it is defined in this way: a person is *tropos
hypaxeos*, a mode of existing in the divine essence.

Two things must be maintained here:

1. How the essence differs from the persons.

2. How the persons differ from one another.

The essence differs from the persons not as one being from another being, nor
by a real distinction, nor formally, nor by reason alone, but in mode of
existing. This occurs in two ways:

1. Essence signifies what the persons are absolutely and by their nature,
whereas person signifies what they are relatively and according to their mode
of existing.

2. The essence is communicable, but the person is incommunicable.

Human nature, or humanity, is predicated equally of several individuals and
is common to them. A person, however, is what each one is through his own
singularity and properties, distinct from every other, and is therefore
incommunicable. That nature is not an imagi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 212 | BSB frame 00122]

—nary thing existing only in the mind, nor something separated from
individuals; rather, in each individual it is that through which he subsists.
So in God the divine essence is not something outside the Father, Son, and
Holy Spirit, nor some concrete whole in which the three are contained. It is
entirely in the Father, entirely in the Son, and entirely in the Holy Spirit.
Yet this is far different from the way human essence is in us. Human essence
is in us in such a way that we could be called *homoiousioi*, of similar
substance, rather than *homoousioi*, of the same substance; but in each divine
person the entire infinite and altogether identical essence exists.

Two things follow from this:

1. A person is nothing other than the entire divine essence with his own
incommunicable property. Some therefore wrongly maintain that the persons are
nothing other than properties, since a person is the divine essence with a
mode of subsisting.

2. Therefore the essence is not communicated to the persons as universals are
communicated to particulars, because God’s essence is most simply one, whereas
what is universal includes potency and consequently imperfection. God’s
essence, however, is purest and most perfect act. Nor is it communicated as an
accident to its subjects, because God’s essence is not an accident and admits
no accident; nor as a cause to the many effects that it produces, because the
persons are not effects of the essence; nor as effects from causes, because
the persons do not differ essentially from the essence;[^p23-2] nor as a whole
to its parts, because God’s essence is indivisible act and therefore cannot
properly be said to be a whole or to have parts; nor as a container to its
contents, for that which contains another thing is finite. Rather, the essence
is common to the persons as a thing is common to its modes which exist in the
thing.

The persons are truly distinguished from one another in two ways: internally,
or externally through works *ad extra*, which make the person manifest in and
through creatures. Although these works are common to all the persons, the
persons are distinguished by their mode of acting: the Father acts from
himself, the Son from the Father, and the Holy

[1620 Tome I | col. 213 | BSB frame 00123]

Spirit from both.

Internally, a person is distinguished by incommunicable properties, which the
scholastics call works *ad intra* and divided works, because they are not
common to the three persons.

The Father’s incommunicable property in relation to the Son is to beget. He
himself is *agennetos*, that is, unbegotten. This generation is eternal, Psalm
2:7, Proverbs 8:14, Micah 5:5, John 17:5, and Colossians 1:15. Therefore the
Father is from himself, not from another, and is the fountain of deity. The
Father’s personal property in relation to the Holy Spirit is to send him
forth, of which below.

The Son’s incommunicable property in relation to the Father is to be begotten,
and that eternally by the Father, by receiving his entire essence. The Son is
therefore *homoousios*, of the same substance, and *synaidios*, coeternal. The
Father’s essence does not beget the Son’s essence; rather, person begets person.
With respect to the essence, the Son is *autotheos*, God of himself, for the
Son is not born from the Father’s essence but from the Father. The Nicene Creed
therefore correctly says that the Son is from the Father as light from light.
He is only-begotten, John 1:14 and 3:16; proper to the Father, Romans 8:32; and
the image of the invisible God, Hebrews 1:3 and John 14:9. By nature he is one
with the Father, John 1:30[^p23-3] and 17:9; he is equal to the Father and is in
the Father, and the Father is in him, John 14:9–10.

It follows from these things that Christ is not called Son with respect to
creation or adoption, but with respect to eternal generation, and that he is
*homoousios* with the Father and equal to him in every respect, John 5:18 and
19:7. Nor is the Father prior to the Son in time, for the Father was never
without the Son, and Colossians 1:15 is not contrary to this. He is prior only
in the order by which some persons exist from others. The Son’s property in
relation to the Holy Spirit is to send him forth, of which below.

The Holy Spirit’s incommunicable property in relation to the Father and the Son
is *ekporeusis*, procession. It is such that, by receiving the entire essence
of the Father and the Son, he exists from both, so that the Father and the Son
breathe forth the Holy Spirit from themselves not by begetting him, but by
communicating the entire essence.

[1620 Tome I | col. 214 | BSB frame 00123]

Consequently, the Holy Spirit is *homoousios* and *synaidios*, consubstantial
and coeternal. Hence he is the Spirit of Jehovah, Isaiah 61:1 and 63:3;[^p23-4]
the Spirit of God and from God, 1 Corinthians 2:11; the Spirit of the Father,
Matthew 10:20; and the Spirit of Christ and of the Son, Romans 8:9, Galatians
4:6, Philippians 1:19, and 1 Peter 1:11. Hence the Spirit is also said to
receive all things from Christ, John 16:13–14, and not only the Father but also
the Son is said to send and give him, John 14:26, 16:7, and 20:22. Therefore
those who have asserted that the Holy Spirit proceeds only from the Father and
not from the Son are in error. Hebrews 9:14 makes clear that the Holy Spirit
proceeds from eternity. The nature of the essence itself also proves that the
entire divine essence is communicated to the Holy Spirit. The divine essence
is most perfect, most simple, eternal, immense, and free of all change,
passion, division, multiplication, increase, and decrease. Consequently, no
partition or division can be imagined in it. Therefore, if the divine essence
is communicated to the Holy Spirit, it is communicated in its entirety.

Justin correctly expressed these mysteries of generation and procession in
his *Exposition of the Faith*: “Just as the Son is from the Father,” he says,
“so also is the Holy Spirit, except that they differ *to tropo hyparxeos*—as we
now speak, in person or hypostasis. For the Son shines as light from light, but
*gennetos*, by generation. The Holy Spirit also comes forth as light from
light, yet not *gennetos*, by generation, but *ekporeutos*, by procession.”

How the Son’s generation and the Holy Spirit’s procession differ, however, and
how they occur is not expressed in the Word of God. The holy Fathers therefore
confessed that they did not know these mysteries. Augustine says in book 1,
chapter 14, *Against Maximinus the Arian*, “Who can explain what the difference
is between being born and proceeding?” Damascene says in book 1, chapter 10,
“We say that generation and procession differ, but what the mode of the
difference is, we do not know at all.” Finally, the procession of the Holy
Spirit occurs not only

[1620 Tome I | col. 215 | BSB frame 00124]

as a reality in the Scriptures, as was proved above, but also in so many words,
John 15:16.[^p23-5]

With these things established, it must be demonstrated, first, that there are
three distinct persons in the one most simple divine essence. This is proved
by the following arguments.

Argument 1. In the Old Testament a distinction and Trinity of persons is
indicated in the one essence: Genesis 1:2, 26; 3:22; 11:7; 19:24; Psalm 110:1;
Proverbs 8:22; 30:4; Isaiah 6:3; 42:1; 61:7; and so forth. Therefore, the
conclusion follows.[^p23-9]

2. In the New Testament the distinction and Trinity of persons is indicated:
Matthew 3:16; Mark 10;[^p23-6] Luke 3:21; Matthew 28:19; John 5:19; 14:13;
1:1, 23; chapter 17:8; chapter 8:16, 27; 1 John 5:7; and so forth, by which the
Son is proved to be from the Father. Likewise John 14:16, 20; John 15:26;
chapter 16:13; chapter 21:8; 2 Corinthians 13:13; Luke 1:35; Romans 1:2, 3;
4:8, 11; Galatians 4:6; Ephesians 1:17; 2:18; 3:16; Titus 3:5; 1 John 2:22, 27;
chapter 4:13; chapter 5:8, by which the Holy Spirit is proved to be another
than the Father and the Son.

3. Scripture introduces God speaking in the plural number about God as about
another person: Genesis 1:26; 3:22; 11:7; 19:24; 14:26.[^p23-7] Therefore, the
conclusion follows.

4. Those who are named in Scripture as two and three distinct ones are
distinct persons from one another. But the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are so
named: John 8:18; Matthew 3:16–17; John 15:26; 5:37; 1 John 5:8; and so forth.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

5. Those who differ from one another by an incommunicable property are
distinct. But the three persons in the divine essence differ from one another
by an incommunicable property, Psalm 2:7, John 15:26, and so forth. No one can
be begotten by himself, proceed from himself, or be his own father. Rather,
one is the Father who begets, another the Son who is begotten, and another the
Holy Spirit who proceeds from both.[^p23-8] Therefore, the conclusion follows.

6. One who receives his being from another is necessarily distinguished from
the one from whom he receives his being; otherwise, one and the same would be
giver and receiver. But the Son receives his being from the Father, and the
Holy Spirit from both: Proverbs 8:22;

[1620 Tome I | col. 216 | BSB frame 00124]

Psalm 2:7; John 5:26; Colossians 1:15; Hebrews 1:3; 1 John 5:20; John 5:20;
John 15:26.

7. Those who are called others in relation to one another are distinct from
one another. But the three persons are called others in relation to one
another, John 5:32 and 14:16. It must be noted, however, that the Father, Son,
and Holy Spirit are called “another”, not “another thing”. “Another thing”
signifies a distinction in essence, which does not exist among the persons,
according to Christ in John 10:30; “another,” however, signifies distinction
in person.

8. Those who are said to be with another, with, from, and in others, are
distinct from those from whom, with whom, with, from, and in whom they are
said to be. But these things are said of the persons in relation to one
another: Proverbs 8:22; John 1:1 and verses 14, 18; John 14:10; and so forth.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

9. Different and distinct actions are everywhere attributed in Scripture to
the persons. Therefore they are distinct from one another and truly subsist in
the divine essence.

10. That which is supremely and infinitely good cannot fail to communicate
itself in a supreme and infinite manner. Since God cannot communicate himself
to creatures in this manner, distinct persons must necessarily be posited in
the divine essence, between whom this communication takes place.

Objections

1. If the persons are distinct from the essence and from one another, then
there will be four really distinct things in God. But the latter is absurd.
Therefore, so is the former.

Response. The proposition must be distinguished. If the persons were really
distinct from the essence and from one another, the proposition would be
true. But they are not distinguished as things from things. Rather, the three
persons are distinguished from the essence as modes of a thing from the
thing, and from one another as distinct modes. A mode of a thing does not
constitute another thing really distinct from it.

Counterinstance. But absolutely they are one, and relatively they are three;
therefore they are four.

Response. The consequence is denied, because that absolute reality is not
distinguished in reality from those three relative ones, but only in reason.
Furthermore, the essence is those three, and those three are

[1620 Tome I | col. 217 | BSB frame 00125]

the essence. Although the essence does not beget, this does not establish a
real distinction, but a distinction in the mode of the thing or of reason.
Thus man and humanity are one thing, and yet man, not humanity, begets.

Counterinstance. Relations are accidents. Therefore they are distinct things.

Response. The antecedent is denied. They are not in truth absolute accidents,
but only mental ones, and thus are not properly things but relations of
things. Moreover, these relations are in God otherwise than in creatures:
namely, most simply, eternally, and infinitely, and therefore not as accidents.

Counterinstance. The essence is the fountain of the persons, and the relations
emanate from it. But a fountain and its streams are really distinguished.
Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition is denied, because persons are produced by persons.
According to Augustine, book 4, chapter 20 of *On the Trinity*, the Father is
called the fountain and principle of deity in relation to the persons because
the Son and Holy Spirit are from the Father, whereas the Father is from
himself. Moreover, properly speaking, relations, precisely as relations, are
not produced, but follow of themselves.

2. The Father is unbegotten, whereas the Son is begotten; therefore they are
not one God. The consequence is proved: otherwise one God will be begotten and
unbegotten, which implies a contradiction.

Response. 1. The consequence is denied.

2. The word “God” must be distinguished. If “God” is taken for the essence,
the consequence is denied, because generation does not apply to the essence
as such. But if it is restricted to a particular person, there will be no
contradiction, because for one person to be begotten and another unbegotten do
not contradict one another.

Counterinstance. Generable and ingenerable differ as corruptible and
incorruptible do. But these differ in their whole genus. Therefore begotten
and unbegotten also differ, and consequently the persons differ in reality.

Response. Generable and ingenerable differ in reality only

[1620 Tome I | col. 218 | BSB frame 00125]

when the nature of that which is generated is produced through generation,
but not when it is only communicated by one to another. Thus the Son is called
begotten not because his nature is begotten, but because it was communicated
through generation. Therefore the Son is distinguished from the Father not
in nature, but only in the mode of possessing the nature.

Counterinstance. Begotten and unbegotten are either accidents or substances.
They are not accidents, because there are no accidents in God. If they are
substances, then the Father and Son differ substantially.

Response. Begotten and unbegotten in God are neither substances nor accidents,
for unbegotten is only the negation of generation. Begotten, however,
signifies a relation, and relations are not proper accidents, as stated above.
Although among creatures they are accepted as accidents, in God they are not
accidents, because there is no accident in God.

3. When the Son is seen, the Father is seen, John 14:9. Therefore the person of
the Father is not distinct from the person of the Son.

Response. The consequence is denied. In the cited passage Christ teaches only
that he manifests the Father’s person by word and deed.

4. The Son is in the Father and the Father in the Son, John 14:10. Therefore
the Father and the Son are one person.

Response. The consequence is denied, because unity of essence, not of person,
is signified in the cited passage.

5. The Father’s wisdom and power are not persons really distinct from him, but
are the Father himself, like goodness, mercy, and so forth. But the Son is the
Father’s wisdom, 1 Corinthians 1:30, and the Holy Spirit is his power, Luke
1:35. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation, because in the proposition
“power” and “might” signify the essence absolutely, whereas in the assumption
they signify the persons of the Son and Holy Spirit relatively.

[1620 Tome I | col. 219 | BSB frame 00126]

6. The entire divine essence is the Father, but the Son is the divine essence.
Therefore the Son is the Father.

Response. Although the proposition seems to be universal, it is particular.
But if it is universal, and if this is its sense—“Whoever is the divine essence
is the Father,” or, “Whatever can be called the divine essence is the Father”—it
is false, because just as the entire divine essence is in the Father, so it is
also in the Son and the Holy Spirit. Furthermore, if the proposition is
understood exclusively—“The entire divine essence is the Father alone”—it is
false. The same response applies to the assumption.

7. Jehovah is the Trinity, but the Father is Jehovah. Therefore the Father is
the Trinity, and consequently the persons are not distinct.

Response. It is an inference from particulars. If the proposition is
understood universally—“Whatever is Jehovah is the Trinity”—it is false,
because each person is rightly called Jehovah.

8. An image is not a *hyphistamenon*, a subsistent thing, but the Son is an
image, Colossians 1:15. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation. In the proposition “image”
signifies an accident, but in the assumption it is taken substantially.

9. If the Father gave the Son a hypostasis, he produces it either from the
divine essence, from his own person, or from nothing. But none of these can be
said. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The assumption is denied, for person begot person.

Counterinstance. If person produces person, then the Son’s *hypostasis* was in
the Father, and so there was a double person: one of the Father and another of
the Son.

Response. The connection is denied, because it is not true even in human
affairs. Furthermore, even if it were true, the consequence would hold only
for things generated in time. But Christ’s generation was from eternity, and
therefore the distinction of the persons was also eternal.

Counterinstance. The Father’s subsistence is indivi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 220 | BSB frame 00126]

—sible. Therefore, if the Son’s person is from the Father’s person, there would
be one person of both.

Response. If the person were communicated, the consequence would hold. But in
the generation of the Son the essence, not the person, is communicated.

10. A person is either finite or infinite. If finite, he is not God; if
infinite, then there are three infinite things. Therefore, the conclusion
follows.

Response. A person, as person, is neither finite nor infinite; the essence is.
Since there is only one essence, there is only one infinite.

11. The same thing cannot constitute different persons, but intellect and will
in God are the same thing. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. The proposition is denied, because the same thing can constitute
distinct modes, and the persons are modes of the essence.

12. Subsistence either belongs to the intrinsic nature of the essence or it
does not. If it does, then the essence is incommunicable; if it does not, then
there are four subsistences.

Response. 1. The consequence is denied. Although subsistence belongs to the
essence, the essence is nevertheless communicable to its modes. It follows
only that the essence is incommunicable to inferiors.

2. Nor does it follow that there are four subsistences, because the three
persons are not three existences of God, but three modes of one existence.

13. He who is invoked through a Mediator is alone the true God. But the Father
alone is invoked through a mediator. Therefore, the conclusion follows.

Response. Invocation must be distinguished, for one kind is absolute and
another relative. Absolute invocation is made to God by reason of the essence,
and in this way we invoke all the persons.
