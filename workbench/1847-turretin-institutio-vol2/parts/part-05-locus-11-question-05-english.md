## QUESTION V

On the division of the precepts of the Decalogue.

Are four precepts rightly assigned to the first table, and six to the second?

It is affirmed.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

I. It is not asked whether the precepts are more or fewer than ten, for this
is agreed among everyone. Hence the law is given the name *Decalogue* because
of its ten words. Nor is it asked whether these ten precepts are distributed
between two tables, for this also is admitted. Rather, the question is how
they should be distributed, and how many and which ones pertain to each table,
for the opinions of the doctors vary on this point.

II. Some assign five precepts to each table, as does Josephus in book 3 of
*Antiquities*, chapter 4: “Moses brings forth two tablets containing ten
inscribed precepts, five on each.” Philo follows him in *On the Decalogue*, p.
477, as does Irenaeus among the fathers, book 2, chapter 42. But this is wrong,
because Christ includes the fifth precept under the second table (Matt. 19:18),
and Paul, in Ephesians 6:2, calls it the first commandment of the second table
accompanied by a promise. For in the first table a promise is attached to the
second commandment. The nature of the matter also proves this, because the
duties of religion toward God are one thing and prescribed by the first
table, while the duties of love and honor toward superiors are another and
prescribed by the second.

III. Others assign only three precepts to the first table by combining the
first and second into one and dividing the tenth into two. This was the
division of Clement of Alexandria in book 6 of the *Stromata*, and of Augustine
in Question 71 on Exodus and Letter 119 to Januarius; the Roman Catholics and
Lutherans follow it. Still others claim four for the first table and six for
the second. This is the opinion of the Chaldean paraphrast on Exodus 20 and of
most of the fathers—Origen, Athanasius, Ambrose, Chrysostom, and others—and we
also follow it. With our theologians we make only this observation: this
controversy is not one about which anyone should contend bitterly and
obstinately, provided the doctrine of the whole Decalogue remains intact and
the number of precepts is not mutilated. Yet the Roman Catholics do mutilate
it here, for they join the second precept concerning graven images to the
first concerning the prohibition of foreign gods, as though it were only an
appendix or explanation of the first. Indeed, they customarily remove it from
the breviaries so that it will not become known to the people. This has caused
some of our theologians to attack that division more vigorously and adhere to
the received opinion, relying especially upon the following reasons.

IV. First, unless our division stands, the duties of religion toward God,
which are contained in the first table, will be confused with the duties of
justice and love toward one's neighbor, which are contained in the second
table. For the first four precepts concern religion and love for God, while
the last six concern love for one's neighbor.

V. Second, the first and second precepts differ in meaning and therefore also
in their ordering. The former concerns τὸ ὄν, or the *object of worship*, so
that the true God is worshiped. The latter concerns τὸ ποῖον, or the *mode of
worship*, so that he is not worshiped through images. Hence one can be
violated without the other being violated, when the true God is worshiped
with respect to the object but by an unlawful worship with respect to the
mode, as when the Israelites worshiped Jehovah in the calf (Exod. 32).

VI. Third, the tenth precept is wrongly divided into two because it is
comprehended under the one common name of covetousness and prohibits the same
kind of sin. Thus in Exodus 20 Moses places coveting a house before coveting a
wife,

but in Deuteronomy 5:21 he changes the order and places it afterward, which he
ought not to have done if they were two distinct precepts. Paul cites this
precept as one, not as two, in Romans 7:7: “I would not have known
covetousness to be a sin unless the law had said, ‘You shall not covet.’”

VII. Although the prohibition of idols is also contained under the
prohibition of foreign gods in the first precept insofar as they constitute
the *object of worship*, it does not follow that the second is not a distinct
precept. For there they are considered under a different σχέσει, or relation,
with respect to the *mode of worship*. Nor does it indicate only idols, or
representations of fictitious gods, but also images of the true God. Therefore,
lest the worship of images, or of God in images, be considered lawful, God
most wisely willed to forbid it by a distinct precept. It does not help to say
that images can very properly be called foreign gods because, according to
Paul, whoever religiously worships an image worships not the true God but a
false one, indeed the devil himself (1 Cor. 10:20). For although by consequence
and in the event the worship of images is referred to false gods or the devil,
this is not so according to the mind and intention of the worshipers, who sin
here not with respect to the object but with respect to the mode of worship.

VIII. Although the promise and threat added to the second precept can be
extended to all the precepts in general with respect to those who keep God's
commands, it was nevertheless fittingly attached to this command in
particular for a special reason. The people at that time were excessively
devoted to idolatry, so that they needed to guard themselves against it more
carefully, both through hope of the promise and through fear of the threat.

IX. Although Moses uses the two words התאוה and חמד to prohibit covetousness
in Exodus and Deuteronomy, it does not follow that he intended to make the
precept double. For even in the second precept, which is one, he employed the
two words שחה and עבד to prohibit the worship of images—*bowing down* and
*serving*. This is done not for the sake of distinction but of greater
emphasis.

X. Although covetousness differs in *degrees*—one is original, another actual;
one is without the will's prior consent, another with it—it is nevertheless
the same in *kind*, and this is what the precept prohibits. Nor, though the
objects considered materially are diverse, do they differ formally, because
they are contained under the formal aspect of things belonging to another. If
precepts had to be multiplied according to the diversity of the things
coveted, not merely two but far more precepts would have to be established.

XI. The division of the Decalogue with respect to its matter, or the things
commanded or prohibited, can be arranged as follows. In general, the worship
of God is commanded and whatever is opposed to it is forbidden. The worship
of God is either *immediate*, comprising duties rendered immediately to God,
or *mediate*, when duties are rendered to one's neighbor for God's sake.
Immediate worship is contained in the first table and is either *internal* or
*external*. Internal worship consists both in retaining the true *object of
worship* by worshiping the one God, which is sanctioned in the first precept,
and in worshiping him in a *lawful mode*, which is handed down in the second.
External worship is either *private*, which must be rendered by each person
always and everywhere and is taught in the third precept, or *public* in the
sanctification of the Sabbath, which is contained in the fourth. Mediate
worship is likewise internal and external. The external consists both in the
duties of superiors toward inferiors and conversely, which are treated in the
fifth precept, and in the duties of neighbors toward one another. These are
the preservation of life and health, commanded in the sixth; of chastity and
marriage, in the seventh; of property or possessions, in the eighth; and of
reputation and truth, in the ninth. The internal is the rectitude of all
affections and desires, which is included in the tenth.
