On calling.

12. Bellarmine. *Calling or mission into the ministry pertains not to the
people, but to the bishops, and especially to the supreme Pontiff.*

Protestants. I. *Calling* and *mission* are badly confused here. For mission
nowhere in Scripture signifies an ordinary calling that is transmitted to
someone through men; nor by its meaning does it denote an act of men calling a
man who is to be called in an ordinary manner. The Apostles themselves never
arrogated this to themselves, so that they would wish their mission to be
regarded as the calling of an ordinary pastor: *how shall they preach unless
they are sent?* Supply (says Cajetan), *by God.* II. The calling of a minister
is most absurdly distinguished from his ordination and election as a distinct
part. Neither in the Scriptures nor in the ancient Fathers is any other
ordinary calling mentioned than that which arises, as a whole, from election
and ordination. But beyond and above those things the Pontificians have
*confirmation, collation,*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n285 | printed p. 68]

*institution:* Bellarmine nevertheless did not wish to explain these, but
sophistically veiled them under the respectable title of *calling*. III. Since
Bellarmine, in chapter 2, has described that calling or mission for us as that
which *bestows jurisdiction and actually makes a pastor*, it is most
ridiculously placed by Bellarmine between *ordination* and *election*. IV. To
follow that order is just as if someone were first to treat the celebration of
nuptials (which is of the nature of ordination), second the nuptial union, and
then only afterward election, or consent to the nuptials.

13. Bellarmine. *Ecclesiastical jurisdiction is not in the assembly of the
faithful itself, but in the head, that is, in the Pope, and from him it is
derived to the other bishops.*

Protestants. I. No ecclesiastical jurisdiction is exercised in the calling of
a minister. All the Pontificians refer ordination to the power of order, which
is distinct from the power of jurisdiction. Election is such an act that
Bellarmine himself admits in chapter 2 that its power was once in the hands of
the people; by the nature of the matter no third thing is given. II. Without
any reason Bellarmine presses a twofold restriction upon us, so that he avoids
rather than proves the question itself. The question concerns the whole
*calling*, but he disputes only about *jurisdiction*, not about the power of
order. The question also concerns the calling of *ministers*, but he recounts
his dream only about the jurisdiction of *bishops*. III. *In* On the Pontiff,
*book 4, chapter 22, Bellarmine himself distinguishes jurisdiction into inward
jurisdiction for governing in the forum of conscience, and outward
jurisdiction for governing in the outward forum.* And he introduces Abulensis
affirming that the former is conferred immediately by God upon all priests; he
himself also seems to assent to him. This at least is the received opinion of
all the Scholastics: the power of the keys, which for them is inward
jurisdiction in the forum of conscience, is communicated by God and by Christ
alone, while the action of men intervenes only as a certain instrument. IV.
The more common opinion, even of the Pontifical Doctors, is that a bishop
receives no jurisdiction from the Roman Pontiff, as may be seen in Azor, part
2, book 3, chapter 50, where this proposition of Peter Soto stands forth as
worthy of careful notice: *Those most ancient bishops, Cyprian and Augustine,
thought that nothing in their dioceses had been committed to them by the Roman
Pontiff; they thought that they took up authority through the election of the
people and the ordination of their fellow provincials, as through a certain
ministry.* In his opinion, therefore, this whole mission and calling, distinct
from election and ordination, is a new fiction by which certain flatterers
sought to gratify the Roman Pope. This whole question, therefore, is most
excellently and judiciously determined by Henry, *Quodlibet* 9, question 22,

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n286 | printed p. 69]

as the same Azor reports, by a distinction: *Bishops have power immediately
from Christ, both the power of order and the power of jurisdiction, in ordering
churches according to the ordinances of divine law; but they have from the
Pope jurisdiction in ordering churches according to the edicts instituted by
the Pope.*

14. Bellarmine. *It was said to Peter, I will give you the keys; feed the
sheep; and to the Apostles, I send you, and so forth.*

Protestants. The most learned of the Pontificians see and acknowledge that all
these things do nothing to establish the derivation of jurisdiction from the
Pope. Among them Victor, who was the father of the modern Scholastics in Spain,
nevertheless contends *that any bishop could establish a law in his province
that the presbyters should elect a bishop for themselves, even without the see
of Peter being consulted.* Azor, in the place cited.

15. Bellarmine. *Paul, Barnabas, and Titus appointed bishops and presbyters.*

Protestants. Paul and Barnabas appointed ordinary pastors *by votes*, Acts
14:23. Titus, moreover, did so just as Paul had *instructed* him, Titus 1:5.
Paul's instruction, however, did not differ from Paul's practice.
