As for God's glory, which Castellio thinks God would serve better if he
cleansed the saints from every pollution of sin long before death rather
than in death itself, I briefly submit the following.

1. Let us safely entrust to God the care of his own glory until “the day of
the revelation of God's righteous judgment.” Then at last he will plainly
reveal both his hidden things and ours, including what he worked in those
who were dying, why he delayed complete cleansing from the remnants of sins
until death itself, and how by prolonging the day in this way he served his
glory best.

2. Yet something concerning this is already partly known to us in advance.
For his glory shines from it.

First, the glory of his faithfulness and truthfulness, because according to
the word of promise he “guides us even unto death,” Psalm 48:14.

The glory of his unfailing love and benevolence also shines from it. What
is said about the indissoluble bond of friendship between Naomi and Ruth,
and between Saul and Jonathan, applies here: not even death can break it;
indeed, death kindles and increases it. Thus what Boaz said of Ruth applies:
“You have surpassed your former kindness with the latter,” Ruth 3:10. So
too does what Christ said of the angel of Thyatira: “Your last works are
more and greater than the former,” Revelation 2:19. This is especially true
here, because what could not occur throughout the whole preceding life
occurs in death itself: a perpetual and complete divorce from sin.

A glorious display of his omnipotence also shines in this, when it “is
perfected in our weakness”; so does his all-sufficiency and self-sufficiency,
when he accomplishes entirely by himself, while we do almost nothing, what
he said to Paul: “My grace is sufficient for you.”

Moreover, Christ's own combatants continually wrestling against sin by
faith furnishes glory and delight to his grace.

Therefore, let no one ask why God permitted sin to enter the world. As
Augustine says, he would not have done so unless he could carve some good
and his own glory from it: namely, the glory of his omnipotent grace, by
graciously pardoning sin thus permitted, powerfully subduing it, and at
last most omnipotently abolishing it altogether.

Nor should anyone ask why God permits this sin to dwell in us as long as we
inhabit this cottage of flesh, not to be cast out until the body has been
put off. For he serves his glory best in this way.

On the one hand, he tests, exercises, and strengthens faith; renews
repentance; kindles prayers; and trains us in humility, charity and mercy
toward those subject to like passions, and continual dependence upon God.
Faith and the grace of pilgrims chiefly consist in these and similar
things, from which the greatest glory we can render here accrues to God.

On the other hand, by these remnants of sin—diminished though they are, yet
remaining until death—he diminishes and cuts down pride and security,
disdainful contempt for others,

[1679 printed p. 289 | IA leaf 297]

and our supine self-confidence, which would do greater injury to his glory.
