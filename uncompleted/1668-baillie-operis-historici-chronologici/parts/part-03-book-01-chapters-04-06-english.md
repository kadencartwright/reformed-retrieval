### QUESTION V

#### How seventy and seventy-five persons descended into Egypt from Jacob’s family

Fifth it is asked how Moses, at Genesis 46:27 and elsewhere, writes that
seventy Israelites descended into Egypt, while Stephen at Acts 7:14 records
seventy-five. Answer: God’s holy word rejects captious handling and abhors
critical quibbling. Ancient and recent interpreters labor to untie knots of
this kind, though after trying everything they cannot always satisfy either
others or themselves. Certainly little that Augustine, Jerome, and others
formerly, or Calvin, Beza, Mercer, Cappellus, Mentzer, Junius, Rivet, and
Velican more recently—or even Lorinus, Pererius, Lapide, and other Romanists—
bring forward to solve this knot will please judicious men.

We readily grant that the Septuagint translators often fell into gross errors
in calculations of dates and numbers. Yet their error here does not consist
in a letter or numeral: to complete their five above Moses’ seventy, they
expressly introduce more than five of Joseph’s descendants whom Moses omits.
Nevertheless, only under the greatest constraint do we admit with many that
either Stephen or Luke followed the Septuagint’s departure from the Hebrew
truth. Nor do we venture with others to correct the New Testament text here
as though corrupted by scribal carelessness—reading *sixty-five* for
*seventy-five* with Theodore Beza, or omitting *all* with Jacob Cappellus—
since all Greek manuscripts, the oldest versions including the Syriac and
Arabic, and virtually all modern versions agree with the printed text.

[Printed p. 40 | IA leaf 70 | access PDF 70 | OCR object 69]

We prefer, therefore, to reconcile Luke’s larger number with Moses’ smaller
one by interpreting Luke of the whole household and Moses only of Jacob’s
sons who came from his seed. The household contained at least five besides
the male descendants who came to Egypt: make up that five from Jacob’s
daughter and his four wives. Although Leah and Rachel were certainly buried
in Canaan, they can nevertheless be numbered as persons belonging to Jacob’s
whole household no less than Er and Onan, who also died in Canaan. For the
plain purpose of this enumeration, both in Moses and Luke, is to display
God’s power in multiplying Jacob’s descendants from so small a beginning to
so great a number in so few years. It serves this purpose well not only to
note all the sons, as Moses did, but also everyone related to Jacob’s house as
closely as a mother or daughter. I would not offer this conjecture if I had
not seen far less probable ones proposed by very great men.

### QUESTION VI

#### How at the departure from Egypt the Israelites multiplied to six hundred thousand

Sixth it is asked how, within 215 years, seventy or seventy-five persons could
grow into six hundred thousand men. Answer: divine power could very easily
accomplish by miracle what ordinarily does not occur in the course of
providence. Yet in the present case we do not much need the support of any
extraordinary power if we admit either Cappellus’s or Calvin’s hypotheses.
Cappellus asks us to grant that each Israelite begot ten children after his
thirtieth year. Nothing here resembles a miracle: Aegyptus, Danaus, Darius,
and Priam
are each said to have had fifty children; Artaxerxes, according to Justin,
had 115, and Hierotimus six hundred. Scripture relates that Abdon had forty
sons, Ibzan thirty sons and as many daughters, Gideon seventy sons, and Ahab
as many. Cappellus’s postulate therefore contains nothing novel or rare.

In the 215 years during which the Israelites remained in Egypt, this source
of fertility would supply each descendant of Jacob who entered Egypt with a
million descendants, as the later number is multiplied seven times by ten.
For 215 years contain thirty years seven times; ten multiplied seven times
into the first and following generations produces the number above. Benjamin,
for example, had ten sons during his life, nine of whom we have proved he
actually begot before his twenty-fourth year. On this hypothesis he should
have had one hundred grandsons, one thousand great-grandsons, ten thousand
descendants in the fourth degree, one hundred thousand in the fifth, and one
million in the sixth.

Calvin arranges his calculation differently. He assigns only four sons over
a lifetime to each Israelite in Egypt, but counts sixteen generations in 215
years. He supposes, as stated above, that Shuthelah was eighth in descent from
Ephraim and that Beriah was born after Shuthelah’s death as though ninth;
Joshua, born many years before the exodus, was at least the eighth. He
multiplies each of the sixteen generations by four and in the last reaches an
almost infinite number. Ephraim had four sons in the first generation. Giving
each of these four sons produces sixteen grandsons in the second generation,
sixty-four great-grandsons in the third, 256 descendants in the fourth,
1,024 in the fifth, 4,096 in the sixth, 16,384 in the seventh, and 65,536 in
the eighth. He supposes Ephraim could have seen so many descendants born from
him before Beriah was begotten. He then continues quadrupling births to the
sixteenth or seventeenth generation down to Joshua. Although he allows ten
thousand Ephraimites slain by the men of Gath and thirteen of these
generations dead in Egypt, in the remaining three he still finds more than
four thousand million Ephraimites able to leave Egypt. No one, therefore,
need doubt Moses’ total of six hundred thousand for all the tribes together
at the exodus.

### QUESTION VII

#### Why the Israelites were so few at the exodus

If someone should raise the contrary difficulty from these calculations—how
the Israelites grew to so small a number in that span of time—the answer is
that subtle inventions commonly stand far from real events. Not only
mathematicians but political projectors, as they are called, and ingenious
innovators in every art communicate many things which vanish into air when
put to practice. We press Cappellus’s or Calvin’s arithmetic only so far as
to demonstrate that Moses’ number was naturally possible without a miracle.

[Printed p. 41 | IA leaf 71 | access PDF 71 | OCR object 70]

We readily acknowledge from sense and daily experience how many and great
the things are that ordinarily prevent the multiplication of mankind in
those higher proportions. Otherwise every nation in every century would grow
to such prodigious numbers that the whole world would long since have been
insufficient to contain the multitude. Yet repeated experience establishes
that even the most fertile peoples, after several centuries and without
notable devastations by plague, famine, or sword, have gained no remarkable
increase in number. We pursue this no further as we hasten to the next epoch.

## CHAPTER V

### Events belonging to the third epoch, from the exodus to the building of Solomon’s temple

The leading chronologers, Protestant and Romanist alike—Scaliger, Calvin,
Salian, and Petavius—place the exodus in the 2,453rd year of the world. We
demonstrated above from the unquestionable computations of Holy Scripture
that this is correct. The Flood was in the 1,656th year of the world. Abraham
was born 292 years after the Flood and migrated to Canaan in his seventy-fifth
year. From there to the exodus Moses and Paul count 430 years. Added together,
these produce 2,453 years. Cappellus, Heinrich Philipp, and others set a
larger total because some give the spurious Cainan an extra thirty years and
all wish Abraham to have been born sixty full years later than Moses allows,
in Terah’s 130th rather than, as we hold, his seventieth year.

As the termini of the first two epochs are sufficiently clear and fixed, so
those of the third now before us seem no less certain. Moses determines the
date of the exodus, and the author of 1 Kings 6:1 places the beginning of the
temple’s construction in the 480th year after the exodus, thus the 2,933rd
year of the world. The total of this epoch is collected in different ways.
Junius in his notes on 1 Kings 6:1 reckons forty years in the wilderness,
seventeen under Joshua, 229 under the Judges, eighty under Eli, Samuel, and
Saul, and forty under David; Solomon’s first four years added to these make
the 480 stated above. We shall examine the many weighty objections in the
next chapter.

Meanwhile let us touch briefly on events throughout the world, inside and
outside the Church. In the exodus itself and the few days following, the
Egyptian plagues were completed. After many years of bitter oppression—the
dreadful drowning of male infants lasted more than eighty years—divine
vengeance at length arose against the Egyptian oppressors. Moses, in his
fortieth year of exile among the Midianites and his eightieth year of life,
was called to liberate the people by divine power. Throughout that year the
Egyptians were scourged with their deserved plagues. Those who wish all the
plagues finished in one month hurry beyond reason.

In Moses’ eighty-first year and the world’s 2,404th, on the tenth day of the
first month—first called Abib and later Nisan, and thereafter the first month
in every sacred computation—a Passover lamb was set apart in every Israelite
house for sacrifice. Four days later, Nisan 14, our April 15, it was killed
at evening. At midnight the firstborn were killed by the destroying angel.
Early on Nisan 15, April 16, the Egyptians, terrified by imminent death,
thrust the Israelites out with gifts and entreaties. On that first day of the
journey, the fifth day of the week, they pitched their tents at Succoth; there
in huts, whence Succoth took its name, they ate the Passover’s unleavened
bread baked under ashes. The next day, Nisan 16, April 17, the sixth weekday,
they traveled to Etham. They appear to have rested there on the seventh day,
the Sabbath. On Nisan 18, April 19, the second day of the first week, they
came to Pi-hahiroth by the Red Sea. After remaining a few days, they had
behind them Egypt’s king—Manetho’s Cenchres—with his army, intending by force
of arms to drag Israel back into its former slavery.

From the first moment of their departure the Lord Jesus, the angel of
Jehovah’s face, preceded Israel’s camp as leader and commander in a cloud
whose darkness tempered the sun’s rays by day and whose heat and light
dispelled the cold and darkness by night. A full month after the exodus, at
their eighth station, the Israelites entered the wilderness of Sin on Iyar
15, our May 26. The food they had brought from Egypt failed, prompting
murmurs against Moses and God. God at once restrained them, supplying from
the rich storehouse of his providence that very evening a delicious abundance
of meat—a flock of quail—and the next morning angelic bread in the dew.

[Printed p. 42 | IA leaf 72 | access PDF 72 | OCR object 71]

The first day on which manna rained was Sunday, the first weekday after the
Sabbath; for the sixth day thereafter was the day before the Sabbath, when
they were commanded to gather a double portion out of reverence for the
following Sabbath. Thus the first day of manna, from which the Sabbath was
the seventh, must have been the first day of creation and of the world—our
Sunday, on which the Lord, the true manna, rose from the dead. God’s people
were fed by this gift of heaven throughout all forty years except one month,
the kind of small interval Scripture commonly omits in round numbers. As the
Egyptian food failed halfway through the second month after the exodus and
was replaced by heavenly bread, so after forty years the produce of Canaan
was brought into the camp on the fifteenth day of the first month and ended
the supply of this extraordinary provision, now no longer necessary (Joshua
5:15).

The eleventh station was Rephidim, on an unknown day of the second month.
There Moses first brought the waters of Meribah from the struck rock before
the elders, and on that same day broke Amalek by his prayers to God and
Joshua’s arms. At the following, twelfth station they came to Mount Sinai on
the first day of the third month (Exodus 19:1). The cloud and camp remained
there almost a whole year, until the twentieth day of the second month of the
second year after the exodus (Numbers 10:11).

On the first and second days of the third month the people were prepared to
hear the law. On the third the law was given while they trembled beneath
Sinai, which smoked and quaked on every side and burned with a great fire at
its summit. This was the fiftieth day from Passover if counted from the
fourteenth of the first month. If, as most authors infer from Leviticus 23,
the seven weeks of Pentecost are counted from the second day of Unleavened
Bread, when the sheaf was lifted, the law was indeed given in the third month
and on the third day, as Exodus 19:16 expressly states—but on the third day
not of the month or week, but from Moses’ first ascent of the mountain. From
that ascent the Lord announced three days for the people’s preparation and
the law’s promulgation. Most interpreters judge this to have been Sivan 6,
our June 5, the sixth weekday and the Sabbath itself.

On the day the Decalogue was pronounced, Moses first ascended alone to
receive God’s commands. Descending, he led the whole people out of the camp
to the mountain’s foot. Then, by God’s command at Exodus 19:24, he and Aaron
ascended as mediators. With them thus positioned, the law thundered with a
terrible trumpet blast. The people fled in fear, turned their backs, and
stood far from the mountain (Exodus 20:18). Moses comforted them and reported
their promise that they would gladly obey God speaking through him. God
kindly accepted it, and Moses sent the people back to camp. After receiving
many ceremonial commands explaining the Decalogue, found in Exodus 20–23, he
returned from the mountain and that night wrote them with the Decalogue in
the book of the covenant. The next day he erected an altar, offered
expiatory and peace offerings, and sprinkled the book and the people with
blood. He returned to the mountain with Aaron, Aaron’s sons, and seventy
elders to report the people’s approval and confirm the covenant with God.
All these delegates saw from afar the Lord expressing his presence by a
visible sign, worshiped, and returned alive to camp.

Moses alone, called by God to receive the tablets of the Decalogue, went back
up the mountain with Joshua his servant on the fifth day of the month—or, as
others say, the eighth—at any rate two days after the law’s promulgation. He
then stayed on the mountain with God fasting without food or drink for forty
days. Joshua could meanwhile eat manna and drink from a mountain spring, for
Moses says nothing of his fasting. Moses remained outside the thick darkness
for the first week; on the seventh day he was called and ascended alone into
the midst of the cloud, where he heard from God what is contained in Exodus
24–27 and the following chapters.

At the end of this first forty days Aaron made the golden calf. To restrain
that monstrous crime, Moses and Joshua were sent to camp with the divine
tablets in Moses’ hands. Before descending, however, his intercession turned
the Lord’s anger away from the immediate destruction of the whole people.
Approaching the camp and seeing the people raging in the very act of idolatry,
he was inflamed with zeal for God, broke the tablets, crushed the idol, and
ordered the Levites to strike down everyone they met. They killed about three
thousand, not Cappellus’s twenty-one thousand.

The next day, after severely rebuking the people, Moses returned to the
mountain (Exodus 32) and spent a second forty days in fasting and prayer
(Deuteronomy 9:15). Having received a fuller promise of God’s favor toward
the people and a command to make new tablets, he went up the mountain a third
time (Exodus 33) to render God still more favorable. The meeting tent had
been pitched outside the camp and the people ordered to remove their
ornaments, so full reconciliation had not yet been obtained. On this visit
Moses saw God’s glory from behind, received the law written by God’s finger
on the tablets he had brought, and acquired a wonderful radiance impressed
on his face through so many intimate conversations with God.

Cappellus and Heinrich Philipp, following others, ascribe a third forty-day
fast to Moses, but no suitable or coherent reasoning supports it.

The rest of the year was spent making the tabernacle’s varied and manifold
furnishings. The whole structure was erected under Mount Sinai, the twelfth
station, on the first day of the first month of the second year after the
exodus. The former tent was set aside as no longer necessary, and the new one
was covered by the clearest sign of the divine presence—the cloud which, as
we said, appeared as darkness by day and fire by night. At its movement or
rest the tabernacle and the entire camp moved or rested. From this covenant
tabernacle Moses delivered the whole of Leviticus before the first day of the
second month, when the Lord began to teach what appears in Numbers (1:1).
Aaron and his sons began their consecration on the first day of the first
month. It was completed on the seventh; on the eighth they offered sacrifices
as fully initiated priests, and a flame from heaven consumed the victims
consecrated upon the altar (Leviticus 9:24). The Lord commanded that this
heavenly fire be fed and perpetually preserved on the altar (Leviticus 1:7;
6:12). Contempt for this command extinguished Aaron’s firstborn sons Nadab and
Abihu in the holy place on that same eighth day, according to most
interpreters.

On the first day of the second month of the second year after the exodus a
census and numbering were instituted. Moses found 603,550 men over twenty in
the eleven tribes (Numbers 1:46). This was the people’s second census, and
the number of persons was exactly the same as at the first registration a few
months earlier (Exodus 38:26). Whatever public calamities or ordinary deaths
had meanwhile taken from the first census could readily be supplied by those
who reached the census age of twenty during those months. After thirty-nine
years the people were numbered a third time and slightly fewer were found:
only 601,730 persons are read in Numbers 26:51, although all who were counted
in the first census except Caleb and Joshua were then dead.

After a stay at Mount Sinai of one whole year less thirteen days—for they
arrived on the third day of the third month of the first year of the exodus
and moved camp on the twentieth day of the second month of the second year
(Numbers 10:11)—they completed a journey of three days to Kibroth-hattaavah,
their thirteenth station. Here quail were caught a second time; the people’s
appetite was sated with them for a whole month even to nausea, but a great
slaughter punished their gluttony (Numbers 11:30). The fourteenth station was
Hazeroth, where Miriam was struck with leprosy. From there they came into the
wilderness of Paran, to their fifteenth station, Rithmah or Kadesh-barnea,
and sent spies into Canaan. Ten returned after forty days and by infamous
slander stirred sedition among the people, thereby drawing from God the
sentence that all the murmurers should wander in the wilderness for forty
years from the exodus until death. Korah’s conspiracy, from envy of the
priesthood assigned to Aaron and his sons, and the matters in Numbers 16–17,
appear to have happened not long after the spies returned, perhaps before the
end of the second year.

For the next thirty-seven years Moses records nothing of Israel’s affairs
except bare station names in Numbers 33. All later history in Numbers and
Deuteronomy belongs to the fortieth year after the exodus. In its first month
they came to Kadesh—not Kadesh-barnea, the fifteenth station on Canaan’s
border, but another Kadesh, the thirty-third station on Edom’s border.
Miriam died there at an age apparently not less than 130. She was older than
both brothers, for when Moses was exposed by the Nile in a basket she was a
girl mature enough for a prudent conversation with Pharaoh’s daughter. At
the same station a new quarrel arose about the waters of Meribah, which
excluded Moses and Aaron from Canaan.

At the next station Aaron died on Mount Hor on the first day of the fifth
month. In the following months occurred the histories at the end of Numbers:
the defeat of King Arad, the bronze serpent, Kings Og and Sihon, Balaam and
Balak, and vengeance upon Midian’s princes. The last events took place while
Israel camped in the plains of Moab beside the Jordan opposite Jericho.
There, in the eleventh month of the fortieth year, Moses dictated Deuteronomy.
At the beginning of the twelfth month he departed this life in a vigorous old
age of 120 and was buried by an angel in an unknown valley, in the 2,493rd
year of the world, the 837th after the Flood, and the 1,457th before Christ.

This follows Scaliger’s, Calvin’s, and Emmius’s calculation. Cappellus, from
the error noted above concerning Abraham’s birth, adds sixty years; Heinrich
Philipp, from another error concerning the younger Cainan, adds another
thirty in all his subsequent chronological computations.

Observe that Genesis contains the history of 2,310 years, from near the
world’s beginning to Joseph’s death. Exodus, if its first two chapters—briefly
recalling what happened between Joseph’s death and Moses’ call—are excepted,
contains the history of only one year, at most two: the Egyptian plagues and
the first year’s events in the wilderness down to the tabernacle’s erection
under Sinai. Leviticus appears completed within one month when its beginning
is compared with Numbers. Numbers, the Pentateuch’s fourth book, has the
history of only two years, the second and fortieth after the exodus. The
fifth, Deuteronomy, records the events of the fortieth year’s final two
months. No profane history yet occurs which we should transcribe into this
abridgment. What chronologers assert here is almost all drawn from fragments
of Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, scarcely worth repeating. Let us proceed to the
times of Joshua and the Judges.

As soon as the thirty days of mourning for Moses ended, Joshua, full of the
divine Spirit, prepared the people at God’s command to cross the Jordan.
After the spies returned from Rahab at Jericho, on the tenth day of the first
month in the forty-first year after the exodus, the priests preceding with
the ark, he led the people dry-shod across the channel of the Jordan, then
swollen by its spring flooding. When Joshua assumed leadership after Moses’
death, the cloud before the tabernacle—on whose movement every encampment and
journey depended—vanished. The first camp across the Jordan was pitched at
Gilgal. There circumcision was first performed after a forty-year
intermission; three days having been allowed for the wounds to heal, Passover
was celebrated on the fourteenth day of the first month. The fifteenth was
the first day of Unleavened Bread, and on the sixteenth a sheaf of Canaan’s
crop was offered. That night Israel ate grain from Canaan. On the morning of
the seventeenth the manna, now unnecessary, ceased. The walls of Jericho
then fell of themselves after a seven-day circuit, at the trumpets’ blast and
Israel’s shout.

Joshua’s subjection of Canaan cost six full years of labor, for only in the
seventh year after crossing the Jordan was the *katapausis*—the division of
the land among a people now at rest and free from the arms of conquered
enemies—made. This deserves careful notice, since upon it turns the certainty
of the sabbatical and jubilee years, principal marks of chronology. Joshua
14:5–6 says that as Joshua and Eleazar at Gilgal prepared to distribute the
conquered Canaanites’ lands, Caleb came with his tribe, Judah’s leading men,
and asked for the territory of Hebron outside the citadel according to God’s
promise. He distinctly says he was forty at the spying, in the exodus’s
second year, and eighty-five when the allotment began. It therefore occurred
forty-five years after the spying, the forty-seventh after the exodus and
seventh after crossing the Jordan. This seventh year was sabbatical by the
statute in Leviticus 25, and Israel’s first sabbatical year in practice.
Though the weekly Sabbath was instituted at creation because of God’s rest,
and even before Moses the Syrians in Laban’s story divided a year into weeks
and called seven years a week of years, no earlier sabbatical year is recorded
in practice. God’s people had never before possessed a peaceful land whose
cultivation he willed them to suspend in the seventh year.

Thus let us fix the origin of sabbatical years here. The allotment was in the
seventh year after the crossing, and the following year—the eighth after the
crossing—was the first *georgia*, when each began to cultivate and sow his
lot. From it every seventh year was sabbatical and every seventh seven a
jubilee. On these principles, making the eighth after the crossing the first
*georgia* with Josephus and others, and the second the first *katapausis*, the
fourteenth after the crossing must be the first sabbatical year. The first
jubilee followed six weeks of years after the first sabbatical. As the
Sabbath day is the seventh day of rest after six of work, so the sabbatical
year is the seventh in which all field labor ceased after six years of
agriculture; and the jubilee is the fiftieth after seven weeks of years—that
is, forty-nine years, whose final forty-ninth year is always sabbatical.

[Printed p. 45 | IA leaf 75 | access PDF 75 | OCR object 74]

For in forty-nine years the sabbatical years or sevens plainly fall in the
seventh, fourteenth, twenty-first, twenty-eighth, thirty-fifth, forty-second,
and forty-ninth. Under the distinctive judicial law of sabbatical years there
was neither sowing nor pruning. Every fruit of the earth in that year was
spontaneous or neglected and common to all. By God’s wonderful blessing the
people were fed from the sixth year’s labor and yield until the ninth year’s
harvest and vintage, gathered at the end, not the beginning, of the eighth
year. Though in Judaea the barley and some pulse harvest began at Passover,
about the middle of Nisan or Abib, our April—Abib was not the fifth month
after Nisan and eleventh after Tishri—the firstfruits of the wheat harvest
did not begin before Pentecost, the Feast of Weeks, nor end before the Feast
of Tabernacles in mid-Tishri, the sacred seventh and civil first month, as is
clear from comparing passages of the Pentateuch.

As the first seventh *georgia* was the first sabbatical year, so seven times
seven, the forty-ninth or fiftieth, was the first *jobel* or jubilee. Whether,
with Usher and his followers, the fiftieth counted inclusively constitutes
the jubilee; whether, with Joseph Scaliger and Calvin, the forty-ninth is the
true *jobel* and the fiftieth is excluded; or whether, according to both, the
fiftieth is numbered in the jubilee both inclusively and exclusively—since
the sacred year begins with Nisan, the seventh civil month, and the jubilee’s
civil year with Tishri, sacred seventh but civil first—will be examined
elsewhere. We now indicate only the first source of sabbatical and jubilee
years: the first *georgia*, the second *katapausis* and eighth year after the
Jordan, the forty-eighth after the exodus and thus the 2,501st year of the
world. On Bucholzer’s calculation it was the fiftieth jubilee from creation;
from it to Christ’s baptism he counts thirty jubilees, or 1,500 years.

As the people lived from the enemy’s labor and sowing for the first seven
years after the crossing, in the eighth, ninth, tenth, and eleventh years—
four years after the *katapausis*—they abstained from the uncircumcised and
polluted fruit of trees and ate none before the fifth year, since it had to be
holy in the fourth (Leviticus 19).

Joshua certainly died aged 110 (Joshua 24), but in which year of his rule or
after the Jordan and Moses’ death is wholly uncertain. Here, as often among
chronologers where no clear scriptural text or compelling argument removes
the freedom to dissent, there are as many opinions as heads. Some assign him
seven years, others ten, eighteen, twenty-five, twenty-eight, or thirty-two. We
follow the conjecture that Joshua transferred the tabernacle from Gilgal to
Shiloh in the fourteenth year after the crossing and died in the seventeenth.
Most authors make Cadmus his contemporary, saying he came from Egypt and
Phoenicia to Boeotia, founded Thebes, and first taught the Greeks letters.

Joshua having died, as we suppose, seventeen years after Moses, and Eleazar
and the other elders who knew God’s mighty works having died, Israel in
sloth and weariness of war allowed the remnant of the Canaanites to live
peaceably among them. From them the pestilence of vice and idolatry invaded
the people. Divine anger therefore handed Israel to Cushan, king of
Mesopotamia, for eight years of chastisement. Othniel, Caleb’s son-in-law and
nephew, shook the tyrant’s yoke from the necks of the repentant people and
administered the commonwealth in peace until the fortieth year after Joshua’s
death.

After Othniel’s death the people returned to their disposition and again
defiled themselves with idolatry. They were therefore delivered to Eglon the
Moabite for eighteen years. Crushed by misery they called upon God, at whose
impulse Ehud the Benjaminite arose, stabbed Eglon, and procured peace from at
least an external enemy until the eightieth year after Othniel’s death.
During this interval Shamgar, the third judge, checked the insolence of the
Philistines. Micah’s and the Danites’ idolatry was also then flourishing. Its
chief minister was Jonathan, either Moses’ grandson or great-grandson, as
Judges 18:30 has it. If with Diodati one understands, from the rabbis,
Manasseh’s father Gershom to be Moses, Jonathan was Moses’ grandson; if with
Junius and others one understands Gershom’s son, he was Gershom’s grandson
and Manasseh’s son, and thus Moses’ great-grandson. But it is rather harsh,
without necessity, to make this gross idolater Moses’ grandson or
great-grandson, especially since in the genealogy of Moses and Gershom—

—in 1 Chronicles 23:15 no Jonathan is mentioned.

The Benjaminite war must also be referred to this period, although described
at the end of Judges. It does not fit Othniel’s preceding age and cannot be
deferred beyond Ehud’s eightieth year, for Phinehas the high priest was then
guarding the Lord’s ark at Shiloh (Judges 20:28). He could scarcely have been
under twenty before Moses’ death, when he broke the Amalekites in the
wilderness. Add Joshua’s seventeen years, Othniel’s forty, and Ehud’s eighty,
and one must extend his life beyond 157—an age unusual in those centuries.

Godly princes are pillars and foundations of their subjects’ safety. When
they are removed, a people’s peace commonly collapses and its happiness
vanishes. Once Ehud and Shamgar were gone, Israel refused to be restrained
from crime until twenty years of dreadful slavery to Jabin the Canaanite
broke its stubbornness. The afflicted people then promptly obtained help
from the merciful God through the ministry chiefly of two women, Deborah and
Jael. The Canaanite yoke was broken and the people rested in peace until the
fortieth year after Ehud; but new sins soon attracted new punishments.

The Midianites and Amalekites afflicted God’s people for seven years. The
afflicted resorted to the customary remedy for otherwise incurable evils:
they cried to God and were heard. The angel infused a new spirit into Gideon.
With his three hundred, by the blast of trumpets and flash of lamps alone, he
routed more than one hundred thousand enemies and procured peace until the
fortieth year after Deborah’s death. His most wicked bastard Abimelech killed
sixty-nine brothers—called seventy by a round rather than precise number—
declared himself king, and exercised cruel tyranny for three years until
heavenly vengeance sent the Furies and destroyed both him and the accomplices
of his crimes by mutual slaughter.

After Abimelech, Tola presided over Israel for twenty-three years, then Jair
for twenty-two. After Jair’s death the Ammonites harshly oppressed Israel,
especially the Gileadites beyond Jordan, for its sins; but the merciful Lord
did not long despise their entreaties. Jephthah, restraining the Ammonite
oppressors by divine strength, governed the people in peace for six years.
Of the next judges we have only names and years: Ibzan seven, Elon ten, and
Abdon eight. After them came savage Philistine oppression and Samson’s
wonderful acts of vengeance for twenty years. Then Eli for forty years,
Samuel and Saul for as many, and David for the same number governed the
people; Solomon laid the temple’s foundations four years later. Their
histories are too familiar to require narration here.

## CHAPTER VI

### Questions and difficulties concerning the preceding chapter

### QUESTION I

#### How the total of this epoch is proved

First it is asked how the total of 480 years expressed at 1 Kings 6:1 is
proved and reduced into what are called expanded years. Answer: we give and
prove the parts of the whole as follows: after the exodus Moses receives
forty years (Numbers 14:33); Joshua seventeen; Othniel forty (Judges 3:10);
Ehud and Shamgar eighty (Judges 3:30); Deborah forty (Judges 5:30); Gideon
forty (Judges 8:28); Abimelech three (Judges 9:22); Tola twenty-three (Judges
10:2); Jair twenty-two (Judges 10:3); Jephthah six (Judges 12:7); Ibzan seven
(Judges 12:9); Elon ten (Judges 12:11); Abdon eight (Judges 12:14); Samson
twenty (Judges 15:20); Eli forty (1 Samuel 4:18); Samuel and Saul forty (Acts
13:21); David forty (2 Samuel 5:4); Solomon four before the temple was
founded (1 Kings 6:1). Adding these parts produces exactly the whole sought.

### QUESTION II

#### How Acts 13:20 is reconciled with 1 Kings 6:1

Second it is asked how Acts 13:20 agrees with Kings, for in Acts Paul counts
450 years after Joshua’s division of the land down to Samuel. Add the
forty-seven years under Moses and Joshua which preceded, and the eighty-four
under Samuel, Saul, David, and Solomon which followed, and there will be 590
from the exodus to the temple’s first foundations—110 more than the stated
number. To dissolve this very difficult knot, both Protestants and Romanists
offer empty answers scarcely satisfying judicious men.

Serarius and other Romanists have no scruple about professing an error in
Kings contrary to the testimony of all Hebrew, Greek, and Latin codices.
Theodore Beza, after Luther, saves everything by adding the single letter
iota in Acts, for he renders the common reading, *about four hundred and
fifty*, as *about three hundred and fifty*. Paul’s number is then 350, which
readily fits the Old Testament count. Beza testifies that he read this
correction in one of his manuscripts. Those who dislike it may accept his
other solution in his marginal note on Acts in his final edition, which
Junius either follows or anticipates in his parallels. Retain the common
reading, but refer the number not to Samuel as its terminus, with Joshua’s
division as its beginning, but to that division as its terminus and the
election of the fathers as its beginning. Between those termini—Abraham’s
calling and Joshua’s allotment—there were indeed about 450 years. Since the
relative pronoun *which* coheres with several antecedents, why must it attach
to the last, the allotment in verse 19, rather than the first, the fathers’
election in verse 17? Especially since the sending of the judges, and thus
the act of Joshua the first judge in the division, is placed after the number
in verse 20.

### Additions to Question II

#### The true reckoning of 480 years from the exodus to the temple’s construction defended against Vossius and Cappellus

[Printed p. 47 | IA leaf 77 | access PDF 77 | OCR object 76]

We cannot pass over this question so lightly, since Gerard Vossius in chapter
7 and following of his *Isagoge* and Ludovicus Cappellus in the tenth of his
prolegomena to Walton stir so much dust around it. They for a while preserve
the integrity of 1 Kings 6:1, as though the prophet spoke only of years of
freedom and none of Israel’s oppression and slavery. But when they perceive
the many inconveniences of this device, both openly profess an error, slip,
and fault in the Hebrew text, asserting that instead of 480 years after the
departure one must read 580. It is harsh to corrupt sacred chronology by the
addition of an entire century; much harsher to condemn the original text of
the clearest corruption. Let us nevertheless weigh their arguments. Vossius
takes three from the Jesuits Serarius, Cano, and himself; Cappellus adds some
in Walton.

Vossius’s first argument: the years collected from Judges, Samuel, and Kings
make one hundred more than the number stated in 1 Kings 6:1. Answer: even if
we were silent because of the ignorance common to all mortals and could not
reconcile the expanded years in Judges and elsewhere with the total in
Kings, far be it from us to ascribe any fault to God’s holy word. Let all
this obscurity be imputed to our blindness and darkness; the Holy Spirit’s
dictates are wholly free of error and lapse.

Second, Vossius and his associates’ correction of Scripture is unsound even
on their own principles. They add not only one hundred years to Scripture’s
480, but their number is still not exact: the years of servitude, by their
admission, amount not to one hundred but 111. A round hundred can perhaps be
extended to 111, but 580 cannot by any rounding extend to 591. Where a full
number, four or five hundred, has lesser broken numbers behind it, all are to
be regarded as exact integers from which nothing can be taken or added by
rounding without corruption.

Third, to establish his calculation Vossius deletes from sacred chronology
the twenty years Scripture expressly assigns to Samson in Judges 16:31. If
these are added, as everyone concerned for Scripture’s integrity does, then
1 Kings 6:1 must read not merely 511 but 531 instead of 480. Vossius himself
denies such an absurd increase. He is exceedingly liberal in inventing new
absurdities: to erase Samson’s express twenty years, he readily grants,
contrary to Scripture, that the forty years of Philistine oppression may be
reduced to twenty and Eli’s forty likewise cut to twenty. What license is
this, to profess so often and without necessity that authentic Scripture
must be corrected as corrupt and false?

Fourth, the common reconciliation of ancient and recent, Romanist and
Protestant writers contains no absurdity: the years of servitude are counted
and absorbed within those of the following judges. Otherwise the clear and
express total in 1 Kings cannot be preserved. Many strong arguments support
this common solution; we shall touch a few.

First, the years of some servitudes are openly merged with the years of the
following judges. Deborah is said to have judged Israel during the Canaanite
servitude (Judges 4:4); its twenty years are therefore not to be distinguished
from Deborah’s forty. Samson judged Israel in the days of Philistine
servitude (Judges 15:20); his twenty years likewise are not separated from
the Philistine oppression. The same account extends to the other servitudes.

[Printed p. 48 | IA leaf 78 | access PDF 78 | OCR object 77]

Second, according to Augustine, in various Scriptures one period is
understood to contain another. Thus the 430 years of Exodus 12:40 are not
only the 210 during which the people dwelt in Egypt but the entire interval
from Abraham’s first calling to the exodus. Likewise Terah’s 205 years in
Genesis 11:32 include not only the years at Haran but all his life. The 150
days in Genesis 7:24 during which the waters prevailed include the preceding
forty days of rain, although these are counted separately in verses 12 and
17.

Third, our adversaries are forced to absorb within the years of servitude all
the time between a former judge’s death and the servitude’s beginning. It
must be supposed that after the judge’s death the people did not immediately
rush into public idolatry, and that even after they fell into that crime
divine patience granted time for repentance before imposing so severe a
punishment as prolonged slavery under foreign enemies. By what right do they
wholly neglect these intervals and absorb them in the servitudes, but refuse
others permission to merge the servitudes’ years into those of the following
judges?

Fourth, the separate counting of servitudes and judges, extended to 511
years, cannot stand with the history. Four persons—Rahab, her son Boaz, her
grandson Obed, and her great-grandson Jesse—would have to span so many years
before David that Boaz, Obed, and Jesse must each have been 120 when begetting
sons; harder still, Rahab, married to Salmon at sixteen or twenty, must have
passed one hundred when she bore Boaz. To solve this, some allege three men
named Boaz, two of whom Scripture always omits although they belong to
Christ’s genealogy—an absurdity too gross to need refutation.

Vossius’s second argument is from Paul in Acts 13:20: he teaches that from the
allotment or division of Canaan until Samuel were 450 years. Before the
division were forty-seven years from the exodus, and after Samuel until the
temple’s foundation eighty-four; together these make 580. Answer: first,
this number does not agree with the expanded years of Judges, which exceed
the Apostle’s number by eleven. Therefore the Apostle does not speak of the
judges. Rounding has no place here, and Paul’s mitigating *about* should not
be stretched to eleven years.

Second, according to most Romanists and Protestants, the Apostle speaks not
of the judges or their period, but from Isaac’s birth, when the promise first
made to Abraham began to be fulfilled. From that beginning to the allotment
there are exactly 447 years, which are about 450.

The ancient Latin interpreter, followed to this day by every Latin codex,
reads so that the 450 years do not follow the allotment and the beginning of
the judges but plainly precede it: “Having distributed their land by lot,
after about four hundred and fifty years, and after these things he gave
judges until Samuel.” Retain this reading, and there is no doubt the years
close at Joshua’s allotment and begin with Isaac’s birth. Why should it not
be retained? Beza admits that the Latin interpreter could have had Greek
manuscripts which he followed, that one of Beza’s own Greek manuscripts
departed from the common reading, and that a Paris Greek codex agreed exactly
with the Latin; yet he gives no reason for saying it was corrupted from the
Latin. Beza sometimes corrects the common reading on the authority of one
codex; here we are not reduced to such scarcity. Walton’s Greek variants on
Acts 13:20 report that the Marquis Velesius, collator of sixteen Greek New
Testament manuscripts—eight then in Spain’s royal Escorial library—read with
the Latin: “after four hundred and fifty years.” The Frankfurt Greek Bible,
which Francis Junius caused to be edited from old manuscripts apparently of
the Palatine library at Heidelberg, agrees with the Latin. An old Greek
manuscript in New College, Oxford, reads likewise.

[Printed p. 49 | IA leaf 79 | access PDF 79 | OCR object 78]

Surely so many Greek manuscripts from French, German, Spanish, and British
libraries, especially when they agree with other holy Scriptures, can
establish a reading different from the common one.

Third, even if the common Greek reading is retained, the words may suitably
be explained of the times preceding the judges rather than of the judges’
period. In the common Greek text the 450 years precede the sending of the
judges and are distinguished from it by a comma. See the notes in our English
Bibles on verse 20 of Acts 13: they retain the common reading and expressly
explain the years from Isaac’s birth to Joshua’s seventh year. I mean the
first Genevan English notes. The newer, more expansive notes often displease
elsewhere and do so here. At 1 Kings 6:1 they count the times correctly, but
in commenting on Acts they seem to contradict themselves by explaining the
450 years of the judges, which cannot agree with their count in Kings. They
also inconsiderately include Moses’, Joshua’s, and Samuel’s times in Paul’s
calculation, though the Apostle expressly excludes them: if the judges’ time
is intended, the beginning is neither Moses nor Joshua but the allotment, and
the terminus is the beginning of Samuel’s government. If Moses’ forty years,
Joshua’s seven before the allotment, and Samuel’s twenty are added, Paul’s
number must extend to 517.

Fourth, the first English annotators, who count the time here from Isaac in
opposition to the second and third sets, follow Junius, who in his Bible and
notes on Acts 13:20 arranges his reasoning the same way and defends it at
length in book 1 of his *Parallels*. Ludovicus de Dieu follows the same path
in his notes. I pass over very many others.

Fifth, these 450 years do not fit the times of the judges but fall short by a
full hundred if we stand by Scripture’s calculation in 1 Kings 6:1. For this
reason Luther, Beza, and Cajetan asserted that Acts was corrupted and, as
said, read *three hundred and fifty* contrary to every copy. No such grave
difficulty presses us, who refer the years to the times before the judges.

Vossius’s third argument is that many ancient writers abandon the calculation
of 1 Kings 6; he enumerates seventeen opinions. Answer: first, the entire
argument and every part of it belongs to the Jesuit Serarius, whose name
Vossius nevertheless suppresses. Second, Vossius or Serarius should not place
Melchior Cano, Glycas, Nicephorus, and the like among the ancients. Third,
all seventeen have little weight compared with the world of learned ancient
and recent writers who defend the integrity of 1 Kings 6. Fourth, it is no
wonder that those who depart from the straight rule stray into a thousand
byways; all who follow Scripture’s standard here walk without exception in
one plain and sound way. Fifth, the author of Serarius’s argument embraces a
number that rightly displeases Vossius: though Vossius errs one hundred years,
Serarius errs two hundred, reading 680 instead of 480, and so cannot please
him. Sixth, of all seventeen opponents of 1 Kings 6 Vossius can follow none.
He professes agreement with Sulpicius Severus alone, but Sulpicius’s chronology
is neither accurate nor of great authority. Passing over what I judge a
typographical contradiction—his placing the temple in both the 580th and
580th year—Vossius cannot agree with him under any reading. The common
codices of Sulpicius count 588; those emended by Carlo Sigonio 510; Vossius
defends only 580, while admitting the servitudes exceed the 480 years of the
judges by eleven. Added, these make not 580 but 591. Whoever wishes to see
all Vossius’s arguments, borrowed from Jesuits Serarius, Cano, and Petavius,
more fully refuted should consult Heinrich Philipp and Torniellus in their
dissertations on this question.

Cappellus adds some things to Vossius in his *Sacred Chronology* in Walton’s
tenth prolegomenon. His comments on jubilees and sabbatical years do not bear
on the present matter, apart from the fact that his additions of several
centuries to the true and received chronology destroy all legitimate use of
sabbatical and jubilee years. But he has one proper argument which must not
be omitted—

[Printed p. 50 | IA leaf 80 | access PDF 80 | OCR object 79]

—namely, that it should not be surprising if 1 Kings 6:1 is corrupt, since
many other scriptural passages dealing with numbers are openly corrupt. He
introduces five which, with many others, he claims in his *Sacred Criticism*
to demonstrate are corrupt. Answer: first, even if Cappellus had proved
several thousand places in the Old Testament corrupt—which he dares attempt,
but with most miserable success—it would conclude nothing about our passage.
No mortal ever wasted so much time and paper assigning corruptions to the
Hebrew text as Ludovicus Cappellus. How insulting this is to both God and men
the replies of Buxtorf abundantly show. Nevertheless, we shall answer
something concerning the five passages alleged here.

The first is 2 Chronicles 16:1 and 15:19, where no war is said to have
occurred between Baasha and Asa until Asa’s thirty-fifth year, yet Baasha came
against Judah in Asa’s thirty-sixth. Cappellus says the passages are
necessarily corrupt: the former must delete ten years and read twenty-sixth
for thirty-sixth, and the latter must delete the words *and thirty*, reading
only *until the fifth*. The reason is 1 Kings 15:33, where Baasha began to
reign in Asa’s third year and reigned twenty-four years, thus dying in Asa’s
twenty-seventh and being unable to wage war after death.

Answer: first, why should the places in the later Chronicles be corrected by
Kings rather than Kings by them? Second, Walton’s rule is to admit an error
in the Hebrew text only when all Hebrew copies and ancient versions do not
agree; yet every Hebrew manuscript and printed codex and every Latin, Greek,
Syriac, Arabic, Dutch, French, English, and Italian version contradicts
Cappellus’s correction. Pererius reports from Grotius that some read according
to these corrections, but neither says who. The Jesuit Cano, after Cajetan,
pronounces the passages corrupt but can produce no codex in any language
reading otherwise. His associates sharply censure his dangerous and
scarcely pious conjectures. It least became a Reformed professor to borrow
dangerous and impious corrections of authentic Scripture from a few
Romanists when most Jesuits reject them as absurd. The Greek interpreter in
the Vatican and London Alexandrian codices reads thirty-eighth instead of
thirty-sixth, which does not support Cappellus but opposes him even more than
the Hebrew. The error in some Greek codices is corrected by others: the
Complutensian and Arias Montano’s Royal edition read thirty-sixth with the
Hebrew. No correction is needed, for the distinction given earlier between
the years of King Asa and the years of Asa’s kingdom—the kingdom of Judah
separated from Israel—reconciles every apparent contradiction. The most
learned Protestants and Romanists rest in this answer, including Junius,
Diodati, the Dutch and English annotators, Torniellus, Salian, Petavius, and
Heinrich Philipp. Cappellus offers neither reason nor authority for diverging
from the common path toward a precipice, while Buxtorf argues against him at
length.

The second passage condemned by Cappellus is 2 Chronicles 22:2, where
Ahaziah is said to have been forty-two when he began to reign, while 2 Kings
8:26 says twenty-two. Cappellus therefore reads twenty-two in Chronicles.
Answer: the conclusion is too rash and borrowed from those who show little
reverence for authentic Scripture—Cajetan, Torniellus, Petavius, and others.
The Hebrew has no variant among *qere* and *ketiv*, Ben Asher and Ben Naphtali,
eastern and western Jews; all Hebrews always and everywhere read forty-two,
and every Latin manuscript and printed codex follows them. There was never a
Chaldean paraphrase of Chronicles. Greek editions vary and therefore have
little authority here: the Wechel edition reads twenty-two, the Vatican and
London Alexandrian twenty, but the Complutensian and Montano’s Royal agree
with the Hebrew. The Syriac and Arabic follow Wechel’s Greek. Since every
old and new Hebrew codex, every Latin, many excellent Greek copies, and all
recent French, English, Dutch, Italian, and Spanish interpreters support the
Hebrew, Cappellus should not assail the authentic text as corrupt.

[Printed p. 51 | IA leaf 81 | access PDF 81 | OCR object 80]

Various learned men offer reconciliations with 2 Kings 8, as we said earlier;
consult Petavius, Salian, Torniellus, Heinrich Philipp, the English and Dutch
annotators, and the many they cite. None offers a worse or more desperate
reconciliation than Cappellus, who breaks a hard knot with a much harder
wedge, a rash correction of sacred text that is not at all necessary.
Cappellus began, as he admits, to collect errors in the authentic text when
only twenty-five and persisted in this grievous labor until late old age and
his final breath. It is sad that so learned a man wasted so many years on so
bad a work.

The third place Cappellus calls manifestly corrupt is 2 Chronicles 36:9,
where Jehoiachin is said to have attained the kingdom in his eighth year,
while 2 Kings 24:8 says he began in his eighteenth. Answer: Cappellus wrongly
follows the Jesuit Cano and other Romanists in spitting without cause upon
Holy Scripture. When some formerly attempted this, Whitaker called their
position exceedingly erroneous and scandalous, leaving men in doubt about
all Scripture. Every Hebrew codex reads this place in Chronicles the same;
no variant has ever been alleged. All Latin codices and recent vernacular
interpreters agree. The best Greek codices, Vatican and Complutensian, agree;
some Greek copies followed by the Syriac and Arabic have eighteen, but this
does not prejudice Hebrew truth. We treated Cappellus’s reasoning earlier.
Whoever wants more should consult Heinrich Philipp, Salian, Torniellus, and
especially Buxtorf’s defenses, and will wonder that Cappellus persisted in a
youthful error against so many eminent men. Even his great teacher Grotius
acknowledges no error in the sacred text here, though he follows Junius in a
reconciliation we scarcely judge sound.

The fourth passage condemned is 2 Kings 14:21, where Rehoboam is said to have
begun to reign in his forty-first year. Cappellus subtracts twenty-nine years
and makes him begin in his twelfth. Answer: this is astonishing boldness in
so often correcting Scripture without necessary reason. We treated the
matter earlier. Every interpreter, old and new, follows the present Hebrew
reading: Jonathan’s Targum; every Septuagint edition; Syriac and Arabic; all
Latin and all current vernaculars. More important, the author of 2 Chronicles
12:13 confirms it. Grotius notes that “some” read the twenty-first year but
does not identify them. If he means Sulpicius Severus, he knew Sulpicius has
little chronological authority, especially against Scripture.

Cappellus’s supporting reasons have little weight. First: Solomon reigned
only forty years and was very young when he received the kingdom. Answer:
he was not so young that he was not twenty or twenty-two. The unsupported
rabbinic fable, followed by Jerome in letter 132 to Vitalis, makes Solomon
receive the kingdom and beget Rehoboam at eleven or twelve; but both differ
greatly from Cappellus’s correction concerning Rehoboam’s accession.

Second: before the kingdom Solomon was under the pedagogy of Nathan the
prophet, who would never have permitted marriage with an Ammonite contrary
to Deuteronomy 23:1. Answer: Solomon was never under Nathan so that his
marriage depended on him. Even if he had been, Deuteronomy did not exclude
Ammonite or Moabite women marrying Israelites when they became proselytes;
no one ever declared Ruth’s first or second marriage illegitimate. Although
Egyptians could not enter Israel’s congregation until the third generation,
that did not invalidate Solomon’s marriage with Pharaoh’s Egyptian daughter
in the first generation, nor David’s with the daughter of Talmai king of
Geshur. Uriah the Hittite and Zelek the Ammonite were among David’s leading
captains. If I denied that Rehoboam’s mother was Ammonite because the best
Septuagint editions, the London Alexandrian and all Latin copies, read
“Ammanite” rather than “Ammonite,” I would follow innovating Cappellus; far be
it from me to infect the Hebrew fountain with the mud of a translator’s
rivulet in any language.

[Printed p. 52 | IA leaf 82 | access PDF 82 | OCR object 81]

Third: Pharaoh’s daughter was Solomon’s first wife, whom he married after
beginning to reign; therefore he had none before. Answer: this conjecture is
not merely gratuitous but contrary to Scripture, from which it necessarily
follows that Rehoboam was born before David’s death. In which year Solomon
married each of his seven hundred wives cannot be determined without rashness
while Scripture is silent.

Fourth: Rehoboam is called young and a child when he began to reign (2
Chronicles 10:8; 13:7). Answer: Solomon too was called a child at his reign’s
beginning because of little experience in governing, although David then
honored him with the title of a wise man. In any case those passages affirm
nothing similar about Rehoboam.

Cappellus’s last reason: Solomon did not marry Rehoboam’s Ammonite mother
until love of foreign women turned his heart from God, which did not happen
before his eighteenth or twentieth regnal year. Answer: that Solomon loved
no foreign woman before the eighteenth year is asserted without proof. That
a foreign woman turned his mind from sincere worship in that year is contrary
to Scripture, which places it in Solomon’s old age; his eighteenth year was
far from old age. God appeared to him a second time in his twenty-fourth
regnal year after he had finished all his building: the temple in the
eleventh and the other buildings, on which thirteen further years were spent.
According to Cappellus he could not marry Rehoboam’s mother before then, so
in Solomon’s fortieth and final year Rehoboam could not be over fifteen—an
assertion no one but Cappellus makes, so far as I know.

The fifth passage, 2 Chronicles 12:13, is identical with the preceding and
needs no other defense. These are the five principal texts Cappellus selected
from his most ample store. If the answer to them is so easy, the same
judgment must be made of all the rest, however numerous.

### QUESTION III

#### Where the years of servitude should be placed

Third it is asked how the true total of 480 years is possible when, besides
them, we read of six servitudes: eight years before Othniel (Judges 3:8),
eighteen before Ehud (3:14), twenty before Deborah (4:3), seven before Gideon
(6:1), eighteen before Jephthah (10:8), and forty before Samson (14). Together
these make 111 above the stated sum. Answer: according to the received view
of ancient and recent theologians and chronologers, the years of servitude
are included and absorbed within the years of the following judges. No other
account can preserve the clear, express total in 1 Kings. Just as the forty
days of rain in Genesis 7:11 are numbered and included within the following
150 days in verse 24, the common calculation must be admitted in the fifth
and sixth servitudes; careful examination will show that the same reasoning
applies to each of the other four.

### QUESTION IV

#### How the ages of Rahab, Boaz, Obed, and Jesse could exhaust the later 480 years

Fourth it is asked how so many years are counted from the exodus to Solomon’s
fourth year when the entire time is completed by only three or four
generations. In the first year after the crossing Salmon married Rahab of
Jericho and begot Boaz; Boaz by Ruth the Moabitess begot Obed; and from him
came David. Subtract from 480 the forty years before the crossing and Rahab’s
marriage, David’s seventy years, and Solomon’s first four—that is, 114. The
remaining 366 must be distributed among Rahab, Boaz, Obed, and Jesse, not
through their entire lives but only to the times when they begot children.
This division troubles the most exact chronologers. Some, after Lyra and the
rabbis, invent three men named Boaz to escape it; but since no shadow of
God’s word supports this triplication, most reject it.

Others suppose Rahab married as early and gave birth as late as possible. At
Jericho’s capture, as a woman living apart from her parents and receiving
strangers in her house, she appears to have been at least sixteen. A year
must be allowed before all ceremonies for a Canaanite woman’s entrance into
Israel’s lineage and a solemn princely wedding could be celebrated. Thus
Rahab married Salmon in her seventeenth year. She may have borne any number
of children at any time but produced Boaz in her fifty-sixth. Histories of
women giving birth at that age are frequent, even recently. Some defer Boaz’s
birth to his mother’s seventy-second or ninety-fifth, but we do not wish to
multiply miracles without necessity. Rahab therefore bore Boaz at fifty-six,
in Othniel’s twenty-second year and the thirty-ninth after the crossing.

[Printed p. 53 | IA leaf 83 | access PDF 83 | OCR object 82]

Subtract these thirty-nine from the stated 366. The remaining 327 are to be
distributed among the births of Obed, Jesse, and David. If divided equally
or nearly so, each father receives 108 years at his son’s generation. On this
calculation Boaz begot Obed by Ruth in his 108th year, Deborah’s eighth;
Obed begot Jesse also in his 108th, at the very beginning of Jephthah’s
government; and Jesse begot David in his 108th, Samuel’s tenth.

It will be objected that it seems hard to extend not only life but vigor for
begetting to 108 in those centuries. Answer: although even in Moses’ days
Psalm 90 confines ordinary human age within the seventy or eighty years that
still commonly contain human life, later and present centuries display no
infrequent ordinary transgressions of those limits. Pliny famously testifies
that in his days, under the Vespasians, Italy’s censuses found two men aged
156, at Bologna and Rimini; in Italy’s eighth district, fifty-four aged one
hundred, fifty-seven aged 110, two aged 125, four aged 130, four aged 137,
and three aged 140. What wonder, then, if men in much earlier centuries,
especially among the Lord’s ancestors, lived or even begot children at 108?
We read that Jehoiada the high priest lived to 130 (2 Chronicles 24), and
Abraham married Keturah at 140 and begot many children by her.

In our own age several are reported to have passed one hundred in vigorous
old age. Master Colin Rhannus lives today at Perth. Once schoolmaster there
and afterward a minister in Scotland and Ireland until sixty, he judges
himself 110. This is quite credible, since he proves that he saw not only John
Knox, George Buchanan, the regent Moray, Queen Mary, Cardinal Beaton, George
Wishart, and John Major, but even the Carthusian and other monastic houses of
Perth standing whole and undamaged before their destruction in the first
Reformation. This most vigorous old man conversed with me in 1650 with such
health and strength that he seemed able to survive many years more.
