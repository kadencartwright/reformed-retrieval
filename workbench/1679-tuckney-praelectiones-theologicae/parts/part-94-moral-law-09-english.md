[Marginalia: Arguments. 1. From the law itself.]

Arguments proving the same

First, they may be drawn from the law itself

1. In the first place, the very manner in which this moral law was delivered seems to commend this point. While the other laws were delivered through Moses, the Decalogue was clearly pronounced by God's own mouth, twice engraved by his own finger, and inscribed on stone tablets. By this he wished to signify not only the hardness of the Israelite people's hearts but also the perpetual duration of this law, just as Pliny says that public constitutions were engraved on bronze tablets to perpetuate their memorial, and Plato commanded laws to be inscribed on cypress tablets as being more durable than bronze. Finally, it was deposited in the ark of the covenant, not only for greater reverence but as a perpetual memorial of the matter.

[Marginalia: Book 34, chapter 11.]

Yet perhaps these things should be numbered among the merely persuasive considerations, which only commend assent. What follows compels even the unwilling to assent.

2. The moral law, a ray from the eternal Sun, is the imprint of divine Wisdom. It represents to the life not only God's will, as I may say with Peter Martyr, but also his nature and character—God's very own image. For the apostle says in Ephesians 4:24 that this image of God consists in holiness and righteousness, the very two heads of the law's two tables in Luke 1:75. Even under the gospel it remains incumbent upon us to be conformed to God in these things, Matthew 5, final verse, and 1 Peter 1:15. Consequently, those who deny that the law requires us to be holy and righteous would likewise deny that God himself is holy and righteous.

[1679 new-series fol. 33 | IA leaf 353]

Let our adversaries therefore beware lest, while wishing to be Antinomians, they at last become atheists. For God cannot deny himself, and it is therefore impossible for him to abrogate this law, which is the pattern of his own holiness and righteousness. I am not ignorant of what the more impudent Scholastics commonly dispute on this subject, nor that Ockham concedes that God could command even hatred of himself. For us, meanwhile, God is supremely good, holy, righteous, and to be worshiped eternally. Therefore his most holy law, which commands these things, can never be relaxed to this extent by equity, interpretation, or dispensation, much less abrogated—not even by God, much less by a God-fighting Antinomian.

3. Just as the moral law is, so to speak, the imprint of the divine nature, so it is also a transcript of that primordial law of human nature. For apart from the assignment of the seventh day in the fourth commandment, which belongs to positive law, the whole remainder belongs to the law of nature and can be deduced by a valid consequence from the principles of natural law. Philip treats this point commandment by commandment in his *Loci*, as does Vázquez in disputation 179, chapter 2.

[Marginalia: A Tertullianic expression.]

Socinus, to be sure, denies that this law was given to Adam and that Adam could have sinned in that state. This is his usual manner, by which he turns everything upside down. As he wickedly tears up other most solidly founded truths with nefarious audacity, so in this matter he impiously contradicts the holy apostle in Romans 5, the common experience of everyone, and indeed his own conscience. From Holy Scripture we believe that Adam was created in the image of God, that this image consists in holiness and righteousness—the two heads of the moral law—and consequently that this law, which Cicero calls not written but inborn, was originally implanted in his heart. Indeed, from this we prove irrefutably that the most righteous God requires obedience to it from us after the fall. He could not justly do this if he had never given the power to render that obedience; but if he did not give it in Adam, he never did. Thus this law was inscribed on Adam's heart in his state of integrity, and it has not been utterly effaced either from us or from Gentiles most alienated from God, in whom the apostle recognizes “the written law” in Romans 2:15. Those characters were admittedly weak and faint, almost effaced by that most wretched fall and, further, by the corrupt habit of life. Therefore, through God's supreme kindness toward us in our ruin, the law was engraved anew as though on Sinai and, as the apostle says in Galatians 3:19, was “added because of transgressions.” Finally, it was confirmed by the Lord in the gospel, who gives us a commandment that is new and yet not new in 1 John 2:7–8: new with respect to sanctions, example, motives, and other circumstances, but not new in substance; rather, it is ancient enough to be coeval with the very beginnings of creation and implanted in nature itself. Grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, and among all sounder thinkers it has always been considered heretical to claim that natural law ceases under grace. Therefore one who contends that the natural law is abrogated under the gospel is no less a monster of nature than a prodigy of heresy and impiety.

Several other arguments remained, drawn partly from the nature of the law and partly from the grace of the gospel, which I willingly pass over.

There was also a third class of arguments, drawn from the dreadful consequences of the Antinomian opinion both for the corruption of doctrine and for the most wretched ordering of life. But I do not wish to stir this Camarina, lest, after already causing weariness by a rather lengthy discourse, I should add nausea at these filthy things.
