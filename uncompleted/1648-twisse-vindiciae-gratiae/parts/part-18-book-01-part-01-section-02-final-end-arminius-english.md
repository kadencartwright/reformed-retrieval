## SECTION TWO

### PERKINS

The final end of predestination is the declaration of God’s glory, partly in his mercy and partly in his justice.

### ARMINIUS

[Marginalia: On the final end of predestination.]

I approve what you say concerning the final end of predestination, if it is rightly understood—namely, if the declaration of God’s glory through mercy and justice is attributed to predestination insofar as it is the foreordination to eternal life of sinners who will believe in Christ, and, contrariwise, the fore-condemnation to eternal death of sinners who will persevere in their sins. They will believe, I say, by God’s gracious gift, while those who persevere in sins will do so through their own malice and God’s just desertion.

2. But if you understand it thus: that God from eternity, apart from any preexistence of sin in his foreknowledge, determined to illustrate his glory through mercy and punitive justice; and that, so that he might bring this about, he decreed to create the human being good but mutable and also ordained that he should fall, so that in this way there might be room for that decree—I say that, in my judgment, this opinion cannot be established by any word of God. So that this may become clearer, a few things must be said about God’s glory and the ways of manifesting it.

3. No one can doubt that, since God is the first and supreme efficient cause of all his acts and works, and also the sole and unique cause of many of them, he has always set before himself, as the highest and ultimate end, the manifestation of his perfection, that is, his glory. For the first and supreme cause is moved to act by nothing outside itself; otherwise it would not be the first and supreme cause. Therefore not only the act of predestination but every other divine act has the illustration of the divine glory as its final end. It is equally certain and known to all who have so much as greeted Holy Scripture from the threshold that the manifestation of divine perfection and the illustration of his glory consist in the unfolding of his essential attributes through acts and works conformed to them. But it requires inquiry to determine through the unfolding of which attributes he decreed to illustrate his glory first of all, through which second, and so on. It is certain, however, that he could not do so first through mercy and punitive justice. The former can be exercised only toward the miserable, and the latter only toward sinners. But since God’s first action of all *ad extra* was—and had to be, so to speak—occupied with nothingness, it is evident that goodness, wisdom, and omnipotence had to be unfolded first of all and God’s glory illustrated through them. These, therefore, were unfolded in creation, from which God appeared supremely good, supremely wise, and omnipotent.

4. But since God created all creatures with this distinction, that some should be only what they were created to be while others should be capable of a greater perfection, he is engaged about the former by preservation and government, administered by the same goodness, wisdom, and power and in the same manner. For preservation is merely the continuation of creation, just as creation is its beginning, and government does not exceed their natural condition, except where it has seemed good to God to use them, for the sake of human beings, toward supernatural ends—as he used bread and wine at Cana to signify and seal to us the communion of Christ’s body and blood. But concerning those creatures which he made capable of greater perfection, such as angels and human beings, the same attributes indeed had to be unfolded, but in a far greater measure. In the former case a finite good was communicated, which individual creatures received as their own according to the diversity of their nature; here, however, the communication of the highest and infinite good, which is God, is instituted, for the blessedness of rational creatures consists in union with him. Reason required that this communication not take place apart from justice, and therefore he gave these creatures of his a law, in obedience to which he placed the condition under which that communication would occur. This, therefore, was the first decree concerning the final end of rational creatures and concerning the illustration of God’s glory through the highest goodness (highest, I say, with respect to the good to be communicated, not absolutely) and justice: through goodness joined to justice in those who were truly to become participants in the highest good through enjoyment; through punitive justice in those who were to make themselves unworthy of it by their disobedience. Here we see retributive justice first taking its place, rendering to obedience what belonged to it by God’s gracious promise and to disobedience what it deserved by God’s just threat: there, justice joined to goodness; here, punitive justice opposed to the gracious communication of the highest good—without any mention of mercy, unless mercy is considered as preserving from possible misery a creature which could fall into misery by its own fault. Mercy is not considered in this way when it is determined by a decree of predestination. That decree, however, was peremptory with respect to the angels, since in accordance with it some were saved and some condemned; and therefore both the predestination and the reprobation of angels were comprehended in it. But I do not now dispute what grace was prepared for them in predestination, in what it consisted, and what was denied to these others in reprobation.

5. With respect to human beings, however, it was not peremptory. God did not determine to deal with them according to that highest rigor of the law, but decreed to unfold all his goodness for their salvation, as Jehovah showed to Moses in these attributes of his: “Jehovah, mighty, merciful and compassionate, slow to anger and abundant in mercy and truth,” and so forth. Therefore the predestination and reprobation of human beings was not placed in that decree. For when Adam had sinned, all who were to arise from him in the natural course of propagation would now have been consigned to eternal condemnation without any hope of pardon. For the decree of predestination and reprobation is peremptory. Up to this point, therefore, neither the predestination of any human beings to life nor their reprobation to death had any place.

6. And since predestination and reprobation occur only in accordance with

those attributes through which human beings are immediately saved and damned—while the predestined are immediately saved through mercy and the reprobate are immediately damned through justice opposed to that mercy—it follows that there was no fixed predestination and reprobation of human beings toward whom mercy and the justice opposed to it could have no place. But they could have no place toward those who were neither miserable nor sinners. Furthermore, since predestination includes the means through which the predestined will certainly and infallibly attain salvation, while reprobation includes the denial of those same means; and since those means are remission of sins, renewal by the Holy Spirit, and his perpetual assistance all the way to the end—things necessary for and communicable to none but sinners—I conclude that there was no predestination and reprobation concerning human beings for whom those means were neither necessary nor communicable. Finally, since God cannot love any sinner unto salvation unless that sinner has himself been reconciled in Christ, it follows that predestination can have no place except in Christ. And since Christ was ordained and given to sinners, it is certain that predestination and the reprobation opposed to it could have no place before human sin—understand, before it was foreseen by God—and before Christ was ordained as Mediator and, indeed, had discharged in God’s foreknowledge the office of Mediator insofar as it pertains to reconciliation.

7. Nor does it follow from this that God created the human being with an uncertain end or failed to attain the end which he set before himself. For in the act of creation and in the act of glorification and its opposite, condemnation, he set before himself as his end the illustration of his glory, and he attained it: through goodness, wisdom, and power in the act of creation, and he attained it; through those same attributes, but in a greater measure and joined with justice, in glorification and condemnation, and he attained it.

8. Although the manner of illustrating glory through mercy—which is a definite mode of communicated goodness and its advance into a miserable creature—and through justice opposed to that mercy could have no place except upon the occasion of human sin, God’s decree does not for that reason depend upon a human being. For God foresaw from eternity what would be and freely disposed, according to his own judgment and compelled by no necessity, concerning the ordering of that future event to this end, as though he could not also procure glory for himself from human sin in another way. That God’s glory is not situated only in the illustration of mercy and the justice opposed to it is clear from the fact that otherwise he would not have obtained glory from the act of creation or from the predestination and reprobation of angels. It must also be understood that mercy is not an essential attribute of God distinct from goodness, but is comprehended in goodness itself as in its matrix and proceeds from goodness; indeed, it is goodness itself unfolding itself toward a sinful and miserable creature.

9. Therefore it can be said simply that in all his acts *ad extra* God decreed to declare his glory through goodness, wisdom, and omnipotence, with justice added wherever equity required it under the prescription of wisdom; but that he adapted the manner to the condition—or, rather, to the change in the object—about which he had determined to unfold those attributes. On this matter Tertullian speaks beautifully and learnedly in book 2, chapter 16, *Against Marcion*: “It is necessary,” he says, “that God employ all things on account of all people, as many feelings as there are causes: anger on account of the wicked, wrath on account of the ungrateful, indignation on account of the proud, and whatever is not expedient for the evil; likewise mercy on account of those who err, patience on account of those who do not repent, beneficence on account of those who deserve, and whatever the good need. He experiences all these things in his own manner, in which it is fitting for him to experience them, just as the human being, on whose account he experiences them, does the same things equally in his own manner.” Thus far Tertullian.

10. Predestination, however, was made not only from goodness considered simply, whose property indeed is to communicate itself to a creature, but also from the mode of mercy proceeding from that goodness into the miserable to remove misery; of grace in Christ proceeding from it into sinners to pardon sins; and of patience and longsuffering proceeding from the same goodness toward those who resist for a time and do not immediately obey their calling, in order to endure the delay of their conversion. Likewise reprobation was instituted not only from justice opposed to that goodness considered simply, but from justice tempered with some mercy and patience. For God with much forbearance and patience endures vessels of wrath fitted together for destruction. Romans 9:22.

11. From these matters so considered, with your good leave, most learned Perkins, may I be permitted to conclude that predestination has not been adequately defined or described by you. If anyone delights to consider the sequence and order of the objects of divine knowledge and will, he will be confirmed more and more in the truth of the things briefly proposed by us.
