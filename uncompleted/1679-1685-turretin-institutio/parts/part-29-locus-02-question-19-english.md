QUESTION XIX

ON THE SENSE OF SCRIPTURE

Is the sense of Scripture fourfold—literal, allegorical, anagogical, and
tropological? We deny, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: The Roman Catholic position on the sense of Scripture.]

I. In order to thrust upon us another visible judge of controversies besides
Scripture and the Holy Spirit speaking in it—namely, the church and the
pope—the Roman Catholics ascribe a manifold sense to Scripture and consequently
conclude that it is doubtful and equivocal. They therefore distinguish the
sense into literal and mystical, and then divide the mystical into three
parts: allegorical, tropological, and anagogical. They call it allegorical when
sacred history is transferred to matters of faith, as when what

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 135 | IA leaf 189 | PDF p. 189]

is said in Galatians 4:22 about the two covenants, or Sarah and Hagar, is so
transferred; anagogical when the words of Scripture are transferred to matters
of the age to come, as what is said about rest in Hebrews 4:3; and tropological
when they are transferred to morals. This is expressed in the familiar verse:

The letter teaches events; allegory, what you should believe;
The moral sense, what you should do; anagogy, where you should tend.

[Marginal heading: And the position of the orthodox.]

II. We hold that only one true and genuine sense belongs to Holy Scripture, but
that this sense can be twofold, either simple or composite. The simple and
historical sense contains the declaration of one thing without signifying any
other, and concerns either precepts, doctrines, or histories. This in turn is
twofold: either proper and grammatical, or figurative and tropological. The
proper sense arises from words used properly; the tropological sense, from
words used figuratively. The composite or mixed sense occurs in oracles that
have the character of a type, one part of which lies in the type and the other
in the antitype. These do not constitute two senses, but two parts of one and
the same sense intended by the Holy Spirit, who had the mystery in view along
with the letter. Thus in the oracle, “You shall not break a bone of it,” Exodus
12:46, the full sense cannot be obtained unless the truth of the type, or the
Passover lamb, is joined with the truth of the antitype, or Christ, from John
19:36.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

III. The literal sense is not said to be only that which is elicited from
proper and nonfigurative words—in which case it would be distinguished from the
figurative sense, as the Fathers also sometimes used the term—but that which is
intended by the Holy Spirit and expressed by either proper or figurative words.
Thus Thomas defines the literal sense as “that which the Holy Spirit, or the
author, intends.” Salmerón, volume 1, treatise 7, defines it as “that which the
Holy Spirit, the author of Scripture, first wished to signify through the
words, whether according to the proper meaning consistent with the grammar or
through tropes and transferred expressions.” Therefore the literal expression
(τὸ ῥητὸν) is not always to be sought in proper words, but also in
figurative ones. Thus we indeed retain the literal expression (τὸ ῥητὸν) in
the sacraments because we retain the sense intended by the Holy Spirit. Such
also is the sense of the parables the Lord used, in which account must always
be taken of his intention. Nor should only that be considered the literal
sense which signifies the thing offered as a similitude, but also that which
denotes its application. Hence there is always one sense, and it is literal,
by which some true thing is explained through such a similitude.

IV. That Scripture has one sense is evident: 1. From the unity of truth,
because truth is one and simple and therefore does not admit several senses;
otherwise it would be uncertain and ambiguous. 2. From the unity of form,
because one thing has only one essential form, while the sense is the form of
Scripture. 3. From the perspicuity of Scripture, which cannot admit various
foreign and diverse senses.

V. The question is not whether there is only one concept within the sense of
Scripture. For we acknowledge that one and the same sense often yields several
concepts, but ones subordinate and corresponding to one another, especially
in the composite sense that embraces the type and the antitype. Rather, the
question is whether one passage has several diverse and unsubordinated senses.
This is the position of Azor, *Moral Institutes*, book 8, chapter 2; Thomas,
part 1, question 1, article 10; Lyra; Gretser; Becanus; Salmerón; Driedo;
Bellarmine; and others.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VI. Distinguish the sense of Scripture from its application. The sense is one,
whether simple in histories, precepts, and prophecies plainly presented,
or composite in typological passages; whether proper, stated in proper words,
or figurative, stated in figurative words. But

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 136 | IA leaf 190 | PDF p. 190]

the application can be diverse with respect to instruction (διδασκαλίαν),
reproof (ἔλεγχον), correction (ἐπανόρθωσιν), and so forth, which are
the theoretical and practical uses of Scripture. Thus allegory, anagogy, and
tropology are not so much diverse senses as applications of the one literal
sense: allegory and anagogy pertain to instruction (διδασκαλίαν), while
tropology pertains to correction (ἐπανόρθωσιν).

VII. Allegory is either innate or introduced, either intended by the Holy
Spirit or invented by human beings. In the latter sense, it does not enter
into the sense of Scripture but is a consequence elicited by human study by
way of application. The former, however, is contained in the composite sense
as a part of it, because there can be no doubt that it was intended by the
Holy Spirit and therefore belongs to his meaning. Thus what is said in
Galatians 4 about Abraham's two wives is extended to the two covenants, and
what is said in Hebrews 4 about rest is extended to heavenly rest. Therefore,
when we advance from the sign to the thing signified, we do not introduce a
new sense but draw out what lay hidden beneath the sign, so that we may have
the whole and complete sense intended by the Holy Spirit.

VIII. Although God's understanding is infinite and can embrace many, indeed
infinite, things at once, it does not follow that the sense of Scripture is
manifold, because there is no valid inference from God's understanding to
God's words. Nor must the meaning of his statements be measured from the
fruitfulness of the speaker, which is infinite here, but from his definite and
determinate intention, accommodated to the capacity of the people to whom he
spoke. When God understands, he understands for himself as one who is infinite,
and therefore infinitely; but when he speaks, he speaks not to himself but to
us, that is, in accommodation to our capacity, which is finite and cannot
conceive several senses.

IX. In Ezekiel 2:10 and Revelation 5:1, the book written on the front and the
back, within and without, does not signify a twofold sense of one Scripture,
but the multitude of things written in both places: in the former, the plagues
to be inflicted upon the Jews, and in the latter, the decrees of God.

X. The difficulty of passages does not indicate a manifold intention on God's
part, but some ambiguity in the words and the weakness of our understanding.
Although words can signify various things in the abstract, yet in the concrete
setting of this or that passage they are referred by the Holy Spirit to only
one of these significations, which must be elicited from a consideration of
what precedes and follows and from the analogy of faith.

XI. The literal sense is sometimes taken broadly for the entire complex of the
sense intended by the Holy Spirit, whether in the type or in the antitype, and
in this way it includes the mystical sense under itself. At other times it is
taken more strictly for that which the words immediately and proximately
present, and in this way it is distinguished from the mystical sense, which is
signified not so much through the words as through the things signified by the
words and arises only mediately from the speaker's intention.

XII. Although we posit a composite sense, we nevertheless do not remove the
unity of truth and the certainty of Scripture, which we charge the Roman
Catholics with doing, because the truth proposed in those oracles embraces
various relations (σχέσεις), all of which are intended by the Holy Spirit.

XIII. Just as Scripture is exceedingly fruitful, containing more things than
words, so there is nothing absurd in saying that the Holy Spirit wished to
convey many things to us at once under one and the same expression, though
subordinated to one another, so that one is the sign and figure of the other or
they have some connection and dependence upon each other. Thus the promise
given to Abraham concerning his seed regarded both Isaac in the type and Christ
in the antitype, Galatians 3:16. The oracle about not breaking the bones of the
lamb, Exodus 12, pertained both to the Passover lamb figuratively and to Christ
mystically, John 19. The promise given to David, “I shall be a Father to him,”
and so forth, 2 Samuel 7, regarded both Solomon and Christ, Hebrews 1. The
prediction in Psalm 16 about the Holy One not seeing

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 137 | IA leaf 191 | PDF p. 191]

corruption was fulfilled in David, but imperfectly, and in Christ perfectly,
Acts 2:29–30. There are innumerable such passages of Scripture that contain
various relations (σχέσεις), all of which must be joined so that we may have
the full sense of the oracle. They must be fulfilled not all at once, but
successively and by degrees. Hence the ancient oracles generally had three
relations (σχέσεις): to the state of law in the Jewish church, to the state
of grace in the Christian church, and to the state of glory in heaven. Thus
the oracle in Isaiah 9:1 about the people walking in darkness and beholding a
great light had to be fulfilled in three stages: 1. In the deliverance from
Babylon. 2. In the preaching of the gospel, Matthew 4. 3. In the final
resurrection, when those who will sit in the valley of the shadow of death will
see the great light of God's glory. The same can be observed in the oracle in
Ezekiel 37 about the dry bones. It was already fulfilled when the people came
out of the most grievous captivity of Babylon as from a tomb, verse 12; it is
fulfilled daily in spiritual resurrection, Ephesians 5:14; and it will be
fulfilled perfectly in the final resurrection, John 5:25.

XIV. The various passages brought forward by the Roman Catholics to prove a
multiplicity of senses—Hosea 11:1 with Matthew 2:15; Psalm 2:7 with Acts 13:33;
and 2 Samuel 7:14 with Hebrews 1 and 5—do indeed show that there is a composite
sense consisting of type and antitype and fulfilled in various stages, first
in the type and second in the antitype. But they do not prove that there are
multiple senses wholly different in kind.

XV. The mystical sense is either sacred, which the Holy Spirit proposed
through the sacred writers and which is therefore founded in Scripture itself.
To this belong the passages in John 3:13 about the bronze serpent; Paul in 1
Corinthians 10:1–4 about the Israelites' baptism in the cloud and sea and their
spiritual food and drink; Galatians 4:22 about the allegory of Abraham's two
wives; and 1 Peter 3:21 about the ark and baptism. Or it is ecclesiastical,
because it is employed by ecclesiastical writers for the sake of either
illustration or delight. Philo the Jew first attempted this in his two books
of *Allegories*. Some of the ancients followed him, especially Origen, who
followed this kind of interpretation more than the rest and often failed to
observe due measure in it. Hence he is deservedly reproved by Jerome in the
letters to Avitus and Amabilis: “Origen regards the clever points of his own
genius as sacraments of the church.” Although this latter sense can serve for
illustration, it nevertheless has no force for proof, because it is a human,
not a divine, interpretation, which can offer a probable suggestion but cannot
persuade. The former, however, has force for proving doctrines of faith because
it has the Holy Spirit as its author and therefore belongs to his intention.
Thus the common saying that symbolic theology is not argumentative holds only
for human, not divine, allegories and parables.

XVI. A mystical sense is not found in every passage of Scripture, but is
legitimately admitted only where the Holy Spirit furnishes its occasion and
foundation. Here great care must be taken that it not be applied beyond the
intention of the Holy Spirit or extended beyond his purpose.

XVII. Just as there is some literal sense, whether proper or figurative, in
every passage of Scripture, so the sense of the letter in each passage is only
one, whether simple as in histories or composite as in types, although its
application can be diverse according to various theoretical or practical uses.

XVIII. To investigate the true sense of Scripture, interpretation is needed,
not only of words, which consists in versions, but also of things, which is
called prophecy by Paul, Romans 12:6, and interpretation (ἐπίλυσις) by Peter,
2 Peter 1:20. This is not to be sought from each person's private judgment,
which would truly be a private interpretation (ἰδία ἐπίλυσις), which Peter
rejects, but must be undertaken from Scripture itself, which is its own best
and most certain interpreter, Nehemiah 8:8; Acts

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 138 | IA leaf 192 | PDF p. 192]

17:11. For this purpose, after fervent prayers to God, there is need to inspect
the sources, know the languages, distinguish proper and figurative words,
observe the purpose and circumstances, compare passages, connect what precedes
and follows, remove prejudices, and conform interpretation to the analogy of
faith. All these can be referred to those three means: analysis (ἀνάλυσιν),
comparison (σύγκρισιν), and analogy (ἀναλογίαν). Analysis is threefold:
grammatical, which examines proper expressions; rhetorical, which examines
tropological expressions; and logical, which observes the purpose and
circumstances and attends to the interrelation of the words
(ἀλληλουχίαν). Comparison (Σύγκρισις) compares passages of Scripture
with one another, Acts 9:22, comparing more obscure passages with clearer ones,
like and parallel passages with like ones, and unlike passages with unlike
ones. The analogy of faith, Romans 12:6, signifies not only the proportion of
faith, or the measure granted to each believer, but the constant harmony and
agreement of all the heads of faith set forth in the clearer statements of
Scripture. All interpretations must conform to it, lest anything discordant
with the articles of faith or the precepts of the Decalogue be maintained.

XIX. One must not rashly and needlessly depart from the proper literal sense
unless it truly conflicts with the articles of faith and the precepts of love,
and unless the discourse is clearly shown to be figurative from the same or
other parallel passages. The surest criterion (κριτήριον) of figurative
speech is this: 1. If the words taken properly yield either no sense or an
absurd and impossible one, as when Christ is called the door of the sheep,
John 10, and the true vine, John 15:1. 2. If it conflicts with the analogy of
faith and clashes with some received doctrine, whether theoretical or
practical. For since it is certain that the Holy Spirit is always consistent
with himself, a sense that opposes other truths delivered by him cannot be
judged to proceed from him. Hence we conclude that the words of the Eucharist
must be understood tropologically, because the proper sense clashes with
various articles of faith about the reality of Christ's body, his ascension
into heaven, and his return for judgment. And the words of Hosea 1:1 must be
explained not properly but symbolically and allegorically, because an outrage
forbidden by the law—taking a prostitute as a wife—is commanded. To this
pertains Augustine's golden rule, *On Christian Doctrine*, book 3, chapter 16:
“If an imperative expression forbids either an outrage or a crime, or commands
either usefulness or beneficence, it is not figurative. But if it appears to
command an outrage or a crime, or to forbid usefulness or beneficence, it is
figurative.” The reason is that it belongs properly to God to command good
things because he is good and to forbid evil things most severely because he
is holy, although he often permits them to occur.

XX. The means that our adversaries bring forward besides this rule of
faith—such as the practice of the church, the consensus of the Fathers, and the
decrees of councils—quite apart from the fact that they are all referred back
to the judgment of one pope, are uncertain and supported by no foundation,
indeed impossible and contradictory. They encumber the mind with innumerable
difficulties instead of resolving them, as we are about to demonstrate in what
follows.
