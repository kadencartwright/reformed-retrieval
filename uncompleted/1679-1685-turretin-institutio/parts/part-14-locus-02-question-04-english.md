QUESTION IV

ON THE AUTHORITY OF HOLY SCRIPTURE

Is Sacred Scripture Truly Authentic and Divine?—Affirmative.

I. The authority of Scripture depends upon its origin, which was discussed in
the preceding questions; for because it is from God, it cannot fail to be
authentic and divine. From this arises the question concerning its authority,
which can have two branches. 1. The first is with atheists and pagans, who
acknowledge no authority in Scripture above other human books. 2. The second
is with Christians who, while acknowledging its authority, nevertheless wish
it, at least as it relates to us, to depend upon the testimony of the church.
With respect to the former, the question is whether Sacred Scripture is truly
“self-authenticating” (αὐτόπιστος) and divine. With respect to the latter,
however, it is how it is evident to us that Scripture is such, or on what
testimony faith in the “authenticity” (αὐθεντίας) of Scripture principally
rests. Here only the first question, not the second, is treated.

[Marginal heading: Authority of Scripture.]

II. Although the first question may seem scarcely necessary among Christians,
among whom it ought to be placed beyond all controversy that Scripture is
“God-breathed” (θεόπνευστον), as the primary foundation of faith,
nevertheless, because even today there are far too many atheists and
libertines among Christians who attempt in every way to undermine this most
holy truth, it is therefore of the greatest importance for salvation that our
faith be promptly fortified against the diabolical cavils of such impious
people.

III. The authority of Scripture, which is in question here, is nothing other
than the right and dignity of the sacred books on account of which they are
most worthy of belief in the things they set forth to be believed, and of
obedience in the things they command to be omitted or done. Its foundation is
the divine and infallible truth of these books, which have God as their author,
because he has the supreme right to obligate people to faith and obedience.
Now this authority can be either intrinsic or extrinsic. The former is the
“trustworthiness” (ἀξιοπιστία) of the Word itself in itself, which is always
equal and consistent with itself, whether human testimony is added or not.
The latter is people's estimation or judgment concerning Scripture, which
varies according to the character of the subjects.

[Marginal heading: Intrinsic and extrinsic.]

IV. Again, one kind of authenticity belongs to history or narration, and
another in addition belongs to truth and the norm. According to the former,
whatever is narrated in Scripture is entirely true just as it is narrated,
whether it is good or evil, or whether it is true or false. Those things are
said to possess the latter

[Marginal heading: Of history and of the norm.]

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 60 | IA leaf 114 | PDF p. 114]

which are so true in themselves that they are handed down as the norm of faith
and morals. Not everything that is in Scripture possesses the authenticity of
the norm, such as the things reported as spoken by the wicked or by the devil;
nevertheless, everything possesses the authenticity of history.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

V. The question is not whether the sacred writers, considered simply as men
and in a divided sense, could have erred, for we readily concede this; or
whether, as holy men moved by the Holy Spirit and in a compound sense, they
actually erred, for I think no one among our adversaries, unless plainly an
atheist, will dare to say this. Rather, the question is whether in writing
they were so moved and inspired by the Holy Spirit, both with respect to the
things themselves and with respect to the words, that they were immune from
every error and their writings are truly authentic and divine. The
adversaries deny it; we affirm it.

[Marginal heading: Scripture proves itself.]

VI. Scripture proves itself divine not only authoritatively and by way of an
inartificial argument, or testimony, when it calls itself “God-breathed”
(θεόπνευστον)—which, although it is properly employed against Christians who
profess that they receive it, nevertheless cannot be pressed against others
who reject it—but also ratiocinatively, by an artificial argument drawn from
the marks which God has impressed upon Scripture and which display
unmistakable proofs of divinity. For just as the works of God exhibit in fixed
characters, as though to be seen by the eyes, the incomparable excellence of
their maker, and just as the sun becomes known by its own light, so he willed
various rays of divinity to shine in Scripture, which is the “offspring”
(ἔκγονον) of the Father of lights and of the Sun of righteousness, by which it
might make itself known.

[Marginal heading: Through marks, which are either]

VII. Now these marks are either extrinsic or intrinsic. Although the former
cannot suffice for a full demonstration of the matter, they nevertheless have
great weight for its confirmation and for the conviction of those who
contradict it. But the principal strength of the argument is situated in the
latter.

[Marginal heading: External.]

VIII. The external marks are: 1. On the part of its origin, its primeval
antiquity above all pagan monuments, for, as Tertullian says, “Whatever is
first is truest.” 2. On the part of its duration, the wondrous preservation of
the divine Word down to this day by God's provident care against the most
powerful and bitterly hostile enemies, who wished to destroy it by sword and
flame, while so many other books against which nothing of the kind was
attempted have utterly perished. 3. On the part of the instruments and
amanuenses, the supreme candor and sincerity in writing of those who do not
conceal even their own “failings” (ἠττήματα), but disclose them frankly. 4. On
the part of its adjuncts, the number, constancy, and condition of the martyrs
who sealed it with their blood. For since nothing is dearer to a person than
life, so many myriads of people of every sex, age, and condition could not
have met even the cruelest death so willingly in defense of Scripture unless
they had been persuaded of its divinity. Nor would God have willed to employ
his omnipotence to perform the many and great miracles which were done in
both the Law and the gospel in order to establish belief in Scripture's
divinity if it had been a mere figment of the human brain. The testimonies of
the adversaries themselves are added, such as those of pagans concerning
Moses, of Josephus and the Talmudists concerning Christ, and of Mohammed
himself concerning both Testaments, which can be obtained from Vives,
Plessis, Grotius, and others. Finally, there is the agreement of peoples who,
although they differed in rites, in opinions even concerning sacred things,
in worship, language, and inclination, received this Word as a precious
treasure of divine truth and regarded it as the foundation of religion and
the worship of God. For it is not credible that God would have allowed so
great a multitude of people, who earnestly sought him, to be deceived so long
by lying books.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 61 | IA leaf 115 | PDF p. 115]

[Marginal heading: Or internal.]

IX. The internal marks, which are the chief ones, are also numerous. 1. On the
part of its subject matter, there is the wonderful sublimity of the mysteries
which could have been discovered by no sagacity of reason, such as the
Trinity, the incarnation, the satisfaction of Christ, the resurrection of the
dead, and the like; the holiness and purity of the precepts, which bring the
very thoughts and inward affections of the heart into order and are suited to
render a person perfect in every kind of virtue and worthy of God; and the
certainty of the oracles concerning even the most hidden and remote matters.
For to foresee and foretell future things that depend solely upon God's will
belongs to the Deity alone, Isaiah 41:23. 2. On the part of its style, there is
the divine majesty which shines forth no less from the simplicity than from
the gravity of the diction, and that supreme “boldness” (παρρησία) for
commanding everyone without distinction, both highest and lowest. 3. On the
part of its form, there is the divine agreement and “complete harmony”
(παναρμονία) of the doctrine, not only between the two Testaments through the
fulfillment of predictions and types, but also among the individual books of
both Testaments. This is the more wonderful in proportion as their writers
were more numerous and wrote at varying times and places, so that they could
not confer with one another about the things to be written. 4. On the part of
its end, there is the direction of all things to the glory of the one God and
to the holiness and salvation of people. 5. On the part of its effects, there
is the light and efficacy of divine doctrine, which is so great that, more
penetrating than any two-edged sword, it reaches the soul itself, produces
faith and piety in the souls of its hearers and unconquered constancy in
confessors, and always triumphs victoriously over the kingdom of Satan and
false religions. These criteria are indeed such that they cannot apply to any
human writing, which always displays proofs of human frailty, but establish a
truly divine writing, especially when they are considered not separately but
all “collectively” (ἀθρόως).

X. Yet these marks should not be thought to shine equally and to an equal
degree in all the books of Scripture. For just as one star differs from
another in light, so in this heaven of Scripture some books emit more splendid
and abundant rays of light and others far fainter and fewer, according as they
are more or less necessary to the church and contain doctrines of greater or
lesser importance. Thus the Gospels and Pauline Epistles radiate far more with
this splendor than the book of Ruth or Esther. Nevertheless, it is certain
that in them all there are proofs of truth and majesty which of themselves
establish the book as divine and authentic, or at least that nothing is found
in them that can render their “authenticity” (αὐθεντίαν) doubtful.

XI. Nor is it necessary that all these marks occur in every passage or verse
of a canonical book by which it is distinguished from an apocryphal book, or
in individual parts of Scripture separated from the whole. It is sufficient
that they are found in the divine writings as these are considered jointly
and as complete in themselves.

XII. Although false religions are accustomed to claim these criteria for
their own doctrine, the true religion will nevertheless be able to ascribe
them to itself no less justly, because the vain and false opinion of people
does not undermine the truth of the matter. Nor, if a Turk most falsely
attaches divinity to the Quran, or a Jew to his Kabbalah—since the fables and
lies with which each book is wholly stitched together sufficiently disclose
the contrary—will a believer therefore be unable truly to proclaim the
divinity of Sacred Scripture, in which he sees the most brilliant rays of
divine truth shining everywhere.

XIII. Although faith rests upon the authority of testimony and not upon
scientific demonstration, it does not follow that it cannot sometimes be
assisted by artificial arguments, especially in establishing the foundation
of faith. For before faith believes, it must have perceived the divinity of
the witness whom it ought to believe from certain marks which it discovers in
him; otherwise, it could not believe him. For where suitable reasons for
believing someone are lacking, the testimony of such a witness cannot be
“worthy of belief” (ἀξιόπιστον).

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 62 | IA leaf 116 | PDF p. 116]

[Marginal heading: The authenticity of the testimony of the prophets and
apostles.]

[Marginal heading: Who were not deceived.]

XIV. The testimony of the prophets and apostles is above every exception and
cannot reasonably be called into doubt by anyone. For if it were uncertain
and false, that would be either because they themselves were deceived or
because they themselves deceived others; but neither can be said. 1. They
were not deceived and could not have been deceived. For if they were deceived,
they were deceived either by another or by themselves. The former cannot be
said, because they were deceived neither by God, who just as he can be
deceived by no one can himself deceive anyone, nor by angels, who do not
deceive, nor by demons, because this whole doctrine tends toward the
destruction of the devil's kingdom. Nor can the latter be said. For if anyone
is mistaken about some matter, this occurs chiefly either because he did not
see it himself but received it from others upon whose testimony he relies, or
because he saw it only incidentally and in passing, or because the matter is
obscure and difficult and far exceeds human comprehension, or because the
subject is poorly disposed and hindered by some illness from judging rightly.
But nothing of this kind occurs here. For: 1. They profess that they did not
receive the things they narrate from an uncertain rumor and from others who
knew them less accurately, but that they had the most certain and thoroughly
tested knowledge of them, inasmuch as they had been eyewitnesses and
earwitnesses and had devoted great attention and effort to knowing them fully.
2. They do not speak about remote and distant events, but those performed in
the very places and at the time in which they were writing. Hence John says in
his first epistle, 1:1, “What we have seen with our eyes, what we have heard
concerning the Word of life, this we announce,” etc. 3. The subject was not
obscure matters placed in mere speculation, in which rude and unlearned people
might easily have been deceived by failing to grasp their sublimity, but facts
which met their senses and eyes—for example, the resurrection of Christ. They
had associated familiarly with him before his death, and after his
resurrection he had presented himself to be seen by them, not incidentally but
for a long time, not once but repeatedly, and not in the presence of one or
another but of many people of every sex and condition. 4. Finally, it cannot
be said that their faculty was poorly disposed. For besides the fact that they
display no signs of a corrupted imagination or a disordered mind, but indeed
manifest wisdom and a well-ordered mind in their words and life, there is also
the fact that not one or another but several in number think and speak the
same thing. It follows that there is no cause for them to be called deceived
here.

[Marginal heading: Nor did they wish to deceive.]

XV. But just as they were not deceived here, so neither did they wish to
deceive. For those who deceive and lie set before themselves some benefit from
the lie and deception, whether to capture glory, court people's favor, or
procure wealth and advantages for themselves. But in delivering this
testimony, what desirable thing was there for the men of God either while
living or when dead? While living, they met all those things to ward off which
people are not rarely accustomed to lie: poverty, exiles, tortures, and the
most extreme punishments; but after death, disgrace and eternal punishment.
Nevertheless, disregarding all these things, they knowingly and deliberately
did not hesitate, in order to confirm this testimony of theirs, to enter
countless dangers, undergo the gravest insults, and endure the most bitter
deaths. Who, therefore, would believe that they did not refuse to suffer such
things for something which they held to be uncertain and false, when even for
the things most certain and fully known scarcely—indeed, scarcely at all—are
people found who dare to risk either reputation or possessions, much less
meet death? Surely no one can arraign them as guilty of deliberately making so
enormous a lie without making them at once the most foolish and the most
wicked: the most foolish, because they willed to lie with no good to
themselves and with certain evil to themselves, contrary to the very doctrines
of the religion which they were writing and which so severely forbids lying;
and the most wicked, because by lying they willed to deceive the entire world
and, with no good to themselves, to involve it together with themselves in
every evil.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 63 | IA leaf 117 | PDF p. 117]

[Marginal heading: Nor could they.]

XVI. Finally, they could not have deceived even if they had wished to, because
they were not writing about remote and distant events, or events which had
been performed in ancient times or secretly, with witnesses far away, in some
corner—as impostors generally do so that they cannot easily be convicted of a
lie. Rather, they related things which had happened at that very time,
publicly and in the light of day, in the very place in which they were
writing, and indeed often while those to whom they were writing were seeing
and hearing them and could easily have uncovered the fraud and imposture if
there had been any. Therefore, if they were neither deceived nor deceivers,
there is no doubt that their testimony is divine, and thus that all the
doctrine which depends upon and rests on it is authentic.

XVII. One cannot call into doubt whether the prophets and apostles once
existed and wrote the books which are said to be theirs without making the
credibility of all antiquity totter and introducing Pyrrhonism. For in this
way one will be able to doubt equally all the other writings which circulate
in people's hands. But since it is certain that such books were written by
particular authors, what sane person would not believe that they were written
by those whose names they bear—which all Christian churches everywhere have
steadfastly believed, about which no controversy was ever raised by Jews or
pagans, and which was already received in the most ancient times when the
matter could be ascertained—rather than by any other person?

[Marginal heading: The authenticity of the books of Moses is shown.]

XVIII. Whatever can be brought forward to weaken the credibility of the
Mosaic history can easily be refuted if it is examined more deeply. For if
someone should perhaps deny that Moses ever existed or that he was the author
of the books ascribed to him, he will be convicted without difficulty: both
because not only Jews and Christians mention him, but also no small number of
profane writers; and because this has been steadfastly believed by a most
numerous people, and there is no cause for doubt unless we wish to overthrow
the credibility of absolutely all histories and deny that Plato, Aristotle,
Cicero, and others ever existed and wrote the books which bear their names,
which no one but a madman would say. Much less can this be said of Moses than
of the others, because no book should with greater apparent reason have been
abolished by the Jews, since in that way they would have freed themselves
from the very harsh yoke of the Law. Yet no book was received and preserved
by them with greater devotion and zeal; no book ever suddenly attained so
great an authority that it was regarded as divine Law and the norm of
religion, surely for no other reason than the conviction of the truth
contained in it.

XIX. Secondly, if someone driven from that position should retreat to another
and indeed acknowledge that Moses existed and composed the books attributed
to him, but assert that he was a notorious impostor and fabricator who imposed
upon the Israelite people with empty lies and false wonders in order to
enslave them to the Law which he was proposing and thus to himself, he will be
refuted no less easily. For besides the fact that even pagans and the most
hostile enemies, such as Porphyry in book 4 *Against the Christians*, grant
Moses the praise of being a truthful writer, it cannot easily be grasped how
that distinguished wisdom and the eminent virtues with which the whole life
of Moses shines can be reconciled with so abominable an imposture, or how he
could have contrived so admirable a Law—from which whatever good the other
laws possessed was borrowed, and which looked to the glory of the one God and
the holiness of the people—so that it would favor his own fraud and
imposture. Further, if he was an impostor, it is remarkable that he followed a
course completely contrary to his plan, by which he could easily have been
convicted of falsehood. For if the account of the origin of the world which he
handed down is false, nothing was easier than to convict him of lying from the
small number of generations enumerated from Adam to the flood and from the
flood to the departure of the people from Egypt, since in the time of Moses
there could still have survived

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 64 | IA leaf 118 | PDF p. 118]

various people who had seen Joseph, whose parent had seen Shem, who up to the
hundredth year of his life could have associated with Methuselah, still
surviving to that time, who had himself seen Adam. Hence the truth or falsehood
of the matter could have been discovered without difficulty. 3. If Moses was
an impostor who wished to deceive the Israelites, he undoubtedly hoped to
persuade the Israelites of his lies and frauds. But in what way could he bring
himself to suppose that he would persuade them of the many and great wonders
said to have been done either in Egypt or in the wilderness if nothing of the
kind had happened? This is especially so since he was writing to those who
ought to have been their earwitnesses or eyewitnesses, and since he was
writing not about things done many ages before, but at that very time, not
secretly and in a corner before a few witnesses who could easily have been
corrupted, but openly and publicly under the eyes of six hundred thousand
people and even of their most bitterly hostile enemies, who could have exposed
his lies. Could he have hoped that no one would be found among the people who
would doubt these things or inquire of the Egyptians into the truth of the
events? Is it credible that from so great a number of people, whom he
repeatedly rebuked most harshly as a rebellious and untamed nation, and even
afflicted from time to time with the most severe punishments, striking down by
sudden death not only hundreds but thousands and performing such deeds by
which they could deservedly have been exasperated against him, there would
have been no one who would charge him with his fraud and imposture, when they
are seen so often to murmur and rebel against him so unjustly? Finally, if he
acted as an impostor, he undoubtedly sought some advantage from it, whether
glory or riches, so that he might secure the rule for himself and his
descendants or obtain among people the praise of wisdom and heroic virtue.
But both the matter itself proclaims how far Moses was removed from desire for
gain or glory, and the sincerity with which he so frankly confesses his own
sins and especially his unbelief sufficiently attests it.

XX. But perhaps the Israelites, recognizing the falsehood of the events
related by Moses, agreed to the fraud and imposture in order to procure
greater glory for their nation. But who would dare believe that the
Israelites were so insane that they did not refuse to agree to so notorious an
imposture by which they were subjected to the “unbearable” (ἀβαστάκτῳ) yoke of
the very harsh Law, if they had been truly persuaded that such a Law was a
mere invention of Moses? Or can it be said with any appearance of truth that,
out of six hundred thousand people, every one of them agreed to such a fraud,
so that no one was found who opposed such a plan? 2. They were so far from
procuring glory and praise for themselves among others from it that, on the
contrary, they deservedly drew upon themselves everyone's hatred and
contempt. For who would say that it contributed to the honor of the nation
that their murmurings and most grievous sins were exposed to people's eyes;
that they were accused as “stiff-necked” (σκληροτράχηλοι) and the most
ungrateful of mortals; and that the most severe punishments with which God had
more than once avenged their obstinacy and rebellion were recounted? Indeed,
who does not see that these things redound to the nation's eternal disgrace?
In a word, there is no reason why so stiff-necked and “pleasure-loving”
(φιλήδονος) a people should have willed so readily to submit to the yoke of a
most burdensome Law which so severely punished the slightest transgression,
unless they had been persuaded of the divinity of Moses' call and the truth
of his words.

[Marginal heading: The conversion of the world proves Scripture's divinity.]

XXI. The conversion of the world and the success of the gospel are the
clearest proof of its divinity. For if the apostles had not been men of God
and brought heavenly truth, it cannot be conceived how it could have happened
that their doctrine, lacking all those supports by which any doctrine can be
commended and propagated by human means and most stubbornly opposed by all
those things with which any doctrine can be resisted—the authority of the
ancestors, the custom of peoples, the favor of princes, the eloquence of
orators, the subtlety of philosophers, and accommodation

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 65 | IA leaf 119 | PDF p. 119]

to human morals and disposition—was promulgated through a few rude and weak
men, utterly removed not only from the fraud but also the suspicion of
fabrication, furnished with no aid of eloquence, trained in no art of
dissimulation, despised and contemptible, by persuasion alone, without any
support of public authority and favor, without the aid of arms, through a
thousand deaths and dangers, and nevertheless was propagated in a very short
time almost everywhere so that it broke through every obstacle and emerged
victorious over other religions fortified by all those supports, so that
whole peoples and kings themselves abandoned the ancestral religions in which
they had been born and raised and, without hope of advantage and indeed with
the certain expectation of evils, embraced this religion, which was absurd to
reason and unwelcome to the flesh and which seemed more likely to repel people
from itself than attract them to itself.

XXII. Certainty can be threefold: 1. mathematical, 2. moral, and 3.
theological. Mathematical or metaphysical certainty is that which belongs to
the first principles known by nature and in themselves, and to conclusions
demonstrated through principles of this kind, as when the whole is said to be
greater than its part and when the same thing cannot at once be and not be. 2.
Moral certainty is discerned in matters which indeed cannot be demonstrated
but are nevertheless commended by such indications and such probable
arguments that a prudent person cannot doubt them—for example, that the
*Aeneid* was written by Virgil and the history of Livy by Livy. For although
the matter is not known in itself, it is nevertheless attested by such
constant report that no one who has any knowledge of affairs and letters can
doubt it. 3. Theological certainty is possessed in matters which, although
they cannot be demonstrated or may be known in themselves and by nature, are
nevertheless supported not only by probable indications and moral arguments,
but by truly theological and divine ones, namely divine revelation, which
therefore produces not only moral and conjectural certainty but truly divine
faith. Scripture does not have metaphysical certainty, for otherwise the
assent which we render to it would produce knowledge, not faith. Nor does it
have merely moral and probable certainty, for otherwise our faith would be no
more certain than any historical assent which is given to human writings. But
it has theological and infallible certainty, which cannot deceive a believer
enlightened by the Spirit of God.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIII. The prophets did not err in those things which they wrote “under
divine inspiration” (θεοπνεύστως) and as prophets, not even in matters of
lesser importance, because otherwise the credibility of all Scripture would
be rendered doubtful. But they could err in other things as men, in the way
that David erred in the letter concerning the killing of Uriah—which indeed
possesses the authenticity of history but not of the norm—and Nathan erred
in the advice which he gave David to build the temple, but without having
consulted God, 2 Samuel 7:3. For the inspiration of the Holy Spirit was
neither universal nor continuous, lest it be thought an ordinary motion or an
effect of nature, 2 Kings 2:17.

XXIV. The apostles were infallible in faith, not in morals; and the Spirit was
to lead them into all truth so that they would never err, not into all piety
so that they would never sin, because they were like us in all things. Peter's
dissimulation and hypocrisy, of which Galatians 2:12 speaks, was a sin of
life, not an error of faith—a moral lapse in conduct from weakness and fear
that he would incur the hatred of the Jews, not an error of the mind from
ignorance of Christian liberty, which he sufficiently testified that he knew
by his familiar association with the gentiles before the Jews arrived.

XXV. When Paul says, 1 Corinthians 7:10, “I say, not the Lord,” he does not
deny the inspiration of the Lord, for whom he claims his words in verse 40,
but only that this precept or this law had previously been expressly given by
the Lord himself. Thus the sense is that this controversy concerning
malicious desertion had not yet been raised in the time of Christ,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 66 | IA leaf 120 | PDF p. 120]

nor had he then had an occasion to decide it, which Paul, now enlightened by
the Spirit, does.

XXVI. The things in the Law which seem frivolous and useless are found by the
godly and wise to be of the greatest importance for exercising obedience,
renouncing idolatry, shaping morals, and pointing out the Messiah, if they are
understood skillfully as they ought to be. The genealogies and the other
things which seem superfluous are testimonies to the rise, propagation, and
preservation of the church and to the fulfillment of the promise concerning
the Messiah to be derived from the seed of Abraham and David.

XXVII. The prophet Hosea is not commanded in chapter 1:2 to take a prostitute
as his wife, for in that case the children from such a marriage could not be
called illegitimate, as they are called in verse 2. Rather, these things are
to be understood allegorically, so that Israel's foul idolatry might be
represented under such a symbol.
