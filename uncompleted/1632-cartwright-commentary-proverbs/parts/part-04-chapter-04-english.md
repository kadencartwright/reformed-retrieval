CHAPTER FOUR.

He exhorts to the study of true wisdom through verse 14; from its contrary he
dissuades through the end of the chapter. In verses 1–10 he again arouses
attention, as though taking a new beginning, and then adds its reasons in the
following nine verses.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - the securing of attention;
  - the reasons supplied.

Verse 1. Hear, sons, the instruction of a father, and attend to know prudence.

By demanding attention he repeatedly rebukes our drowsiness and relaxation of
mind in sacred matters, from which it is evident that not even the prophets
were free, not even when the most majestic visions were revealed to them. See
Zechariah’s prophecy in several places, especially 4:1. This warns preachers,
remembering this inertia and stupor in hearers, to awaken them with a short
address. He also reminds the hearers themselves to address and spur
themselves. Thus Deborah addresses herself in Judges 5, and David frequently
addresses his soul. For just as a musical string loosened by striking must be
tightened again, so must our weary and exhausted hearts.

Next, because he asks not only to be heard but also that we apply the ear and
receive the doctrine, he teaches that we must not only not be deaf, but must
receive these things neither with dull and heavy ears, but with ears pricked
up. He who has ears to hear, let him hear.

Verse 2. For I deliver good instruction to you; do not forsake my doctrine.

[Analytical diagram: The principal reasons for attention follow, which are
two: the former from an adjunct, the excellence of the doctrine he delivers,
in this verse; the other from testimonies in verse 3 and following.]

The sense of the former is that what he teaches is not commonplace or widely
known, but, as he says elsewhere, leading and primary maxims and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 41 | printed columns 56–57]

(as he called them before) *meshalim*, proverbs (משלים). Everyone seeks good and eagerly
pursues it; many say, “Who will show us good?” Yet those things are not truly
good except according to how one uses them. Meanwhile they neglect and
drowsily pass by what is truly good. Perhaps in words we do not call evil good
and good evil, sweet bitter and bitter sweet; but in deed and reality we do
this.

Someone may object here that this praise of his own doctrine is distasteful,
or at least renders the matter suspect. For who will believe a man who praises
his own merchandise? But it must be understood that this flows from Solomon
as from God’s mouth, and nothing prevents God from adorning all his works,
especially saving doctrine, with praises. There is no danger of vainglory in
God, to whom all glory is owed. And since only a seller’s desire for profit
renders his trustworthiness suspect—when otherwise he is regarded as the best
and most suitable witness to his merchandise—nothing accrues here to God, but
only to us. Therefore, let us not doubt the doctrine he praises, but proclaim
his incredible zeal to do good in commending his wisdom to us in so many
words.

He concludes from the goodness of the instruction that it must not be
forsaken. Who, unless bereft of reason, will forsake good, especially when by
forsaking good he is not only deprived of good itself, but falls into the
greatest evil? Who will forsake a fountain of living water to drink from wells
or troubled waters? Who will leave the king’s highway to wander through
trackless and inaccessible places?

Verse 3. When I was a son with my father, tender and the only one before my
mother.

The other reason follows from testimonies. It is as though he says: You know
my father’s celebrated authority in both the church and the commonwealth, his
renowned wisdom and distinguished godliness, so that he was called a man after
God’s own heart. He instructed me in these commandments which I now deliver
to you. Thus anyone who holds these commandments in contempt or neglect will
reject God not only in me, but also in him. From this it follows that rejection
of proposed truth is a graver sin in proportion as it is conveyed to us by
more people, by those more cultivated in doctrine and godliness, and by those
more eminent in authority in the church. For a precious gift such as a jewel,
although brought to us in a leather box, must be received gratefully; much
more when it is presented in a silk or gilded purse. Yet it does not follow
that being taught something by one’s parents is sufficient argument for
admitting it, as papists and most uneducated people commonly contend, unless
the parents were of greater renown and approved godliness, 2 Timothy 1:3.

Next he enlarges this argument from the efficient cause of this instruction:
namely, his parents’ intimate love toward him. From this it shines forth that
instructing one’s children in godliness is a most certain effect of true and
lawful love. Parents must be judged to love their children more in proportion
as they are more solicitous to instruct them; conversely, children whom their
parents do not instruct are held in hatred by them. Yet there are many parents
who, because of some foolish sort of love for their children, do not allow
them to be instructed in godliness, lest, forsooth, they cause them any
difficulty. But it belongs to a good man to instruct his whole household, much
more his children. Indeed, as is taught elsewhere, the more a parent loves a
son and thinks to name him heir to a greater inheritance with him, the more
he ought to form him. For David fathered several sons by Bathsheba, yet we do
not read that he was as solicitous in instructing the others as he was with
Solomon.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 42 | printed columns 58–59]

This is next enlarged from the contrary: although he was tender in age, he was
nevertheless imbued with these commandments. See 2 Timothy 3. Therefore, so
far as possible, let infants suck godliness together with their milk and learn
the language of Canaan from their tender years. Here let us consider the happy
success of this doctrine in Solomon, who, instructed in this manner by his
parents, became most skilled and thoroughly equipped on every side.

Verse 4. As he taught me, he said to me, “Let your mind retain my words; keep
my commandments and live.”

He adds the summary of his father’s doctrine through verse 10. First he pursues
it generally and concisely, then particularly and more fully.

[Analytical diagram]

- Duty.
- Reward.

The duty in general is, first, that the words of wisdom be deeply impressed
upon the mind, lest they flow away, Hebrews 2:1, or be disgorged; that they be
long-lasting (μακροχρόνιος) and not like seed that is neither well harrowed nor
hidden. Next, they must be observed. For just as food that is well digested
nourishes and fattens, so doctrine worked out through observance and as it
were assimilated—or rather, since this is the nature of this food, assimilating
and converting us into its own substance—makes us complete for every good
work and conformed to God. The reward is to live, that is, to possess present
and future life. Here ask how life is attributed to observing the commandments.

Verse 5. Acquire wisdom; acquire prudence; do not forget, nor turn aside from
the words of my mouth.

Verse 6. Do not forsake her, and she will preserve you; love her, and she will
guard you.

Now he treats the preceding doctrine more particularly: first simply and in
itself, and afterward by joining with the duty a corresponding and appropriate
reward.

In the duty he requires three things: first, knowledge of godliness and
uprightness; second, memory; third, observance. In verse 6 he requires
perseverance in those three and, since that exhortation is exceedingly
difficult, fortifies it with a promise.

Then he assigns the cause of this duty: love of godliness. He also proposes
the effects of that love, namely, that a person prefer it to all things and be
prepared to redeem it by the loss of all things, as though the person who does
not love it above all things does not love it at all. Here he also adorns the
effect of that love with a simile.

In the former he alludes to the acquisition of a field or inheritance, or to
the purchase of merchandise and commerce of merchants. Amos 8:12. See the
example of the Queen of Sheba, 1 Kings 9. See Psalm 119:2. Here one must guard
against perverse understanding, Matthew 15:6–7, and also against stupor; hence
one must abstain from gluttony and drunkenness, by which the heart is weighed
down.

The second is that it be laid up in faithful memory, Psalm 119:16. It is
compared to a pearl, Matthew 13, and a treasure, and must therefore be
faithfully stored.

The third is that one turn to neither side, neither right nor left, but walk
with a straight foot, accurately (ἀκριβῶς), Ephesians 5:15, not departing from
the straight path even the width of a fingernail or a finger laid crosswise.

In verse 6 there is a fourth command concerning continuance in all those
three, without which they will not profit, but will even do great harm.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 43 | printed columns 60–61]

Second Peter 3. Therefore, a promise is attached to it. It does not say that
the man will be preserved who acquires wisdom, remembers it, and expresses it
in deeds for a time, but the one who perseveres. By this promise he sustains
the patience of the godly, for the one who persists must endure many things.
The most fitting consolation against those temptations is that wisdom will
preserve them amid those things. The evils of the godly are many, but God
delivers them from all, Psalm 34. And however much the righteous person falls
seven times in one day, he nevertheless rises again.

He notes the efficient cause of all these things: love of wisdom. If a person
loves wisdom, he will pursue it, remember it, express it in life, and preserve
it to the end. Who does not pursue a beloved thing? What covetous man ever
forgot where he hid his treasure? Indeed, love of wisdom will cause us to
observe her commandments, Psalm 119:167, and not forsake her, 2 Thessalonians
2. For vehement love cannot be quenched by an immense mass of waters, Song of
Songs 8:6–7. See 1 Corinthians 13.

Verse 7. As wisdom is the principal thing, acquire wisdom; and with your entire
acquisition acquire prudence.

Having earlier admonished that wisdom must be acquired, he now explains more
particularly, teaching both the order in which each thing must be pursued and
the ardor and affection of mind with which to do so. First, as to order, he
teaches that principal wisdom and the head and fountain of all wisdom are
acquired by the fear of God. He prefers this to the wisdom concerned with
kindness toward one’s neighbor, 1 John 5:2. To prove his fairness to his
brothers, Joseph put forward his fear of God. And Abraham notes that the reason
he feared for himself and his wife among foreigners was that they did not fear
God. The kingdom of heaven must be sought first, and so forth, Matthew 6, not
only because it is the first commandment, but because all kindness that is not
referred to the fear of God is hateful to God.

Next he shows at what price it must be acquired: namely, the greatest, as in
Matthew 13.

Verse 8. Exalt her, and she will exalt you; she will honor you when you embrace
her.

We exalt her, first, when we receive her as the wisdom of God; second, when we
follow her ambassadors with honor, receiving them like angels of God, indeed
like Christ himself; third, we also honor her when we honor all who profess
her, even the most abject and poor. Hence James censures some because they
held the poor in contempt, although they were godly. Finally, we follow her
with honor when we prefer her to every possession and even to our own life.

Verse 9. She will place upon your head an addition of grace; she will deliver
to you a most ornate crown.

He adds the particular honor with which wisdom will attend those zealous for
her: a kingdom will be given them, for they are heirs of God and coheirs with
Christ. This kingdom, begun here by the Spirit, will afterward be completed
when sin is abolished.

Verse 10. Hear, my son, and receive my words; thus the years of life will be
multiplied for you.

Thus far were David’s words repeated by Solomon; what follows belongs to
Solomon. Here he takes a new beginning and arouses attention, which repeatedly
flags unless sustained by frequent admonition. First he exhorts

[1632 witness | PDF p. 44 | printed columns 62–63]

us to hear all his words; next to receive them. He adds a promise of long life
both in this age and the future. In this life it will render you vigorous,
Psalm 103:5. See the excellent examples of Moses and Caleb. Then, when the
course of this life is finished, it will crown you with the eternity of days.

Verse 11. I teach you the way of wisdom; I lead you through the paths of
uprightness.

Verse 12. When you walk, nothing will constrain your step; even if you run,
you will not stumble.

He teaches that he transmits the way of wisdom which all desire, and that
wisdom joined with uprightness and free from fraud, whereas most rejoice in a
wisdom by which they deceive others. He also teaches that he has not merely
shown the way, but has performed the duty of a guide to lead us into it—and
indeed a broad way where there is no danger of stumbling, not even when we do
not merely walk but run in it. In this way he meets those who turn away from
zeal. It is a broad and easy way, first, because errors are forgiven through
faith; next, because the Holy Spirit makes it pleasant for us, John 1:5.

Verse 13. Grasp that instruction; do not desist; guard it, because it is your
life.

Having completed the exhortation to pursue good, he begins another concerning
flight from evil through verse 20.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - preparation of the mind to receive the doctrine;
  - the doctrine itself, which he delivers.
- In the preparation there are two things:
  - a command;
  - a promise.

The command concerns two things: first, that we grasp firmly the instruction
that follows; then that, once grasped, we hold it eagerly, firmly, and
tenaciously, allowing it neither to fall away through negligence nor to be
shaken or wrested from us by force. See Matthew 11:12; Philippians 3:12, 14.
Do not let it go, for it will have been little to grasp it firmly unless one
retains it. Some burn hot at the first entrance but afterward cool, like those
who receive the seed with pleasure and cheerfulness, Matthew 13. They are like
stomachs that eagerly seize food but lack the power to retain it. They are
like children who rejoice upon seeing a new thing but despise and cast it away
after using it for one hour. Therefore, let us devote great effort lest we
flow away; it would have been better never to have known the way of truth or
received wisdom than to cast it away or lose it. Galatians 5:7; Revelation
3:11.

The promise follows: observing the commandments is life itself, and life
consists in obeying them. As much as you fall away from these, so much does
your life depart from you. For the wicked are regarded as dead even while
living, 1 Timothy 5; Matthew 8. That which has the beginning and horror of
eternal death attached to it should not be called life. By contrast, those
who act rightly by believing in Christ live even if they die, John 11.

Verse 14. Do not enter the path of the wicked, nor walk in the way of evil
people.

He exhorts two things: first, not to enter the way of the wicked; second, if
we have entered it after being overtaken and intercepted by sin’s deceit, to
beware of continuing in it. Although we may enter the path of the wicked, we
must not tread their road, Psalm 1:1.
[1632 witness | PDF p. 45 | printed columns 64–65]

Verse 15. Withdraw yourself from it; do not pass through it; turn away from it
and pass by.

He teaches at length what he proposed before: we ought to have no dealings
with the practices of the wicked, but must turn away by every means and avoid
even the slightest occasions. We must not only reject and openly abominate
the road itself, but must not even pass along it. Here he introduces a sort of
contradiction by warning the man to pass by whom he previously forbade to pass
through. It must be understood that he ought immediately to turn aside from
that road, lest, if he delays, he fall into a snare. A similar exhortation
occurred in chapter 1, verse 15. Thus, a man who comes upon a stinking corpse
will not merely avoid it, but will hurry as he passes beside it, lest he draw
into his nostrils the stench it exhales. So one who meets an ungodly man
should avoid him so far as possible, while preserving the necessary duties of
outward society. For no one but a demoniac will linger among the tombs, Mark 5.

Verse 16. For they do not sleep unless they have done evil, and their sleep is
taken away unless they have caused someone to stumble.

The first reason is from the attached restlessness by which they are tormented
unless they have completed the crime they desire. They are driven to evil
deeds with such force that, unless they obtain their desire, they cannot
sleep. See the examples of Ahab and Amnon. The former was seized by sickness
because he could not obtain Naboth’s vineyard; the latter wasted away daily
because he could not possess his shameful love. Here, therefore, a distinction
is established between the sins of the godly and those of the wicked. The
former err and do so as though unwilling; the wicked are driven toward sin,
which rules them, with great force like an armored horse.

In this passage we are also admonished how zealously we must strive toward
good, so that we do not go to sleep or give rest to our eyelids unless some
excellent thing has been accomplished. The church is sick from love for
Christ, Song of Songs 2.

Verse 17. Who feed upon the food of wickedness and drink the wine of every
kind of violence.

He persists in the former argument and teaches it from its efficient cause,
which he illustrates by a simile. Those who go to bed hungry cannot sleep; in
the same way, these people, whose wickedness is like their food and drink,
cannot sleep unless a crime has first been completed. Conversely, the godly
regard obedience rendered to God as their food and drink, John 4. Thus David
possessed nothing sweeter than being occupied with God’s commandments. See
Job 23:12.

Verse 18. For the path of the righteous is like a splendid light, proceeding
to shine until midday is established.

Verse 19. But the way of the wicked is like darkness; they do not know against
what they will stumble.

The other argument is from the opposite way of the righteous, which is full
of consolation. The righteous person is like the day, which shines in the
morning and advances by certain degrees until it reaches midday and a
perfection complete in every respect. He must therefore travel his journey
with the greatest confidence, especially since, as was said earlier, he walks
upon a broad road. But conversely,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 46 | printed columns 66–67]

when the wicked enter their path in the evening, the darkness increases by
degrees until they arrive at the thickest darkness, like midnight lit not at
all by moon or stars—that is, at hell itself. The wicked are at first illumined
by some light, namely reason and humanity, but afterward lose even this little
light. Next they are illumined by some light of prosperity, which light
represents, but this afterward ends in supreme unhappiness. Hence it happens
that they not only stumble through ignorance, but, contrary to what occurs
with travelers, even after falling do not understand that they have fallen.
Unless perhaps they are to be compared with drunkards, who are so stupefied by
drink that even when they have fallen, they do not understand it.

Verse 20. My son, attend to my words; incline your ear to my sayings.

At the end of the chapter there is a new exhortation to the full perception
of wisdom and, more particularly, to its effective practice inwardly and
outwardly.

[Analytical diagram]

- In the exhortation are:
  - commands;
  - promises, Matthew 7.
- The outward commands require ears and eyes; the inward require the mind,
  understanding, and affection.

He requires this with the greatest attention: “Give ear” (ἐνωτίσασθε), says
Peter in Acts 2:14. Let the ear be like an awl piercing it at the door of the
sanctuary. He also requires the eyes to be occupied in inspecting these things
and warns that they should be like frontlets, necklaces, and rings upon the
fingers, which are always before the eyes; that they should be written not
only in books, but engraved or painted upon doorposts, both publicly upon
gates and privately upon doors. Nevertheless, it would not therefore be as
useful to paint them in churches, because by this means minds are frequently
drawn away from attentive hearing of the word.

Verse 21. Do not let them cause these things to depart from your eyes; keep
them in your mind.

He further confirms the same matter by removing an efficient cause, when he
warns that the wicked, of whom he recently spoke, must not be allowed to drive
us from the study of wisdom or remove this doctrine from our eyes and ears.
Thus in Psalm 1 those who would meditate upon the law are commanded to avoid
the company of the wicked, since the latter will endeavor by both words and
the pursuits of life to draw the godly from studying and embracing wisdom.

There follows a command concerning the inward storing of wisdom in the mind
as in a little chest, and not upon the surface of the mind but in its deepest
recesses. By this he denotes not only a full and accurate understanding of it,
but an inward affection toward it. Deuteronomy 6:6.

Verse 22. For the words are life to those who attain them and healthful to the
whole flesh of each of them.

[Analytical diagram: The promise has two members: first, life, and that
eternal; the other, a person’s health.]

It is therefore like a tree of life, by tasting whose fruit we will be
delivered from death; it is an antidote and amulet. Moreover, like a skilled
and faithful physician, it supplies health. It was said earlier that the godly
are kings,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 47 | printed columns 68–69]

and that God deals with them most generously, as with kings. For they are
attended by wisdom as by a bodyguard; they have the same as guide of their way
and for steering (οἰακισμῷ); it serves them as a more sacred senate and, in this
passage, as a college of physicians. And whereas individual physicians
usually excel in curing one particular member, this medicine serves the whole
body and even the soul. It does so when it restrains from gluttony,
drunkenness, unrestrained lusts, excessive sorrow, joy, anger, and so forth,
from which innumerable diseases spring.

Verse 23. Above every other guard, keep your heart, for from it proceed the
actions of life.

There follows a particular exhortation to practice. First observe that, in
reversed order, the beginning is taken from practice. In the full perception
of wisdom, beginning with the ears and eyes, one finally comes to the mind;
but in practice, beginning with the heart, progress is made to the outward
members.

He warns that, whereas great care must be applied in guarding every member,
the greatest and utmost care must be applied in guarding the heart. If
elsewhere we station guards by night, here let us station them both night and
day; if elsewhere we post a guard, here let us increase the squads of four.
For just as in guarding the present life we especially fortify the heart with
an antidote because the fountain of life is sought from it, so in securing
future life by far the greatest care must be taken over the soul and affection,
lest it be polluted or double. See Matthew 15. Yet most people sin here. Many
take care that nothing be outwardly committed in their bodily members which
may turn to their discredit, while they never purify their hearts. And although
these may outwardly lead a blameless life such that they can be summoned to
judgment in this way, nevertheless they have accomplished nothing by all that
apparent innocence, because they do nothing from a conscience toward God—at
least a pure conscience cleansed by faith—but only from fear of divine
judgment or desire to obtain reputation and empty acclaim among people.
Indeed, this does not always keep them safe from disgrace; something shameful
will burst out from them unawares unless the soul or heart is first purified.
For what will it profit if all the other tasks on a ship are diligently done
but the man who holds the helm errs?

Verse 24. Remove perversity of mouth from yourself, and put depravity of lips
far away from you.

Guarding the mouth is added to guarding the heart, words to thoughts, because
it comes next to it in difficulty. We cannot avoid thinking evil, and hence
the evangelical command is not so much that we should not desire as that we
should not obey desires. Similarly James teaches that one who does not sin
with his tongue is perfect, that is, that no one exists who does not do so.
But to make us abhor this vice more, he compares perverse and twisted words to
the foul appearance of a distorted mouth; likewise, a stammering tongue and
difficult speech (μογιλαλία) may be compared to these. Let us therefore
remember to pray with
David that God guard the door of our lips. Indeed, it would have been better
to be mute than to abuse the tongue for insolence and blasphemies.

Verse 25. Let your eyes look directly ahead, and let your eyelids look straight
before you.

After the warning he explains the use of the eyes: they must not wander and
stray here and there, but look straight toward what is godly and righteous.
The metaphor seems drawn from those who guide a plow and look straight at it,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 48 | printed columns 70–71 | ends above the ruled beginning of Chapter V]

so far are they from looking backward. See Psalm 119, section 5: “Turn my eyes
away, that they may not look upon vanity”; likewise Job 31: “I have made a
covenant with my eyes.” Although by synecdoche he understands all the senses
by one that is especially dangerous.

What he adds concerning the eyelids—that they direct the gaze toward what is
in front—inculcates the same thing in different words. At the same time he
indicates that the eyelids were given by God as a covering and bulwark for the
eyes, solely for this purpose and on this condition: that they preserve the
eyes’ sight and gaze for the delightful sight of upright and righteous things.

Verse 26. Weigh the path of your foot, so that all your ways may be stable.

To the eye he adds the foot, by which metaphor he denotes actions and commands
that they be tested by the balance. Actions, when committed to execution,
either profit or harm more than when they are merely conceived, determined in
the mind, or expressed in speech. He therefore wishes all things to be
examined with just weights. Thus the apostle rebukes the Galatians because
they “do not walk straight” (οὐκ ὀρθοποδοῦσι), Galatians 2:14, and says in
Ephesians 5, “walk accurately” (ἀκριβῶς περιπατεῖτε). Those who accuse every
stricter and more religious observance of the commandments of curiosity or
foolishness oppose this admonition. Our balance or measuring line, however,
is God’s law or doctrine, Psalm 119, sections 8 and 13. These are just and
certified weights; the others, like Lesbian rules, are deceitful.

He renders this exhortation pointed by adding a promise of stability for works
weighed by the balance of divine law, 1 Chronicles 22:13; 2 Chronicles 24:20;
Proverbs 12:2. This is argued from the contrary in Psalm 119:6. Indeed, even a
royal throne is established by righteousness, Proverbs 16:12, much more the
affairs of private people. See Matthew 7 and 1 Corinthians 3:12.

Verse 27. Do not turn aside to the right or left; remove your foot from evil.

He enlarges this accurate practice by another metaphor drawn from those who
walk upon the king’s highway, as appears in Deuteronomy 2:27. It is greatly in
their interest to remain upon it as though danger lay on both sides if they
departed even slightly—as though the king’s highway were enclosed and hemmed
in by the sea, so that anyone who turned even slightly toward either side
would immediately be in danger of drowning. See Deuteronomy 5:32 and 17. It is
attributed to pride if a person turns toward either side: as when someone
either loosens the reins of gluttony or draws them too tightly; when he
approaches God either far too boldly and rashly or far too fearfully through
new mediators and petty saints; when he flees the cross or voluntarily seeks
it; when he either does not turn away from papal ceremonies, or so flees them
that on their account he sacrifices even all the most excellent things.

What he said before metaphorically he afterward explains in plain and simple
speech, when he commands each person to depart from evil. By this very fact he
establishes that even the slightest deviation toward either side is among
evils, however people think otherwise. If turning toward either side is
dangerous, how much more turning back.
