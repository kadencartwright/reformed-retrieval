# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 22.* 1 Timothy 1:9: *Knowing this, that the law is not laid down
for a righteous person*, and so forth.

*Answer.* That this saying of Paul does not favor the Antinomians can be
proved as follows.

1. Because he says in verse 5 that *the end of the commandment is love*, and
so forth, by *commandment* he means the

[Printed p. 81 | IA PDF 89 | IA leaf 89 | IA decausadei02brow]

law itself, of which certain people wanted to appear to be teachers, although they had
strayed from this purpose, as is clear from verses 6 and 7. He therefore
assumes that this law, or this commandment, whose end is love from a pure
heart, remains in force. For he censures those false teachers on the ground
that they had turned aside from love, and calls everyone else away from the
most empty pursuits to this true pursuit of godliness.

2. In verse 8 he openly affirms, as a most certain truth known to all the
godly, that *the law is good*: *But we know*, he says, *that the law is good.*
In what respect, however, could the law be called good if it is not at least a
rule of conduct for the godly? Moreover, he speaks about the time of the
gospel, when the ceremonial law had been abolished. He therefore speaks about
the moral law and says, without anyone dissenting, that this law is good even
now. He thus assumes that this law remains in force even under the gospel.
For we never hear him speak in this way about the ceremonial law after the
gospel was proclaimed. In what, then, does its goodness under the gospel now
consist, if not in its being a constant rule of life and conduct?

3. He further adds, *if anyone uses it lawfully*. Hence it is evident that
people can use the law lawfully even now. It has therefore not been entirely
abolished and abrogated, as the ceremonial law has, which no one can now use
lawfully.

4. In verses 11 and 12 he shows how perfectly the gospel agrees with the law.
There he says that the gospel condemns the same things the law condemns and,
consequently, does not abrogate that law but rather confirms and establishes
it as a firm and immutable rule of life and conduct. The gospel does not
command and prohibit the same things the ceremonial law commanded or
prohibited, since that law has now been abolished. But since it commands and
prohibits the same things the moral law commanded and prohibited, it is
evident that this moral law has not been abrogated under the gospel.

2. Hence it is clear that when the apostle says *the law is not laid down for
a righteous person*, the meaning is not that the law does not bind righteous
people to obedience, but that it does not condemn the righteous—that is,
those justified by faith. Instead, it is laid down for the wicked and
insubordinate, and for all who commit what is opposed to sound doctrine and
the glorious gospel. [*“By the verb κεῖσθαι,”* Piscator says in his scholium,
*“he denotes that burden of the curse which rests upon transgressors of the
law.”*] It is also clear that the law was not given to terrify with its threats
those who attempt to obey it, not unwillingly, but from a spontaneous impulse.
The law was not enacted [*“against the person,”* Beza says on the passage,
*“who voluntarily conforms his conduct to its rule. Such are only those whom
the Lord imbues with true doctrine and the Holy Spirit, the very things which
those who nevertheless wish to appear teachers of the law care about least
of all. This is indeed a general statement, but it must be applied to the
people under discussion. For otherwise the law was laid down to govern the
life of the saints, and indeed is effectual only in those whom it does not
itself justify or sanctify, but finds justified, against whom it cannot
pronounce judgment. Likewise, when it finds them sanctified by Christ's
Spirit, it does not treat them as enemies, but teaches and delights them as
those who agree with it. Therefore the detestable heresy of the Antinomians
and Libertines must be condemned.”*]

3. Apollonius, at the place cited above, p. 194, holds that the meaning is
that the law was given to restrain people not yet regenerate so that they
would not break out into manifest crimes. He further explains it thus:
[*“The devil,”* he says, *“reigns throughout the whole world and drives people
to every kind of crime. Therefore, just as fetters and chains are placed upon
possessed people so that they may harm no one, so God restrains the hands of
the whole world—which is possessed by the devil and rushes headlong into every
crime—with the bonds and chains of laws, so that it may not rush with impunity
into every kind of*

[Printed p. 82 | IA PDF 90 | IA leaf 90 | IA decausadei02brow]

*crime. And this restraint is supremely necessary, not only for preserving
public peace and all civil and natural affairs, but especially so that the
church of God in this world may not be suppressed or removed by the tumults
and rebellions of savage people, or the course of the gospel impeded.”*]
Nevertheless, the law was also imposed upon the regenerate for this purpose:
namely, that they might be restrained from evil.
