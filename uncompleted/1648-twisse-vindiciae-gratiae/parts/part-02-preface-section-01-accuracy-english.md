PREFACE

SECTION ONE

ON THE MANNER AND ORDER OF PREDESTINATION,

and on the extent of divine grace: a Christian and lucid discussion.

PERKINS

May God Most Good and Most Great will that it be happy and auspicious.

Just as these things were

1. written by a candid mind sincerely devoted to the peace of the English Church, without any desire to contradict anyone, either here or elsewhere;
2. approved, in accordance with the custom of our university, by the most reverend theologians;
3. published to quiet the disputes now gathering strength and to provide a fuller explanation of the truth;

so

1. let them be dedicated to those who profess the study of theology;
2. let them be read, weighed, and judged by those men from the pure Word of God, without prejudice and without bitterness of spirit;
3. and may God grant that we all exercise charity and candor toward one another.

ARMINIUS

In recent days, reverend sir and beloved brother in Christ, while my eager eyes were ranging over a certain library abundantly stocked with newly published books, a little book presented itself to me with this title: *A Christian and lucid discussion on the manner and order of predestination and on the extent of divine grace.* When I saw that it bore your name—a name I already knew more favorably from other published books—I thought that I should acquire it and read it diligently:

1. so that I might discover whether you, who are a student of more accurate doctrine, could by this labor remove the scruples that had long troubled my mind.

I therefore read and reread it, with an eye as unwarped as I could manage and with the candid mind that you desire.

2. Yet as I read I perceived that not every scruple could be removed from me by this writing; indeed, I seemed to perceive that you had written certain things which deserve to be examined by the standard of truth.

3. I therefore thought it would not be inappropriate to institute a peaceful conference with you about this little book of yours. I do so all the more willingly and confidently because on the second page of your book you give me courage to venture it, when you testify that you wrote these things for this purpose: that they might be read, weighed, and judged from the pure Word of God by students of theology—among whom I willingly profess my own name, unless this is done against theology's will—without prejudice and without bitterness of spirit. For my part, I promise and undertake that I will do this to the best of my ability, stipulating in return that you likewise read and weigh these remarks of mine in the same spirit, examine them by the standard of Scripture alone, and pass judgment on them.

4. May God grant that we all think as one in those matters necessary for his glory and the salvation of the Church; and if in the rest we cannot come together in our opinions, may we at least come together as one in our affections and preserve the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace.

Having therefore fittingly placed this prayer before the conference we have undertaken, I shall approach the matter itself, following the tracks you went before me and laid down by your writing. I shall begin with the epistle to the reader and then, with God's favor, proceed to the book itself.

RESPONSE

When I first proposed to investigate these things and submit them to scholastic examination, I supposed that a laborious reply to them was hardly needed. I thought that, without harm to our cause, Arminius could be allowed to enjoy his own prudence while with any sort of preface he merely prepared for himself a more convenient and well-mannered entrance into the conference that follows. Upon further reflection, however, the author's devices have emerged—or are at least suspect. He covertly prepares, in the estimation of an insufficiently cautious reader, an easy and unobstructed road for his own cause but an obstacle and no slight occasion of stumbling for ours. We have therefore judged it advisable to add a few notes even on these matters wherever it seems appropriate.

Arminius repeatedly displays how highly he esteems that “more accurate doctrine.” “Here,” he says on p. 7, “I see a labyrinth which I shall not now enter, because I could not get out unless guided by the thread of an accurate exposition.” And on p. 9: “For why should a serious and solid conference about the truth not be seasoned, as it were, by a friendly and pleasant contest over a more accurate treatment?” He repeatedly censures a lack of this *akribeia*, or exactness, in this writing of Perkins—sometimes rather mildly. Thus on p. 19: “If you intended to define the creation of man, it should have been done more accurately”; likewise on p. 113: “Those words could also be corrected if someone wished to examine them more accurately.” At times, however, he is harsher. For example, on p. 119: “But please look at what you append, and you will see and confess that you threw those things onto paper while attending to something else.” Related to these too are his words on p. 205: “I see, Perkins, that you wrote those things at a running pen, without examining the syllogism as you have proposed it.” For although in the very entrance hall

of this examination he acknowledges that Perkins is a student of more accurate doctrine, nevertheless—even if he grants Perkins that praise in perhaps other writings—he plainly detracts from it at least in this little book with these words on p. 49: “Most learned Perkins, you seem to me to have forgotten your own self and to be an altogether different man from the one you revealed in your other little books.”

For my part, I readily profess that not only can the same subject sometimes be treated learnedly and sometimes unlearnedly; an error can in turn be supported scholastically and with varied learning, while even a truth can be asserted carelessly enough and childishly. Yet it also makes a very great difference in what manner each thing is presented. For a man who defends truth negligently and unlearnedly acts more as a betrayer than as the legitimate champion of the cause. Under his patronage the cause is not so much established as prostituted and exposed to ridicule. Nevertheless, when one looks at the apostles and the earliest Fathers of the Church and considers their manner of handing down the doctrines of faith, it becomes apparent that the former, in proposing articles, did not attend so anxiously to that accurate manner of treatment, while the latter, as controversies arose over those same articles, devoted their labor to explaining, defending, and vindicating them more simply than accurately. But far be it from us to spread the veil of their simplicity over our own indolence or negligence. Come then, let us be carried where the ray of divine light leads us: not only into all truth, but also into the growth of Christian theology—not merely the growth belonging to a learner but that belonging to a teacher—and into progress toward the most accurate explanation of it that can be attained. Only let us, content with that simplicity of faith which befits Christians, rest in divine revelations and neither make an empty display of human wisdom nor cultivate it more eagerly than is fitting.

Next, a method of treating theological subjects ought not immediately to be condemned as less accurate merely because an adversary brands it with that name. It is implanted in us all by nature to have sharper eyes for other men's blemishes than for observing and pointing out our own warts. Very often the man who sees the speck in his brother's eye does not perceive the beam in his own. The more acute we are in detecting what may seem liable to accusation in those who displease us, the duller we are in discerning—or the readier to pass over—what might deservedly be turned into a fault in those who please us. Did Adam, then, not sin among the Arminian brethren? Are they to be held more upright and incorrupt in this respect than all others? Would that they were not instead excessively haughty and disdainful in despising other men's learning; would that they were not excessively generous and prodigal in enlarging and extolling their own. For this is how Bertius, like some latter-day Heraclitus, complains about the present times: “I see that this has become the true and proper custom of teaching and disputing. To have said anything today is to have refuted it. The weight of arguments is not assessed; only the titles of books are read.” Unless I am mistaken, he means to sting only outsiders with this judgment—those, namely, who stand outside the Arminian school. Concerning even those whom he calls “our disputants,” Thomson wishes “that they were either learned or knew themselves to be unlearned.” “But now,” he says, “the situation is reversed: the less equipped each man is with every learned discipline and wide reading, the more haughtily and confidently he arrogates to himself judgment on all the gravest questions. Foolish, novel, and very young orators spring forth.”

Most of all, a prudent reader will easily perceive with what contrived and studied modesty Arminius himself conducts himself, yet also with how much haughtiness—which cannot be concealed. But notwithstanding these things, let us devote our chief attention to what they put forward and to the reasoning by which they either establish their own positions or attack ours. If they insolently taunt us without cause, let them sing to themselves and their Muses: they will do no harm at all either to our cause or to us.
