# Whether Participation in the Worship of the Roman Synagogue Is Permissible

21 June 1558

This is a fresh English translation of John Calvin's complete Latin advice at
CO X/1 189–192. The CO editorial heading and bibliographical footnote are not
part of the letter body. Biblical wording is translated from Calvin's Latin;
no wording from Beaty and Farley's modern translation or from a modern English
Bible supplied the translation.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 189]

For nearly ten years I have been subjected to trouble from various quarters
over the questions you raise. Even with three hundred replies I have not
managed to obtain permission to rest thereafter. Overcome by justifiable
weariness, I therefore judged nothing better than to give place to my earlier
writings, lest the implacable curiosity of many people wear me out to no
purpose. Consequently, if I reply to you briefly and concisely, attribute this
not only to the constraints of time,

### [CO X/1, p. 190]

by which I am nevertheless bound, but also to a decision formed long ago. If
some people's ears itch so badly that they desire to delight themselves with
new replies every day, I will readily allow them to seek elsewhere. Amid so
many tasks and almost continual illnesses, I do not have enough leisure to
accommodate everyone by writing publicly and privately.

Some ask why, if the apostles were permitted to go up to the temple at the
hour of prayer, and Paul also to perform a solemn rite of purification, it is
not also lawful for us today to mingle prayers with the papists. First, we
must hold that the prayers then in use among the Jews were pure and formed
according to the law's prescription. Indeed, if the papists composed a pure
form of prayer, I would have no religious scruple about entering a church
with them.

“Yet it was very unbecoming for the apostles to join the assembly of those
who had crucified Christ.” I answer that an action which is godly and holy in
itself is not corrupted by people's fault. As I am accustomed to say, even if
all the angels attended the mass, they could not wash away its filth by their
holiness; conversely, all the devils could not by their presence cause the
holy Supper, when it is celebrated according to Christ's institution, to lose
its purity.

It is nevertheless certain that the apostles did not come to the temple at
every hour of prayer, since they held sacred assemblies in private houses.
Rather, when a hope of edifying presented itself, they made use of that
opportunity. They therefore did not deliberately frequent the temple in order
to join their nation in public daily prayers. They did so because it was
likely that reverence for the place would make people more teachable and
tractable in receiving the doctrine of the Gospel—people who would have paid
less attention in profane places.

This is still clearer in Paul. He certainly did not undertake the vow under
the compulsion of any religious obligation, but only so that, once the
suspicion with which he had been falsely burdened was cleared away, he might
open a door for himself to spread the Gospel. He would not have performed
this ceremony of his own accord. Nor was he asked by James (Acts 21:23) to
worship God in this way, but to make it known to the weak brothers that he was
not an apostate from the law of Moses. Meanwhile, they were free both to enter
the temple and to attest their piety by a sacred offering, since their only
purpose was to profess that they worshiped the God of Israel.

Now a second question arises: if the shadows of the law had been abolished by
Christ's coming, why did they still observe them? It is easily answered if we
hold that, as long as the temple stood, it was a sacred dwelling of heavenly
doctrine and God was rightly worshiped there. Christ brought the ceremonies
of the law to an end in such a way that, by abolishing them, he did not
immediately condemn them outright as impious and polluted, until he became
clearly known through the Gospel. If you object that the light of the Gospel
was already sufficiently conspicuous, the facts plainly teach that Christ was
still unknown to many. Others were illuminated by his rays in such a way that
they nevertheless remained far from the full brightness. From this the wide
difference between the rites of the law and

### [CO X/1, p. 191]

papal ceremonies becomes apparent. The former were pure and holy from their
origin; it is established that Satan's craft fabricated the latter to
extinguish Christ's light. It is therefore easy to conclude that papal
ceremonies were corrupt from their beginning.

Accordingly, the abrogation of the law must be understood properly. Faithful
servants of Christ did not have to abandon the temple and rites which were in
themselves neutral and indifferent before they had discharged their own part
in promulgating the Gospel. Meanwhile, it is certain that as
often as the apostles came to the temple, they took account of the weak
common people. Likewise, wherever they went, it was their custom to enter the
synagogues—not for the purpose of praying, although prayers were also
included, but because a larger crowd of people was there and their minds were
better prepared to learn, so that the apostles might benefit them by teaching.

It is objected: “Unless they prayed in the name of the Messiah, their prayers
were perverse.” I answer that, as they had been instructed by the fathers,
they prayed in the name of the Messiah even though he was unknown. The result
itself certainly made it plain that a hidden seed of piety was in them, since
very many passed over to Christ so easily and with eager readiness of mind.
It is probable that the faith Paul praises in Timothy's mother Eunice and his
grandmother Lois (2 Timothy 1:5) was present before they were imbued with the
Gospel, so that conversion to Christ was the fruit of that good root.

I do not concede that the shadows of the law were simply contrary to the
truth, except insofar as they were wrongly mingled with or set against Christ
by superstitious and obstinate people. Jews who sacrificed according to
ancestral custom before receiving the light of the Gospel were partners with
all the godly in the lawful worship of God. This principle must remain fixed:
the figures of the law were given to the ancient people until Christ dawned,
which happened gradually.

Add that most people, after tasting the first elements of the Gospel, sought
righteousness nowhere but from Christ and placed their hope of salvation in
his sacrifice; yet it is clear that they did not immediately advance to the
point of

### [CO X/1, p. 192]

freeing themselves from every doubt. That weakness was indeed faulty and
deserving of censure, but Paul teaches
that it was tolerable for a time (Romans 14:1 and following), because they had
no intention of detracting anything from Christ. It was simply hard and
painful to depart immediately from a custom received over a long time.

Now when the ceremonies of the law are discussed, a careful distinction must
be applied between sacrifices from which expiation was to be sought and other
observances whose use was freer. In the same way, certain frivolous and
trivial ceremonies today are far removed from others in which open and gross
impiety appears. In peace offerings and similar sacrifices, thanksgiving once
held the first place.

Let us grant that while Christ was still unknown the use of a figure was
lawful, or that while he was not known clearly enough it was excusable. It
would not therefore have been lawful for believers converted to Christ to
expiate themselves by an offered sacrifice, or to discharge a vow of
thanksgiving—not simply to attest their piety, but chiefly out of regard for
charity and for the purpose of clearing away a most destructive slander—as
happened to Paul at Jerusalem on James's advice.

Meanwhile, this must always be maintained: rites which were pure from their
beginning and were later obscured and wholly abolished when the sun of truth
arrived—as Christianity thus succeeded Judaism—or rites which degenerated
through use into corruption, as that same Judaism was already being employed
superstitiously and literally by the Jews, do not stand on the same level as
sheer superstitions fabricated at the devil's instigation to corrupt God's
worship. If someone compares the law's washings with the papists' lustral
water, he will do a grave injury to God, who usefully trained the ancient
people by that sign, whereas by this foul washing the father of lies has, by
repeating baptism, in a manner erased it.

Farewell, best and dearest brother. Other writings compel me to break off this
letter. May the Lord protect all of you beneath his shadow, govern you by his
Spirit, and sustain you by his power. 21 June 1558.

## Translator's notes

1. The heading's “Roman synagogue” is Calvin's polemical designation for the
   Roman church, not a reference to Jewish worship. The body uses the Jewish
   temple and synagogues as historical comparisons while distinguishing them
   from papal worship.
2. The letter itself supplies 21 June 1558 but no place, recipient's name, or
   closing signature. Its singular “dearest brother” and plural final blessing
   suggest an individual correspondent associated with a wider community;
   the text does not identify either.
3. “Neutral and indifferent” translates *medios et indifferentes*. The terms
   describe rites neither impious nor obligatory in themselves during the
   transition Calvin reconstructs; they do not make every use prudent.
4. “Vow of thanksgiving” renders *votum eucharisticum*. Here *eucharisticum*
   means thanksgiving and should not automatically be identified with a later
   Eucharistic liturgy.
5. The scope of the negative in *non ideo ... se expiare, aut voto
   eucharistico ... defungi* at p. 192 is syntactically difficult. A strictly
   coordinated reading forbids both sacrificial expiation and Paul's vow,
   although p. 190 calls entry and a sacred offering permissible when their
   purpose was to attest worship of Israel's God. The translation preserves
   the surface coordination rather than silently changing *aut* to an
   adversative; the parenthesis supplies Calvin's charitable purpose for
   Paul's action, and the tension should remain visible.
6. The final comparison distinguishes rites pure in origin but later
   abolished, rites corrupted through superstitious use, and superstitions
   invented as corruptions from the outset. Calvin's point is not that these
   categories have identical moral status.
