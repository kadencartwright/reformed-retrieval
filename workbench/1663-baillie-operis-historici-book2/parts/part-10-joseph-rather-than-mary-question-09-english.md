## QUESTION IX

### Why is Christ’s genealogy referred to Joseph rather than to Mary?

In Christ’s genealogy we shall raise no more difficulties than this one, which in former times our opponents, both pagans and heretics, pressed above all others against the church. If, they say, Jesus descended from Joseph and his ancestors, then Joseph was his father, and Jesus was not born of a virgin and therefore could not be the Messiah. But if he was not begotten by Joseph, what does Joseph’s genealogy have to do with him? Why was not the lineage of Mary, who was certainly his mother, described instead? To untie this knot, the opinion was invented which holds that Mary’s genealogy, not Joseph’s, is woven into Luke’s Gospel. Yet besides the fact that we have opposed many reasons and authorities to this opinion, even if it were granted it would not untie the knot. For although the difficulty would not touch Luke as he narrated Mary’s lineage, it would not shield Matthew, who records Joseph’s genealogy alone, from the enemies’ shafts. I therefore prefer the ancient and common answer which frees both evangelists from our opponents’ cavils: namely, that among the Jews women were not customarily counted in men’s genealogies, but men alone; and furthermore, that in—

[Marginalia: The Jews did not count women in men’s genealogies.]

[Logical printed p. 65 | IA leaf 403 | access PDF 403 | OCR object 402]

—a husband’s genealogy the lineage and family of his wife were customarily described. Chrysostom, Ambrose, Jerome, and many other fathers, both Greek and Latin, rested in this answer.

The first part of the answer must be understood so that it does not deny that a woman’s ancestry was sometimes recorded among the Jews. Grant that Judith’s lineage is related truly, although that apocryphal book, composed by some Judaizing petty Greek, teems with trifles and fables. It is also true that 1 Chronicles 1 and 2, and other Jewish genealogies elsewhere, contain the names of various women. What is asserted, however, is that in Hebrew genealogies men were customarily referred to a tribe and family not through their mothers but through their fathers, according to the familiar rabbinic saying, “the mother’s family is not family.” A man whose mother was from the tribe of Judah or Levi was not himself reckoned in those tribes unless his father also belonged to them. The Messiah had to be excepted from this common rule, since he necessarily received his tribe from his mother because he was fatherless and could have no father among men. Yet he was not expressly and by name referred through his mother either to a tribe or a family, lest the ordinary and customary method of genealogies be abandoned without necessity. I say “without necessity” because the tribe and family of Joseph, Christ’s putative and legal father, were the same as those of Mary, his true and natural mother. It was unnecessary to refer Christ under Mary’s name to David’s family and the tribe of Judah contrary to the nation’s custom, since the same thing could be accomplished just as suitably under the name of Joseph, Mary’s husband, while preserving the ordinary custom.

But this is the second part of the answer, a necessary addition to the first, by which this point may also be illustrated. First, then, it must be observed that this custom of the Jews, by which men married women only from their own tribe, was founded upon God’s law, which is stated explicitly in Numbers 36:6 and the verses following.

Second. According to that law, women were required to marry not only within their own tribe, but also within their own family in that tribe, and indeed to marry the man most closely related to them in their family. The reason for the law was to preserve possessions not only in the same tribe and in the same family understood broadly, but in the family understood as narrowly as could suitably be done. Otherwise many individual families would in a few years have been stripped of their inheritances through women’s marriages to outsiders. Indeed, the daughters of Zelophehad, who gave occasion for the law and in whom the first practice of the enacted law took place, were given as wives to their very nearest agnates, namely their first cousins, the sons of their paternal uncle.

Third, it must be observed that the law speaks in the first place about landed heiresses, whose dowry consisted in inheritances of land, not about dowered women whose dowry consisted not in fields, which passed to their brothers, but in money or other movable family property. Hence sacred Scripture supplies various examples of women who married not only outside their family but even outside their tribe, especially among the Levites, where Aaron and Jehoiada married wives from the tribe of Judah. For in the tribe of Levi women as well as men could readily contract marriages with outsiders, because the Levites had no landed possessions and through their marriages could neither give nor take away fields. Although the law was enacted first and chiefly concerning landed heiresses, nevertheless, because through the death of relatives and other circumstances dowered women or their descendants often became landed heiresses, the common custom—which a few exceptions do not violate—required that no woman marry outside her tribe, indeed outside her family. In this way, when the husband’s tribe and family had been discovered, they disclosed the wife’s tribe and family. Accordingly, since Joseph’s lineage was traced to Judah and David, no one among the Jews, Christ’s greatest enemies, ever raised any doubt that the lineage of his wife Mary was traced to the same Judah and David, especially since Joseph was acknowledged by all as a just man who religiously observed his ancestral laws and customs.

Finally, it must be observed that the virgin Mary was a landed heiress. Although she was poor, her property and family prospects were not so meager that no little plot of land was left her by her father, or that no small portion could come to her under any circumstances. This was especially so because, when the house of David had been reduced to very few, it was quite easy for any survivor among those remnants to acquire, if not possession in those tyrannical times, nevertheless a right to quite extensive estates.

But what proves above all that Mary was an heiress—indeed, the greatest of heiresses, a point badly neglected by most interpreters—is her prerogative above all mortals, that the Son of God should take flesh from her. The distinction of all the Israelites’ tribes, families, and inheritances was instituted chiefly for this end: that the Messiah’s birth from the blessed virgin should occur distinctly, certainly, and clearly, according to the promises, prophecies, and order appointed by God. This was the primary end for which any person among the Jews, whether man or woman, was ever a landed heir, or possessed even a handful of soil in the land of Canaan as its distinct heir. Should not she, then, deservedly be considered the greatest heiress, poor though she was in earthly things, when all other people’s possessions tended toward her one surpassing inheritance as their end, aim, center, and antitype? Ought she not, for her incomparable privilege, to enjoy before all others every right of landed heiresses? This applies especially to the point before us: that she was bound to marry only within her own tribe and her own family, and there to marry her nearest agnate.

These points are sufficient to resolve the question. Before I leave it, however, I shall warn of several things to be avoided. First, no one should suppose that the virgin Mary was from the tribe of Levi, for this very claim was what the Gnostics and Faustus the Manichaean pressed against the Christian faith. We grant that Augustine, Nazianzen, and many other fathers affirm that Christ descended not only from David’s royal line but also from Levi’s priestly line, and that Luke describes his priestly lineage just as Matthew describes his royal one. But those statements must be understood with a grain of salt.

Casaubon rightly censures Baronius because he quotes the fathers’ testimonies concerning Christ’s priestly lineage and approves them without any qualification. It is certainly inconsistent with the Christian faith for Christ to be a Levitical priest, for, as the apostle says at Hebrews 7:13–14, Christ was from the tribe of Judah, from which no one could serve at the Aaronic altar. Perhaps the fathers, whom Casaubon and others there mark as grossly mistaken, can be excused from error by some suitable distinction. Suárez, in the passage cited above, diligently attempts this with the following two inventions. The first is that the blessed virgin Mary belonged to Aaron’s family through either her own mother or the mother of one of her ancestors, and thus through the female rather than the male line. The second is to assert that the fathers called Christ’s line in Luke priestly only in a mystical, not a literal, sense, because the son of David to whom Luke refers Christ’s genealogy had the same name as the prophet Nathan, who proclaimed to David the expiation of his sin, which was a priestly action. But I fear that these remedies cannot save the fathers themselves from blemish. The first claim, concerning priestly descent through the maternal line, is entirely gratuitous and rests upon neither reason nor authority. The second is an invention plainly worthy of the subtlety of the Angelic Thomas. Although it may be perceived by the sharpness of a Spanish Jesuit, our dullness discerns in it no spark of reason and no shadow of an argument.

The fathers indeed assert Luke 1:5 and 36 in support of their opinion, reasoning as follows. Elizabeth, Zechariah’s wife, was one of Aaron’s daughters. But Mary, Christ’s mother, was Elizabeth’s kinswoman. The answer is easy: Elizabeth’s mother or grandmother could have been from the tribe of Judah and therefore closely related in any degree to Mary’s father or grandfather, for nothing prohibited priests from marrying wives from the tribe of Judah, as has been said. Baronius rightly censures Ambrose here for placing Elizabeth in the tribe of Judah, although Luke expressly affirms that she was one of Aaron’s daughters. Yet Baronius himself is no less deserving of censure because, in this age of learning, he champions many utterly foul fables about Mary: as though she had been born among the priests, handed over to the priests by her parents when she was three, and placed by them in the Holy of Holies, where she remained for eleven full years and never went out until, in her thirteenth year, those priests gave her to Joseph in marriage. Elsewhere Baronius acknowledges that Nicephorus, who spread these fables if he did not invent them, was an exceedingly frivolous man, and that the authors cited here by Nicephorus, Hippolytus of Portus and Evodius of Antioch, are entirely fictitious. Nevertheless, because we refuse to embrace their trifles against the clearest Scriptures—which admit no mortal within the Holy of Holies except the one high priest, and him only once each year—we must be adorned with the titles of innovators and heretics.

Second, the opinion of Spalato in book 6 of *On the Commonwealth*, chapter 1, paragraph 7, must be avoided. Papal flatterers, gathering sophisms from every quarter to establish the pope’s temporal monarchy, have seized upon this one among others. Christ, they say, as David’s successor had a right to the temporal kingdom of Israel. Therefore his vicar, the pope, has a right to the temporal kingdom of the whole universal church. Spalato, not content to reject the inference, attacks even the premise far too rashly. Certainly many of the most learned Protestants and Roman Catholics maintain Christ’s right, as David’s son, to the temporal kingdom of the Jews, and the arguments Spalato brings forward on the other side are not difficult to answer. But what he is the first after the Gnostics and Manichaeans to advance in this unnecessary question against all Christians cannot be tolerated. To demonstrate that the temporal kingdom over the Jews did not belong to Christ at all, he affirms that it cannot be proved that the blessed virgin belonged either to David’s family or to the tribe of Judah. He nevertheless says that Christ is referred to Judah and David through Joseph, whose legal son he was, and that by this one right he would have belonged to David’s family even if his mother had arisen from the tribe of Levi, Ephraim, or any other.

This is a bold and novel opinion, which Spanheim hews down quite handsomely in part 1 of his *Gospel Difficulties*, difficulty 12, paragraph 36. We shall add only a few points.

First, Spalato gains nothing from his hypothesis. According to it, Christ succeeded to all the rights of Joseph’s legal son, while legal sons no less than natural sons succeeded to inheritances and even kingdoms. By this very right, therefore, succession to David’s kingdom would belong to Christ as a legal son. If it is objected that Joseph had other natural sons older than Christ, his legal son, reply: this is indeed asserted by some writers both ancient and recent, but gratuitously and without any solid proof, and is denied by more.

—assign him a place in both. Therefore, just as one can, while preserving every right of legal sonship, deny simply and absolutely that Christ was Joseph’s true son, so one could also deny simply and absolutely that Christ belonged to David’s family or the tribe of Judah, and grant only that he belonged to each in a certain respect, namely according to legal sonship. But this is immensely repugnant to the Scriptures of both Testaments: Genesis 49:10, Shiloh, the Messiah, is born from Judah, from between his feet, as the Hebrew has it; Isaiah 11:1, a rod arises from Jesse’s roots and seed from his inward parts; 2 Samuel 7; Hebrews 1:5; Acts 11:30. To deny that these statements concern the Messiah’s natural generation from David’s family and the tribe of Judah appears not to be a mere error but, as Suárez rightly calls it in the passage cited above, an error in the faith.

Third. The one testimony Spalato brings from Pseudo-Justin in his own support is wholly irrelevant: “It pleased God to give Joseph a son from Joseph’s wife, a son whom he himself did not beget by nature.” What then? Must Mary therefore be expelled from David’s family? Who doubts the truth of the premise? Nor need any scruple remain concerning the falsity of the conclusion.

Fourth. In this argument Spanheim appears wrongly to deny that Christ was Joseph’s legal son. All his arguments on this matter concern only the right of levirate marriage, but, as was said above, legal sonship among both Jews and gentiles rested upon many other rights besides that of levirate marriage. Spanheim is certainly singular in this assertion and does not appear worthy of being followed in it.

Third, particular determinations must be avoided. Most writers, ancient as well as recent, remain with generalities in this question as in the preceding one, content to assert that Mary was related to Joseph and that her lineage and genealogy were comprehended in her husband’s genealogy. Before John of Damascus no one dared to determine the degree in which Mary was related to Joseph. But in book 4 of *On the Orthodox Faith*, chapter 15, he most boldly relates their entire kinship, which the ancients had not known for a thousand years, as follows. Levi, he says, Joseph’s great-grandfather, begot two sons, Melchi and Panther. Panther begot Barpanther, Barpanther begot Joachim, and Joachim begot Mary. But Melchi, Panther’s brother, begot Eli by Estha, the wife of Matthan, Jacob’s father; and when Eli died childless, Jacob, his maternal half-brother, begot Joseph, Mary’s husband, by Eli’s wife. In this way Joseph, through his natural father Jacob and his grandfather Matthat from Solomon’s family, was related in the third or fourth degree of consanguinity to his wife Mary from Nathan’s family. For Mary was the daughter of Joachim, granddaughter of Barpanther, great-granddaughter of Panther, and great-great-granddaughter of Melchi, whose son Eli by Estha, Matthan’s wife, was the brother of Jacob, Joseph’s father.

Both Grotius and Vossius, in the passages cited above, patronize these inventions of John of Damascus too much. Nevertheless, we reject them. First, they rest upon the authority of no testimony except that of John of Damascus himself, who lived a thousand years after Christ, in a very corrupt age, and sprinkled many fables, trifles, and errors through his writings, if we give credit even to Baronius himself. Second, John of Damascus here openly contradicts Luke when he makes Levi the father of Melchi, whom Luke makes Melchi’s son. Third, he placed Panther and Barpanther in Mary’s lineage by drawing them, as Origen testifies and as may be read today in the Talmud according to Vossius’s own citation, from the impure falsehoods of the Jews, Christ’s enemies. Nor do the passages Vossius cites from Epiphanius agree in many respects with the fables of John of Damascus, as will be evident to anyone who looks. Fourth, he makes Mary’s father Joachim an entirely different man from Eli: namely, the son of Barpanther and grandson of Panther, Melchi’s brother, whose son Eli, according to him, was Joachim’s paternal uncle. Neither Grotius nor Vossius dares to follow John of Damascus in these badly stitched fables.

The Jesuits prefer another invention. From Nicephorus, Christopher de Castro, whom Cornelius a Lapide follows, traces Christ’s genealogy so as to make Matthan the common grandfather of Joseph and Mary in this way. Matthan, he says, begot Jacob and Jacob’s sisters Sobe and Anna. Sobe was the mother of Elizabeth, who bore John the Baptist to Zechariah. Anna married Eli, or Joachim, and bore him the blessed virgin, while Jacob begot Joseph. Thus Mary’s consanguinity with Joseph and Elizabeth shines through without any difficulty. But Nicephorus drew these claims from authors of a bad stamp, Evodius of Antioch and Hippolytus of Portus, straw men who were not only fictitious but full of false fables, whom Baronius, with too little regard for the dignity of his *Annals*, esteems much too highly.

In any event, I note the following about this part of the invented genealogy. First, Nicephorus and his reprobate authorities Evodius and Hippolytus call Matthan, whom they make Joseph and Mary’s grandfather, a priest. This contradicts the evangelists, who place the ancestors of both in the tribe of Judah. Second, they place Joseph and Mary’s kinship in the fact that Mary’s mother Anna was the sister of Jacob, Joseph’s father. But affinity through the maternal line gave Joseph no right to marry Mary. Hence Lucas Brugensis refuses to say that Anna, Mary’s mother, was Jacob’s sister, but asserts that Joachim, Mary’s father, was Jacob’s brother. If this were true, the close consanguinity of Joseph and Mary would be plainly demonstrated.

—Solomon and the former to Nathan. Third, they take Eli and Jehoiakim as one and the same name, which cannot be granted, although even eminent Protestant men affirm it. First, although among the Jews one man sometimes had two names, those two names of one man were not one name. Eliakim and Jehoiakim were certainly no more one name than Solomon and Jedidiah, or Gideon and Jerubbaal. Second, Eliakim and Jehoiakim do not even signify one thing, unless we hold that Jehovah and El, and all the divine names, signify not only one God but also one and the same attribute in God. Third, even if Eliakim and Jehoiakim were one name, Eli and Eliakim are not one. For why is Eliakim one with Eli any more than Elizabeth, Elimelech, Elishama, and many other names compounded from Eli and something added? Fourth, Galatinus, if not the author then at least the principal advocate of this invention, cannot show that Eli and Jehoiakim signify one thing unless he first corrupts the name Eli by writing עֵלִי, so as to derive it from עָלָה, “he ascended,” whereas Eli comes from the name אֵל, in which there is no *gnajin*. If *gnajin* were the first radical in this word, it would have an affinity with *jakim*, the latter part of the words Jehoiakim and Eliakim, but none with the earlier elements El and Jehovah.

Finally, there is another particular reconciliation by Grotius, which appears no more plausible than those preceding. At Luke 3:23, with astonishing boldness, he confuses everything in various ways through inventions which he nevertheless admits are entirely arbitrary. He does this contrary to the judgment of all Christians, except perhaps that of his follower in the worst things, Dr. Hammond, who refers us back here to Grotius himself. First, although Grotius grants that within an equal period one family can and commonly does surpass another by a great number of persons, he nevertheless refuses to admit this with all learned men in the evangelists. Instead, to match the seven persons by whom in Luke Nathan’s family between Matthat and Melchi surpasses Solomon’s family, he brings out of his own storehouse seven anonymous persons between Eleazar and Matthan in Matthew, with whom he may supposedly fill the gaps in the apostle Matthew. Second, with his friend Cordesius he dares to put the sickle to Luke’s very text by cutting out both Matthat and Levi. Third, he embraces nearly all the Jewish trifles about the Panthers that John of Damascus revived. Fourth, after excising the evangelist’s Levi, he himself brings another Levi from his second storehouse, whom he invents as the brother of Jacob and Eli, the father of Barpanther, and the blessed virgin’s great-great-grandfather. Fifth, he holds that Jacob, Matthan’s son and Joseph’s natural father, died childless; he makes Joseph Eli’s natural son and merely the successor to the goods of Jacob, who was really his paternal uncle. Sixth, in an astonishing fiction, he makes Jacob, Eli, and Levi full brothers, the three natural sons of Matthan. Seventh, although he counts four persons in Luke’s family of Nathan between Melchi and Mary, but only one in Matthew’s family of Solomon between Matthan and Joseph, here he is nevertheless inconsistent and does not fill Matthew’s gaps with three anonymous persons. Eighth, although with the Roman Catholics he identifies Mary’s father as Jehoiakim, whom he holds to be the same as Eli, Joseph’s father, he nevertheless makes Joseph three degrees removed from Eli: namely, the son of Panther, grandson of Barpanther, and great-grandson of Levi, Eli’s brother, who, he holds, begot Joseph. Thus Grotius most wretchedly perverts the evangelists’ sacrosanct genealogy, adding, removing, and inverting more than any mortal before him.
