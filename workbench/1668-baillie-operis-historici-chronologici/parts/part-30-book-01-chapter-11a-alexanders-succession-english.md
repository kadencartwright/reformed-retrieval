### CHAPTER XI

#### Containing the events of the sixth epoch, from the death of Alexander the Great to the kingship of Aristobulus in Judaea

In Chapter IX, on the preceding epoch, I explained how the two-horned ram—the
kings of Persia and Media—turned everything up and down at their pleasure in
the east, north, and south for 236 years, until the he-goat from the west, the
king of the Greeks, charged the ram, cast it to the ground, broke both its
horns, and trampled all the power of the Persians underfoot. We also showed
how the notable horn growing from the forehead of the he-goat was broken
within a short time by Alexander’s premature and unexpected death. It remains
for us now to explain how the four horns arose on the head of the he-goat;
how the empire of the Greeks or Macedonians was divided into four principal
kingdoms—the Ptolemies’ Egypt, the Seleucids’ Babylon and Syria, Antigonus’s
Asia Minor, and Antipater’s Macedonia and Greece under his successors, or
also under Antigonus; how the little horn, Antiochus Epiphanes, grew from the
root of the Seleucids; and finally how the he-goat itself and all its horns
were shattered and crushed by the fourth beast, exceedingly terrible and
strong, the Roman Empire, which with its iron teeth broke all the strength of
every preceding empire.

The outstanding histories of those times furnish splendid light and a true
commentary not only on the seventh, eighth, and eleventh chapters of Daniel
(otherwise exceedingly obscure), but also on many passages of Scripture, both
canonical and apocryphal. We have the sources of these histories in
Plutarch’s *Eumenes*, *Phocion*, and *Demetrius*; in Justin’s abridgment of
Pompeius Trogus; and in both Josephuses, Flavius and Ben Gorion. Before all
others, however, they appear most fully and clearly in Diodorus Siculus;
Curtius and Arrian end with Alexander. What we read in Capellus, Ussher,
Pesellius, Rhalegus and its supplement by our countryman Rosa, Salianus, and

Torniellus too, Petavius’s *Chronological Reckoning*, and the whole other vast
host of more recent historians is all drawn from the sources just named and,
according to each writer’s particular ability and eloquence, handed down in
many fashions and with ample intricacy. According to my own very small
ability, may I too be permitted to have drawn the following narrative from
both the ancient and the more recent writers.

As soon as Alexander breathed his last in Babylon, the greatest mourning
first filled the city and the palace; afterward, immense consternation seized
the army. It contained many men fully fitted by strength of body and mind and
by the excellent training of Philip and Alexander over many years to command
great armies and govern kingdoms. Yet this was the origin of ruin for them
all. While all aspired to the highest place, and no one was sincerely willing
to obey any other, they all plunged into mutual destruction. Those who had
shed such great streams of blood from noble families and peoples in so many
kingdoms of Asia executed divine vengeance upon themselves with their own
hands while the conquered regions remained at rest and looked on. Within a
few years they utterly eradicated the entire families of Alexander and the
kings of Macedonia, and indeed of all the commanders and all their friends.
They even allowed very few of the common soldiers in the triumphant and
everywhere victorious Macedonian army to die a natural death. They reduced
the Macedonian nation to the most worthless, impoverished, and contemptible
province, as it had once been, and became the cause of infinite calamities
and of the entire Greek nation’s servitude, which has lasted to this day.

The first act of this tragedy was staged in Babylon. After several hours of
lamentation and weeping, all the leading commanders and a great mass of
soldiers rushed into the palace and began to deliberate about a successor to
the empire. Alexander’s throne, diadem, and royal robe were set in the center.
Perdiccas, to whom Alexander had handed his ring, placed it upon the throne
beside the diadem, as if he were willingly casting the burden of government
from himself upon anyone else. After much deliberation, Perdiccas advised them
to await Roxane’s delivery. He hoped that after two months she would bear a
male, to whom his father’s throne would be given, and that in the meantime the
state should be administered by ἐπιμεληταί, or guardians. Nearchus spoke on
behalf of Heracles, whom Barsine, Memnon’s widow, had borne to Alexander
eleven years before. Ptolemy argued for an aristocracy of the leading
commanders. But Aristander advanced Alexander’s judgment, since he had handed
the ring, and with it the empire, to Perdiccas. When many urged Perdiccas to
take back the ring, he feigned excessive modesty merely so that they would
press him more. Rising from the commanders’ assembly, he mingled with the
crowd as though fleeing far from the throne. While he hesitated, Meleager
assailed Perdiccas bitterly and at length as wholly unworthy of the summit of
kingship. He urged the soldiers to follow him and plunder and divide
Alexander’s treasures among themselves as their inheritance, the reward
earned by their labors and blood.

While Meleager went off to the treasury with a crowd of followers, an unknown
common soldier delivered a weighty speech advising them to place the diadem
upon Arrhidaeus, son of Philip and brother of Alexander. The soldiers’ favor
immediately turned toward him. Meleager summoned the young man, who had no
thought of such a thing, dressed him in the royal robe and diadem, and seated
him upon the throne. The leading commanders disliked the choice. Arrhidaeus
would indeed have possessed quite a good and lofty nature from the womb, but
Olympias had deranged his mind with drugs because Philip had acknowledged him
as his son by the concubine Philene. His understanding was therefore too dull
for him to be fit to bear the burden of so great an empire. While the
commanders and soldiers disputed about him, and he himself prepared to lay
aside the insignia of kingship, missiles began to fly between the factions. After many
were wounded, they agreed that Arrhidaeus and Roxane’s child, if it were a
son, should reign together, with protectors selected from the commanders
because of the child’s infancy and the man’s infirmity. Despite this peace,
the anger of Meleager and Perdiccas boiled over. The cavalry stood with the
latter and the infantry with the former. Meleager advised Arrhidaeus to send
men to summon Perdiccas and kill him on his way. Perdiccas terrified the
agents coming to arrest him by the majesty of his countenance and the force
of his speech, and fled outside the walls to Leonnatus and the cavalry. The
city was besieged by the cavalry and oppressed by hunger, but peace was soon
made: Meleager was to join Perdiccas and Leonnatus as a third governor of the
state. At Perdiccas’s instigation, some of his friends expressed indignation
against Meleager. They declared that they would refuse to have Perdiccas put
on the same level and to obey Meleager’s commands. Meleager raged and cried
out that this was treachery by Perdiccas, perfidious above all others, against
the covenants they had made. Perdiccas concealed himself deeply, utterly
denied any knowledge of what the rebels had done, and sincerely offered his
aid so that they might all pay for their treason with their heads. Meleager
was appeased, and he and Perdiccas embraced each other with every sign of
friendship. On the pretext of arresting for punishment those who had insulted
Meleager, Perdiccas persuaded them to hold a purification of the army. By
this snare of Perdiccas, Meleager was caught along with all his men. For while
the whole army stood under arms, in such an order and place that the infantry
was held within the power of the cavalry and elephants,

the foremost friends of Meleager who had most fiercely carried Arrhidaeus off
to the throne with him—three hundred according to Curtius and, after him,
Salianus and Rhalegus, but only thirty according to Diodorus—were cast before
the elephants and immediately crushed. Meleager saw that Arrhidaeus remained
silent at this and realized too late that it was all over for him. He fled to
a sanctuary, but in vain: Perdiccas, now master of everything, ordered him
dragged out and stabbed to death.

Freed from his present enemy, Perdiccas aspired more openly to sole rule, but
advanced gradually, lest haste overturn hopes that were not yet mature. His
first concern was to entrust all the provinces to governors in the way most
convenient to his plans. Yet to avoid offending his rivals, he committed
Macedonia and Greece to Antipater; Egypt and Africa to Ptolemy; Thrace to
Lysimachus; Phrygia on the Hellespont to Leonnatus; Greater Phrygia to
Antigonus; Persia to Peucestas; the command of the cavalry to Seleucus; the
infantry camp to Python; and custody of the treasury to Craterus. Besides an
annual revenue of thirty thousand, Alexander had left fifty thousand talents
in the treasury.

Roxane, Alexander’s wife, summoned his other wife Stateira, Darius’s eldest
daughter, and her sister Drypetis, Hephaestion’s widow, to the palace with
very affectionate letters sealed by Alexander’s ring. Once they arrived, she
had them both immediately killed without the shadow of any charge, with
Perdiccas’s aid, and buried together in an obscure pit. These were the
beginnings of such great evils.

> Marginalia, printed p. 205: *Roxane cruelly kills Stateira*.

When news of Alexander’s death reached the Greek colonies he had planted in
the upper satrapies, twenty-two thousand of their bravest infantry and three
thousand cavalry set out on the road toward their native land. Perdiccas sent
Python to restrain them. Python eagerly undertook the expedition, intending
to win all these Greek forces to himself and, chiefly with their help, seize
supreme power. Foreseeing this, Perdiccas commanded Python in the army’s
hearing to massacre all the Greek rebels and distribute their goods to the
soldiers as plunder. When Python reached the rebels, he corrupted with
promises Lypodorus, a commander of three thousand of them, so that at the
height of battle he would desert to Python. Having gained the victory by this
agreement and persisting in his own design, Python granted the defeated
Greeks whatever terms of peace they desired: they were to be escorted safely
to their homeland with their property intact. Although these terms had been
agreed and ratified by a solemn oath, the Macedonians nevertheless remembered
Perdiccas’s commands. Out of greed for plunder, and in contempt for the
sanctity of the oath and Python’s authority, they stabbed every last Greek to
death and divided among themselves the wealth the Greeks had gathered from
the spoils of Asia over twelve years.

Once this was done, Perdiccas pressed ahead under full sail to seize the
empire. Yet many obstacles still prevented him from disclosing his intentions:
Ptolemy, Antipater, Leonnatus, and Craterus, rivals for the same power, had to
be removed. Ariarathes, king of the Cappadocians, whom Alexander in his haste
had passed over, still stood under arms with thirty thousand infantry and
fifteen thousand cavalry. Perdiccas assigned Eumenes to subdue him with the
royal army. He broke the Cappadocians in one battle and then another, captured
Ariarathes with all his relatives, and fastened them to crosses. The Pisidian
cities of Laranda and Isaura had also rebelled. Perdiccas captured Laranda,
razed it to the ground, and killed every adult male. Isaura reduced itself to
ashes by fire in order to escape the savage hands of the Macedonians.

> Marginalia, printed p. 205: *Perdiccas’s cruelty toward the Cappadocians
> and Pisidians*.

Meanwhile, the Lamian War was blazing in Greece. Shortly before his death,
Alexander had announced by a herald’s voice at the Olympic games to all the
Greek cities that exiles who were guilty of neither murder nor sacrilege
should be permitted to return home. The Athenians and Aetolians took this
edict extremely ill. As long as Alexander lived, they did not dare make any
move. But when they heard that he had died, they immediately called them to
arms and drew as many Greeks as they could into an armed alliance against the
Macedonians. The Argives, Thessalians, and others immediately joined them.
They ordered Leosthenes to hire eight thousand of Alexander’s veteran
soldiers then living in the Peloponnese. Against this army of more than forty
thousand valiant men, Antipater could scrape together scarcely fourteen
thousand from Macedonia, which had been exhausted by continual levies. Battle
was joined, the Macedonians fled, and the victorious Leosthenes shut Antipater
within the walls of Lamia. After a close siege he nearly captured him, but
while Leosthenes oversaw the clearing of the trenches nearest the walls, he
fell, pierced by a missile from the wall.

Meanwhile, Antipater sent men to summon Leonnatus and Craterus from Asia to
his aid. Leonnatus arrived immediately, but with the plan of destroying
Antipater too once the Greeks had been defeated, for he himself was among the
foremost aspirants to the empire. His ambition was soon extinguished,
however: Antiphilus, Leosthenes’s successor, met him, defeated his forces, and
killed him.

While they fought, Antipater, now escaped from liberated Lamia, collected the
remnants of his shattered army and withdrew to Macedonia to await Craterus’s
arrival from Asia. The complacent Greeks did not press their defeated enemy;
instead, a large

part of them dispersed to their private affairs. Consequently, Antipater and
Craterus soon attacked Antiphilus’s much smaller
forces with forty-five thousand men, easily defeated them, and reduced all
Greece to its former servitude. Antipater nevertheless gave the defeated
better terms than they themselves had hoped, since he was preparing war
against Perdiccas and foresaw that the friendship of the Greeks would be
necessary to him.

He poured out his old anger upon Demosthenes alone. Before Alexander’s death,
Demosthenes had been living in exile on the island of Calauria. Yet by his
letters he was a great firebrand of the Lamian War, while Aeschines, also in
exile at the time, discouraged that war with all his strength. After the
victory, Antipater sent men to Calauria to bring Demosthenes to him. Mindful
of his perpetual hostility toward the Macedonians, Demosthenes kept himself
from falling into their hands by biting a pen poisoned with hemlock while he
wrote, and died.

At that same time Aristotle too was an exile from Athens, living at Chalcis
as though guilty of impiety toward the gods. Entirely removed from the public
upheavals in the world that had been greatest in his own days and had been
caused by his pupils, he died peacefully soon afterward, just as Diogenes the
Cynic had died at Corinth on the same day Alexander died at Babylon. But let
us continue with the general history.

In order to catch Antipater off guard and crush him, Perdiccas had married
Antipater’s daughter Nicaea and had given Eurydice, born to Cyna, Alexander’s
half-sister, in marriage to King Arrhidaeus. But now, seeing that his power
had increased and that he had less need of Antipater’s friendship, he killed
Cyna, Eurydice’s mother, and formed a plan to repudiate Nicaea and marry
Cleopatra, Alexander’s full sister. Antigonus arranged to inform Antipater
secretly of these frauds of Perdiccas. Perdiccas, detecting Antigonus’s
betrayal, prepared to kill him, but Antigonus escaped with his son Demetrius
to Antipater. Once he had demonstrated to Antipater in person Perdiccas’s
settled intention to seize tyrannical power, there was no further delay:
Antipater, Craterus, Ptolemy, and Antigonus entered into an alliance against
Perdiccas. Perdiccas, thinking that he must strike, advanced into Egypt as
quickly as possible to destroy Ptolemy while separated from his allies. First,
however, he ordered Eumenes to take up a position at the Hellespont with the
troops of Neoptolemus and of Perdiccas’s brother Alcetas, in order to prevent
Antipater’s crossing into Asia.

Alexander could scarcely enjoy the benefit common to the most wretched of
mortals, a peaceful burial in the bosom of the earth. His body lay for seven
full days without anyone’s care while the Macedonians disputed over the
government of the empire. Only then was it disemboweled by Chaldean and
Egyptian apothecaries, preserved within and without by their craft with
spices, and placed in a golden coffin. Care of the burial was entrusted not
to Arrhidaeus the king, as Diodorus, Justin, Capellus, and others wrongly
state, but to Arrhidaeus the satrap of the Hellespont. He spent more than two
years making preparations. Finally spurred by Olympias’s letters, which
bitterly complained that the honor of burial was being denied her son, and
by the false predictions of Aristander the seer that the land possessing
Alexander’s remains would enjoy blessed and perpetual peace, he at last set
out with the most costly procession. The carriage, on which Alexander’s
remains and armor were drawn by more than forty mules, surpassed belief in
gold, gems, and craftsmanship—if what Diodorus and others after him report is
true. Ptolemy came from Egypt as far as Syria to meet it with a most splendid
retinue. He conducted it as far as Alexandria and there, passing over the
extremely troublesome journey to Ammon because of a very violent storm,
committed the coffin to the earth at enormous expense in a temple built for
this very purpose. By these services to Alexander, Ptolemy greatly won the
hearts of the Macedonians; by his great humanity toward the Egyptians, he
also bound them uniquely to himself. But Perdiccas’s envy was stirred all the
more, and he hastened into Egypt to bring about his own destruction more
quickly.

> Marginalia, printed p. 206: *Alexander’s burial*.

Meanwhile, Antipater and Craterus hurried to Ptolemy’s aid. To delay them,
Eumenes set out with Alcetas and the forces of Neoptolemus. Burning with envy
of Eumenes, Neoptolemus secretly conspired with Antipater. The very shrewd
Eumenes detected this, openly attacked and defeated Neoptolemus, and joined
his troops to his own. Neoptolemus escaped with a few men to Antipater,
Craterus, and Antigonus. Antipater continued on his way, intending, if he
could, to bring Ptolemy timely aid against Perdiccas. Neoptolemus persuaded
Craterus to attack Eumenes, confidently asserting that most of Eumenes’s
soldiers would desert the moment they saw Craterus. But Eumenes’s cunning
frustrated this hope. As Craterus approached, Eumenes arranged for a rumor to
be spread that only Neoptolemus and a Cappadocian were bearing down upon them,
and took care that none of his Macedonians was set against Craterus.

Craterus stood on one wing of the enemy and Neoptolemus on the other. Eumenes
himself attacked Neoptolemus, while he set Pharnabazus son of Artabazus
against Craterus, ordering him to attack without saying who he was. In the
heat of battle, Craterus fell to the ground when his horse stumbled and was
trampled by combatants who did not recognize him. Eumenes fought Neoptolemus
in single combat and killed him. Having won a signal victory, Eumenes made
terms with the defeated before there could be extensive slaughter: they were
to return

peacefully to Greece. But they broke their word and hurried away to Antipater.

At this juncture Perdiccas perished in Egypt, charging toward his doom in
stubborn pride. Ptolemy had fortified everything in Egypt as firmly as he
could against Perdiccas’s attack. Perdiccas spent his first assault in vain
upon an insignificant fortress near Pelusium: the Nile, overflowing from an
open channel, forced him to move his camp with losses. Ptolemy himself
valiantly defended another fortress against Perdiccas’s attack for an entire
day. On his third attempt, as Perdiccas tried to cross the Nile near Memphis,
he met an unexpected fate. Part of the infantry crossed the ford only with
great difficulty, the water reaching almost to their chins. Although
elephants broke the Nile’s current above them and cavalry stood in the river
below to catch anyone swept away by the force of the water, suddenly and
unexpectedly either the Nile rose or the sand disturbed on the bottom of the
ford sank under the weight of elephants, horses, and men, making the river
impassable to the infantry. Those who had crossed first were consequently
forced to return, and many died in the water. A full thousand men were
devoured by crocodiles and other beasts in the Nile as their fellow soldiers
looked on. A great outcry against Perdiccas arose in the camp, as though he
were needlessly destroying his soldiers in a futile war through his own
pride. The murmuring increased when they heard of Ptolemy’s kindness: he most
cordially received every one of their men who had been carried alive to the
opposite bank, and performed the solemn rites customary among the Greeks for
all whose bodies the Nile cast out near his camp. Everyone abhorred the
inhuman and ill-fated Perdiccas. One hundred of his commanders conspired
against him; Python burst into his tent and stabbed him and all his friends
whom they could find.

Ptolemy came to the Macedonians and was received with every honor. They would
gladly have entrusted him with care of the kings and the supreme command of
the army, but the prudent man was sufficiently content with his ample lot in
Egypt and arranged for this burden and honor to be imposed upon Python.
