QUESTION IX.

The Necessity of Scripture

Bellarmine says in book 4, chapter 4 of *The Word of God* that he will first
demonstrate that without traditions the Scriptures were neither simply
necessary nor sufficient. He then says that they are necessary for us because
of human corruption, and that this is understood not of necessity simply, but
for the church's well-being. We differ from the papists because they assert
that the Scriptures are not simply necessary for the church's existence. In
this respect they agree in part with the Libertines and Anabaptists, since the
former reject all Scripture and the latter reject the Old Testament as
unnecessary. We, however, assert that Scripture is not only useful but also
necessary for the church's existence—not by an absolute and simple necessity,
because God was not bound by a necessity of Scripture so that he could not
teach the church without it if he willed, but by an ordained necessity based
upon the supposition of the divine arrangement, because after Moses God willed
the church to be bound to the Scriptures in two respects: 1. With respect to
their writing. 2. With respect to their perpetual existence. For without
Scripture true doctrine could no more be preserved amid so many heresies and
corruptions of morals than natural life can be preserved without the daily
use of food and drink.

The state of the question, therefore, is whether Scripture is necessary for
the church's existence. They deny it; we affirm it, relying upon the following
arguments.

Argument 1. That without which there is no faith is necessary not only for the
church's well-being, but also simply for its existence. But such is Scripture
(John 20:31; Rom. 10:17; 2 Tim. 3:15).

Argument 2. That without which we err in faith and morals is simply necessary
for the church. But without Scripture we so err (Matt. 22:29; 1 Tim. 3:14;

[1620 Tome I | col. 122 | BSB frame 00075]

2 John 10). Jerome therefore rightly says, “Ignorance of the Scriptures is
ignorance of Christ.”

Argument 3. What God commands simply is necessary simply. But God commands
Scripture simply, both with respect to its writing (Exod. 17:14; 34:27; Jer.
30:2; Ezek. 43:11; Matt. 28:19, where by “teach” he understands two modes of
teaching, namely by living voice and in writing; Rom. 15:4; 2 Pet. 1:12, 17;
Jude 3; Rev. 1:11; 21:5) and with respect to its perpetual existence (Deut.
4:2; John 18:18, 20; Luke 26:24; John 5:39; 1 Thess. 5:27; 1 Tim. 4:13; 2 Pet.
1:19).

Argument 4. A thing whose every part is necessary is necessary. But every
part of Scripture is necessary, namely the law (Rom. 7:7) and the gospel,
which must be drawn from Scripture much more, because its doctrine is far more
remote from us than the law, and God no longer teaches us by visions, dreams,
revelations, and oracles as formerly, but by the Scriptures. Therefore.

Argument 5. What preserves the purity of doctrine and religion so that it is
not corrupted or destroyed is necessary. Such is Scripture (Hos. 8:12; Luke
1:3 and elsewhere).

Argument 6. Pastors, even those with an extraordinary calling, are commanded
to read the Scriptures diligently (1 Tim. 4:13). Therefore.

Argument 7. If the apostles—and indeed Christ himself after the resurrection,
so that his hearers' faith might be more certain—did not wish them to rest in
his assertion alone, but referred them to the Scriptures, then Scripture is
simply necessary for the church's existence. But the former occurred in Luke
24:26–27 and Acts 26:22. Therefore.

Argument 8. Definite doctrine and judgment in controversies are simply
necessary for the church. But these cannot be given without Scripture.
Therefore.

Argument 9. If a ruler's command is not believed when alleged even by leading
men unless it is proved by the ruler's written document,

[1620 Tome I | col. 123 | BSB frame 00076]

then much more are God's writings simply necessary in matters concerning the
worship of God, faith, and salvation. Experience teaches the former.
Therefore.

OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. From Adam to Moses, God's church existed in the world, but there
was no divine Scripture before Moses, because he was the first sacred writer,
and Genesis mentions no written doctrine, but only what was handed down (Gen.
18:19). Therefore, religion was preserved without Scripture for two thousand
years, and Scripture is thus not simply necessary.

Response. 1. From Adam to Moses there was scripture, although not the written
word, which was then necessary just as the written word is today. 2. It does
not follow: Scripture or writing was not necessary then; therefore, it is not
necessary now. The church had to be governed differently in its different
ages, and in the government of the church one must argue not from what God can
do, but from what he wills. Before Moses, he willed the church to be without
Scripture; afterward, he willed the church to depend upon the Scriptures. The
Scriptures are therefore now necessary, not by absolute necessity, but on the
supposition of God's will. 3. Augustine, in book 15 of *The City of God*,
asserts that certain things were written by Enoch before Moses. There were
also other means, such as sacrifices, types, and ceremonies, by which religion
was propagated, as is evident from history. Chrysostom rightly says in homily
1 on Matthew that writing was not so necessary for the patriarchs because
their minds were purer. Ecclesiastical history before and after the flood
nevertheless teaches that the purity of religion can be preserved only with
the greatest difficulty by unwritten tradition. Finally, the tradition the
patriarchs used was truly divine, whereas the papists' traditions are
doctrines of demons.

[1620 Tome I | col. 124 | BSB frame 00076]

Objection 2. From Moses to Christ, for another two thousand years the
Scriptures indeed existed, but they belonged only to the Jews. The remaining
nations among whom true religion and faith existed in some, such as Job and
his friends, had only unwritten tradition. Therefore.

Response. 1. This does not establish against us that Scripture is not
necessary upon the supposition of God's will. 2. The antecedent is denied,
because the Scriptures were also read by Gentiles, as is evident from the
history of Ptolemy Philadelphus and from the eunuch in Acts 8. We also read
that there were many proselytes who could communicate Scripture to various
nations. 3. Job and his friends lived before Moses' time and therefore before
the giving of the law, around the time of Jacob, according to Athanasius in
his *Synopsis of Holy Scripture* and Augustine in book 18, chapter 47 of *The
City of God*. Finally, it does not follow that because some lived without
Scripture even after Moses' time, it is therefore not necessary now.

Objection 3. The Hebrews used tradition more than Scripture, as in Exodus
13:8; Deuteronomy 32:7; Job 8:8; Judges 6:17; and Psalms 44:1; 62:6; 78:3–4.

Response. The antecedent is denied, and the cited passages do not prove it.
Parents are indeed commanded to relate God's marvelous works to their
children, but these works are contained in the Scriptures. The argument must
therefore instead be reversed, and we must conclude that this narration was a
written tradition.

Objection 4. The reasons the Jews used tradition more than Scripture were,
first, that the Scriptures were not reduced to the form of books until Ezra's
time; and second, that the priests were often so negligent that Scripture was
frequently not found (2 Kings 22). Therefore.

Response.

[1620 Tome I | col. 125 | BSB frame 00077]

This is a fallacy of accident. It does not follow that the Scriptures are not
necessary because Scripture's outward form was not then such as it is today,
because the priests were negligent, or because during persecutions there were
few and scattered copies. Furthermore, the sacred books were never lost at
any time, as is evident from the passage cited by Bellarmine. The copy given
by Moses in Deuteronomy 31:25 was found in Joshua's time. 3. As some think,
Ezra only put the books of the law in order. The final clause of Deuteronomy
which Bellarmine discusses was added either by Joshua or another prophet.

Objection 5. For many years after Christ's advent, the church existed without
the Scriptures. Therefore, Scripture is not necessary.

Response. The inference is denied, because many things formerly were not
necessary which are necessary now. Furthermore, the antecedent is false, for
that church had the Old Testament and the apostles, who taught by living voice
what they afterward wrote. According to Theophylact on Matthew 1, Matthew
wrote his Gospel in the eighth year after Christ. Finally, what Bellarmine
cites from Irenaeus is alleged most ineptly, because Irenaeus speaks only of
certain nations which for a time did not have the Scriptures, not even the Old
Testament.
