10. Likewise, *hallowed be your name; your kingdom come; your will be done,*
and so on. The Remonstrants answer in the same place, fol. 145: “It is absurd
that Christ commanded the faithful to pray to the Father that he determine
not only our will but the will of absolutely every man to sanctify his name,
when Christ knew God's counsel concerning the reprobation of the greatest part
of the human race;

[1651 logical printed p. 380 | IA leaf 398 | PDF p. 398]

for the faithful know that God does not will this, and consequently that they
will never be heard.” I answer: 1. Our teachers do not teach us to pray that
God determine the wills of each and every man to sanctify God's name, to the
prejudice of the absolute dominion of God's decree, election, and reprobation
concerning salvation. We pray only that God determine the wills of each and
every man insofar as he himself wills, leaving him such power over creatures
as the potter has over clay—a power which the Remonstrants are neither ashamed
nor afraid, with sacrilegious boldness, to take away from God. 2. This
consequence is invalid: Christ knew the counsel of reprobation; therefore he
could not command the faithful to pray that God determine everyone's will.
For we may pray for things which conflict with God's decree. A godly son can
pray for the salvation of a father whom God reprobated from eternity. Our
wills are bound to be conformed to God's revealed will, not everywhere adapted
to his secret will. Indeed, every believer can rightly pray that God not
permit him to be tempted or to sin, from the first moment of his conversion to
the final point of his life, while knowing for certain that this conflicts
with God's decree by which he resolved absolutely to permit the falls and sins
of the regenerate. Indeed, Peter and the disciples, who denied and abandoned
Christ during his passion, were bound to believe God's prediction, and
therefore the decree by which he resolved to permit the sheep to be scattered
and scandalized in Christ, as he himself had predicted

[1651 logical printed p. 381 | IA leaf 399 | PDF p. 399]

in Matthew 26:31 from Zechariah's prophecy, chapter 31, verse 7. Yet they were
bound absolutely to will and pray that God would not permit their wills to
fall into so great and scandalous a sin. The same is certain concerning Judas
the betrayer and the Jews who crucified the Lord, supposing that it had been
revealed to them that they would be the authors of so horrendous a sin against
the Savior. Therefore a man can rightly and without sin will absolutely what
he knows for certain to conflict ἐν διαμέτρῳ with God's absolute decree. Nor
would the prayer be foolish, but godly and lawful. In that case they could
also believe with certain faith that they would be heard insofar as it would
conduce to God's glory, to their humiliation, and therefore to the advancement
of their salvation. They will never escape here by any sophistical trick. If
they deny that Peter's sin was decreed by God, what advantage will their cause
then have? The permission had been both predicted and decreed by an effective
decree of God. For the permission of sin is not sin, but a holy and lawful
thing, properly willed by God, even in Arminius's judgment. Yet Peter was bound
to will against that permission and to pray that God not permit him. Thus in
Peter there will be a godly and holy will contrary to God's effective decree.
Let the sophists therefore see how they can extricate themselves. 3. Christ
knew by middle knowledge that the reprobate would never believe, antecedently
both to middle knowledge and to God's decree. For according to the principles
of the Arminians and Jesuits, the future occurrence of all free acts precedes
both middle knowledge and God's decree.

[1651 logical printed p. 382 | IA leaf 400 | PDF p. 400]

Therefore Christ could not command the faithful to pray that God morally
incline either the wills of the reprobate in the visible church or their own
wills to sanctify God's name and do God's will. For how would anyone pray that
God do what had a future occurrence before every act and knowledge of God's
will? Do the Arminians not attribute truly foolish prayers to the saints by
Christ's command? I appeal to their own conscience. 4. This prayer, in which
we ask that men on earth do God's will promptly and cheerfully as the angels
do in heaven—as the Palatine Catechism, Lord's Day 49, question 124 has
it—proves most invincibly that men's wills are undeviatingly bent to
obedience. For if we ask God by moral persuasion to grant that we do God's
will, then we ask no more that God grant us promptly to obey than not to obey
at all. But who would think that God so bends the wills of the angels to
obedience that they can fail to obey God? 10. Psalm 86:11: *Unite my heart to
fear your name.* To unite the heart to God includes in its concept an actual
adherence of the heart to God, unless they should say that “unite my heart to
you” also means “do not unite my heart to you”; that is, “unite or do not
unite”—which would be monstrous commentary. Psalm 5:8: *Lead me, Jehovah, in
your justice.* Psalm 25:4–5: *Lead me in your truth and teach me.* Psalm
119:18: *Open my eyes.* 27: *Cause me to understand the way of your precepts.*
29: *Remove the way of falsehood*

[1651 logical printed p. 383 | IA leaf 401 | PDF p. 401]

*from me, and graciously grant me your law.* Verse 35: *Cause me to walk in
the path of your precepts.* 37: *Turn my eyes away, lest I look upon vanity.*
Verse 133: *Establish my feet in your word.* Would that the Remonstrants would
say sincerely what David asks in these petitions. Only the power to obey? But
so many very clear texts cry out against this. For he asks for the actual
opening of the eyes, actual leading in the way, and walking in the path of
God's commands. But if he asks for actual obedience, God cannot provide it
undeviatingly except by physical determination. The Remonstrants say: “The aid
that is asked, therefore, is not a free power of doing well, nor indeed even
the act of faith itself, but everything which can be done to animate,
stimulate, and strengthen the will of him who already believes, so that he may
have the weightiest reasons why he should will to persevere in the act of
faith with which he has now been imbued, against every allurement and
enticement of the world and the flesh.”

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, fol. 145, chapter 14.]

I answer: 1. They deny that the power to do well is asked, but say that
whatever animates, stimulates, and strengthens the will is asked. Yet what is
this? Either it stimulates and strengthens the power, or it assists the act.
But these are persuasive arguments by which the will has the weightiest
reasons why it should will to persevere. This is nothing other than reasons by
which it is able to persevere. Thus there is a plain contradiction here: a
free power to do well is not asked, yet the power to do well is asked. If
something by which the will is strengthened is asked, what is it? Moral
persuasion does not physically strengthen the will

[1651 logical printed p. 384 | IA leaf 402 | PDF p. 402]

by adding inward strength, according to their doctrine. For in that case a
new and greater power, physically and really effecting and strengthening the
will, would be added to those who pray earnestly, and therefore the power to
do well would still be asked. But the strengthening of the will by persuasive
arguments is a fiction. For then someone who addressed a man bound with iron
shackles by the strongest and sweetest arguments would strengthen the bound
man and add powers by which to extricate himself from the chains. What could
be more absurd? 3. They deny that the saints ask for a free power of doing
well. If they mean the power of nature, as they seem to insinuate by adding
the word “free,” they are playing games. For that power was implanted by the
Creator. But if they mean the power of grace, what sane person would deny that
the saints ask God for strength and power to obey him? Paul asks and prays for
this in Ephesians 3:18: *that they may be mightily strengthened by the Holy
Spirit in the inner man, so that they may be able to comprehend with all the
saints what that breadth is,* and so on. Does he not ask God here for the
spiritual power and strength to comprehend the greatness of God's love in
Christ? Or do they rather wish the Ephesians to attain this by the powers of
nature? They must say one or the other. 1 Peter 5:10; Hebrews 13:20–21; 2
Corinthians 12:9: *My grace is sufficient for you.* Who denies that this grace
is the power to bear that burden? I ask whether the saints request the Holy
Spirit, grace, and an increase of grace by their prayers. But as for grace
including the power to obey and adhere to God in temptations,

[1651 logical printed p. 385 | IA leaf 403 | PDF p. 403]

if anyone denies it, he does not deserve the name of theologian. 4. They deny
that the saints ask God for the act of faith, but what then do the disciples
ask when they say, *Lord, increase our faith*? 5. Why do they deny that the
act of faith is asked rather than the acts of love, hope, and repentance?
Because for them faith is the condition of election, and they wish it to be a
condition demanded by God and effected by God through grace which is
undeviatingly assisted. But by law and gospel God demands from us all free
acts of obedience, not only faith; and yet he himself works in us both to will
and to accomplish in all acts of obedience, and especially faith. 6. They
could not contradict Scripture more explicitly than by denying that the
saints ask God for the act of obedience. *Cause me to understand the way of
your commands* means either “cause me to understand” or “cause it so that I am
able to understand.” *Cause me to walk in the path of your commands* means
either “cause me actually to obey” or “cause me to be able to obey.” But they
deny that the power to obey is asked; therefore the act is asked. Yet it is an
enormous license to explain it thus: “Give moral arguments by which I can be
moved to walk in the path of your commands or not be moved.” Who would believe
either that the saints pray not to obey or that God commands the saints to
pray for this? But if the saints ask for what morally stimulates the will to
obey, they ask either that they obey or that they not obey. For we know that
whatever stimulates their will

[1651 logical printed p. 386 | IA leaf 404 | PDF p. 404]

to obey, they ask either that they obey or that they not obey. For we know
that whatever stimulates their will to obey leaves the will as open to the
non-act as to the act. But
they say that those aids are given from God's intention that they obey, while
their failure to obey is from their own wickedness. But what does this have
to do with the matter? Indeed, they deny that by any aids God absolutely and
precisely intends our obedience. He intends obedience conditionally, or, if it
does not occur, intends by his consequent will to punish us. But I greatly
desire to know what is asked by these words: *Remove the way of falsehood;
open my eyes,* and so on, and similar expressions by which we ask God to remove
the very darkness of the mind, the wickedness of the heart, and the hardness
by which it comes about that we are able not to obey God. For here it is prayed
that God's action itself really and physically terminate upon the very
wickedness and perversity. Does God remove wickedness on the hypothesis that
we do not place an obstacle? But placing an obstacle before God as he removes
wickedness is itself the greatest wickedness. Therefore we ask God actually
to bring it about that we do not place an obstacle, and actually not to place
an obstacle is actually to obey God. Otherwise we do not ask for the opening
of the eyes and the removal of the way of falsehood.

11. Solomon says that the will is determined in absolutely every act, both of
nature and of grace, in Proverbs 24:1: *The king's heart is in Jehovah's hand;
like streams of water, he turns it wherever he wills.* The word נטה means to
direct, incline, and bend, and signifies an act of divine omnipotence (Exodus
15:12; Zechariah 1:12; Psalm 116:2). Will is joined to omnipotence; therefore

[1651 logical printed p. 387 | IA leaf 405 | PDF p. 405]

Augustine explains it thus: “By a most hidden and effectual power God turns
the king's heart from indignation to gentleness; that is, from a will to harm
to the power to show favor.”

[Marginalia: Book against the Two Letters of the Pelagians, chapter 20; *On
the Grace of Christ*, chapter 24.]

If God turns the heart of a king, who appears the freest among mortals,
wherever he wills, much more does he turn the hearts of all men. This one
passage, if there were no others, seems to me most invincibly not only to
prove our assertion but to be our very thesis itself. The adversaries make
many replies here. For 1. Corvinus says: “We do not deny that the hearts of
kings are so in God's hand that he can bend them where he wills, even by
necessitating them (for we freely grant that hearts can be necessitated). We
also admit that hearts are often inclined by God to what he wills. But when
God inclines hearts to something, I deny either that it is done in such a way
that he determines them, at least ordinarily, or that we must hold that it is
done necessarily. He can incline them both by external objects and by inward
aid while nevertheless preserving the complete nature of freedom.”

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 5, section 14, p. 87.]

I answer: 1. He grants that God can extraordinarily necessitate the hearts of
kings, but does not say whether this passage speaks of such necessitation.
That is to evade the blow of the argument, not answer it. For the passage
concerns ordinary providence, by which God directs waters through their
channels and in the same way bends the king's will just as he wills. 2. He
says that God can necessitate the hearts of kings, but this passage says that
he necessitates and bends them wherever he wills. “But,” he says, “through
external objects.” Granted: in whatever manner he bends, it is enough for us
if he so turns wills about wherever he wills. But if only persuasively, it is
not wherever he wills,

[1651 logical printed p. 388 | IA leaf 406 | PDF p. 406]

for he leaves the will open to the act or the non-act. 3. If the king's heart
is in Jehovah's hands because it is subjected to the stroke of moral
persuasion through external objects, then the king's heart is in the devil's
hands. Thus the devil will have his own providence. It is also in Aristotle's
hands, who would prove that the world is eternal; indeed, in the hands of any
heretic, so that by his persuasion the king might believe false and
heterodox things.

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 361.]

2. Grevinchovius answers that the sense is not that God moves freedom by a
predetermining influx, “but that he consequently directs the man who is
already willing toward the end and object which God wills, whether or not the
man wills the same end and object; indeed, sometimes toward an end altogether
contrary to the one the man wills.” I answer: 1. If God does not by a preceding
motion apply the will to not willing rather than willing, but by a collateral
influx merely accompanies the will which is already willing, then the will is
not in God's hands for him to bend it wherever he wills. If the will can
absolutely will either a or b or c, and God intends morally to bend it to
willing c but not a and b, since he has forbidden these volitions by his law,
then it can happen that the will wills a or b and not c (for it can
indifferently and freely will either a, b, or c). God then could not bend the
will wherever he wills, because he does not bend it to the volition τὸ c. And
in this manner the king's will is in the king's own hands, not God's, since
the king bends his own will wherever he wills. 2. When two men pull a ship
with collateral powers, and one

[1651 logical printed p. 389 | IA leaf 407 | PDF p. 407]

wishes to turn the ship toward the east and the other toward the west, it
cannot be said of both turning it simultaneously that each bends the ship
wherever he wills. Now the Arminians and Jesuits say that the king bends his
own will wherever he wills, and the Arminians and Jesuits also grant that God
bends the will wherever he wills. Therefore man's will will always conspire
toward the same object to which it is morally bent by God. But it is always
morally bent by God toward good, never toward evil. Therefore man will never
sin. 3. Grevinchovius means what Bellarmine means: that this does not concern
the internal acts of the will but commanded acts. Therefore, from the
expositions of Mercerus, Tossanus, and Calvin, the wise man means only that
God often prevents kings from carrying out what they will and intend; and
that it did not belong to the evil will of the Jews to direct the Savior's
crucifixion toward this best end, the redemption of the human race, but that
God directed it toward so excellent an end.

[Marginalia: Bellarmine, *On Grace and Free Choice*, book 4, chapter 11.]

But although Mercerus and Tossanus, with Calvin, apply this passage to the
external events of things which are subject to God's special providence, they
nevertheless interpret the passage concerning the elicited acts of the will.
Indeed, the text itself most clearly speaks of the inward volitions of the
human heart, by which it is turned about by God at his pleasure from anger and
hatred to grace and favor, from not willing to willing, just as streams of
water flow through their channels, conducted along this or that course by a
farmer to irrigate fields or gardens.

[1651 logical printed p. 390 | IA leaf 408 | PDF p. 408]

For God draws a good event from evil acts of the will, but then he in no way
acts upon the will or bends it wherever he wills. He only directs external
events beyond the intention of the human will, while the will bends itself
wherever it wills. This would also be to put the hand in place of the heart
and is plainly foreign to the genuine sense of the text. 4. Nor can it be
denied that the Jews were free and contingent causes of the Lord's crucifixion
and therefore, according to the Arminian school, absolutely could have failed
to crucify him. In that case it would not even have been in God's power to
draw out the redemption of the human race. For what event could be drawn from
a non-being? Indeed, the human race would not even have been redeemed through
such a means, and so not even external events will be in God's power. For how
would it be in God's power to draw from Joseph's sale and sending into Egypt
the feeding of the church as it wasted away from famine if the sale and
sending into Egypt were not in God's determination, but in the plainly
indifferent choice of free causes—namely, Joseph's brothers and the
Ishmaelites? Thus it will be in men's power for God to elicit all the good
events which he intends through contingent means. 5. If there is no
determination of the will by God here, then the text would run thus: “Jehovah's
will is in the king's hands; wherever the king wills, he bends both his own
will and Jehovah's.” This is proved as follows: the Jews freely crucify the
Lord of glory. Therefore, notwithstanding any motion of God, they can crucify
him

[1651 logical printed p. 391 | IA leaf 409 | PDF p. 409]

or not crucify him. If they crucify him, they incline God's will to that
general and collateral concurrence, since there could be no action of a second
cause without the influence of the first cause, which is wholly impossible.
If they do not crucify him but, moved by godly reverence for the divine
majesty, abstain, they then draw God's action to that godly not-willing. Thus
from either action, whether not willing or willing, they will draw God's decree
to elicit some good event, lest God's providence and wisdom be idle here.
Bellarmine says in the same place, and the Arminians with him, that God does
not depend here upon the nod of the created will but upon his own free decree,
by which he bound himself always to concur with the second cause. But I answer:
God's dependence upon his own decree does not remove his dependence upon the
nod of the contingent cause, in whose power it is absolutely to act or not act
whatever God's decree and will has resolved to the contrary. Thus, despite a
most unwilling God, human and angelic volitions and nolitions, and all
contingent acts, occur. 3. answer. Gregory of Valentia says that the king's
heart, strengthened by God's most special aid, is bent wherever he wills. But
I reply: 1. If he means the aid of special grace, he contradicts the text,
which concerns God's common providence over the wills of kings and,
synecdochically, of all men. 2. The Jesuits attribute such aid only to those
confirmed in grace. 4. answer. Suarez says that God morally bends the heart
by inspirations. But Suarez, Valentia, and Vasquez posit a congruous motion

[Marginalia: Gregory of Valentia, tome 1, disputation 8, question 3, point 3,
section 1.]

[Marginalia: Suarez, in the *Opuscula*, book *On the Concurrence of God's
Will with Human Freedom*, book 1, chapter 11, figure 3.]

[1651 logical printed p. 392 | IA leaf 410 | PDF p. 410]

of God upon human wills. But, God willing, this fiction will be refuted later.
For congruous motion, as Fonseca observes, includes in its concept the consent
of the one congruously moved. They thus tell us: God moves where he wills the
wills of those whose wills he moves where he wills. 2. It is known that in
congruous motion not willing and willing precede middle knowledge in their
future occurrence. Therefore not willing and willing are indifferently in
free choice and not in Jehovah's hands.

2. Daniel 5:3: *The God in whose hand your soul is and in whose power your
whole way lies, you have not glorified.* The ways and journeys of that
Babylonian tyrant were altogether free acts, so that it was not in his power
to wage war against this nation rather than that one, against the Israelites
rather than the Ammonites—or not to wage war—but in God's power. God also
promises favor among men to those who observe his commands (Proverbs 3:1, 2,
3, 4). But if men determine and bend from hatred to favor, and from favor to
hatred, the person who observes the commands, even when God's most effectual
motion is posited, it will be in men's power that God be true in his promises.
But they say that promises of this kind are conditional, on the hypothesis
that men will it. Yet there is no justice here, no truthfulness of God, if God
should procure favor among the Chaldeans from a special promise for Daniel,
Jeremiah, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego as they earnestly observe the Lord's
commands at the greatest cost to life, property, country, reputation, and
peace, and with extreme danger,

[1651 logical printed p. 393 | IA leaf 411 | PDF p. 411]

on the hypothesis that perhaps the Chaldeans wish to treat them with favor.
But perhaps they are unwilling to treat them without hatred. Were the
worshipers of God defrauded of their reward by the Chaldeans' fault,
especially since they were joined to the Chaldeans neither by blood nor by any
bond of relationship? Will God be just and true here only if the Chaldeans
will it, and will God fail to perform promises to his servants through no
fault of their own?

Proverbs 16:7: *When a man's ways please God, he reconciles his enemies to him
in peace.* Jeremiah 15:11: *Would I not intervene for you in the time of evil
and in the time of distress before the enemy?* Isaiah 14:27: *For Jehovah of
hosts has formed a counsel, and who will make it void? Who will turn away his
outstretched hand?* But if God does not undeviatingly determine the will, then
the Medes, Persians, Macedonians, Parthians, and others who freely devastated
the Babylonians could have failed to devastate them, and thus God's decree
concerning their devastation would not have stood. “Indeed,” the Arminians
say, “your outstretched hand was the sword of the Medes and Persians, which
they could have drawn against other nations and not against the Babylonians.”
The Arminians answer that God decreed that devastation from his foresight of
the hostile minds of the Medes and Persians toward the Babylonians, and that
from this foresight the decree could not be made void. I answer: was that
hostile disposition decreed by God or not? And if decreed, was it absolutely
or conditionally? If conditionally, on what condition? The Arminians will be
silent

[1651 logical printed p. 394 | IA leaf 412 | PDF p. 412]

here. 2 Samuel 15:31: “*Turn, I pray, Jehovah,*” David says, “*Ahithophel's
counsel into foolishness.*” But he knew that Absalom could freely embrace or
reject that counsel. To what purpose, then, does he pray to God if it was
absolutely in Absalom's power to follow or not follow that counsel? He would
certainly have prayed without faith, which is plainly false. Job 12:17: *God
causes counselors to become mad and makes judges fools.* Verse 20: *He removes
speech from the eloquent and takes away the judgment of the aged.* Verse 24:
*He removes the heart of the chiefs of the people of the earth.* Ecclesiastes
9:1: *The righteous and the wise, and their deeds, are in God's hand.*
Jeremiah 10:23: *I know, Jehovah, that a man's way is not in his own power, nor
does it belong to a man to direct his own steps.* Proverbs 16:9: *A man's
heart devises his way, but Jehovah establishes his steps.* Proverbs 20:24:
*There is a multitude of thoughts in man, but the answer of the tongue is from
Jehovah.* James 4:15: *Instead, you ought to say: if the Lord wills and we live,
we shall do this or that.* What is freer than to travel to a city and trade?
Yet this is not a matter of free choice, but of God's will. Will it not be
ready at hand for the Arminians to say: “The merchant travels to the city if
he himself wills; God concurs by a collateral influx. But if the journey does
not please the merchant, God does not concur”? 2 Corinthians 8:16: *But thanks
be to God, who put the same concern for you into Titus.* There are countless
other passages in Scripture which I now refrain from alleging.

Because the arguments and testimonies also

[1651 logical printed p. 395 | IA leaf 413 | PDF p. 413]

prove that the efficacy of divine grace depends not upon free choice but upon
God's actual determination, I shall attach them to the chapter concerning the
efficacy of grace.
