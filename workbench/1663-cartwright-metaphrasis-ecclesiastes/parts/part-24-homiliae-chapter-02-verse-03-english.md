VERSE 3. Of laughter I say, It is insane; and of gladness, what does it
accomplish?

Indeed, he confesses that something more grievous and sorrowful followed this
experience of pleasure than what followed the preceding attempts. He confirms
this from the madness accompanying laughter—laughter produced, that is, by the
enjoyment of all good things and existing as the greatest and most fruitful
offspring of pleasures—when the mind is flooded with so great an amount of joy
that it cannot contain itself within but bursts into laughter. Likewise, he
confirms it from the accompanying uselessness of gladness, which begot laughter
and is its cause. To our pleasure seekers, who have given themselves to
pleasure, Solomon himself would be judged insane, since he charges laughter
with insanity and gladness with uselessness. Yet the judgment of Solomon—or
rather of the Holy Spirit—stands: that the pursuit of rejoicing and laughing is
sheer madness; that pleasure seekers are mad and have passed beyond the control
of their minds; and that those who consume their lives in pursuing pleasures
oppose not only the Holy Spirit but even the light of nature and reason. For it
seems more beastlike than human to adapt oneself to what is at hand and present
only insofar as one is moved by sense; but it is most fitting to human nature to
desire something, to hear, to see, to learn, and to regard knowledge of hidden
and marvelous things as necessary for seeing well and happily. Next, what
madness is it to spend zeal, labor, and industry in acquiring those things when
you are uncertain whether you will enjoy them or even so much as taste them?
What of the fact that the greatest and most ample joys often end in the greatest
sorrows, so that joy and sorrow may be regarded in a certain manner as twins?
What of the fact that some, carried away by excessive joy, have breathed out
their last breath? To this is added that for one who is subject to so many
miseries, burdened with sins, and surrounded by so many enemies to give himself
over to gladness is not far from insanity. Finally, immoderate pleasure has
rendered men witless and mad, since it weakens the keenness of reason and
intellect.

He affirms so indubitably that it has no usefulness, as though this were beyond
dispute. To this belongs the fact that he presents it as a question, challenging
all—even the most expert craftsmen of pleasure—to bring forward anything that
might prove the contrary. And certainly, once joy is ended and brought to
completion, what remains? In other pursuits, such as wisdom and knowledge, some
fruit remains, which can be useful both to the one who devoted effort and to
others. This should not be understood as though laughing were insanity, or as
though joy had no use, provided that it does not exceed moderation; rather,
devoting zeal and spending labor upon it cannot be free from the gravest
censure. For here we must remember what the Apostle teaches in 1 Corinthians
7:29–31. If [the forms] of honorable and lawful pleasure drive a mind addicted
to them into madness, what must be thought of shameful and dishonorable
pleasures?

*Translator's note:* The edition prints `videndum` in `ad bene beateque
videndum`, “for seeing well and happily.” The expected idiom would likely have
`vivendum`, “for living well and happily.” The Latin transcription and English
translation retain the printed reading rather than silently emending it.

*Translator's note:* In the final sentence the edition prints the genitive
singular `honestæ & licitæ voluptatis` alongside the plural pronoun `illis` and
verb `adigunt`. The translation supplies “[the forms]” as the implied plural
subject without changing the Latin.
