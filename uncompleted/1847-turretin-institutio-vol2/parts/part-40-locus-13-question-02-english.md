QUESTION II.

*Is Jesus of Nazareth the true Messiah? We affirm against the Jews.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. Since it was proved in the preceding question that the Messiah has already
come, we must now inquire further into his person so that we may know who he
is. We will demonstrate that he can be none other than Jesus of Nazareth,
Mary’s son. Since this is the chief basis of our religion and the support of
faith, this argument must be weighed with all the greater care, so that the
mouths of the Jews—the fiercest enemies of the Christian name, who strive
with great effort to attack this saving truth—may be stopped.

II. The question is not whether the Messiah has come, which was proved before,
but whether

[Printed p. 255 | Internet Archive PDF 283 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Jesus of Nazareth, whom we believe and proclaim to be the Messiah, is that
true and proper Messiah promised by God who was to come for the salvation of
human beings. The Jews stubbornly deny it, but we steadfastly affirm and
invincibly demonstrate it.

[Marginal heading: Jesus of Nazareth is proved to be the Messiah from his
criteria.]

III. First, all the Messiah’s κριτήρια, *criteria*, by which God willed to
designate him in the Old Testament for the confirmation of our faith pertain
to him and have been fulfilled in him—both those which concern his origin and
those which concern his person and state, or his doctrine and works. They
either fit no one at all or fit him.

[Marginal heading: The first demonstration, from Jesus’ origin:]

As for his *origin*, whether we consider the *time* of his birth and its
*forerunner*, or its *place*, or his *tribe and family*, or its *manner*, it is
agreed that all converged upon him and attained their fulfillment. This can
easily be shown part by part—not from each taken separately, in which case
they would prove nothing, but from all taken together, which makes a solid
argument.

[Marginal heading: 1. With respect to the time;]

IV. 1. With respect to the *time*, the Messiah had to be born when royal and
legislative power was to be taken away from the Jews, after seventy weeks of
years, that is, after 490 years had elapsed from the Babylonian captivity; and
when the house of David was to be held in the utmost contempt and, like a
trunk, cut down even to the root (Isa. 11:1). It has already been proved above
that our Jesus was presented at that time, and the fact itself speaks.

[Marginal heading: 2. With respect to the forerunner;]

V. 2. With respect to the *forerunner*: according to the prophetic oracles, a
forerunner had to precede the Messiah’s advent to prepare his way (Isa. 40:3;
Mal. 3:1; 4:5): “Behold, I send you Elijah the prophet before the great and
terrible day of Jehovah comes.” This appears to have been fulfilled in John
the Baptist, who was the voice of one crying in the wilderness. He preceded
Christ in Elijah’s spirit to prepare his way (Matt. 3:3; 11:14; Luke 1:17),
not only by preaching repentance but also by giving an illustrious testimony
to Christ and honoring him when present with a divine commendation. In this
sense he is called the greatest of all the prophets and the first of the
evangelists (Matt. 11:9, 11). When his ministry was completed, he yielded to
Christ and closed the Old Testament (Matt. 11:13).

[Marginal heading: 3. With respect to the place;]

VI. 3. With respect to the *place*, the Messiah had to be born in *Bethlehem*
according to Micah’s oracle in chapter 5:2: “And you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, are
not the least among the thousands of Judah; from you will come forth for me a
ruler in Israel, and his goings forth are from the age.” The Chaldean
Paraphrase, R. S. Jarchi, and Kimchi acknowledge that this concerns the
Messiah, as do the Pharisees and scribes who answered Herod when he asked
about this (Matt. 2). Yet no one is ignorant that Jesus was born in Bethlehem.

Lest anyone suppose that this happened by chance or by the contrivance and
concerted or deliberate plan of Joseph or Mary, God willed to arrange the
matter by his singular providence to establish confidence in his prediction.
Not only were Jesus’ parents ignorant of and not thinking about it, but they
were nearly unwilling and desired the contrary. For no other end did God
rouse Caesar Augustus—when the empire had already been pacified and he enjoyed
deep peace everywhere, thinking of nothing less than fulfilling a prediction
concerning the Messiah—to conduct a registration of the whole Jewish world
than that this oracle might be fulfilled. Thus it would be established by an
indubitable argument that Joseph belonged to David’s family, since his family
had fallen into obscurity and had now been reduced to the lowest condition of
life, displaying nothing of a royal pedigree.

In vain, therefore, does Menasseh in *On the Council*, book 1, depart from the
ancients with the more recent Jews and maintain that “Bethlehem is called the
Messiah’s native land because he was descended from David the Bethlehemite,
so that the Messiah’s birth is indicated not as actual but as original.” The
prophecy speaks of the future arising of the

[Printed p. 256 | Internet Archive PDF 284 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

ruler at Bethlehem, which cannot without absurdity be referred to David’s
birth, already long past. Nor does what is added about his goings forth from
the age prove this interpretation, because it pertains to the Messiah’s
eternal generation from the Father’s bosom, by which he is the Son of God,
just as his birth at Bethlehem indicates his temporal origin from his
mother’s womb.

[Marginal heading: 4. With respect to the tribe and family;]

VII. 4. With respect to the *tribe and family*, according to the oracles the
Messiah had to arise from the tribe of Judah and the family of David (Gen.
49:10; Isa. 11:1; 2 Sam. 7:13, 14), as the crowds also confessed (John 7:42).
Hence the prophets call him *David* more than once. It is agreed that this was
fulfilled in our Jesus, both from the already mentioned place of his birth,
which was the house of David, where Jesus’ parents had to go to prove that
they were descended from that family, and from both genealogies of Christ
found in Matthew and Luke.

It cannot be objected that Joseph’s ancestry does not prove that Jesus arose
from David’s family, since on our hypothesis Joseph was not his father.
According to the law in Num. 36:8, wives had to be taken from the same tribe
and family. Although this rule was not absolutely universal, it had to apply
where the virgins were ἐπίκληροι, *heiresses of their paternal house*, as Mary
was. Nor is it an obstacle that Mary is called the συγγενὴς, *kinswoman*, of
Elizabeth in Luke 1. This could have resulted either because one of Mary’s
ancestors had married a woman from the tribe of Levi or because some priest
had married a daughter from Mary’s kindred.

[Marginal heading: 5. With respect to the manner of the birth.]

VIII. 5. With respect to the *manner of the birth*, according to Isaiah’s
prediction in chapter 7:14 he had to be born of a virgin: “Behold, a virgin
will conceive and bear a son, and she will call his name Immanuel.” This is of
such weight that by itself it suffices to prove this mystery. Yet to establish
it against the cavils of the Jews, we must observe the prophet’s purpose. It
is to strengthen the people against the fear which overwhelmed them because
Rezin and Pekah, kings of Syria and Israel, were besieging Jerusalem.
Therefore God commands the prophet to go with his son to the king and to tell
him to put away fear and be certain of the preservation and liberation of
himself and his people.

To persuade him all the more of the truth of this promise, the prophet gives
him the choice of asking for any sign, either from heaven or from earth. But
when Ahaz impiously refused a sign, and many remnants of the faithful still
remained, the prophet adds for their consolation and confirmation, as well as
for Ahaz’s conviction, that despite the king’s impiety God would give them a
twofold sign, future and present. The *future* sign in verse 14 was in
Immanuel, who in his time would be born of a virgin. The *present* sign was in
his own son Shear-jashub, before whose adolescence those kings were to be
destroyed (verse 16).

IX. It is clear that the oracle cannot be understood of anyone other than the
Messiah, because: 1. it concerns a child who will be Immanuel and the Lord of
the land (verse 14; chapter 8:8); 2. it concerns one who would be born of a
virgin; and 3. it concerns one who would be an unheard-of and wonderful sign.

This cannot apply to Hezekiah, Ahaz’s son, because he had already been born
and was not yet to be born. Furthermore, his birth was not from the העלמה,
*virgin*, but from Ahaz’s wife, nor is it called miraculous or ever elsewhere
presented as a peculiar sign. Nor can it apply to the prophet’s son
Shear-jashub or to the other one mentioned in chapter 8, called Maher-shalal-
hash-baz. The former had already been born, since Isaiah brought him to the
king, and his birth was neither miraculous nor from a virgin, although the
imposition of his name was symbolic. The latter is distinguished from
Immanuel in chapter 8:8 and was conceived not from the העלמה, *virgin*, but
from the הנביאה, *prophetess*. Nor can it apply to some other ordinary child,
because this is not ordinary speech and ordinary things are not said about
him. It is the promise of an illustrious sign to be presented not in a woman
but in a virgin. Hence

[Printed p. 257 | Internet Archive PDF 285 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

it follows that this oracle could refer to no other and was fulfilled in none
other than Jesus of Nazareth.

X. Although the sign given here by the prophet was to be presented only many
ages later, it could nevertheless serve to confirm the promised liberation.
1. It is neither absurd nor unusual to present future things as a sign of
something which will happen in the near future, as is clear from Exod. 3:12; 1
Sam. 2:34; 1 Kings 22:25, 28; and Jer. 44:29. Consequent signs are given there,
just as antecedent signs are, and the events of prophecies are presented as
signs in Deut. 18:21 and following. 2. It is not unusual in Scripture to build
confidence in lesser promises by recalling great ones, especially where
those greater promises cannot stand without confidence in the lesser. Such
is the reasoning of the present passage.

Since the Messiah had once been required to arise from David’s royal stock,
and this while that commonwealth still stood, it is clear that the land Ahaz
so greatly feared for could not be surrendered entirely into the enemies’
power and the Davidic house could not be wholly rooted out. Hence an argument
can be made from the similar or from the lesser to the greater: God will
certainly fulfill the principal promise concerning the Messiah; therefore he
will much more grant the lesser thing, namely, liberation from the kings. 3.
It is the prophets’ custom to confirm all particular promises by the general
promise concerning the Messiah, because it is the foundation of the others,
in whom all the promises are Yes and Amen (2 Cor. 1:20).

4. The promise of the approaching liberation is founded not only on the
future sign, namely, the Messiah to be born of a virgin many ages later, but
also on the present sign, namely, the prophet’s son who had already been born
and whom he had brought with him to the king. Verse 16 properly refers to him:
“Before this boy”—δεικτικῶς, *pointing him out*—“knows to refuse the evil and
choose the good,” that is, reaches the years of discretion, “the land will be
liberated.” Indeed, scarcely four years passed before those kings were
destroyed. Or it refers to the son who would be born a little later, of whom
chapter 8:3–4 speaks.

XI. Although Jesus of Nazareth is never recorded as having been called
Immanuel, it can nevertheless be said that he was to be so called. The phrase
τὸ קרא, *to call*, is often put for *to be* in Scripture, as when he is said to
be called “Wonderful, Counselor,” and so forth in Isa. 9, and “Jehovah our
righteousness” in Jer. 23. This does not so much indicate the name by which he
would be distinguished as what he would actually be, whether with respect to
his person as θεάνθρωπος, *God-man*, or with respect to his office as
Mediator, who would reconcile God to us. Hence Chrysostom says well on this
passage: “It is Scripture’s custom to put things which happen in place of
names. Therefore ‘They will call him Immanuel’ means nothing other than ‘They
will see God with human beings.’”

If a certain *ignorance* is attributed to Immanuel, it is an ignorance of pure
negation, which could occur in Jesus according to Luke 2:40, not an ignorance
of *evil disposition*, which is sin and from which he was always free. What is
added about eating butter and honey serves to indicate the customary
upbringing of others through the use of ordinary foods, which are included
under butter and honey because the land of Canaan flowed with milk and honey.

XII. It is clear that עלמה here denotes a *virgin*, not just any woman. 1. This
follows from the word’s derivation from עלם, *to hide*, by which is denoted a
virgin’s condition as hidden from the sight and company of men, as though she
were עלומה, *hidden*, and not known by a man. Hence among the ancients virgins
were called κατάκλειστοι and ἀπόκρυφοι, *shut away and hidden*, in 2 Macc. 3:19
because they remained hidden and guarded at home. 2. This is supported by the
authority of interpreters who cannot be suspect to the Jews—the Targumim,
Onkelos and Jonathan, and the Septuagint, which renders it παρθένος,
*virgin*—although Symmachus and Theodotion render it *young woman* to gratify
the Jews. 3. Evident reason requires it, because the passage concerns a divine
and wonderful sign whose origin is no less wonderful than its name. But what
sort of sign is it—what is natural, common, and ordinary—that a woman should
conceive and give birth through intercourse with a man? 4. The ordinary use

[Printed p. 258 | Internet Archive PDF 286 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of the word in Scripture supports it in the six other passages in which it
occurs: Gen. 24:43; Song 1:3; 6:8; Exod. 2:8; Ps. 68:25; and Prov. 30:19. In
the last passage, the Jews vainly contend that it designates a *prostitute*,
because immediately after it says that *the way of a man בעלמה, with a maiden,
is hidden*, it adds, “Such is the way of an adulterous woman.” The use of this
note of similitude shows that a likeness is indicated, not identity. The sense
is that a man’s way to a hidden maiden is concealed, namely, the arts by which
men seek to win virgins for themselves, whether for honorable marriage or for
corruption. She can also be called a virgin *antecedently*, because she was
such before but not afterward, or δοξαστικῶς and συγχωρητικῶς, *according to
opinion and concession*, whether because she was held to be such or wished to
be held such. This cannot apply here.

XIII. Not only does the gospel history testify that Mary, who bore our Jesus,
was a virgin, but common report itself declares it. For if this were not true,
how could the Virgin have lied about so great and incredible a thing? How
could the apostles, who could easily have been convicted of falsehood, have
dared to propose it so boldly with no one protesting? But if she was caught in
adultery, as some more recent Jews blasphemously claim, why was this never
reproached against her by the Pharisees and scribes? Why was a suit not
brought against her under the law and punishment decreed, so that her son’s
reputation might be pierced through his mother’s side? Instead she survived
her son, with no one making inquiry against her life.

[Marginal heading: The second demonstration, from the criteria of the
Messiah’s person and state;]

XIV. The second demonstration is drawn from the *criteria of the person and
state*. 1. It is certainly inferred that the Messiah would be God, not only
from the oracles which give him this name but also from the divine works he
had to perform to execute the work of redemption. But it is indubitably
proved that Jesus, Mary’s son, was both the Son of God and God, both from the
various divine works he performed, as will be shown below, and from Christ’s
own testimony, for he so often proclaimed himself the Son of God.

If he had falsely claimed this for himself, are we to suppose that God, the
most severe avenger of his own glory, would have allowed his glory to be given
to another? Would he not have avenged so great a crime, than which none could
be greater, with some severe punishment? If an angel was punished so severely
for aspiring to equality with God, what would have happened to that usurper
and robber of the very nature of divinity? Yet not only was Jesus not punished
by God, but he was crowned with the highest glory and majesty. Within a short
time he filled the whole world with his doctrine and established a most
powerful empire.

XV. 2. The Messiah is described as one who would be equipped with a threefold
office, for which reason he is said to be anointed in Dan. 9:24: the prophetic
office from Deut. 18:15 and Isa. 61:1–2; the priestly from Ps. 110; and the
royal from Ps. 2:6; 45:6–7; and Zech. 6:12–13. We see this fulfilled in our
Jesus, who discharged the office of prophet in the proclamation of the
gospel, of priest in the offering of himself, and of king in establishing the
heavenly kingdom, as will be proved in its place.

XVI. 3. A *twofold state* is assigned to him: first, the state of humiliation
and suffering, in which he had to die; second, the state of exaltation and
glory, in which he had to rise again and ascend into heaven, as is evident
from Pss. 22 and 110; Zech. 9:9; and Isa. 53. The gospel history shows that
this very thing is observed in Jesus of Nazareth, for it describes his
sufferings and glory: the state of emptying, in which he humbled himself even
to the death of the cross, and the state of exaltation, in which, raised from
the dead, he obtained the name above every name and sat at the right hand of
the Majesty on high (Phil. 2:6–9; Heb. 1:3). This can be shown more fully with
respect to all the circumstances of the sufferings and glory predicted of the
Messiah in the Old Testament, which are found accurately fulfilled in Jesus
under the New

[Printed p. 259 | Internet Archive PDF 287 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Testament.

[Marginal heading: The third demonstration, from Christ’s doctrine and his
works;]

XVII. The third demonstration is taken from *Christ’s doctrine and works*.
According to the prophetic oracles, the Messiah would proclaim the year of
good pleasure and bring the saving doctrine of the gospel to heal the
brokenhearted and proclaim liberty to captives and redemption. Who can
consider the doctrine preached by our Jesus and fail to see that he fulfilled
this most completely in himself? He brought us the gospel from heaven, which
everywhere displays the brightest shining rays of divinity. In it he revealed
to us God’s most perfect will, both in its precepts and its promises, and
instituted a religion purer and more divine than any which ever existed. It
breathes nothing but piety and love and is ordered to God’s glory and the
salvation of human beings.

XVIII. His works were also so many and so great that none more illustrious,
more numerous, or greater could have been desired or expected from the
Messiah, as the crowd itself confessed in John 7:31: “When Christ comes, will
he perform more signs than this man has performed?” Hence Jesus so often
appeals to his works as the surest sign of his calling (John 4:36; 7:37–38;
10:37; 15:24). Indeed, when John asked through his disciples whether he was
the Messiah ὁ ἐρχόμενος, *the one who is to come*, he furnished no other proof
that he was the one than the multitude and greatness of his miracles. He
answered by deeds, not words—by the event sooner than by speech (Matt. 11:4–5).

For Isaiah 25 [sic]:4–5 teaches that the Messiah had to be θαυματουργὸν, *a
worker of wonders*, and distinguished by miracles: “God comes and will save us; then
the eyes of the blind will be opened and the ears of the deaf unstopped,” and
so forth. For if the law was glorious with signs and wonders, why not the
gospel (2 Cor. 3:7–8)? And if the Messiah had to be פלא, *wonderful*, in his
person, no less so in his works. The Jews themselves could not deny that
Christ made himself renowned by such works. Josephus in book 18, chapter 14,
calls him παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητήν, *a doer of wonderful works*. Certain
examples of them are related in the Talmud, and even Julian, a mortal enemy of
the Christian name, could not deny them.

His miracles could not have been *more illustrious*, because they were not
performed secretly in a corner and retreat, which deceivers seek, but openly
and publicly, in the light of day, at crossroads and public squares,
everywhere, and before the eyes of his bitterest enemies. They could not have
been *more numerous*, for John says that there were so many that if someone
were to write each one separately, the whole world could not contain those
books (John 21:25). They could not have been *greater in their nature*,
because they were not merely horrific portents and wonders which inspired
terror, as the legal signs were, but saving benefits. Far from shrinking from
them, people flocked in crowds to see and receive them.

They were not *ordinary signs* and wonders which human or diabolical art could
perform, but great and unusual works which could be done only by divine power,
such as raising the dead, healing those born blind and ἀνιάτων, *incurable*,
diseases, and so forth. With respect to their *objects*, they were not
*particular signs*, restricted to a certain species of things, but
*universal*, testifying to his power and dominion over every creature—in
heaven, on earth, in the waters, in demons, in human beings, in loaves, in
trees, and so forth. With respect to their *manner*, he did not need great
effort to perform them but accomplished them by his word and will alone,
sometimes without means, sometimes with means—usually useless or even
contrary—sometimes while present, sometimes from far away, and indeed even
while absent.

XIX. What will the Jews reply to these things? That others also performed
miracles? But who is ever recorded as having performed so many, such great
works, such kinds of works, and in such a manner? That they were only wonders,
not true miracles? But what is a true miracle if not something surpassing the
powers of all finite and created nature, such as raising the dead, healing
incurable and desperate diseases, and driving out

[Printed p. 260 | Internet Archive PDF 288 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

demons, and so forth, which Christ is agreed to have done more than once? That
they were mere tricks by which he deluded and deceived people? But why was
this never objected against him by the Jews, his bitterest enemies, who
seized every opportunity to destroy him? Why did they not expose the impostor
and mountebank?

Who was ever more foreign to the practices of deceivers? They seek hiding
places and seclusion and flee the light so that they may more easily impose on
the simple. But Christ works openly and publicly before innumerable
witnesses—great and small, learned and unlearned—so that no suspicion of fraud
can remain. They pursue empty, ridiculous, and useless things. But Christ does
nothing except what is holy and saving. They act for the sake of sordid gain
and to grasp glory or smoke.* But what resemblance is there to Christ, who
directed everything to the salvation of his own, even willing to become poor
so that we might become rich through his poverty? They are enslaved to the
most shameful passions and sacrifice to the flesh and the demon. But Christ,
the teacher of piety, proposed this one thing to himself: to engender holiness
in our souls.

Did Christ do those miracles by magical power, as the Pharisees objected
against him more than once when they slanderously claimed that he cast out
demons by the power of Beelzebul, prince of demons? But why can the Jews
suspect this more readily of Jesus of Nazareth, who was considered the son of
a craftsman, than of Moses, who was educated in the Egyptian court and could
have been imbued with the most intimate knowledge of magic? Furthermore, who
could even faintly suspect either that any magician could have performed or
any demon would have wished to perform so many works which were pure benefits
bestowed upon the human race? Who could believe that the devil wished to arm
Christ for his own ruin and the destruction of his tyranny?

Nor could this be done through collusion, because friends collude, not
enemies. Yet the demon had no more mortal enemy than our Jesus and his
religion, which was wholly directed to destroying the works of the devil and
inspiring piety and holiness in souls. Finally, if he was a magician, why was
he never charged with and convicted of magic at trial by the senators of the
Sanhedrin, whom the Jewish school asserts were all thoroughly skilled in
magic? Why did they search diligently for charges against him when the charge
of magic alone sufficed for his condemnation under the law (Exod. 22:18)?

XX. But if the works Christ performed in the days of his flesh proclaimed him
the Messiah, the things he accomplished after his resurrection and ascension
into heaven through the sending of the Holy Spirit and the work of
Christianity prove the same no less effectively. First, Christ’s
*resurrection* itself bears clear testimony to him. In it he was approved by
God, as though by a solemn decree, and declared his Son, believed by the
faithful, and acknowledged by his enemies. Hence he presents no more
illustrious and certain argument for his divinity and calling than this:
“Destroy this temple,” he says, speaking of the temple of his body, “and on
the third day I will raise it” (John 2:19). When the Jews ask for a sign in
Matt. 16, he wills to give no other than his resurrection under Jonah’s type.

Indeed, if Jesus had not been sent by God and had not truly been the Lord’s
Christ, as he professed himself to be, who could believe that God, the most
severe avenger of his own glory, would have exerted his power in raising him
to confirm so great a fraud and imposture and would have crowned such great
impiety with so great a reward? Second, there is added the wonderful and
unheard-of *sending of the Holy Spirit*, which occurred at the precise time
and place Christ himself had predicted. The apostles were so filled with him
that they were transformed into new men and said and did things which not
only carried spectators and hearers away into astonishment and wonder but
wonderfully drew them to faith in and obedience to Christ by a most powerful,
soul-moving force. From where could so great a miracle have proceeded except
from God bearing

[Printed p. 261 | Internet Archive PDF 289 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

testimony to his Christ? Thus men of lowly condition, uneducated and ἀγράμματοι,
*unlettered*, seemed to be transformed in a single moment into the most
learned and eloquent men, imbued with knowledge of every language and
acquaintance with the greatest matters. They argued their Lord’s cause
fearlessly before all, and with great παρρησίᾳ and ἐνεργείᾳ, *boldness and
power*. They did so by strongly establishing their religion, not with
disguised and contrived arguments but with true and clear ones which surpassed
every exception, so that people seemed to rush to the gospel in companies and
crowds despite threats, edicts, prisons, tortures, and every kind of extreme
punishment.

[Marginal heading: Confirmed from the calling of the nations and the founding
of Christianity;]

XXI. This became still more clearly evident afterward in the *calling of the
nations and the founding of Christianity* throughout the world. The calling
of the nations was the Messiah’s proper work according to the prophetic
oracles (Gen. 17:5; 49:10; Pss. 2:8; 22:27, 30; 68:31–32; 47:7–9; 110:2–3;
Hos. 1:10–11; Joel 2:28; Isa. 2:2–3; 11:10; 42:1, 4; 49:6; 54:1–2; and
everywhere else). The fact itself proclaims, and the records of history
testify, that this was accomplished through the gospel: within a short time
it was sown far and wide throughout the whole world, nations flowed to Christ
from everywhere, and Caesars themselves and kings laid their crowns and
scepters at his feet. No reason can be given why, from this mark alone, we
should not believe that our Jesus was the true Messiah.

[Marginal heading: Despite various obstacles:]

XXII. For unless Jesus were the true Messiah and his doctrine truly divine,
how could he have been received as the Messiah and faith in his doctrine have
been maintained in the world when so many and such nearly insuperable
obstacles stood in the way? These arose from *Christ* himself, the founder of
the religion; from the *doctrine* which was to be preached; from the *heralds*
themselves; and from the *manner of teaching*.

[Marginal heading: 1. From Christ;]

If *Christ* himself, the Prince and Head of the religion, was considered, he
appeared to be a lowly man, the son, as was supposed, of a craftsman, without
any dignity, name, or rank. Indeed, he was despised by the leaders of his own
people and subjected to reproaches of every kind. Finally, he was taken
prisoner and bound like a slave, scourged and made a victim of the cross. Like
a traitor and one guilty of injuring the divine and human majesty, he was
fixed to an infamous tree. Who could have supposed that God would have wished
to use so lowly and miserable a little man, as he appeared, to perform so
great a work? This was certainly the greatest stumbling block to the Jews and
foolishness to the Greeks, but to the called it was God’s supreme power and
wisdom (1 Cor. 1:18).

[Marginal heading: 2. From the nature of the doctrine;]

XXIII. The *nature of the doctrine* confirms it. It was such that it should
have driven people away rather than attracted them. It not only condemned all
the other contrary religions, which had been received in their native lands
from nearly every age and were ingrained by long usage, and urged that they be
utterly rooted out as empty, false, unworthy of God, and hostile to him. It
also contained precepts most unwelcome and troublesome to the flesh:
self-denial, love of enemies, endurance of evils and even the hardest cross,
and abstinence from all vices and pleasures. It taught mysteries absurd and
impervious to reason, such as that the God of glory and Prince of life was
crucified. If he was God, what could be more absurd? If a man, what could be
more empty?

It offered either small promises or none at all in this age, but rather the
honor of martyrdom and the burden of the cross (Matt. 16:24; John 15:18–19;
Acts 9:16). Who would persuade in this way and marshal troops? Is it credible
that so many nations would agree to such weighty and difficult precepts and
undertake such a mass of labors without any reward or any enticement of
pleasures—indeed, with the most immediate loss of all things dearest to them,
that they could be drawn to calamitous

virtue—unless they had been utterly persuaded of this doctrine’s divinity?
Alexander the Great is admired because he subdued the world with a few
soldiers; Joshua more because he conquered Jericho with trumpets. But who
would not admire Christ above the others, for he conquered the world by
tongues whose voice passed into all the earth (Rom. 10:18), even though they
were naturally no more capable of heavenly speech?

The word was foolishness to human beings, and yet it conquered the wise. It
was horrible and should have driven people away, and yet it won souls and drew
hearts. It possessed no art of persuasion, no embellishments or allurements of
words. Yet it contained “ἀπόδειξις πνεύματος καὶ δυνάμεως, the most powerful
demonstration of the Spirit” (1 Cor. 2:4). We must not suppose that the part
which is called the foolishness of preaching was hidden or painted with other
colors. Rather, it was openly displayed to all, as Paul himself testifies when
he explicitly professes that he determined to know nothing among them except
Jesus crucified.

It was attacked by innumerable enemies, not only by external and foreign ones
but by the carnal man himself, who leaves nothing unattempted to repel it from
himself. Yet it broke through all these obstacles and triumphed over man and
his resistance. Cicero marvels that Romulus, in a still uncultivated age,
attained the status of a god. How much more must we marvel that in a most
learned and cultivated age our Jesus attained by the preaching of the Word
alone that he should be believed to be God, and that for this confession his
disciples were prepared to suffer every evil. Caligula, Nero, and Domitian
received divine honors while alive, but when dead were exposed to a thousand
mockeries. Jesus was considered the most desperate and miserable of mortals
while alive, but after death he obtained divine glory and was exalted above
all kings. What could cause so great a change? Nothing other than persuasion
of the divinity of his doctrine.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the condition of the heralds;]

XXIV. The *condition of the heralds* adds no small weight to this
demonstration. They were few, twelve in number, common, obscure fishermen and
tax collectors. Their family did not make them illustrious, power or wealth
respected, favor beloved, the favor of the great welcome, or learning and
eloquence renowned or fit to attract mortal souls. They lacked all the things
usually required to persuade human beings. Who can believe that such great
things could have been accomplished through such men and that so many
synagogues, academies, companies of philosophers, subtleties of intellect,
tribunals, and scepters would have yielded to them if the one whom they
preached had not been the true Messiah?

A few are sent to innumerable people, the unlearned to the learned, infants to
the most eloquent, the simple to the cunning, the unarmed to the armed, the
poor to the most powerful, and yet they draw everyone after them. Compare with
them all the most celebrated founders of sects who ever lived. They applied
all the power of their intellect to become renowned throughout the whole
world and lacked nothing for propagating their teaching everywhere. Yet who
among them all drew after himself—I do not say so many followers—but even a
thousandth part? Thus God willed to place his treasure in earthen vessels, so
that the excellence of this power would be from God, not from human beings,
and that piety and religion would owe none of their growth to the world but
would depend wholly upon God.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the manner of teaching.]

XXV. Finally, the *manner of teaching* clearly proves the same. It was not
elaborate and ostentatious, adorned with the allurements of speech and
inflated words, and fashioned to deceive. It was simple, unaffected, and
accessible to the unlearned and learned alike, so that every suspicion of
fraud and pretense would be absent. Therefore they used neither force to
compel dissenters, nor deceit to mislead the unwary, nor threats to frighten
the resistant. They desired free faith or none at all. They thought it worthy
of God and Christ to lead not

bodies but souls in triumph. If they had used force, if they had wished to
found religion by arms or legions, slaughter or blood, in the way the impostor
Muhammad established and preserves his tyranny, it would be no wonder if they
had somehow obtained their desire. But for the entire armed world to be
subdued by unarmed and weak men equipped with no power or authority, by
persuasion alone, clearly proves the divinity both of the Author and of the
doctrine by which this was done. To attribute this work to the devil would
amount to wishing the devil to be the author of his own ruin and to conspire
for the destruction of his own kingdom. No sane person would say this.

XXVI. If the *conversion of the world and founding of Christianity* amid so
many intervening obstacles was a sure argument for the truth of the Messiah,
its *preservation and propagation* is no less clear a testimony to the same
truth. Through so many ages it has not only not been destroyed, although Satan
and the world left nothing unattempted to overthrow it completely, but has
been strengthened and increased all the more and has always put down deeper
roots. Yet other religions and sects supported by the authority of princes,
the subtlety of philosophers, and the eloquence of orators were finally
abolished. Who would not judge that this work is not human but divine, and
therefore that its Author was truly what he professed to be, the Lord’s
Christ?

[Marginal heading: The fourth demonstration, from absurdities.]

XXVII. The fourth demonstration is drawn from *absurdities*. 1. If Jesus had
not been the Christ he professed himself to be, his name and doctrine could
never have emerged among the kings and princes who strove to suffocate it.
Instead, his glory would have been buried with the names of the others who
were pseudo-Christs, especially since it was supported by neither worldly
power and authority nor the wisdom of the age. 2. If Jesus was not the Christ,
he was—let the blasphemy be far from the utterance—the most insolent of all
who have lived. He so often asserted things concerning God, himself, and us
which would be utterly false, and even confirmed them with an oath, than which
no greater or more dangerous lie could be given. Yet it is agreed, by the
admission of the Jews, Gentiles, and Muslims themselves, that the highest
purity of life joined with integrity of soul shone in him, and these wholly
remove any suspicion of such a lie.

3. If Jesus was not sent by God, God, the most severe avenger of his own
glory, would either have predicted this through a prophet so that people would
beware of such imposture, refuted him by raising up prophets, or demonstrated
it by the events themselves and the course of time, as Gamaliel said: *If the
work is not from God, it will be destroyed*. Yet in the course of the ages his
fame has not only not been extinguished and erased, but his doctrine has grown
and filled the whole world. It is like water springing from a fountain which,
as streams converge, rises from reaching only the ankles to the knees and
covers the breast and head.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXVIII. When Christ says that he did not come to send peace upon the earth but
a sword (Matt. 10:34), the sense is not that Christ would be the *efficient
cause in itself* of war, but an *accidental cause* because of the world’s
wickedness. Unable to endure the gospel, the world would resist it and stir up
wars against its professors. This does not oppose the peace which according
to the prophetic oracles the Messiah would bring into the world (Isa. 2:4;
11:6–7; Mic. 4:3): 1. because that peace is not worldly but mystical and
heavenly; 2. because it is not with the world of the impious and the serpent’s
seed, with whom there must be ἄσπονδον, *implacable*, war (Gen. 3:15), but
among the faithful, who, laying aside hatreds and enmities, had to be
reconciled in Christ and become one; and 3. because if wars are seen among
Christians, this arises not from Christ’s doctrine but from human vice.

XXIX. The destruction of idolatry predicted for the Messiah’s time did not
have to occur in a single moment, but gradually and by various stages, as we
see also happened. 1. Christ’s religion openly and expressly

condemns all idolatry with the greatest severity. 2. Through it idolatry has
also been completely rooted out in every assembly which truly professes
Christ’s doctrine. 3. It is daily removed from the world through the preaching
of the gospel. If some among Christians still retain it wholly or in part,
they show by that very fact that they are not Christ’s church but
Antichrist’s. This is the greatest obstacle to the conversion of the Jews.

XXX. When John the Baptist sent men to ask Jesus whether he was the coming
Messiah (Matt. 11:2–3), he did not do this because he doubted him. He had been
assured of him in an illustrious manner and had borne such explicit testimony
to him (John 1:29, 32–34). Rather, he did it so that by seeing and hearing
Jesus his disciples might be more fully confirmed. They were not yet
sufficiently persuaded concerning him and perhaps for that reason were too
attached to their own master.

XXXI. So far was it from necessary for Christ to be approved and received by
the synagogue that his rejection was clearly predicted in Ps. 118:22: “The
stone which the builders rejected,” and so forth, and Isa. 53:1: “Who has
believed our report?” Hence Rabbi Judah says in the *Talmud, Tractate
Sanhedrin*: “When the son of David comes, wise men will be rare in Israel, the
wisdom of the scribe will rot, and the schools of the theologians will be
brothels.”

XXXII. If Jesus abrogated the ceremonial law, it does not follow that he was a
violator of the law, but its τελειωτὴν, *perfecter*, and consummator, because he
abrogated it by fulfilling it. Nor for this reason did he oppose the law
itself, because that law had not been given to endure forever but only until
the time of διορθώσεως, *reformation*. Nor did he oppose God’s counsel, for
God had decreed this change from eternity. If ceremonies and sacrifices are
said still to be future under the Messiah, this must be understood mystically
with respect to the internal truth of spiritual and evangelical worship, not
literally with respect to the external figure. This follows from the Holy
Spirit’s style, which retains the old name when the state has changed and
describes evangelical mysteries in legal terms.
