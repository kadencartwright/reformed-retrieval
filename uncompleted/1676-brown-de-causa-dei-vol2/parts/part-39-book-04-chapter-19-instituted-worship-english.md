# CHAPTER XIX

## Certain matters concerning the second precept of the Decalogue are set out beforehand; and it is shown that this precept commands the worship instituted by God

So that what we are going to say about the *fourth precept of the Decalogue*
may become clearer, it will be worthwhile to set out beforehand certain
matters concerning the nature of the *second precept* and to inquire whether
anything positive with respect to God is commanded by this commandment.

Our opponents say that only the worship of images is prohibited by this
second precept, and that its affirmative requirement is to glorify God—that
is, to regard God as incomparable (Isaiah 40:13, 18)—and therefore to worship
him in a spiritual manner conformable to his nature. They deny that instituted
worship pertains to this commandment.

[Printed p. 190 | IA PDF 198 | IA leaf 198 | IA decausadei02brow]

Other orthodox theologians, however, affirm that this precept commands the
pure and uncorrupted observance of the worship prescribed by God and of all
religious ordinances established by God in his Word. They affirm that it
thereby forbids us to worship God through images or in any other manner not
prescribed by God.

Indeed, Dr. Burmann himself openly affirms in *Synopsis*, book 4, chapter 5,
that the Decalogue is a summary of all holiness and virtue. In order for this
to be established, he says that several rules must be observed, of which the
second is this: *Its precepts admit of the broadest exposition; since they
express only the chief heads of matters, they include an immense sum of
things, and all kindred virtues and vices, as well as whatever touches them in
any way, can be referred to them.* This is nothing other than what is commonly
asserted by expositors of the Decalogue: namely, that a synecdoche must be
recognized in every precept of the Decalogue, by which, when one species is
set forth, all the species under the same genus are understood. See Rivet on
the Decalogue and Zanchi on the second precept, who says that all these
commands are very concise synecdoches.

The *Shorter British Catechism* says that the second precept requires us to
receive, observe, and finally preserve pure and entire all worship and every
ordinance of religion which God has established in his Word (Deuteronomy
32:46; Matthew 28:20; Acts 2:42). It also says that the precept forbids us to
worship God through images (Deuteronomy 4:15–19; Exodus 32:5–6) or in any
other manner whatsoever which God has not prescribed in his Word
(Deuteronomy 12:31–32).

But in the *Larger Catechism* all these things are explained more fully. At
question 108, *What are the duties required in the second commandment?*, the
answer is: [The duties required in the second commandment are the receiving,
observing, and keeping pure and entire all such religious worship and
ordinances as God has instituted in his Word (Deuteronomy 32:46–47; Matthew
28:20; Acts 2:42; 1 Timothy 6:13–14), such as prayer and thanksgiving in
the name of Christ (Philippians 4:6; Ephesians 5:20); the reading, preaching,
and hearing of the Word (Deuteronomy 17:18–19; Acts 15:21; 2 Timothy 4:2;
James 1:21–22; Acts 10:33); the administration and reception of the sacraments
(Matthew 28:19; 1 Corinthians 11:2–30); church government and discipline
(Matthew 18:15–17; 16:19; 1 Corinthians 5 throughout; 12:28); the ministry and
its maintenance (Ephesians 4:11–12; 1 Timothy 5:17–18; 1 Corinthians 9:7–15);
religious fasting (Joel 2:12–13; 1 Corinthians 7:5); swearing by the name of
God (Deuteronomy 6:13) and vowing to him (Isaiah 19:21; Psalm 76:11). They also
include disapproving, detesting, and opposing all false worship (Acts
17:16–17; Psalm 16:4), and, according to each person's place and calling,
removing it and all monuments of idolatry (Deuteronomy 7:5; Isaiah 30:22).]

Likewise, the answer to the next question, *What sins are forbidden in the
second commandment?*, is: [The sins forbidden in the second commandment are
all devising of any religious worship not instituted by God himself (Numbers
15:39); promoting it by counsel (Deuteronomy 13:6–8); commanding it (Hosea
5:11; Micah 6:16); practicing it (1 Kings 12:33); or approving it in any way
(Deuteronomy 12:30–32);

[Printed p. 191 | IA PDF 199 | IA leaf 199 | IA decausadei02brow]

tolerating a false religion (Deuteronomy 13:6–12; Zechariah 13:2–3;
Revelation 2:2, 14–15, 20; 17:12, 16–17); devising any representation of God,
of all three persons, or of any one of them, whether inwardly in our spirit
and mind or outwardly in the form of any image or likeness of any creature
whatsoever (Deuteronomy 4:15–19; Acts 17:29; Romans 1:21–23, 25); all worship
of it (Daniel 3:18; Galatians 4:8) or worship of God in or through it (Exodus
32:5, 8); making any representation of false deities (Exodus 32:8), all
worship of them, and all duties pertaining to them (1 Kings 18:26, 28; Isaiah
65:11); all superstitious devices (Acts 17:22; Colossians 2:21–23) which
corrupt divine worship (Malachi 1:7–8, 14) by adding to it or taking from it
(Deuteronomy 4:2), whether devised and adopted by ourselves (Psalm 116:39) or
received from others by tradition (Matthew 15:9), even under the name and
title of antiquity (1 Peter 1:18), custom (Jeremiah 44:17), devotion (Isaiah
65:3–5; Galatians 1:13–14), good intention, or any other pretext whatsoever
(1 Samuel 13:11–12; 15:21); simony (Acts 8:18); sacrilege (Romans 2:22;
Malachi 3:8); and finally every neglect (Exodus 4:24–26), contempt (Matthew
22:5; Malachi 1:7, 13), hindrance (Matthew 23:13), or opposition (Acts
13:44–45; 1 Thessalonians 2:15–16) directed against the worship and divine
ordinances which God instituted.] From all these things it is abundantly plain
that external and instituted worship of God can and must be referred to this
second precept.

One sins against this law, Maresius says in locus 7, thesis 41, by
self-chosen worship and by every superstition—namely, either when God is
worshiped otherwise than he prescribed, for *in vain they worship me, teaching
doctrines which are the commandments of men* (Matthew 15:9), and indeed
self-chosen worship itself is expressly condemned in Colossians 2:23; or when
things appointed for God's worship are used otherwise than was proper, and a
certain power and efficacy *ex opere operato* is ascribed to external rites.

This commandment, Alting says in his common place on the law, directs that in
God's external worship we should be content with rites or ceremonies
prescribed by God.

The law, Wendelin says in *Christian Theology*, book 2, chapter 4, thesis 10,
prescribes the manner of true and divine worship by the second precept. And in
thesis 11: [The manner of true and divine worship is when the true God is
worshiped as he commanded in his Word that he should be worshiped. He is
worshiped in this way—2. when the true means of divine worship prescribed by
God himself are lawfully employed, such as invocation of God, thanksgiving,
the preaching and hearing of the Word, and the administration and use of the
sacraments.] In his explanation he says: [Idolatry is twofold: (1) when that
which is not the true God is worshiped—which is prohibited by the first
precept; and (2) when the true God is indeed worshiped, but not in the manner
in which he ought to be—for example, when he is worshiped through images or
idols—which is prohibited by the second precept. Worship of God cannot be
lawful unless it has God himself as its author, even with respect to its
external means. For (1) whatever is otherwise does not proceed from faith,
and whatever does not proceed from faith cannot be pleasing to God (Romans
14:23). (2) Worship not commanded by God does not possess the character of
obedience, because only the person who carries out God's precepts is said to
obey God.]

[As by the first commandment, Scharp says in his *Course of Theology* on the
law of God, one true God is commanded to be acknowledged and worshiped, in
opposition to false gods, so by the second commandment

[Printed p. 192 | IA PDF 200 | IA leaf 200 | IA decausadei02brow]

the lawful worship of that one true God and the means pertaining to the
worship of that one God are treated. As he alone is the true God, so he has
prescribed the manner in which he is to be worshiped.] He then shows that
self-chosen worship—that is, as he himself defines it, the addition of
something from human will to the divine precepts in divine worship—is
condemned here. To the question, *May Christians worship God by employing
ceremonies other than those he commanded?*, he answers no, for these reasons:
1. When God prescribed the ceremonies of external worship, as in the
sacraments, he said, *Do this.* 2. It was not lawful to add anything to or
alter the legal ceremonies (Deuteronomy 4; Numbers 15). 3. Whatever is added
to divine worship beyond what he prescribed is self-chosen worship. But that
is condemned in Colossians 2:22–23.

Polanus says in *Syntagma of Theology*, book 6, chapter 10, that this second
precept differs from the first because the first precept determines the
substance or proper subject of religious worship, while the second precept
defines the manner of that worship. The manner of religious worship is that
which is divinely prescribed, with all the modes which man, reason, opinion,
the world, and the devil can and customarily do offer being rejected and
avoided. A little later he says that all material, bodily, and imaginary evils
of divine worship, taken from any source and not commended by God, are
forbidden.

Tilenus thinks that the good works enjoined by this second precept can most
conveniently be reduced to the single virtue of religion (*Syntagma*, part 1,
disputation 35, §8). In §9, with the ancients, he understands here by the word
*religion* the observance of ceremonies and rites prescribed by God. And in
§§21–22 he says: [Superstition is therefore distinguished according to its
objects. It occurs either when the worship offered to the true God is not the
worship he requires but another, devised by a creature—that is, self-chosen
worship—or when a creature or a fiction of the mind is worshiped in place of
the Creator. This latter kind of superstition, with respect to internal
worship, is opposed to faith and therefore conflicts with the first precept.
But with respect to external profession, just like the former kind, it is
condemned by this second precept.]

Instituted worship, Ames says in *Marrow*, book 2, chapter 13, §1 and
following, is a means ordained by God's will for the exercise and advancement
of natural worship. All such means instituted by God are indicated in the
second precept by the prohibition of all the opposing means of worship devised
by men. They are placed under the title of a carved thing and an image,
because these were formerly the principal human inventions corrupting God's
worship, and by a synecdoche which runs throughout the Decalogue they very
appropriately stand in place of all devices of human ingenuity pertaining to
worship.

Zanchi locates the sum of this precept, in his work on the second precept, in
the external worship owed to God, or in ceremonies. In these it is lawful for
us neither to devise anything from our own minds nor to adopt anything from
the rites of idolatrous nations. We ought to be content with that worship and
those ceremonies which he has been pleased to prescribe or permit, whether
they are sacraments, sacrifices, or other sacred things. He says: *It does
this synecdochically, comprehending and condemning all external idolatrous
worship under one grosser and more evident species, namely, the worship of
images. And it commands us to retain the ceremonies and sacred rites which he
has approved and to exercise ourselves in them without superstition.* Then he
adds the following by way of confirmation and explanation:

[Printed p. 193 | IA PDF 201 | IA leaf 201 | IA decausadei02brow]

[*For just as by the former commandment he reformed internal religion,
teaching that one God alone and not many gods is to be worshiped, explaining
both who that God is and what true internal worship is, so also by this second
precept he reforms external worship, which consists in ceremonies. Therefore,
after handing down the doctrine of the true God and of the true manner of
worshiping him—namely, by inward piety, whose sum is contained in the first
precept—God also prohibited to his people all the external abominations of
the Gentiles. These can be seen abundantly in the other books of Moses and
also in other chapters of Exodus; but he comprehended their sum in this
second word of the Decalogue under the name of images and the adoration of
them.*] And further: [*Therefore he also wrote in Exodus 23: “You shall not
adore their gods, nor worship them, nor act according to their works.” That
last clause quite plainly teaches that not only images and their adoration,
but also the remaining corrupt worship and the abominations of the Gentiles,
are condemned by this precept. Conversely, it teaches that external worship
purified according to the rule of the divine Word is required of us.*]

From these things it is plain that those do not err who maintain that this
precept commands us to worship God with that kind of worship which he himself
prescribed, and no other. Instituted worship of God therefore pertains to this
precept. As Ames says in the place cited above, this worship does not
specifically and immediately depend upon God's nature or upon that honor
which we owe God by virtue of creation, but upon God's entirely free
institution. It is mutable and has been changed, for it is now different from
what it once was.

That God's external worship is commanded here is plain from the fact that the
actions prohibited here are external actions—namely, the making of images and
the worship of them by external gestures. The contrary actions enjoined here
pertain to God's worship, since everything contained in the first table is
referred more immediately to God and to his immediate worship. It is also
plain from the fact that this law is perfect and therefore prohibits whatever
is displeasing to God and, conversely, commands and prescribes whatever is
pleasing and acceptable to God. Now it is certain that external worship is
most pleasing to God, for from the creation of the world he wished to be
worshiped in this way by men. The sons of Adam worshiped him in this way;
and, when Enosh was born, Scripture testifies in Genesis 4:26 that men began
to call upon the name of Jehovah. [Zanchi says in the place cited above: *This
passage must not be understood as though the name of Jehovah had never been
invoked before. Rather, since by then all external religion had been publicly
profaned—for piety remained among few—Seth, whose family had now grown,
publicly restored religion. For under the name of invocation the whole
worship of God is understood.*] Then the sacred page testifies approvingly
that men worshiped God by sacrifices and other external ordinances of God.
Indeed, every consideration of reason requires that this be done, for God is
the Author and Creator, Redeemer and Savior, of our body no less than of our
soul. [Zanchi says in the place cited above: *Is it not therefore just that we
should worship God by both parts of ourselves, soul and body? Internal
worship resides in the soul; external worship is exercised by the body.
Consequently, the whole religion by which we are united to God and worship
him is compared to marriage. This signifies that, just as a wife ought not
only to love her husband in her soul but also to serve him alone with her
body, so we also must worship God with a pure mind and chaste body, by
internal piety and external exercises of piety.*] Religion also requires that
we glorify God before men, which cannot be done without external worship; and
internal piety cannot fail to display itself in words, gestures, signs, rites,
and other external expressions. See Zanchi, chapter 16, on external worship,
who in his answer to this question,

[Printed p. 194 | IA PDF 202 | IA leaf 202 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Besides internal worship, does God also will to be worshiped and adored by us
with external worship and adoration?*, confirms this abundantly, so that there
is no need to add more here. Moreover, it is certain that this external
worship is commanded either by this second precept of the Decalogue or by
none, for all the others enjoin something else, as is evident. The *first*
prescribes the true object of divine worship; the *third* commands the manner
in which external worship is rightly to be rendered; and the *fourth*, as we
shall hear, prescribes the solemn time for divine worship.

Furthermore, that this commandment requires us to worship God only with that
external worship which he himself prescribed is also plain from the fact that
it is unlawful to worship God with another kind of worship not prescribed by
him. Scripture testifies to this abundantly when it forbids every addition to
the Word which God commands. Deuteronomy 4:2 and 12:32 say: *You shall not add
to the word which I command you, nor diminish from it.* He therefore wills us
to worship him only with the worship he himself prescribed. Likewise Exodus
23:13: *In everything which I have spoken to you, be watchful; and do not
remember the name of other gods, nor let it be heard in your mouth.* And
Numbers 15:39–40: *It shall be a fringe for you, so that when you see it you
may remember all the commandments of Jehovah and do them, and may not follow
your own mind and your own eyes with attentive regard, after which you go
whoring; so that you may remember and do all my commandments, and be holy to
your God.* [Zanchi says in the place cited above, in the answer to the final
question: He therefore wills us perpetually to remember all his precepts,
including those concerning ceremonies. 2. He wills us not only to remember
them, but also to do them. 3. Conversely, he does not will us, even in
ceremonies and external worship, to listen to our own heart, to institute
things which have pleased our eyes, or to follow them. 4. He says that in
God's worship to follow the things which please us is to go whoring from God,
because we follow them with the passion and zeal of adulterous love. Finally,
he teaches that we cannot be holy unless we keep all God's precepts, and only
his, in his worship. Holiness therefore consists in observing God's
commandments, not human traditions. Indeed, when we indulge ourselves and the
devices of our hearts in devising worship, we profane him, commit adultery
away from the Lord, and, so that he may not be our God, cast God aside.]

This is further plain from these words of the precept itself, *You shall not
make for yourself*—that is, from your own head, without my express word and
command, from your own mind or judgment. [Ames says in *Marrow*, book 2,
chapter 13, §11: *Although elsewhere the particle “for yourself” sometimes is
redundant or has another force, here the exceptionally exact brevity of these
precepts excludes redundancy. That it excludes the vanity of human thoughts
is plain from other passages of Scripture tending to the same point, such as
Amos 5:26, “which you made for yourselves,” and Numbers 15:39, “do not follow
your own mind and your own eyes, after which you go whoring.”*] This is also
plain from the fact that Scripture condemns two kinds of idolatry. In the one,
images, high places, and the like were instituted for the worship of idols,
and idols were thus worshiped terminatively. In the other, these things were
instituted for the worship of the true God. Just as the former vice is
condemned by the first precept, so the latter is prohibited by this second
precept, which prohibits that idolatry in which an image is worshiped
relatively, as a means or aid, as in Exodus 32:4–5 and Judges 17:3.

[Printed p. 195 | IA PDF 203 | IA leaf 203 | IA decausadei02brow]

See both these evils condemned in Deuteronomy 4:2–5, 30–32: *Completely
destroy all those places where the nations whose land you are going to
possess by hereditary right worshiped their gods—on every high mountain, on
every hill, and beneath every green tree. You shall therefore tear down their
altars, break their statues, burn their groves with fire, and cut down the
carved images of their gods. Thus you shall destroy their name from that
place. You shall not do so to Jehovah your God. But in the place*, and so
forth. *Beware lest you are ensnared by following them after they have been
destroyed before you, and lest you inquire about their gods, saying, “How did
these nations worship their gods, so that I may do the same?” You shall not do
so to Jehovah your God, for they did to their gods everything that is an
abomination to Jehovah, everything he hates. Every matter which I command
you—take care to do it.* Moreover, the things which the most distinguished
Ames says in the place cited above abundantly confirm this. [He says: The same
point is also declared by the universality of the prohibition, which is
explained distributively in the precept: *Those things which are in heaven
above, or on earth beneath, or in the waters under the earth.* No one except
God himself can either know what will please him or add to any worship that
power by which it can become effectual and useful to us. Nothing can be
honoring to God which does not proceed from God himself as its author. Nor,
finally, do we ever read that God gave anyone such power as to institute any
worship at his own discretion. Matthew 15:9: *In vain they worship me,
teaching doctrines which are the commandments of men.* Consequently, by
implication and according to God's own interpretation, we constitute someone
as our God and render him the honor due to God when in religious worship we
subject ourselves to his authority or ordinances. For this reason people are
also sometimes said to worship the devil when they observe those forms of
worship which the devil introduced (1 Corinthians 10:20; Leviticus 17:7;
Deuteronomy 32:7).]

There is no reason for us to pursue this any further, since this truth has
been abundantly confirmed by the orthodox in their disputations both against
the Roman Catholics and against the Formalists. Therefore, if it is unlawful
to worship God with any worship not prescribed by him, it follows that this
second precept prohibits it and, conversely, commands us to worship God only
in that manner and only by those means and ceremonies which he has prescribed.
Instituted worship of God therefore pertains to this precept.

Dr. Essenius rightly says in *System*, disputation 23 on title 16, §254: [*By
the second precept of the Decalogue a manner of worshiping God is expressly
prohibited in which some external image would represent the deity in some way
and a person would therefore prostrate himself before it. But because that
manner of worshiping God was devised by men and brought into use in this way,
not instituted by God himself nor consonant with his nature or command, by the
same act all other similar manners of worshiping God and practicing religion
which have likewise been devised, invented, and instituted by men are also
prohibited. The worship instituted by God himself, or the manner of
practicing religion ordained by him, is instead commanded.*]

This precept therefore condemns all idolatry by which the true God is worshiped
through images or external representations (Exodus 32:5, 8; Acts 7:41; 1
Corinthians 10:7; Judges 17:3; 18:5–6); the making in any way of images or
similar things to represent the true, invisible, and spiritual God
(Deuteronomy 4:15–16; Acts 17:29; Romans 1:21–26; Isaiah 40:18, 25; 45:5;
Jeremiah 10:8, 14; Habakkuk 2:18; Zechariah 10:2); and all superstition—that
is, worship of God through other means and in another manner than he himself
instituted, whether those means were devised by the worshiper himself or
devised and prescribed by any other ecclesiastical or civil person

[Printed p. 196 | IA PDF 204 | IA leaf 204 | IA decausadei02brow]

(Colossians 2:20–23; Matthew 15:9; Acts 17:22, 25; 1 Kings 11:33;
Deuteronomy 12:30–32; Isaiah 29:13), received from antiquity (Jeremiah 44:17;
1 Peter 1:18), or commended by good intention and devotion (Isaiah 65:3–5;
Galatians 1:13–14; 1 Samuel 15:21). It condemns every invention and devising
of self-chosen worship (Numbers 19:39; Leviticus 10:1–3; Psalm 106:39); every
persuasion and command to undertake self-chosen worship (Deuteronomy 13:6–8;
Hosea 5:11; Micah 6:16); every use of ceremonies once instituted by God but
now abrogated (Colossians 2:14, 16–17; Ephesians 6:15–16; Galatians 4:9–11;
5:2–3); every institution and use of symbolic ceremonies, because they are
part of worship and have not been instituted by God (Matthew 15:9); all
toleration or approval of worship that has not been commanded (Deuteronomy
13:6–12; Zechariah 13:2–3; Revelation 2:2, 14–15, 20; 17:12, 16–17); and
every neglect or contempt of instituted worship (Exodus 4:24; Malachi 1:7,
13; Hebrews 10:25), corruption of it (Malachi 1:7–8, 14), and hindrance of or
opposition to it (Matthew 23:13; 1 Thessalonians 2:16; Acts 13:44–46).

Conversely, this precept commands us to avoid all idolatry—that is, worship of
God through images—and all superstition, and according to our calling and
ability to root them out. It commands us rightly to use, preserve pure, and
hold with zeal the ordinances of God, to which the inventions of men are
opposed (Jeremiah 7:31; Deuteronomy 20:30–31; Matthew 15:9). Accordingly, it
commands us to worship God externally through means instituted by him, by
voice, word, gesture, and so forth. Repudiating our own discretion, judgment,
and will and that of all other men, and rejecting men's devices, precepts,
and doctrines, we are to seek out and follow only the will of God revealed in
the Word, which prescribes the manner of divine worship. Finally, it commands
us to administer God's worship spiritually—that is, without gross and carnal
imaginations of the mind, and so forth. Thus this precept includes: 1. All
instituted external means of divine worship. 2. All external obedience
required concerning those ordinances, whether signified by voice, gesture, or
in some other external manner. 3. Spirituality opposed to gross and carnal
thoughts and imaginations. 4. All means necessary for advancing that external
worship, preserving it safe and intact, rightly rendering it, and so forth.
5. Finally, the removal of all hindrances and adversaries.

But enough of these things, since they have been said only for this purpose:
to make it evident that instituted worship, which flows from God's free will,
must be referred to this commandment. Consequently, things which do not flow
from God's nature but are freely determined by him can be enjoined for
observance by a moral precept.
