QUESTION II.

THE NATURE OF THE COVENANT OF GRACE.

*Who are the contracting parties? Who is the Mediator? What are the clauses of
the covenant, both on God’s part and on the part of human beings?*

I. Just as God willed from all eternity to have a church with which he might
dwell and which might cultivate communion with him for the enjoyment of
happiness, so it pleased him to institute that communion in no other way than
by way of a *covenant*. In it the contracting parties have mutual access to one
another and a close and familiar association. On God’s part this demonstrates
his wondrous συγκατάβασιν, *condescension*, by which he willed to lower himself
from the incomprehensible splendor of his majesty to the most wretched state
of creatures; and on the part of human beings it demonstrates the extraordinary
dignity to which God’s grace raises them, so that from the deepest abyss of
misery they may be admitted to the communion and glory of God, who is blessed
forever.

II. Although, properly and strictly speaking, there cannot be a covenant
between God and man, because there is no place for a contract, which is made
between equals, nor any obligation of God, but only his voluntary
self-communication, as was proved in Part I, Locus VIII, Question 3,
nevertheless by singular grace God willed to enter into a covenant with man.
This has the character of a *quasi-contract*, as jurists say.

III. God entered into a twofold *covenant* of this kind with human beings: the
first, the *legal covenant*, or *covenant of works*, with man in integrity; the
second, the *covenant of faith*, or *evangelical covenant*, with fallen and
sinful man. In the former, God promised eternal life to the man who perfectly
fulfilled the law and threatened death to the one who sinned, according to the
clause, *Do this and you shall live*, and *Cursed is he who does not continue*,
and so forth. In the latter, he promises salvation in Christ and for Christ’s
sake to the one who believes. The former compact was made with Adam before the
fall, and in him with all human beings, concerning which see Part I, Locus
VIII, Question 3. The latter was made in Christ after the fall with the elect.

IV. Once that first covenant had been broken through man’s fall, God, if he
had willed to deal with our first parents according to strict justice, could
have consigned human beings to death immediately after the sin, and no one
could justly have remonstrated with God on that account. But it did not please
God to employ this supreme right against the entire human race. Instead, moved
by mercy, he himself found and established a remedy for such great misery,
graciously sanctioning a new covenant in Christ. In it we have the means not
only of emerging from that misery but also of attaining the most perfect
happiness. This remedy or means

[Printed p. 156 | Internet Archive PDF 184 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

was required by Justice, found by Wisdom, and provided by Mercy and Power in
Jesus Christ, the only-begotten Son of God, our Mediator and the foundation of
this covenant.

[Marginal heading: What the covenant of grace is.]

V. *This covenant of grace* is a gracious compact made in Christ between the
offended God and offending man, in which God freely promises man the remission
of sins and salvation for Christ’s sake, while man, relying upon the same
grace, promises faith and obedience. Or it is a gracious agreement between the
offended God and offending man concerning grace and glory to be bestowed in
Christ upon sinful man under the condition of faith. Two things concerning it
must be considered: 1. Its *nature*. 2. Its *properties*. For knowledge of its
nature, four things especially must be examined: 1. Who is the *Author* of the
covenant. 2. Who are the *contracting parties*. 3. Who is the *Mediator*, and
what his functions are here. 4. What are the *things covenanted*, or the
clauses of the covenant, both *on God’s part* through promise and *on man’s
part* through the stipulation of duty.

[Marginal heading: God is the Author of the covenant, moved by εὐδοκίᾳ alone.]

VI. I. The *Author* and principal efficient cause of this covenant is *God*,
who was led to make it with man solely by goodness and gratuitous εὐδοκίᾳ,
*good pleasure*. Hence in Scripture it is everywhere called *God’s covenant*
and never man’s covenant. For he alone thought of it, while man not only did
not think of it, but intended everything else, since he was God’s enemy in
mind and works. God alone, as supreme Lord and Prince, had the power to
contract with the creature and to relax that supreme right by which, under the
covenant of works, he could punish the sinner without any hope of pardon. He
alone could raise fallen man and save the lost by substituting a new covenant
in place of the former one.

Moreover, he is its Author in such a way that the glory for it must be wholly
credited to him alone, and no impulsive cause outside himself moved him to
institute it: neither man’s *merit* nor his *misery*. For if innocent man could
merit nothing before God, how much less could the sinner and guilty man? And
if the misery of the fallen angels did not impel God to save them, why would
the misery of fallen human beings have impelled him instead to redeem them?
Therefore, the merciful God’s εὐδοκία, *good pleasure*, and gratuitous love
alone were the προηγουμένη, *antecedent cause*, of this covenant. Zechariah in
his song calls it σπλάγχνα ἐλέους, *bowels of mercy* (Luke 1:78), and Paul
calls it ὑπερβάλλοντα πλοῦτον χάριτος, *the surpassing riches of grace* (Eph.
2:7).

VII. Since this work is external, it is common and undivided to the whole
Trinity, yet with this distinction: each Person has here his own peculiar mode
of operation corresponding to this saving economy (1 Pet. 1:2). *God the
Father* concurs in it because he first instituted this manner of communicating
himself and, in accordance with that decree, gave his Son (John 3:16); he
invites us to communion with him, first through the prophets, then through the
Son, and finally through the apostles and ministers; and he sends the Holy
Spirit as the one who applies the covenant (Gal. 4). *God the Son* concurs
both as the *cause* and *foundation* of the covenant through his blood (Matt.
26:28; Eph. 1:3, 4), and as its matter, because he is promised in this
covenant. He is also its *Angel* (Mal. 3:1), that is, its messenger and
executor, and thus the Mediator between God and human beings (1 Tim. 2:5; Heb.
9:15) and the Surety for human beings (Heb. 7:22). *God the Spirit* concurs as
a *cause* together with the Father and the Son, and as the *matter*, because
he is promised (Gal. 3:14), the *witness* (Rom. 8:16), and the *pledge* of the
heavenly inheritance (Eph. 1:14; 2 Cor. 1:22).

VIII. The instrumental causes are both the *Word* and the *Sacraments*, and the
*ministers* of each. The *Word* is not the *moral law*, that is, directly and
in itself, because for the sinner it is the ministry of death and the letter
that kills (2 Cor. 3:6), but the *gospel*, which also takes its name from the
fact that in it are revealed both

[Printed p. 157 | Internet Archive PDF 185 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the grace of God and the righteousness of Christ (Rom. 1:16, 17). The
*Sacraments*, on God’s part, are *signs* which represent the substance of the
covenant and *seals* which seal that same substance (Rom. 4), as well as
*moral instruments* through which it is applied to us. On our part, however,
they are restipulations by which we bind ourselves to God (1 Pet. 3:21), and
*external marks*, or symbols of covenant union. The *ministers* act as
messengers and *servants* through whom God addresses us and invites us to the
covenant (Matt. 23:3; Heb. 1:1), and as *ambassadors* through whom he makes
stipulations with us authoritatively (2 Cor. 5:20).

[Marginal heading: The contracting parties: the offended God.]

IX. II. The *contracting parties*, or the subjects of the covenant, are three:
the *offended God*, *offending man*, and *Christ the Mediator*, who reconciles
offending man to the offended and angry God. Here *God* acts not, as in the
covenant of nature, insofar as he is *Creator* and Lord, because sin destroyed
that natural communion which joined the creature to the Creator; nor insofar
as he has an exalted and infinite ὑπεροχήν, *preeminence*, over created things,
because man, guilty of sin and stricken by the sense of it, could not have
borne such majesty, far less than even the angels, who are compelled to cover
their faces before it (Isa. 6:2); nor as *Lawgiver* and the strict exacter of
moral perfection from man, because such perfection could not be expected from
a corrupted creature, nor is any access to the most just and holy God open to
the sinner. Rather, he acts insofar as he is the *merciful Father and
Redeemer*: offended indeed, but to be appeased, who through benevolent love
willed to reconcile offending human beings to himself (Rom. 5:8; Eph. 2:4).

[Marginal heading: Offending man.]

X. Here *man* is not regarded simply as a *creature*, or as an upright and
righteous creature, as in the first covenant, but as an *offending sinful
creature*: as man dead in sins, powerless, a child of wrath, and alienated
from God and his life. Moreover, he is regarded insofar as he is *conscious of
his own misery*, stricken by a sense of it, and thus *contrite under the law*
(Matt. 11:28). Thus the sinner’s σχέσις, *relation*, must not be regarded here
without qualification, although it is a prior condition required in the
object; otherwise this covenant would have to extend equally to each and every
sinner, which the very matter cries out cannot be said, and the decree of
reprobation sufficiently proves. Rather, it must necessarily be joined with
the σχέσει, *relation*, of the contrite sinner who will believe by God’s free
election.

[Marginal heading: The Mediator.]

XI. Third, *Christ the Mediator* intervenes here, reconciling offending man to
the offended God. In the first covenant, since no dissension yet existed
between God and man, there was also no need of a mediator. But in the later
covenant, because sin had intervened, the Mediator was so necessary that
without him no covenant between God and man could have been given: God, on
account of his justice, could not endure guilty man, and man, on account of
his injustice, could not approach God. In this appears God’s wonderful mercy
toward man, by which he willed not only to contemplate entering into a
covenant but also gave a Mediator, without whom it could not be made.

This Mediator had to be God and man, and a mean drawn from both. He had to be
man so that he could suffer and die, because sin could be expiated and the
sinner restored to favor only through suffering and a bloody death (Col. 1:20,
22; Heb. 2:14, 15). He had to be God so that his death might have infinite
value exceeding the measure of a creature, and so that, having overcome death,
he might live forever with his own. And he had to be a mean drawn from both so
that he might first do in nature what he later had to effect in grace.

XII. Here it seems superfluous to ask whether this covenant was contracted
with Christ as one of the contracting parties, and in him with all his seed,
as the first covenant had been made with Adam, and in Adam with all his
posterity. This view pleases not a few, because the promises are said to have
been made to him

[Printed p. 158 | Internet Archive PDF 186 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

(Gal. 3:16), and because, as the Head and Prince of his people, he holds the
first place in all things, so that nothing can be obtained except in him and
from him. Or should it be said instead that the covenant was contracted in
Christ with all the seed, so that he does not so much have the character of a
contracting party as of an intermediate party standing between those at
variance to reconcile them, as seems more satisfactory to others? It is
superfluous, I say, to debate this, because the matter comes to the same thing.
It is certain that *a twofold compact* must necessarily be considered here, or
two parts and stages of one and the same compact. The prior compact is the one
which exists *between the Father and the Son* for carrying out the work of
redemption. The later is the one which God contracts *with the elect in
Christ* concerning their salvation through and for Christ’s sake under the
condition of faith and repentance. The prior compact is made with the Surety
and Head for the salvation of the members; the later is made with the members
in the Head and Surety.

[Marginal heading: The compact between the Father and the Son is proved.]

XIII. The compact of the Father and the Son contains the will of the Father,
who gives the Son as λυτρωτήν, *Redeemer*, and Head of his mystical body, and
the will of the Son, who presents himself as Surety for his members to
accomplish this ἀπολύτρωσιν, *redemption*. For Scripture represents the Father
to us in the economy of salvation as stipulating his Son’s obedience even to
death and, in return for it, promising him the name above every name, that he
might be the Head of the elect in glory. It represents the Son as presenting
himself to do the Father’s will and promising the faithful and constant
execution of the office entrusted to him, and finally restipulating the
kingdom and glory promised to him. All these things are gathered from
Scripture without obscurity.

XIV. This compact is expressly mentioned in Luke 22:29: “I appoint a kingdom
for you, just as my Father appointed it for me”; and in Isa. 42:1, 2, 6, 7.
There the Father is presented as calling the Son and entrusting to him the work
of our salvation: “This is my servant; I will protect him; my Elect, in whom my
soul is well pleased,” and so forth. “I, Jehovah, have called you in
righteousness, and I will take hold of your hand and preserve you, and I will
give you as a covenant of the people and a light of the nations.” And chapter
49:6 says, “I have given you as a covenant of the people, and that you might be
my salvation to the ends of the earth.” This calling extends to the three
parts of the mediatorial office: the priesthood (Ps. 110:4), prophecy (Isa.
61:2), and kingship (Ps. 2:8).

The law of the covenant is laid down by the Father: that he be the covenant of
the people and Light of the nations and lay down his life for the sheep, as
John 10:18 says, “I received this commandment from my Father.” To this law the
Father adds a promise, not only of protection and aid in executing the office
(Isa. 42:6, 7) and of the work’s successful outcome through God’s acceptance
(Isa. 49:1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8), but also of recompense and reward in a spiritual
seed and the exaltation granted to him. Thus Isa. 53:10 says, “After his soul
has made itself a sacrifice for guilt, he shall see his seed, and Jehovah’s
good pleasure shall prosper in his hand.”

The acceptance on the Son’s part consists in this: he freely submitted himself
to the Father’s will and the law of the covenant, becoming Surety for us. Heb.
10:5 says, “You prepared a body for me”—that is, you appointed me as your
servant by preparing a body for me through the incarnation—“Then I said,
Behold, I come to do your will, my God; it is my delight, and your law is in
the midst of my inward parts.” The Son not only took this upon himself but also
actually performed it, being made of a woman and under the law (Gal. 4:4).
John 15:10 says, “I have kept my Father’s commandments and remain in his
love.” And he restipulates his own glory and the salvation of his body in John
17:4, 5: “I glorified you on earth; I completed the work which you gave me to
do. And now, Father, glorify me with yourself, with the glory which I had with
you before the world existed”; and verses 16, 17: “Preserve in your name those
whom you gave me; sanctify them in your truth; your word is truth.”

[Marginal heading: The three periods of this covenant. 1. In designation. 2.
In promise. 3. In execution.]

XV. This covenant can be considered in three moments. 1. With respect to
*designation*, when from eternity in the counsel of the most holy Trinity a
Surety and Mediator was given to the church. To this refer the words of
supreme Wisdom in Prov. 8:23, who, speaking about herself, says, *I was anointed
from eternity*—that is, designated by my will and my Father’s, which is one, to
carry out the office of Mediator in time. To this also refer Peter’s words in
the first chapter, 1 Pet. 1:20, when he says that Christ “was foreknown before
the foundations of the world were laid”; and Ps. 2:8: “I will recount the
decree. Jehovah said to me, You are my Son; today I have begotten you. Ask of
me, and I will give you the nations as an inheritance.” There Christ describes
the eternal designation, made in God’s decree, of himself—the one who is the
Son of God, begotten from eternity, and therefore alone equal to bearing so
great an office—as King of the whole church.

2. With respect to *promise*, when immediately after man’s fall, with God now
offended by sin, he offered himself actually to perform those things which he
had promised from eternity. Thus he was actually constituted Mediator and
declared as such in the Word; and in accordance with that undertaking, he
began to do many things pertaining to the Mediator’s office. With respect to
prophecy, he acted as interpreter of the divine will, both revealing himself
directly to the fathers as the Angel of the covenant and mediately through his
ministers, whom he moved by his Spirit (1 Pet. 1:10, 11; 3:19). With respect
to kingship, he conducted himself as the leader and Prince of the people (1
Cor. 10; Josh. 5:13). With respect to the priesthood, he interceded for the
elect by virtue of the future payment (Job 33:24).

3. With respect to *execution*, in the incarnation, through which he assumed
our flesh in order to accomplish in it the work of our salvation. To this
refer the words of Heb. 10:5, in which an antithesis is drawn between the legal
sacrifices and Christ’s one most perfect sacrifice: “Therefore, entering the
world, he said, You did not will sacrifice and offering. Then I said, Behold,
I come to do your will.”

XVI. Since the Mediator stood midway between the parties at variance, he had
to deal with each: *on the part of human beings*, with God, through undertaking
all things owed by human beings; and *on God’s part*, with human beings,
through guaranteeing that all things promised by God would most certainly be
fulfilled. He had to deal with the *offended* God for human beings by
*satisfying* for their sins and *interceding* for them with the Father to
obtain the Spirit with his gifts. *With respect to the past*, he guaranteed
the perfect payment of all debts and actually presented that λύτρον, *ransom*,
through his obedience and death; and *with respect to the future*, he
guaranteed their sincere and constant observance of the covenant thereafter,
to be effected in them by the power of the Spirit.

On the other hand, he had to deal with *offending human beings* on God’s part
as the Father’s Ambassador: declaring his good will concerning their
salvation; signifying his mission for the work of redemption; requiring faith
in the promises and obedience to the commands as the duties and conditions of
the covenant; and, in turn, promising all the benefits of the covenant and
guaranteeing that all of them would most certainly be bestowed. He did this
not only by the Word of the gospel and the sacraments, but especially by the
shedding of his blood, by which he sealed this new covenant before God, and by
the efficacy of the Holy Spirit, by which he makes it valid in us.

Thus a twofold obstacle to the covenant had to be removed: *on God’s part, his
enmity* on account of man’s sin; and *on man’s part, man’s enmity* on account
of God’s justice. God had to be reconciled to man, and man to God. Christ
accomplished the former through the merit of his blood, by which he satisfied
for us and reconciled God to us; the latter through the efficacy of the
Spirit, by which he sanctifies and converts us, and by converting us
reconciles us to God. Thus he perfectly fulfilled what had to be accomplished
either on God’s part among

human beings or on the part of human beings before God, for the full
consummation of the covenant of grace.

[Marginal heading: The things covenanted: on God’s part, that he will be our
God.]

XVII. III. The *things covenanted in this covenant* are either on God’s part
or on the part of human beings: *on God’s part*, the benefits promised; *on
the part of human beings*, the duties prescribed to them. Each is proposed
either *generally* or *specifically*. *Generally*, they are contained in the
formula of the covenant, by which God promises *that he will be our God* and
requires that we be his people. This formula is indeed expressed in very few
but very weighty words, which embrace whatever God promises to man or requires
from man, as will easily appear to one who examines the formula a little more
distinctly. The two things included here are both the promise of a benefit and
attestation of love, and the requirement of worship and obedience.

As for the *promise*, it is of such a kind and so great that it is rightly
called the soul and substance of the covenant, the foundation and compendium
of all the other promises given to the faithful in Scripture. Nothing more
sublime, more complete, sweeter, or more certain can be given than it.
Therefore, it was not proposed only once or twice but very often, whenever the
covenant was first instituted or renewed, under both the Old and New
Testaments. It was first made to Abraham in Gen. 17:7, 8: “I will be your God
and the God of your seed after you”; confirmed to the Israelite people in Exod.
20:2; 29:45; Deut. 5:2, 3, 6; repeated to the Jews in the Babylonian captivity
in Jer. 24:7; 30:22; Ezek. 11:20; reiterated after the captivity in Zech. 13:9;
and renewed under the New Testament from the oracle of Jeremiah 31:33 in 2
Cor. 6:16 and Heb. 8:10. It is also to be fulfilled in heavenly glory, as Rev.
21:3 says: “Behold, God’s tabernacle is with human beings, and he will dwell
with them, and they will be his people, and God will be with them as their
God.” Thus it is the sum of all God’s benefits to the faithful, both in grace
and in glory. Hence the psalmist not without reason proclaims *blessed the
nation whose God is God* (Ps. 33:12).

[Marginal heading: What is implied by God being our God.]

XVIII. To obtain the genuine sense of this promise, it must not be taken
*broadly*, insofar as it signifies God’s authority, care, and dominion. In that
sense God belongs not only to the faithful but also to human beings generally,
and indeed to all creatures by reason of creation and governance; and nothing
singular and extraordinary above the other nations would be promised to the
people. Rather, it must be taken *strictly*, insofar as it implies a covenantal
relation and that gracious communion by which God communicates himself to
sinful man in order to endow him with immortal and happy life, as that formula
is commonly explained. Thus, if God is the God of all through providence, he
is the God of the faithful through grace. If God is the God of creatures in
the world through authority and power, he is the God of his people in the
church through benevolence and love, and through the benefits which he imparts
to them.

[Marginal heading: All saving benefits are included here, but four
especially.]

XIX. Although these benefits are innumerable, they can nevertheless be
referred to these four principal ones. 1. *Reconciliation and communion with
God*, through which he is not only appeased toward us but also gives himself
to us, so that henceforth he is ours both οὐσιωδῶς, *essentially*, with
respect to nature and attributes, and ὑποστατικῶς, *hypostatically*, with
respect to the Persons and personal operations. 2. *Communion in goods*, so
that all God’s goods and whatever belongs to him are ours. 3. *Conformity with
God*, through participation in the divine nature and transformation into his
image. 4. The *constancy and eternity* of God’s love and our union with him.
Because these are of great importance in this subject, we shall not hesitate
to develop them a little more fully.

[Marginal heading: 1. Reconciliation and union with God.]

XX. I say first that *reconciliation* and communion with God are signified,
because he cannot be called ours unless he is appeased and, from an angry
Judge, made a merciful Father. He deigns not only to impart his goods
liberally to us but also gives himself to us, so that henceforth we may glory
in him as our God and portion (Pss. 16:5; 73:26; 119:57; Hos. 2:23). He gives
himself to us as a *Husband*, who betrothed us to himself in everlasting
mercies (Hos. 2:19; Song 2:16); as a *Father*, who adopts us as his children (2
Cor. 6:18); and as a *Prince and King*, who chooses us as a περιούσιον,
*peculiar*, people, with whom he contracts an offensive and defensive
covenant, so that he may protect us with his shield against every undertaking
of our enemies.

[Marginal heading: So that God is ours in his nature and properties.]

Moreover, God gives himself to us in such a way that he is *ours* with respect
to *all the attributes* which conduce to our advantage and salvation, and
which are rightly called ours by enjoyment and use, because their saving
effects flow down to us. God’s wisdom is ours for direction; his power for
protection; mercy for the remission of sins; grace for sanctification and
consolation; justice for vengeance upon enemies; faithfulness for the
execution of promises; and sufficiency for the communication of happiness in
every form. And just as sin brought innumerable evils upon us, we find a
remedy against all of them in the divine properties. Wisdom remedies ignorance
and blindness; grace, guilt; power, weakness; mercy, misery; goodness, malice;
justice, iniquity; God’s sufficiency and fullness, poverty and need;
faithfulness, inconstancy and fickleness; holiness, impurity; and life, death.

[Marginal heading: In the Persons.]

XXI. God is *ours personally*, insofar as the individual Persons are ours and
give themselves to us for accomplishing the work of redemption: the Father by
choosing, the Son by redeeming, and the Holy Spirit by sanctifying. The
*Father* becomes ours through adoption, when he receives us into his family
and regards, cherishes, and loves us as children (1 John 3:1). The *Son*
becomes ours through suretyship, when he presents himself as Surety to satisfy
for us and as Head to govern and vivify us. He becomes our *Prophet*, who
reveals salvation by the light of doctrine; our *Priest*, who acquires it by
the price of his merit; and our *King*, who applies the salvation acquired by
the efficacy of the Spirit. The *Holy Spirit* becomes ours when he is sent to
us and gives himself to us as Sanctifier and Comforter, so that he may dwell
in us as in his temples and enrich us with his gifts: light, strength, joy,
liberty, holiness, and happiness.

Thus our communion is with the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (1 John
1:3; 2 Cor. 13:14). Hence baptism, which is the seal of the covenant, is
administered in their name, so that we may thus be consecrated as children of
God the Father, members of the Son, and temples of the Holy Spirit, and may
enjoy the goods arising from the individual Persons: the Father’s mercy, the
Son’s grace, and the Holy Spirit’s power.

[Marginal heading: 2. The communication of goods.]

XXII. 2. God is ours by reason of the *communication of goods*. For God cannot
be ours without whatever belongs to God also being ours; and as all things
belong to God, so all things in heaven and on earth are also ours. “All things
are yours,” Paul says in 1 Cor. 3:21, “and you are Christ’s, and Christ is
God’s.” God’s creatures on earth are ours to serve us; the angels in heaven to
guard us; heaven as our homeland; earth as the place of our pilgrimage; and
the world as the inheritance given to us with Abraham (Rom. 4:13). Thus all
God’s promises for both the present and the future life are ours, as are God’s
goods both of grace and of glory: “God is a Sun and Shield to us; he gives
grace and glory, and denies nothing to those who walk in integrity” (Ps.
84:11). He is the Sun of every kind of blessing for the communication of
goods, and the Shield of invincible protection for repelling evils. Hence we
may rightly conclude with Paul

[Printed p. 162 | Internet Archive PDF 190 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

that nothing will harm us because God is for us: “If God is for us, who is
against us?” (Rom. 8:31); and with David that nothing will be lacking because
God is our Shepherd: “God is my Shepherd; nothing will be lacking to me” (Ps.
23:1). For he denies nothing to those who fear him, but grants all things
liberally and blesses us with every spiritual blessing (Eph. 1:3). “Christ is
all in all” in grace (Col. 3:11), and “God will be all in all” in glory (1 Cor.
15:28). “He who overcomes will inherit all things, and I will be God to him,
and he will be a son to me” (Rev. 21:7).

[Marginal heading: 3. Conformity with God.]

XXIII. 3. God is ours *by reason of conformity*. It is not enough for him to
diffuse the saving effects of his properties into us; he also wills to imprint
their character and likeness upon us, so far as the condition of a finite
creature can bear it, so that *we may be partakers of the divine nature* (2
Pet. 1:4) and become like him, which is the most perfect mode of communion. As
the sun, sending its rays into crystal or some transparent body, renders it
bright so that it shines like the sun, so the Father of lights irradiates us
with his light, not only to scatter the darkness of error, sin, and misery,
but also to communicate his light to us and make us, as it were, so many
shining suns. Hence the “righteous” are said to “shine like the Sun in the
kingdom of the Father” (Matt. 13:43). To this refer Paul’s words in 2 Cor.
3:18, when he says that, “beholding as in a mirror” of the gospel “the glory
of the Lord with unveiled face, we are transformed into the same image from
glory to glory”; and 1 John 3:2: “We shall be like him, because we shall see
him as he is.”

Indeed, no communion can be had with God without conformity to him. This is
twofold: first, in *holiness* and the moral virtues, that we may be holy, just,
patient, merciful, and perfect as God is (Matt. 5:48; 1 Pet. 1:15); and second,
in *life and happiness*, that we may enjoy his immortality and glory: “Because
I live, you also shall live” (John 14:19), and “You always remain the same and
your years do not fail; your offspring will be established forever in your
sight” (Ps. 102:27).

To this belongs Christ’s invincible argument when, against the Sadducees, he
demonstrated the immortality of souls and the resurrection of bodies from the
formula of the covenant: God is called *the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob*
even after their death, yet *God is not the God of the dead but of the living*.

[Marginal heading: Matt. 22:34.]

Two things are necessarily presupposed here. First, this formula of the
covenant implies the communication of life and immortality, so that all who
are received into his communion become like him in this respect, because the
God who lives forever cannot cultivate communion with the dead. Hence the
patriarchs necessarily always lived in some part of themselves, since God
calls himself their God even after death. Second, the covenant was contracted
not merely with one part of man but with the whole man, so that the benefits
of the covenant must pertain to the whole man. Hence follows the necessity of
the resurrection of bodies, so that the faithful may become capable of life
and happiness in soul and body.

[Marginal heading: 4. The eternity of the goods.]

XXIV. Although these goods are extraordinary, they could not make us
perfectly blessed unless perpetuity were added to such great gifts. Therefore,
by this very promise God willed to commend the constancy of his love and the
*eternity of the goods*. Since Jehovah in his concept denotes eternity, when
he promises that he will be our God, by that very fact he intimates that he
will be ours forever. As long as God will be God—and he will be so forever—he
will also be our God, so that we may forever enjoy communion and happiness
with him. This is intimated in Ps. 48:14: *God is our God forever*. This is
undoubtedly the greatest argument of consolation and confidence for the
faithful against all temptations, and

an inviolable support for hope. For if God will be ours forever, what further
evil could we fear or what good could we hope for, since we shall live forever
in him?

XXV. Besides this general promise contained in the formula of the covenant,
many other specific promises are included which are founded upon it and flow
from it. Such are the *bestowal of the Holy Spirit* (Ezek. 36:27); the
*remission of sins* (Jer. 31:34); *regeneration*, designated by the inscription
of the law (v. 33); *adoption* (2 Cor. 6:18); the *removal of the stony heart
and the impression of a fleshly and new heart* (Ezek. 36:26); *perseverance*
(Jer. 32:40), which is a peculiar benefit of this covenant because it must be
inviolable and eternal; and *every spiritual blessing* of grace and glory
(Gen. 12:3; Gal. 3:8, 9, 14; Acts 3:26; Eph. 1:3).

All these are customarily designated by the name *salvation*. Salvation
implies not only the possession of life, which had a place in the first
covenant, but also deliverance from the death brought about by sin. It is
completed in four parts, which are opposed to the same number of parts of
death, to which we were made liable through sin. 1. *Justification*, which
frees us from the curse hanging over us. 2. *Sanctification*, which removes
the stain and corruption in which we lay dead. 3. *Consolation and peace of
conscience*, which sustain us against the afflictions of life and the terrors
of conscience that in Scripture come under the name of death. 4. *Eternal
glorification*, which renders us immune from eternal death and brings us to
the completion of happiness.

[Marginal heading: The duties required from man in the covenant.]

XXVI. Since this formula of the covenant is mutual and includes, along with
the *benefits* which God promises to those in covenant, the *duties* which he
requires from human beings, we must now see what those duties are. They are
expressed in Scripture *generally* and *specifically*. *Generally*, God wills
that, just as he promises to be our God, so in turn “we may be his people”
(Jer. 31:33): “I will be God to them, and they will be a people to me.” Here
the relation between God and us is designated, which implies a mutual exchange
of benefits and duties: if God is our Husband, we are to be his chaste and
faithful Wife; if he is the Father, we are to be children; if he is King and
Redeemer, we are to be a peculiar People who live as those redeemed by
Jehovah. Just as all God’s benefits toward us are contained in that one
promise, so all the duties of human beings toward God are prescribed by this
one condition, which at once intimates both what they must *be* and what they
are bound to *do*.

[Marginal heading: What it is to be the people of God. Their duties.
Separation from the world and consecration to God.]

XXVII. *To be the people of God* is not merely to be subject to his dominion,
to depend upon him, and to be governed by him, for in this sense all human
beings universally are his people. Rather, it is to adhere to him through
worship and obedience, so that we *are* nothing, *have* nothing, *can do*
nothing, and *do* nothing which *is not God’s*, that is, which is not referred
to his glory. Thus first is designated *separation from the world* and
*consecration to God*. Just as we are not our own but belong to him who created
and redeemed us and gave himself to us, so we are no longer to live for
ourselves, Satan, sin, or the world, but for him alone. Just as whatever we are
comes from him, so whatever we are and can do must be referred to him. Just as
God passed over other human beings and chose us, with whom he might contract a
covenant and whom he might adopt as a people, so we in turn must pass over all
others and choose him alone as our God, that we may worship and follow him
alone. The Holy Spirit urges this so often in Deut. 7:6, 7; 26:17, 18; 1 Cor.
6:19, 20; 2 Cor. 5:14, 15; 6:17, 18; and Rom. 14:7, 8, 9. This consecration
must be such that we dedicate ourselves wholly, soul and body, to God as
temples and spiritual sacrifices (Rom. 12:1; 1 Cor. 3:16, 17),

so that *our mind is God’s* for knowing him; our *will* for worshiping him;
our *affections* for loving him; our *eyes* for contemplating his wonders; our
*ears* for hearing his voice; our *mouth* for celebrating his glory; our
*hands* for performing his work; and each of our members as instruments of
righteousness to God for glorifying him (Rom. 6:13). Thus whatever we *have*,
whether of goodness, honor, wisdom, or power, must be expended for God’s glory
and worship.

[Marginal heading: Worship and obedience.]

XXVIII. 2. *Worship and obedience*: we are to be solicitous to worship him
religiously and exclusively, and diligently perform whatever we can to advance
his kingdom, proclaim his truth, defend the good cause, or fulfill his
commands, so that we may answer his calling and show ourselves grateful for
his benefits. For these things are so connected by an indivisible bond that
they neither can nor ought to be separated. In vain do we hope that God will
be ours unless we in turn are his people; that he will attest a Father’s love
unless we render to him the obedience of children; or that he will bestow the
benefits which he promised unless we fulfill the duties which he requires from
us.

[Marginal heading: In faith and repentance.]

XXIX. The two principal duties required are *faith and repentance*. The former
embraces the promises; the latter fulfills the commands. The former answers to
the promise of grace, *Believe and you shall be saved*; the latter is commanded
by the evangelical law in Gen. 17:1: “Walk before me and be upright.” For just
as there are two principal benefits of the covenant on God’s part, the
remission of sins and the inscription of the law upon hearts, so there must be
two duties on man’s part corresponding to them: faith, which applies the
remission of sins to itself; and repentance, or the pursuit of sanctification,
which puts the law inscribed upon hearts into practice by walking in its
statutes. Christ intimates this in Mark 1:15: “Repent and believe the gospel.”

XXX. Although these two duties are commanded by God as works owed by man, they
are nevertheless also promised by him as gifts of God. Thus they must be
regarded here simultaneously both as man’s duties and as God’s benefits. Ezek.
36:27 says, “I will put my Spirit in your midst and cause you to walk in my
statutes,” and so forth. This is the singular privilege of this covenant above
the covenant of nature: the conditions themselves depend upon God’s grace and
pass over into promises. Hence it cannot be rendered void as the first
covenant was, which rested upon man’s powers, but remains effective and
eternal, because it depends wholly and entirely upon God, and everything in
this covenant is gratuitous, even the conditions themselves. The following
Question will teach what these conditions are and how they function here.
