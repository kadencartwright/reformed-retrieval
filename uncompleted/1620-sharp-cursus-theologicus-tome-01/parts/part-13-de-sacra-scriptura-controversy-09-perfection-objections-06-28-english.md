Objection 6, from the last verse of the last chapter of John: “There are also
many other things which Jesus did. If they should be written one by one, I do
not think that the world itself could contain the books that would be
written.”

Therefore he did and said many things which are not written, because one hand
could hold the books which exist.

Response 1. There is a fallacy from ignorance of the elen—

[1620 Tome I | col. 152 | BSB frame 00090]

—chus, because the passage does not deal with doctrine but with deeds or
miracles. For it says that he “did many other things,” and John 20:30 calls
these deeds signs or miracles. All of them were written to the extent
necessary to confirm Christ’s doctrine and divinity.

2. The cited passage contains a hyperbole very commonly used in Scripture.
But in hyperboles not everything is to be pressed literally, and the gloss
rightly says that the books could not be contained—not because of a lack of
space, but because of the readers’ capacity. Lyranus likewise interprets this
passage to mean that these things cannot be explained or comprehended by men
because of their excellence in relation to our understanding.

3. The passage itself distinguishes and explains itself. When it says that
not everything is written καθ’ ἕν, individually, it concedes that there are
things written κατ’ εἶδος, according to kind, which suffice for our salvation.

4. This does not follow: not everything is written; therefore, not everything
necessary is written. For John 20:30–31 teaches that everything necessary is
written. Cyril therefore rightly says in book 12 on John: “Not everything the
Lord did was recorded, but the writers knowingly recorded what they thought
sufficient concerning both morals and doctrines, so that, shining with right
faith, works, and virtue, we might attain the kingdom of heaven.” Therefore
nothing necessary was omitted.

Finally, if the papists take these words literally, and if the things which
are not written are papist traditions, then those traditions are infinite.

Objection 7, from Acts 1:3. Christ appeared to the disciples for forty days
and spoke about the kingdom of God. But the evangelists wrote very few of the
Lord’s deeds and words after the resurrection, and it is not likely that the
apostles failed to hand them down to the churches, since they were neither
envious nor forgetful, such that they were unwilling or unable to relate
them. Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because the apostles have most
faithfully writ—

[1620 Tome I | col. 153 | BSB frame 00091]

—ten everything necessary.

2. If the things Christ said about the kingdom of God were not written by the
apostles, how is it established that papist traditions contain them?

3. The things he said about the kingdom of God were not different from those
he taught before his death, as was shown above.

4. What he taught about the kingdom of God is readily gathered from Matthew
28:9, 10, and 29; Mark 16; Luke 24; John 20 and 21; and Acts 1.[^1] Namely, he
expounded the Scriptures and gave the apostles authority to cast out demons.

Likewise, he gave them authority to retain and remit sins, preach the gospel,
administer the sacraments, and so forth, so that there is no need here for
papist conjectures.

5. The fact that fewer words and deeds of Christ after the resurrection exist
in writing occurred, as Oecumenius, the ordinary gloss, and Lyranus note,
because Christ associated with the disciples at intervals rather than
continuously; see John 20:26.

Finally, what Bellarmine adds concerning the institution of the sacraments,
that Christ handed down many things which are not written, is false. For in
the New Testament Christ instituted all the sacraments before his death,
whereas the remaining things which the papists add were never instituted by
Christ.

Objection 8, from 1 Corinthians 11:2: “I praise you because in everything you
are mindful of me and keep my precepts just as I delivered them to you.” But
these precepts about the manner of praying and receiving the Eucharist are
never found in writing, and something was still lacking from the precepts
handed down, because he adds that men should pray with heads uncovered and
women with heads veiled. And to show that something must be accorded to the
Church’s tradition and custom even without writing, he concludes: “But if
anyone seems contentious, we have no such custom, nor does the Church of God.”

[1620 Tome I | col. 154 | BSB frame 00091]

Response 1. There is a fallacy from ignorance of the elenchus, because the
question concerns doctrines of faith, whereas the cited passage concerns
adiaphorous rites and external ceremonies—namely, the manner of praying and
receiving the Eucharist. These can vary according to times and persons, but
doctrines cannot vary.

2. The assumption is denied, because these precepts about the manner of
praying and receiving the Eucharist are contained in 1 Corinthians 11:30. If
the passage is to be understood of the whole doctrine, and specifically of
the Eucharist, then within the doctrine itself some things are necessary and
perpetual while others are free and can be changed according to circumstances
in their species, though not in their genus. For all these things must be
directed toward order and edification.

3. This does not follow: the apostle praises them because they observed the
things he handed down by the living voice; therefore, those things are not
written, either in those very passages or elsewhere. Compare 1 Corinthians
15:3.

Finally, we admit that the things the Church establishes concerning order and
decorum are not to be rejected contentiously. It does not, however, follow
that traditions of every kind are to be retained indiscriminately, much less
papist traditions.

Instance 1, from 1 Corinthians 11:23. He begins the question about the Supper
from tradition: “I received from the Lord what I delivered to you,” and he
concludes in verse 34, “The other things I will arrange when I come.” But what
he arranged is nowhere found in writing. He arranged matters concerning the
ordination of ministers, the sacrifice of the altar, and the matter and form
of the other sacraments, and the heretics cannot show the contrary in any
way.

Response 1. Bellarmine ineptly cites the first passage for unwritten
traditions, because that tradition exists in writing in the very same place.
But the question concerns unwritten traditions.

2. Response. The assumption is denied. Indeed, the things Bellarmine produces
are ἀσύστατα, incoherent, for

[1620 Tome I | col. 155 | BSB frame 00092]

he says that the things Paul arranged exist nowhere, yet he recounts those
very same things.

3. Although the things Paul arranged were not written when he wrote to the
Corinthians, it does not follow that they are not written now, that the things
the papists now foist upon us are those same things, or that those things
exist or are necessary in our age. For Paul speaks only of matters pertaining
to the order and decorum of the Corinthian church, as Chrysostom and Thomas
interpret this passage concerning a clearer declaration of the things handed
down and the arrangement of matters that were not especially necessary.

4. The apostle taught the Corinthians everything necessary for salvation even
before this epistle was written; see 1 Corinthians 1:5 and 15:1–2. Therefore,
if the things Bellarmine requires are apostolic, they too were handed down by
the apostle. Here it should be noted upon what foundation the matter and form
of the sacraments and the papist ministry rest according to the papists:
namely, upon no written Word of God.

Objection 9, from 2 Thessalonians 2:15: “Hold the traditions which you have
received, whether through discourse or through our epistle.” Therefore, apart
from Scripture there are certain unwritten traditions.

Response. There is a fallacy from equivocation in the word *tradition*. It can
signify any doctrine handed down in any way—that is, either by discourse or
in writing—which is what the apostle speaks of. Or it can signify unwritten
doctrine in opposition to Scripture, the sense in which the papists take the
word *tradition* in their conclusion. The conclusion is therefore inept.

2. Some think the apostle speaks about certain rites and adiaphorous matters,
but those judge more correctly who think that he speaks about all his
doctrine, which he delivered to them both by the living voice and in writing.
The papists, however, understand their own most foul superstitions here.

3. The inference is denied, because these tradi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 156 | BSB frame 00092]

—tions delivered through discourse were nothing other than what is contained
in Paul’s epistles. If, however, they say that papist traditions are those
things, let them prove this to be true. Finally, only this follows from the
passage—even if we concede that these traditions were different from the
things he wrote in this and other epistles: not all doctrines had been written
when the apostle wrote this epistle. But that is not in question.

Instance. The disjunctive particle εἴτε, “whether,” clearly indicates that the
things he delivered and the things he wrote were different. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because this particle is not always
disjunctive, but is often conjunctive, as in 1 Corinthians 13:8, Colossians
1:20, and elsewhere.

2. Let the particle be disjunctive. It does not follow that the things
themselves are different, but only that the modes of the thing are different,
as one and the same thing very often occurs in different modes. Thus
regeneration is signified either through regeneration or through baptism, but
the diversity of modes does not make the thing of regeneration itself
different. The following is similar: whether we live or die, we are the
Lord’s. Therefore, we belong to one if we live and another if we die. This
does not follow. Likewise, whether I or they, so we preach. Therefore, Paul
preaches a different gospel from the other apostles. This does not follow.

Instance 2. If this were so, the Epistle to the Thessalonians would have to
contain all the doctrine which the apostle had preached. But he preached the
whole gospel, as is evident from chapters 1 and 2 of the first Epistle to the
Thessalonians, whereas this epistle does not contain a hundredth part of the
gospel. Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is denied, nor does Bellarmine prove it, because
the cited passages do not say that Paul preached the whole gospel fully to
the Thessalonians. Moreover, Paul undoubtedly delivered all the doctrine of
the gospel to the individual churches, yet it was not necessary for him to
write to each of the individual

churches every doctrine. Likewise, when Paul wrote to the Thessalonians, it is
likely that no books of
the New Testament had been written except Matthew’s Gospel; and according to

does not follow: when Paul wrote to the Thessalonians, not everything necessary
had been written; therefore, it was not written afterward either. Nor does
this follow: at that time the Thessalonians did not receive the doctrine of
the gospel fully because they did not then possess the other books of the New
Testament; therefore, now we do not possess the complete doctrine and a most
perfect canon. Nor: Paul did not write everything in this epistle; therefore,
neither did he nor others write it elsewhere. Paul does indeed mention
written and unwritten doctrine.

Instance 3. In this chapter the apostle says that he preached to them about

10 of *The City of God*: “They knew what the apostle taught by the living
voice, but we cannot know it, because we did not hear the apostle.” Therefore,
not everything he said was written.[^4]

Response 1. We concede what the argument concludes, provided it is understood
καθ’ ἕν, individually, and not κατ’ εἶδος, according to kind.

2. Paul said or wrote nothing about a definite time—that is, the month or year
of Antichrist’s coming. He does indeed indicate what held back Antichrist’s
coming, namely the Roman Empire, whose powers were still intact; this is
contained from verse 5 onward. He does not indicate a definite time, but only
recalls to their memory what he had told them.

3. Let it be true that Paul indicated the time of Antichrist’s coming. This
does not follow: definite knowledge of that time is an unwritten apostolic
tradition; consequently, they can indicate the definite time of Antichrist’s
coming.

4. Bellarmine cites Augustine falsely, because he does not say that the
Thessalonians knew the time of Antichrist’s coming, but what impeded his
coming—whether the continuance of the Roman Empire, or the fact that the
gospel had not yet been preached throughout the whole world. We can be
ignorant of these things without loss of faith.

Instance 4. Whether the things the apostle taught were written or not written
afterward is irrelevant to the argument, because the apostle does not promise
that he or another person will write what he had delivered orally, but simply

than the things received through an epistle, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. The apostles taught and wrote as they were moved by the Holy Spirit,
and they taught and wrote many things which Paul did not teach. Paul wishes
these to be observed no less than his own.

2. This concludes nothing, because he must show that the things the apostle
said are not the things he wrote, and then that the things the papists peddle
are the things he said.

3. They maintain that the things Paul delivered by the living voice were
delivered to certain perfect and wise men, according to Cano in book 3,
chapter 3, foundation 3, who wishes to establish from Hilary and Origen that
Moses delivered many things to Joshua in this way. But the things the apostle
mentions here he delivered to all the Thessalonians. Finally, if the words
“whether through discourse or through an epistle” imply that certain
unnecessary things are not written, then by equal reasoning they will also
imply that certain necessary things were not delivered by the living voice.
This is absurd. Therefore.

Objection 10, from 1 Timothy 6:20: “Guard the deposit.” But *the deposit*
cannot mean Scripture, but rather the treasure of doctrine and understanding
of divine doctrines—that is, both the sense of Scripture and of other
doctrines—which entire body of doctrine he wished to be propagated through
tradition. If he were speaking about words

[1620 Tome I | col. 159 | BSB frame 00094]

that were written, he would not commend the deposit so anxiously, for the
Scriptures can be preserved in bookcases and by copyists. Therefore.

Response 1. The assumption is denied, because Scripture is the true treasure
of sound doctrine—not indeed the letters or syllables, or the books or sheets
of paper, which he does not chiefly commend to Timothy here lest he lose them.
Tertullian therefore rightly calls the things delivered above and below “the
deposit.”

2. This does not follow: he had to guard the deposit; therefore, unwritten
apostolic traditions now exist, and papist traditions are this deposit.

3. Cardinal Cajetan calls the flock entrusted to Timothy, which he commands
to be diligently preserved, the deposit in this passage, as does Lyranus. In
his explanation of this passage, however, Thomas understands by the deposit
every good and every grace entrusted by God to anyone, so that he might
preserve and multiply it, but especially a prelate’s care for his neighbor,
because it is said, “Feed the sheep,” and in Hebrews 13, “They keep watch over
your souls.”

4. What Bellarmine says about writing itself is profane and impious, as though
Scripture could be preserved better in libraries and on sheets of paper than
in men’s hearts.

For Paul does not speak about the external preservation of paper, but about
the inward preservation of true doctrine.

Finally, it is likely that Paul speaks chiefly about the office and the gifts
necessary for that office. Consequently, since the question concerns
doctrines alone, the passage is altogether irrelevant.

Objection 11, from 2 Timothy 1:13: “Have a form of sound words which you heard
from me; guard the good deposit through the Holy Spirit who dwells in you.”
But that form is necessary and is not written. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because the form

[1620 Tome I | col. 160 | BSB frame 00094]

of sound words is not an unwritten tradition, but Scripture itself, or the
express image of the gospel shining forth in its matter and form.

Both the matter and the form of the gospel exist in writing. Indeed, in the
same place it is expressed in the words *faith* and *charity*, and elsewhere
the things pertaining to faith are manifestly noted to have been written:
John 20:31. The things pertaining to charity are in Matthew 22:37–38 and
elsewhere.

Finally, the argument must be turned back upon them: he commands him to change
nothing in the matter and form of the gospel. Therefore, traditions must not
be admitted.

Objection 12, from 2 Timothy 2:2: “You therefore, my son, commend to faithful
men the things which you heard from me among many witnesses,” and so forth.
But not everything Timothy heard is written, yet it is to be commended to the
faithful. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, for the things he heard are written. That
things heard can be written as to their substance, though not in the very same
words, may be seen in Acts 17:11. Nor does this follow: commend to others the
things you heard; therefore, they are not written.

Moreover, the things delivered and to be commended to others are either the
same as the things which are written or different. If the same, then they are
contained in writing. If different, then the apostle’s declaration in
Galatians 1:8 must apply.

Objection 12, from 2 John 12 and 3 John 13: “I have many things to write to
you, but I was unwilling to do so with paper and ink.” Therefore the apostle
said many things to the disciples, and through the disciples to the universal
Church, which are not written.[^2]

Response 1. There is a fallacy from what is said with a qualification. It is
indeed true that many things are not written in those brief epistles, but they
are written elsewhere.

2. What were those things which he deferred until his coming? Whether they
were different from what he wrote and intended is not established, and the
question does not concern the number of words but doctrines. The individual
words indeed are not written, but the doctrines are, 2 Timothy 3:15.

3. This does not follow: John wished to write many things in those two epistles and not
elsewhere; therefore, they were not written by others, or the things he had to
write are papist ceremonies and traditions.

Objection 14, from 1 Corinthians 11:16: “If anyone seems contentious, the
churches of God have no such custom.” Therefore, those who cannot be refuted
from Scripture or reason can at least be refuted from custom.

Response 1. The inference is denied, because they restrain contentious people
only by the authority of the Church; he does not confirm in this way the
doctrines with which alone the question is concerned. It is therefore a
fallacy from what is said with a qualification and from a non-cause treated
as a cause.

Objection 15, from 1 Timothy 6:3: “If anyone teaches a different doctrine and
does not accede to sound discourses,” and so forth. Here he mentions discourse
alone, not Scripture. But not all those discourses are written. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because all the doctrines are written.
Furthermore, he speaks about doctrine which exists no less in writing than in
discourse, and the apostle subordinates the word *discourse* to the word
*doctrine*, because doctrine is not to be hidden in books but brought into the
light and proclaimed by the living voice. Finally, where he mentions
discourse, he does not exclude Scripture.

Objection 16. “If anyone evangelizes to you a gospel other than what you have
received, let him be accursed.” Here he does not say “what I wrote,” but “what
you received.” Therefore.

Response. This does not follow: they received it; therefore, it is not
written. Nor does this follow: they received it by the living voice;
therefore, it was not written. Nor: they received something different from
what was written by Paul or others.

Objection 17, from 2 Timothy 3:8. Where Paul mentions those two magicians, he
received their names from tradition. The papists also maintain that the names
of those three Magi who came from the East in Matthew 2 are re—

a similar tradition about the name of the good thief, that he was called
Isaiah, and the name of the man who pierced Christ’s side, and so forth.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied, because Paul received and wrote those
names not from tradition but from divine revelation, just as he did all the
other things which exist in his books.

2. Let him have received from tradition the names of the magicians who
resisted Moses. Nevertheless, knowing this was not necessary for salvation as
a doctrine of faith, nor were the other things which were mentioned. If they
had been necessary, the evangelists and apostles certainly would not have
withheld them. Therefore, let them prove some doctrine necessary for salvation
which can be proved from tradition alone but not from Scripture.

Objection 18. The evangelists name many of Christ’s ancestors whose names are
not taken from Scripture but from tradition, as in the final part of Christ’s
genealogy. Therefore.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied, because the evangelists wrote those
names not from tradition but at the dictation of the Holy Spirit.

2. Faith could remain safe without knowledge of those names.

3. After Christ’s coming, however, our faith needed us to know with certainty
that Christ is the son of Abraham and David, so that it might be confirmed.
Knowledge of that genealogy is of great value. Therefore, since it was
necessary, it came to exist in writing and exists in Scripture.

Objection 19, from Acts 20:35. A saying of Christ is recited—namely, that it is
better to give than to receive—which is nowhere found in the Gospels.
Therefore.

Response 1. Although this saying of Christ does not appear in the very same
words, it is nevertheless contained in substance in these passages; see Luke
6:35 and 38, and chapter 16, verse 9. Thus there is no need here to resort to
traditions

[1620 Tome I | col. 163 | BSB frame 00096]

that are unwritten. Furthermore, we concede that not every individual saying
of Christ is written word for word, because it is enough that everything
necessary is written.
And because this necessary saying, if omitted by the evangelists, was spoken
by Paul and written by Luke at the dictation of the Holy Spirit, this
statement is now not ἄγραφος, “unwritten.”

Objection 20, from 1 Corinthians 15:3: “I delivered to you what I had
received.” Therefore.

Response. There is a fallacy from equivocation in *tradition*, for the papists
understand an unwritten tradition, whereas in the same passage the apostle
teaches whence he took that tradition: namely, from Scripture.

Objection 21, from verse 9 of the Epistle of Jude. There he proves that a
magistrate is not to be reviled by the example of Michael, about whom there is
nothing in Scripture. Therefore, what is narrated there was taken from
tradition.

Response 1. The inference is denied, because Jude had this from divine
revelation. Moreover, let him have had it from tradition; nevertheless, that
deed does not contain a doctrine of faith. The doctrine of obedience to be
rendered to magistrates, which that deed confirms, is understood from other
passages of Scripture. Likewise, that deed, because and insofar as it is
necessary to know, is now contained in writing. Finally, the apostles indeed
cited certain things from the poets, as in Acts 17, Titus 1, and elsewhere,
not to confirm doctrines of faith, but where morals are treated. But this
does not follow: they cited certain things from the poets; therefore,
Scripture is insufficient, or doctrines of faith cannot be gathered from
Scripture without the poets’ writings.

Objection 22, from Jeremiah 31:32–33. The prophet speaks about the new covenant
which is to be written not on tablets but in hearts. Therefore, the necessary
doctrine of the gospel is written not in books but in hearts.

Response 1. The inference is denied, because the doctrine of the gospel is
written both on tablets and in hearts. Furthermore, there is a fallacy of
qualifi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 164 | BSB frame 00096]

—cation. The difference between the old and new covenants is noted here:
namely, the ancients had almost nothing besides those tablets and did not
possess the power of the covenant, while in the New Testament the gifts of the
Holy Spirit are much more abundant and many more people have the covenant
impressed upon their minds in a greater measure. Finally, this does not
follow: the new covenant is written in hearts; therefore, the traditions the
papists foist upon us belong to the apostles and are necessary for salvation.

Objection 23. Certain popes and councils speak about unwritten traditions. A

themselves observers of apostolic and ecclesiastical traditions. The Council
of Trent teaches the same in session 4. Therefore.

Response 1. The testimonies of the popes prove nothing, because they are given
in their own cause. Moreover, they do not concern doctrines of faith and
morals, but only certain rites, whereas the question concerns faith and
doctrines. Finally, they are merely human testimonies.

2. What he says about the First Council of Nicaea—namely, that it condemned
Arius from Scripture—is false.[^3] Nor does this follow: the Arians produced
many things from the Scriptures; therefore, they could not be refuted from the

could be refuted from Scripture, because they all produce Scriptures on their
own behalf, though badly. Moreover, Athanasius and the other Fathers re—

—futed the Arians by testimonies of Scripture.

3. What Bellarmine says is false: that Theodoret in book 1, chapter 8 of his

4. The Second Council of Nicaea, celebrated around the year of Christ 770,

Fathers rightly thought that the veneration of images could not be proved
from Scripture. Therefore, the papists who now attempt to prove it from the
Scriptures are in error.

5. The eighth general council, around the year 870—or, as others maintain,
around the year 900—established εἰδωλολατρείαν, “idolatry,” along with many

Finally, the conventicle of Trent confirmed all the heresies of Antichrist.
Thus even if these councils established that traditions must be received, it
does not follow that they must be received.

Objection 24. The Greek and Latin Fathers teach the same thing. Therefore.

Response 1. The Fathers teach the same thing as we do, as we proved above. If
they teach what Bellarmine wishes, they contradict themselves, which we do not

because they are to be followed only to the extent that they follow
Scripture.

2. In the citation of almost all their testimonies there is a fallacy from

—trines, but matters which pertain to the Church’s εὐταξίαν, “good order,” and

such as those in the papacy.

3. There is a fallacy from a non-cause, because certain Fathers, especially
those who lived in more corrupt times, attributed more than was right to the
institutions of their forebears as though they had proceeded from the
apostles. This error of the Fathers ought not prejudice the Word of God or us.

4. The things Bellarmine often cites as testimonies of the Fathers are either
additions, corrupt passages, or plainly spurious writings, such as those
falsely ascribed to Dionysius the Areopagite and others.

Objection 25, from the agreement of heretics. Just as we gather from the
agreement of the Fathers who receive traditions that they are to be received,

therefore, whoever rejects traditions is a heretic. Nor were the people of
whom Bellarmine speaks heretics because they preferred Scripture alone, but
because they abused Scripture.

2. The antecedent is denied, because true heretics have always fled the light
of Scripture and commonly boasted of their own obscure traditions, as is
evident from Irenaeus and Tertullian, whom Bellarmine cites.

3. The Valentinians and Marcionites indeed rejected traditions, but only the
kind Irenaeus means—namely, the doctrine of the apostolic churches preserved

even despised the Scriptures themselves.

4. Cyprian did not err in this:

he appealed from custom to Scripture in the question about rebaptism. He erred
in being unable to confirm his opinion from the Scriptures.

5. The Arians appealed not so much to Scripture as to the word ὁμοούσιον,
“consubstantial,” which they rejected because it does not occur in the
Scriptures, although religion and piety do not consist in words. Finally, the
argument has no more force than if someone said: we eat, drink, and speak in
the same language as heretics and unbelievers; therefore, we are such people.

Objection 26, from the practice of all nations. Before and after Moses the

that unwritten traditions are necessary or that they are exceedingly useful.

mysteries were concealed from Moses and not written, but revealed to certain
wiser men.

2. Bellarmine admits that many papists deny what he asserts. Let him therefore
reconcile himself with his own people.

3. If the Jews had any customs beyond Scripture, they were not doctrines of
faith, but certain ceremonies and indifferent rites. Indeed, Christ cleanses
the law even from human traditions of this kind in Matthew 5 and elsewhere.
Finally, the Church is governed in a different manner from secular nations,
for in the things of this world a certain natural light remains, but not in
divine matters. God therefore willed his Word to be written so that we might
depend wholly upon it.

Objection 27, from the dignity of the Church. Just as the Jews once excelled
all others because the oracles of God had been entrusted to them, Romans 3,
so now the Church excels others because she alone, as Christ’s bride, knows
all the mysteries of true religion and is con—

[1620 Tome I | col. 168 | BSB frame 00098]

—scious of the Bridegroom’s secrets, and is therefore called the pillar of
truth, 1 Timothy 3. But if everything were written and perfectly clear, the
Church would have no privilege, because heretics, pagans, and Jews would know
the mysteries of our faith no less than we. Nor would what Irenaeus says in
book 3, chapter 4 be true: “The rich knowledge of divine things was deposited
by the apostles in the Church alone as in a repository.” Therefore.

Response 1. The proposition is not true of the whole multitude of Christians,
but of the truly faithful, who alone are Christ’s bride and privy to the
mysteries of God.

2. The assumption is distinguished, for there are two kinds of knowledge and
understanding of Scripture, one of the letter and the other of the Spirit. As
for understanding of the letter, the faithful have no privilege, because the
impious can have as much knowledge as the pious, and sometimes more. Thus Saul
was among the prophets and Judas among the apostles. Nor will the papists say
that all the most learned prelates and scholastics were truly pious. Does not
the devil also surpass all men in knowledge of the letter? As for
understanding of the Spirit, this is proper to the elect alone, because they
alone are θεοδίδακτοι, “taught by God,” Luke 8:10, while the rest are not, 1
Corinthians 14. Finally, the Church neither knows mysteries otherwise nor
knows other mysteries than in and through Scripture, John 5:39. Paul therefore
earnestly commended the reading of Scripture to Timothy in 1 Timothy 4:16 and
2 Timothy 3:15, because all the Church’s true knowledge is contained in
Scripture.

Objection 28. The dignity of many mysteries requires such silence that it is
not fitting for things which are set before the whole world to be explained
in the Scriptures. Therefore, Scripture is not sufficient, and these
mysteries are contained in traditions. Therefore.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied, because Christ wished everything he
said and the whole gospel to be preached to all nations, Matthew 28:20, and
the apostles publicly preached the en—

[1620 Tome I | col. 169 | BSB frame 00099]

—tire counsel of God, Acts 20:27. Furthermore, not everyone who possesses
Scripture understands its mysteries. Finally, there is an equivocation in the
word *mystery*. First Timothy 3:16 does not say of Christian doctrine that it
ought not be revealed and explained to individuals, but that it altogether
surpasses human sense and comprehension, 1 Corinthians 1:7.

Instance 1. It is not permissible to admit the unbaptized to behold the
awesome mysteries of the Mass. Therefore, much less will it be permissible to
hand those mysteries to them in writing.

Response 1. First, we admit that Christ and the apostles said or wrote nothing
about the Mass. But they preached about the sacred Supper even to the
unbaptized and wrote all the mysteries of the Supper so that everyone might
read them.

Instance 2. Christ privately interpreted to the disciples the parables which
he had spoken to the people, Luke 8:11 and Matthew 13:37. Therefore, he did
not wish everyone to understand the mysteries.

Response 1. We too concede that these mysteries are not understood by
everyone. Nevertheless, we say by Christ’s example that they are to be set
before everyone, because the preaching of Scripture has different ends
according to the diversity of its hearers, 2 Corinthians 2:16.

Instance 3. The apostle says in 1 Corinthians 2:6, “We speak wisdom among the
perfect.” Therefore, not everything is to be related to everyone, but certain
things are to be reserved for the perfect and wise.

Response. In this argument Bellarmine agrees with Marcion and Valentinus who,
according to Irenaeus in book 3, chapter 2, abused this same passage to prove
that not everything is to be sought from the Scriptures. Furthermore, the
apostle does not speak about things other than those which he wrote, because
one and the same doctrine is full of wisdom with respect to the faithful and
foolish with respect to unbelievers, 1 Corinthians 2:4. But wisdom is
justified only by her children, Matthew 11:19, who are truly wi—

[1620 Tome I | col. 170 | BSB frame 00099]

—se and perfect.

Instance 4. When the ancients speak about the sacrament of the Eucharist,
they commonly say, “The faithful know; the initiated know.” Therefore.

Response. Let the faithful and the initiated know the things pertaining to
the sacraments. It does not follow that those things are not set forth in
Scripture or are not to be set before unbelievers. It follows only that the
ancients were exceedingly prudent not to give pagans and profane people an
occasion to ridicule the sacred mysteries, in which respect they are also to
be imitated by us.
