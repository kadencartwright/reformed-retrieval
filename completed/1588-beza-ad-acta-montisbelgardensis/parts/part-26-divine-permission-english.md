## Part XXVI: Divine Permission

[Part II | PDF page 183 | logical source scan 182 | printed page 181 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

From these passages compared together, I think it sufficiently appears how we should judge the opinions of those who, although striving with the best intention, nevertheless seem to me to judge these matters by human wisdom, fearing that otherwise God would be held to contradict himself, command what he does not will, punish what he commands, and finally be the author of evils. All these claims are certainly horrible and full of impiety and blasphemy.

[Part II | PDF page 184 | logical source scan 183 | printed page 182]

Some of them therefore teach that God indeed does good things but permits evil things, according to Psalm 81:13, “I permitted them to the depravity of their heart,” and Acts 14, “He allowed all nations to walk in their own ways.” To this is also twisted that law permitting divorce because of the Israelites’ hardness of heart. We do not reject this distinction when rightly understood according to Augustine’s meaning, since the Greek fathers also commonly employ the term *synchōrēseōs* [συγχωρήσεως]. It distinguishes what God himself does by his Spirit or through good instruments by goodness and grace from what he also does by loosening the reins of evil instruments. For God is so powerful and good that he not only performs good works by good instruments and causes, but—what is his especially admirable work—uses evil people to do good and brings light out of darkness.

I therefore ask those who interpret this “permission” differently whether they attribute it to God as willing or unwilling, or as an idle spectator and a pilot abandoning the ship’s helm and permitting the ship to the discretion of stubborn sailors. If to a willing God, they agree with us, and what they fear does not follow, as we have already shown and shall show again. If to an unwilling and resistant God, what sort of God is this who suffers violence? If to one who has abandoned the world’s government, how far are they from Epicurus’s impiety? Some therefore moderate this opinion. They too posit no permission separated from all will and action, but maintain that the work of evil people is merely ordered by God—that is, enclosed within limits and directed toward God’s glory, the godly’s salvation, and the wicked’s destruction. But if this opinion is true, it follows that God’s providence does not govern the world, since infinitely more things are done badly than well in it, but is subjected to the discretion of Satan and the wicked, except only for limiting their discretion and attempt. Satan and his followers will therefore devise whatever they please against the church, although they will not accomplish everything they please.

Yet Satan speaks very differently in Job’s history, daring to attempt nothing unless God grants it; indeed, he did not even enter the pigs without first asking and obtaining permission. What? When the Lord chastises his people, will he not prescribe and decree their punishments but only moderate them? Why, then, is the Lord himself said to have aroused, summoned, and brought the Assyrians and Chaldeans? When the Lord sets evil people against one another, does he not sit as a judge deciding after hearing the case? Finally, does Satan act simultaneously as judge and executioner, with God merely restraining his savagery? Since all these consequences follow from that opinion, one can see how much weight it deserves. To put it briefly—

[Part II | PDF page 185 | logical source scan 184 | printed page 183]

—whatever is alleged for permission of this kind does not help those who think they can excuse God from sin by any human reasoning. Does not one who permits someone whom he can save to fall furnish a cause for his destruction? Others therefore contrive another way by which God—who certainly does not need human advocacy—might seem capable of being excused. As appears from book 2, distinctions 45 and 46 of the *Sentences*, they say that with respect to the evil of fault God has no positive act either of willing or not willing, but only the negative act of not willing, which is indifferent and signifies permission. Those permit, they say, who neither will nor refuse, but do not will to prevent. If they are believed, it follows much more plainly that by far the greatest part of what occurs in the world happens either without God’s knowledge or without his care, the former wholly alien to his providence and the latter to his power.

Finally, some say God is the cause of sin, but only as the sun is called the cause of darkness, since darkness arises in the air when it departs and vanishes when it approaches. Thus those from whom God withdraws his grace necessarily rush into sins, since that grace alone enables sins to be avoided. It must indeed be confessed that God’s grace is either not bestowed upon or withdrawn from those whom God abandons to themselves, delivered to Satan and their lusts. But it does not follow that God thereafter does not move those thus abandoned to themselves and use them as instruments, both for their own just destruction and for testing others whom he pleases, as occurs in persecutions of the church, or chastising them, as daily occurs to the godly and to those in the church delivered to Satan for a time for the destruction of the flesh. Moreover, not even in this way can God be excused by human reasoning, since when God withdraws his Spirit, a human being can do nothing but sin. Proud flesh would conclude from this that when God withdraws his grace—which he does only willingly—he leads people, if not directly, nevertheless into the very path of sin.

What, then? Our God is certainly most holy and pure, enemy of every vice, and so severe an avenger that he does not spare even his elect except by avenging all their sins in our one Mediator. Yet far be it that on this account he should have lost his dominion over wicked angels and by far the greatest part of humanity, so that he cannot only restrain their wickedness and turn it in the opposite direction, but also holily, justly, and powerfully move and incline their very perversity—whether for the benefit of his elect, whenever he has pleased to exercise them through those evil instruments so that his power might be perfected in their weakness; or to chastise them so that they may be instructed, humbled beneath his mighty hand; or by his just judgment to set the wicked themselves against one another until they bring final destruction upon themselves—all without being in any way—

[Part II | PDF page 186 | logical source scan 185 | printed page 184]

—contaminated by the fault of the sinful instruments. All those evil actions of evil instruments, whether internal or external, possess the character of sins so far as the instruments themselves are concerned, even when they do evilly what God wills well to be done; the Lord afterward justly avenges them. But with respect to God himself, who well moves and inclines those very instruments whose evil wills move themselves evilly, their evil actions are considered not as sins but as divinely inflicted punishments of sins, among the greatest of which sins themselves are numbered. Scarcely anything worse can happen to sinners than that, abandoned to themselves and enslaved to their lusts by God not idly watching but judging, they should heap sin upon sin.

The defect from goodness on which the deformity of all evil actions depends does not reach up to the throne of the most pure divine Majesty. His operation is always best and, whatever instruments he uses, always produces the best work. Thus with respect to God’s eternal decree, the use of the instruments subordinated to it, and the end finally arising from it, nothing in the created order occurs by God except most wisely and excellently. But all viciousness stops and fixes its boundary at the defect of the evil instruments—not insofar as they are instruments of God, who works through them also, but insofar as they possess within themselves the principle of their evil actions. In this sense we must explain what I cited above from Augustine’s *Enchiridion to Lawrence*: “Great are the works of the Lord, sought out according to all his wills, so that in a wondrous and ineffable manner what is even contrary to his will does not occur apart from his will, because it would not occur unless he permitted it; and he certainly permits it not unwillingly but willingly.”

And shortly before: “It must not be doubted that God DOES WELL by permitting whatever is done evilly to be done. For he permits this only by JUST JUDGMENT”—therefore also by a decree, sometimes hidden but always just, as Augustine declares in many other places. “Although evil things, insofar as they are evil, are not good, nevertheless it is good that not only good but also evil things should exist. For unless it were good that evils also existed, evils would by no means be permitted to exist by almighty God, for whom it is doubtless as easy not to permit what he does not will to exist as to do what he wills. Unless we believe this, the very beginning of our faith is endangered, in which we confess that we believe in almighty God.” Thus Augustine plainly shows that, if faith in God’s almighty providence both universally and in individual things is preserved, nothing can occur from which either his will is excluded or what he wills is separated from his eternal decree. This will takes the standard of justice and rectitude from nothing but itself and therefore must be adored, not scrutinized. When we have reached it, we must hear the apostle crying, “O the depth!” The many Scripture testimonies clearer than daylight which I cited above compel us to this, even against our will—

—especially those expressly mentioning fate [εἱμαρμένης], counsel [βουλῆς], and foreknowledge [προγνώσεως]. Thus all may understand how greatly the permission we acknowledge—which distinguishes what God himself does by his Spirit from what evil instruments, although well moved instrumentally by him, nevertheless do evilly as causes—differs from the permission some fabricate while seeking a knot in a bulrush and, as we showed above, obtaining nothing less than what they seek.
