QUESTION XIV

ON FUNDAMENTAL ARTICLES AND ERRORS

Are Some Theological Topics Fundamental and Others Not, and How Can They Be
Distinguished from One Another?

[Marginal heading: The Socinians err by defect.]

I. The question concerning fundamental articles is weighty and difficult. It
is raised by various people, and error concerning it has occurred in different
ways, both by defect and by excess. 1. The Socinians err by defect. They admit
very few things as fundamental, and only practical things, almost entirely
setting aside theoretical ones. They thus teach that religious disagreements
can be settled easily, since these concern theological conclusions and the
doctrines of the schools rather than the fundamental articles of faith, which
are few in number and almost universally acknowledged by both sides. Under this
pretext they remove the principal doctrines of faith from the fundamentals,
such as the doctrine concerning the Holy Spirit, the Trinity, the person of
Christ, satisfaction, and so forth. The Arminians plainly approach their
position. They too reduce the fundamentals to those heads which are almost
entirely beyond controversy among all Christians and are contained in these
three things: faith in the divine promises, obedience to the divine commands,
and due reverence for the Scriptures.

[Marginal heading: The Roman Catholics and Lutherans err by excess.]

II. Those who err by excess include, on the one hand, the Roman Catholics,
who are often not ashamed to press their own hay and stubble, and whatever the
Roman church defines, as fundamentals; and, on the other, the more rigid
Lutherans. In order to make religious union with us more difficult, the latter
extend the fundamentals more widely than is right, often turn every error into
heresy, and make necessary things out of matters indifferent, so that they may
the more easily prove that we disagree in fundamentals.

III. Between the two, the orthodox hold the middle course. Just as they
necessarily rest upon certain fundamentals, so they neither restrict them too
much with the former nor extend them too much with the latter.

[Marginal heading: What a fundamental article is.]

IV. Just as the foundation in a building is said to be that which is laid
beneath it in such a way that without it the building can neither be erected
nor stand, so in religion that is called the foundation upon which the whole
of religion rests, by whose standing it stands and by whose removal it falls.
It is taken in two ways. It is taken either incompositely and personally and
is applied to Christ, the foundation of all salvation, upon whom the church
and religion are built as upon an immovable and unshaken rock, Matthew 16:18;
1 Peter 2:6–7; and 1 Corinthians 3:11: “No one can lay another foundation
besides what has been laid, which is Jesus Christ.” Or it is taken complexly
and naturally for the fundamental truth prescribed for everyone to believe,
which is accordingly called the foundation of faith.

[Marginal heading: It is taken either broadly,]

The latter is extended broadly to the first rudiments of the Christian religion customarily
delivered to catechumens at their initiation. In Hebrews 6:1–2 the apostle
calls them a “foundation” or “beginning”: repentance from dead works, faith in
God, the doctrine of baptisms, the laying on of hands, the resurrection of the
dead, and eternal judgment. Yet these heads do not possess an equal degree of
necessity. Some are necessary primarily and in themselves, while others are
necessary only secondarily and through another. In this sense, the fundamental
articles of religion are referred to the Decalogue, the Apostles' Creed, the
Lord's Prayer, the sacraments, and the power of the keys, because they contain
the necessary and fundamental doctrine of salvation without which we cannot
grasp the rest.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 45 | IA leaf 99 | PDF p. 99]

[Marginal heading: Or strictly.]

Strictly, however, it denotes the essential doctrines of the Christian faith
whose contemplation or practice is necessary simply with respect to the thing
itself. That is, these doctrines are simply and absolutely necessary for all
the faithful to believe and can neither be unknown nor denied without loss of
salvation. It is in this sense that we now speak about fundamental articles.

[Marginal heading: Not all articles are fundamental.]

V. Although all the truths revealed in Scripture are necessary to believe as
divine and infallible, not all are equally necessary. Here the breadth and
extent of faith must be carefully distinguished from its necessity. Whatever
belongs to the breadth of faith does not immediately belong to its necessity.
Not all truths have the same weight; some possess a greater and others a
lesser degree of necessity. For example, there is one reckoning for things
necessary by a necessity of means, and another for things necessary only by a
necessity of command; one for things that concern doctrines strictly so
called, and another for things that concern only rites and ceremonies; one
for a particular doctrine or article with respect to its substance—for
example, that Christ suffered and died—and another for that same article
considered only with respect to its circumstances—for example, that Christ
suffered under Pontius Pilate and was crucified between two robbers. The
latter circumstances could indeed be unknown without loss of salvation.

[Marginal heading: This is proved from Scripture.]

VI. Scripture indicates this distinction among articles clearly enough in 1
Corinthians 3:11–13, where Paul distinguishes the foundation from the things
built upon it, and in Philippians 3:15, where he teaches that there can be
certain heads of doctrine over which Christians disagree while peace and love
are preserved. If anyone touches the fundamentals, he is subjected to an
anathema: “Even if we or an angel from heaven proclaims to you something
beyond what we proclaimed to you, let him be accursed,” Galatians 1:8. In
other matters there is room for Christian toleration: “Receive the one who is
weak in faith,” Romans 14:1; and, “Let as many of us as are mature think this,
and if you think anything differently, God will reveal this also to you.
Nevertheless, in what we have attained, let us walk by the same rule and think
the same thing,” Philippians 3:15–16. Now, just as there is a distinction in
faith with respect to doctrines, so there is one in the law with respect to
commands. The necessity of moral commands is one thing and that of ceremonial
commands another: the former is absolute and indispensable, but the latter is
hypothetical and changeable.

VII. Hence some doctrines are simply necessary to know for the very existence
of faith; others are necessary only in a certain respect and for its
well-being. Some are necessary for the generation of faith and others for its
perfection, Hebrews 6:1. Some are necessary in themselves and absolutely for
everyone, whether infants or mature people; others are necessary only
accidentally for the mature and advanced, Hebrews 5:13–14, and in relation to
the instruction of others. This latter necessity must in turn be understood
with some latitude in proportion to gifts, education, calling, sex, and age.
Some belong to the flock, while others are pastors to whom the oracles of God
have been entrusted and whose office it is “to teach and to refute those who
contradict,” Titus 1:9.

VIII. Again, just as the object of faith is one thing when it is general and
adequate—namely, the whole Word of God—and another when it is special and
proper—namely, the doctrine concerning Christ together with its related heads
and God's promises—so some doctrines of faith are primary and immediate, such
as the articles concerning the Trinity, Christ the mediator, justification,
and so forth. Others are secondary and mediate, that is, consequences,
hypotheses, and conclusions born and drawn from those primary doctrines. Some
are positive, affirming a true doctrine, such as that Christ is the Son of God
and that his death is a ransom for our sins. Others are negative, rejecting a
falsehood, such as that there is no Mass or purgatory, and so forth. According
to the differing character of these doctrines, some are necessary to believe
explicitly and formally, such as the special and proper object of faith, the
primary and immediate doctrines, and the positive articles concerning the
principal heads of faith. Others are necessary only implicitly and virtually.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 46 | IA leaf 100 | PDF p. 100]

[Marginal heading: Three kinds of error: against the foundation, around the
foundation, and outside the foundation.]

IX. What we say about the articles of faith must also be said about the errors
by which they are attacked. Just as not all truths have the same necessity,
so not all wounds inflicted on the truth are immediately fatal, nor is every
error deadly. Hence three kinds of error can be established: 1. against the
foundation; 2. around the foundation; 3. outside the foundation. An error
against the foundation directly overthrows one or more articles of faith, as,
for example, one that denies Christ's deity and the Trinity of persons. An
error around the foundation does not directly deny a fundamental article but
nevertheless maintains an antithesis whose standing indirectly and by
necessary consequence overthrows that article. An example is someone who
believes in God but does not wish to acknowledge his providence, because when
providence is removed, God is removed. An error outside the foundation either
does not touch the foundation at all or concerns it only by a remote and
obscure consequence. It is occupied with problematic and curious questions
which are neither revealed in the Word nor necessary to know. Such are the
doctrines the apostle calls hay and stubble in 1 Corinthians 2:11. They can
coexist with the true foundation and do not prevent the person who professes
them from being saved, though he loses the reward for his labor.

X. One error directly, primarily, and immediately overthrows the foundation;
another does so indirectly, secondarily, and by consequence. The former is
without doubt more serious than the latter. Again, an error can overthrow the
foundation indirectly by a proximate, evident, and necessary consequence, or
by a remote, obscure, and forced one. The former is properly fatal, but the
latter is not. Thus the Roman Catholics do not directly attack the sufficiency
of Christ's satisfaction, justification by faith, and other doctrines of that
kind. Yet they attack them indirectly and by an evident and necessary
consequence through their errors concerning the merits of works, their own
satisfactions, the sacrifice of the Mass, purgatory, and so forth. They must
be judged no less to overthrow the foundation than those who attack it
directly, who stand in terms of implicit contradiction, where there is an
opposition in an apposition and a contradiction in an adjective.

XI. An error deduced from a doctrine by an obscure and violent consequence,
or an error earnestly and solemnly detested and rejected by those who retain
that doctrine, cannot justly be charged against them. For example, it is
charged against the Reformed that they make God the author of sin; that they
deny God's omnipotence because they refuse to admit the ubiquity of Christ's
body; and that they turn the sacraments into bare signs because they deny
Christ's bodily presence in the Supper and the oral eating of him. These
charges and the like cannot legitimately be ascribed to them, because those
errors do not follow but are merely supposed to follow. Nor are those
consequences evident and innate consequences drawn out of the true doctrine
of the Reformed, but forced consequences contrary to their meaning. Nor are
they received by the Reformed, but condemned and rejected.

XII. One error is verbal and concerns only phrases; another is real and
concerns the doctrines themselves. The former cannot be fundamental, because,
as Hilary says in *On the Trinity* 8, “Heresy lies in the understanding, not
in the Scripture,” and the meaning, not the expression, becomes the offense.
As Jerome says on Galatians 1, “The gospel does not lie in the words of the
Scriptures but in their meaning, not on the surface but in the marrow, not in
the leaves of the expressions but in the root of the sense.”

XIII. It is one thing to speak of theological doctrines and principles and
another to speak of their conclusions and consequences. Knowledge of and
assent to one side or the other of these conclusions are indeed necessary for
theologians to possess a fuller knowledge of divine things and to establish
the true and refute the false. But they are not always necessary for
Christians in general, and Christians can be ignorant of them without

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 47 | IA leaf 101 | PDF p. 101]

danger to salvation. Here, however, when we speak about fundamental articles,
we do not mean those which are explicitly necessary for a mature person, and
especially for a man of God, to know in order to fulfill the measure of his
office and name. We mean precisely those which every believer and Christian
must necessarily believe for salvation.

XIV. Some things have a necessity of means; others only a necessity of
command. The former in themselves and primarily constitute the foundation;
the latter are built upon it secondarily to strengthen and seal the
foundation. The former are necessary absolutely and always, but the latter
only in a certain respect. Salvation cannot be obtained without the former,
but it can be without the latter. Nor does the mere lack of the latter
condemn, but only contempt for them. The sacraments are of this kind.

XV. The body and substance of an article or doctrine must be distinguished
from its mode and circumstances. The substance of a fundamental article
cannot and ought not to be unknown and has always been the same, but the case
of its circumstance or mode is different, for ignorance of this does not
immediately condemn. Hence an error either concerns the substance of the
thing or its mode, circumstances, or degree; it concerns either the fact that
it is, the manner in which it is, or the reason why it is. It is true that
sometimes the thing itself is removed when its mode is overthrown, because
the mode enters into the essence of the thing and partly constitutes it. Thus
in practical matters, one who removes the mode of divine worship established
by God overthrows worship itself, Matthew 15:9. One who removes the mode of
redemption through Christ's ransom and the mode of justification through
faith overthrows redemption and justification themselves. At other times,
however, it is possible for an error to concern the mode and circumstances
while the thing itself remains intact. For example, the Greeks are charged
with error concerning the mode of the procession of the Holy Spirit, although
they confess the mystery of the Trinity and the deity of the Spirit.

XVI. Faith can be considered either according to the fullness and degree of
its light or according to the number of things to be believed. In the former
respect, faith could increase according to the measure of revelation and be
fuller under the New Testament than under the Old. It could not do so in the
latter respect, because in this sense there has always been one faith,
Ephesians 4:5, and “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever,”
Hebrews 13:8.

XVII. The doctrine that Jesus, the son of Mary, is the true Messiah is not a
new article of faith with respect to its substance and in the thesis, since
the ancients believed that the Messiah would come. It is only a determination
and special application of the prophetic oracles in the hypothesis.

XVIII. Some fundamental topics are such in themselves; others become such
only accidentally insofar as they run into some fundamental topic. For
example, circumcision is not fundamental in itself, but it becomes fundamental
when it is imagined to be necessary for salvation and runs into the article
of justification. In this sense the apostle inveighs against the
pseudo-apostles who wished to retain the use of circumcision as people who
were erring fatally, Galatians 5:2–3, and so forth. Likewise, the article
concerning the Supper is not fundamental in itself, but the Roman Catholics
make it so when the Supper is imagined to be a propitiatory sacrifice for
sins.

XIX. These are the properties of fundamental articles. 1. They must be
catholic, because they are required for the catholic faith which is necessary
for every individual who is to be saved, according to the words of the
Athanasian Creed: “Whoever wishes to be saved must above all hold the catholic
faith. Unless one preserves it whole and inviolate, he will perish eternally.”
2. Faith in them must necessarily bring salvation after it; ignorance of them
must be damnable, doubt about them full of danger, and denial of them impious
and heretical. 3. The faithful must foster true agreement concerning them,
and one group must not think differently from another, because anyone who
thinks or speaks differently is subjected to an anathema, Galatians 1:8.
Hence, where there is disagreement in fundamentals, no religious union can
exist. 4. All theological doctrines must be

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 48 | IA leaf 102 | PDF p. 102]

referred to them as to a rule, which the apostle calls “the analogy of faith.”
5. They must be primary and principal truths upon which all others rest as
upon a foundation, and when they are removed faith is utterly overthrown. They
must not be secondary and less principal truths, whose removal only shakes
faith.

[Marginal heading: The criteria of fundamental articles are sought.]

[Marginal heading: 1. From the nature of the doctrines.]

XX. The criteria by which fundamental articles can be distinguished from
nonfundamental ones can be sought, 1. from the nature and condition of the
doctrines themselves. Such are the doctrines that contain the necessary
causes and conditions of salvation, its end, and the means necessary to that
end, because the effect is also removed when its causes are denied, and the
end cannot be attained when its means are denied. Hence, since the grace of
God by which we are chosen, the merit of Christ by which we are redeemed, and
the Spirit by whom we are sanctified are the principal causes of salvation;
faith is its instrumental cause, John 3:16–17; and repentance and conversion
to God are necessary conditions, Hebrews 6:2; Matthew 3:2—we call all these
fundamental doctrines.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Scripture.]

XXI. Second, the criteria can be sought from Scripture's own declaration. For
those doctrines whose knowledge is said to be necessary and saving, and whose
ignorance or denial is fatal, are rightly judged fundamental. Such is the
article concerning the one and triune God, both positively in John 17:3 and
negatively in 1 John 2:23; concerning sin, 1 John 1:10; Ephesians 2:1;
concerning Christ with respect to his person, natures, and offices, 1
Corinthians 3:11; Acts 4:12; 1 John 4:3; Ephesians 1:11–12; concerning the
gospel, Romans 1:16–17; Galatians 1:8–9; concerning faith, Hebrews 11:6; Mark
16:16; concerning justification without works, Romans 3:27; Galatians 2 and
3; concerning sanctification and the worship of God, Ephesians 2:10; Hebrews
12:14; and concerning the resurrection and eternal life, 1 Corinthians 15:14;
2 Timothy 2:8; Romans 10:9; and so forth.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the creed.]

XXII. A third mark of fundamental articles can be sought from the Apostles'
Creed, in which the ancients embraced the sum of fundamental doctrine from
the apostolic writings. Hence the name “creed” was given to it, because it was
like a token and seal of Christianity. Yet the following must be carefully
observed. 1. It cannot be an adequate mark of fundamental articles, because
it treats only theoretical articles which concern faith, not practical ones
which concern worship. 2. The things to be believed are set forth here not in
so many words and explicitly, but implicitly and by consequence and analogy.
For example, although nothing is said about God's grace and Christ's
satisfaction, or about providence, preservation, and similar things, they are
nevertheless easily inferred from what is said. 3. The creed must not be
considered only with respect to its words but with respect to its meaning,
because, as Hilary says, the Scriptures consist not in reading but in
understanding; and, as Jerome says, the fundamentals are not in the words but
in the sense. Therefore, although heretics profess to receive the creed, they
do not actually do so, because they do not retain its true and genuine
meaning. Thus in ancient times Sabellius, Arius, Macedonius, and the other
antitrinitarians vainly professed from the creed faith in the Father, Son, and
Holy Spirit, while undertaking to attack that very faith not by secret tunnels
but by open engines. Their disciples, the Socinians, do the same today. The
Roman Catholics vainly profess faith in the creed as well, since they corrupt
the meaning of its various articles concerning Christ's death and sufferings,
the descent into hell, the catholic church, the forgiveness of sins, and
similar matters.

XXIII. Although theology is not only theoretical but practical, the
Socinians wrongly say that fundamental articles are distinguished from
nonfundamental ones by this single criterion: obedience to be rendered to God
and Christ, or the pursuit of piety and good works. This is because not only
commands concerning things to be done are necessary for salvation, but also
doctrines concerning things to be believed, according to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 49 | IA leaf 103 | PDF p. 103]

John 20:31 and 2 Timothy 3:16. Thus, just as there are practical fundamental
articles, there must also be theoretical ones.

XXIV. Although some of the orthodox establish more articles of faith and
others fewer, they do not disagree in reality but only in their words and
manner of presentation. Some embrace them more generally and summarily, while
others recount them more particularly and in detail. Hence some describe the
foundation of salvation by the single truth concerning Jesus Christ crucified
as our redeemer from 1 Corinthians 2:2, but this truth embraces many others.
Others describe it by two things, the knowledge of God and of Christ, from
John 17:3. Others extend it to four heads, both theoretical and practical, and
others to six. Nevertheless, everyone agrees that the following are
fundamental articles: the doctrines concerning Holy Scripture, God-breathed
and the sole and perfect rule of faith; the one and triune God; Christ the
redeemer and his most complete satisfaction; sin and death as its wages; the
law and its inability to save; justification through faith; the necessity of
grace and good works for sanctification and the worship of God; the church;
the resurrection of the dead, the last judgment, and eternal life; and any
other doctrines connected with these. All of them are so bound together that
they depend upon one another, and not one can be taken away without the others
falling.

[Marginal heading: The question concerning the number of fundamental articles
is rash and useless.]

XXV. The question concerning the number of fundamental articles, which our
adversaries repeatedly raise against us, is rash because Scripture defines
nothing precisely on this point. It is also found useless and unnecessary for
the controversies between us and them. We need not know individually how many
such articles there are if we can prove that they err fundamentally in one or
more of them, as can easily be demonstrated against the Roman Catholics,
Socinians, Anabaptists, and similar heretics.

XXVI. Nor does it follow from this either that Scripture itself labors under
an imperfection in necessary matters or that we lack a standard of
ecclesiastical communion. Scripture does not cease to contain everything
necessary for salvation in the fullest manner even though it does not define
their number precisely, and the truth of the fundamental articles which ought
to serve us as a standard is sufficiently gathered from the criteria given
above.

XXVII. If the orthodox sometimes establish that the fundamental articles are
few, this must not be understood absolutely and without qualification. It is
true both with respect to the primary heads, which, taken together, are few in
comparison with the rest, and in comparison with the Roman Catholics, who
increase them beyond measure. They turn the canons of the church, the publicly
received opinions of the schools, and the traditions of the fathers into
articles of faith from which no one can depart without the charge of heresy.
