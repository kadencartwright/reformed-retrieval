QUESTION II

Are There Atheists Properly So Called? We Deny.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. Since the number of atheists in the world today is so great, at first sight
it may seem surprising that the existence of such people could either be
called into doubt or denied. But once certain distinctions have been made, it
will readily become clear in what sense it can be true both that there are
many atheists and that there are no atheists.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 161 | IA leaf 215 | PDF p. 215]

[Marginal heading: The speculative or practical atheist.]

II. First, one atheist is *speculative* and another *practical*. The former is
such with respect to belief, since he does not acknowledge God; the latter is
such with respect to morals and life, since, though acknowledging God, he does
not worship him but lives as though God did not exist. He may also be such by
desire, because he would wish that God did not exist. For although there is no
practice which does not presuppose some knowledge, and no knowledge—at least
none concerned with the worship of God—which does not in some way tend toward
practice, nevertheless no practical atheism arises without corrupt knowledge
and a corrupted judgment of the mind, and no speculative atheism fails also
to draw corruption of practice after it. Yet nothing prevents the name from
being taken from the predominant feature, so that atheism which consists more
in boastful words and blasphemies is called speculative, while that which is
occupied more with the impiety of deeds is called practical. We do not deny
that there are many practical atheists; we deny that there are speculative
atheists.

[Marginal heading: The speculative atheist.]

III. Second, the speculative atheist is either *direct and explicit* or
*indirect and interpretive*. A direct atheist casts off every knowledge,
sense, and belief in deity. An indirect atheist attributes such things to God,
or denies such things about him, that God is denied by necessary consequence.
For example, if someone denies God's providence and justice, then even though
he professes to acknowledge God, by that very fact he denies him, because God
cannot exist without providence. We do not treat the indirect here, but the
direct.

IV. Third, the direct atheist is either one who disputes outwardly and even
maliciously denies God contrary to his own sense; or one who doubts inwardly,
who is troubled for a time by doubts about whether God exists and seems to
succumb to them momentarily in a paroxysm of temptation; or one who
deliberately strives after total insensibility to deity (ἀναισθησίαν); or
one who is plainly and certainly persuaded in his heart that there is no God.
We do not treat the former, whom we do not deny can sometimes be found, but
the last, whom we judge cannot exist. The question here, therefore, is whether
there are atheists who are not practical but speculative, not indirect and
interpretive but direct; not those who outwardly dispute or deny, or who
inwardly doubt and try to persuade themselves that there is no God, but those
who expressly believe this in the heart and profess it with the mouth. We deny
this.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that there are no atheists.]

V. The reasons are these. 1. There is an implanted knowledge of God and a
sense of divinity in the human being, which can no more be absent from a human
being than the rational intellect. Thus a person cannot strip it off without
stripping off his own humanity, as was proved before, Topic I, Question 3. If
Scripture denies to sinners a saving and practical knowledge by which they
are converted, 1 John 2:4, it does not thereby deny an ineffectual and
theoretical knowledge. And if an ignorance arising from a corrupt disposition
is attributed to them, 1 Thessalonians 4:5; Ephesians 4:17–18, an ignorance of
pure negation cannot likewise be attributed to them.

VI. 2. The work of the law is written in the human being, Romans 2:14, and
there is a natural *synteresis* and conscience in which God has erected his
throne. This cannot be extinguished except together with the light of reason.
For although people of desperate impiety often attempt to cast it off, they
cannot. Though for a time they may seem to have formed a callus over it and
conscience may be lulled to sleep, it finally awakens and drives sharper
goads into their hearts. Thus, willing or unwilling, they are held ensnared
and from time to time thoroughly perceive the one whom they desire not to
know, as was seen in Caligula, Nero, and not a few others. Nor can it be
objected that this conscience is not always joined with some knowledge or fear
of God, because the scourge of conscience can remain from fear of human
beings. For although this can have a place in known crimes, in hidden crimes
which escape human beings and which are perpetrated by those who

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 162 | IA leaf 216 | PDF p. 216]

acknowledge no superior on earth, whence could such terror arise except from a
sense of God's justice, which strikes their consciences the more forcefully
the more they attempt to flee, so that something divine (θεῖόν τι) must
truly underlie it?

VII. 3. God has revealed himself so clearly in his works that human beings
can find him even by feeling after him, Acts 17:26–27, and cannot open their
eyes without the majesty and splendor of so great a deity immediately
affecting them. The expression “if perhaps” (εἰ ἄρα), which the apostle uses,
does not call the evidence and certainty of the divine revelation into doubt,
but refers to the dullness and negligence of those who seek, as in Acts 8:22.
Likewise, what is added about God's presence, by which he is not far from each
one of us, does not signify a special promise of grace to faithful worshipers
of God, but indicates a benefit of providence common to all human beings.

VIII. This acquired knowledge of God is customarily obtained in three ways:
causality, eminence, and negation. By the way of causality, we understand the
cause from the effects and ascend from secondary causes to the first, Psalm
94:9: “Will the planter of the ear not hear, and will the former of the eye
not see?” By the way of eminence, whatever perfection exists in created things
we attribute to God eminently and preeminently (κατ’ ἐξοχὴν). By the way
of negation, we remove from God the things which indicate imperfection in
creatures, as when he is called invisible, immortal, and immutable. By the way
of negation we arrive at a knowledge of the negative attributes; by the way
of eminence we know the positive attributes; and by the way of causality we
ascend to a knowledge of the relative attributes.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

IX. When it is said in Psalm 14:1 that “the fool says in his heart that there
is no God,” this describes not so much a certain and firm conviction of an
atheistic person who denies God as the doubt and effort of an impious person
striving to stifle this knowledge. Hence it does not say *he believes and
determines*, but *he says*—that is, he silently speaks with himself and tries
to persuade himself of this. 2. A direct speculative atheist cannot be
understood here, because the passage treats the impious and sinners in
general, as is clear from comparison with Romans 3. But it is certain that all
sinners cannot be called atheists in this sense. 3. The denial signified here
is not so much of God's existence as of his providence. This is clear from
Psalm 10, where what verse 4 says about the impious person—“in his loftiness he
does not inquire; all his thoughts are, There is no God”—is referred in verse
11 to the denial of providence: “He says in his heart, God has forgotten; he
has hidden his face; he will never see it.” Hence Owen rightly says in book 3
of his *Epigrams*, on the atheist:

The fool says in his heart, “There is no God”;
he said it, yet no fool believed it.
If no fool denies that God exists, then who is the atheist?
One who wishes that there were no God.

X. An outward denial of God, or merely a wandering thought and doubt about
God, is one thing; a fixed and constant denial in the heart is another. We do
not deny that the former can exist in a human being, but we deny the latter.

XI. Whatever authors say about the suffocation and obliteration of the
natural light for some time in a paroxysm of madness, and whatever is said
about the cauterization, lethargy, and insensibility (ἀναισθησίᾳ) of
conscience, ought not to be referred to the total extinction of this natural
knowledge or to the certainty of a contrary assent and conviction. It should
be referred to some suppression and obscuring of that light and knowledge,
by which impious people cast off an active thought about God as far as
possible and try to persuade themselves that there is no God; nor do they
believe in or dread the vengeance of God the judge.

XII. The Gentiles are called “without God” (ἄθεοι), Ephesians 2:12, not
because they acknowledged no

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 163 | IA leaf 217 | PDF p. 217]

deity at all, but because they lacked the knowledge of the true deity. For one
who does not have the true God by that very fact has no God, and polytheism
(πολυθεότης) is really atheism (ἀθεότης).

XIII. If any among the ancients were stigmatized by the infamous name
*atheist*, they were not so much enemies of every deity as despisers of idols
and fictitious deities. Apuleius and Aulus Gellius testify to this concerning
Socrates, who was judged the wisest of all Greece by the oracle of Apollo.

XIV. It is one thing for an active thought about God to be absent for a time
from the mind of the atheist through negligence and malice; it is another for
the knowledge of God to be absent. That knowledge has the character of a
permanent disposition or habit originally impressed upon the mind and is then
formed by reasoning from both the book of nature and the book of Scripture.
The former can sometimes occur, but not the latter.

XV. As to the examples of atheists who seem to have abjured every sense of
deity, as is said of Julius Caesar Vanini and others who persisted in their
obstinacy up to the last breath of life, we answer that they indeed denied God
outwardly and inwardly attempted with all zeal to stifle this sense, but it
cannot be established that they were fully persuaded of the denial. Indeed,
if their conduct is weighed, it can be discerned clearly enough that they were
not free from the inward goads of conscience, whatever they feigned outwardly,
as they sought glory in an obstinate profession of atheism. Nor is it an
obstacle that they remained in that madness even to death. For because the
human heart is deep and desperate, they could profess this contrary to their
inward sense and the dictate of conscience in order to win among their
surviving associates the praise of constancy and invincible courage.

XVI. Although no one knows what is in a person except the person's spirit
which is in him, 1 Corinthians 2:11, this does not prevent us from establishing
with certainty that no one can entirely cast every sense of deity out of the
heart, just as no one can ever strip off conscience. For these are self-evident
principles which depend upon the constitution of the human being. Thus, even
if someone were to protest that he knows and understands nothing, that he does
not perceive the truth of the principle “nothing can both be and not be at the
same time,” and that he has no law of nature and no movement of an accusing or
excusing conscience, universal experience, reason, and Scripture can rightly
be opposed to that utterly vain skeptic who protests falsely and contrary to
the inward sense of his mind. We may thereby determine that, whatever he says,
he does violence to his own conscience.
