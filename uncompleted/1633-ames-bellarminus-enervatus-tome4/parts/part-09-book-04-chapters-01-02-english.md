## Book IV

### On Free Choice

#### Chapter I. On the nature of free choice

##### Thesis I

1. Bellarmine. *Free choice is the free power, from among those things which
conduce toward some end, to choose one rather than another, or to accept or
reject one and the same thing at one's discretion—a power attributed to an
intelligent nature to the great glory of God.*

Protestant reply. Although this definition can bear a sense which gives no
cause for dispute about it, it is nevertheless not precise, nor can it be
defended in the sense in which Bellarmine explains it, as will appear from
what follows. The simple and true definition of free choice is that it is “the
faculty of acting from deliberation.” Bellarmine himself admits this in
chapter 17: “For the essence of freedom, it is enough that someone have a
choice between different things and that the selection be made with the full
and perfect judgment of reason.”

2. Bellarmine. *For the constitution of free choice, freedom from neces-*

[Printed p. 282 | Google Books PA282 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*sity is altogether required; freedom from compulsion is not sufficient.*

Protestant reply. These points are stated by Bellarmine more confusedly than
the title “explanation” promised. For I. Compulsion is a necessity; it
therefore ought not to have been opposed to necessity. II. It is slanderously
attributed to our theologians that they maintain only freedom from compulsion.
For those who use that phrase always presuppose the will's freedom from the
necessity of a natural determination to one thing. Bellarmine himself
interprets the same phrase in approximately this way when it is produced from
Thomas in chapter 6, just as it is most frequently employed not only by Thomas
but also by all the early Scholastics. III. Bellarmine himself concedes in
chapter 6 that “conditional necessity is not incompatible with freedom,” and
in chapter 11 he says that “the will is moved necessarily by the final
judgment of reason and in the manner of a natural cause, which cannot fail to
act when its object is present.” IV. Bradwardine, book 3, chapter 10, variously
distinguishes necessity and freedom: “There is,” he says, “natural necessity,
fated necessity, the violent necessity of compulsion, and the spontaneous
necessity of abiding stability, or of firmness, which is also called—though
not so properly—the necessity of immutability. Freedom is spoken of in
opposite ways corresponding to these. Natural, fated, and violent necessity
are incompatible with freedom, but spontaneous necessity is by no means
incompatible with freedom.” The same Bradwardine, in book 3, chapter 1,
teaches “that God can in a certain manner necessitate every created will to
its own free act, and to a free cessation and withdrawal from an act.” We
nevertheless concede that free choice, in what it does, is free from every
necessity, so that, properly speaking, it cannot act necessarily with respect
to the exercise of its act, although with respect to the divine ordination it
acts certainly and infallibly. This certainty is called necessity by many
others and also by Bellarmine, book 3, chapters 12 and 13.

3. Bellarmine. *Freedom is formally in the will and radically in reason.*

Protestant reply. Freedom of the will is said to have its root in reason for
no other reason than that the object is presented, for acting or not acting,
with the indifference of intellectual cognition. From this, however, it
follows that freedom of the will does not exclude those predefinitions and
predeterminations of God which the understanding cannot penetrate, or about
whose indifference it cannot judge.

4. Bellarmine. *The will's choice depends necessarily upon the final judgment
of practical reason.*

Protestant reply. I. If this is true, then what was said before is false: “For
free choice there is required freedom from every necessity altogether.” II.
This position was publicly condemned among the Roman Catholics long ago. Thus
Azorius the Jesuit,

[Printed p. 283 | Google Books PA283 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

book 4, chapter 23: “There are three Parisian articles. 1. When universal and
particular knowledge are present, the will cannot choose the opposite. Error
1. Or when the major and minor premises in a syllogism are actual, the will
cannot will the opposite. Error 2. If reason is right, the will is also right.
Error 3. When the will is in its final disposition, a man cannot will the
opposite.” These articles were condemned by Stephen, Bishop of Paris, with
the consent of the theologians of that university, in the year 1226. IV.
Scotus and the Scotists refute this assertion with firm arguments, as may be
seen in John de Rada, Arragonius, and the Bishop of Badajoz in the controversies
between Thomas and Scotus, part 2, controversy 15, article 3. One of those
arguments is that “after the final judgment, the will remains free.”

5. Bellarmine. *Free choice is not a power partly passive and partly active,
but simply and absolutely active.*

Protestant reply. I. From this one statement it is sufficiently apparent that
free choice is the Jesuits' God or idol. For this is the difference between
God's will and man's, as Álvarez observes in disputation 91, number 13: “The
divine will is pure act, possessing freedom through its essence; but the
created will is not pure act. Rather, it has a participated freedom which
includes something of potentiality, and therefore, in order to go forth into
act, it must be premoved.” II. From this it is also apparent that, whatever
else Bellarmine may profess, he holds with the Pelagians that man's free
choice has not truly been injured by sin and does not need to be healed by
grace.

### Chapter II

#### On the Harmony of Providence, or Divine Cooperation, with Free Choice

1. Bellarmine. *One fairly suitable way of explaining this harmony is the way
of Scotus, Gabriel, and others: that God's concurrence imprints nothing and
does not operate upon our will, but flows immediately into the effect. It is
like two men carrying an immense stone which one could not carry. God and the
will operate simultaneously, at precisely the same moment in time. Yet God
operates because the will operates—not conversely—because, when he created a
free will, he freely bound himself in a certain manner to this course.*

Protestant reply. I. This way is falsely called suitable. For I. It withdraws
all operations of the human will—or the will with respect to its operation—
from the dominion of providence and the efficacious government of God. II. It
destroys the subordination of the first and secondary causes in their
operations. III. It makes man not God's subject but his associate. IV. It
makes the government or direction

[Printed p. 284 | Google Books PA284 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

or direction of human actions, and the things which follow from them, depend
first and principally upon man's choice. V. It represents God as dependent
upon man's nod, even in those things which have chief importance in the
administration of the whole world. VI. It reverses and overturns that piety
which God taught us: “If the Lord wills, I shall do this or that.” James 4.
And it teaches God what he, as though brought under orders, ought to say: “If
man wills to do this or that.” VII. It is contrary, as Álvarez shows in
disputation 18, not only to Aristotle, Augustine, Thomas, and the Thomists,
but also to the Tridentine-Roman Catechism itself. In its treatment of the
first article of the creed, number 22, these words occur: “By his providence,
God impels those things which are moved and do something toward motion and
action by an inward power.”

2. Bellarmine. *The other way of reconciling human freedom with divine
cooperation—and perhaps the more probable way, according to Thomas's
position—is that God moves the operative powers of free causes and applies
them to the work.*

Protestant reply. I. Bellarmine shows clearly enough here that he plays the
skeptical sophist in a most weighty question: “perhaps the more probable.” II.
This is the true position, which Bellarmine tried to refute earlier in chapter
14, but he distorts it in the following manner by a sophistical, obscure, and
absurd interpretation.

Bellarmine. 1. *The power impressed by God in a transient manner while the
secondary cause operates is not distinguished from the natural power itself;
it is that same power insofar as God uses it to perfect that action.*

Protestant reply. I. Here Bellarmine, contrary to all logic, confounds the act
of the efficient cause with its effect. II. He holds that the secondary cause
is moved to the work, or to operating, while it operates, not beforehand. This
is plainly contrary to the position he undertook to explain. III. He says
ambiguously that “God uses the natural power to perfect the action.” For this
can be understood either to mean that it is moved by God to produce the whole
action, or only to perfect that action which it began without God's motion.
IV. The question concerns that first motion from which the first movement of
the will proceeds. This, however, can in no way be the will's own power unless
it is both prior and posterior to itself.

Bellarmine. 2. *The mode or condition in the will according to which God's
motion is received in it is nothing other than a certain negative
determination, preceding both God's influx and the act elicited by the will:
namely, that the will allows itself to be moved by the object proposed to it
by reason, or does not allow it.*

Protestant reply. By sleights of words, Bellarmine has at last brought the
matter to this: that divine operation depends upon human permission.

[Printed p. 285 | Google Books PA285 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

But it cannot be conceived by any reasoning that a man's permission is the
determination of the divine operation toward this or that. Nor does it make
any sense that God applies the will to doing this or that because it allows
itself to be moved in this way by the object, or does not allow it. For if the
will allows itself to be moved by the object, no motion of any mover follows
from this except the object's; if it does not allow it, no determinate motion
follows, but only the privation of motion. The sum is this: in Bellarmine's
position, God does whatever he does concerning the actual exercise of free
choice only on sufferance, and he has it subject to his providence no farther
than the will itself permits out of its liberality. One thing, however,
Bellarmine ought to have explained: how the matter proceeds in evil actions,
when God permits and man permits; and whether God equally moves the will to
evil actions and to good actions, and equally leaves the will to itself in
good actions and in evil ones, with respect to the cooperation of his
providence.
