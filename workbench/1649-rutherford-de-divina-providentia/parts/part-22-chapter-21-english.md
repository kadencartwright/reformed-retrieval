### GOD does not harden human beings by bare permission. On the manner of
hardening; the Jesuits and Arminians are summoned to answer

The sixth argument for God's active providence concerning sin is as follows.

[Marginal note: Argument VI.]

*He who by a positive action so blinds human beings and hardens their hearts
that the sinner's passive blindness and hardening is nevertheless, on the part
of the hardened person, a most grievous sin, does not merely permit but wills
sin to occur while he permits it.*

[Marginal note: On the sinner's passive hardening.]

*But GOD, by a positive action in the manner just stated, blinds human beings
and hardens their hearts.*

*Therefore GOD does not merely permit but wills sin to occur while he permits
it.*

The reason for the major premise is that if a positive action of GOD intervenes
in the sinner's hardening, a positive volition of GOD, and not bare negative
permission, must intervene whenever a person culpably hardens himself.

[Marginal note: The manner of hardening must be investigated cautiously in the
Fathers, especially the Greek Fathers.]

I do not wish this argument to be altogether the same as the preceding one.
For although a certain hardening is the punishment of sin, hardening in general,
from which this argument is drawn, is not so. The first hardening, which
distinguishes the converted or Elect person from the unconverted and Reprobate
person, although materially an evil, is not formally an evil of punishment
comparatively inflicted as punishment on this person who is not softened and
converted rather than on the other whom God converts. It is instead an act of
GOD'S most free good pleasure, which the Savior refers to GOD'S sheer good
pleasure (εὐδοκίαν), Matt. 11:26–27; Paul to GOD'S good pleasure, Rom. 9:15,
18; and James to God's free will, James 1:18.

The reason for the major premise, as well as for the assumption, depends upon
the manner in which God blinds or hardens human beings. We think that in this
matter the Fathers—especially Chrysostom and the Greek Fathers, who were not
well practiced against the Pelagian heresy—are by no means to be trusted.

[Chapter XXI; printed page 288; IA PDF page 348; BSB canvas 340]

(a) Origen and (b) Theodoret, to whom I add (c) Jerome, maintain that GOD
hardens human beings by delaying punishment and by longsuffering. But although
this is one manner in which God hardens, it is plainly insufficient. The reason
is that God hardens the reprobate in a very different manner from the Elect and
Regenerate. Something peculiar that pertains to the execution of the decree of
reprobation is intimated in the blindness of the Reprobate, but not in the
blindness of believers, as is evident from John 12:39: *Therefore they could
not believe, because Isaiah had said in chapter 40, He blinded their eyes and
hardened their hearts,* and so forth. But God also uses longsuffering toward
believers by the very fact that he does not harden them in this way,
although they have deserved hardening just as much as the Reprobate. 2. The
delay of punishment in itself, unless God delays punishment with this end—that
human beings be hardened—is not an act of hidden judgment but rather an act of
mercy. Yet hardening is an act of hidden judgment executing the free decree of
reprobation and is sometimes an act of vindicatory justice because of crimes
committed before. But the Adversaries will not say that God intends hardening.

[Marginal references: (a) Origen, book 3, *On First Principles*. (b) Theodoret,
question 12 on Exodus. (c) Jerome, letter to Hedibia, reply to question 10.]

2. (d) Tertullian, (e) Origen, and (f) Chrysostom maintain that GOD hardens by
forsaking and by leaving a person to his own will. Thus Chrysostom uses the
example of a commander who withdraws when a battle grows fierce and hands the
army over to its enemies, not by driving it into slaughter but by depriving it
of his aid. But the most learned Dr. (g) Rivet rightly says: *Tertullian,
Origen, and Chrysostom say something, but not everything that needed to be
said.* For if God hardens negatively, he does not harden at all. What is it to
harden by doing nothing? A stone hardens a person by doing nothing. Indeed, I
say that the Jesuit (h) William Baily rightly infers from this opinion of the
Fathers a most absurd conclusion, which he nevertheless holds: *From this it
may clearly be judged that hardening proceeds not from God but from us.* For
hardening does not at all proceed from one who hardens by not acting. Thus
Scripture deceives and makes sport of us when it says so many times that *GOD
hardens sinners.* 2. *Such a commander who withdraws from battle* (says the
most distinguished David Pareus) *would be a betrayer of his army, not a
Judge.* People are far too bold here in binding God by laws by which a human
being ought to be bound. For a human being who hardened another human being by
not softening him would certainly be a culpable cause of the hardening. GOD,
therefore, is here above the law. Indeed, (k) Bellarmine says that *it is a most
grievous punishment when a person, as punishment for a past-*

here* (he says) *is for GOD not to will to have mercy, nor is anything inflicted
by him whereby a person becomes worse, but only that whereby he does not become
better.* Again: *By forsaking, not by helping.* Again: *Not by imparting
wickedness but by not imparting mercy.* But it is easy to answer on Augustine's
behalf: Augustine's negative hardening indeed denies an infusion of wickedness
into the hardened person, but not every positive act of GOD. Augustine rightly
explains this elsewhere: *It must be believed with absolute certainty that
Pharaoh was so often hardened against GOD'S precepts, not by GOD'S violence,
but by his own iniquity and indomitable pride.* I know that Augustine, sermon
88 *On Time*, says that God hardens through indulgence, but in the same place
he explains himself: *Pharaoh was hardened not by GOD'S violence but by his own
impiety.* Pererius, on Exodus 11, says that the Elect are softened by this
indulgence of GOD.

[Marginal references: (l) Augustine, *To Simplicianus*, book 2, question 2;

(m) Gregory, (n) Damascene, (o) Isidore, and (p) Theophylact adhere too closely
to bare permission. *Especially the Greeks* (says (q) Pareus), *who had less
business with the Pelagians.* (r) Origen can be explained otherwise, since he
uses the analogy of the sun, which, he says, appears to possess a twofold power:
it both illuminates and hardens according as it falls upon different subjects.
From this it can rightly be inferred that GOD acts in hardening. And (s) Cyril
says that GOD *turns an impious will so that it may be the occasion of good*,
which he illustrates by the example of Joseph's brothers, “taking it as a
ground” (εἰς ὑπόθεσιν λαμβάνει). It is well known how (t) Tertullian answers
Hermogenes when the latter objects as follows: *Either he could have corrected
matter but did not will to, or he willed to but could not. God is weak if he
could not, and if he would not, he is evil because he favored evil.* His answer
is: *Thus GOD is found to be not the author but the approver of evil.* On
Tertullian's account, then, GOD is no more than a bare approver or observer, as
though, while evil was being done, God had no will at all concerning evil's
existence until after it already existed. This was refuted above.

Amazing is the audacity of the adversaries who maintain that GOD'S permission
in hardening is sheer non-action and that this is taught by the Fathers. Yet it
is not taught by the Fathers. For those who acknowledge a penal withdrawal,
and that with a definite prior intention of GOD to punish antecedent sins by
that withdrawal, do not acknowledge that permissive hardening is non-action.
For although the denial of grace—when that grace is denied, the one to whom it
is denied cannot but sin—is a non-action, the intention-*

[Chapter XXI; printed page 290; IA PDF page 350; BSB canvas 342]

*and will to punish is nevertheless not a bare negation or negative action. Yet
the Fathers here acknowledge a will to punish: (u) Augustine, (x) Chrysostom,
(y) Jerome, (z) Fulgentius, (a) Anselm, (b) Damascene, (c) Isidore, (d) Hugh of
St. Victor, (e) Theodoret, (f) Theophylact, (g) Origen, (h) Basil, (i) Ambrose,
and (k) Gregory. I appeal to the Adversaries. (l) Cardinal Toletus asks: *Why
does GOD blind? I answer,* he says, *that we may fear, knowing that because of
our sins God denies us his aid by which sins would be prevented.* The great
(m) Cajetan says: *GOD, the author of every evil of punishment, therefore
blinded; for blindness is punishment, and it is a deprivation of the power to
see, that is, of the gratuitous aid in the intellect by which it can see with
the eyes of the mind—and hardening of the heart is also punishment,* and so
forth. *This desertion is the most grievous punishment,* Bellarmine says. So
(n) Vasquez and (o) Gregory of Valencia. So (p) Driedo, (q) Suárez, and the
Jesuit (r) Theodore Smising. Moreover, (s) Canus and (t) Michael de Medina add
four positive acts to permission in the hardening of the sinner: 1. That GOD
takes away the light and sweetness of grace. 2. That GOD uses Satan as an
instrument for executing his judgments. 3. That he supplies occasions by which
they might be corrected, astonishing signs by which they might be convinced,
or grants the preaching of the Gospel and benefits by which they might be
converted, all of which they abuse through their wickedness. But we leave it to
be examined whether (u) Vasquez, following Canus and the pontifical doctrine,
does not rightly make an objection about GOD'S slumbering providence: *For this
second manner to be true,* he says, *it must be understood through permission
alone, since GOD instructs Satan in no other way than by allowing him.* I grant
that the Fathers—such as Chrysostom, homily 67 on John; Augustine, letter 105 to
Sixtus; and Gregory, *Morals*, book 13, treatise 11—contend that GOD hardens by
withdrawing softening-*

*grace but superadd other manners.* Yet Pererius adds, on Exodus 11,
disputation 6: *But beyond the withdrawal of divine grace, something more
contributes to hardening.* It surpasses all amazement that the Jesuits and
Arminians draw Augustine over to their side here. For today Bellarmine, Baily,
Theodore Smising, Vasquez, Jacob Arminius, the pseudo-Lutheran Balthasar
Meisner, and the Socinians seize Julian the Pelagian's empty evasions from the
mouth of Pelagius and Julian.

[Marginal note: The Adversaries seize upon the evasions of Pelagius.]

Julian's first distinction was that *GOD hardens sinners not by effecting, not
by positively inclining, but by permitting.* Julian's second distinction was
that *GOD hardens through patience*; Meisner says materially (ὑλικῶς) or
objectively; Corvinus, Arminius's follower, says occasionally, through benefits,
scourges, and miracles, but *by tolerating through patience, not by hardening
through omnipotence.* Julian's third distinction: God hardens not by willing
sin to exist but by ordering sin after it already exists. Julian's fourth
distinction was that GOD willed to foreknow that human beings would be
hardened, but did not predestine that they be hardened. 5. Julian's evasion:
God does not intend the sinner's hardening in any way but his repentance. 6.
Evasion: hardening is just and praiseworthy if hardening is punishment. 7.
Evasion: hardening is not simultaneously sin and the punishment of sin. 8. The
Pelagians' evasion: sin is evil, and likewise that sin exists is evil, and
hardening must be treated in the same way. 9. Evasion: GOD negatively permits
evil wills to rush into this evil object rather than that one but does not
incline evil wills wherever he wills. 10. Evasion: sinners properly harden
themselves, but GOD does not. 11. Evasion: God is a participant in iniquity if
he is in any manner a properly so-called cause in the hardening of human
beings. 12. A person's free choice is destroyed if God hardens human beings by
a just judgment. 13. The hardening of this person and not that one does not
flow from GOD'S inscrutable good pleasure but from the person's free choice.
14. Even those judicially hardened by GOD are free not to be hardened if it
seems so to free choice.

Unless I prove from Augustine's writings both these fourteen evasions of Julian
and the Pelagians and that they are the same evasions as those of the Jesuits
and Arminians, I will lose this very case.

The first evasion is expressly Pelagian: *Did* (he says) *the Philistines and
Arabs come to devastate the land of Judah without their own will-*

[Chapter XXI; printed page 292; IA PDF page 352; BSB canvas 344]

*or did they come by their own will in such a way that it was falsely written
that the Lord stirred up their spirit to do this? Indeed, both are true*
(contrary to what the Pelagians think), *because they both came by their own
will, and yet the Lord stirred up their spirit. For the Almighty acts even upon
the motion of their will in human hearts, so that through them he may do what
he himself willed to do through them—he who does not know how to will anything
at all unjustly.*

—In the same place, (x): *As far as I judge, it is sufficiently manifested
that GOD works in human hearts to incline their wills wherever he wills,
whether to good things by his mercy or to evil things according to their
deserts, and thus by his judgment, sometimes open and sometimes hidden, but
always just.* Likewise: *You hear this* (Julian) *and ask pointlessly how GOD'S
“handing over” is to be understood; you labor hard to show that he hands over
by forsaking* (that is, by not acting—and the Jesuits and Arminians labor much
at this).

[Marginal references: (x) Book 5 *Against Julian*, chapter 3; Augustine, same
place. (y) Vasquez, First Part, disputation 45, chapter 10, no. 60. (z) Prosper,
on behalf of Augustine, book 1.]

Julian's second evasion also is: *But what is it* (Julian) *that you say—that
when they are said to be handed over to their desires, they are to be
understood as abandoned through divine patience, not forced into sins by
power?* Thus Julian quibbles. And so the Jesuits and Arminians deny that God
hardens human beings by an act of Omnipotence. Augustine answers: *As though
the same Apostle had not placed these two together, both patience and power,
when he says, “But God, willing to show wrath and demonstrate his power, bore
with vessels of wrath in great patience.”* This is what (y) Vasquez says on
behalf of the rest: *GOD hardens not merely permissively, but also because,
inwardly and outwardly, he offers certain occasions by which he knows for
certain that a person will be provoked to evil—not with the intention that the
person whom GOD is said to harden should fall, but indeed with the sole
intention of bringing him to repentance.* This is to use Julian's style. God
hardens through patience while intending the hardened person's obedience; he
does not compel by power while intending punishment. But let the reader judge
whether God, formally as a Judge who punishes, intends neither punishment nor
punishing. On that account God, formally acting justly and mercifully pardoning
sins, would will or intend neither a work of justice nor a merciful pardon. (z)
Prosper demonstrates the opposite against the Pelagians in his reply on behalf
of Augustine.

[Marginal note: The Pelagians, Jesuits, and Arminians teach that GOD hardens in

The Pelagians' fourth evasion, which is also the Jesuits', is that GOD hardens
from foreknowledge but not from preordination. (d) Augustine says: *But they
say, as you write* (the Pelagians), *that no one can be aroused by the goads of

GOD'S will by which many are left to their natural hardness, no hardened person
ought to be reproved. (e) Augustine treats this argument at greater length in
the same place. And (f) Prosper, teaching against the Pelagians on Augustine's

against God's will, nothing but God's will was fulfilled.*

The fifth evasion is that GOD does not intend hardening when he hardens, but
obedience. So (g) Augustine. *But you will say* (Julian)-*

*that the Apostle says, “GOD'S goodness leads you to repentance.” It is agreed
that this is true, but* (Augustine says in answer) *he leads the one whom he
predestined.* Hence Julian, who in the preceding chapter had contended that God
*hardens through patience and tolerance alone*, infers that GOD intends and
awaits the repentance and obedience of the hardened person. It is so
everywhere. (h) Arminius cites this very text to prove that when GOD hardens the
reprobate by the external proclamation of the Gospel, he intends their
conversion, Rom. 2: *Do you not know that GOD'S kindness leads you to
repentance?* So also the Jesuit (l) Vasquez, who, contrary to all reason,
maintains that God hardens penally and yet does not intend penal hardening, but
obedience. Yet what Judge, I ask, formally as a Judge punishes on account of sin
in the very act of sinning and intends not punishment but reward and the good
of obedience, even while he punishes?

(l) Vasquez, above.]

The sixth evasion is that hardening is not just if hardening is punishment. So
also is the eleventh, namely, that God is a participant in iniquity if he is
properly a cause of hardening. It is found in (m) Augustine: *But is anything
done* (when he punishes sin with sin) *by error, or is anything judged or done
unjustly or rashly? Far be it! But it was not said to him in vain: “Your
judgments are like the great deep.”* And (n) in the same place: *But as an
intelligent and acute man you say* (Julian) *that lust is just and ought to be
proclaimed with an encomium of praise if it punished the person who did not
obey GOD by not obeying him. If you were wisely discerning here* (Julian), *you
would surely see that the lower part of man resisting the higher and better
part is iniquity; yet the unjust person was justly punished by the iniquity of
his own flesh.* From this Julian infers in the same place that *lust as the
avenger of sin is to be praised*, which is absurd. Hence Augustine contends
that it was just that the Gentiles polluted themselves with Sodomitic and male
lust as punishment for idolatry, as in Romans 1: *GOD therefore hands them over

the bodies of human beings, but also in their very hearts.* Julian infers in
response that the Sodomitic lust is therefore praiseworthy because it is the
just punishment of sin, which is utterly absurd. God therefore acts unjustly

The seventh evasion is that hardening is not at the same time sin and the
punishment of sin, which the Jesuits and Arminians teach, as we heard above.
And this is Julian's view in (o) Augustine: *Is not* (Augustine says to Julian)

fear you”? Is it not sin and the punishment of sin where God is again told,
“Behold, you were angry, and we sinned”?* And so forth.

[Marginal reference: (o) Augustine, *Against Julian*, book 5, chapter 3.]

The eighth evasion is that sin is not good, nor is it good that sin should
exist. So (p) Arminius: *Augustine's saying* (he says), *when rigorously
examined, cannot stand—for it is not good that evils* (of fault) *should exist;
but God makes every good thing. Therefore God causes evils to exist.* Thus (q)
Augustine's *Enchiridion*: *For this reason the works of the Lord are great,
sought out in all his wills, so that in a marvelous and ineffable manner

will. For it would not happen if he did not permit it, nor does he permit it
unwillingly but willingly; nor would one who is good permit evils to be done
unless, being omnipotent, he could make good even from evil.* And (r) Augustine:
*I ask you, reader, to attend to the nerve of the argument: surely if we permit
those over whom we have power to commit crimes before our eyes, we will be
guilty with them; but how innumerable are the things he permits to be done
before his eyes, which he certainly would by no means permit if he did not will
them.* Hence it is clear that Julian denied God's absolute good pleasure in
permitting sin and believed that sins occurred while GOD was unwilling and
opposed. This is what the Jesuits and Arminians also teach.

The ninth evasion has been sufficiently proved in the first and second. The

toward a good object. Again, this hardening in us or actively flowing from us

indisposition inborn in a depraved heart by which it is bent toward the good
only with difficulty, Ezek. 36:26. Acquired hardening is gained by the practice
of evil acts. Each of these is either negative or privative. Negative
hardening is a heart not softened insofar as God does not care for it and is
not obligated to care for it. Privative hardening is a heart culpably not
softened.

From this, the connections made by the Holy Spirit must be noted. 1. God
intends to harden the hearts of those in whom he wills the glory of his justice
and power to be displayed in the sending of plagues. But God wills the glory
of justice, wisdom, and power to be displayed in Pharaoh and the Egyptians.
Therefore, assuming the Arminian hardening, the argument is fallacious and
sophistical, proceeding from an accidental cause. For God does not harden
Pharaoh's heart so that the glory of justice may be displayed in sending
plagues and drying up the Red Sea; rather, God hardens Pharaoh with the end
that Pharaoh should obey and that God should display no wonders and no glory
of justice. 2. This inference belongs to the Spirit of GOD: *GOD made Sihon's
mind firm. Therefore Sihon denied GOD'S People friendly passage.* If GOD'S
active hardening does not necessarily entail culpable hardness intended by
GOD, so that from the passage denied to the people a just war and Sihon's most
just slaughter and destruction would result, this inference is sophistical,
from an accidental cause, and plainly sophistical; for when the antecedent is
posited, the consequent does not follow. But it is preferable to make-*

*the Jesuits and Arminians Sophists rather than Moses. Indeed, according to the
Sophists there is no greater connection between GOD'S active hardening and the
denial of friendly passage to GOD'S People than between active hardening and
non-denial—indeed, the friendly grant—of passage. For in that hardening God
intends that Sihon grant GOD'S People friendly passage; if they are to be
followed, he cannot intend sin. 2. No thanks are owed to God because the
Canaanites hardened their heart so that their land should by GOD'S just
judgment pass to the Israelites as an hereditary possession. Instead,
sacrifice ought to have been offered to the bow and military strength, because
the refusal of passage—which is a grave sin and thus most justly merited
Sihon's slaughter—is
from God accidentally and (if it had seemed so to the Canaanites) rested with
their free choice, which was just as able not to be hardened as to be hardened.
But this conflicts with Moses's purpose, since he exhorts the people to
gratitude toward God, who hardened Sihon's mind so that the land of a tyrant
thus most justly slain should pass into the possession of the Israelites,
according to GOD'S gracious covenant made with Abraham.

3. What fear of God, what confidence in praying to him, what hope of averting
culpable hardening would be owed if penal hardening came from God accidentally?
For if penal hardening occurred when God did not act but permitted—that is,
when he plainly did not will it but opposed it—then God would say with very
good right: *You have no reason to fear me or pray to me to avert penal
hardening from you. For the existence in the nature of things of such hardening
as is penal to you is as foreign to my will as my denying myself would be.* Nor
may they say that merely permissive hardening is punishment and is from God as
a cause in itself. But they cannot say that the culpable hardening in the
sinner is from God in no way except permissively. For when culpable hardening
does not follow in the hardened person, GOD'S active hardening is not
punishment but matter for virtue and praise. 2. God nowhere in Scripture
threatens that active and merely permissive hardening, but always culpable
hardening. I bring these reasons for the assertion. 1. From holy Scripture,
Exod. 14:4: *I make Pharaoh's heart firm so that he may pursue them.* Behold,
he threatens the hardening that causes the most unjust persecution of GOD'S
People. 2. There he threatens a penal hardening that is a means formally
displaying the glory of justice and power in sub-*

*merging Pharaoh and the Egyptians, as is evident from the same words: “That I
may be glorified in Pharaoh and all his forces”* (submerged in the Red Sea as
the just punishment of persecution), *“and the Egyptians may know me to be
Jehovah.”* But GOD'S permissive hardening separated from culpable hardening is
not at all a means destined to display the glory of justice and power in
punishing Pharaoh, for culpable hardening alone is punished. And according to
the adversaries, GOD'S permissive hardening is a means destined in itself for
the person's obedience, so that the glory of vindicatory justice may remain
hidden, not so that such glory may be revealed. Thus John 12:39: *He hardened
their hearts lest they see—and be converted and I heal them.* Behold, he
threatens culpable hardening that produces culpable non-conversion. 3. The
hardening which Scripture threatens is by far the most grievous punishment of
those who perish eternally and are not converted or saved, Matt. 13:15; Isa.
6:9–10; who stumble against CHRIST the stone and are crushed to powder, Matt.
19:44; Isa. 8:14; 1 Pet. 2:7–8; Luke 2:34. But GOD'S active hardening separated
from culpable hardening is not by far the most dreadful punishment. But I
return to confirming the assertion.

3. The hardened have no reason to submit themselves with religious patience
to GOD'S will, because they are culpably hardened when GOD plainly does not
will but merely permits it. 4. If the judicial act of hardening is from God
accidentally, it will be from another cause in itself; for every accidental
cause is reduced to some cause in itself, and everything accidentally just to
something just in itself, as sound Logic teaches us. But it is just that
Pharaoh be culpably hardened because of his preceding crimes and contempt for
GOD, since he said: *Who is JEHOVAH that I should obey his voice and release
Israel?* Exod. 5:2. Therefore this just effect is from the first just cause in
itself, nor can Pharaoh himself exercise this act upon himself, since Pharaoh
acted against all right and law in hardening himself. Therefore this work of
justice can arise in itself from no cause other than God alone. 5. If the act
of hardening is an act of punishing, it necessarily places some penality in the
hardened person. But GOD'S active hardening, which is a bare suspension of
action, places no evil in the hardened person but leaves him equipped with
sufficient grace by which he can be culpably hardened or, according to his own
choice, not hardened. It remains, therefore, that the just penality in that
culpable hardening of Pharaoh—which alone is the intrinsic matter of the divine
and angry Deity's vengeance—takes its origin from fortune or chance alone.

Finally, the unhappy spirit of Jacob Arminius speaks against us: *We
distinguish* (he says) *three things here: active blindness from GOD, active
blindness in the person, and the result, or work and effect, existing in the
blinded person, which is called passive blindness. These actions concur but do
not coincide, nor are they one numerically identical action consisting of the
efficiency of these concurrent causes, which joined together make one total
cause of that act called passive blindness. Although learned men, taking
blindness confusedly and as a complex, say that blindness is sin, the
punishment of sin, and the cause of sin, I deny that GOD is the cause of that
blindness which is both sin and an evil of fault.*

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Against Perkins*, Bertius edition, p. 729.]

I answer. Arminius's sole concern is to free God from every causality in
inflicting the hardening that is both culpable and penal, so that in inflicting
this punishment God is a bare and idle permitter who acts in no way but leaves
a person to his absolute indifference. Thus the sinner is punished or not
punished by this penal hardening according as the pivot of slippery choice
turns itself. But nothing more unworthy can be ascribed to the supreme Judge
than that he should commit punishment, and escape from punishment, to the free
and changeable will of the guilty person.

[Marginal note: The same blindness is both from GOD and from the sinner,
contrary to what Arminius holds.]

2. We willingly embrace with open arms what Arminius says: *active blindness
from GOD and the active blindness in the person differ.* For they differ in
principle, matter, form, manner, and end, as a culpable, unjust, iniquitous
action differs from an action that is just, godly, equitable, and holy—if the
discussion concerns a formal and moral difference. But if the discussion
concerns physical and real coincidence, it is false that the person's passive
blindness, which is penal, does not consist of the efficiency of these causes
concurrent together. For the person's really identical blindness is compounded
of these two actions—namely, the active blindness of GOD justly punishing and
the active blindness of a person unjustly departing from GOD'S Law—yet in such
a way that the blindness insofar as it is sin is from the person alone and by
no means from GOD; and the same blindness, insofar as it is something penal,
justly inflicted because of the crimes of an earlier evil life, is from GOD
alone and not from the person. For culpability (so to speak) is from him alone
who is subject to law, namely, the person; and the penality in blindness (for
it is by far the most grievous evil of punishment and most harmful to the
sinner) is from GOD alone. For it is something just, and therefore proceeds
from the first just cause and supreme Judge. The unjust sinner does not here
invade the throne of the Supreme Judge by exercising this-*

[Chapter XXI; printed page 309; IA PDF page 369; BSB canvas 361]

*act of Justice upon himself. 2. GOD threatens this same blindness, which is
simultaneously culpable and penal, as the most grievous punishment, Isa. 6:9;
Ps. 69:23; Rom. 11:8. Therefore he inflicts what he threatens. 3. Every time
Scripture says that God hardened Pharaoh so that he would not release GOD'S
People, it signifies both that Pharaoh hardened himself and that GOD exercised
the same action in reality that Pharaoh exercised—but God judicially and
Pharaoh culpably, GOD justly and Pharaoh most iniquitously. The matter is plain
in the taking of Job's oxen, for the very same taking of the oxen was done by
God and the Chaldeans. The numerically same oxen, not different ones, were
taken by God and by the Chaldeans; but it was not the same taking morally, nor
was it from the same moral causes. For the taking on GOD'S part, who has a
right over Job's goods and fortunes and *to whom the earth and its fullness
belong*, is holy and equitable, and God does all these things in the taking
with the holiest principle, form, matter, and end. But the Chaldeans, mere
thieves and robbers, take the same oxen with plundering hands and a bloodthirsty
mind, most iniquitously despoiling a holy man. Thus Lam. 1:15: *He tramples all
my strong men in my midst.* Yet this trampling was done not by GOD'S feet but
by the Chaldeans, and that as punishment for idolatry. And Lam. 2:1: *How has
GOD covered the daughter of Zion with the thick cloud of his wrath?* But that
cloud was the army of the Chaldeans raging with fire and sword. The same evil
was inflicted by the Chaldeans and by God as the principal punisher. Nor is it
absurd—indeed, it is necessary—that the same result or effect consist of the
efficiency of the principal cause joined with the efficiency of the other
causes when they are really the same, but differ morally in efficient cause,
matter, form, and end and thus in their causes.

Arminius adds: *If one and the same act is simultaneously sin, the punishment
of sin, and the cause of sin, then GOD cannot be the cause of it according to
the latter two respects and not according to the first, for two reasons. 1. A
distinction of respects cannot cause GOD to be the cause of one thing but not
the other that is really conjoined with it. Such a respect has a use in an
intelligent mind able to distinguish the most closely connected things. But it
cannot have a place in actual efficiency, whose terminus is a moral entity,
because of these three respects that by which sin is considered as the evil of
fault is prior in order, nature, and causality; the respects according to which
it is punishment and the cause of a subsequent sin are later. But GOD is the
first cause of all effects which he produces with or through creatures. Here-*

sin, punishment, and the cause of sin.]

[Chapter XXI; printed page 310; IA PDF page 370; BSB canvas 362]

*he will instead be the later cause and will produce an act according to its
later respects which the creature produces according to the prior respect.
This is absurd and inverts the order of causality between the first and second
cause.*

I answer: 1. Although one and the same blindness is both sin and the cause of
sin, God can nevertheless very well be the cause of penality in sin, or in
blindness, and not the cause of sin. For the diversity of respects can cause
GOD to be the cause of one thing and yet not the cause of another really
conjoined with it. A master commands one servant to turn the mill as punishment
for some offense and forbids another servant to turn it at all, commanding him
to attend to other business. Yet at the request of the servant who is
commanded, the forbidden servant turns the instrument. This same rotation of
the mill is punishment for the former servant and sin, contrary to the
master's command, for the latter; and in the same turning the former servant
undergoes punishment while the latter commits sin. Thus the horse's lameness
is really conjoined with the horse's motion, and the rider is the cause of the
horse's motion, while the dislocation of the horse's leg is the cause of the
lameness; but in no way is the rider the cause of the lameness. The cause of a
discordant sound in harp-playing is a broken string of the harp, not the hand
of the skilled harper. When the sun shines and gleams through green glass, the
greenness is from the medium, namely the glass, and the brilliance is from the
sun. Yet motion and lameness, discordant sound and harp-playing, greenness and
brilliance are really conjoined. Nor can it be said that the lameness is first
from the rider and later from the dislocated leg, because it is in no way,
either earlier or later, from the rider.

2. Nor does Arminius speak with sufficient logic when he calls the punishment
in sin *an entity of reason*, for it is an exceedingly real entity that has an
existence other than merely objective existence in the intellect. 3. Nor does
Arminius's argument hold. For although *GOD is the first cause of all effects
which he produces with or through creatures*, this is nothing against us, who
teach and openly profess before the world that God is neither the first nor
second cause of sin, nor the cause of sin in any sense, and that GOD does not
in any way cause sin either through himself or through creatures. For although
God willed penality to inhere in sin, he did not make sin according to any
imaginary order between the first and second cause. In a matter so grave
Arminius should have used a syllogism. For since God does not produce
punishment and sin as a whole, Arminius dreams in vain about an order of
production. From our opinion he assumes that God as the first cause culpably
hardens Pharaoh and that Pharaoh as the second cause culpably hard-*

[Chapter XXI; printed page 311; IA PDF page 371; BSB canvas 363]

*ens himself. This is Arminius's imaginary order, not ours. Pharaoh culpably
hardens himself, while GOD in that respect acts as the principal agent in no
culpable manner; but God, judicially and as the sole Judge, penally hardens
Pharaoh. There is no order here. I illustrate the matter as follows: muddy
water is not first from the spring and later from the bank, because insofar as
it is muddy it is not from the spring at all and therefore not first from the
spring; and insofar as it is water, it is not first from the bank and later
from the spring, because insofar as it is water it is in no way from the bank.
Likewise, lameness is not first from the rider and then from the dislocated
leg, because it is in no way from the rider but from the horse alone. So also
blindness is not first from God insofar as it is fault and later from the
person insofar as it is punishment. For blindness as fault is from the person
alone and in no way from God; but as punishment it is from the supreme Judge
alone, from whom every punishment formally comes, although materially it comes
from the creature.
