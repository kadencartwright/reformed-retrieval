CHAPTER IX

The Mode of Determining the Various Aspects of Time in Relation to Religion

We said above in Chapter IV that the determination of time may be considered
either with respect to the various attributes of time—namely, *how long, how
often,* and *when*—or with respect to the *mode* according to which the
determination is made: that is, the determination is either *loose* or
*strict*. We have already spoken about the determination of time taken in the
former way. It follows that we should say something about the mode of
determination and how, in this

[1674 logical printed p. 271 | IA leaf 289 | PDF p. 289]

respect, the determination of time is useful both for performing other affairs
and for celebrating the worship of God.

As regards other affairs, the following points may suffice.

1. Where an undertaking is of the greatest importance and extends throughout
the whole course of our life, so that the more the work advances, the more the
person himself is perfected, *exclusive determination* is not useful, much less
necessary, to the work considered generally, even though the determinate
proportion of time with respect both to *how long* and *how often* may be the
greatest possible. For example, it is not useful, much less necessary, for a
student to be so bound to a determinate time that he is not permitted to apply
himself to study earlier, longer, or more frequently than has been established,
unless perhaps the student's health or some similar accident prohibits it.

2. Where a determination can be made wisely enough to present no obstacle to
other necessary affairs, it is useful to this extent that an *initial
determination* be made with respect to *how long, how often,* and also *when*:
that is, that we apply ourselves to our work throughout the whole determinate
time and not for some shorter time; as often as it recurs according to the
determination and not more rarely; and as early as, and no later than, the
determination requires.

3. Where no aversion of mind appears and danger of interruptions threatens,
and where a determination cannot always be made wisely enough by a person
himself, an initial determination of this sort is necessarily required. For
someone whose mind is little inclined toward this work will be brought in no
other way to spend at least so great a portion of time upon that undertaking,
nor can all the other impediments be guarded against in any other way.

4. Those who cannot discern wisely enough what portion of time is ordinarily
necessary and sufficient to be spent upon such a work, who are not sufficiently
willing to spend that entire time, and who lack authority to compel other
unwilling people, cannot usefully make a *conclusive determination of principal
time*. For when wisdom is lacking, it is improbable that a just proportion of
time in all its aspects will be determined. In particular, where willingness
is lacking, the determination will be much too scant with respect to
continuation and frequency, and much too ill-timed with respect to order.
Finally, where authority is lacking, the determination will be wholly invalid,
exposed to contempt, and easily rejected.

5. Conversely, those who possess the wisdom to make an adequate determination
which is neither excessive nor deficient in any respect, who earnestly desire
the undertaking to be completed or advanced which must be performed during
that determinate time, and who also possess authority to compel inferiors, can
make a determination of principal time very usefully. For in this way help is
given to the ignorant, the unwilling are compelled, and the willing enjoy the
greatest security in the determination made.

6. When someone resolves to test whether others have a mind well disposed
toward study, a *loose* determination of time with respect to all, or only
certain, aspects is useful. Those who delight in study have an opportunity to
pursue it; but those who are moved by no desire for learning will readily
invent excuses and thus quickly demonstrate their aversion of mind.

7. Outside this or a similar case, this loose determination is not sufficient,

[1674 logical printed p. 272 | IA leaf 290 | PDF p. 290]

either with respect to all the aspects of time or to the two principal ones,
namely, *how long* and *how often*. For notwithstanding this loose
determination, the unwilling can indulge their inclination. The ignorant do
not know how the evil may be remedied, and no means is established for meeting
interruptions or removing other impediments.

8. Where it is necessary that there be certain interruptions, or where it is
very probable that there will be such interruptions, it is useful and
necessary that a determination of principal time be made with a general or
particular reservation. That is, notwithstanding that precise determination,
time is to be granted for refreshing bodies with food, drink, and sleep and
for performing certain other supremely necessary and urgent things. By this
means the determination, though it always remains in force, is burdensome to
no one, nor does it provide anyone an occasion for raising such an objection.

9. It must nevertheless be noted that the *conclusive determination of
principal time* can remain firm, immutable, and always useful for advancing a
principal complex action, even when some particular determination of an
action pertaining to the principal undertaking is changed because what had
previously been useful might now be burdensome. For example, the determination
of twelve hours together every week can remain firm and always useful for
advancing study, although the previously useful determination of one or two
whole hours which were to be given to the study of this or that particular
science or liberal art is changed and removed because of some incidental
inconvenience.

To apply these things to religion, we set down the following assertions.

1. No *exclusive determination* is useful to religion considered in general.
That is, it is not useful for the length of time to be determined in such a way
that it must be neither longer nor shorter, or for the order and season of time
to be determined in such a way that the beginning may be neither earlier nor
later. For religion, whether it concerns God's glory or a person's salvation,
is a person's principal work, and it is always permissible—if it is not indeed
necessary—to pursue and apply oneself to that work whenever necessary earthly
affairs permit it. Nowhere in Scripture is any such exclusive determination
mentioned as regards religion in general. As regards certain particular acts
of worship, however, time was sometimes determined exclusively. For example,
the eighth day was exclusively determined for the circumcision of a male
infant. But this exclusive determination was only with respect to order, or
*when*, not with respect to frequency or continuation. There was also a
certain exclusive determination with respect to the frequency of particular
duties—namely, those which had to be performed once each year, such as the
celebration of Passover and similar annual festivals—as there was also of those
things which had to be administered only once, such as circumcision. But with
respect to the duration of time, or *how long*, we nowhere read that an
exclusive determination was made such that it was unlawful to give ourselves
to divine worship or acts of religion for longer than the determinate time.
Hence it is clear that celebrating sacred assemblies on working days is not a
sin, because the determination of time made in the Fourth Commandment of the
Decalogue is not exclusive. For the Jews themselves never had such a
determination, since God himself had prescribed for them many other festival
days besides the weekly Sabbath. But we will say more about this later in Book
IV, where we will treat the Decalogue.

[1674 logical printed p. 273 | IA leaf 291 | PDF p. 291]

2. An *initial determination* can be useful when it is instituted so wisely
with respect to the various aspects of time that it presents no impediment to
our necessary worldly affairs. That is, it can be said that at least so great
a portion of time, recurring so often, can be required and ought to be
assigned. For example, on each day both in the morning and evening, at least a
quarter of an hour is to be spent upon solitary divine worship. Notwithstanding
this determination, those who make it for themselves can, when an opportunity
is given, spend a quarter of an hour three times a day, or half an hour twice
each day, upon this solitary worship.

3. As for the *conclusive determination of principal time*—that is, of time
with respect to *how long* and *how often*, by which so great a portion of time
recurring so often is determined to be sufficient—it cannot be made usefully by
one who lacks the wisdom, goodwill, and authority required here. For if they
lack the wisdom to determine the total time or rightly to divide that total
time between continuation and frequency, they can easily err. If they are
superstitious, they may determine time longer than is fair with respect both
to *how long* and *how often*. Conversely, if they seek earthly things with
excessive care, they will necessarily determine a portion of time smaller than
is fair; or, if perhaps they select a fair portion, they will divide it badly
and make either the duration longer than is fair and the repetition less
frequent, or, conversely, the repetition or recurrence more frequent and the
duration shorter than is fair. Therefore, one who is not equipped with
sufficient wisdom must not be believed capable of rightly determining this
time which is *principal, solemn, necessary, and ordinarily sufficient*. If
they are ill-disposed toward religion, it is certain that they will not wish
to determine sufficient time, whether with respect to *how long* or *how
often*, if they determine any at all. And if authority is lacking, the
determination will be useless, however wisely it is made, because no one will
be compelled to observe it in a holy manner.

4. A *conclusive determination of principal time*, or of duration and
frequency, will be exceedingly useful when made by those who possess wisdom,
are well disposed toward religion, and are endowed with authority. For then no
one can raise a scruple about the necessity or sufficiency of that time. Those
who are unwilling or refractory will be compelled, while those who are willing
will set aside other business and observe the determinate time in a holy
manner with the greatest eagerness. Hence it is clear that the determination
of one day in the weekly cycle made by God is supremely useful, and that the
time so determined is the *solemn, principal, necessary, and ordinarily
sufficient* time of divine worship. A command enjoining such a time is
substantially useful to all people in every age.

5. A *loose determination* of both continuation and frequency with respect to
certain particular acts of religion can be useful: partly to commend religion,
as a supremely necessary thing, to our care; and partly to test the affections
of inferiors toward religion. Thus, when God commands us to continue in prayer
(Colossians 4:2), he requires that a suitable proportion of time with respect
to continuation be given to this exercise. Yet he does not name and designate
that particular proportion, in order to test how well or badly inclined we are
toward this duty. Similarly, when he commands us to pray at all times
(Ephesians 6:18), to meditate night and

[1674 logical printed p. 274 | IA leaf 292 | PDF p. 292]

day (Joshua 1:8), and frequently to celebrate the sacred synaxis (1
Corinthians 11:25–26), he determines frequency, but loosely, without determining
precisely how often this must be done. And from the frequency, other things
being equal, our affection toward these duties is discerned.

6. Yet a *loose determination* of time for religion in general with respect
both to duration and frequency is not useful and cannot be useful unless there
is some other *strict and conclusive determination* which constitutes solemn
and principal time that is ordinarily sufficient. For that determination
would be wholly insufficient for the unwilling, for those who do not have
religion at heart, or for those to whom many impediments are commonly
presented. But once a conclusive determination of the principal and solemn
time of worship is given, other determinations, though loose, can be useful.

7. Since it is necessary that there be certain interruptions, it is useful for
time to be reserved for performing those necessary and urgent things. By this
means the determination made will be less burdensome, even if it is strict and
the determinate time is longer, and the remainder of the time will be observed
more holily and scrupulously. Thus, in the determination of the Sabbath, or a
seventh part of time, time is reserved for refreshing our body with food,
sleep, and so forth, for performing certain works of mercy, and for doing
certain necessary works, as we will see in the proper place. This reservation
not only does not render empty the determination made of solemn time, as the
adversaries pretend, but makes it firmer and more closely binding, since God
allowed this reservation for the very purpose that we might give the remainder
of the time to sacred things more scrupulously.

8. A particular determination for some special end can become obsolete and no
longer be observed under the gospel state when some change has occurred in
that end, either because it would be burdensome or because it is wholly
unsuited to that state. Hence the feasts of the Jews, new moons, and the like
are made obsolete and abrogated (Romans 14; Galatians 4; Colossians 2), and
nothing is instituted in their place. But when the seventh day in order was
abrogated, the first was instituted in its place, because it was necessary
that one of seven be instituted. For it was conclusively determined that a
seventh part of time be the principal and solemn time of divine worship. And
this determination of one day in the weekly cycle could not be observed unless
a particular day was precisely designated. Hence it is clear that, when this
determination of the day was changed from the last to the first of the week,
the substantial determination of principal and solemn time—namely, one day in
the weekly cycle—remained unchanged. It is also clear how great the difference
is between the determination of one day in the weekly cycle and the
determination of other festivals, and how, while the former remains, the latter
can be removed without any detriment.
