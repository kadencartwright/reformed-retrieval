### QUESTION XX

#### How is Haggai 2:19 to be reconciled with Ezra 3:10–12? In the former passage the temple is said to have been founded in Darius’s second year; in the latter, in Cyrus’s second year, many years before Darius—namely, immediately after Zerubbabel’s return from the Babylonian captivity.

Answer. Most interpreters of Scripture and chronologists, Protestants no less
than Roman Catholics, pass over this difficulty without touching it, I think
through inadvertence—for who sees everything? Yet even through inadvertence
it could scarcely be passed over after various writers had raised it in their
published writings. Lyranus indeed attempted to solve this difficulty several
centuries ago by his distinctions, and Bede many centuries before him. It is
certain that writers knowingly and deliberately pass over many questions,
either despising them because they are too easy or avoiding them because they
are too difficult. The Jesuit Sanctius professes to offer an easy solution to
this difficulty, but it does not please others and does not seem to remove the
difficulty altogether.

> Marginalia, printed p. 193: *Interpreters conceal many difficulties*.

The first and most ancient answer is that of the venerable Bede, set down in
the Ordinary Gloss on Ezra 6 and approved by Pererius and Torniellus: namely,
that the temple was twofold, internal and external. The former contained the
Holy Place and the Holy of Holies; the latter had open-air precincts
containing the altar of burnt offerings, courts, porticoes, and many
appendages of the temple. Bede would have the foundations of the former laid
in the days of Cyrus, in Ezra 2, and those of the latter in the days of
Darius, in Haggai 2.

> Marginalia, printed p. 193: *Bede’s distinction of the external temple from
> the internal does not satisfy*.

But this answer does not satisfy. For although it seems sufficiently probable
that the inner house and its external appendages were not founded at the same
time, and that the entire work was obstructed by the Samaritans even before a
hand was applied to founding the appendages, both Prophets nevertheless speak
not about the appendages but about the inner house itself. The verb in both is
the same, יסד. It never signifies only the appendages, but always either the
inner house alone, as it usually does, or the whole palace together, both
internal and external with all its appendages. It never signifies only the
exterior parts to the exclusion of the inner and principal house. Moreover,
the foundation Haggai mentions was undoubtedly that of the innermost house.

The second answer is Hugo Grotius’s: the Hebrew verb יסד, “to found,” is
πολύσημον, “polysemous”; it signifies various things, including “to begin,”
and is translated ἄρχεσθαι, “to begin,” by the Seventy Interpreters. Thus in
the passage of Haggai it signifies nothing other than that the temple began to
be built again after an interruption. But neither does this answer please.
The word הוסד is indeed πολύσημος, but it nowhere signifies “to begin.” It is
true that once, and only once, in the passage the learned man cites—2
Chronicles 3:3—the Seventy Interpreters render it ἄρχεσθαι, but altogether
inaptly. The passage is admittedly difficult in the original, but the
Seventy’s rendering, *ἢ ταῦτα ἤρξατο Σολομῶν τὸ οἰκοδομῆσαι*—“or these things
Solomon began to build”—is contrary to all the other ancient and modern
interpreters I have seen in any language, and indeed to the meaning and
purpose of the passage. What is transmitted there is not the beginning of the
work but its arrangement, and Solomon’s instruction and establishment by the
Spirit or by his father David concerning how he should carry out the entire
work. Therefore, whether you take הוסד there as a noun or a verb, you will
wrongly interpret it as “beginning.”

And even if it were correctly translated so there, that interpretation would
nevertheless be wrongly applied to the passage before us, contrary not only
to every other version but also to that of the Seventy themselves, who
everywhere in Ezra and Haggai translate יסד by θεμελιεῖν, “to found,” not
ἄρχεσθαι, “to begin.” Finally, this interpretation would not itself untie the
knot, since there would be as great a question about the beginning of the
construction as about its foundation. For does not every building begin from
the foundation?

> Marginalia, printed p. 193: *Grotius wrongly rejects the common
> interpretation*.

The third answer is that of the Jesuit Christophorus à Castro on the passage,
which Salianus approves: namely, that the Prophet speaks of no foundations
laid in the days of Darius, but only of the foundations laid in the days of
Cyrus. To this end he wishes the Prophet’s words to refer to the past, not the
future. But this is the worst answer, although the two most recent Jesuits
hold it in high regard. Besides expressly contradicting their own authentic
Bibles, which clearly translate Haggai 2:19, *set your hearts from that day and
into the future*—just as the Seventy Interpreters also have ἐπέκεινα,
“beyond”—the subject matter of the passage admits no translation

> Marginalia, printed p. 193: *Salianus wrongly puts the past in place of the
> future*.

[Printed p. 194 | IA leaf 224 | access PDF 224 | OCR object 223]

other than this. For what in the original and also in the Chaldean Paraphrast
is “onward” must necessarily be understood of the future.
The Prophet expressly adds, “after this twenty-fourth day of the ninth month,
when the temple’s foundations were laid,” not “before this twenty-fourth day,
back as far as the first foundations under Cyrus.”

The fourth opinion is Lyranus’s, which Arias Montanus, Ribera, Sanctius, and
Cornelius follow, although they explain it in different ways. Salianus
pronounces it harsh and ranks it below the former opinion, but to us it seems
the most probable: namely, that the foundations laid in the days of Cyrus were
the first and imperfect foundations, but in the days of Darius other,
secondary foundations were built upon them, completing and perfecting them.
In matters of faith the metaphorical distinction between primary and
secondary fundamentals is common; it seems to us that it may rightly be
applied here to foundations in the proper sense. Zerubbabel seems to have been
obstructed in his first construction before he could make the wall of the
house visible above the surface of the earth. Thus in the second work under
Darius, construction below the earth’s surface—although above the lower work
deep down—could rightly be called foundation work.

> Marginalia, printed p. 194: *The first foundations are rightly distinguished
> from the second*.

Certainly Zerubbabel’s first work in the days of Cyrus could have had beneath
itself some foundations laid by Solomon. For although the malice of the
Edomites spurred the Chaldeans to tear down the temple from its lowest
foundations, as we have in Psalm 137, it is not probable that they dug out all
its foundation stones. For when Annius Rufus had leveled with the plow the
second temple destroyed by Vespasian, so that no stone was left upon another,
many notable stones nevertheless remained in the deep ruins down to Julian’s
days. The miraculous fire, sent by God to restrain, if not convince, the rage of
Julian and the Jews against Christ, is said to have spewed them out.

I shall add no more here. Consider only a few points concerning Ezra 3:12. It
says that priests who had seen the first temple when it was founded wept. Did
anyone living in Ezra’s days really see the first temple when it was founded
by Solomon four hundred years earlier? Salianus thinks he has answered this
difficulty by placing a comma after the word “first.” But this punctuation
does not transfer the foundation from the former temple to the latter unless
the following comma is also removed and we read verse 12 in this way: “when
it and this temple had been founded.” Yet this sins twice against the
punctuation of the Bibles authentic for those of Trent, and also against the
punctuation of the Hebrew text. For the word ביסדו, “in its foundation,” has
over the final *daleth* the *zaqef qaton*, which joins the foundation to the
former temple and separates it from the latter. The Greek Interpreter agrees
with Jerome: *τὸν οἶκον τὸν πρῶτον ἐν θεμελιώσει αὐτοῦ*, “the first house in
its foundation.”

Grotius also changes the punctuation, and in the same way both the earlier and
later English versions refer the foundation to the later house present before
their eyes, not to the former house that had been destroyed. But this is
certainly wrong, as it seems. For who would dare to change in the original
text not merely one *iota*, the smallest of letters, but even one stroke, one
point, or one distinguishing accent? This is especially so when not only the
ancient Greek and Latin Interpreters follow this Hebrew punctuation—there is
no Chaldean paraphrase of Ezra and Daniel, because they themselves are written
for the most part in Chaldean—but the Latin version of Junius, Montanus’s
interlinear version, and Diodati’s Italian version also construe the
foundation with the former temple, not the latter.

> Marginalia, printed p. 194: *The bad punctuation of Salianus, Grotius, and
> the English Annotators*.

Indeed, even setting punctuation aside, the words themselves do not allow the
foundation to be construed with the later temple without κακοφάτω, an
ill-sounding expression. For the passage has not the bare word “foundation,”
but “foundation” joined with the pronoun “its,” which seems necessarily to
join the foundation to what precedes, not to what follows. Thus the sense
flows quite beautifully: “the first temple in its foundation, on its own
foundation,” and, as Diodati has it, “on its own foot.” This means nothing
other than that some who were seeing the later house had also seen the former
standing upon its foundation. And although there may be some emphasis in the
word “foundation,” it may refer to the stones in Solomon’s temple foundation
and above it that were remarkable for their extraordinary size, such as did
not appear in the later temple.
