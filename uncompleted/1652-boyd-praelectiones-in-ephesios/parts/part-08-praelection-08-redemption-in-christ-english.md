On verse 7: “In whom we have.”

[Marginalia: Lecture VIII. The relation of the argument.]

Although, as we noted above in its proper place, this whole discourse of our
Apostle coheres like a carefully wrought chain, connected by certain sinews
and bonds of its own—as is evident to anyone who considers the connection of
this very clause with the preceding one, since he does not so much make an
obvious transition to a new benefit to be set before us as gradually descend
from the former one and lead our minds with him from eternal predestination
to redemption accomplished in time—yet by looking more closely here we can
observe another cause of our salvation, one dependent upon that former and
eternal cause. We therefore establish here the second member of that
induction, concerning the benefit of our redemption which Christ obtained by
his precious blood shed for us on the cross. We noted above that the
spiritual blessings with which God the Father has abundantly enriched us in
Christ may be considered in three ways: either in God’s eternal counsel,
insofar as he predestined us to them from eternity in Christ; or insofar as
Christ, having become man, actually acquired them for us and actually
obtained and procured them on our behalf even before we ourselves were born,
much less actually made—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 64 | Caput I p. 22]

—sharers in them; or finally insofar as we are now actually called to
participate in them, whether participation begun in this life through faith
and the Holy Spirit or consummated in the age to come. Since, therefore, the
Apostle has already considered these blessings in the first way in the three
immediately preceding verses, it follows that he next considers them in the
second way. He does this in verse 7 when he says, “in whom” (namely, in Christ,
or in that Beloved whom he had just mentioned and who serves the Apostle here
as a kind of common term, so to speak, as he passes from one member to
another. For his subject is those benefits pertaining to eternal salvation,
all of which come to us in and through Christ; it is therefore no wonder that
the Apostle binds together such varied and numerous clauses of his discourse
with this most fitting bond), “we have redemption,” or liberation from the
power and dominion of Satan, sin, and death—I mean “the forgiveness of
trespasses”—“through his blood,” namely the blood which he shed on the cross
for this end, that he might pay the *lytron*, or the very price of our
redemption. He did so “according to the riches of his grace,” namely the grace
of God the Father, to whom these words refer no less than the earlier words,
“to the praise of his glorious grace.” For the grace which he there called
glorious he here calls rich, that is, abundant, plentiful, and immense. The
Apostle customarily uses this term *riches* whenever he wishes the vast power
and abundance of something to be understood, as in Romans 2:4: “Do you
despise the riches of God’s kindness, forbearance, and gentleness?” And in
9:23: “That he might make known the riches of his glory toward the vessels of
mercy which he prepared for glory.” And in 11:33: “O the depths of the riches
both of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God!” Similar forms of expression
occur throughout this very epistle.

[Marginalia: Exegesis. Romans 2:4; 9:23; and 11:33.]

Under the name of redemption, therefore, we may here include those saving
benefits or spiritual blessings insofar as they were obtained for us in
Christ’s own person and were procured and gained by Christ through his death
and obedience perfectly rendered to God the Father here on earth. The Apostle
will treat in the immediately following verses the same benefits insofar as
they are communicated to us through faith. Hence, by a memorable *symploce*
and synecdoche, when he mentions the redemption which we have in Christ
through his blood, he at the same time as it were touches upon and enfolds the
remaining stages among the means of our salvation. These are our primal sin
and the ruin of the whole of human nature and race which followed from it;
death, the wages of sin, and the condemnation by which the divine law pressed
us; slavery to sin and Satan’s dreadful tyranny; then the law’s inability to
free, justify, or give us life out of this abyss of misery; finally the
mystery of Christ’s incarnation and everything that preceded his cross and
death. For the benefit of redemption which he mentions necessarily
presupposes all these things and so implicates and enfolds them within itself.

[Marginalia: Division.]

In order to understand this more clearly, four things in this verse must be
considered in order, but with all the brevity we can manage, lest too much
time be spent. First is the benefit of redemption itself, which cannot be
discussed without also mentioning the price of this redemption. We therefore
refer it to the same heading as redemption itself, since redemption properly
appears to be nothing other than the payment of a price for those to be
redeemed. Second is the one in whom we have this benefit. Third is how the
Apostle explains it here when he interprets it as the forgiveness of sins.
Finally, with the Apostle we shall touch upon the impulsive cause in God
himself from which so great a benefit flowed to us, namely the riches of his
grace.

[Marginalia: Concerning redemption.]

As for the first, since to redeem properly means nothing other than to free a
slave or captive, or someone oppressed by any other tyranny, from another’s
power—whether by bare force and greater power or by a price paid with the
agreement of the parties—and to assert him into liberty, although the Greek
word *apolytrōseōs* which the Apostle uses here, as derived from *to lytron*,
properly signifies the latter mode of redemption, the matter itself mentioned
here, namely the benefit of our spiritual redemption in Christ, is rightly
extended to both modes of redemption, though in different ways or respects.
This will become clear to anyone who weighs our native condition outside
Christ. There are two states of humanity for us to consider here: one of
misery under sin and death, the other of grace under righteousness and life.
Holy Scripture commonly sets the former condition before us as that of a
captive detained in another’s power—indeed, of one serving the harshest
slavery, oppressed by the fiercest tyranny, and destined for a most miserable
end and destruction. In a word, it presents him as a slave of the devil and
sin, subject to God’s wrath and condemned to eternal death. It presents the
latter condition as that of someone freed from such dreadful and harsh
slavery and asserted and vindicated into the light and liberty of God’s sons.

[Marginalia: Reasons for the name. Inference from the conclusion.]

From this it is now evident that by God’s just judgment we were not only
handed over and enslaved to Satan’s tyranny and the slavery of sin, but were
subject to God’s own wrath and judgment and were debtors to God, bound to
eternal punishment because the covenant of works or nature—that is, the
eternal law—had been violated. From this follows what has just been asserted:
Christ obtained and covenanted our redemption in both those modes, though in
different respects. He asserted us from this manifold misery into liberty
both by paying a price for us and by his mighty arm, according to the
effective power of his strength. He did so in the former mode if you consider
God himself, to whom we were debtors because the law had been violated,
according to the law of his immutable justice—debtors, I say, to eternal
punishment, so that his justice might be satisfied. A price therefore had to
be paid to God the Father to free us, and satisfaction had to be rendered to
the Father’s justice. Christ did this by bearing the punishment we owed.
Christ paid God his Father the price for us, namely his blood considered not
simply in itself but as shed on the cross, for the shedding of his blood
necessarily carries with it and presupposes his death. Hence our Apostle says
in 1 Corinthians 6:20 that we were “bought with a price,” and in 1 Peter
1:18–19, “You know that you were redeemed from your futile way of life handed
down by your fathers, not with corruptible things, silver or gold, but with
precious blood, as of a blameless and spotless lamb, namely Christ.” With
respect to God the Father, therefore, to whom Christ offered this atoning
sacrifice on the cross, the price of our redemption—which the Apostle here
designates by the name *blood*—we are said to have been redeemed, that is,
released from the law’s curse or malediction, God’s wrath, and eternal
punishment, through this action or passion of Christ, by which he became a
curse for us. We were—

[Marginalia: Exegesis. 1 Corinthians 6:20. 1 Peter 1:18–19. Galatians 3:13.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 65 | Caput I p. 23]

—released and freed. In the latter mode he will be said to have redeemed us
“with a strong hand and a mighty arm,” as the royal Psalmist sings in Psalm
136:12, if you consider the devil himself, the prince of this world. He held
us captive in the bonds of sin and would at length have executed God’s wrath
and just judgment upon us as an executioner or headsman assigned by God to
that office, had not our most powerful and merciful Savior snatched us from
his hands by his magnificent strength. In this respect our Apostle says in
Colossians 2:15 that Christ “stripped principalities and powers, made a public
spectacle of them, and triumphed over them through his cross.” In the same
sense Hebrews 2:14 says that through death he abolished or destroyed the one
who has the power of death, namely the devil. The Lord himself also expressed
this to us in a parable when by this parable he beat back and repressed the
Pharisees’ blasphemous words and thoughts: “When a strong man, fully armed,
guards his hall, his possessions are at peace; but when someone stronger
comes upon him and overcomes him”—indeed, as Matthew 12:29 adds, “binds the
strong man”—“he takes away all the armor in which the man trusted, plunders
his house, and distributes his spoils.”

[Marginalia: Psalm 136:12. Colossians 2:15. Hebrews 2:14. Luke 11:21–22.
Matthew 12:29.]

Here he plainly compares the devil to a strong and powerful man who guards
his house in arms and possesses everything in peace, and himself to a far
stronger and more powerful man who comes, conquers and binds him, and plunders
all his weapons. Most marvelous and magnificent of all, he does so through
his death, outwardly so lowly and abject, and through his cross, outwardly so
shameful, yet on it Christ gloriously triumphed over all the powers of
darkness. He led the devil himself, previously unconquered, with all the
companies of hell publicly before his cross as before a triumphal chariot, as
the Apostle said above.

[Marginalia: Extended treatment.]

This magnificent mode of liberation from the power of Satan and all
spiritual enemies was especially foreshadowed for us by the magnificent and
glorious deliverance of the Israelite people from Egyptian slavery and
captivity. Pharaoh with his army represented the devil; Egypt, the kingdom of
darkness; that ancient people, the faithful gathered into Christ’s Church;
the Red Sea, our baptism; and finally their paschal lamb and the sprinkling of
its blood upon the doorposts of the Israelites’ houses represented as so many
shadows and types Christ’s shed blood applied by faith to our hearts through
the power of the Holy Spirit.

[Marginalia: Objection. Answer.]

But someone may object against what has been said: How have we been freed from
Satan’s power when the price for us was paid not to him but to God the Father,
as has already been said? In redeeming a captive, however, the price must and
ordinarily does have to be paid to the one in whose power the captive is and
from whose oppression he must be freed. The preceding discussion supplies an
easy answer. Although Satan detained us as captives through and because of
sin, as the ruler of the darkness of this age, the father of lies, and the
first author and craftsman of sin, nevertheless he had no right to condemn us.
That right belonged only to God, against whom we had sinned, whose law and
covenant we had violated, and to whose judgment we were therefore subject.
Through a necessary consequence we fell into the devil’s power, whose snares
we put upon ourselves through sin, since the state of sin is the devil’s very
kingdom; and wherever sin fully lives and thrives, Satan reigns through
blindness, impiety, impurity, and necessarily every kind of sin. Therefore,
for us to be rescued from Satan’s service, it was enough for that sacrifice
of immeasurable value to be offered by Christ on the cross to God the Father.
On account of its merit and worth, the Father absolved us from the law’s
condemnation by freely forgiving our sins. From this necessarily follows our
liberation and vindication from Satan’s power, since that tyrant can have no
further right or power over those already absolved and reconciled to their
heavenly Father, relieved of sin’s burden and cleansed of sin’s stain through
Christ’s blood. Thus, as we said above, the prey was torn from him by force,
his power was broken and abolished, his works dissolved, and his kingdom
overturned and destroyed by that sacrifice and *lytron* of immeasurable and
inexhaustible value offered to God the Father through Christ. So much for the
benefit of redemption itself.

[Marginalia: The one in whom we have redemption.]

The second member concerned the one in whom we have this benefit: “in Christ,”
says the Apostle, and that “through his blood.” We think that two things are
denoted by this form of speech. First, Christ is the matter, as is commonly
said, both of redemption and of our whole salvation. He has been made all
things for us—wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption—as the
Apostle says in that celebrated passage, 1 Corinthians 1:30, so that outside
him we can obtain no degree, cause, means, or part of salvation. But besides
this, it denotes our most intimate union with Christ, by which it comes about
that we possess the things which are in him and that those which he obtained
for us are called ours and pertain to us.

[Marginalia: Confirmation. 1 Corinthians 1:30.]

For us to have anything in Christ certainly supposes that we first are in
Christ, united to him as members to the head. Hence something greater and
more intimate is conveyed to us by this expression, “In Christ we have
redemption,” than if it had said only, “Through Christ we have it.” It can be
said, for example, that the Israelites were redeemed from Egypt through Moses,
that is, that God used his work and ministry in redeeming his people from
Egypt; but one would very foolishly and improperly say that they had
redemption *in* Moses. Therefore, when we are said to have redemption in
Christ, it is understood not only that he is our Redeemer but also that we are
in him and are supposed to be grafted and incorporated into him.

[Marginalia: The manner of redeeming.]

As for the manner of redemption which he adds, “through his blood,” why the
Mediator’s blood had to be shed for him to be our Redeemer is already clear
in part from what has been said. God the Father’s wrath had to be appeased and
his justice satisfied, for it necessarily demanded that the sinner die and
inflicted certain death upon the transgressor of the law, without which there
could be no atonement for sin. The same is also clearly taught in the Epistle
to the Hebrews: without the shedding of blood there can be no forgiveness of
sins, Hebrews 9:22. Yet the Apostle himself explains this redemption as the
forgiveness of sins in the words that follow. It is therefore with good reason
that we are said here to have redemption in Christ through his blood, namely
his blood shed on the cross. In a word, this occurred through the intervening
death of the Testator and Mediator of this new covenant, by whose blood this
covenant had to be ratified and sealed. For he presented himself as our
surety before the judgment of God the Father, as the target whom the Father’s
wrath and justice might strike in our place.

[Marginalia: Question. Answer. Hebrews 9:22.]

But if someone raises this difficulty—How could the blood of Christ the man
satisfy God’s infinite justice and so free and release us from the
condemnation of the eternal punishment to which we were subject?—we answer
briefly that

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 66 | Caput I p. 24]

Christ’s bloody death was of infinite price and merit. This was not only
because of
the severity of the punishment he bore, since that vast force and, as it
were, immeasurable weight of divine wrath was poured out all at once upon him
as he carried our sins upon himself on the cross, bearing them in such a way
that by bearing he removed and atoned for them. No creature—not even all
humans and angels together—could have borne either the burden of sins or the
infinite mass of wrath pressing and pursuing him; they would necessarily have
been overcome and collapsed beneath so great a weight. Its inexpressible
horror wrung from the Son of God himself both clots of blood and those
lamentable and plaintive cries. Yet, I say, his death was of infinite worth
not only because of the severity of the punishment, but much more because of
the infinite excellence and dignity of his person, and thus because of the
Deity joined to humanity, which imparted to this single sacrifice, offered
once, an infinite power to merit. For the blood of Christ dying on the cross
was the blood of God, not of a mere man. It was the blood “with which God
acquired the Church by his own blood,” says the Apostle in Acts 20:28. This
work of redemption was a work common to God and man, not proper to Christ the
man, although each nature of Christ did not accomplish precisely the same
thing in it. Rather, each nature performed what was proper to itself and
together they concurred in the same *apotelesma* of redemption, that common
work which had to be completed: the Word doing what belongs to the Word and
the flesh carrying out what belongs to the flesh, each in communion with the
other because of the personal union of the two. So Pope Leo, bishop of Rome,
rightly wrote long ago in his letter to Flavian.

[Marginalia: Acts 20:28. Pope Leo. Consequence.]

From this it is also evident how and why this work of redemption is properly
and peculiarly attributed in Scripture to Christ the Mediator, the Son of God,
but not to the Father or the Holy Spirit. Yet far be it from us to exclude
them from this same work, since the Father, as he is the first author of all
grace, is also first in order to forgive us our sins, while the Holy Spirit
seals the forgiveness of those sins in our hearts. But this action of
redeeming us by the shedding of blood is proper and peculiar to the Son of
God because he alone became man and alone assumed our nature; he alone was
appointed by God to this work by reason of the office of Mediator; and he
alone possessed that right of kinship to redeem us of which we read in
Leviticus 25:48. When someone from the people had sold himself to another
because of poverty and need, and had enslaved himself to an alien or a
sojourner from a foreign family, the right of redemption belonged to one of
his brothers, either brothers properly and strictly so called or some near
relative from the same stock and family. Therefore, although the right of
ownership and dominion by which they may assert us as their creatures into
liberty and vindicate us from the hand of any enemies belongs equally to all
three Persons, the right of kinship was proper to Christ alone, because he
alone became man and deigned to contract with us that nearer kinship and so a
certain brotherhood by reason of human nature. This is the second member,
concerning the one in whom we have redemption through his blood.

[Marginalia: Leviticus 25:48. Forgiveness of trespasses.]

As for this explanation added by the Apostle, in which he interprets
redemption as the forgiveness of sins, that redemption—that is, the action of
redeeming through Christ’s death and the shedding of his blood, which was an
atoning sacrifice offered to God—cannot properly be the forgiveness of sins
itself, but only metonymically, by transferring the name of the cause to the
effect. For the forgiveness of our sins followed this redemption as one of
its necessary effects, and so did our justification through the imputation
of Christ’s obedience and satisfaction, which necessarily coheres with the
forgiveness of sins. Even from this it is evident, as we said at the outset,
that under the name of redemption the Apostle includes every spiritual
benefit insofar as it was gained and procured for us from God the Father by
Christ’s death and sacrifice, and so insofar as it is viewed in Christ
himself as the matter—or rather, as a kind of storehouse and treasury—of our
salvation. In the same way we showed above that the same word implies and
includes the other means of our salvation which preceded Christ’s death. We
could not have been redeemed unless we had first fallen and become captives,
nor could we have been redeemed by the Mediator’s blood unless he had first
assumed our flesh and blood, that is, human nature.

[Marginalia: Threefold liberation. John 8:34. Romans 6:16. Titus 3:3. John
8:44. Romans 6:14, 22. Romans 16:20.]

Now just as we were oppressed by a threefold slavery—namely, first, the curse
of the law, which rested upon us and bound us to both kinds of death according
to the rule of divine justice; second, the most miserable slavery of sin,
according to John 8:34, “Truly, truly, I say to you, whoever devotes himself
to sin is a slave of sin,” and Romans 6:16, “Do you not know that you are the
slaves of the one to whom you present yourselves as slaves for obedience,
whom you obey, whether of sin unto death or of obedience unto righteousness?”
and Titus 3:3, “For we also were once foolish, rebellious, wandering, and
serving various desires and pleasures”; third, the most cruel tyranny of the
devil, which he exercised over us through sin, holding us subject to himself
to fulfill and carry out his evil will, blinding and hardening us, leading us
away into every sin, and at last dragging us away with himself into eternal
destruction as the prince of all those upon whom the burden of sin rests and
who remain under the law’s curse and God’s unbearable wrath, according to
John 8:44, “You are of your father the devil, and you wish to perform the
desires of your father”—just as, I say, we were detained in this threefold
captivity, so a threefold liberation is denoted for us by this forgiveness of
sins. First is liberation from divine wrath, the law’s curse, condemnation,
and the eternal death which follows it. Then comes liberation from the
slavery of sin, so that we may serve God in holiness and righteousness
through the obedience of new life, as the Apostle teaches in the same Romans
6:14: “Sin will not have dominion over you, for you are not under law but
under grace”; and below in verse 22, “But now, freed from sin and made slaves
of God, you have your fruit unto holiness and the end, eternal life.” Finally
comes liberation from Satan’s fiercest tyranny. We overcome and conquer him
in the one who loved us and handed himself over for us, in the manner and with
the weapons which the Apostle himself will teach us toward the end of this
epistle, until the God of peace at last wholly crushes Satan under our feet,
never to assault us afterward.

[Marginalia: According to the riches of his grace.]

As for the final member, in which that *proēgoumenē*, or impulsive cause in
God, is denoted: What else, I ask, could have moved our most merciful God to
appoint such a Redeemer for us from eternity and give him in time, sending
him into the world to—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 67 | Caput I p. 25]

—redeem us; or to accept the redemption and price which he paid for us on the
cross and impute it to us as our own, but the riches of his grace? What but
that same glorious grace which he wishes us to celebrate and praise forever?
Nothing else at all. This is that grace—that is, free benevolence,
*philanthrōpia*, and mercy—which, just as he called it glorious above, he
rightly commends to us here as rich. For if this grace is to be measured by
the magnitude of the price paid for us, the resources of that grace had to be
infinite: from it flowed a price of infinite value and merit to redeem us;
from it such precious blood, so dear to God, was expended and poured out to
redeem us.

[Marginalia: Consequence.]

From this we see in passing how the Apostle wishes us always to look to that
first fountain of our salvation and continually turn our eyes toward that
free *eudokia* for us, whether we have before our eyes our election, our
predestination to adoption as sons, the redemption we have through Christ’s
blood, or any other part, cause, or means of salvation. And to what end does
he wish us always to observe God’s grace except that we may acknowledge it as
it is, both glorious and rich, and, having acknowledged it, celebrate it
forever?

It remains, after these things have been thus explained, for us to derive
several observations from this same verse.

[Marginalia: First observation. Isaiah 63:1, and following.]

First, how free is that benefit of redemption which comes to us through
Christ’s blood! Of the price so often pressed upon us, we see that not even
the smallest portion, not even a penny or farthing, was paid by us, but it was
paid by Christ alone, who alone shed his blood and had no partner or, so to
speak, co-redeemer in this work. This accords with that notable and
wonderfully moving dialogue which we have at the beginning of Isaiah 63: “Who
is this coming from Edom, from Bozrah, with garments stained?” asks the
Church, scarcely recognizing Christ the Savior, spattered with the blood of
his enemies. He replies in such a way as to show himself the sole Savior,
King, and Teacher of his Church: “I who speak justly,” he says, “mighty to
save.” Then later, “I have trodden the winepress alone, for no one from the
peoples was with me.” Again, “When I looked and there was no helper, and was
astonished that no one upheld me, my own arm brought salvation for me.” “For
me” means for my mystical body, the Church, for which by my power alone I
obtained salvation—or rather, by my power I vindicated myself from all my
enemies: I conquered the devil and the world, and death and the grave as
well, when by my own authority I took up again the life which I had laid down
of my own accord, John 10, and gloriously rose from death. Since, therefore,
in forgiving us our sins God required not our blood but that of the Mediator
and Testator himself, it is clearly evident that this benefit is given to us
entirely freely, apart from any merit of ours, and is thus wholly free with
respect to us.

[Marginalia: Consequence. Distinction concerning the fourth mode of
redemption.]

There are four modes in all of redeeming captives. We have already explained
how two of them have a place in this matter, namely by paying a price and by
snatching the captive through force from the enemies’ power. Of the remaining
two, one, which occurs through an exchange of captives and is often practiced
in war, has no place here. No one is found, nor can anyone be given, who was
in the same condition as us—that is, oppressed by a similar captivity—and
was so exchanged with us that, just as we on one side, so he on the other
attained liberty and the forgiveness of sins. Such a mode of our redemption
is plainly absurd and inexplicable. The other occurs when someone is freely
manumitted with no price exacted for his liberty. With a distinction, this
can be adapted to the present subject. With respect to us, we may say that
God the Father gave us liberty entirely freely, forgave our sins, and
absolved us from wrath, condemnation, and the curse of his law, with no price
at all exacted from us, as has already been said, from his bare and infinite
mercy and so according to the riches of his grace, as the Apostle speaks
here. With respect to Christ the Redeemer, however, this mode has no place at
all. He paid a fully sufficient price to God the Father for our liberty, and
from him the heavenly Father exacted our debt to the very last farthing, so
that his inviolable justice might be satisfied and that infinite mercy might
do it no injury.

Let us therefore conclude that this benefit is free to us in every way,
whether we consider our own exemption, since nothing was exacted from us, or
the free imputation of a price paid by another, by which the most gracious
Father credited to our account what Christ alone expended, as soon as we are
found united through faith to Christ as our head.

[Marginalia: Note secondly the twofold union.]

For we should observe in passing that in the redemption we have through
Christ a certain twofold union comes into consideration. Upon the first
depends the merit and efficacy of Christ’s blood, namely the hypostatic union
of the human nature with the eternal *Logos*. This imparts every power and
vigor to the death and blood of Christ the man, so that it may be a *lytron
autarkes*, abundantly sufficient for the perfect redemption of all. Upon the
second depends its derivation and application to us, namely our spiritual
union with Christ, through which we become partakers of his redemption
because we are in Christ himself and are therefore redeemed in him, or have
redemption in him, as was observed above.

[Marginalia: Second observation. Objection. Leviticus 16:21.]

Second, since we have redemption—that is, the forgiveness of sins, as the
Apostle explains it—through Christ’s blood, this also proves the Apostle’s
axiom in Hebrews 9: “Without the shedding of blood no forgiveness takes
place.” Consequently, that fabricated sacrifice of the Mass—I mean that
bloodless papal victim offered for the remission of sins, both for the living
and the dead—is rejected as an abominable and blasphemous superstition,
injurious and insulting to Christ’s death and the redemption we have through
his blood. Yet the papists attempted to defend it by, among other things,
this sophism; I pass over their other evasions, which are more remote from
the present subject. It seemed to me that this sophism should be examined
here as directly pertinent to the words of the Apostle which we have been
explaining. I know that some of their doctors, pressed by that apostolic
authority in Hebrews 9, tried to evade the testimony cited from it by
appealing to the solemn sacrifice of two goats described in Leviticus 16:—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 68 | Caput I p. 26 (through Lecture VIII close)]

—one of which, the one upon which Jehovah’s lot had fallen, was slain and its
blood used for the atonement of the altar and sanctuary, and so forth; while
the other, upon which Azazel’s lot had fallen, was sent alive into the
wilderness. It was loaded with all the people’s sins, iniquities, and
transgressions, which Aaron the high priest laid upon its head, and had to
carry them with it to a desert and uninhabitable place so that they might
never afterward come into the sight or memory of God or men, but might be
utterly abolished as though sunk into the depths of the sea, as Micah 7:19
says. “See, therefore,” they say, “even an atoning sacrifice without blood,
and an expiation and abolition, without the shedding of blood, of the sins

[Marginalia: Micah 7:19. Answer.]

Now, in order not only to answer this objection but also to reconcile those
two passages of Scripture which in appearance—or at least on their
assumptions—conflict, we shall not answer with the commonplace that no firm
conclusion is drawn from symbolic theology. Rather, according to the measure
of understanding granted to us, we shall attempt to elucidate both the
mystery of this sacrifice and the difficulty raised by our adversaries. We
say, therefore, that in that solemn annual rite for atoning for the whole
Israelite Church, at least typically and symbolically, both goats discharge
the office of one sacrifice and signify one and the same future sacrifice of
Christ for the full and perfect atonement of the sins of all the elect. Our
opponents therefore wrongly separate things which must be taken together,
and so commit that kind of sophism commonly called reasoning from things
joined to things divided.

Although the atonement of our sins through the Redeemer’s blood was not
foreshadowed in that typical sacrifice with respect to the goat for Azazel,
whose blood was not shed, it does not therefore follow absolutely that any
typical sacrifice exists which represents Christ’s sacrifice offered on the
cross without blood. The shedding of Christ’s blood, and thus his bloody
death, was foreshadowed by the other goat which was slaughtered and slain.

[Marginalia: Explanation. Romans 4:25.]

We should therefore say instead that, when the Lord wished to portray to his
people our redemption to be accomplished through Christ more fully and
perfectly in this solemn annual rite for atoning for the whole Church each
year on the appointed day, the first day of the seventh month, than in the
ordinary sacrifices which were customarily slain daily, either by the
perpetual decree of the continual sacrifice or whenever an occasion arose,
he additionally ordained two goats to represent mysteries which could not
conveniently be shown through one animal alone. Either its blood would not be
shed at all, which was contrary to the divine institution that sins be atoned
for only with blood; or the slain animal would have to return miraculously to
life, which was contrary to his ordinary administration and counsel in his
works. These two goats therefore represented both that Christ would shed his
blood for our sins and that by his death he would carry them away with
himself outside the Church, so that they would never again come into God’s
sight and memory but be utterly abolished as if they had never been done and
committed. Yet, after atoning for our sins by his blood, Christ himself could
not be held in death, but by his own power freed himself from the bonds of
death and escaped, just as Romans 4:25 says that he “was handed over to death
because of our offenses, but raised for our justification.”

In a word, therefore, the slain goat foreshadowed Christ dying for us on the
cross. But the other goat, the *aphetēsios* or *apopompaios*—for so the
Septuagint translated what in Hebrew is called the goat for Azazel—represented
the same Christ as abolishing and blotting out our sins from the sight of God
and the saints. It thus represented the fruit of Christ’s death and blood in
relieving us of sin’s burden. The *aphetēsios* goat would never have carried
away the people’s sins unless the other goat had first been slain and atoned
for them all by its blood, as is evident from the very order and sequence of
that sacrifice, most carefully described by Moses in the chapter of Leviticus
just cited. In the same way, Christ would certainly never have abolished our
sins and relieved us of their burden unless he had first atoned for them on
the cross by his blood and satisfied God the Father’s wrath for them.

[Marginalia: Noteworthy comparison. Leviticus 16:20. Conclusion.]

We therefore see that this symbolic and typical sacrifice of the two goats is
so far from weakening or invalidating in any way that apostolic axiom in
Hebrews 9, that sins are not forgiven without the shedding of blood, that on
the contrary nothing more clearly establishes and confirms it. This is
evident to every sound-minded person who considers what has been said.

[Marginalia: Third observation. Consequence. Contrast. 1 Corinthians 6:20.
Extended treatment.]

The final thing we observe from this same passage is this: How great is the
gravity of sin, which could be atoned for only by the blood of God’s Son! How
great was the force of divine wrath and the weight of eternal condemnation
which lay upon us, which no one but the one Christ, the *Theanthrōpos*, could
bear and remove! How foul and abominable is the stain of sin in the sight of
the most holy God! How shameful and hideous is the native stain of our
corruption, which had to be washed by such precious blood and cleansed and
purged by such a divine bath! From this we should learn utterly to hate sin
as the soul’s plague and most horrible destruction. We should not only shun
sin itself, but studiously avoid all its handles and occasions, all the
slopes and precipices on our paths by which the impulse of nature itself
carries and inclines us toward its borders. We should guard ourselves
anxiously and circumspectly against all its snares, traps, and enticements,
and always suspect whatever is pleasing to our flesh and its deceitful
affections.

Conversely, when we hear that we have been bought and redeemed at this
price—indeed, at so great a price that nothing of equal value or weight can
be found anywhere in heaven or on earth to balance its infinite weight,
except the riches of the grace which freely gave us this precious sacrifice
and at the same time forgave us our debts—should we not draw from it the same
conclusion as the Apostle does in 1 Corinthians 6:20? When he dissuades the
faithful from every impurity of sexual immorality, he reminds them that they
were bought with a price and immediately infers: “Therefore glorify God in
your body and in your spirit, which are God’s.” Certainly, if we have been
redeemed by Christ, and that by his precious blood, we are no longer our own
to live for ourselves and pollute our bodies or souls as we please with
vices, pleasures, vanities, or any defilements of sin. Rather, to the degree
that we wish to be certain that we are in him in whom alone we have
redemption from such wrath—which still remains and will forever remain upon
all who are outside Christ—and from such misery and tyranny, to the same
degree let us diligently avoid all those things which procured his so bitter
and shameful death. Let us instead serve throughout our lives in spirit and
truth the one who died, rose, and lived again in order to have dominion over
both the living and the dead. To him, therefore, who lives and reigns forever
together with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be all honor, dominion, and
majesty forever. Amen.
