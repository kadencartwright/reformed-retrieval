The final matter is what appears at the end of the clause and is said of
Abraham's faith: ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην, that it was reckoned or
credited to him as righteousness. These words require a deeper inquiry. Two
principal questions may be asked here:

1. Did the apostle appropriately cite this passage from Genesis for his
present purpose?

2. In what manner or sense is Abraham's faith, or his believing, credited to
him as righteousness?

Regarding the first question, whether the apostle appropriately cited this
passage from Genesis for the matter in hand, two objections may be raised.
Here the apostle is intent on proving that a sinful person—or, as he calls
him in verse 5, the ungodly—is justified not by works but by faith alone. Yet
an argument from Abraham's example seems scarcely suitable, at least when
drawn from Genesis 15:6, from which the words before us are recited.

First, Abraham had been justified before the time when Genesis 15:6 said of
him, “Abram believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness.” He
had already left his homeland at God's command in chapter 12, an act he
performed by faith, as the apostle testifies in Hebrews 11:8. God had also
already made a covenant with him in Genesis 12:2–3, and had renewed and
confirmed it in verse 7 and again in Genesis 13:14–17. Thus the apostle
seems wrongly to cite this testimony here to prove the first justification
of an ungodly person by faith alone. When this was pronounced of Abraham he
was not ungodly, and it was not the first justification about which the
apostle speaks here.

Second, the apostle here treats faith in Christ for justification to eternal
life, yet nothing like that seems to appear in Abraham's faith commended in
this passage from Genesis. His faith regarded the promise made to him there,
which was not about eternal life but about offspring in verse 4—offspring as
numerous as possible in verse 5. This is plain both from the promise's very
words and from the occasion on which it was given, namely Abraham's
complaint over his lack of a child in verses 2–3. What, then, does Abraham's
faith concerning bodily offspring have in common with a sinner's faith in
Christ for justification and the eternal salvation of his soul?

As to the first objection:

Some answer that the apostle is not speaking of Abraham's first
justification, in which he was first made righteous from being ungodly, but
of a second justification by which the first was increased and declared.
They say that he nevertheless proves his principal proposition here by an
argument from the greater to the lesser: that in first justification a
person is justified not by works but by faith. The argument runs thus: if
Abraham was not justified by works in his

[1679 printed p. 203 | IA leaf 211]

second justification, when good works were present, much less was he so
justified in his first, when good works were absent because he had
previously been ungodly. But Abraham was not justified by works even in his
second justification, but by faith. This is evident from the passage in
Genesis, where his faith is said to be credited to him as righteousness
although, with respect to first justification, he was already righteous and
had performed good works. Adam Sasbout and Pererius follow this opinion;
Estius praises it above the rest. Cornelius a Lapide considers it probable,
although he prefers another.

We rightly reject this division of justification into first and second in
the Roman sense. We do acknowledge a secondary declaration before human
beings, through good works, of a true justification by faith. Some of our
writers understand the apostle James's “justification by works” in that
sense, although it is not entirely clear that James treats that declaration.
Paul, however, speaks neither here nor throughout this whole disputation on
justification about such a justification, but about justification before
God. Insofar as it removes the guilt of particular daily sins, or continues
the application of justification already accomplished, it may in some
manner be called a second justification. Yet with respect to the general
state of the justified person, from which by God's grace he does not
subsequently fall, and the irreversible acceptance of that person for life,
this first justification occurs all at once and once for all, and admits no
second justification.

I acknowledge that their argument, as they formulate it, is sound: if we are
not justified by works in a second justification, as they call it, much
less are we so justified in the first. On this account we thank them for
conceding that Abraham's good works, even those performed after and from
faith, do not justify in either a second or, still less, a first
justification. Yet this very point they deny most vehemently. With great
force, though unsuccessful effort, they contend that the works excluded
from justification in this chapter and throughout Paul's disputation mean
works done before faith, not those done after faith.

Perhaps, however, the inference may seem invalid: because Abraham, already
made righteous, was justified by faith, it does not follow that he was
previously justified by faith when ungodly. Indeed, as I have said, the
argument from the greater to the lesser is quite firm. If even the works of
Abraham, who was righteous at this point, could not justify him, and both
those righteous works and the righteous man himself needed to rest on faith
alone to be accepted, then much less could his former works justify him
while he was ungodly, when those works either did not exist or were evil.
Thus in that condition above all he needed to rest upon faith alone for his
first acceptance or justification.

Second, therefore, others—even among the Roman writers (I mean Toledo and
Cornelius a Lapide)—understand first justification here. This is not because
Abraham had not previously believed and been justified, as Augustine and
Anselm indeed suppose Abraham was still ungodly when Genesis 15 spoke this
of him. We, together with the Roman writers, rightly reject that view; the
commentators plainly prove the contrary from Genesis 15 and Hebrews 11.
Instead, they say that the words also refer to Abraham's first justification
and should not be referred merely to what immediately precedes, but to all
the promises in the prior chapters, namely chapters 12 and 13. Genesis 12
mentions God's first initiation of the covenant with Abra-

[1679 printed p. 204 | IA leaf 212]

ham and therefore his first calling and justification.

Estius considers this improbable and forced for two reasons. First, the
plain text of Scripture requires the word “believed” to be referred
specifically to the immediately preceding promise of numerous offspring. It
does not permit it to be drawn back across many intervening events to other,
much earlier promises. Answer: that is the very question. Although the word
is attached to the immediately preceding promise of offspring, can it not
nevertheless be referred to everything promised before? Second, Estius
urges that “if the passage speaks of first justification, the word
‘believed’ cannot possibly be referred to the present promise, but must be
referred only to the one made when Abraham was not yet justified. Therefore
it cannot stand as referring at once to the present and the earlier
promises.” But this too is the point in question; indeed, the contrary is
asserted. Estius needed to prove that “believed” is so confined to this one
promise just made that, on its occasion, it could not be extended to the
earlier promises.

Third, then, to state my own judgment and make the solution to this
objection clearer, I would say the following.

First, it is absolutely certain that Abraham had both believed and been
justified before Genesis 15 said these things of him, even though neither is
previously stated in the words of Genesis 15:6. Before this fourth occasion,
he had received the promise three times: first in Genesis 12:2–3, second in
Genesis 12:7, and third in Genesis 13:14–17. In connection with each of them,
something is separately said of Abraham which plainly declares that he then
believed and consequently had been justified. For example, after the first
promise in Genesis 12, we read that he immediately obeyed God and left his
homeland in verse 4. Without faith he would neither have willed nor been
able to do this, and so Hebrews 11:8 attributes it to his faith. Immediately
after the promise's second and third repetition, the text mentions his
further progress on his journey and his calling on God's name after building
an altar (Genesis 12:8; 13:18). These things testify clearly enough to his
faith and justification. Although Genesis does not have the words of 15:6
before that point, they must necessarily be understood: Abraham had also
believed and been justified earlier. The promises in chapters 12 and 13
were the same as those in chapter 15, and indeed more extensive. Here only
numerous offspring are promised. There, possession of the land of Canaan
and the blessing of all nations in this offspring are also promised to that
offspring. Although those things are not expressed here, they are
understood, so that the passages may explain and complete one another. This
often occurs in Scripture's repeated histories and prophecies, and
especially in the Gospels, where omissions in one writer are supplied by
another. The promises, therefore, were the same here and there, as was
Abraham's faith. Since that faith was homogeneous with itself, there is no
doubt that just as he is said here to have believed and to have had it
credited to him as righteousness, so he had believed similarly before and
had that faith credited as righteousness, although on this fourth occasion
his faith became more firmly established.

My second point is this. Although Abraham had believed and been justified
before, it is first said or committed to writing here because he now gave a
more excellent and illustrious demonstration of justifying faith

[1679 printed p. 205 | IA leaf 213]

concerning the promised offspring, and emerged from a most grievous struggle
over it, as commentators have rightly noted. Abraham's complaint about his
childlessness in verses 2–3 plainly indicates this: the greater his struggle
with unbelief, the greater the victory he won over it. Mercer shows that the
struggle was greater because, when the Lord had previously promised him
offspring, he was not yet beyond hope on account of his age. Nature did not
then resist, nor did his bodily condition oppose it, and so the faith by
which he then believed was not as remarkable as it now was. With his body
nearly dead, he said of himself in verse 2, “I go childless,” that is, I have
long walked as a man now grown old and indeed shall soon depart this world,
according to the Jerusalem paraphrast. The Septuagint likewise renders it,
ἐγὼ δὲ ἀπολύομαι, “I am departing.” In Hebrew idiom a dying man is called
one who is “walking,” as in 1 Kings 1:1; Joshua 13:1; Luke 2:29; and 1 Kings
2:2. Thus Abraham thought he would die without offspring, being now more
than eighty years old and having a barren wife, Sarah. Therefore, when he
believed against hope in hope as God promised offspring to one so aged and
exhausted, it was a remarkable work of faith. Our apostle amplifies it at
length in Romans 4:17–21 and therefore says specifically in verse 22 that it
was credited to him as righteousness. Cornelius a Lapide says, “Believing
something so difficult and improbable was the most extensive act of faith,
silently embracing all other things to be believed.” Origen says on the
passage, “In those things faith was manifested in part, but here perfect
faith is declared. Through each of the matters which we have recalled in
part, the whole of faith is now gathered in this one, and so credited as
righteousness.”

This, then, was a kind of resurrection of Abraham's faith in which it began
to live anew, and although he had been justified before, he perhaps now
became more certain of it. Hence what had to be understood in the earlier
promises is first expressed here. For a similar reason the apostle James
takes and applies to the still more remarkable victory of Abraham's faith
in the sacrifice of his son in Genesis 22 what is said of him only here in
Genesis 15. James 2:21, 23 thus says: “When he had offered his son Isaac on
the altar, the Scripture was fulfilled which says, Abraham believed God and
it was credited to him as righteousness.” Yet we do not read this in
Genesis 22, where the obedience of Abraham with which James deals is
narrated, but only here in Genesis 15. Therefore, just as James
transferred to the later, more remarkable victory of faith in the sacrifice
of his son the statement expressed only in Genesis 15, so what is expressly
said in Genesis 15 about Abraham's strengthened faith must likewise be
understood of his earlier demonstrations of faith in Genesis 12 and 13.
Indeed, though Moses does not say this, Paul expressly does in Galatians 3.
The very words, “Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as
righteousness,” appear in verse 6, and verse 8 applies them to Abraham's
faith in the first promise made to him in Genesis 12:3. This deserves
particular attention in resolving the difficulty.

Third, to dispatch the matter briefly, we must say that Paul's whole aim
here is to show that Abraham obtained the praise of righteousness through
the crediting of faith. Because this is stated in express words only in
Genesis 15,

[1679 printed p. 206 | IA leaf 214]

Paul attends to the fact in general, unconcerned whether it was spoken of
the first or second acquisition of this praise. Faith is homogeneous, as I
said before: it is the same faith which gives the same assent to all the
promises, although made at intervals. What is expressly said of it on one
occasion must therefore be understood of all the others. Moreover, faith is
always and everywhere of equal value with respect to justification, for it
justifies insofar as it receives Christ, and that can be done by a weak hand
as well as by a strong one. Justification accordingly admits no greater or
lesser degree according to faith's weakness or strength. Thus, although
Genesis 15 alone expressly mentions faith credited to Abraham as
righteousness, in praise of a more greatly strengthened faith, the
homogeneous character of both faith and justification permits and requires
it to be said and understood of his earlier, less-confirmed acts of faith in
chapters 12 and 13. It is therefore perfectly evident that Paul quite
appropriately accommodated to first justification, as it is called—or to
faith when it first justifies—what Genesis 15 said of Abraham's now
developed and firmly established faith.

So much for the first possible objection against what might appear to be
Paul's less-than-suitable application of that Genesis passage to his present
purpose.

The second objection can be dispatched more easily and briefly. The Genesis
passage concerns God's promise and Abraham's faith regarding numerous
offspring alone, as is plain from the preceding words. But what has this to
do with faith concerning justification and eternal life, which Paul treats
here?

The answer is easy.

The first answer is that of Melanchthon, Bucer, Calvin, Hyperius, and others
of our writers: faith by its nature regards God's mercy as its principal
object in the first place. Faith and promised mercy are correlates, and so
faith does not expect a benefit without antecedent mercy. As Melanchthon
says, “No one believes God concerning any particular matter unless he first
believes God to be propitious toward him.” But believing in God's mercy is
true justifying faith, and truly justifies. Therefore, although the
particular promised good may perhaps be temporal, in believing it faith does
not cling to that particular good. It rests on the mercy of a propitious
God, which after the fall cannot be ours without Christ. Through the medium
of this particular good it rises to the mercy of a God propitious in Christ,
and so it truly justifies. Accordingly, even if only natural offspring had
been promised to Abraham, if in believing this he rested upon God as
propitious to him in Christ, his faith would be truly justifying and could
rightly be credited to him as righteousness. Calvin says on the passage:
“Thus in believing, Abraham does nothing but embrace the grace offered him,
lest it be void. If this is credited to him as righteousness, it follows
that he is righteous only because, trusting in God's goodness, he dares to
hope for everything from him.”

I would have this understood cautiously, however, of true and saving faith.
Some people can rest on God's goodness and power concerning a promised
matter which they are either to perform or receive, without thereby being
justified. Some in former times had faith for miracles, yet not all who had
it were thereby justified. But if the faith is genuine—the faith called
that of God's elect—the prior matter has already been accomplished. That is,
when faith, as here in Abraham, believes some particular promised good, if
it rises by this handle to God's mercy in Christ and clings to that alone,

[1679 printed p. 207 | IA leaf 215]

it truly justifies, although the particular promised good was merely
temporal.

Second, however, that was not the situation here. The passage did not
promise natural offspring alone, as is evident in several ways.

First, in verses 23–24 of this chapter Paul says that this faith was credited
as righteousness not only to Abraham but also to all of us who now believe.
Faith concerning natural offspring is not given to all true believers, much
less credited to them as righteousness. Therefore, Abraham's faith which
was reckoned to him as righteousness regarded not only natural offspring,
but something else common to all believers in every age.

Second, the offspring promised to Abraham is said in verse 5 to be
innumerable like the stars. Pareus draws two things from this: its heavenly
glory and its immense number. Both apply more to Abraham's spiritual than to
his carnal offspring, from which he gathers that carnal offspring is neither
principally nor exclusively meant in that promise. His first point—that the
glory of this heavenly offspring was signified by a comparison drawn from
the stars of heaven—seems excessively subtle and forced. His latter point,
that the comparison designates the immense number of the offspring, touches
and proves the matter more closely. Although Abraham's natural posterity
grew to a very great number, the promise, shadowed forth by this comparison
with the stars, found its full fulfillment in the innumerable multitude of
all believers from every nation. Thus merely carnal offspring cannot be
understood in the promise.

Third, this is additionally evident from the promise's own words. Verse 5
does not promise only numerous offspring, for verse 1 says, “Do not fear,
Abram; I am your shield and your exceedingly great reward.” When God said
this he promised more than merely numerous posterity. Even if carnal
offspring had become most numerous, it would not have been a sufficient
reward for Abraham's faith, much less the שכרך הרבה added there—not merely
an adequate reward, still less a great reward, and least of all a very great
reward. Among the Hebrews that expression denotes the superlative, as if it
were the highest and greatest of all rewards. Abraham's carnal posterity did
not rank as such a reward, at least for his faith, without spiritual
offspring, and least of all without Christ.

This leads directly to the third answer. Even if the promise had been only
of offspring, it was not merely of natural or carnal offspring, but
principally of Christ. Paul expressly affirms in Galatians 3:16 that Christ
was the offspring promised to Abraham long before. Abraham foresaw Christ
by faith in his posterity, as Christ says that he saw his day and rejoiced
in John 8:56. Christ was also the foundation of every promise, in whom alone
they are “yes and amen,” as Paul testifies in 2 Corinthians 1:20. Thus all
the ancient faithful looked back to the proto-gospel in Genesis 3:15, the
primary promise of the woman's offspring. Indeed, in every particular
promise, even one concerning temporal matters such as natural offspring
here, which contained something typical, they looked in accordance with
that economy either to Christ himself or to something foreshadowing Christ.

[1679 printed p. 208 | IA leaf 216]

It will not be out of place to add Peter Martyr's point: the one who spoke
with Abraham here and promised him these things was the Son of God, Christ
himself, who appeared and spoke to the patriarchs of old. Thus, if Abraham
believed this God, as the passage says, he believed in Christ, for the God
who addressed him was Christ. Christ must therefore by no means be excluded
from the object of Abraham's faith in this passage.

Nor should we entirely omit what others offer in answer to this objection.
It was enough for Paul to show from Abraham's example, whatever the precise
object of his faith, that he had been justified by faith—especially to the
Jews, who venerated all Abraham's deeds—and thus that justification by faith
was not absurd, since it agreed so well with what is said of Abraham. Paul
therefore rightly infers our justification by faith in Christ, which God
now requires of us, from Abraham's justification by believing the promise
of offspring, which God then required of him. This is especially so because
Christ was included in that promised offspring as the chief and firstborn
of all.

Let this at last suffice in answer to the matters objected in the first
question: whether the apostle appropriately enough cited that passage of
Genesis for his present purpose.
