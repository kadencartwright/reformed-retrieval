SECTION FIVE NOTION III

PERKINS

God acts prudently, that is, with a definite end in view; he is not ignorant of anything; he does not will or decree what he cannot; he does not idly watch what will happen or what is happening, but disposes all things for his own glory; and therefore he has decreed to act in this way.

ARMINIUS

It belongs to a wise man to do nothing in vain. But he acts in vain who does not act for the sake of some end. God, however, is supremely wise. Yet take care, I pray, that you do not extend that expression “to watch idly” too broadly or interpret it otherwise than is fitting. To act and to permit are really distinguished. He who permits for the sake of a definite end does not watch idly. Hence it is clear that to act and to do are not the same as not to watch idly. I also give this warning for the sake of a definite end. But consider whether the phrase you use is proper. The word *prudence* seems too meager to be fitted to such great wisdom. Nor is someone said to act “with a definite end employed,” but “for the sake of an end.” The statement “God does not will or decree what he cannot” is ambiguous and not sufficiently complete. It is ambiguous because it can be understood of what he cannot will or decree, or of what he cannot do. It is less than complete because one must add that he does not will or decree *to do or permit*. Hence the conclusion too is imperfect. To “and therefore he has decreed to act in this way,” add “or to permit.”

RESPONSE 1

To act or to do and not to watch idly are not the same, he says; but Perkins neither wishes them to be the same nor signifies that here. For he does not set “to watch idly what is happening” against “to do” or “to act,” as Arminius’s contrast presents the matter. Indeed, Arminius conducts himself throughout as if he had shaken every blush from his brow. Rather, Perkins sets these against each other: “to watch idly what will happen or what is happening,” and “to dispose all things for his own glory.” But who would deny this—namely, that God must idly watch what will happen and what is happening unless he disposes all things for his own glory? Yet Arminius attacks these words just as if the author had said, “God does not idly watch what will happen or what is happening, but does all things.”

2. “The word *prudence* is too meager,” he says, and so forth—a grave error indeed, and one whose censure befits Arminius, a grave theologian indeed. But is it a meager thing to say that God acts with right reason? For prudence is defined by the Philosopher as “an active disposition accompanied by right reason.” According to the same philosopher, prudence also properly concerns things to be done, whereas wisdom concerns the contemplation of things. But let it be so: let Scripture be both our right and our rule of speech. Yet the Holy Spirit uses *wisdom* and *prudence* interchangeably in Proverbs 8:1: “Does not wisdom cry out, and prudence give forth her voice?”

3. I acknowledge that it is customary to say that someone acts “for the sake of an end.” But neither would I dare condemn the phrase employed by Perkins: whether one says “with a definite end employed” or “with a definite end proposed,” what difference is there? For I would neither wish to be detained myself nor do I desire to detain anyone with grammatical niceties. For if it were fitting for us to be occupied in finding fault with phrases in this manner, which of the Prophets or Apostles would escape untouched, since Erasmus observes that Jerome asserted that Paul had been ignorant of Greek grammar?

4. “But the statement ‘God does not will or decree what he cannot’ is ambiguous,” he says. Yet, I say, no one who considers it can doubt the author’s meaning. Certain things are here propounded concerning God which belong to him by the distinctive property of his nature, not things that can be common to him and others. But not even the vilest man wills or decrees anything that he cannot will or decree; therefore this cannot be the author’s meaning here. Yet neither is it necessary that one immediately fall into the other sense proposed by Arminius—namely, that God wills or decrees nothing which he cannot do. Rather, this can be the meaning, and a more suitable one: God wills or decrees nothing which he cannot procure to occur, either by doing it himself or by permitting it to be done by others. Once this explanation is given, the second exception at once collapses. Immediately it appears that this statement is sufficiently complete. Nor is it necessary that the opponent’s disjunctive supplement be added in the first place, since it can be understood without difficulty even without the disjunction—namely, that God does not will or decree that something should occur. This can be stated fully enough even without a supplement, as Perkins states it: “God does not will or decree anything that he cannot”; that is, he does not will anything to occur which he cannot efficaciously procure to occur, whether this is done, as I noted above, by his doing it himself or by his permitting it to be done by others.

5. Next, Perkins’s words “and therefore he has decreed to act in this way” do not seem to be interpreted as Arminius interprets them. For they mean nothing other than that, just as God disposes all things for his own glory, so too he has decreed to dispose all things for his own glory. For Perkins does not mean that God has decreed to do all the things that he disposes, or has decreed to dispose, for his own glory. For he has also decreed to dispose evils for his own glory, yet Perkins removes their production from God, as Arminius knows very well. I speak, however, of evil formally. As for evil taken materially, that is, the act underlying the evil, not only Perkins but also Arminius subjects this to divine production. I am speaking of well-known matters.
