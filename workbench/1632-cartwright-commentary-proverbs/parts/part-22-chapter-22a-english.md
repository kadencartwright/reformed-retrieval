CHAPTER XXII.

Verse 1. A good name is more desirable than ample riches, and good favour than gold and silver.

In שם (*nomen*, name) there is a synecdoche of the genus for a good reputation, as is evident from the latter hemistich. Likewise, זהב (*aurum*, gold) and כסף (*argentum*, silver) stand for much silver and much gold, as is clear from the latter hemistich.

The purpose is to incite us to the pursuit of virtue, from which a good name is obtained.

Reputation and esteem are commended by comparison with lesser things, namely riches, and even gold and silver. One who has an abundance of these is thought able to accomplish astonishing things, and it is thought that everyone will become devoted and, as it were, compliant toward him.

First, a good reputation is more useful than riches for the preservation and security of life. For in people’s goodwill, from which esteem and reputation arise, lies the safest guardianship of this life. Thus it comes about that snares are not often laid for one’s life; or, if snares are laid by those envious of our reputation, people are not lacking who uncover the snares that have been set. Riches, gold, and silver, on the contrary, serve as enticements by which thieves secretly and oppressors openly seek one’s life; and they do this all the more as people are more abundant in riches, gold, and silver.

Next, a good reputation is surer and firmer for the peace and tranquillity of life than riches are. Scarcely, if indeed ever, is someone found who is furnished with great wealth and yet is not also entangled in many lawsuits and legal disputes. To this pertains the fact that a good reputation wins more friends, and more faithful ones, than riches do. For it is

[1632 witness | PDF p. 486 | printed columns 956-957]

like poured-out ointment, Song 1. Hence it is that a good name is joined with good favour. From this come our proverbs: *A mans cause is ended, as he is friended*; and *A friend in Court is better then a penny in the purse*.

Just as an honourable reputation is more advantageous for this life, so it is more excellent after this life. For although riches perish and are taken from us with death, an honourable reputation nevertheless remains surviving after death, Prov. 10.7, and causes the dead in some manner to live. Hence it is that at 2 Sam. 7.23 it is noted as a singular benefit from God that he bestowed upon the people of God a name and reputation more celebrated than upon any people, although there existed many peoples better furnished with riches, gold, and silver.

Riches are often acquired by reputation; reputation is not redeemed by riches. Reputation falls only to the good, whereas riches fall even to the wicked.

How diligent the godly were in guarding a good name may be seen at Neh. 6.10, 11 and 2 Cor. 8.20, 21, 22. All the more, therefore, are those to be condemned who, for the empty little pleasure of wine or sex, or for a little profit, squander as it were a precious thing, bring upon themselves a mark of infamy never to be erased except by repentance, and render themselves more contemptible than the mud of the streets—indeed, than human excrement itself.

Once an honourable reputation has been obtained, however, so much greater care must be applied to guarding it, because lost riches are recovered, but reputation is not.

But here it is worthwhile for us to set before ourselves the proper end for guarding our name. There are very many who shrink from excessive wine, gluttonous indulgence, sex, plunder, and the corruptions of bribes, and who take such great account of their reputation that they judge death itself to be undergone rather than admit those things by which they would incur disgrace and contempt. For they are exceedingly thirsty for glory and honour; they would even oppose God and the Gospel rather than be willing to suffer the loss and forfeiture of their own glory. The godly, however, are solicitous to guard their esteem so that the gifts God has bestowed upon them—learning, wisdom, and eloquence—may profit people more, since, after they have rendered themselves infamous and contemptible by their own wickedness, everything they say and do is lightly valued. Hence it is that the Apostle requires those who are going to undertake the Ministry not only to be cultivated in learning and letters, but further to be adorned with a good testimony to a blamelessly conducted life, even among outsiders and those alien to religion, 1 Tim. 3. The same thing is required of Magistrates. For just as wholesome and even delicious food, when presented upon a filthy dish, may provoke disgust and nausea, so splendid gifts of God are distributed by a foul and filthy hand.

But if it is more excellent than riches to be esteemed by people and to remain in their good favour, how much more excellent than riches is it to be esteemed and loved by almighty God? Thus those must be profane Esaus who exchange the grace and kindness of God for the momentary conveniences of this life.

Therefore, that reputation by which God is lost must be avoided; conversely, the reproach which arises from duty rendered to God must not be feared, Matt. 5.

Verse 2. The rich man and the poor man meet; Jehovah is the maker of them all.

The purpose is to bind the rich and the poor to one another by mutual duties.

Because of the unlike condition of the poor and the rich, there is also often a great separation of minds: the rich love none but the rich, and the poor none but those who are pressed by want

[1632 witness | PDF p. 487 | printed columns 958-959]

(as they themselves are); the poor decide that the rich are unworthy and unfit for the kingdom of heaven, while the rich decide that the poor are unworthy to inhabit the earth. Hence the rich even shun the neighbourhood, much less the friendship, of the poor, while the poor shrink from the neighbourhood of the rich, lest they be swallowed up by them. It is as though it would be expedient for there to be two worlds, one of the poor and the other of the rich.

But the Prophet, in order to extinguish these hostilities, draws an argument from their common effect of meeting one another. He therefore teaches that they ought not to burn with mutual hatred and shrink from one another; they are not to be like wild beasts, which God has removed far from human beings into forests and deserted places lest they harm them, but like tame and domestic animals useful to us for the needs of life. Thus the poor ought to be useful and advantageous to the rich, and the rich to the poor. For if those beasts which are gregarious contribute something of importance to the convenience of the lives of those with whom they associate, how much more does this occur among human beings, who participate in reason? The matter is so arranged that everywhere, not only in all kingdoms and provinces but in towns and villages, Churches, and finally larger households, the rich are mingled with the poor. Hence that saying of Christ: “You will always have the poor with you, but you will not always have me,” Matt. 26.

But the principal argument is from the efficient cause of this meeting, namely God, who by his most wise providence has so ordered it, not only that he might make his wisdom known in this manner—since, as happens in the arts, he made his work more artfully elaborate in this way when he willed human society to consist of various orders of people, nobles, rich and poor, Magistrates and subjects, as a tapestry consists of various colours and images—but also for the good of human beings themselves. For most functions of this life are unsuitable for the rich and noble to discharge, yet these functions are so necessary that human society cannot do without them; such are service and a master’s authority. For this society could not stand unless there were those who, compelled by poverty, gave themselves into service. Just as the head cannot say to the foot, “I have no need of your work,” so neither can the rich man say it to the poor man. Next, if there were no rich people who supplied what is lacking to the need of the poor, the poor would be killed by hunger or cold.

Moreover, the poor bring great assistance to the rich not only in servile and bodily ministries, but sometimes they also bring very great aid by their sound and ingenious counsels, as we read of Naaman’s servant at 2 Kings 5. Solomon also testifies that a besieged city was delivered by a poor man, Eccles. 9. For God sometimes bestows his treasures upon the abject so that he may vindicate them from the contempt of the rich and of Magnates. And just as no region is so barren that it is not endowed with certain gifts from God by which it may profit the wealthiest kingdom—so that in this manner he might bind together peoples separated by a long interval of sea and land—so no order of human beings is so humble that it does not contribute its share to the preservation of human society.

Away, therefore, with those hostilities between the rich and the poor! Away with the rich people’s disdain for the poor! Away with the poor people’s envy of the rich, unless perhaps it pleases us to wage war with God after the manner of the Giants!

Verse 3. The prudent man foresees evil and hides himself, but fools pass on and are punished.

In ערום (*astutus*, prudent) there is a metaphor drawn from naked people, who turn themselves this way and that.

The purpose is to rouse people to caution and circumspection, after shaking off stupor and security.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 488 | printed columns 960-961]

This prudence arises first from the use of reason, the mind’s counsellor, when it is by no means obscured or oppressed by the passions. For God placed this light of reason in human minds so that by its benefit and discourse they might guard themselves against evils hanging over them. And certainly, by the benefit of this reason there are many imbued with no spark of the Holy Spirit who, foreseeing long beforehand the disadvantages arising from drunkenness, adultery, contention, and ambition, conduct their lives soberly, chastely, peaceably, and modestly, as far as the civil manner of life is concerned. Thus those who plan these shameful deeds sin against the very standard of reason and are rebels against the light of nature. But the light of the word, like the splendour of the sun, far surpasses this light of reason, as the sun’s light surpasses that of a star; it renders God’s servant cautious and circumspect, Ps. 19. It also renders a young man more prudent than his elders, teachers, and enemies, Ps. 219.98, 99, 100.

An example of the prudence in the first part is set forth in Joseph. When by the prophetic spirit he had foreseen seven years of famine, by the discourse of reason he also foresaw how great the losses were which consequently threatened the kingdom of Egypt and especially the Church. He therefore entered upon a plan by which he might repel those losses and hide himself and others from the evil that was bearing down upon them.

An excellent example of the prudence in the latter part appears in Rechab. Carefully weighing the Prophets’ threats and the people’s sins which would bring upon them the announced Babylonian captivity, he fashioned his sons by a more austere manner of life to bear that captivity more equably. The same can be said of the Church which migrated from Jerusalem to the town named Pella in the desert in order to avoid the deadly siege of the Romans which Christ had foretold would come within the century, Rev. 12.14. So Noah, seeing from the word that the flood was bearing down, withdrew into the ark.

Therefore, the light of reason granted to us by nature must not be overwhelmed with drunkenness, fornication, and gluttony, and we must labour especially so that, illuminated from heaven by knowledge of the word, we may obtain for ourselves a refuge from future judgments. This occurs most of all when with confidence in God we cast ourselves into his protection. See Isa. 26.20; it occurs when we call upon him for aid by prayers, Ps. 17.8, 9.

This teaching is illustrated by its opposite, that “fools pass on,” and so forth. Fools, therefore, are those who, after the light of nature is extinguished and the heavenly light set aside, are driven by a storm of passions and, as it were, by a whirlwind, and rush headlong into evils, no differently than animals devoid of reason and made for capture, 2 Pet. 2.12. Indeed, they are worse than the brute beasts themselves. For there are birds, such as cranes and Storks, which by a certain sense and forewarning of the approaching winter withdraw at the proper time into warmer regions. Beasts of burden also turn toward their shelters through a presentiment of rain soon to come. But these fools, blinded by their desires, are driven headlong into dangers and perish. For, as though deprived of their eyes, they do not even see the things set before their feet, but like oxen led to slaughter, thinking of nothing, they are led by the cord of their desires. An illustrious example of this appears among the citizens of Laisbis at Judg. 18.17, 8. From this improvident manner of acting springs that saying of fools, *Non putâram*, “I had not thought it.”

From all these things it is gathered that divine providence, which governs everything and necessarily brings to completion each thing that occurs at its own time and in the manner appointed by God, does not prevent us from being praised for deeds performed prudently and condemned for rash ones. For our will does not take away providence, nor does providence take away the will.

Those who also misuse this statement to defend themselves from persecutions at the expense of truth, as though

[1632 witness | PDF p. 489 | printed columns 962-963]

Solomon were praising ἐμφορτίζειν τῶν ποδῶν κωδώνιον, loading a little bell upon the feet, drag it into a wholly alien sense. For that is not to be accounted prudence, but a certain crafty cunning, by which evil is not avoided but attracted and summoned.

But when the Prophet does not say that they foresee nothing, but only that they pass on and are punished, he thereby teaches that it sometimes happens that these fools foresee the evils to come but nevertheless are so swept crosswise by their desires that, living and seeing—as the saying goes—they perish and, like Amphiaraus, knowingly hasten to destruction. So we read it happened to the woman who said, “I see and approve the better things; I follow the worse.” Thus, though endowed with eyes for seeing the evil, they are caught by their feet and cannot flee from it.

For drunkards and adulterers are not ignorant of the infamy and disgrace, the diseases and sickness, which they contract from their intemperance; yet a sense of the disadvantages present in this age, or to come in the age to come, does not deter them from those shameful deeds of theirs.
