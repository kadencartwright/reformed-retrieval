He nevertheless strives on pages 27 and 28 to show by three further reasons
that this position is dangerous. The first in summary is this: that this
confusion of precepts first arose from the Jews, who did not distinguish
precepts of such different natures as they ought to have done, but thought
that they all contributed to righteousness. Reply: Although he strives at
length to show us that the Jews thought all the precepts contributed equally
to righteousness, and therefore judged them all equally perpetual and
immutable, he nevertheless accomplishes nothing. We can answer briefly that
the Jews were faulted for thinking they would have life in commanded works of
every kind, or that they would possess a right to eternal life through good
works, ceremonies, and the like, as the Apostle teaches us in Romans 9:31–32
and 10:3. We therefore have nothing in common with them, since we oppose
Christ to works and affirm that we are justified and obtain a right to eternal
life through faith in Christ alone, apart from works of any law, whether moral
or ceremonial. “Is it any wonder,” he continues, “that those who believed such
things did not require the obedience and death of Christ for the forgiveness
of sins and righteousness? Is it any wonder that they sought righteousness
through their 613 precepts, which was the reason for their unbelief and
rejection, as the Apostle teaches in Romans 9:31–32? It is surely no wonder,
since they regarded all the precepts as contributing to life—that is, as we
say, moral—and did not distinguish from the former the other precepts that
were not good, in which no one will live. How great a stumbling block is thus
placed before the Jews, and how difficult their conversion is made, when that
first error of theirs is restored by a right of return!” Reply: If the Jews
believed that through good works, offerings of sacrifices for sins, and the
like they would be just before God and possess a right to life, as the Apostle
testifies in the cited passage that they believed, it is surely no wonder that
they did not require the obedience and death of Christ for the forgiveness of
sins and righteousness. It can nevertheless be a wonder that they sought
righteousness through their 613 precepts—some of which at least he will
concede were moral—or thought that through them they would attain life, even
if they regarded all of them as moral, that is, as things which had to be
obeyed because of God’s precept. I am then quite unable to cease wondering
that the adversary says moral precepts are the same as precepts contributing
to life, and that through our assertion we place a stumbling block before the
Jews and make their conversion more difficult, when this charge is directed
against him, not us. For their first error was seeking righteousness in works
of law, and this was by far the most dangerous. The adversary restores this
error at least in part by a right of return when he says that all moral
precepts “contribute to life”—that is, are such that a right to eternal life
is acquired through obedience rendered to them. What else is this than to
pursue the law of righteousness from works of law rather than from faith, and
to establish our own righteousness? Does the Gospel teach this? What? Are we
being carried at full sail toward the Papists? We know how to distinguish
between ceremonial precepts and precepts properly called moral. But as regards
our justification, or acquiring righteousness before God and a right to
eternal life, we equally reject all our works, both those which he calls moral
and the others. For anyone who believes that he will acquire a right to
eternal life by rendering obedience to moral precepts restores the Covenant
of works by a right of return, overturns the covenant of grace, and does not
wish to require the

obedience and death of Christ for the forgiveness of sins and righteousness.
Let Dr. *Burmann* now see where he is rushing.

His second reason is that “all σαρκικὴ τυραννὶς, ‘carnal tyranny,’ is thus
confused with true piety.” Why so, I ask? Because, as he supposes, nothing else
can result from this “than Pharisaism and the righteousness of works, when
external things are regarded as morally good and contributing to life. This,”
he says, “introduced Papism just as much as Pharisaism. The same spirit,
worship, and exertion must therefore be spent upon carnal precepts as upon
inward ones, for they are all equally moral and contribute to life.” Reply: If
we were to say that we must spend the same spirit, worship, and exertion upon
external things—as he implies that we ought to expend upon inward things—then
we would indeed establish Pharisaism and Papism. He must see for himself how
he will free himself from the charge of Papism. We say that all Christians
must endeavor to obey all of God’s precepts, whether they command external or
internal things. Yet they must do so not for the end of pursuing righteousness
before God and a right to eternal life, for in that way we would not keep
ourselves free from the Papists’ taint, but for other, very different reasons.
It would be lengthy to enumerate them all here, and they may be found in the
practical theologians.

What does he finally offer? “How great a yoke,” he says, “is thereby imposed
upon consciences!” Why, I ask? “Whatever positive precepts he gave in the New
Testament, and whatever the Apostles established or advised concerning rites,
order, and ceremonies, will therefore be moral and contribute to salvation.”
Reply: No yoke at all is thereby imposed upon consciences other than what
ought to be imposed, namely, that we obey God in all his precepts, including
positive ones concerning rites, order, or ceremonies. 2. We do not say that
obedience rendered to those precepts which he regards as moral contributes to
salvation in the sense suggested above any more than obedience rendered to
these. By the latter obedience no less than the former, God’s supremacy and
our subjection are declared, and we can attest our love, respect, reverence,
and so forth toward God.

“What place,” he continues, “will such people assign to that Apostolic
ἐπάναγκες, ‘necessary things,’ in Acts 15:24, by which abstinence from things
sacrificed to idols, blood, and what is strangled was enjoined upon all the
faithful from the Gentiles? This precept too is certainly Moral. And since it
is enjoined far more expressly in the New Testament than the Lord’s Day, and
no express restriction upon its duration was added, why do they not equally
believe that conscience remains bound by its obligation? Further, what will
not result concerning all rites and indifferent things if they are urged as
moral precepts?” Reply: 1. We say that the obedience rendered to that
Apostolic constitution was good and moral, with the word taken broadly—as we
always take it here—for as long as that constitution remained in force, or as
long as the offense continued and those ceremonies had not become deadly. Yet
we do not call that precept moral insofar as *moral* is distinguished from
what is *positive* and *temporary*. 2. It was indeed enjoined more expressly
than the Lord’s Day because at that time there was no doubt concerning
observance of the Lord’s Day and no scruple had arisen in anyone’s mind. 3.
There was no need to add an express restriction upon the time of its duration,
since the foundation and principal reason for that constitution or decree
sufficiently indicated that it would remain in force only as long as the
offense occasioned continued. 4. We do not urge rites or indifferent things
as moral precepts. It is nevertheless moral, and not indifferent, that we
observe in holiness the rites instituted by God.

What further? “What disquiet, anxiety, and worry will not arise from this?

What disturbances of Churches, commonwealths, and consciences will not spring
from it? What subjection of the
people under the beggarly elements and rites of the world? What conversion of
true piety into external works?” Reply: None at all, unless obeying God in all
things is regarded as disquiet, anxiety, worry, a disturbance of Churches and
so forth, subjection under the beggarly elements of the world, and a
conversion of piety into external works. This alone is what we maintain: that
God must be obeyed in whatever he enjoins us to do or omit; that it is good to
obey God; and that the action which he commands to be done, insofar as it is
considered in this way, is morally good, not evil or indifferent, because the
precept removes the indifference of the act, either in its species or only in
its individual instance.
