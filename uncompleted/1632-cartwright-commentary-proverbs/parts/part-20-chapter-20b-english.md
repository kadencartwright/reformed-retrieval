Verse 5. Deep waters are

[1632 witness | PDF p. 430 | printed columns 834-835]

counsel in the heart of a man, but a man of understanding will draw them out.

In איש (*viri*, man) there is a common synecdoche. In דלה (*exhauriet*, will draw out) there is a metaphor drawn from buckets and pails, by which waters, however deep, are drawn from a well. It is transferred to prudence, as though by a pail the hidden thoughts of another person’s mind are summoned into the light and are sounded as though with a plummet, or a rope sunk into the deep.

The purpose is to exhort us to true prudence on account of its excellent fruit.

Psalm 64.7 confirms what is said here, that a person’s heart is deep. And at Jer. 17.9 the Prophet affirms that the human heart is deceitful above all things and has many hiding places and recesses. This is amplified by the illustrious metaphor in this Proverb, when the heart is compared to waters sunk deeply and lying hidden in a well, which appear when drawn out by a pail.

But what follows seems to contain a contradiction of this Proverb. For when the Prophet asks who knows the heart, the answer is that the Lord searches it and, as though with the plummet of his all-knowing eye, sounds it and penetrates its bottom. With this agrees what is written at 1 Kings 8 and Acts 1, that there is one God who knows the hearts of human beings. Since this is true, how does the affirmation of this Proverb stand, that a prudent man draws it out? I answer that the prophet does not embrace here everything that lies hidden in a person’s mind, but only those things which it is important for human beings to know. Next, God’s knowledge extends to those things which will ever afterward enter a person’s mind in the whole course of life, whereas human knowledge fishes out only those things which are presently active in the mind. Third, the Prophet is not speaking here of the precise and exact knowledge of the heart which belongs to God alone, but of probable conjecture and discernment, to which a person can attain.

For since prudence and understanding in a mortal person’s mind are a certain small part of the divine image, it ought not to seem surprising if they obtain marvellous fruits and effects in the investigation of hidden things. But if someone asks from what sources and by what arts a person may attain the knowledge of such hidden matters, first it is by an extraordinary gift of the Holy Spirit, which God formerly bestowed upon very many when the Church was being born and which the Apostle calls διακρίσεις πνευμάτων, discernings of spirits, at 1 Cor. 12.10. Yet this passage must not be understood of that gift, since it descended from heaven in a single moment without any human work or industry. Solomon, however, speaks of that discernment of spirits which, although it is a gift of God bestowed upon human beings, nevertheless does not arise except through human study and labour, especially meditation upon the divine law. In this, knowledge of history, above all sacred history, will be of very great benefit; next, comparison of the things previously done by the person whom we wish to investigate with those things which we now inquire into; and finally, observation of the countenance, the winking of the eyes, and the biting of the lips, Isa. 30. By the former, the devising of evil is indicated; by the latter, the contriving of that same evil. Likewise, judgment may be made from a person’s very gait, as at Eccles. 10.3, and from other gestures of the body, Prov. 6.3; next, from his company, since each person must be judged to be such as those are with whom he is accustomed to associate, Prov. 13.20. Hence it is not difficult to expose those who, although they profess the Gospel, nevertheless maintain as their own both papist functionaries and papist attendants. So also a judgment concerning the mind may be made from a contracted affinity. See the example of Joram at 2 Kings 8.17, who had married a wife from Ahab’s family. Finally, it may be made through questions prudently posed, by which a person, as though attending to something else, fishes out what he desires. Those are especially furnished with this understanding

[1632 witness | PDF p. 431 | printed columns 836-837]

who study the word of God zealously and continually and conform themselves to obedience toward God, Ps. 119, section 13.

Verse 6. Among the multitude of people, each person proclaims his kindness, but who can find a man of fidelities?

In איש אמונים (*virum fidelitatum*, a man of fidelities) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for a man who is sincerely and truly liberal, and there is a synecdoche of the class: a faithful person in general stands for one who is faithful in liberality.

The purpose is, by condemning the painted mask of liberality, to exhort us to true and sincere liberality.

Painted and masked liberality is exposed in many ways. First, indeed, it is exposed from its efficient cause, when what is given does not proceed from love toward the person upon whom it is conferred, nor from compassion for another’s want and poverty. For what does not flow from love is unworthy of the name of kindness and beneficence, 1 Cor. 13. Likewise, it is exposed from its end, when it is done not so much for the sake of helping and relieving another’s necessity as for display and the vaunting of one’s own glory, like the banquet which Ahasuerus prepared at Esther 1; or when it is done so that the giver may receive from the person whom he has benefited things equal to or even greater than those which he gives. In that case it will have to be called commerce rather than kindness. This is especially so when a gift is given in order that we may ensnare someone and, as though he were a mouse, catch him by fraud with traps and enticements. Hence the enemies’ δῶρα ἄδωρα, “gifts that are no gifts,” is celebrated as a Proverb. Here the chief place is held by papist almsgiving, which they are accustomed to dispense in order to merit eternal life and escape hell. Third, it is exposed from its form, as when its gifts are of a kind that does no good to the person affected by the benefit: money given to the rich; dogs and hawks given to young men who do not know how to use them uprightly and whose minds, already more than sufficiently devoted to pleasures and games, are thereby kindled all the more to pursue pleasures; fruit given to girls; and so forth. This can also be accommodated to others more advanced in age. For when people notice that someone is wonderfully delighted by hunting, fowling, and other recreations of the mind, it is customary to gratify him with gifts of this kind, by which a pursuit is kindled which either is incited of its own accord or passes beyond due measure. See Prov. 17.16. Fourth, it is exposed in the subject upon which that kindness is conferred, namely, those who have no need of it, as when people load the exceedingly rich and wealthy with gifts. Therefore, those whose largess is extended either only, or for the greatest part, to the rich certainly have no fellowship or commerce with liberality. But if they confer something upon the needy, they nevertheless bestow it upon vagrants and idle people, and do so at their gates in order to be seen by everyone. By that very thing they imitate the Pharisees, who do not bestow alms except at the sound of a trumpet, a practice which is expressly noted in this Proverb. For although there is almsgiving which must be given publicly and in everyone’s sight, nevertheless the person who does not also dispense to the poor out of his own bosom in such a manner that the left hand does not know what the right hand does is proved certainly never to have given anything truly.

Since these things are so, what is proposed in the latter half follows: a person who is truly liberal and who is in reality what he presents himself to be by countenance and words when giving is rarely found. See Luke 21.1–4. From these things it is further clear how shamefully those people are mistaken who suppose that almost all papists were exceedingly liberal toward the poor. For since Solomon asserts that in the Church of God itself this was rare and could not easily be found, much less can it be found in the synagogue of Satan. At the same time it also becomes clear how few are just, since those who are just must also be liberal, Ps. 112 and 37.27.

Verse 7. The just man who walks continually

[1632 witness | PDF p. 432 | printed columns 838-839]

in his integrity—blessed are his children after him.

In מתהלך (*continenter ambulans*, walking continually) there is a customary metaphor. In בניו (*filij ejus*, his sons) there is a double synecdoche: sons stand for sons and grandsons, and also for daughters.

The purpose is to stir up everyone to perseverance in the course of piety once undertaken, by proposing the extraordinary reward of constant piety.

Here there is considered first what God requires and then the reward of the obedience rendered to God.

When it is said that the just man walks in *his own* integrity or simplicity, and not merely in integrity or simplicity, this seems to indicate not obscurely that there is a certain simplicity and integrity which does not belong to the just man, or to one justified by faith. This occurs when someone does some matter which is even iniquitous and unjust, but does it in such a way that he is conscious of no evil in himself, as is the case with the person who does it through ignorance. An example of this matter exists in Abimelech, Gen. 20. When God had afflicted him because he had resolved to commit adultery with Sarah, he answered that he had done this in the simplicity and rectitude of his heart, being ignorant, namely, that she was another husband’s wife. This simplicity, however, is not praiseworthy, since God affected him with a grievous plague and was going to affect him further if he had carried out in deed what he had designed in his mind. Such are very many papists, who order their lives in the simplicity of their own hearts while pursuing idolatry which they persuade themselves is pleasing to God, but who will nevertheless not escape God’s dreadful judgment for that reason. For if Abimelech was punished along with his whole family merely because he resolved, in ignorance, to commit adultery, what will become of the papists who eagerly pursue the spirit of adultery and can never be satiated with it? Does he suppose that he can escape God’s avenging hand, when God has so severely avenged even the mere will to sin which arose from ignorance?

It is true, indeed, that they will be dealt with more mildly than those papists who walk perversely and crookedly in their own ways, thinking one thing in their mind while feigning another in their countenance. Nevertheless, they will not meanwhile escape God’s judgment, especially in this so great light of the Gospel.

Therefore, in order to obtain this promise which is proposed in this Proverb, it is not enough for someone to conduct himself frankly and openly, unless, further, having been justified by faith, he determines within himself to measure the institutions of his life by the standard of the divine law which he himself has known and perceived, and, after hypocrisy has been dispelled, to pass a sincere and blameless life.

Nor is it enough if someone who has been justified by faith and has entered the right way interrupts that rectitude of life with evil deeds. For God requires this of Abraham: that he should walk continually before him in integrity, Gen. 17. Not all the just do this, even though they are truly just and justified by faith; but those do not who have a righteousness like dew which quickly vanishes, concerning which just man Ezekiel discourses copiously at chapter 18, verse, and so forth. For David was just and justified by faith, yet he interrupted his justice and integrity by committing several crimes: adultery and homicide, and that not of one person but of the many who fell in battle with Uriah. To this also belongs the fact that, by rashly adjudging Mephibosheth’s patrimony to his servant Ziba, he broke to some degree the faith given to Jonathan by oath; likewise, he broke his integrity by the unlawful census of the people. Solomon likewise was a just man, but he strayed very far from the straight path, and did so for a good and large part of his life. The same can be said of Jehoshaphat. Abraham, however, especially, and Isaac, Jacob, and Job are read to have continued steadfastly in the ways of the Lord and never to have contaminated themselves with any more grievous crime.

Now the promise must be considered. Here he passes over in silence the blessedness of the person who walks continually in his own integrity. For if those who act impiously through the whole span of their life up to its end and then

[1632 witness | PDF p. 433 | printed columns 840-841]

return to God through repentance at the close of life are blessed, and if those who interrupt their righteousness are blessed, how much more are those who pass the whole course of their life incorruptly?

As for those who act wickedly through the entire course of their life but nevertheless turn to God, and those who interrupt for a time a righteousness and innocence rightly begun, it happens not rarely that God passes over them but visits their posterity, as is clear in Jehoshaphat, Solomon, and others. By contrast, matters turned out splendidly for the posterity of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob’s sons, and so forth.

This, however, must not be understood as though the just had merited this benefit through their works of supererogation, but rather that God’s gratuitous mercy is so great that it overflows from parents into their children. This may be understood even from the fact that God showed himself beneficent to the posterity of Jehu, an impious idolater, merely because he had freely promised this to him.

Verse 8. A King sitting upon the throne of judgment winnows every evil with his own eyes.

In מלך (*rex*, King) there is a synecdoche for every supreme class of governors. In ישב (*sedens*, sitting) there is a double synecdoche: sitting stands for standing and sitting together, and specifically the former stands for pronouncing and executing a sentence. The reason is that the person who sits upon the throne is also responsible for declaring the law. In מזרה (*ventilat*, winnows) there is a metaphor from farmers who separate the chaff from the grain by winnowing. In עיניו there is a metonymy of the cause: the eyes stand for inspection.

The purpose is to exhort Princes to declare and execute the law in their own person because of the extraordinary fruit which proceeds from it.

Very many Kings and Princes suppose that they are raised to the royal throne in order to pursue delights; see 1 Sam. 7. But the Prophet indicates another end here, which the Prophet Jeremiah amplifies exceptionally at 22.15–16, and the very throne raised on high admonishes them that they are the ministers of that justice which descends from heaven.

Next, people for the most part suppose that Kings pass an idle life full of pleasures and are tormented by no cares and anxieties; hence they neglect their duty of praying for their Princes.

Finally, when he says that the King does this with his own eyes, he teaches that it is the duty of Princes to execute judgment themselves and in their own person, without the service of a substitute. We see this done by Moses at Exod. 18, and likewise by Samuel, who is said both to have judged the people of the Kingdom of Israel at his own home and also to have travelled through the kingdom by each of its tribes to declare the law, until, worn out by old age, he was compelled to entrust the duties of judging to his sons. Thus Jotham was substituted and appointed to the throne of his father Uzziah for no other reason than that his father, through the plague of leprosy, had been cast out by law from the assembly and fellowship of human beings and could no longer discharge the duties of judging. Thus David, confined to his bed because of old age and the utmost feebleness of his body, raised Solomon to his throne. For if the saying is true in agriculture that “the master’s footsteps are the best manure for a field, and the master’s eye makes the horse exceedingly fat,” how much more important is it that this be done in a matter so holy and august? Hence God’s law provides that the bearer of the cudgel should discharge his duty while the Magistrate and judge looks on. See into how great a danger the people of Israel fell because of Moses’ absence for forty days.

If this must be observed in declaring the law, how much more must it be retained in the ministry of the Church, inasmuch as it is more important for human beings to be delivered from external destruction than from oppression.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 434 | printed columns 842-843]

The fruit follows: wicked people are dispersed in this manner. And this is confirmed from the contrary at Eccles. 8.10, and likewise by that statement in the law, “so that the others may hear and fear.” For the wicked do not repent of their wickedness by the execution of civil justice—for this is proper to the ministry of the Church—but nevertheless their wickedness is, as it were, sealed up within and held shut in. And although all wicked people are not removed from the Commonwealth by the administration of justice, they are nevertheless dispersed so that they cannot gather together to devise crimes.

From this it is gathered that God will not let the wicked go unpunished. For he who severely commands justice to human beings will much more execute justice himself. See Ps. 94.9–10.
