It is asked whether the magistrate's power in the external government of the
church is supreme, immediately subject to God alone, and superior in kind to
the power of the church. The Remonstrants say so, *Apologia*, chapter 25,
folios 291–292.

We deny it and determine the matter by the following assertions.

Assertion 1. The king, as a member of the church, is subject to the kingly
power of Christ reigning in the Word and in ecclesiastical discipline.

1. Because, of everyone without exception—and a distinction must not be made
where the law makes none—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 740 | PDF p. 789 | split word joined: dicitur]

it is said, “He who hears you hears me; he who despises you despises me.”

2. Because ministerial authority in God's name over kings is given by God to
God's prophets and pastors. Jeremiah 1:10: “See, I appoint you this day over
the nations themselves and kingdoms, to uproot,” and so forth.

3. Because every created power is subject to Christ's kingly power in the Word
and in the exercise of ecclesiastical discipline, which is an exercise of
Christ's royal authority. Psalm 2:12: “Kiss the Son, lest he be angry.” Psalm
72:10: “The kings of the ocean, the coastal peoples, and the islanders will
render tribute; the kings of Sheba and Seba will offer a gift.” Verse 11:
“Finally, all kings will bow down, showing him honor; all nations will serve
him.” Christ is King of kings and Lord of lords, Revelation 17:14 and 19:16.
Therefore kings are his subjects and answerable to his laws.

4. Everyone in the church is either the supreme Pastor or one of the sheep.
Christ alone is the supreme Pastor; therefore the king is a sheep. He is
therefore bound to hear the voice of Christ speaking and commanding through
his servants, and is therefore subject to the power of binding and loosing
granted to the church. For what belongs to Christians as Christians belongs to
all Christians, and thus also to a Christian king.

Assertion 2. The king, as king, is the guardian of both tables and the supreme
governor in absolutely all causes—both ecclesiastical and spiritual and civil
and temporal—by political government and civil influence. But he is not the
true, proper, or supreme governor and judge of ecclesiastical causes as
distinguished from political ones. The reason is this. In

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 741 | PDF p. 790 | split word joined: omni]

every ecclesiastical cause there are two elements. One is political: it
concerns the king and human law, insofar as the subject is under the king
commanding in his own sphere; to that extent the king is the supreme judge.
The other is spiritual and ecclesiastical, and this is subject to
ecclesiastical judgment, not to the king. For if the king were simply the judge
of all causes, including ecclesiastical ones, he would then be the judge of a
translation of the Bible from the Hebrew and Greek sources into Latin or the
vernacular, even if he did not know the Hebrew letters. Indeed, he would be
the judge of every philosophical disputation and every question concerning
navigation, warfare, and agriculture. In their eagerness to honor the king,
this would place the king beneath a king. Whatever is political in such
questions—whatever concerns civil right and lawfulness—belongs to the king in
his own sphere. Romans 13:4: “He is God's minister.” Isaiah 49:23: “Kings will
be your foster fathers, and female rulers your nurses.” Psalm 101:8: “Every
morning I will cut off all the wicked of the land, to cut off from God's city
all who practice iniquity.”

Assertion 3. Hence the doctors rightly say that by his royal authority the
king exercises commanded acts, not elicited acts, concerning ecclesiastical
matters. So the will, as the δεσποτική or ruling faculty, exercises royal and
commanded acts concerning all the faculties of both soul and body. The will
commands the eye to see and the ear to hear.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 742 | PDF p. 791 | split word joined: Voluntas]

The will commands the faculty of locomotion to walk. But the will itself has
no elicited acts in these faculties: the will itself does not see, hear, or
move from place to place.

1. Because the offices of the king and of ecclesiastical governors are
distinguished by their object, nature, and kind. Therefore the king, as king,
cannot preach, ordain pastors, frame canons synodically, and impose them upon
the church.

2. What belongs to a king καθ᾿ αὑτό, “as such,” belongs to all kings, including
pagan kings. But doing this does not belong to all kings, as our opponents
admit.

Assertion 4. Nor is it true, as they say, that the king, though not as king yet
as a Christian—that is, as such a king—properly directs and orders in
ecclesiastical government, while ministry and exercise properly reside with
ecclesiastics, as Cameron speaks in *Lectures*, volume 2, page 50. Empty
nonsense!

Who could tolerate the claim that the king has the power to preach the Word,
administer the sacraments, exercise ecclesiastical discipline, and prescribe
canons, while the act and exercise of preaching, and so forth, properly belong
to pastors?

For 1. the power to exercise a pastor's act is in the pastors—namely, the
authority conferred on them for edification and for exercising acts of
discipline. Matthew 18:18–20; Mark 16:15–16; Ephesians 4:11–12; 1 Corinthians
4:1–2, 21; 2 Corinthians 2:10–11; 2 Corinthians 10:8.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 743 | PDF p. 792]

2. On this account ministers would be only the hands, instruments, and
executors of the will of kings; pastors would preach in the king's name and by
his authority and would exercise acts of discipline. But they are God's
servants, ambassadors discharging their office in Christ's name. 2 Corinthians
5:20; Galatians 1:1–2.

3. It must especially be noted that Christianity, which comes to the king's
office from outside it, does not add to a Christian king some new authority he
did not previously possess, by which he could order ecclesiastical affairs.
Rather, Christianity only extends and intensifies, as it were, the
pre-existing civil and political power more broadly and more intensely. For a
pagan or Muslim king is just as essentially a king and, by the duty of the
royal office—given that the gospel is revealed to him—is just as obligated as
a Christian king to contribute his royal authority to Christ and to the
enlargement of his kingdom. I mean this concerning the obligation of law, not an obligation
under the special form of gratitude, according to the saying, “From him to
whom much has been given, much will be required,” Luke 12:48. But a Christian
king, not as king but as such a king, as Christian, is bound to contribute his
authority more abundantly and earnestly to the church's good in proportion to
the grace received than a king simply as king, or a pagan king who has heard
nothing about Christ. The king as Christian is therefore under a greater
obligation than a pagan king, but he is not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 744 | PDF p. 793]

endowed in first actuality with greater royal power. For it is said of every
king in Proverbs 8:15, “By me kings reign.”

From this it is easy to judge the canon of the pseudo-bishops of the Scottish
Church imposed in the *Book of Canons* in the year 1637, chapter 1, canon 2:
“Whoever hereafter affirms that the king's Majesty does not have the same
power in ecclesiastical causes as the pious kings of the Jews and Christian
princes in the primitive church possessed, or in any way diminishes his royal
prerogative, shall be excommunicated,” and so forth. But 1. the canonists are
themselves guilty of *lèse-majesté*: they maintain that royal power belongs to
the king insofar as he is pious and religious, as the Jewish kings and
Christian princes were pious, and not insofar as he is king. It follows that
if the king becomes impious or heretical, no royal prerogative belongs to him.
This is plainly thoroughly Jesuitical and is the foundation of the Jesuits'
doctrine that an impious king hostile to the Roman Catholic faith must be
driven from his throne. That this is their meaning is clear, because they say
that denying the king the same royal authority the pious kings once possessed
is to diminish the king's royal authority. But the king's authority is thus
diminished unless we say that the authority belonging to the king as king is
the same as the authority belonging to him as a pious and religious king. Yet
these are distinct. In this

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 745 | PDF p. 794 | split word joined: Canone]

canon they themselves say not a word about the king's authority insofar as he
is king.

2. The canonists leave undecided what this authority of the king is, since
they confuse the pious kings of the Jews with Christian emperors. For the
Jewish kings are considered either as kings or as prophets, as David and
Solomon were. If they are considered as prophets, they wrote canonical
Scripture and prophesied. Are those who deny the king the power to write
canonical Scriptures, prophesy, preach, and exercise truly pastoral acts to be
excommunicated? Is that so? If they are considered as kings, then the canon
says nothing except that those who deny the king the authority in
ecclesiastical causes which belonged to all kings—not only the pious but also
the impious, including Ahab, Domitian, Nero, and Julian—are to be
excommunicated. Furthermore, it is certain that pious princes in the primitive
church did not have power equal to that of the pious Jewish kings, who were
both kings and prophets. If whatever they in fact usurped in the primitive
church is attributed to our king, these men contend for royal authority on a
slippery basis and in bad faith. If whatever belonged to the emperors by right
in the primitive church is to be attributed to the king, then they say
nothing, and they wrongly specify pious princes here. It is also a begging of
the question, because they ought to have explained what authority in
ecclesiastical affairs is owed by right to the king, lest innocent people be
struck in the dark by their excommunication.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 746 | PDF p. 795]

Assertion 5. Some spiritual acts belong to the king as king. For as king and
by the duty of his royal office, he can and ought to exhort and command
royally that all the idle bellies, all the ecclesiastics gaping after courtly
honors, take themselves away from there to feed the Lord's flock. Indeed, he
ought to command that everyone in his kingdom diligently perform what is his
duty by office. 2 Chronicles 29:5: “And Hezekiah said to them, Hear me, O
Levites; now sanctify yourselves, and sanctify the house of your fathers, and
carry out of the sanctuary what must be removed. For Jehovah had so
commanded.” Numbers 8:6, 11 and 18:32. In the substance of the command this
exhortation does not differ from a ministerial exhortation, but it differs in
the authority of the one commanding and the penalty to be inflicted. The king
commands by royal authority and under threat of civil censure and punishment
—would that kings would scourge idle pastors intent only on their bellies, the
court, and honors! The pastor commands it by ecclesiastical authority and
under penalty of spiritual censure.

Assertion 6. In a case of apostasy by the universal church, when the pastors
are heretics and neglect what belongs to their office and the people fall away
from the true worship of God, the king can and ought to reform religion.

1. Because Josiah piously and commendably did so in 2 Kings 23. He

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 747 | PDF p. 796 | split word joined: renovavit]

renewed the covenant between God and the people, cleansed the temple,
demolished the altars of idols, and killed the priests of the high places.

2. Because it belongs to the law of nature that every member—and especially
so eminent a member as the political head—look to and care for the welfare of
the whole body to the best of his ability. But he can do nothing here from an
absolute and independent royal liberty; everything must be done according to
the prescription of the divine Word.

Assertion 7. The king as king can rightly deny his royal sanction to impious
statutes even if they have been canonically determined by a synod.

1. Because the magistrate's conscience is subject to the church's canons not
absolutely but conditionally, insofar as they agree with God's Word.

2. What belongs to every Christian also belongs to the king insofar as he is a
Christian. But private discernment whether a law or canon agrees with God's
Word belongs to every Christian. Here the king has public judgment as king and
private judgment as Christian, so that he does not execute or approve by his
authority anything contrary to God's Word.

Nor may anyone object: If in his conscience the king judges canons to be
impious and heterodox, and so refuses his royal sanction, while the governors
judge the church's canons to be pious and orthodox, who will settle the
dispute? Certainly this is the same controversy that can arise between us

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 748 | PDF p. 797]

and the papists, between any servant and master, and between a flock and its
pastor. Here God's Word must certainly be consulted by both litigating parties;
it pronounces sentence in God's name. The church's determination is a
condition of faith required according to the order prescribed by God, but it
is not the formal ground of faith, nor as such does it bind the conscience of
the king, the church's governors, or the people. It belongs to the church's
governors to determine, and to the king to execute what has been determined,
provided it agrees with God's Word. Here it belongs to the king to obey those
who discharge an embassy in Christ's place.

Nor do the Remonstrants' contrary inferences hold. Hence they infer, 1.
“Ministers or pastors therefore have power and authority immediately—that is,
the first and supreme power—under and from Christ. The magistrate, therefore,
either does not have that power, or also has it immediately from Christ
together with the ministers, or is in this matter the ministers' minister and
subject.”

We say that power in the external government of the church has been given
immediately to the church alone—not to the whole assembly, but to the college
of governors. They alone immediately from Christ have the power of loosing and
binding, as the passages prove: Matthew 18:16–18; John 20:21–23; Acts 1:24–26;
Acts 15:22–24. Thus they also have power in external government. Nowhere has
Scripture granted the magistrate

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 749 | PDF p. 798 | split word joined: talem]

such power. Nor anywhere in Scripture does it belong to the king to
excommunicate or to frame canons for εὐταξίαν, “good order,” and right order in
the church; it belongs to the church alone.

2. The magistrate, not as magistrate but as a politically preeminent member of
the church and guardian of both tables, has a collateral authority with the
church, conferred on him immediately under Christ together with the church.
This power belongs to the king because and insofar as he is a member of the
church.

3. Nor does it follow that the king is the church's minister and subject
because it is his responsibility to see that the church's laws and canons are
put into effect. For by a plainly royal command he commands that the church's
canons be put into effect. Indeed, because the king commands everyone
lawfully, and it is his responsibility to see that pastors diligently preach
the Word and canons conforming to God's Word are observed, in this respect he
is God's minister, not the church's.

2. They infer: “Architectonic power under Christ must belong immediately
either to both parties—that is, both the magistrate and the church's
ministers—or to only one of them. It is absurd that it should belong to both
together. For otherwise two collateral architectonic powers, neither of which
depended on the other, would have to be established in Christ's church. This
is contrary to the nature of good government. It therefore belongs to the
ministers without the magistrate.” Response: Some say that it is by no means

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 750 | PDF p. 799 | split word joined: absurdum]

absurd for there to be two collateral architectonic powers immediately under
Christ, neither of which depends on the other, because each power is supreme
in its own kind. As Barclay says against Bellarmine, chapter 14, page 110:
“Neither is subject to the other, and neither can invade the other's bounds or
jurisdiction without the greatest crime; rather, both are joined to the head,
who is Christ, like the shoulders of one body.” Meisner, *Sober Philosophy*,
part 3, section 2, chapter 2: “Civil power depends on the ministry in spiritual
matters, and spiritual power depends on the magistrate in temporal matters.”
But properly speaking, architectonic ecclesiastical power resides in Christ
alone, the church's King and head; only ministerial power belongs to ministers.
The supreme power of punishing and the actual punishment of the disobedient
belong and remain with Christ the King. Ministers have been left only the
ministerial pronouncement of punishment according to the Word and
excommunication. But the king and supreme magistrate truly has the
architectonic power to make laws agreeing with God's Word and right reason,
and from the fullness of his power to punish transgressors not only with a
lighter penalty but also, in proportion to the offense, with death. That two
powers supreme in their own kind should, in different respects, be subordinate
and not subordinate to one another is not only not absurd but necessary. For
the king, politically and by δεσποτικῷ, “ruling,” government, is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 751 | PDF p. 800 | split word joined: supremus]

the supreme guardian of both tables. But his supreme power here is
*cumulative*: that is, he can and is bound to add and accumulate his royal
authority by convening a national synod, presiding over it politically, adding
civil sanction to its canons, and punishing obstinate violators of those
canons. But his power is not *privative*, such that he could deprive the church
of the power conferred by Christ or the exercise of ecclesiastical power.
Hence Christ's power is positive and privative; the king's is permissive,
cumulative, and supplementary, not privative. The church's power belongs to
another kind: in the kind and order of ecclesiastical, pastoral, and spiritual
power, it is supreme in its own kind and is subject not to the king but
immediately to Christ. Thus the king as king is less than God alone, a second
god, and by civil power superior to the church; while by spiritual power the
church's governors are superior to the king. For the king as a member of the
church is subject to the church as a son to his mother, a part to the whole, a
member to the body, and a sheep to the college of pastors. Nor is this opinion
papist, as the Remonstrants suppose.

1. Because the papists subject the king and the king's conscience to the
church's decrees and canons as to the infallible Word of God. But our opinion
subjects the king's conscience to no synod and no decree of the church except
insofar as it agrees with God's Word.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 752 | PDF p. 801 | split word joined: Pontificii]

2. The papists maintain that the virtual church—that is, the Roman pope—so
surpasses kings that the pope may dispose of kings, drive them from the throne,
and deprive them of their crowns from the fullness of papal power. We detest
such tyranny and teach that every soul, even the souls of pastors, must be
subject to the king in the Lord, and that for conscience's sake. The papists
also maintain that the pope may command kings imperiously; that kings must
subject their conscience to his word as to God's living oracle speaking on
earth; and that the king is subject to the pope as his executor and minister.
We teach that kings are subject to the church as sheep to their pastors, yet
in such a way that they are not bound in conscience to execute the church's
decrees because they are the church's decrees, but insofar as the king sees
that they agree with God's Word. Nor do the British theologians believe that
the king is head of the church—that is, that he has the same power of a head
which the pope once usurped over England. I do not concern myself with what
servants of the court and slaves of kings teach. Nor is the controversy
between the Anglican Church and the Jesuits whether the king is head of the
church in the sense in which the pope boasts that he is its head. It is
whether the king, as king, is not the church's political head, subject to no
pope. The English affirm it; the Jesuits deny it.
