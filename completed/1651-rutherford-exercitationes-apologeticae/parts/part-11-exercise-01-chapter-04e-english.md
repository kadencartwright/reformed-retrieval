29. Conquered by these testimonies and by the truth, Fr. Suárez concedes a
certain immanent, not transitive, predefinition, one which is efficacious and
absolute concerning supernatural acts. From this I infer that, because
supernatural acts are truly free acts, our absolute decree is entirely
consistent with freedom of choice, both as to power and its use; and it is
also consistent as to power and the exercise of power, even according to
Suárez's doctrine. His foundations must be subjected to examination to see
whether they are sound. 1. A supernatural act is morally good. Therefore, in
this respect it is not inconsistent for God absolutely to will and love it.
Reply. 1. If it is not a free act, it is not properly morally good; but on the
Jesuit's principles no free act can arise from absolute predefinition.
Therefore, etc. Suárez adds: “Next, God foreknew that such a calling or aid (he
is speaking of congruous aid) was a suitable means by which the human will
would in fact be inclined

[Marginalia: Suárez, in the treatise *On the Aids*, question 10, *On Grace*,
book 3, chapter 17, folio 43.]

[1651 logical printed p. 170 | IA leaf 188 | PDF p. 188]

to elicit such an act. Therefore, by a single and most simple act of his will
God can will that the created will elicit such an act, and so forth.” Reply.
1. By knowledge not of simple intelligence but by middle knowledge, God
foreknows that a congruous counsel, in fact suited to the nature of freedom,
will incline the will to a supernatural act. This knowledge presupposes the
free supernatural act antecedently to every act of the divine will. It by no
means follows, therefore, that God can decree such an act absolutely. For the
absolute decree does not precede the futurition of the free act but follows it
as an effect follows its cause. Thus the Jesuit very cunningly makes sport of
his reader. 2. Therefore God cannot will a free act absolutely but only
conditionally, for here God wills a free act on the hypothesis that it has
already been produced by congruous aid. 3. By the same reasoning, all
contingent acts are determined by an immanent, though not transitive,
predefinition. For all contingent acts occur through congruous or incongruous
motion, inasmuch as they occur by middle knowledge, by which God knows whether
a motion is so suited that it in fact inclines the second cause or does not
incline it. The Jesuit adds: “That there is nothing in this decree inconsistent
with the freedom of grace is evident, because through such a decree God wills
that act in relation to the cause and the means through which it will freely
be future, and by virtue of such a decree he employs no other cause for such
an effect. Therefore it does not injure freedom, because he directly wills the
creature's act, and wills it as free, and by its force

[1651 logical printed p. 171 | IA leaf 189 | PDF p. 189]

ordains a means suited to cause a free determination.” Reply. 1. I marvel at
the Jesuit's versatile and cunning ingenuity. God wills a free act which, in
relation to its cause, will be freely future. Either he wills it antecedently
and absolutely before it is a future free act—and thus middle knowledge
perishes, since it establishes free acts as future antecedently to the decree
of the will, while antecedent necessity also agrees perfectly well with
freedom, the very thing the Jesuit strains with all his strength to overthrow
and upon which alone he directs the powers of his mind—or he conditionally
wills that it will be freely future, and thus plays with us, for then this
predefinition is conditional. Or, finally, let the Jesuit give us a will of
God which is neither absolute nor conditional. This certainly cannot be
given, for the two are contradictorily opposed. 2. “He directly wills the
creature's act.” For the word “directly,” I ask you, substitute “antecedently
and absolutely”; otherwise you will not advance your cause. You will thereby
see your cause's ruin—namely, that God absolutely wills the creature's act as
free. 3. He ordains, says Suárez, a suitable (say “congruous”) means to cause a
free determination. But congruous causation and motion include foreknowledge,
and foreknowledge does not make its object but presupposes it as already made.
Therefore he ordains a congruous volition which already exists antecedently to
the ordaining. Where now, Jesuit, is your absolute predefinition? Do you see a
conditional predefinition which presupposes the being of its object? Alas! He
did not blush;

[1651 logical printed p. 172 | IA leaf 190 | PDF p. 190]

the Jesuit blushed, and I marvel that the white paper did not blush.

22. Proceed, Jesuit. “Such a will is absolute and efficacious. For the decree
by which God determines to give a person such a congruous calling that he may
believe is a predefinition in relation to such a calling, because he simply
wills to give it before he foreknows it as absolutely future, although he does
not will to give it except for such an end or act. Therefore, conversely, the
decree concerning the act itself will be its predefinition, although the act
is not willed except through such a means. Finally, that act is related to our
will as something to be determined not merely concomitantly but antecedently
and causally, because through it God wills not only to concur if the will
consents, but wills that it consent; and this not by a mere wish or simple
affection, but by an absolute and efficacious decree, such as Augustine's
statement that no human choice resists God when he wills to save.” Reply. What
is this? God simply wills to give a congruous calling before he foreknows it
as absolutely future—that is, the congruous calling. What has this to do with
the absolute predefinition of a free act? The question is whether he wills to
give the congruous calling before by nature he foreknows that a person will
obey such a calling by a free act. Unless you say this, you will never prove
absolute predefinition. 2. It does not follow, “Therefore the decree
concerning the free act will be a predefinition,” because either he foreknows
that act as absolutely future before he wills to give the congruous calling,
or he does not foreknow it. If the former, the predefinition will still be
conditional, not absolute. If

[1651 logical printed p. 173 | IA leaf 191 | PDF p. 191]

the latter, where has middle knowledge vanished? Indeed, it can happen that
the will dissents. 3. If God antecedently wills that the will consent, and does
not merely will the act if it consents, then God's efficacious decree will
precede the free act, and thus the free act will be necessary by antecedent
necessity—something Jesuit ears will not bear. Or, after God's efficacious
decree concerning the free act is posited, that free act can fail to occur;
for the Jesuit defines freedom as the capacity, once all requisites for acting
are posited, to act or not act. The absolute decree can therefore be resisted,
which is absurd. 4. If that act is related to our will as something to be
determined antecedently and causally in relation to the divine decree, the
futurition of the free determination will be given antecedently to God's
decree, and the absolute decree is thus removed. If it is subsequent to the
decree, the decree must establish an antecedent necessity together with free
acts, which is absurd to the Jesuits. 5. By whom is the will to be determined
antecedently and causally—by God? The Jesuit utterly denies this. By the will
itself? Then God antecedently wills the will to determine itself, and thus
antecedent necessity and a free act will still be combined and compatible
with each other, which is our position. 6. The Jesuit himself unties the knot
and cuts the throat of his own cause. In the same place, at figure 10: “For
providence to be perfect and sufficient for the predefined effect,” he says,
“there must be a particular permission concerning the act with all its
circumstances which are necessary for the predefined effect; and the

[1651 logical printed p. 174 | IA leaf 192 | PDF p. 192]

permitted act must be infallibly future if it is permitted. This could in no
way exist or be understood unless conditional knowledge were presupposed
before the predefinition of such an effect. The same thing must therefore be
presupposed when the free act through which an effect predefined by God occurs
is good and supernatural.” Why say more? He wishes conditional knowledge to
be presupposed by the absolute predefinition of supernatural acts; but the
futurition of supernatural acts is presupposed by conditional knowledge,
because the object of knowledge is prior to knowledge itself. Therefore, much
more will the supernatural acts themselves be prior to this Jesuitical
absolute predefinition. What the Jesuit has here comes down to this: God
absolutely decrees supernatural acts on the hypothesis that they already
exist. Who would not laugh here at Jesuit acuteness—or rather, a dizzy head?
Yet in words at least the Jesuit yields to the truth, book 1, *On God's
Concurrence with the Will*: “Indeed,” he says, “it seems in itself incredible
and unworthy of God to say that he cannot by an absolute decree of his will
determine something to be done, if it is to be done through a free created
will, unless he first sees such effects as absolutely future. For thus God's
power over an act of the human will would be excessively constricted and
limited; and all free effects, although they would not be beyond God's
knowledge, would nevertheless necessarily be beyond or without his
efficacious intention, which is contrary to all reason.” From this I infer it
to be contrary to all reason that free acts should not truly be free even as
they are subject to God's absolute decree, which precedes the foreknowledge of
the absolute futurition of free acts.

[Marginalia: Book 1, chapter 16, folio.]

[1651 logical printed p. 175 | IA leaf 193 | PDF p. 193]

2. It is contrary to all reason that freedom should essentially be such a
power that, after all requisites for acting are posited, it can act or not
act. But I ask the reader to hear the way in which the Jesuit preserves
freedom under this immanent predefinition. In the same place, at folio 8:
“Although,” he says, “this decree is in itself efficacious and absolute, it
nevertheless does not of itself immediately change the will or determine it
to acting, nor does it draw the will away from its connatural mode of acting;
but it merely employs and applies harmonious and most suitable means so that
the act may infallibly occur in that mode in which it was predefined. For the
infallibility of the effect,” he says, “another moral motion with common
concurrence is sufficient, together with conditional knowledge by which God
foresaw that, when these are posited, the human will would freely consent to
such an act; for this is an infallible and efficacious means by which that
divine will is fulfilled.” But reply. 1. The Jesuit presupposes something
false and unworthy of God's perfection—namely, that God's absolute decree, by
which the will is physically predetermined, draws it away from its natural
mode of acting. Is this what we teach? Prove it by argument if you can, and
prove that God cannot bend the will irresistibly and yet in the mode most
connatural to it. He casts imperfection upon God and gravely undermines his
dominion over free acts. 2. Physical predetermination also establishes the
most suitable moral means, since the will is a truly ethical and moral agent,
even under God's determination, and because of moral persua-

[1651 logical printed p. 176 | IA leaf 194 | PDF p. 194]

sion it also freely yields and grants itself to physical determination. 3. It
is more false than false that moral motion with general concurrence and
conditional foreknowledge suffices for the infallibility of the effect. For
by conditional foreknowledge God either foreknows that the will will assent to
the moral motion antecedently to the efficacious and absolute decree, or
subsequently. If antecedently, the Jesuit plays games, for then he does not
absolutely decree and predefine the free act, but does so upon a hypothesis.
If subsequently, I argue thus: according to the Jesuit, God absolutely decreed
all good and supernatural acts; those acts therefore must be future. Here
antecedent necessity coexists with the exercise of freedom, which the Jesuit
has frequently denied, as we saw above. But I press further: when that decree
is posited, Peter freely believes. Therefore, according to Jesuit doctrine he
can refrain from believing, if Peter can refrain from believing even when moral motion and
general concurrence are employed (for neither of these irresistibly
determines the will, as the Jesuits teach). Therefore God cannot foreknow a
congruous means by which Peter's will is infallibly moved to believe. For God
cannot foreknow as future that which will never be future. What can the Jesuit
think up here to answer the argument? This single refuge remains: in his
infinite wisdom God foreknows that the wills of all people, if moved by such
and such persuasions and when such conditions are posited, will nevertheless
infallibly yield and be persuaded.

[1651 logical printed p. 177 | IA leaf 195 | PDF p. 195]

But come, I dissolve this very easily: under all conditions and persuasions
the will believes freely, and accordingly it can refrain from believing. For,
according to the Jesuits, no circumstances, conditions, or persuasion finally
determines the will. Therefore God's infinite knowledge cannot foreknow
infallibly that Peter will believe when he will never believe. Indeed, in that
case God's foreknowledge would not be infinite, but fallible and conjectural,
which is blasphemous. If the Jesuit says that God does not absolutely predefine
the act of believing in Peter until by conditional knowledge he first
foreknows that the will will yield to such congruous persuasions, then behold:
by this absolute immanent predefinition God still predefines free acts after
they are already future. Surely here it is impossible for the Jesuit not to be
caught and stuck fast in this invincible argument.

23. But see in the same place, folio 10, what differences the Jesuit
establishes between immanent predefinition, predefining free acts inwardly,
and predetermination outwardly. First difference. The former will has joined
with it—or rather, is at the same time—the will of such a means as does not
determine free choice but, insofar as lies in itself, moves it contingently,
though infallibly by foreknowledge. The latter, predetermining will requires
no other means than its own efficacy by which it carries the will along with
it, determining it. Reply. 1. It is false that a means infallibly moves the
human will by foreknowledge,

[1651 logical printed p. 178 | IA leaf 196 | PDF p. 196]

for all infallibility is founded upon entitative necessity. But here there is
no necessity at all between the means' moral motion and the free act; instead,
the two are altogether as able not to coexist as to coexist. 2. It is plainly
false that the predetermining decree requires no means. For by that decree a
means is established which moves the will in a connatural mode and does not
carry it away by its efficacy, but sweetly determines and bends the will.
Second difference. “The former will,” he says, “predefines the act in relation
to the second cause's mode of acting, whence it includes in its object the act
as to be done from the determination of free choice itself, in a congruous
mode. The latter has for its object not only the act but also its mode, or
physical predetermination through God's efficacious motion.” Reply. It is a
contradiction in terms absolutely to predefine a free act as to be done from
the determination of free choice itself, which, when all congruous motions
are posited, can act or not act, as the Jesuit teaches. For when the predefining
decree is posited, it cannot fail to act. Third: “Hence it happens,” says the
Jesuit, “that the former predefinition, conceived abstractly insofar as it is
considered apart from the production or application of a suitable means, is
not immediately active outwardly or applicative of divine power until it is
understood as determined to such a means. The other applies divine power to
introduce force or necessity or predetermination into the created will.”
Reply. 1. Although that predefining will does not immediately apply

[1651 logical printed p. 179 | IA leaf 197 | PDF p. 197]

the power or suitable means to move the will, nevertheless, when it is
posited, the moved will cannot fail to be moved through. But in the Jesuit
sense this is a force that overturns freedom. Otherwise, if the will can fail
to be moved through when predefinition is posited, there is no absolute
predefinition here. 2. It is false that force is introduced into the will by
the transitive decree, for it applies divine power in such a way that it
carries freedom itself with it to that object toward which it freely inclines
itself. Fourth: “By virtue of the former predefinition, God's concomitant
concurrence by which God concurs with the will is not varied; rather, the will
is merely so directed and governed that it infallibly uses that concurrence
and accommodates itself to it. But by virtue of the other, which
predetermines the will, God's concurrence must be varied and furnished in
such a way that it uses the will rather than the will using it, and the will
does not accommodate itself to it, but it instead compels the will to operate
with it. Nor is such concurrence indifferent, but determined to one thing.”
Reply. 1. By virtue of absolute predefinition, God's concurrence is determined
to the absolutely predefined act of assent, so that if an act of dissent were
posited instead of the act of assent effected by predefinition, God's
knowledge would be fallible and the predefinition would not be efficacious,
which is a grave absurdity. 2. To the praise of the glory of his riches, we
confess with our whole heart that God uses the created will in all its acts.
Otherwise he could not reach the end intended through such means, nor would
God retain dominion over creatures or providence in all things unless we say
this;

[1651 logical printed p. 180 | IA leaf 198 | PDF p. 198]

yet he does not compel the will, because it follows willingly and follows
God's will freely—just as freely as if we supposed there were no decree in
God. 3. If the created will used God's concurrence at its pleasure, the
creature would then necessitate God to concur in sinful acts, good acts, and
indeed all moral acts. Thus the honor and glory of good acts would not belong
to God, and he would be stripped of freedom in concurring.

24. Finally, in the same place at figure 12, the Jesuit most wretchedly makes
this absolute predefinition into a non-predefinition. “This predefinition,” he
says, “does not simply precede the use of freedom, because it presupposes
foreknowledge of it at least as future under such a condition.” Therefore God
absolutely predefines free acts upon the hypothesis that they are free acts.
But who would deny that all morally good and evil, natural and supernatural
acts are defined by God after they exist? They are defined in this way by us
and by the angels. 2. What cannot be absolute on the hypothesis that it already
exists? The Jesuit thinks he gratifies God by this predefinition, which in the
same place, folio 15, he thinks necessary—not because contingent effects
depend upon the first cause, but on God's side, so that he may fittingly
foresee and preordain the things which fall under his will and action. But tell
me, Jesuit, why does God not predefine every act by this immanent
predefinition? The reason is plain: in some things God fittingly preordains
what falls under his will and action,

[1651 logical printed p. 181 | IA leaf 199 | PDF p. 199]

but in others unfittingly; in some wisely and from his infinite knowledge, but
in others with no providence. Is this to philosophize in a manner worthy of
God?

25. I proceed to the other things the Jesuit has on this subject. He says that
necessary actions, or actions in fact ordered to a particular end
efficaciously intended and predefined by God, are determined by absolute
predefinition. Such was the consent of Joseph and Mary to marriage. Tell us
the reason, Jesuit: because special providence concerns means necessary to the
ultimate end. But Jehovah made all things for himself, Proverbs 16; and on the
hypothesis of the divine decree they are necessary means for displaying God's
glory, while special providence shines forth in the smallest things. The
second part of the distinction is this: “When human acts,” he says in the same
place, figure 12, “even morally good acts, are not ordained by God from an
efficacious intention of some special end, but arise from the human will with
general concurrence and providence, they need not proceed predefined by God's
special will, nor is there any sufficient reason for believing this. Such for
the most part are all moral acts, because they are so done by people that they
profit them nothing toward attaining eternal life, nor do they specially serve
God toward any particular end of his providence. Proof: those things are
properly said to be predefined by God which he particularly intends, chooses,
or wills, either as ends or as means. Therefore, because in this way they do
not fall under the divine will,

[Marginalia: Suárez, book 2, *On Concurrence*, chapter 7, at figure 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 182 | IA leaf 200 | PDF p. 200]

and otherwise follow from the general order of second causes with general
concurrence, they need not be predefined.” Reply. God's efficacious and special
intention in everything he wills and does (and he wills and does every good
thing) is that he himself should be glorified, as Scripture expressly says:
Proverbs 16:4; Romans 11:36; Revelation 4:11. Therefore all things are
predefined by God. 2. That there are certain beings which do not serve God
toward any particular end of providence is to make God do many things for no
end; thus God will not be an intelligent agent, which is blasphemous. For he
will do many things external to himself either by a force of nature, as
raindrops fall, or by natural instinct, as birds build nests. But things that
act in this way are ordered by a higher intelligence. Yet what higher being
directs the Spirit of the Lord? Who gave him counsel? Romans 11:34; 1
Corinthians 2:16. But the Jesuit wishes God to have intended these good acts
for a common end—namely, good in common, either this or that. Yet such a
confused intention in God is elsewhere rejected even by the Jesuit, since it
smacks of imperfection. 3. Scripture itself explicitly says that the very
smallest things, such as the flight of sparrows and the falling of hairs, are
intended and cared for by God, and that God specially wills them, Matthew
10:29; that God directs the answers of the tongue, Proverbs 16:1; the coming
forth of lots from the lap, Proverbs 16:33; the occasion of a man's apparently
accidental killing, Exodus 21:13; the shooting of the arrow at Ahab, 1 Kings
22:34; that God makes rich and poor, Proverbs 22:2, and exalts or abases whom

[1651 logical printed p. 183 | IA leaf 201 | PDF p. 201]

he wills, Psalm 75:7–8. Who would say that God wills these things thus—namely,
upon the hypothesis that they exist? And after God's will or action is
posited, who would say these things may occur or not occur? Is this to make
God, with the Scriptures, their author? Or who would say that God wills these
things for an end conditionally or ineffectually intended, since, even when
God wills them, they can fail to happen and he can therefore fall short of the
end toward which they tend as a means, and then attain some other end, having
been shaken from this end by fortune? Yet these things cannot be said
precisely to serve the attainment of eternal life, for Jonah or Matthias can
attain eternal life whether the lot falls to him or not. 4. If these do not
occur even by that immanent predefinition, God will then operate toward
producing these effects, but not by a congruous operation which he knows will
not occur, and consequently without any knowledge and thus without
providence. For either he intends that this lot should fall to someone or he
does not, and so the lots come forth apart from God's intention, which the
Jesuit said is contrary to reason. If he intends it, he intends it either
absolutely or conditionally. If absolutely, we have what we desire. If
conditionally, then it is from foreknowledge. But this foreknowledge either
employs congruous means or does not. Whatever he says, it follows that these
things are predefined in the same way as those which serve predestination as
necessary means. Therefore the Jesuit's distinction is empty. 5. “God intends
all these things in themselves—the essential perfection and preservation of
the world, until

[1651 logical printed p. 184 | IA leaf 202 | PDF p. 202]

the number of the elect is completed, which is most of all intended in
itself. But because it is often accidental to this end and to the world's
essential perfection that there should be so many or more, they must therefore
be predefined by God neither as ends nor as means, but merely foreknown as
things consequent and future from the established order of causes, and
permitted or admitted and effected through God's will willing to bestow its
influence upon them.” Reply. 1. Before Bellarmine, the atheist Averroes taught
the same thing, denying God's particular providence. 2. Whatever God does, he
wills; otherwise he does many things unwillingly. But the Jesuit does not deny
that God produces particular good acts. Therefore he wills them. Yet whatever
God wills is either an end or a means, because whatever he wills he wills
either for the sake of another, as all created beings, or for his own sake, as
he wills his glory. Every object of volition is either a means or an end. This
is an axiom accepted by all philosophers down to the present day. 3. Who could
bear this theology: the coming forth of lots, the flight of sparrows, and the
answer of the tongue are from God, yet God wills them neither as means nor as
an end? Then they surely occur from God for no end, and in these God will not
be an intelligent agent. 4. Scripture says that God decreed to overturn
Ahithophel's counsel and that the ten tribes' defection from David's house was
from God, and yet these things stood only accidentally toward the
predestination of David and the saints in Israel. 5. This distinction is
therefore blasphemous.

26. Since God produces good acts by permission alone, they will be no more
from God's will and providence than evil acts.

[1651 logical printed p. 185 | IA leaf 203 | PDF p. 203]

Suárez replies in the same place that this does not follow, because God
intends and wills good acts, but does not will or intend evil acts; he arouses
and morally inclines toward good acts, but not toward evil acts. Reply. But if
God wills and intends all good acts, he wills them either as means or as ends,
which Suárez denied above. 2. Thus occasions and inducements to evil are cast
before God's providence, although not according to his revealed will. 3. I do
not see how God does not intend these acts. Does he intend them absolutely? In
that case they truly are predefined. Or conditionally, upon the hypothesis
that they exist? Yet it is an absurd intention to intend that something
should exist which already exists. 4. If we speak of concurrence physically
required for the entity of the act, good acts do not occur by a greater real
influence than evil acts. The Jesuit concedes this entire absurdity; there is
therefore no physical influence of grace upon good acts rather than evil ones.
The sole difference consists in Pelagian persuasion through an ethical
arousal. 5. For what end does God intend these good acts? For his glory, for
which he does all things, as Scripture says, or not? If the former, all means
for displaying his glory cannot fail to be predefined, since they serve God
toward a particular end of providence, as Suárez admitted above. Yet he says
these acts do not specially serve the declaration of God's glory but stand
accidentally toward this end, and are therefore not predefined. But if this
reasoning were sound, I say, nothing would be predefined by God,

[1651 logical printed p. 186 | IA leaf 204 | PDF p. 204]

for he could declare his glory by other means than these predefined means;
otherwise he is not infinitely wise.

27. Some, seeing that it is unworthy of God to remove absolute predefinition,
have therefore said that God attains an irresistibly intended end—or rather,
reaches it—through contingent means, so that if one fails he will seize
another. Thus Jackson says that God intends to apprehend a robber by a hundred
means; when ninety-nine means fail, the last, as if by chance, will
irresistibly produce the end. But here they shamefully wander. For when
ninety-nine fail, the final means, since it operates contingently, can fail no
less than the ninety-nine failed. Indeed, this final means could have been
first in order and thus have failed. As for the trifler's statement that this
last means operates necessarily, as though by chance, to produce the end: if
by “chance” he means fortune, God's providence will be plainly overturned. Or
does he mean that this final means operates through the employment of God's
infinite power or wisdom, lest perhaps God should fall short of his intended
end? Then, on these doctors' principles, God reaches the end by acting
irresistibly and necessarily precisely through this means. But why did he not
do this in the other means which failed? And then, contrary to their
hypothesis, this final means will not be contingent either. 2. “Moreover,”
says Suárez, “who would believe that, while God calls a person in this or that
way, he does not know whether the person will consent or not, and therefore
proceeds as if making a trial, so that if the person does not consent now he

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, section 2, chapter 8, p. 95.]

[Marginalia: Book 3, *On the Aids*, chapter 17, folio 14.]

[1651 logical printed p. 187 (partial) | IA leaf 205 | PDF p. 205]

does consent, he calls him again or in some other way, and so forth?” 3. Thus providence will
be uncertain and not infallible merely from the multitude of means, for one
fails here, and two or all can fail. 4. By such a predefinition God's will
will be confused and suspended.

Here, reader, you have our position concerning the disjunctive decree; second,
the conditional decree; and third, the immanent predefining decree. All of
these, in our judgment, are Jesuitical chimeras.
