## On True Theology

### A Little Book on Its Origin, Nature, Forms, Parts, and Mode, by Which All
Christians Are Admonished, According to God, Concerning Their Dignity, and
Theologians Concerning the Weight of Their Ministry

Second edition, revised and corrected by the author himself before his death.

To the most noble and most distinguished men, the lords curators of the
University and magistrates of the city of Leiden in Holland, Franciscus Junius
sends greetings.

It has now been exactly two years, most noble and most distinguished men,
honored lords, since God Most Good and Most Great, while I was engaged in
something else and thinking of going elsewhere at the will and command of a
higher authority, planted me by his marvelous counsel in this most flourishing
university of yours and willed that my labor should be employed in it. At the
same time, the authority of the illustrious States and your own will were added
to that counsel. When I was thinking only of touching the borders of your
country in passing, you ordered me to plant the foot that I was lifting to
depart and to rest in this harbor beside the sea upon which I and my family
would very soon have had to be tossed.

I certainly acknowledge and gladly and deservedly proclaim this most wise
counsel of God and this most gracious disposition of the States and yourselves
toward me. If any service can proceed from me in this mortal life by which I
might return some thanks to you all, I shall take care that my labors, as the
offspring of that mind of mine, answer your expectation, or at least [*Opera*,
fol. 1369] my office and ability. I am not unaware that many things sometimes

[Printed page 40; PDF page 70]

come to nothing in human expectation, and that an opinion is easily created
both by the simple persuasion of others and by the empty arrogance of many
swaggerers and extravagant talkers. As for myself, I intend to cherish neither,
nor have I ever done so. Those who promise themselves from me what is not in my
power do an injustice; while they think that they judge me favorably, they
silently reproach me with my ignorance and weakness. But if I were to assume
many things and proclaim them of myself, I would do a graver injury both to
myself and to you, as though I did not know how to measure myself by my own
measure and foot, or to examine the relation of my office to your dignities.

Nevertheless, your opinion of me, and the persuasion of others (wherever it
came from), caused you to desire my labor while I was absent and to make use of
it when I was present. My readiness to acquiesce answered that humane and kind
generosity which you showed in forming an opinion of me. This was not because I
trusted that I could fulfill the expectation of that desire of yours. Rather,
I resolved in [*Opera*, fol. 1370] my own mind that my obedience, however
slender, ought to be a witness to my mind and will, even though it should fall
very far short of your preconceived opinion and judgment.

Thus, established by your decision in this most renowned and flourishing
university, I have not ceased—both in private studies and writings and in public
lectures and exercises—to tend the Sparta allotted to me by God's will and your
judgment. I occupy myself in this Sparta all the more gladly because for many
years I have learned all the more diligently to flee other occupations. My mind
shrinks from other employments, especially because I am conscious that I was
called to these and not to others; and perhaps my natural ability is also
better suited to these than to those. I call you as witnesses of this fact; I
call the university as witness, and the body of students which has thus far
attended my lectures in no small numbers.

Why say more? I bring this little book itself before you as a witness. For
almost two whole years I have been made liable for the promise of it by the
insistence of certain good colleagues who are exceedingly fond of me. In this
book I explain in order what theology is—of which we are ministers—and what a
theologian is, and I pursue the matter by such a path that, when I reflect upon
its subject and dignity, I cannot help exclaiming, “And who is sufficient for
these things?” Here is set forth the common dignity of [*Opera*, fol. 1371] all
Christians, the singular dignity of the stewards of divine wisdom, and the
majesty of divine things which God has willed to deposit in the weakness of
earthen vessels and potsherds.

I desire all Christians to be assured of their dignity in Christ Jesus, and
all theologians and ministers to be assured of the weight of their office, so
that the former may strive toward their dignity and the latter may attend to
the stewardship entrusted to them. Let them beware of other affairs as of the
most dangerous reefs and sandbanks, and let them most devoutly occupy
themselves day and night in these things before the Lord, who by his Spirit
teaches those who consult and reread them.

I ask you, most noble and most distinguished men: just as you were the chief
promoters and authors of my teaching this subject in your university, so
receive with a favorable mind this first principle of my discipline, taught in
that university and offered to you, as a pledge of my fidelity and a proof of
my most devoted zeal toward you and your university, of which you willed me to
be a part. Farewell, most noble and most distinguished men. Continue to love
true piety; the university, as a school of piety; me; and all learned persons,
as pupils of piety, truth, and humane learning. Leiden, 19 July 1594.

## Thirty-Nine Theses Explained in This Treatise

1. Theology signifies either God's own discourse or a discourse and rational
account concerning divinity—to use Augustine's words in *The City of God*,
book 8, chapter 1. We are going to speak of the latter meaning. Chapter I.

2. That theology exists is taught both by the things themselves and by the
consensus of all peoples. The things themselves teach it, for God exists, he
is the principle of every good in the nature of things, and God speaks and
acts. Consensus teaches it, for all acknowledge by the light of nature that it
exists.

3. Although everyone believes that theology exists, the word is nevertheless
used equivocally in common speech: for this theology is true, while that is
false and merely a matter of opinion.

4. False theology is twofold: one kind is common, the other philosophical.
Common theology, resting upon the imperfect principles of our nature, does not
rise higher by reasoning. Philosophical theology, wandering by an error of
reasoning into false conclusions, produced from those principles a mythical,
a natural, and a civil theology.

5. Theology is wisdom concerning divine things. Chapter II.

6. This theology is either *archetypal*, namely God's own wisdom, or *ectypal*,
formed by God. Chapter III.

7. Archetypal theology is God's wisdom concerning divine things. We worship it
but do not investigate it. Chapter IV.

8. Ectypal theology, whether considered simply (as they say) or relatively, is
wisdom concerning divine things, formed by God from his own archetype [*Opera*,
fol. 1371] through a communication of grace for his glory. Chapter V.

9. Theology considered simply is the whole of the wisdom concerning divine
things that can be communicated with a created thing, according to the measure
of the one communicating it.

10. Theology considered relatively is wisdom concerning divine things
communicated with creatures according to their measure. It is communicated by
union, vision, or revelation.

11. The theology which we call the theology of union is the whole wisdom
concerning divine things communicated with Christ the God-man—that is, insofar
as the Word was made flesh—according to his humanity. Chapter VI.

12. The theology of vision is that which is communicated with the angels and
with the spirits of the consecrated saints, or the perfected ones in heaven.
Chapter VII.

[Printed page 42; PDF page 72]

13. The theology of revelation is that which is communicated here with the
human race. It may also quite suitably be called our theology. Chapter VIII.

14. There is a twofold mode of communicating theology: nature and grace. The
former is, as it were, the internal principle of communication; the latter is,
as it were, its external principle. Hence one theology is called natural and
the other supernatural. Chapter IX.

15. Natural theology is that which proceeds from principles self-evident by
the natural light of the human understanding, according to the measure of
human reason. Chapter X.

16. The conception of this natural theology in the human understanding is
concerned with common things and is obscure and imperfect. Therefore it must
receive its perfection from supernatural theology. [*Opera*, fol. 1372]

17. This was the condition of natural theology in Adam while his nature was
whole: it had to be cultivated and increased by reasoning from common, obscure,
and imperfect principles, and perfected by grace.

18. But after this nature was corrupted, those same principles remained in
each person—common, obscure, and imperfect—but were most thoroughly corrupted
in themselves and, by our depravity, thrown into the greatest confusion among
themselves, like the ruins of our nature.

19. Therefore this theology can bring nothing at all to perfection and never
does so. Indeed, by itself it is not even capable of the perfection which comes
upon it from grace.

20. For this reason it was necessary for that inspired theology to come upon
human beings. From its origin we call it supernatural; and from its mode of
communication, the theology of revelation. Chapter XI.

21. Supernatural theology is wisdom concerning divine things which proceeds
from principles self-evident by the light of a higher knowledge, above the
measure of human reason.

22. This supernatural theology has a twofold account: it is either absolute
and in itself, according to the measure of the one communicating it, or it is
relative, according to the little measure of those to whom the communication
comes.

23. Our theology considered absolutely is wisdom concerning divine things,
inspired by God according to divine truth, committed in Christ through
declarative speech to his servants, and recorded in the Old and New Testaments
through the prophets, apostles, and evangelists, to the extent that it is
expedient for it to be revealed to us here for his glory and the good of the
elect. Chapter XII.

24. The matter of theology consists of divine things: namely God and whatever
things are ordered toward God, according as God's teaching about his nature,
works, and right had to be handed down. Chapter XIII.

25. The form of theology is divine truth. This is considered in theology in
two ways: either the whole, or some part of the whole, is considered simply in
itself; or different parts are considered with one another in a fitting
comparison. Chapter XIV.

26. This truth is holy, just, and perfect. It teaches nothing profane, unjust,
or imperfect, and it leaves nothing holy, just, or perfect untaught, so that we
may be directed as perfectly as possible toward holiness in ourselves, justice
toward all, and perfection in every respect.

27. Thus this theology is one, eternal, and immutable. For that which is
necessarily true is also necessarily one; that which is just and holy can

[Printed page 43; PDF page 73]

never fail to be just and holy; and finally, that which is perfect according
to God is altogether and always immutable. [*Opera*, fol. 1373]

28. We posit a twofold efficient cause of our theology: one principal and the
other instrumental. Chapter XV.

29. The principal and absolute efficient cause of our theology is God the
Father, inspiring it in the Son through his Spirit, for he alone is the author
and the supreme and most perfect producer of this wisdom in his servants.

30. The instrumental cause of this wisdom is the *logos prophorikos*, or God's
declarative speech: spiritually when it is spiritual, and corporeally when it
is corporeal.

31. Theology has a twofold end. One is remote or supreme; the other is
proximate and secondary to it, and subordinate (as they say) to it. Chapter
XVI.

32. The primary or supreme end of theology is God's glory. Theology sets this
before everyone to behold, and by a lawful use of this wisdom all the godly
also bear witness to it, just as wisdom is justified by her children.

33. The secondary or subordinate end of our theology is the present and future
good of the elect. The promises of this life and of the life to come were made
to their godliness, and this theology is the wisdom of true godliness.

34. Our theology considered relatively, or theology as it is said to exist in
a subject, is that same wisdom concerning divine things, modified according to
the condition of the human beings in whom it exists and from which they are
called theologians. Chapter XVII.

35. The measure of this theology in a subject cannot be circumscribed, because
it varies both in each individual and most widely among them all.

36. It varies in each individual because there is in everyone a twofold
principle, nature and grace. The former must be diminished and the latter
increased from glory to glory by the power of the Spirit and the efficacious
communication of theology.

37. It varies among them all because in some nature is diminished and grace
grows strong to a greater degree than in others, although no one here among
them all comprehends the entire form of our theology perfectly in every
respect.

38. The prophets and apostles indeed received the whole and perfect form of
this theology, but not perfectly in themselves. By the singular power of the
Spirit they handed the whole of it down. All the rest receive neither the
whole, nor do they receive it perfectly.

39. Therefore, as we said before, the form of our theology is one in itself,
but in us it is now multiple and will remain so until, coming together into the
unity of faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God, we become a perfect man
and together are raised to the measure of the stature of the church, which is
the fullness of Christ. Chapter XVIII. [*Opera*, fol. 1374]

## Treatise on True Theology

### Chapter I: Whether Theology Exists

As we are about to speak of theology, the common treasure of divine and saving
wisdom, we first humbly pray God Most Good and Most Great, from whom all wisdom
and every good gift proceed, that in this most holy subject he would be pleased
to shine before us by the light of his eternal Spirit and, according to his
promise in Christ Jesus, lead us into all truth. We pray, secondly, that if by
God's blessing we offer anything saving and good in this cause, he would make
that same blessing saving to all who will read this work of ours, so that his
glory may be established in us all and we may grow in him until we have risen
to that proper stature of a perfect man and attained the fullness of Christ.

In every matter being treated, however, one must first see to it that the terms
for things and the things signified by those terms are defined, lest one rashly
depart from the intended purpose. We shall briefly do both in this chapter
before we begin to speak about the thing itself. Once this road has, so to
speak, been laid down and a convenient approach opened to the matters we are
going to discuss, we trust that everything will be far more evident to the
reader.

We therefore make the following determination about the name “theology.”
Thesis I: Theology signifies either God's own discourse or a discourse and
rational account concerning divinity—to use Augustine's words in *The City of
God*, book 8, chapter 1. We are going to speak of the latter meaning. If we
attend to its etymology, then, this name has two meanings, both received in
customary Greek usage. For just as *theopropion* is an oracle which God
declares, and *theosēmeia* a signification which God exhibits, so theology is
also a discourse which God himself declares in created things. Nevertheless,
the other meaning seems always to have been more common in Greek usage: by the
name theology we understand the discourse which is declared concerning God, or
the rational account of divine things, just as the orthodox fathers also
called wisdom concerning divine things *theosophia*.

Both of these meanings can accord with the proper nature of theology; indeed,
they must accord with it essentially and simultaneously, inseparably. Yet both
interpretations cannot properly be attributed to this name in the same place
and contained in the force (as they call it) of the word. We therefore
undertake hereafter to explain the latter meaning, especially since God has
previously spoken, and may hereafter speak, many things which do not at all
belong to the substance

[Printed page 46; PDF page 76]

of theology. For theology, like every science, concerns common or universal
things, while God's discourse is not infrequently occupied also with
particulars.

The first question we must resolve concerning this theology therefore seems to
be whether that which we commonly call theology exists at all. Among the
things named in our speech, some exist in the nature of things and others do
not; some can be known and others cannot. We would therefore treat in vain of
a thing which neither existed in itself nor could be known by a human being.

Thesis II: That theology exists is taught by the things themselves and by the
consensus of all peoples. The things themselves teach it, for God exists, he is
the principle of every good in the nature of things, and he speaks and acts as
God. Consensus teaches it, for all acknowledge by the light of nature that it
exists. The fact that this theology whose meaning we have just circumscribed
truly exists could indeed be demonstrated by more arguments from the things
themselves, and very lucid ones. These two, however, seem sufficient for our
present discussion; we shall defer the rest to another occasion, if God wills.

The former [*Opera*, fol. 1375] argument is from the things themselves, that is,
from the subject of theology. The latter is from the consensus of all peoples
and their natural knowledge of that subject, insofar as it can enter human
knowledge. There can be no doubt that there is some knowledge of things which
exist and that knowledge of them exists in those who know, according to their
measure and capacity. If, then, God exists and knows, we must surely say that
God knows himself according to his own measure. That measure is infinite, and
he knows himself by an infinite and altogether divine knowledge. If God is the
principle of every good in the nature of things, we rightly conclude from this
that all creatures which obtain the good of understanding and knowledge from
that universal principle are endowed with at least some knowledge of God and
of theology, by which each acknowledges the principle from which it proceeded.

If God speaks, and speaks not only particulars but also universals pertaining
to true knowledge of himself; if, finally, he acts as God toward all creatures,
upon the nature of some of which he has impressed obscure footprints of his
majesty and upon others a definite image, theology must certainly exist. Created
things perceive it by communication, while God possesses it in himself with
such perfection that it can neither be apprehended nor expressed according to
its dignity, but only lightly outlined according to the little measure of our
weakness.

Just as we confirm by an argument from its marvelous subject that theology
exists, so by an argument from ourselves and our nature we all acknowledge
that some theology exists. If you consider nature according to the common
account of this universe, it certainly declares God's glory and, as though
speaking aloud and pointing with an outstretched finger, displays that discourse
and rational account concerning divinity which we call theology. If you
consider the common nature of the human race, so great is the light of nature
in it, and so thoroughly implanted is its common notion, that it has taught all
human beings that some theology and divine wisdom exist just as surely as it
teaches that God exists. Finally, if each of us examines himself and searches
more deeply, as it were, those winding inner chambers and most hidden recesses
of our mind, there will be nothing in us which does not show that theology
exists and most manifestly refute those who contradict it. Such is the power
of nature; such the power of truth.

These things are so commonly known and disclosed by God to each person that no
human being, however uncultivated or savage and alien to all humanity, can be
ignorant that the theology of which we speak exists. Yet what we are taught in
common by the implanted light of nature is obscured in each person by the
blindness and weakness of his individual nature. The vision of our mind is
somehow dimmed, so that the particular nature in each of us does not rightly
perceive what common nature teaches. Consequently, we see something of the
truth from afar, as though through a shadow; but because of the darkness born
within and surrounding us, we see falsely the very truth of God which we see.
We express this as follows. Thesis III: Although everyone believes

[Printed page 47; PDF page 77]

that theology exists, the word is nevertheless used equivocally in common
speech, for this theology is true, while that is false and merely a matter of
opinion. The truth of the thing, when compared with our false and corrupted
judgment and sense, produces the equivocation which we establish here. From the
truth of the thing it follows that wisdom concerning divine things, whatever
and whosever it may be, exists and is even called true. From the corruption of
our judgment, however—as though phlegm were taking away from our minds the
sense of spiritual taste—it happens that in this most weighty matter also, as
in others, we embrace the false in place of the true.

Thus [*Opera*, fol. 1376], according to the truth of the thing, a theology
which is altogether true is said to exist, the term being used univocally.
According to the opinion which we have conceived by our corrupted and stupefied
judgment, there is also what is called theology equivocally, which we call
false and opinable. It is false because it is as far as heaven from the truth
of the subject properly belonging to theology. It is opinable because it
subsists only in opinion—if that can be called subsisting—in our mind and
imagination, which fashion mere dreams and mockeries in place of truth, and
idols and goat-stags in place of the true God.

It is not our purpose to speak at length about false and opinable theology,
since labor in attaining true theology is most useful and error concerning it
dangerous, whereas labor in investigating false theology is useless and an
error about it very far removed from grave danger. Nevertheless, to say
something about it briefly, insofar as pertains to demonstrating this
equivocation, we make the following determination. Thesis IV: False theology is
twofold. One kind is common, the other philosophical. Common theology, resting
upon the imperfect principles of our nature, does not rise higher by reasoning.
Philosophical theology, wandering by an error of reasoning into false
conclusions, produced from those principles a mythical, a natural, and a civil
theology.

In these few words we think both that the single body of false theology can be
circumscribed, and that each of its parts and members can be separately shown.
The root and head, so to speak, of this false theology is what we call common
theology. Its body lies in philosophical theology, which from there has grown
luxuriantly into three very broad and exceedingly wild branches. We call that
theology common which belongs to everyone, as its principles, notions, and
common preconceptions are sketched in our minds. It is neither worked by the
cultivation of reasoning, so to speak, nor increased by help coming upon it
from elsewhere, but rests in its own condition and imperfect notions and sits
idle, as it were, in the dregs of its natural imperfection and corruption.

We call philosophical that theology which, once culture and additions from
within and without have been added to common theology, raises itself by an
error of reasoning and, as it were, by the maze of our mind, to altogether
false conclusions about divine things. These are consequently alien both from
the truth of those things and from the duty of our piety toward God. As soon as
this trunk began to proceed, so to speak, from the root of common theology,
philosophical theology spread into the three branches which we distinguished
by name above: namely mythical, natural, and civil theology, as Augustine
explained from Varro and Seneca in *The City of God*, book 6, chapter 5.

They called mythical or fabulous that which poets chiefly use for theatrical
pleasure; natural or physical, the kind of theology which philosophers employ
in interpreting the world and searching into natural truth in their schools
and encyclopedic studies; and finally political or civil, that which the
powerful employed to establish the laws of cities and commonwealths by a
certain authority of religion. Pleasure produced the first; the study of
nature the second; and public utility situated in human society the third. It
is incredible into how many and how various errors these three kinds flowed in
every time and place until God graciously disclosed his truth.

But we have by no means resolved to deal with the false here. We intend rather
to expound the truth of that saving theology, especially since very many
authors have pruned that whole forest of false theology most diligently, and
Augustine in books 6, 7, and 8 of *The City of God* most

[Printed page 48; PDF page 78]

devoutly and diligently overthrew it. Leaving aside, therefore, discussion of
false theology—which is nothing other than an opinion and shadow of wisdom
that apprehends anything else in place of divine things—we shall, with God as
our guide, leave the false and pass to the definition and investigation of the
true.

### Chapter II: What Theology Is

True theology, since it is supreme wisdom, possesses the highest breadth and
dignity in itself and the highest usefulness in us—provided that we receive it
from the Lord with humility of mind and consciousness of our smallness. It can
be defined in various ways, as many have done. Yet among so many definitions,
that one [*Opera*, fol. 1375] seems most worthy of approval which expresses from
the truth the whole account and nature of the thing according to the simplest
rules of art. We therefore choose here to define theology according to its
genus and difference, in words as few in number as possible, but nevertheless
most significant in content. Since the best authorities direct that every
simple question under discussion be given its own proper definition according
to its genus and its specific difference, as they call it, we judge that both
can most fittingly be handed down in this way. Thesis V: Theology is wisdom
concerning divine things. Wisdom is indeed the genus of true theology. We
establish its difference by the designation “divine things,” things which are
truly divine.

First, we call theology wisdom because it embraces within itself, in an
altogether most excellent way, every property pertaining to understanding,
knowledge, and saving use, both from nature and above nature. It is the most
certain interpreter of principles, the most comprehensive chief of all
contemplative and practical sciences, and the wisest judge of every action and
rational account, superior to every exception. It teaches us to apprehend all
simple principles—whether implanted by nature or inspired through God's grace,
and indeed immediate, as they are commonly called—as fixed and necessary. It
teaches us to combine and divide those principles once apprehended; then to
reason in a divine manner from what has been combined and divided; and by such
reasoning to rise to knowledge itself, which is supremely necessary, most
certain, and content with the contemplation alone of eternal, immutable, and
most constant truth.

Why would a wise person not rest in that divine certainty which inheres both
in the things themselves, as theology hands them down according to what they
are, and in the mind of the knower, insofar as they are divinely known by him
through theology? All these excellences just mentioned, situated in the
understanding, are generally assigned to those sciences commonly called
contemplative. This same theology shows most excellently the necessary mode of
our true holiness and justice—a mode, I say, that is certain and most
self-sufficient. It sets before our eyes the supreme end both in the nature of
things and above nature. That supernatural end is in truth the only end set
before nature; or, if one wished to add still other ends, it must be called by
far the most excellent. In these matters consists the entire excellence of the
sciences commonly called active.

Finally, theology is the true mother and queen of those disciplines which
really are and are called sciences. It is the supreme perfection of principles
and reasonings, of the conclusions drawn from them, of sciences and use, of
actions and every judgment, and of the whole rational account pertaining to
all these things. Whatever proceeds from understanding, reason, and knowledge,
theology judges most perfectly and orders most wisely. Consequently, those who
define true theology merely by the name of understanding, knowledge, or art do
it an injustice,

[Printed page 49; PDF page 79]

because understanding, situated only in principles, establishes the rational
account and performs reason's own work which we call reasoning; knowledge
draws its conclusions from principles by a right rational account; and art,
proceeding from understanding and knowledge, terminates in some work. Our
theology comprehends all these together: the understanding of principles, the
knowledge of conclusions and determinations, and the most wholesome art of our
work insofar as we strive toward God. Nothing at all except wisdom can judge
all these things with certainty, order them fittingly, or perform them in a
saving way. Since this is so, no genus other than wisdom can be assigned to
theology: wisdom which judges the principles of sciences in the understanding
and their conclusions, embraces by its power everything necessary to the
perfection of every good, and uses them all most wisely.

We certainly seem to add to this genus its own most proper difference, so to
speak, when we call it wisdom concerning divine things. When we call the
things divine, we embrace in them a threefold excellence and marvelous
condition. First, the object about which theology is occupied is divine;
second, the mode of that wisdom is divine; and finally, its end is also divine
and most perfect. These three excellences or conditions must concur in a proper
account both of all the contemplative and practical sciences and of this
wisdom, which is readily the chief of them all.

If you consider the object or matter of this wisdom, it is God, whatever
belongs to God, whatever comes from God, and whatever is ordered toward God. If
you consider its mode, that is divine in its principle, action, and effect: it
not only infinitely transcends everything human [*Opera*, fol. 1376] but also
soars by far beyond this whole nature. If you consider its end, God is its end,
together with that saving and glorious vision of God himself toward which we
strive under the guidance of this wisdom. As many of us as have been sealed by
God's grace with the Spirit of promise run toward this end in such a way that
we do not run uncertainly. We exercise our arms in boxing so that we do not
strike the air (1 Corinthians 9:26). Finally, forgetting the things behind and
straining earnestly toward the things before us, we are carried without ceasing
toward the goal, the prize of God's heavenly calling in Christ Jesus our Savior
(Philippians 3:14).

### Chapter III: How Many Kinds of Theology There Are

Although from what we have just shown the account of true theology which we
undertake to discuss here seems to have been formed “as in an outline,” a
certain equivocation nevertheless attaches to that most august name
“theology” and to the definition which we have just used. It must be
distinguished before we come to a fuller explanation of the theology under
discussion. Its breadth is so great that neither can things so very different
be indicated adequately by the same word, nor can the disparate things which
we signify by one word be comprehended under one genus. Therefore, just as the
things set forth under the name of theology differ in their nature and
condition by an entire genus yet fittingly share a certain analogy with one
another, so we must also establish a homonymy of the word, or equivocation as
people commonly call it. Yet we must first distinguish equivocation itself at
this point, because this is not pure equivocation but the kind commonly called

[Printed page 50; PDF page 80]

analogical in the schools. We call that equivocation pure in which the account
of the things spoken of equivocally is plainly and wholly diverse—to speak in
the manner of the scholastics. We call that equivocation analogical in which
the account of the things spoken of equivocally is in part or relatively the
same and at the same time in part diverse. This kind of equivocation very often
occurs in applying names to divine and human things.

According to this homonymy which we call analogical, we therefore establish a
twofold mode of theology in these words: this theology is either archetypal,
namely God's own wisdom, or it is ectypal, formed by God. We must first explain
the meaning of this analogical division and then demonstrate its necessity.
Its meaning is this: one theology so called in our speech is essential and
uncreated, which for the sake of teaching we shall hereafter call archetypal or
prototypal in this little book. The other is accidental and created, or
habitual; this may fittingly be called ectypal, as though it were an expressed,
or rather a sketched, image of that formal, divine, and essential theology.

It seems to me that orthodox fathers formerly called that archetypal theology
“exemplary.” According to its divine and immutable exemplar, the other theology
has been conformed by God to the capacity of creatures. More recent writers
have called the former theology “theology in itself” and the latter “theology
considered relatively.” The former theology is the very infinite wisdom which
God has concerning himself and all things insofar as they are ordered toward
him necessarily, properly, and in themselves by a perpetual relation according
to his own infinite account. The latter is the wisdom which creatures possess
concerning God, according to their measure, and concerning things ordered
toward God through his communication of it.

These two kinds of theology are so disparate that they cannot in truth be
referred to any one definite head and common genus. The theology which we call
divine and prototypal belongs neither to this genus nor indeed to any genus.
The nature of each teaches that it does not have a common genus with this
created theology. Archetypal theology is essential to God's nature and is a
part, so to speak, of that infinite knowledge which is essential in God.
Whatever is essential is properly reckoned in the same genus as the very
essence to which it is essential, just as we call a part homogeneous with the
whole and a member with the body, sharing its genus. God, however [*Opera*, fol.
1377], is above every genus, a “hyperessential essence,” as the holy fathers
most aptly called him. Created theology is not essential to those to whom it
belongs, but comes to them as a habit, clothing their essence as it were.
Consequently it is as far removed from that theology—to speak with the
common people—“as heaven is from earth.”

Nature itself and reason show most clearly that archetypal theology belongs to
no genus, which was our second assertion. For if it is essential, as it is, and
therefore homogeneous with God's nature, its account will surely be the same as
that of the divine essence. But since that divine essence or nature is truly
hyperessential and is contained in no common genus with any thing, whether
properly or improperly, simply or by reduction, we most aptly declare this
theology also to be hyperessential. Our created theology, however, is the
communicated outflow of that theology. Since it is nothing other than a certain
impressed, seal-like image from that essential theology, it certainly does not
share a genus with it and cannot be contained by one. It only possesses an
analogy derived from and shared with it. Similarly, a painted image of a human
being is not homogeneous with a human being, but obtains the designation
“human” equivocally by an analogical account alone.

Accordingly, when the apostle rightly compares this wisdom with that in 1
Corinthians 3, he says: “Let no one deceive himself. If anyone among you seems
to be wise in this age, let him become foolish so that he may become wise. For
the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God; for it is written, ‘He
catches the wise in their craftiness,’ and again, ‘The Lord knows that the
thoughts

[Printed page 51; PDF page 81]

of the wise are vain.’” Therefore what we said before necessarily stands: we
call both of these “theology” together only by an equivocation which they call
analogical.

### Chapter IV: Archetypal Theology

Now that we have disentangled ourselves from that homonymy of sacred theology,
we must at last hand down separately about each kind those things which belong
to our purpose. We shall first say briefly about archetypal theology what seems
appropriate here. Then we shall unfold more freely the explanation of the other,
ectypal theology. As we prepare to speak about archetypal theology, in Thesis
VII we briefly comprehend both what can and what cannot be said by us:
Archetypal theology is divine wisdom concerning divine things. We worship this
wisdom, but do not investigate it. In the former clause we reverently disclose
what can be said by us concerning it, and in the latter what cannot be said.

We show in some manner what can be said concerning it by a proper description,
for the infinite certainly cannot be defined. We do so by declaring common
things which nature and Scripture teach, when we say that archetypal or
prototypal theology is nothing other than divine wisdom concerning divine
things. By these words we do not establish a definition, but a quasi-definition
or a certain analogous description taken from the example of our things and
from some accommodation of our language applied to divine things. Wisdom is
not the genus of this theology, but is posited as a quasi-genus for the sake of
teaching—unless perhaps, as the scholastics hold, and altogether rightly, we say
that wisdom is predicated univocally of God but equivocally of us. On this
account wisdom is not a genus but an adequate property, as they call it,
attributed supremely and properly to God and then transferred equivocally in
our speech from him to created things.

Similarly, what serves as a difference for us neither exists nor produces a
difference in God. For how could a difference be attributed to that most simple
essence which is as far removed as possible from all composition? A difference
constitutes the account of its species, just as form constitutes the account
of its matter, whereas God is so very simple in essence that no composition
can be attributed to him even by a plausible thought: no composition of matter
and form, of parts, of essence and existence, or of subject and accidents,
because whatever is in God is God.

This is why, by way of restriction or in place of a specific difference, as it
is commonly called, we added that this wisdom is *divine*: to teach that we are
here treating wisdom which is essential to God, supereminent, and raised
outside and above every genus. For it is not wisdom according to the genus of
wisdom distinguished by the philosophers [*Opera*, fol. 1378]. Human wisdom
derives its origin from elsewhere, as something accidental. It is a habit; it
is generated from principles; it is nourished by the understanding's combining
and dividing; it joins rational accounts and connects conclusions from them;
it produces knowledge; it is arbiter and judge of all these; and it establishes
the parts and their order which are subject to human wisdom.

This wisdom, however, is eternal and essential and therefore the very essence
of God. All things are most immediately present to it, not from any principles,
combination and division of the understanding, reasoning, conclusions,
knowledge, judgment, and ordering, but most simply, by one simultaneous
intuition of them all rather than successively as happens in created things.
This wisdom generates principles from itself

[Printed page 52; PDF page 82]

and is not generated from them. It produces understanding, rational account,
conclusions, sciences, and wisdom itself in others while remaining immutable
and unchanged in itself. Finally, while varying everything outside itself in
others through parts, order, and succession, it is the universal and unmoved
principle of all principles, understandings, rational accounts, conclusions,
and sciences: the wisdom which is mother of all wisdom.

Concerning it one may say most truly with Job in chapter 28: “There is indeed
a source for silver and a place for the gold which metalworkers refine; iron is
taken from the dust and bronze is poured from stone,” and so forth. “But where
could that wisdom be found, and where would be the place of understanding? A
mortal does not know its valuation, nor is it found in the land of the living.
The abyss says, ‘It is not in me,’ and the sea says, ‘It is not with me,’” and
so forth. “God alone understands its way, and he knows its place. For he looks
to the ends of the earth and sees beneath all the heavens,” and what follows.

Hence, if we come to the difference—if it can indeed be called a difference—we
shall observe not only that divine things are known, judged, and ordered by
this wisdom according to its object, mode, and end, but also that it is divine
wisdom according to its subject itself. For the sake of teaching, we may call
God, although most simple, its subject. This wisdom grasps all divine things,
both collectively and individually, without ceasing and all at once, by a
single and infinite intuition, without parts of things, order of parts, motion,
or succession of times—in the language of the scholastics, the whole at once
and totally. For this reason also we declare that this wisdom is divine in a
proper rather than figurative sense, so that whenever it is discussed we may
always lift our minds outside and above all human, temporal, and created things.

To make this better understood, let us now bring forward certain attributes of
this theology by which its distinction from our created theology may be
recognized. Archetypal theology is uncreated, formal, absolute, infinite, and
wholly simultaneous wisdom. It is incommunicable and communicates with created
things only its images or even its traces.

It is uncreated, for it has no beginning from anything, no mode of beginning
or creation, indeed not even any kind of beginning. It is itself the principle
of principles, “reaching from one end to another mightily and disposing all
things beneficially” (Wisdom 8).

It is formal, for it is essential to the divine form or Deity and therefore is
God's very essence, insofar as God is in every respect most simple and his
being, being wise, and knowing are the same, although we distinguish them by a
rational account in our minds.

It is absolute and most perfect, containing nothing deficient, redundant, or
variable, but in itself wholly free from defect, progress or increase, and
change.

It is infinite, for as the principle of all things it extends to all things
universally and individually. To use the apostle's words in Hebrews 4:13,
nothing is hidden from it, but all things are naked and manifest before its
eyes.

It is wholly simultaneous by reason of that spiritual continuity which
Aristotle himself also named in book 2 of *On the Soul*. It is present in God
neither by parts nor successively, nor does it vary in itself, but exists
according to the mode of God himself. Since his essence is wholly simultaneous,
indivisible, and immutable, his eternal wisdom is in precisely the same way
free from parts, succession, and every motion.

It is incommunicable, for it is proper to the Deity and consequently cannot be
communicated with any created thing, nor has it in fact been communicated with
anything which is other than God. Neither can ever occur: God himself cannot
yield this glory of his to another, as he formerly said through his prophets,
nor can another assume and possess it for itself.

Finally, it is communicative—not properly of itself, but at most only of its
image. The image, I say, of that inaccessible, infinite, and incomprehensible
wisdom, which no creature can sustain, much [*Opera*, fol. 1379] less contain,
is transmitted to created things and impressed upon them. It is derived as an
outflow, shines forth as a radiance, and descends as though it were a little
fragment for their illumination, while nevertheless that supreme exemplar,
fountain, sun, and trunk

[Printed page 53; PDF page 83]

or body, so to speak, remains whole. “With God,” Job says in chapter 12, verse
13, “are wisdom and might; counsel and understanding are his.”

What more, then, shall we say concerning this infinite and marvelous wisdom of
God, since we said before in a single phrase that it is not to be searched out
by us but rather worshiped? Whatever we can say about it is nothing in
comparison with it; whatever it is, is infinite and cannot be expressed. It is
marvelous in itself and to be revered by us with the utmost devotion. On this
account the wise man testifies that God's honor consists in concealing a
matter, and by that very fact bears witness to our smallness, because as a
gracious father he cares for our weakness by hiding it from his children
(Proverbs 25:2). Accordingly, when the apostle was carried away in admiration
of this wisdom, he exclaimed most vehemently, “O the depth of the riches of the
wisdom and knowledge of God!” (Romans 11:33). Struck with awe, we too stop at
this point. Since it is an abyss of wisdom, it is better now to come to the
streams communicated through it and flowing from it, lest its immensity swallow
our smallness if we entrust ourselves to that sea.

### Chapter V: What Ectypal Theology Is and How Many Kinds There Are

That secondary theology which we call ectypal is the offspring of the primary
theology which, as we said before, must be worshiped and not investigated by
us. We shall traverse its exceedingly broad and fruitful field as briefly as
possible, surrounding it with definite boundaries. We judge that its definition
can fittingly be handed down as follows. Thesis VIII: Ectypal theology, whether
considered simply (as they say) or relatively, is wisdom concerning divine
things, formed by God from his archetype through the communication of grace for
his glory. This definition sets forth both the preceding genus and difference,
and the very causes pertaining to theology's proper constitution.

Its genus is wisdom, by which we comprehend at once all principles, both natural
and supernatural; reasoning itself as it combines and divides; the conclusions
and determinations proceeding from it; knowledge of all those things; and use
tempered by judgment and ordering. Its difference and species are established,
as before, by the designation “divine things.” By divine things we comprehend
both the truth of those things which exist and are to be known, and the right
governing those things which are to be done and avoided. Since this knowledge
is perfect in itself in every respect, while both the true and the just are
necessary to the proper perfection and goodness of this divine image, both had
to be established in this wisdom: the theoretical and the practical. The former
consists in contemplating the true, and the latter in useful knowledge of what
is just and unjust, which neither has nor can have anything in common with the
false and unjust.

We acknowledge that all these things, insofar as they are explained in
theology, are divine not only in their object and mode, matters in which human
contemplative sciences seem to creep along, but also in their end and use. Wise
persons customarily observe these latter two in the active class of sciences,
as we demonstrated above in chapter 2. So much for theology's common genus and
difference. Let us now come to the causes expressed in the definition. The
chief place among causes in our definition belongs to our declaration that
this theology is formed by God.

[Printed page 54; PDF page 84]

God himself alone, and no created thing, is the efficient cause of the habit
which we call theology. For God alone is the true light, subsisting through
himself, and there is no darkness in him, as Saint John says most aptly in 1
John 1:5. Finally, since God himself is self-luminous, as the orthodox Greek
fathers elegantly declared, it would be ridiculous for anyone to believe that
light comes to created things from anywhere other than from him who is himself
self-luminous. Just as the sun lends light to the moon, so from the words of his
apostles we certainly establish that God, in whom there is light—that true
light [*Opera*, fol. 1380] which illuminates everyone coming into this world
(John 1:9)—is light to created things, communicates his light with them, and
makes known his manifold wisdom in heaven and on earth (Ephesians 3:10; James
1:18).

Together with this efficient cause we must join another which is commonly
called final. We expressed it in that definition by saying that this is done by
God for his glory. For just as God himself is the principle and most powerful
efficient cause, so he is also most self-sufficient. Consequently, he never
establishes the end of his counsels and actions in the nature of things or in
the dispensation of his grace outside himself. He needs nothing outside
himself, while all things are ordered toward him as needing him. “For from him,
and through him, and unto him are all things,” says the apostle. “To him be the
glory forever. Amen” (Romans 11:36). We sufficiently explained the matter of
this wisdom for our purpose a little earlier, when we treated the divine things
in which theology is occupied.

There remains, then, the most weighty topic of its form and mode. We briefly
but distinctly outlined both in our definition when we said that theology was
formed from that archetype through the communication of grace. Properly
speaking, form is posited in two ways with respect to anything made by an
artisan: one in the artisan's mind and the other in the work. Consequently,
just as we consider an internal and external act in our things, so form is also
twofold, internal and external. We call internal that eternal conception, so
to speak, of the divine will and grace considered in God himself. We call
external the influence of that eternal conception made in due time upon other
things, so to speak. God forms this wisdom in both ways: inwardly by his most
wise counsel and outwardly by his most powerful work.

But because this twofold form subsists in God as in a fountain and is derived
into other things as into pools, the result is that the learned have always
rightly and fittingly observed a twofold account of this wisdom. One is
absolute, or in the fountain of wisdom itself, which the common run of
scholastics call theology considered simply. The other is relative, as though
residing in its pools and receptacles, which they call theology considered
relatively. This distinction must be observed most diligently in every
discipline, but especially in this one, in which straying from the truth is
most dangerous. In this question, however, we shall consider not so much the
theology called simply as theology considered relatively, because the latter
is most particularly our theology, by whose communication we all draw from the
fullness of God in Christ Jesus (John 1:16).

If anyone asks what that ectypal theology considered simply is, I shall answer
by following the path of the preceding definition. Thesis IX: Theology
considered simply is the whole wisdom concerning divine things that can be
communicated with a created thing according to the measure of the one
communicating it. What human being could contain this theology in the mind or
describe it in speech? In a certain way it corresponds most closely to the
archetypal theology which we described in chapter 4. It is plainly nothing
other than the entire internal form and conception which God possesses in
himself concerning the communication of his wisdom—not according to the measure
of this or that creature, but according to the measure of God who communicates
and, from his fullness, prescribes the form and mode of his communication by
his gracious will. This is why, when defining this theology, we called it
*communicable* rather than *communicated*: by this name

[Printed page 55; PDF page 85]

we wish to comprehend not only whatever has in fact already been communicated
with created things, but also whatever can ever be communicated with created
things in this age and the age to come. To be brief, archetypal theology is the
infinity of all divine wisdom, both communicable and God's proper and
incommunicable wisdom. This theology is like the fullest storehouse, hidden
within God's infinity, of all spiritual wisdom and understanding. From that
storehouse and fullness treasures are brought forth to be communicated by God
with creatures through grace. While the treasures are in the storehouse they
are called communicable; while they are in creatures, communicated.

Therefore we must define theology considered relatively in the same manner.
Thesis X: This theology is wisdom concerning divine things communicated with
creatures according to their measure. It is communicated by union, vision, and
revelation. In this definition, first, I do not add “whole,” because this
theology is always partial, as they say, except in Christ our Savior. Further
[*Opera*, fol. 1383], I do not call it communicable as before, but actually
communicated, because only when it is communicated by the Lord does it receive
its relative determination. Finally, I do not add “according to the measure of
the one communicating it,” but only “according to the measure of the creatures
themselves” which receive its communication. And how very small, I ask, is this
measure when compared not only with the infinity of the Deity himself and his
archetypal theology, but even with that divine storehouse which we just
touched? For clarity, we shall hereafter designate these two kinds by definite
names, calling the former theology in itself and the latter theology in a
subject.


They all have this in common: we acknowledge that wisdom is either communicable
or communicated by divine action. By these words we contemplate its principle
in God, its origin from God, and its being brought forth in its subjects,
according to the communication of divine grace. The nature of the subject does
not produce this wisdom actually, to speak in the manner of the scholastics,
nor possess it virtually—much less formally—nor receive it potentially by its
natural light. God himself implants and inspires it by his supernatural light
and brings it forth by grace for his glory. Finally, he claims its origin,
progress, and entire completion for himself, so that whoever boasts may boast
in the Lord (1 Corinthians 1:31).

The attributes of this theology are very different from those we previously
assigned to archetypal theology. This theology is created and habitual, and
only according to its own mode absolute, finite, discrete, and divinely
communicated. It is the true and sure image of that theology which we taught to
be uncreated, essential or formal, most absolute, infinite, wholly
simultaneous, and incommunicable.

It is created, for it is not something belonging to the Creator in himself but
is from the Creator in a subject which he created.

It is habitual, for by God's grace it can come to a subject while the subject
remains whole, and can depart from the same subject through the absence of
grace.

Likewise, it is absolute in itself and according to the measure of the subject
in which it exists, but it is not absolute according to that perfection of God
which infinitely surpasses everything in every respect, since all wisdom,
cognition, and knowledge exists in any knower only according to the measure of
the person knowing.

It is finite, since it does not extend to and reach all things in precisely the
same way as that divine theology, but is most fittingly circumscribed by its
own ends and limits.

Nor is it wholly simultaneous. It is distinguished into parts, troubled by the
successions of things which seem to come and go by turns, and subject to change
and motion. Consequently, as we said last, it is wholly communicable by parts
and in fact also communicated by parts, according as God wills to kindle his
gracious light in his created things member by member, successively, and
mutably.

Now that we know what ectypal theology is in general, let us see how many kinds
there are. Before explaining this, however, we wish readers to remember well
the distinction

[Printed page 56; PDF page 86]

which we posited a little earlier: a twofold account must be employed in
contemplating this theology, for theology is considered both in itself and in
a subject, where it is, as it were, determined and modified according to the
measure of the subject in which this wisdom exists. We shall add nothing more
about theology in itself; what follows will concern theology in a subject.

According to the account either of the subjects or of the common and particular
mode by which God communicates it with subjects, we distinguish this theology
into three kinds. The first is the theology of Christ the God-man and our
Mediator; the second, the theology of spirits in heaven; and finally the third,
the theology of human beings on earth. According to the character of the mode
used by the author of all wisdom in communicating it, these three kinds are
also called by three other names of equivalent meaning. Some have called the
first the theology of union; the second, the theology of vision; and the third,
the theology of revelation.

The first theology is the highest and fullest of all, from which we all draw
(John 1:16), and it is in Christ according to his humanity. The second is full:
by it the blessed spirits obtain the glorious vision of God in heaven, and we
shall see God just as he is (1 John 3:2). Finally, the third is not full in the
thing itself, but rather is so furnished through the revelation of faith with
the principles of the same truth that it may fittingly be called full and
perfect in relation to us, although imperfect if compared with the heavenly
theology for which we hope, as the apostle taught the Corinthians (1
Corinthians 13:12). This, at last, is our theology. We shall therefore speak of
these in order hereafter. [*Opera*, fol. 1384]

### Chapter VI: The Theology of Union in Christ

The first kind of that ectypal theology, which is uniquely the theology of
Jesus Christ our Lord and Savior, is the fullest and most perfect of all that
can come into a created thing. By its condition this kind is nearest to the
archetypal theology and most closely joined to it in the unity of the subject,
that is, the person of Christ; and it subsists in him as its inseparable
principle. Thesis XI: The theology which we call the theology of union is the
whole wisdom concerning divine things communicated with Christ the God-man,
that is, insofar as the Word was made flesh, according to his humanity.

That this theology exists in Christ Jesus our Mediator and must be
distinguished from the archetypal theology which he possesses in common with
the Father and the Holy Spirit according to his divine nature in the unity of
essence can be demonstrated by three arguments: from the account of each nature
in Christ's person, from their personal union in Christ, and from our salvation.

First, if you consider the divine nature, which is most simple, knowing is the
same for the divine nature as being. When we distinguish them, we distinguish
only by a rational account things which are in reality one in the most simple
way. Its being is altogether incommunicable; therefore we must also call its
knowing incommunicable. This divine knowing, by which God knows divine things
divinely or according to the mode of his infinite perfection, is the very
essential and prototypal theology of which we spoke before. Further, if you
turn to the human nature, it is no more capable in itself of that divine
knowledge than of divine being. It is assumed by the divine being, but it does
not contain infinite being, because it is limited in itself and there can be no
proportion between the finite and infinite. The human nature therefore does not
contain that divine and archetypal knowledge within itself. It belongs to
divine perfection that it cannot

[Printed page 57; PDF page 87]

be communicated, and to human smallness that it cannot contain those things
which belong to divine perfection.

Second, the personal union itself of the two natures in Christ proves by
absolute necessity that the matter stands this way. Personal union produces
neither confusion nor transfusion of the properties belonging to this or that
nature, but requires the properties of each nature to be preserved intact in
their common supposit and its operations. Thus in a human being the mind, not
the body, understands. The body serves the mind instrumentally, not the mind
the body. Yet the supposit constituted from body and mind which we call a human
being acts in common, at once intelligently and instrumentally. If the mind's
property remains whole in the mind and cannot belong to the body, and the same
account holds for bodily properties—for if the contrary were established it
would no longer be a property—what shall we establish concerning divinity, or
that formal knowledge which is supremely proper to the divine essence, or any
other property?

Finally, we cannot establish the matter otherwise if we wish the complete
account of our salvation to stand. No salvation can come to a human being from
Christ Jesus unless we posit that voluntary dispensation according to which
Christ, to use the apostle's words in Philippians 2:7, Hebrews 2:14, and 4:15,
“emptied himself,” took the form of a servant, was made like human beings, was
like his brothers in every respect, and was tempted similarly in all things,
yet without sin. If he was like us in every respect, then he must also be
called like us in knowledge, although his knowledge was otherwise different
from and far superior to the knowledge of us all.

Why say more? Whoever thinks that Christ's knowledge as man should not be
distinguished from his knowledge as God is surely, though unwittingly, slipping
little by little into the camp of Apollinaris. Just as the substance of the
divine person does not abolish the reality of the human soul in our Savior,
which Apollinaris seemed to hold, so that divine knowledge which is an
essential property of the Deity in Christ's person does not destroy the
knowledge which is a property of humanity. Both concur in the unity of the
supposit, and both preserve their reality in the same Christ.

Divine knowledge in Christ is incommunicable, but it nevertheless communicates
its radiance. That radiance or splendor exists most fully and perfectly in
Christ's human nature. Therefore all the fullness of the Deity is said to dwell
in him bodily, and the apostle most solemnly declares [*Opera*, fol. 1385] that
from that fullness it follows that we are complete in him who is the head of
all rule and power, and so forth (the edition cites Colossians 1:12; compare
Colossians 2:9–10).

Thus this theology which we call the theology of union exists in Christ. What
it is has been almost entirely made known from the preceding discussion, so far
as it can be known in our weakness. This theology is altogether coextensive
with what in the immediately preceding chapter we called theology considered
simply or in itself. As far as that created and communicated or participated
theology considered simply extends, so far extends the theology of Christ our
Savior according to his humanity. Nothing in created things is in any way
theological except from this theology of Christ. He received this theology from
the Father for our sake, and we receive it from him: he is full of grace and
truth, and we all receive from his fullness (John 1:16).

A proper comparison of each theology according to its definition demonstrates
what we say. In the preceding chapter we said, “Theology considered simply is
the whole wisdom concerning divine things that can be communicated with a
created thing according to the measure of the one communicating it.” Now turn
your eyes and thought to the present definition. You will see that everything
is exactly the same in both except for the determination of act and subject,
when we say, “The theology of union is the whole wisdom concerning divine
things communicated with Christ the God-man, that is, insofar as the Word was
made flesh, according to his humanity.” In the former definition it was called
communicable, namely in an indeterminate potency, so to speak; here it is called
communicated in a determinate act. Further, because the former theology was
considered simply and in itself, no subject appeared in its definition. Here
the most comprehensive and incomparable subject is supplied when we say that
this theology was communicated with Christ the God-man.

[Printed page 58; PDF page 88]

This is just as if you were to say that the whole theology considered simply
in potency is at last determined actually in Christ. Yet if anyone perhaps
desires another account, let us try to define this theology of union according
to its causes. This theology, then, is wisdom concerning divine things,
divinely communicated with a human being in the Spirit of God without measure,
for the illumination of all who were created according to God's image. This is
that theology in itself. When it is viewed in a subject, as it must be, it is
the theology of Christ our Savior alone according to his humanity, not that of
any angel in heaven or of any other human being.

Since the knowledge of the Deity is an inaccessible fountain and a great
abyss, wisdom had to be supplied to the humanity which God assumed as a most
abundant river, yet one suited to created things, from which we will all draw.
In the same way, water engineers provide those who thirst with water drawn from
a cistern or pool after it has flowed from an inaccessible spring.

In this definition the genus, as before, is wisdom; the differences are posited
through the causes. The matter is divine things, insofar as they can be
communicated and must be set forth to us all in Christ. The form is the Spirit
of God given without measure. As John answered his disciples in John 3:34, “The
Son whom God sent speaks God's words, for God does not measure out the Spirit
to him. The Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand.”

The efficient cause is divine communication: both that by which God the Father
communicates the Spirit to the Son emptied according to his humanity, and the
Son's divine communication when he communicates the Spirit with his own
humanity through what the scholastics call the grace of union and its efficacy.
The printed Latin is syntactically compressed at *divinam ipsius*; this
translation follows the twofold communication clarified by the next sentence.
The same Christ is both the one anointing and the one anointed: anointing
according to his divinity and anointed according to his humanity, in the unity
of the person.

The end is the illumination of those who were created according to God's
image. The angels and blessed in heaven receive this illumination in the
present vision of God, while the faithful here on earth receive it by his
revelation, as those absent from the Lord. Although he is not far from each of
us, as Paul most excellently demonstrates in Acts 17:27, nevertheless, while we
remain here in the body, absent from the Lord as travelers abroad, we each walk
by faith and not by sight (2 Corinthians 5:7).

There is therefore a twofold mode of theological wisdom in Christ our
Redeemer: one divine according to his Deity, the other quasi-divine according
to his humanity. This quasi-divine mode is both divine in its eternal principle
and remains eternally in the unity of the person, and is quasi-divine or nearest
to the divine according to its own mode. Both had to exist together in Christ
our Mediator so that in this way he might associate and unite divine things
with human things, reconcile God to human beings, and lead human beings to God.
He is the one Immanuel, the Word made flesh and God made manifest in the flesh,
in whom [*Opera*, fol. 1386] all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are
hidden and who was made wisdom from God for us, as the apostle teaches (the
edition prints Isaiah 17:14, evidently for Isaiah 7:14; John 1:14; 1 Timothy
3:16; Colossians 2:9; 1 Corinthians 1:30).

This theology, considered as most absolute in Christ himself, is also
distinguished from the archetypal theology of which we spoke first in the very
ways which we established in chapter 4. Although the two have one common
subject or supposit, as they call it, namely the person of Christ, archetypal
theology is uncreated from eternity; essential or formal, one and the same in
the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; most absolute in itself, since it possesses
all things in itself from its own light; infinite; wholly simultaneous; and
incommunicable.

Ectypal theology, however, is created and began in time. It is habitual. It is
most absolute according to the mode of created nature, since in the person it
possesses light from the other nature personally united to it, as from its most
simple and absolute principle. It is quasi-infinite and nearest to the infinite,
so that from different perspectives one may quite suitably call it infinite
and finite: infinite when compared with our measured and meager knowledge, and
finite when compared with that essential and

[Printed page 59; PDF page 89]

infinite wisdom of God. It is not wholly simultaneous but participates in
vicissitudes, changes, and succession. Nor is it incommunicable: it is
communicated with created things and in fact according to the measured allotment
of his gifts. So much for the theology of union set forth in Christ our Savior
for the salvation of all.

### Chapter VII: The Theology of Vision in Heaven

Although in an altogether singular mode, that prototypal and essential wisdom
of God which we posited first is nevertheless the common principle of Christ's
created and habitual wisdom which we call the theology of union. In the same
way, that ectypal theology of union in Christ our Savior is the common principle
of the remaining theology which is received both by those blessed spirits in
heaven and by miserable human beings here on earth. Archetypal theology is the
womb of them all; ectypal theology in Christ is the mother of the rest. The
former is the fountain of all; the latter, as it were, their common cistern or
receptacle.

Created things draw from the divine fullness of this saving receptacle in two
ways: some by vision face to face, others by revelation, since they are absent
from and journeying away from the Lord (2 Corinthians 5:6–7). From these two
modes proceeded those other two kinds of ectypal theology, one of which the
orthodox fathers called the theology of the blessed and the other the theology
of wayfarers. We may therefore call the second form of ectypal theology the
theology of the blessed or exalted, and the third the theology of wayfarers or
the lowly. Christ sanctified both forms of theology in his person: he used the
lowly theology in the humiliation of the flesh and uses the exalted in that
exaltation by which he is now exalted above every name. In this way he showed
that the common principle of both theologies exists in himself.

Thesis XII: The theology of vision is that which is communicated with the
angels and with the spirits of the consecrated saints, or the perfected ones,
in heaven. We deliberately omitted the common genus and difference in this
definition because both are the same as was set forth in the immediately
preceding thesis: namely, that this theology is wisdom concerning divine
things. It remains, then, for us to consider the proper and, as they call it,
specific difference of this theology. We determined it by the words of our
definition in which we declared that it is communicated with the angels and
the spirits of the consecrated just, or the perfected ones, in heaven.

In these very few words we establish the distinctive circumscription of this
theology by a threefold argument. The first concerns its mode, since we call it
communicated by vision. The second concerns its subject, since it is
communicated with angels and the spirits of the just. Finally, the third
concerns its circumstances and especially its place, since it is communicated
in heaven. These things most clearly distinguish this theology both from the
preceding theology which we call the theology of union and from the following
theology of revelation.

First, the greatest difference is situated in its mode of communication, since
this theology is said to be communicated by vision. The communication of
spiritual things can only be spiritual, and spiritual [*Opera*, fol. 1387]
things can be seen by spiritual beings only in a spiritual mode, because
spiritual things are altogether most simple in their essence, power, works,
and experiences. We therefore establish that this mode of theological
communication is spiritual

[Printed page 60; PDF page 90]

and most simple. It follows that this theology is an intellectual, permanent,
and perfect light, communicated after the manner of an infused habit. By this
light those heavenly creatures see their Creator just as he is, through a
vision communicated with them from the fullness of Christ which we briefly
outlined in the preceding chapter.

Yet this theology cannot be compared with the preceding theology in any
respect. Christ's theology is a permanent and perfect habit of intellectual
light from and in itself, because his person is the God-man, subsists in that
archetypal and divine theology, and is a habitual fullness from that infinite
and essential fullness of the Deity which assumed flesh into the unity of the
person. The theology of vision, though it is a permanent habit of intellectual
light and perfect in its own mode, nevertheless neither remains, nor is
perfect, nor even has its origin from itself. It derives the principle of its
origin and of its whole nature from Christ our Savior, and it possesses its
preservation from nowhere except the power of Christ our Savior.

The fact that two subjects of this theology are declared, angels and the
spirits of the just, most strongly confirms our judgment. Neither the angels
nor the spirits received into glory with them possess their own theology in
heaven except according to the mode of communication which we just
circumscribed. It is no objection that the angels attained that divine light
by a simple vision, whereas human beings at last arrived there, as though
through the many circuits and windings of principles, reasonings, conclusions,
and manifold knowledge. This makes no difference to the thing, because in both
there is a permanent and, in its own mode, perfect intellectual habit through
the communication of grace and glory made by the Lord.

Here we are not treating the mode of a thing that is imperfect and striving
toward perfection, for that pertains to the account of theology considered in
a subject, of which we shall speak in its order. Rather, we treat its perfect
condition, as the circumstance of its place itself demonstrates. Everything in
heaven obtains perfection according to its own mode and is as far removed as
possible from all imperfection. For this reason the apostle to the Hebrews also
called the spirits of the just *teteleiōmenōn*, that is, “consecrated,” as it
is commonly explained, or perhaps more fittingly “raised to perfection” by
God's kindness (Hebrews 12:23). The Greek word fittingly signifies this.

Our establishment that this theology is only in heaven and not on earth most
excellently demonstrates its dignity. Heaven is the dwelling of God our Father
and the inheritance of his children. As long as we are here, we are absent
from the Father or, as Paul says, away from the Lord as travelers; we walk by
faith, not by sight, and strive toward the place of our inheritance (2
Corinthians 5:6–7). The condition and knowledge of one who is with the Father
in delights, enjoying his dwelling and inheritance, are far superior to those
of one who is very far away and grasps those things almost solely by the
imagination or thought of the mind. So, in those divine and spiritual matters,
the theology which is in heaven must be preferred to this theology which is on
earth.

If anyone desires a fuller definition of this theology, we shall now also
bring forward another according to its causes: the theology of the blessed or
exalted is wisdom concerning divine things communicated in the Spirit of God,
from Christ's allotted measure, with those who dwell in heaven, according to
which they enjoy an eternal, gracious, and glorious vision of God for his
glory. Here we have briefly joined all the causes pertaining to the definition
of this theology. Three things are common with the preceding theology of
Christ: it is wisdom, it concerns divine things, and it is communicated, as we
established universally in chapter 5. All the other elements, more narrowly
modified, constitute the species of this theology.

We indicated the efficient cause clearly enough when we said that it is
communicated in the Spirit of God, that is, by the power of the eternal Spirit
communicating spiritual gifts with the elect. We expressed the most manifest
effect of this efficient cause—yet one far inferior to and by no means
coextensive with its cause—

[Printed page 61; PDF page 91]

when we said that this theology was communicated from Christ's allotted
measure. For God does not measure out the Spirit to Christ (John 3:34), but
Christ dispenses to us according to the measure of his gift and graciously
bestows what, on whom, when, and as much as he wills [*Opera*, fol. 1388]
(Ephesians 4:16).

The material cause, or the subject of this theology as is customarily observed
in spiritual things, is indicated by the words “with those who dwell in
heaven,” whether angels or human beings carried there by grace, as we
distinguished in our preceding definition. The form is set forth by the words
which follow, when we add that according to this wisdom heavenly souls enjoy
the eternal and glorious vision of God. To express this in a way fitted for
understanding: the essential form of this theology is situated in a relation,
or in the ordering and application of heavenly things toward God as their most
absolute principle and exemplar. From him all light, grace, and glory are
diffused into created things, and he is also their end and that of every created
thing. We briefly touched this end alone in the definition by saying “for his
glory.”

This, then, is that theology of vision for which we hope, which we believe, in
which we rest by faith, and toward which we nevertheless strive, desiring to
depart and live with Christ, who is our light, life, and glory. When he is
revealed at his second coming, we too will be revealed with him most perfectly
in glory.

## Chapter VIII. Theology of Revelation in This Life

Thus far we have spoken of theology's intellectual light, abiding and perfect
in each of those to whom theology belongs. For that essential theology of God
which we have called archetypal is abiding and perfect with infinite
perfection. The habitual theology of Christ which we call the theology of union
is likewise abiding and perfect with a perfection that is, as it were,
infinite. Finally, that theology of vision which the angels in heaven and the
spirits of the righteous possess is perfect and abiding with the fullest
perfection proper to their mode. Having therefore explained those abiding forms
of true theology, we must now come to true theology which is nevertheless not
abiding, so that by setting some bounds around it we may better grasp the mode
of our wisdom.

For the apostle writes most correctly to the Corinthians: “Prophecies will be
rendered useless, tongues will cease, and knowledge itself likewise. For we
know in part and prophesy in part; but when that which is perfect has come, then
that which is in part will be abolished. When I was a child, I spoke as a
child, thought as a child, and reasoned as a child; but when I became a man, I
put away as useless the things that belonged to childhood. For now we see
through a mirror and in a riddle, but then we shall see face to face. Now I know
in part, but then I shall know just as I also have been known,” 1 Corinthians
13:8–9 and following.

This is not because we judge that this theology of ours, of which we will speak
from this point onward, suffers from any imperfection considered in itself. In
the very thing that it has, whatever it has, it is most perfect. Its perfection,
however, is tempered to our meagerness and imperfection, because it comprehends
only the principles of that most perfect theology in heaven. By these
principles—perfect indeed in themselves, yet imperfect in a certain
respect—we are carried toward heavenly perfection. Just as principles are
implanted in our nature,

[Printed page 62; PDF page 92]

which, as time proceeds, rise through the cultivation
of reason to the conclusions and determinations of a right knowledge according
to the mode of human nature, so also in the work of grace God imitates the
order of nature and begins from the principles of that grace and wisdom. These
principles, infused into us by grace in this life, are watered and grow by that
same power until we obtain their completion in the glorious vision of God and
communion with him in heaven.

These principles, or seeds, are therefore the subject matter of that theology
which we call the theology of revelation.

**Thesis XIII. The theology of revelation is that which is communicated here
with the human race; it may also quite appropriately be called our theology.**

When we call it the theology of revelation, we make quite clear the very thing
that we were saying a little before. With respect to saving grace, what usually
happens to a human being in this life resembles what happens to the acuity of
our eyes in seeing things. The eye does not see things that are extremely far
away, however immense they may be, because the power of the eyes is weakened
and dulled by the excessive distance of the things. As they come nearer, the
things appear obscure and are not distinguished from one another; but when
brought very near and accommodated to the condition of our sight, they are
plainly known.

The same rationale applies to the contemplation and communication of grace.
None of us can see that grace and light of [*Opera*, fol. 1389] divine things,
as though it were placed exceedingly far above us and all things. This is
especially so because the blindness of our mind has been added as a further
impediment by which we are hindered in contemplating divine things, even if
those things could in themselves be perceived by our contemplation. But when
it pleased God to move those supernatural things nearer to the eyes of our mind
through this theology which we call the theology of revelation, then those
benefits of grace are seen as if from far off, and God is seen as the author of
such great benefits. Yet we still see only generally, obscurely, and
imperfectly, as those who gradually draw nearer to something placed before
their eyes are accustomed to see it.

But when we have completed the course of this life in which we are absent from
the Lord—and have done so under the guiding light of the torch of revelation
which this theology teaches—then we shall see at close hand and attain in
Christ that perfection of knowledge which is called the theology of vision.

We define this theology of revelation, which is also called ours, in precisely
the same order in which we defined the preceding theology. Its common genus and
difference are “wisdom concerning divine things.” We did not state these words
expressly because they are clearly understood from what precedes. We indicated
the particular difference between this theology and the others which we
sketched above by the words “which is communicated here with the human race.”
The mode is communication; the human race is established as the subject to
which this communication belongs; and place and time are defined by the word
“here,” that is, in this station and the present age.

From these considerations there necessarily follows what we touched on before:
the intellectual light of this theology can be called not abiding but passing,
and not simply perfect but only perfect and absolute in a certain respect. For
if we consider the mode of communication, it is a revelation in which God does
not disclose the whole object of theology as it is, but as it can be
comprehended by a human being in this station and weakness. If you consider the
subject himself, he is assuredly nothing in comparison with the infinite
vastness of that object, which cannot enter a person's mind even in a dream.
Finally, if in mind and thought you contemplate this station of ours, so very
far removed from God, and the brevity and vanity of this age of ours, it is
utterly impossible not to recognize that the meagerness of these principles is
very far removed from the supreme perfection of that heavenly theology. The
purpose is that through these slender testimonies of divine grace we may strive
all the more reverently toward those supreme things and that every one of us
may press on with the greatest diligence.

But if anyone should nevertheless ask for another definition of this theology
through its causes, as we did before in a very similar matter, I think the
following will serve suitably: **Our theology is wisdom concerning divine
things, communicated by revelation through the Spirit of God, by the benefit
of this nature or of grace, with those

[Printed page 63; PDF page 93]

who dwell on earth; according to it, by an intellectual light—yet one which is passing
and imperfect in its manner—they contemplate God and his divine things through
their several stages of growth until they attain his perfect vision for his
glory.**

In this definition, after the common genus and the difference belonging to a
proper definition have been stated, the individual causes are presented in the
same order that we followed before. First, God is named as the efficient cause,
communicating through the Spirit by revelation, and doing so by the benefit of
this nature of ours or of grace toward those human beings who dwell on earth,
the saints who are called and the heirs of the saving promises in Christ Jesus.
Then the matter, or object, which theology sets before us to be apprehended by
faith is shown: namely, **God and his divine things**.

Next the form is explained: according to this wisdom, by an intellectual light
kindled in us through natural or supernatural grace, we contemplate that object
of ours. Yet the light is passing and imperfect, and therefore—as Paul
says—will at last be abolished at the time when that which is perfect has come.
While awaiting this perfection, the godly grow through their several stages
into a perfect man, to the measure of the stature of the mature Christ, and
rise to his perfect vision, Ephesians 4:13. Finally, the end of this whole
wisdom is **the glory of God**: first set before our faith here, that is, the
faith of believers, and afterward to be enjoyed most perfectly in heaven in
God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

## Chapter IX. How Many Modes There Are of Communicating Theology

From what has been said above, we observe that theology as a whole is
distinguished in two ways [*Opera*, fol. 1390], for one must be called infinite
and the other finite. We believe that the theology which is archetypal
(ἀρχέτυπος) and essential to God is properly and absolutely infinite. That
habitual wisdom of Christ which embraces all the treasures of wisdom and saving
knowledge is infinite in a certain respect, as they say, and in relation to us.
Correspondingly, the theology communicated with created things is properly and
absolutely called finite, whether communicated perfectly in heavenly glory or
imperfectly in this earthly course, where Christ our Lord and Savior shines
before us with the light of his grace. But because this theology of ours, like
the heavenly theology belonging to creatures, is communicated by God through a
fixed way and rationale, before we speak more precisely about the theology of
revelation we must distinguish as clearly as possible the modes by which
theology is communicated with created things.

**Thesis XIV. There are therefore two modes of communicating theology: nature
and grace. Nature is like the internal principle of the communication, and
grace like its external principle. Consequently one theology is called natural
and the other supernatural.**

Paul disclosed this way of distinguishing the communication so openly when he
wrote Romans 1 that no wise person can deny it. For he declares that every
human being is imbued with some knowledge of God and of himself, first by the
light of nature and then by the authority of Scripture. Even one of these
testimonies is sufficient to deprive sinners of every excuse for their sin. He
thus establishes an altogether twofold mode of communication by which we have
been made participants in this theology. There are therefore two principles of
that communication, from which there likewise proceeds a twofold

[Printed page 64; PDF page 94]

mode of communication. Of these principles, we call one
nature and the other grace.

God, the author of every good in the nature of things, is the common principle
of nature just as much as of grace, whether that good exists according to
nature or above nature. But because the Lord graciously saw fit at the very
time of creation to endow some of his creatures with a certain natural theology
and to sow its principles into their intellect, we will certainly be ungrateful
to God if we omit this grace, even though it is natural. Yet precisely because
this grace is natural, we must remember that it is set over against that grace
of revelation from which our theology is called the theology of revelation.

From this common principle was begotten in nature that internal and particular
principle which we call the image of God. According to this image, Adam as
constituted in this life directed himself toward God as his exemplar by his own
proper and internal motion. God had implanted the common principle of this
motion both in Adam's particular nature and in the nature common to all.
Another mode of communicating theology, which the orthodox fathers called
supernatural grace, always had to be joined to this internal principle
established in human nature, which we call natural grace. For that natural
principle remained in us only so long as it adhered to that supernatural and
external principle; as soon as it turned away from it, it became corrupt and
most wretched.

It has therefore come about that theology is established as twofold by analogy:
one is called natural and the other supernatural. When we say “natural,” we do
not wish it to be taken here in the sense which we presented above in Chapter I
from Varro and Augustine, but properly and absolutely, as we will soon define it
if God wills. In our division we set this theology analogically over against
that supernatural and revealed theology, but not properly, because in truth
they differ from one another in their entire genus.

For just as that supernatural principle, God, cannot be included in any common
genus with the natural principle, that is, our intellect, so too the actions
that properly flow from each principle cannot be forced by us into any common
genus. They are plainly disparate and, as the saying goes, differ by the whole
distance of heaven. Things which stand in this relation to one another cannot
be divided by a coordinate counterdivision (ἀντιδιῃρημένως), since the one is
superior to the other in every respect and cannot be equated with it either by
reason of any common genus or by reason of species.

## Chapter X. Natural Theology: Thesis XV

**Natural theology is that which proceeds from principles known in themselves,
by the natural light of the human intellect [*Opera*, fol. 1391], according to
the mode of human reason.**

In this definition of ours we state neither a genus nor a difference, lest this
theology appear to share a genus or difference with those that precede it. For
this theology cannot be called wisdom according to its genus except
equivocally. Nor can the divine things from which the difference of this
theology must be constituted be attributed to this theology properly and
absolutely, but only with the same homonymy. Perhaps you might put knowledge, or
even consciousness if that seems right, in the place of its genus; but wisdom
least of all. The divine things whose rationale constitutes the difference of
true theology are called divine here partly properly and partly improperly and
according to opinion. Nevertheless, I am content for it to be called knowledge
of

[Printed page 65; PDF page 95]

divine things, so that some genus of this theology, although equivocal, may be
agreed upon among everyone.

The particular circumscription of this theology according to its causes was
given by us in the preceding definition. The efficient cause of this theology
is nature itself and the natural light of our intellect. Its material cause is
the principles of divine things known in themselves, in which this natural
theology consists and with which it is occupied. Its form is that this theology
proceeds from those principles according to the mode of human reason. We did
not take pains to include the end, because it is certain under nature's
teaching and yet did not seem especially relevant to this definition of ours.

The efficient cause of this theology, therefore, is not art, chance, or
anything else, but nature itself, which the philosophers have taught to be the
principle and cause, primarily and in itself, of motion and rest in that in
which it exists. This is the common principle from which this natural theology
is individually impressed upon every human being. But this common principle
acts in individuals in such a way that it employs certain instruments for its
several functions, like parts of nature ministering to the whole. The human
intellect is therefore laid beneath it as the proper subject of this theology.
By its natural light the principles are perceived, and the deductions from
those principles follow in nature's proper order.

We call principles those things which are known in themselves by the light of
nature, which are known immediately, and which are fixed or immutable, so that
certain knowledge may at last follow from them. From these principles,
reasoning draws fixed procedures as streams from springs. It compares the
principles of nature with the truth of things, or distinguishes the principles
from one another; it joins causes with effects and draws conclusions from them;
it determines common and particular things; finally, it so orders everything
that it may attain knowledge of all the things which human reason can
investigate and grasp.

But the mode of human reason is exceedingly narrow because of the weakness of
our intellect. It stands in relation to the clearest things in nature just as
an owl's eye stands in relation to the light of the sun, as that celebrated
philosopher Aristotle acknowledged in the *Metaphysics*. We therefore saw fit
to delimit the form of this whole cognition by the words **“according to the
mode of human reason.”** From this we necessarily conclude that if human reason
is so weak not only in human matters but even in natural ones, much narrower
bounds must be placed around our reasoning in matters above nature. Otherwise,
in such lofty matters whose concealment causes God's glory to shine most
brightly, as the Wise Man once taught, the natural person might dare to indulge
and flatter himself.

No one endowed with even moderate reason will deny this general statement, for
the common notions produced by nature teach it, and the experience of every age
and people proves it. Nevertheless, to place the matter more conveniently
before everyone's eyes, it seems that three things must be explained in order
in this question. First we must see what those principles which adhere to us
from nature are and what kind they are. Then we must consider the character of
this nature in which those principles have been begotten as in their subject.
Finally, we must consider what the work and effects of that nature and its
principles are, or what they can additionally be, both in the investigation and
comprehension of human and natural things and in that of divine things, with
the aid of our human reason.

We therefore describe the capacity of the principles which are in us in these
few words: **Thesis XVI. The notion belonging to this natural theology in the
human intellect deals with common things and is obscure and imperfect; it must
therefore receive its perfection from the supernatural.**

Although, in view of the subject which we have undertaken to explain here, we
are dealing specifically with the ordering of our reason toward divine things
according to the power of nature, nevertheless the things said here [*Opera*,
fol. 1392] about the principles of our nature can also be applied in a certain
general way to all human things, and especially to moral things. By this path
we may attain knowledge of our nature, insofar as it pertains to this

[Printed page 66; PDF page 96]

subject.

We designate those principles of which we are speaking by the word “notion,”
as Cicero calls them in very many places; the Greeks called them *ennoiai* and
*katalēpseis* (ἐννοίας and καταλήψεις). This is correct. For the intellect of
our mind, insofar as the natural philosophers set it over against reason, is
clearly the seat of natural principles. Those principles adhere to each
person's intellect in such a way that they are in it simply, apart from the
contemplation of things outside. These principles are therefore rightly called
notions or preconceptions. This simple knowledge of our intellect is so
implanted inwardly by nature that it ordinarily precedes all other knowledge
and is laid beneath every kind of knowledge as a common foundation.

The capacity of this notion and these principles is bounded in three ways by
our words, when in the subject of theology we call those notions **common,
obscure, and imperfect**. Three things are needed for a proper comprehension of
things: we must comprehend the subject in question truly, clearly, and fully.
If, instead of the true subject, we look to something false or merely
conjectural—as commonly happens when we recognize the truth in general but
depart from it in the particular—the principles have been falsely applied. If
the light of the principles does not let us see the subject or the actions
pertaining to it sufficiently, the notion is obscure. But if we can view the
things which must be considered only in part, the notion is imperfect.

For example, it is a natural principle that each person should be given his
right. We commonly err in three ways when using this principle. We restrict by
exceptions what nature dictates generally about everyone, and we veil those
principles and common notions with our particular conclusions, the obscurity of
the intervening means, and the imperfection of our judgment. Finally, to plant
our foot firmly in the very question we have undertaken, a natural principle
dictates to everyone that **God must be worshiped**. But this principle is
common in everyone, obscure, and imperfect. It is common because it does not
teach God particularly, as though pointing with a finger to indicate that this
one is God himself. It is obscure because it does not show clearly the very
thing it indicates to us. Finally, it is imperfect because it does not show it
fully.

The apostle demonstrates this state of affairs most clearly when he says to the
people of Lystra who wished to sacrifice to him: “Why are you doing these
things? We too are human beings subject to the same affections as you,
proclaiming that you should turn from these vain things to the living God, who
made heaven and earth and sea and everything in them. In past ages he permitted
all the nations to walk in their own ways, although he did not leave himself
without testimony: he bestowed good things, giving us rains from heaven and
seasons fit for producing fruit, filling our hearts with food and gladness,”
Acts 14:15–17.

The people of Lystra themselves demonstrate by their action that a common
notion about God had been impressed upon them all, but not that particular
notion by which they would recognize this one as the true God; and Paul proves
the point. His arguments demonstrate that the notion is obscure, since the
apostle offers nothing but testimonies of nature or deeds performed by God in
nature to disclose the truth of that God who is supremely to be adored. These
are only the faintest footprints of that true and living God. They testify not
what God has been to the human race from the beginning, but rather what belongs
to God. By the same rationale, these principles are also imperfect. If they
testify anything about God, as they certainly do, they testify most imperfectly
and are exceedingly far removed from his perfection.

Of the same kind is what the apostle declared when he recalled the Athenians
from idolatry: “As I passed through,” he says, “and observed your sacred
objects, I also found an altar on which was inscribed, ‘To the unknown God.’
Therefore the one whom you worship without knowing, this one I proclaim to you.
That God who made the world and everything in it, since he is Lord of heaven
and earth, does not dwell in temples made by hands,” and so forth, Acts
17:23–24. By these words he explains the condition of those natural principles
most clearly. They did not know God particularly, whom the common principles
teach must be worshiped

[Printed page 67; PDF page 97]

in general. Nor did they know him evidently, since they had lived in the times
of ignorance which God overlooked. Finally, they did not know him perfectly,
since God is said to give everyone life, breath, and all things and to have made
the whole race of humankind from one blood so that they might seek the Lord and
perhaps find him by feeling their way—and the remaining things which Paul
presents there with the greatest weight.

The same apostle therefore affirms most fittingly that “the wrath of God is
revealed from heaven against every impiety and injustice of human beings who
unjustly suppress” [*Opera*, fol. 1393] “the truth, because that which can be
known about God by the faculty and order of nature is manifest in them, for God
made it manifest to them. From the creation of the world his invisible things
are perceived, being understood from the things that have been made—namely, his
eternal power and divinity,” and they are perceived by that common, obscure,
and imperfect mode from the very principles of nature, Romans 1:19–20.

This common, obscure, and imperfect character of the principles is recognized
in searching out the truth of things, and especially of divine things. Thus, if
some truth about God is discovered by reason, it comes to a person only through
a few people, over a long period, and with an admixture of very many errors.
The same disadvantage also occurs in searching out that right which the apostle
there calls **the righteous ordinance of God** (*to dikaiōma tou theou*, τὸ
δικαίωμα τοῦ θεοῦ). Whether you contemplate the divine right and ordering
established concerning all things in themselves and among themselves, or the
moral right pertaining to the creatures whom he made in his image, there is
nothing in which this same disadvantage cannot easily be proved.

After weighing everything, therefore, we conclude most appropriately that
these principles and notions possess neither a perfection capable of divine
things nor any aptitude or power whatsoever for undertaking the investigation
of those things. Natural theology plainly needs to receive from supernatural
grace both its perfection, if it is to receive any, and a **receptive power**
(*dynamin dektikēn*, δύναμιν δεκτικήν), that is, one fitted to receive that
perfection. So unfit is the acuity of this whole nature of ours for seeing and
perceiving the things which God has placed above nature.

We have spoken about the principles. Now let us consider the whole of nature
itself, in which those principles have been begotten as in their subject. We
must observe a twofold account of our nature, in which these principles and
common notions reside. This nature is considered either in itself as it was
created by God, or according to the accident of sin and the corruption which
followed from the fall of our first parents. Concerning the former mode and the
condition of our original nature, together with the principles implanted in it,
we state the following.

**Thesis XVII. This was the condition of natural theology in Adam's uncorrupted
nature: it had to be cultivated and increased by reasoning from common,
obscure, and imperfect principles, and perfected by grace.**

I am not unaware that what we have just said will seem novel and unheard of to
very many: uncorrupted nature as it existed in Adam from creation was furnished
with what were, as it were, these boundaries of its theology and advanced no
farther according to nature. Nevertheless, those who weigh the following two
points at the same time will, I think, cease to marvel. The first is that the
very designation “natural theology” excludes supernatural wisdom as something
differing in its entire genus. Nor can the one be confused with the other
without establishing an opposite within its opposite, as the scholastics say,
and a most manifest collision between the two members, something utterly alien
to every science.

The second is that this does not abolish the rationale of the growth which a
human being with uncorrupted nature would have attained from the principles
until perfected by supernatural grace and raised to its summit. Human nature is
so constituted that it has principles within itself but attains progress by
reasoning and study until it arrives at proper knowledge. No one can therefore
doubt that this account of nature was also common in Adam and would have
continued in his descendants if they had stood firm in that original
integrity. Adam consequently possessed principles that were incorrupt, as
principles of nature are. Then by comparing these principles with things and

[Printed page 68; PDF page 98]

signs, through the work of reasoning he could successively combine, divide,
conclude, and determine, even according to nature, whatever nature would attain
in divine things by that faculty;

and finally acquire some knowledge of divine things according to the mode of
uncorrupted nature. But he could not transcend above nature, because each of
his faculties had its own particular mode. Adam would indeed have risen beyond
that mode, but by the benefit of supernatural grace, not by the power of his
own nature. For everything in him was whole and incorrupt, but bounded by the
limits of nature and not roaming beyond nature.

The principles in him were therefore common. Had they been particular, they
would not have been principles but conclusions and determinations of reason
belonging to true and proper knowledge. They were also obscure in him. Had they
been plainly clear and evident, they would no longer have been principles only,
or even rightly called principles; the middle terms would also have been
implanted in him. By everyone's agreement, these middle terms properly belong
not to the intellect as the permanent seat of principles, but to reason.
Finally, those principles were imperfect—not with a faulty perfection or an
imperfection that was a defect of nature, for from creation they possessed the
imperfection proper to nature, but with imperfection in a certain respect, as
they call it [*Opera*, fol. 1394]. That is, they were imperfect by comparison
with the infinite perfection of God and divine things, a perfection which no
created thing can reach by the power of its nature and which none can
comprehend by itself.

These principles, like seeds deposited in the field of the human intellect,
likewise had to be cultivated and increased by adding nature's work to the
principles. Reason is nature's worker in a human being made in God's image. By
the cultivation of this reason, those principles had to be advanced from their
generality to particular works, from obscurity into light, and from
imperfection into some perfection according to the mode of the natural faculty,
so that knowledge might be produced in the human mind from the seed of the
principles and the cultivation of reason.

What then? Could the knowledge of divine things be perfected in an uncorrupted
human being by these instruments of nature? Certainly not. Reason itself could
not help laboring under obscurity and imperfection, since it had no material
other than the principles from which to generate knowledge. For, as the more
recent philosophers are accustomed to say, all the things which human reason
and knowledge comprehend particularly are potentially present in the
principles in a general way. It follows immediately that even in uncorrupted
human nature reason could not ascend to a higher summit of human knowledge than
it could build upon the principles and from the principles themselves.

Once these points are established, we rightly conclude that even in
uncorrupted human nature theology could not have been perfect according to the
perfection of human nature absolutely considered. That theology had to be
perfected by God's supernatural grace, or rather, once perfection entered in
its place, had to be abolished, as it were, so that a human being enriched with
supernatural theology would remain and by supernatural power be conveyed
through grace to that blessed condition, into the living hope of which we have
been regenerated by the Lord, 1 Peter 1:3.

No one has reason to marvel that we say natural theology would have been
abolished by supernatural theology. The apostle teaches us to speak in this way
when he talks about this theology of ours and that which will exist in heaven:
“For we know in part,” he says, “and prophesy in part; but when perfection has
come, then that which is in part will be abolished,” and so forth, 1
Corinthians 13:9. That superinduction (*epeisagōgē*, ἐπεισαγωγὴ) of a form not
merely different but belonging to another and most perfect genus will, as it
were, absorb this form of our theology and claim it for the perfection of that
theology.

This is sufficient concerning the condition of natural theology in
uncorrupted human nature. Let us now consider its condition after nature was
corrupted by depravity. **Thesis XVIII. After this nature was corrupted, the
very same principles indeed remained in individuals—common, obscure, and
imperfect—but were most corrupt in themselves and most disordered among
themselves, like the ruins of our nature through our depravity.** It is not the
purpose

[Printed page 69; PDF page 99]

of our present plan to deal particularly with the corruption of our nature or
with the origin and mode of that corruption. For now we will do only this: come
to know the mode or condition of that natural theology after the human being
fell wretchedly into sin.

The familiar saying is well known, which the orthodox fathers and the
scholastics following in their footsteps taught most fittingly: the natural
gifts were corrupted, and the supernatural gifts were lost. From this saying
we establish that supernatural theology, as though most shamefully disowned and
repulsed by human sin, withdrew from here into heaven, while natural theology,
like everything else arising from nature, was corrupted. For how could it have
remained incorrupt in every respect in a corrupt subject?

The mode of this corruption must be observed in two ways: first in the
constitution of the subject itself and its individual parts, and second in the
ordering of the subject and its parts among themselves and toward all other
things. As we said above, the subject contained the following: principles in
the intellect, and reason in the mind, from which the natural human being could
acquire some knowledge. But those principles in uncorrupted human nature
suffered from their own disadvantages, and in view of the condition of those
principles human reason was not free from mutability. The corruption that
overtook the human being certainly could not cure these disadvantages, preserve
them in the same state, or defend them from any diminution.

Thus, if they were previously common in individuals, they also remained common,
but with vice attacking them. If they had been obscure, they became much more
obscure. Finally, if they had been im[*Opera*, fol. 1395]perfect, they fell into
a much graver imperfection. Human reason therefore could not help being carried
toward something worse and being most grievously and shamefully corrupted.
Consequently, it can be said with the greatest truth about human nature that,
just as the whole nature, so too all its principles were most corrupt in
themselves. This was especially true of the principles pertaining to theology,
whose subject lies above the whole nature of all created things and cannot be
apprehended even by uncorrupted nature except according to the mode of nature
itself.

Not only were those principles most corrupt in themselves, but, what is more,
they were utterly estranged from every proper order and fitting relation among
themselves. For what could be imagined more disorderly (*atakton*, ἄτακτον) than
someone who observes the due order neither with his own whole, nor with the
parts of that whole, nor with other things outside his whole? Those very
principles were immediately found to be of this kind in the fallen human being.
They remained the same principles in foundation but were most diverse in mode,
since they could maintain no measure either in themselves, among themselves, or
with other things. This is the reason we say they were most disordered among
themselves, like the ruins of a nature lying fallen and wretchedly prostrated by
our grievous fall.

If an elegant house is thrown down by a most grievous fall and collapses in a
single moment through some greater force, all its form disappears. Its parts,
although skillfully made and beautiful beyond measure, lie battered and corrupt
and remain hidden, buried in heaps of ruins. In the same way, whatever was
elegant in human nature has passed away and lies buried, as it were, in the
unformed and unordered mass of our depravity.

The last thing remaining is to consider the work and effects of that nature and
the natural principles in it. If the work of the principles and of our whole
nature is already so meager even in the matters to which nature can rise, then
the weakness and impotence of nature and its principles must be maintained all
the more in natural theology. For this nature cannot comprehend the subject of
theology; that subject infinitely surpasses the whole of nature, not merely the
nature of each human being.

We therefore conclude the following from what precedes about natural theology
if we consider its work and effects: **Thesis XIX. This theology can lead
absolutely nothing to perfection and never does so; nor is it even capable in
itself of the perfection which comes upon it from grace.**

We have briefly declared three things here about natural theology, according to
the instruction of those skilled in the sciences that three things be weighed
according to the essence of the things under consideration:

[Printed page 70; PDF page 100]

**capacity, action, and passivity** (*dynamin, ergon, kai pathos*, δύναμιν,
ἔργον, καὶ πάθος). Concerning the capacity of this theology, we say: **This
theology can lead absolutely nothing to perfection.** This is no wonder. Since
it cannot itself attain perfection, how could it advance others to proper
perfection?

Concerning its work or action, we say that **it never leads anything to
perfection**. Everyone endowed with reason and judgment will regard this matter
as beyond all dispute, especially if they consider that an imperfect—and indeed
most imperfect—cause can produce only imperfect effects. Otherwise, which is
utterly absurd, an imperfect tree would be said to bear perfect fruit.

The third point which we affirm about this natural theology may perhaps seem
more burdensome and difficult to believe to some people who cannot bear that
everything should be taken away from our nature and attributed to God's
glorious grace. We say that **this natural theology is not in itself even
capable of the perfection which comes upon it from grace**. That is, it has in
itself, by its own nature or power, no affection, ordered relation, or
disposition, as they call it, by which it might receive that perfection poured
over it by heavenly grace.

Thus, anything in nature and from nature may receive something according to
that genus of potential which they call passive, on the basis of our nature's
aptitude and disposition. Nevertheless, if you look to the fruit and reception
of those heavenly and spiritual gifts which God communicates with human beings
by particular revelation, this theology has in itself no passive potential, no
receptive potential, no obediential potential, as the scholastics call it, and
finally no disposition by which natural theology might share anything with
supernatural theology.

The subject around which they are occupied is indeed common to both, but these
two wisdoms differ from one another, so to speak, in the totality of their
nature. The sharing of the same subject does not make sciences cognate; their
mode does. The musician and the arithmetician, for example, are both occupied
with numbers, but they differ from one another in their mo[*Opera*, fol. 1396]de
of treatment. This is demonstrated by even one argument of comparison between
the two: nature draws a disposition only from matter which already exists, but
the Spirit of God works all things in all. Thus, for natural theology nature
employs the intellect and uses the seeds of the principles for its disposition;
but for supernatural theology the Spirit of God claims every part entirely for
himself, so that it is called supernatural by every right.

## Chapter XI. Supernatural Theology

Having explained the weakness of this nature of ours, from which the mode of
natural theology can be ascertained with certainty, we must now see what nature
seeks and whence the mode of attaining the thing it seeks can come. Whether you
consider our nature as common, by which everyone seeks good in general, or as
particular, by which individuals are drawn to seek this or that according to
their condition, we see that it is always borne toward attaining its own goods
and fleeing their contraries. This striving of nature ordinarily shines so
strongly even in the most trivial matters that even the most inexperienced
person perceives, with nature as guide, that good is to be sought.

Among all things, however, nothing appears so necessary, useful, and worthy as
that the human being, established as prince in this universe of things, should
be raised toward his good,

[Printed page 71; PDF page 101]

a good to which he can rise neither by the faculty of nature, nor by human art,
nor with the aid of any created thing. It was therefore necessary either for
the human being himself to investigate by every means whence he could obtain
the way and rationale of that good, or by the benefit of the one who can confer
it to be led to the knowledge and apprehension of the way by which he might
attain the good.

Yet it could never have come about that the human being would know the very
author of that good of his, the way to him, and the mode of receiving the good
by the powers of his nature, by any art, or by the assistance of any created
thing. For how could a creature lost in himself and justly adjudged away from
the author of his good ever reach him? How could one who is blind see the
invisible? How could one who is dead apprehend and establish the way by which
he might strive toward the good which is truly good?

**Thesis XX. Therefore that inspired theology had to come upon the human being.
We call it supernatural from its origin, and the theology of revelation from
the gracious mode of its communication.**

Otherwise, the human mind, emerging from the awareness of its evil, could never
have arrived at a sure hope and apprehension of the good. The author of the
good therefore had to descend to the wretched and cast-down human being, since
the human being could by no rationale ascend to him. God, the author of every
good, approaches human beings in various ways according to the various modes of
his operation. But if any such approach is saving for us while we dwell in this
life, it is surely the gift of cognition by which we learn to know God just as
we are known by him.

This is the good which we call inspired theology. For by inspiration God knows
us, or rather testifies that we are known by him; and by the theology which he
inspires in us, we know God just as we were known by him. We say that this
saving inspiration from God and the cognition communicated by his benefit come
upon us because no principle or seed of this good which is effectual for human
salvation can exist in us, in our possession, in the things surrounding us, or
in the whole universe of things. Rather, that God who is the author of the
good, who is in all, through all, and above all, must pour over us that saving
grace and cause it to come upon us from him, so that we may believe him when he
speaks heavenly things, though we did not believe him even when he spoke
earthly things.

The origin, nature, and mode of this entire mystery had to be effected from
above. From this arose our practice of calling this inspired theology
supernatural from its origin and the theology of revelation from its gracious
mode. For by its origin, its na[*Opera*, fol. 1397]ture, and its mode of
communication, it demonstrates most powerfully that everything in this matter
comes from God and is accomplished by divine power.

It must nevertheless be observed that when this theology is called the
theology of revelation, it is so called preeminently (*kat' exochēn*, κατ'
ἐξοχήν) and in a narrower sense than was presented in Chapters VIII and IX.
There we called the theology of revelation that which God communicates here
with the human being by either natural or supernatural grace. Here, however, we
compare the revelation of supernatural grace with nature and the goods of
nature, establish their opposition to one another, and undertake to speak only
about supernatural revelation and the gracious mode of communication.

**Thesis XXI. Supernatural theology is wisdom concerning divine things which
proceeds from principles known in themselves, by the light of a higher
knowledge, above the mode of human reason.**

In these words we follow the path of the definition which we previously gave
of natural theology and define supernatural theology briefly. Everyone can
easily understand the genus and difference of this theology from what precedes.
Its genus is wisdom—not the archetypal wisdom of which we spoke at the
beginning, for that is the universal principle and therefore cannot be enclosed
in any genus, but wisdom so called equivocally, flowing from that universal
principle through Christ Jesus, in whom all the treasures of wisdom and
understanding are hidden. Its difference is set forth by its causes in a fixed
order. The efficient cause of this theology is God, who infuses the light of
that higher knowledge.

[Printed page 72; PDF page 102]

Its material cause is the principles known in themselves in divine things.

Its formal cause, finally, is that by that higher light it proceeds from those
principles above the mode of human reason. We place God, who infuses the light
of that higher knowledge or wisdom into these principles, here as the efficient
cause. For, as we said before, in this communication of grace the one Spirit is
the only one who works all things in all as he wills and communicates the light
of that higher and heavenly knowledge by the illumination of his power.

All sciences are so constituted that they either possess their light in
themselves from nature or borrow it from some other, higher science. This
science surpasses in every respect all the others which are known and which can
be known. We therefore saw fit to call it a higher science comparatively, to
show that this science is heavenly, superior to every science of this earth or
of the whole world—or, if you prefer, the highest of all those things which can
ever be known and apprehended by a human being. What is more, by this very name
we proclaim that this marvelous science is superior not only to other sciences
but even to ourselves, our nature, and the universe of things itself.

Thus, carried as far as possible away from familiarity with and knowledge of
these things, we pass beyond everything in created things and fly upward to
that inextinguishable light which is troubled by no changes or shadows—that is,
to God himself. From this light which God by particular grace willed to
communicate with us, it comes about that we comprehend the material of
supernatural theology: **divine things**, that is, God himself and everything
that exists insofar as it is ordered to God. We apprehend them, I say, as the
most just and holy subject of this wisdom, and we possess inwardly those
heavenly principles known in themselves by which God has illuminated us here,
as instruments necessary for comprehending divine things.

From this follows the form of this theology and divine wisdom, situated in the
act of our cognition. We establish that this supernatural theology proceeds in
the cognition of divine things, from principles divinely implanted, above the
mode of human reason and indeed of every created intelligence. For if the light
of a higher knowledge is from God, if the principles of that light are from
God, and if the light's progress is from God, then the progress or procession
of our apprehension of divine things above the grasp of our reason must also be
credited to him.

**Thesis XXII. There is a twofold account of this supernatural theology: it is
either absolute and in itself, according to the mode of the one communicating;
or relative, according to the measure of those whom the communication reaches.**

What we say stands as follows. Whatever science or wisdom comes into question
is considered either as it exists simply by nature and in its own genus, or as
it exists not by nature and in its genus but in a certain concrete mode,
compositely [*Opera*, fol. 1398]. That which exists by nature and is in a genus
is called abstract; that which is otherwise is called concrete. For example,
justice is said to exist by nature, but a just person, in whom justice adheres
to a subject, does not exist by nature, because he is not properly and in
himself one thing. Everything that exists, however, is one by the very fact
that it exists.

That which exists by nature and in a genus is more excellent than that which
is concrete. For whatever consists in its pure and absolute nature and has
nothing mixed or concrete, disparate from and unlike itself, exists by nature
and in a genus. Justice, for example, is always one and the same, mutable in no
person, time, or place. A just person has a mutable nature and undergoes change,
from which justice is free.

When we are to speak of theology in this way, we must take care that the form
of theology according to its nature and genus, as it is most complete and most
incorrupt,[^p72-incorrupt] is not deformed and adulterated by admixture with
some concrete thing, or rendered, so to speak, a debased alloy
(*heterochalkos*, ἑτερόχαλκος). For if you confuse what belongs to the subject,
or what is according to its subject, with that most holy and undefiled mother
and guide of our salvation, if it is permissible to call it that, you will not
rightly

[Printed page 73; PDF page 103]

attain either of these two things: the dignity of theology or the truth of the
subject which this theology generates and with which it is communicated by God.

We must therefore establish an altogether twofold account of theology, since
it is observed in one way in itself according to its nature and essence, and in
another way in that frail and weak subject of human beings, who are called
theologians from their perception and comprehension of it. We delimit the
former mode of theology both by its names and by a fixed determination, saying
that this theology is absolute and theology in itself, according to the mode of
the one communicating it.

When we call it absolute, we show that our purpose is to deal with theology
considered absolutely and simply, apart from admixture with another thing,
whether its subject or other things that come to it from any source. Therefore
this theology is also customarily called theology in itself, that is, according
to its natural condition and proper essence, as they call it, so that nothing
alien is in it and nothing of its own is absent from it, insofar as it is
formed by God. Its determination was given by us in those few words,
**“according to the mode of the one communicating.”**

For the mode of things that proceed from another power and efficacy comes from
their maker. Theology, however—the most worthy subject among created things
and above all created things—cannot have been brought to us by chance or at
random. Those who infer its mode from its maker therefore act altogether
rightly and wisely. Things which an external power effects have a twofold mode.
One is commensurate with the power of the efficient cause, as when a human
being is begotten by a human being. The other is inferior and by no means
commensurate, as happens in very many things. The works of artisans are not
commensurate with their artisans, nor are external works commensurate with the
internal works formed in the mind by an artisan's will and counsel.

God is the author and maker of our theology and communicates it graciously with
the human race according to his own mode. Yet this is not according to his
commensurate mode, that is, according to the mode of his essence, which is
infinite, but according to a noncommensurate mode, that is, the mode of his will
which tempers his revelations to suit our condition. The commensurate wisdom is
what we called archetypal or prototypal theology. The theology of union in
Christ Jesus comes next to it, then the theology of vision in the saints in
heaven, and finally this theology of ours occupies the third place. If it is in
no way to be compared with the theology of the saints in heaven, it would
certainly be much more inequitable to compare it with the theology of union,
and most inequitable to set it against that archetypal and essential theology.

The mode of this absolute theology is therefore given by the one communicating
it, according to the mode of his wisdom and goodness, by his fixed will and
counsel. By this counsel he tempered his revelations to our condition and
freely accommodated himself to the condition of human beings and the salvation
of his church.

This is that absolute theology, perfectly comprehending the whole perfect
account which it pleased God to reveal in common for the praise of his glorious
grace in Christ Jesus. From this plainly divine and perfect theology, set
before the human community by God's grace, flowed that other account of
theology which we call relative. Yet when we say [*Opera*, fol. 1399] that this
divine theology is modified, it must not be thought that we wish for any reason
to detract from its truth. We venerate that theology and worship it most
reverently, and we would sooner lament our own weakness and the narrowness of
our mind than detract the smallest amount from that most holy and perfect
theology.

But because, as we said above, all virtues are by nature far more excellent in
themselves than in any subject of theirs—just as justice in itself is one thing
and the justice of a human being with the stated restriction is another—we
call the theology that is in a human being and according to a human being
relative; its simple form is what we called absolute theology. Thus absolute
theology is like a most pure form, while relative theology is like a form
superadded to

[Printed page 74; PDF page 104]

matter and more narrowly determined and rendered finite according to the
condition of its matter. It is clear that the narrower and less worthy that
matter is, the narrower the form that comes to it is made.

Since the subject of this theology is the human being, who is called a
theologian from comprehending it, and since this subject is surrounded to the
greatest degree by vanity and weakness, it is extremely important for both
divine truth and our instruction that relative theology be distinguished with
the greatest precision from absolute theology. Absolute theology is a certain
pure conception offered by God; relative theology is a conception apprehended
by a human being but polluted by his condition and rendered imperfect.

Those therefore appear all the more unjust toward God's church who in this age
know no theology other than that which is according to and from human beings,
and who depend upon human judgment and authority in this matter just as though
that pure conception and divine form of theology which we call absolute had
either vanished altogether or come into human hands and power under the law of
slavery. That form and that conception are permanent, not mutable according to
the will of human beings. All human beings must believe it; it must not yield
to ignorant and weak human beings.

The determination, or circumscription as they call it, of the theology which we
name relative is expressed by our words **“according to the measure of those
whom the communication reaches.”** The mode of the giver is one thing and the
mode of the receiver another. God is the one who gives and supplies theology;
we receive what God supplies. The giver most wisely tempered his gift—that is,
our absolute theology—so as most graciously to pour out and set before humanity
in common whatever is necessary for his church in common. But those who receive
it fall exceedingly far short of receiving what God confers in common. Each
person has his own measure, and the whole church has its measure in every age;
in view of the ignorance, weakness, and meagerness of all, it is most imperfect.

The contempt against God of those who think that the fullness of this
incredible treasure can reside in such weak and unfit vessels in this wretched
and mortal life is therefore extraordinary and intolerable, and their injury
against his church even more atrocious. This is sufficient concerning
supernatural theology in general. With God as author, let us now pass to its
definition and particular exposition.

## Chapter XII. What Theology Called Absolute Is: Thesis XXIII

**Our theology, called absolute, is wisdom concerning divine things, inspired
by God according to divine truth, entrusted through declarative speech in
Christ to his servants, and recorded in the Old and New Testaments through the
prophets, apostles, and evangelists, insofar as it is expedient for it to be
revealed to us here for his glory and the good of the elect.**

Although some definition of our theology called absolute could be derived from
what precedes, it seemed necessary to employ one here that is somewhat fuller
and more precise, so that there may be a more convenient place to speak about
the individual things pertaining to its essence and true nature. Just as a
proper definition must contain the essence of the thing under discussion, so
too each of the seeds, as it were, of the thing defined must be sought from a
proper definition [*Opera*, fol. 1400], so that the loci of the definition may
be explained one by one. Before we undertake to speak about the parts of this
definition, for the sake of order

[Printed page 75; PDF page 105]

we must explain the boundaries, as it were, by which this theology of ours
called absolute is separated from that other which we said is called relative.
Once this rationale and path are established, we may then consider each in its
order as the nature of this one and that one requires. For brevity's sake, we
will therefore give two boundaries for each theology, from which we may come to
know their nature at least to some degree.

The theology called absolute, which God offers to the human race as a most pure
and incorrupt gift and hands down to his church, properly has two boundaries.
One concerns the subject which our theology treats; the other concerns the mode
belonging to this theology and divine wisdom according to the capacity of our
nature and the rationale of our salvation. After explaining these two, we will
come to establish the boundaries of the theology called relative as well.

The one subject concerning which human beings are instructed by the teachings
of this theology of ours is most vast and indeed infinite: God. He is the
universal principle, exemplar, and end of all the things that have existed, now
exist, or will ever exist. He is infinite in himself and testifies to his
infinity in all things by the most ample proofs of his essence, power, and both
common and particular operations. No creature—not even a heavenly one—can in
actual fact perceive these perfectly, express them in speech, or encompass them
in understanding.

No rationale for comprehending that infinite essence and majesty, or for
defining it, as it were, within the narrow bounds of mind, speech, and this
nature, can be discovered or devised by created things. Consequently, no
knowledge or comprehension of our God as infinite would ever have existed in
created things unless God Most Good and Most Great, though infinite, had
voluntarily bounded himself in some fashion so that knowledge of him and a
certain wisdom concerning him might reach created things.

God provided first that Christ our Savior, according to his human nature,
would comprehend him in the grace of union to the degree that created nature,
raised to the highest and incomparable summit of wisdom, can grasp him through
that particular theology which we therefore called the theology of union. By
the same providence he further brought it about that angels and spiritual
intelligences, being present with him, would perceive the present God by a
certain vision insofar as those heavenly essences could enjoy the glorious
vision of the present God. From this it was likewise called the theology of
vision.

Finally, by the same providence, but through the most ample and never
sufficiently praised mercy, that wisest Father of mercies provided and
accomplished that a particular mirror and a particular form communicated by God
would stand before the eyes of the human race. Through the theology of
revelation, which we also call ours, he presented himself—though infinite
through all things—to the lower creatures as an object to be viewed, known,
understood, and wisely apprehended, so to speak, in a finite mode.

Since the mode of that subject insofar as it is infinite cannot be grasped by
anyone, we must therefore consider the mode of the theology, that is, of that
wisdom by which the infinite one willed to exist as though defined. Reason
itself teaches and our nature requires that the mode of this wisdom which we
call our theology or the theology of revelation is by no means commensurate
with its subject and can never become so in actual fact. For what is more
reasonable than to understand that there is one infinite and that nothing can
be compared or made commensurate with the one who is infinite? Otherwise the
nature of each would have to perish from that collision, as it were, between
them, and the infinite would cease to be infinite because each would be bounded
by the other.

Whoever does not know that our nature is not so constituted as to comprehend
the infinite God or even things nearest to the infinite knows neither the
common nature nor himself. This is especially so if we consider that God willed
his wisdom and goodness to be communicated with the human race. This could
never have been accomplished unless, for the measure of our nature and
condition and that of his church,

[Printed page 76; PDF page 106]

he had provided a mode tempered by that same wisdom and goodness.

Certainly no human being will fail to establish that the mode communicated by
his wisdom and goodness had to be offered to us for our benefit as far inferior
to and by no means commensurate with his infinity. This is also the reason why
he established the communication of his wis[*Opera*, fol. 1401]dom in its
content and mode so as to be most suitable to the capacity of human beings, his
elect, and his church.

In its content he declared everything that needed to be known, but most wisely
passed over everything which he saw was either unnecessary or could not be
usefully communicated with us. In its mode he made his illustrious grace
effective when he clothed divine, spiritual, and heavenly things, so to speak,
in a human, bodily, and earthly mode, lest any of those things which it
concerned us to know should escape us.

Above all, one thing is distinctive in the mode of this divine communication:
he conformed that discipline of saving grace to the order of the natural
sciences and disciplines and accommodated the doctrine of grace to our nature.
For the perception of any human discipline requires principles, reasoning
which arises from those principles as an intervening means, and conclusions
and determinations belonging to proper knowledge of the disciplines among
human beings. In the same way, when that heavenly discipline was to be
communicated with the human race, God Most Good and Most Great provided
principles established from the higher light of that grace as from a heavenly
torch. From them he imbued our minds with divine reasoning and opened a way to
conclusions and determinations fitting heavenly knowledge, which according to
the condition of his elect church savingly sketch that heavenly wisdom on
earth.

It is indeed true that the perfection of the heavenly things which this wisdom
teaches is infinite, and the perfection of words—whatever those words may
be—could not attain the perfection of the things. Nevertheless, God most
graciously and wisely brought it about that nothing perfect which we needed to
know was left unexpressed and that no imperfection inhered in the truth of that
theology communicated by God's grace. By the communication of this theology,
God displayed the perfection of the things which he prepared for those who
fear him, which was itself sufficient. He provided most wisely that every
necessary head of the most perfect subject would be possessed through the most
perfect communication of divine theology. The subject, then, is most perfect
but with infinite perfection; the wisdom is most perfect but with finite
perfection; finally, the mode of wisdom is most perfect but with a perfection
analogous to the truth of that wisdom which we call our absolute theology.

Those very things by which we demonstrated that absolute theology is bounded
are in fact shared, according to their rationale, with that other genus of
theology which we call relative. Its subject is common, and the mode of that
wisdom and knowledge is the same in substance. For there cannot be more than
one theology according to its essence and the truth of the thing in itself.
But because that other theology which we call relative has something mixed and
joined with it—something, if we wish to speak properly, materialized as it were
in its subject—two corresponding boundaries were therefore added to the
account of that theology called relative. Without them relative theology can
never be conveniently handed down or savingly perceived.

The theology called absolute is truly an essential form belonging to God's
church by divine communication, while relative theology is like a material
thing to which that form cannot adhere without observance of these boundaries.
The first boundary is the subject in which this theology resides by divine
communication; the second is the mode by whose divine adhesion the theology
called absolute is joined with this subject, though the subject is in itself
most unfit. The subject, not to linger long, is the human being: a living
creature adorned by God's benefit with the most ample gifts of nature, but with
those gifts

[Printed page 77; PDF page 107]

corrupted by his depravity. Yet even in the state of his integrity those gifts
would never have raised the human being to the summit of salvation and heavenly
glory unless God's particular and supernatural grace had acted upon them.

This subject therefore possessed according to its nature its own instruments
suited to perceiving disciplines, but instruments which could never rise above
natural things and always remained within them. There were principles partly
perceived outwardly by sense and partly adhering to and inborn within our
intellect. There was reasoning which collected, compared, and established
thoughts from those principles. There were conclusions and determinations
drawn from the principles by sure reasoning. Finally, there was knowledge, but
it was natural, earthly, and carnal, and was constituted by the most imperfect
natural [*Opera*, fol. 1402] modes so as to rise to nothing supernatural or
perfect and not even to be affected by any sense of supernatural and perfect
things. For, as the apostle says, the natural human being is not capable of the
things of the Spirit of God; they are foolishness to him, and he cannot know
them, 1 Corinthians 2:14.

What relation, then, does this boundary, so very far removed from divine
things, have with that most holy theology called absolute? Or what relation
does that most chaste theology have with a most impure and unfit human being,
unless that most good and heavenly Father who supplied the mode of communicating
the theology called absolute also most graciously prepared for us the mode of
applying relative theology?

This is the other boundary necessary for perceiving relative theology which we
established above: namely, the mode of usefully applying that knowledge to the
weak and wretched subject of which we spoke briefly just now. Establishing the
mode of applying that most perfect knowledge to this most imperfect subject,
God in his infinite mercy provided human beings the divine light of heavenly
grace. By the power of the Spirit, through this light he accomplishes all
things in all which are necessary for this saving application and provision.

Consequently, God so occupied by the power of his Spirit whatever is begotten
in human nature for the perception of things that he joined gifts of grace
corresponding to each good of nature. To the principles placed in the intellect
by nature, he attached the principles of grace. Upon reason as it rises from
the principles, he built increments of his divine light. To the conclusions
and determinations which reason attained by nature's most imperfect light, he
added supernatural and most perfect demonstrations, from which heavenly
knowledge would be constituted in the minds of the godly. Finally, he both
placed heavenly objects before our mind and will to be perceived in a heavenly
mode, and roused that same will to apprehend those objects.

These, then, are those two modes which God's wisdom judged must be particularly
employed in addition to the things we explained concerning theology called
absolute, so that this other theology which we call relative could reside
conveniently among human beings and be usefully perceived by them. Otherwise,
so alien a subject as the human being could not have become capable of that
heavenly wisdom, nor could heavenly wisdom have ever reached such unfit human
beings by even the slightest trace.

Having established the things which pertain to distinguishing the account of
this theology from the other, we must now speak in order about each according
to our purpose, insofar as the matters seem relevant to the present subject. At
the beginning of the chapter we stated the definition of our theology called
absolute, setting forth its genus as in what precedes and its difference by its
causes. The genus is **wisdom**, and truly wisdom—not wisdom so called
abusively (*katachrēstikōs*, καταχρηστικῶς) or according to human opinion, as
very often happens. The individual loci of the causes which pertain to the
difference of theology are surveyed in their common order. We first declared
the efficient cause and everything pertaining to it, saying that this theology
is **inspired by God and entrusted through declarative speech in Christ to his
servants, and recorded in the**

[Printed page 78; PDF page 108]

**Old and New Testaments through the prophets, apostles, and evangelists**. We
encompassed its material cause under the designation “divine things,” its form
as truth, and finally its end as **insofar as it is expedient for it to be
revealed to us here for his glory and the good of the elect**.

Let us therefore also conclude this locus briefly. The genus and common
difference of this theology which concerns divine things is, as before,
wisdom. Its particular difference is explained in what follows through its
causes. Come, then, let us consider them in the same order, omit the genus and
common difference, and undertake the explanation of its causes.

## Chapter XIII. The Material Cause of Our Theology

Although in the definition we placed all the causes needed for a proper
definition of our theology called absolute in the fixed and customary order of
nature, for convenience in teaching we decided to speak first about the
internal and essential causes of theology and only afterward about those joined
with it from outside or externally [*Opera*, fol. 1403]. We call internal causes
those which constitute the essence of the thing under discussion, such as
matter and form in all things. External causes are whatever adhere to the thing
constituted as something external, whether for its generation, as the efficient
cause, or for its operation and effect, as the end. The rationale of a
discipline requires this order more; nature observes the other. We will
therefore undertake the internal causes first and defer the external causes
until later.

To consider first the material of sacred theology, we follow the footsteps of
Scripture and of the nature of theology itself and explain its material as
follows. **Thesis XXIV. The material of theology is divine things: namely, God
and whatever is ordered to God, insofar as the doctrine of God concerning his
nature, works, and right had to be handed down.**

Whether we call it material, subject, or object, as these names can be
predicated of theology's subject matter from different perspectives, we define
it by the designation “divine things.” By divine things here we mean things
which either are truly divine or, if they belong to the nature of things, are
nevertheless ordered to God in some way. Wishing to express this point, some of
the orthodox fathers defined the material of theology by declaring that it
contains everything which either is **God** in the direct case, as the
grammarians say, or which can be said to be **of God, to God, toward God, or
from God** in an oblique case—finally, whatever in every grammatical case has
its fixed relation and ordering to God.

Properly and absolutely, God is the subject or material of theology, just as
every science or discipline necessarily has some one fixed and defined subject
or material. In each science, the subject stands in the same relation to the
science as the object stands to a natural power or to our habit. It is agreed
that the proper object assigned to a human power or habit is that under whose
rationale everything is referred to that power or habit. For example,
articulate speech and a crash are referred to hearing insofar as they are
sonorous, because sound, or a sonorous thing insofar as it is sonorous, is the
proper object of our hearing. Similarly, a human being and an animal are
referred to sight insofar as they are colored, because color, or a colored
thing, is the object of our sight. Yet everything in sacred theology is treated
under the rationale of God as the proper material treated in

[Printed page 79; PDF page 109]

theology, because either God himself is under discussion or things are ordered
to God as their universal principle and end.

The holy apostles, or apostolic men near their times, also observed this very
rationale in the holy creed. They established God as the subject of the
principles in which all knowledge is contained potentially, and after the
articles concerning God they placed fixed heads concerning the church and
God's benefits toward it. The principles placed first thus showed what is the
subject in itself of our theology, while the latter articles maintained the
things which relate to and are affected toward the subject in a particular way.

It follows from what we have just said that a twofold order of the things
called divine must be established: one concerns God, whom we must enjoy; the
other concerns the things of God, which we must use according to God, as
Augustine explained fully in Book I of *On Christian Doctrine*. Two things
belong simply to the locus concerning God: the account of his nature and the
account of his will. His nature is considered either according to the essence
subsisting in three persons or according to the distinction of the persons in
the unity of essence. His will is considered first as that which is in some way
borne universally toward all created things, then as that which is common and
set forth commonly in the human race, and finally as that which is displayed
by the particular rationale of grace in his church.

Three things explained in the sacred Scriptures belong broadly to the other
locus concerning things ordered to God: the nature of things insofar as it is
ordered to God, God's works in them, and the right assigned to them by God. We
say that the discipline of sacred theology explains the nature of things, not
simply insofar as they exist—for the task of scrupulously investigating,
examining, and teaching this belongs to natural philosophers, physicians, and
philosophers, who immerse themselves without measure or end in investigating
and examining the works of nature—but only insofar as the things have their
relation and reference to God by the necessity of nature, and God has his
relation to them by the freedom of his will [*Opera*, fol. 1404].

Theology does not teach the nature of angels as the metaphysicians do, or the
nature of animate or elemental things as the natural philosophers do, or the
method **by abstraction** (*tēn ex aphaireseōs*, τὴν ἐξ ἀφαιρέσεως) as the
mathematicians do. Rather, theologians hand down the nature and condition of
each thing belonging to this discipline only insofar as the things are referred
and ordered by their disposition toward God.

The judgment is the same concerning works. With the torch of divine theology
shining before them, God's works in all created things are observed so that the
things which God accomplishes in them do not come into consideration simply
and universally, but only insofar as, according to the ordering we have just
described, either God acts in them or they act according to God. God acts most
freely by his most free will: universally, both ordinarily according to nature
and extraordinarily according to his providence; commonly, according to the
common calling of saving grace set before everyone; and finally particularly,
according to the particular calling of saving grace implanted in his elect by
the Spirit of sanctification unto eternal life.

Conversely, the created things act in service of God's ordering: universally,
obeying the instinct of universal nature and the common law of his nature and
providence; commonly, regarding that common calling set before everyone in a
common mode; and finally particularly, pursuing with every fitting honor and
obedience his manifold and marvelous wisdom, which is most effectual for their
salvation.

But because all praise of virtue consists in action, as Cicero said, and the
only true praise is that which proceeds not from human beings with false
judgment and unstable zeal but from God himself, the clearest locus of the
material upon which sacred theology customarily insists is that which Paul
several times called **the righteous ordinance of God** (*to dikaiōma tou
theou*, τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ θεοῦ), Romans 1:32. A fixed right of nature has been
imposed by God, and there is a fixed right of grace. This right is asserted
both here with the principles of nature and from above by the principles of
grace communicated by

[Printed page 80; PDF page 110]

God.

There is a twofold rectitude, or **right action** (*katorthōma*, κατόρθωμα), and
duty established by God for the human race. One is common, shared in good part
with the other things in this universe: namely, the right of nature which we
are taught by its instinct. The other is particular to the human race, since it
alone was created in God's image: the right of conduct, or what is called moral
right. By the former right, things are judged good and evil according to
nature; by the latter, good and evil according to conduct, that is, virtues and
vices. The former proceed from the common principle which they call the
instinct of nature or the natural will; the latter from the particular and
internal principle which they call the mind, that is, the source of reason and
will.

Sacred theology most perfectly explains this moral right by which human beings
must approach God and be conformed as fully as possible to his holy will. It
digs out again, as it were, the natural principles buried in us by our
depravity and endows them with perfection. It sets supernatural glory before
our eyes with such light, faith, and power that all other disciplines are as
nothing beside this wisdom. This, therefore, is the most certain material with
which our theology, under God as author and guide, is occupied. If we occupy
ourselves with it, it will truly come about that, beholding the Lord's glory
with unveiled face as in a mirror, we shall be transformed into the same image
as by the Lord, the Spirit, 2 Corinthians 3:18.

## Chapter XIV. The Formal Cause of Our Theology

Now that we agree about the material of our theology, we must come to examine
its other internal cause so that we may hold its essential constitution with
certainty. This cause is form, without which all matter is, as it were, infinite
and incomprehensible (*akatalēptos*, ἀκατάληπτος). When form is added to matter,
the nature of a thing is both constituted in itself and perceived by our
intellect. We therefore delimit the form of theology called absolute in a few
words as follows. **Thesis XXV. The form of theology is divine truth, which is
considered in theology in two ways: either the whole, or some part of the
whole, is considered simply in itself; or different parts are considered with
one another by a proper comparison.**

In these words we briefly explain two things [*Opera*, fol. 1405] which are
exceedingly necessary for our purpose. The first is the account of this form in
itself; the second, the account by which it is to be set forth and perceived by
us according to that saving discipline which God willed to bestow upon human
beings by grace. We define the account of this essential form by saying that
the form is divine truth; we explain the second locus in the words which
follow.

No godly person will doubt that truth is the essential form of theology called
absolute if he believes Christ, our supreme prophet and teacher, who says,
“Sanctify them by your truth; your word is truth,” John 17:17. But because the
name “truth,” though indiscriminately spoken among the people, is ordinarily
carried about on everyone's lips more than it is understood in the mind, we
will now explain it briefly. The ancients most fittingly declared that truth is
convertible and coextensive with Being, as they call it, that is, with that
which exists. Whatever exists is true by the very fact that it exists; and,
conversely, whatever is true exists by the very fact that it is true. These
things always cohere, reciprocate, and pass into one another so necessarily
that nothing at all in the nature of things or above nature

[Printed page 81; PDF page 111]

can be offered which is not held by both of these common laws.

Although these two laws are perpetual in all things and must perpetually be
applied to everything, each is nevertheless spoken in a different respect. If
we say that something exists, we contemplate it in the thing; but if we
predicate that it is true, we contemplate that which exists in our intellect.
There is therefore the truth both of things which exist and of the signs by
which the things that exist are said to exist. Or, to speak more briefly and
perhaps more clearly, there is the truth of things and the truth of a
proposition stated about things.

There is a twofold account of the things which exist: some are in reality and
others in the intellect. In reality are **particulars** (*ta kath' hekasta*, τὰ
καθ' ἕκαστα), that is, particular and individual things as perceived by sense.
In the intellect are forms, both of the things which we have individually
perceived by sense and of all other things which we perceive in any other way.
Consequently, the truth of things commonly called intelligible, or things which
fall within our intellect, is far worthier and broader than the truth of those
things perceived outwardly in reality. It is broader because it embraces both
particular and common things within itself. It is worthier because common
things are worthier than particulars, and forms abstracted and purified from
matter are worthier than material things. The generation and constitution of
material things, composed from mutually different principles, plainly proclaim
that truth does not reside in those things properly and in itself.

Although the truth which can be apprehended by the human intellect stands in
this way, the truth which we call divine raises itself exceedingly far above
the truth which nature teaches everyone. By this particular designation we
vindicate theology's formal truth from every change and mutability of nature
and raise it above the nature of this universe and of everything in the
universe. Divine truth is not merely truth which has its origin of truth from
God, for the truth of nature and of the natural intellect also arose from God.
Rather, we call that truth divine by its own proper and particular right which
is divine in actual fact and by its own rationale, in every mode in itself and
all its parts, and in each of its orderings.

Divine truth stands in two modes: infinitely, insofar as it is essential in
God, and finitely, insofar as God has communicated it with created things. We
must observe both most carefully in our theology called absolute. In it we
recognize infinite truth as the immutable archetype and first form of truth,
and most reverently worship communicated truth as a mirror and expressed image
of that archetypal truth. Therefore, in the definition of this theology we did
not simply say that theology is wisdom concerning divine things or wisdom of
divine truth, but expressed both conjointly: **this wisdom concerning divine
things is inspired by God according to divine truth**, and so forth. In this
way we expressed a necessary relation intervening between truth which expresses
itself and truth which is expressed.

Truth which expresses itself is that immutable conception and form in God
which we reverently adore. Expressed truth is nothing other than a certain image
and mirror of that first and eternal truth: what kind, what extent, and finally
what mode God communi[*Opera*, fol. 1406]cated with us to testify to the praise
of his glorious grace and to set forth our salvation. This truth according to
that truth is a perfect stream from a perfect fountain and a perfect ray from
the perfect sun of truth. Finally, it is a perfect image expressed in the
mirror of the word of truth, an image which will shine until we all arrive at
that principle of truth in heaven.

What we said concerning the account of this truth—that it is considered in two
ways in our theology—is manifest in itself. We are so constituted by our nature
that wherever our mind and thought turns, we either contemplate simply and
universally the object of our intellect, that is, truth, in some whole, or
institute a comparison of this whole with another. Thus, for

[Printed page 82; PDF page 112]

example, someone may consider the universal church absolutely or compare it
with the rest of the world; or someone may wish to weigh separately in itself
all the divine truth expressed in this theology of ours, or compare it with the
truth of our nature for its instruction and enlargement by divine grace.

Again, the parts of the very thing whose truth is considered as a whole are set
forth to us in the same way and examined by us once set forth, as though each
part of the whole constituted some whole in itself. Thus, someone might
undertake to consider the church of the Corinthians, the Philippians, or any
other church particularly, as they say. When the parts considered by our
intellect are compared and examined among themselves according to the rationale
of one whole or even of different wholes, the same theology therefore shows
the truth and proper rationale of these things which are to be compared with
one another, lest anyone depart from divine truth, which alone is saving and
sure. For example, someone may compare the benefit of creation with providence
in the nature of things, or natural benefits with benefits proceeding from
grace, so as to investigate and attain the truth more and more.

This truth divinely expressed in our theology has certain attributes which God
properly sealed in it so as to vindicate it most powerfully from association
with the rest of the truth observed in the nature of things. Although very many
attributes of this kind can be adduced, we have embraced certain chief ones and
the highest heads in one or two theses. From these, godly and prudent souls will
very easily attain other things of the kind, if there are any, from God's
teaching. We therefore establish the following briefly: **Thesis XXVI. This
truth is holy, just, and perfect, since it teaches nothing profane, unjust, or
imperfect and leaves untaught nothing holy, just, or perfect, so that we may be
directed most perfectly toward holiness in ourselves, justice toward everyone,
and perfection through all things.**

In these few words we saw fit to set forth some essential properties of divine
truth before speaking about the powers which follow from that same truth, as we
will do in the next thesis. We predicate three attributes of this truth: it is
**holy, just, and perfect**. If we wish properly to examine the proper
constitution of any doctrine, three things must altogether be observed: first,
that it is good in itself; second, that it is good toward others; and third,
that it is good throughout. If any of these is lacking in a doctrine, its
constitution cannot rightly be pronounced proper. With these functions in
view, we claimed the same number of attributes for divine truth when we called
it holy in itself, just toward everyone, and perfect throughout.

What, I ask, could be affirmed as holy, just, and perfect in any human or
created things in comparison with that truth which is from God and according
to God? If you consider archetypal truth, it is the perennial fountain of
holiness and perfection. If you consider that truth communicated by God and
set before the human race, it is the most holy, just, and perfect cistern of
that fountain, to speak with the artisans, or the receptacle and head of saving
water. From it we all draw the surest doctrine of holiness, justice, and
perfection to which we reverently conform ourselves while awaiting the reward
of our faith, that is, the salvation of our souls, 1 Peter 1:9.

We show and comprehend the mode of these attributes which we most rightly
ascribe to divine truth in two ways: in the substance and in the effect of that
truth. To demonstrate the substance of divine truth according to these
attributes among human beings, two things are needed [*Opera*, fol. 1407].
First, whatever is opposed to these attributes must be removed entirely;
second, whatever belongs to the rationale of these attributes must be affirmed
fully of divine truth. We expressed the former in the words **divine truth
teaches nothing profane, unjust, or imperfect**, and the latter in the words
**nothing holy, just, or perfect which is teachable and learnable** (*didakton
kai mathēton*, διδακτὸν καὶ μαθητόν), that is, which can be taught, learned,
and known,

[Printed page 83; PDF page 113]

**is left untaught**.

These powers so inhere in the doctrine of truth that nothing at all in created
things or among the things which they can perceive by intellect can be compared
with this truth. If you look to the holiness of any doctrine in itself, what
holiness can proceed from profane things? If to its justice, what justice from
unjust things? If to its perfection, what perfection can proceed from most
imperfect things? As we read in the book of Job, “Who can bring a clean thing
out of an unclean? Not even one,” Job 14:4.

The proper nature of individual causes is known from their quality, and their
power from their effects. Nor can causes ever be surpassed by their effects
unless other higher causes are added above them, whose effect is then
attributed to the lower causes only improperly. Since every human doctrine is
from a profane, unjust, and imperfect human being, we rightly judge that every
other doctrine lacks these attributes. This doctrine alone, which has the most
holy, just, and perfect God as its author, is adorned with these praises. All
other doctrines, although they are from God, are nevertheless from instruments
stained with some blemish of profanity, injustice, and imperfection. Indeed,
they teach nothing so holy, just, or perfect that some defect does not always
adhere to it from its author, the nature of the doctrine, its rationale, or its
mode.

This doctrine of truth, however, has holiness from God and teaches it in God in
such a way that, separating it from all created things and even from the human
being himself, it shows that holiness is obtained in God alone. No doctrine
handed down by human beings so teaches the justice by which each is given his
due—God according to piety and human beings according to nature and their
common and particular rights—without greatly diminishing God's right and our
duty toward him and, under whatever appearance this happens, turning aside
from the proper rationale of human right. Either something just which ought to
have been commanded is not commanded, or something unjust which ought to have
been guarded against is established. Every human doctrine consequently always
abounds and overflows with its imperfection. Neither in the rationale of
holiness, justice, nor all the things needed for the perfection of holiness,
justice, or the mutual order and fitness of all things does it display the
perfection which God, nature, and order require.

Nothing of this kind can exist in this doctrine. It is from a holy, just, and
perfect author; it is just, holy, and perfect according to its author; and it
always exists in him as its exemplar, who is himself holiness, justice, and
perfection. Consequently, no evil inheres in it and absolutely no good fails to
inhere in it. God's eternal holiness, justice, and perfection ceaselessly
represent this doctrine among the human race as a mirror of themselves. Let us
remain fixed upon it with our eyes most attentively and reverently.

From the contemplation of this divine and saving mirror, effects corresponding
to those attributes follow in the human beings before whose eyes it is placed.
Since this doctrine of truth is holy, just, and perfect, a good cause must
produce good fruits, and it produces them only in the one in whom it is planted
and takes root by God's will. This doctrine was brought from heaven to human
beings, who are called God's field, God's building, and God's cultivation, 1
Corinthians 3:9.

We therefore explained these effects by saying that this doctrine was given
and formed by God **so that we may be directed most perfectly toward holiness
in ourselves, justice toward everyone, and perfection throughout all things**.
It is like a torch placed by God before our eyes so that, with it shining
before us, we may walk ceaselessly in the way of his truth. For what could be
so necessary to a human being as to know God, if not as he is, at least as he
can be known? What could be so useful as to be conformed to the God whom the
human being knows, according as he knows him—or rather, according as God
presents himself to be known? What could be so glorious as to strive by this
saving conformity toward God as the perennial fountain of holiness [*Opera*,
fol. 1408], justice, and perfection

[Printed page 84; PDF page 114]

for him?

For this purpose, three things are briefly embraced in the declaration of these
effects: their genus, mode, and duration. The genus of effects is threefold:
holiness represented by God in us through the Spirit of sanctification; justice
toward everyone, giving God his due and each person his due; and the fitting
perfection of the subject and of the effects in the subject. At present we can
desire this perfection here and hope for it in the future, but we will not
fully attain it in actual fact until we depart to the Lord, the author of our
holiness, justice, and perfection. The mode is that by that most perfect torch
of divine truth we are most perfectly directed toward those effects and led by
the power of God, who works all things in all, 1 Corinthians 12:11. The
duration and perpetuity of these powers is demonstrated by our saying that
these attributes and effects are taught throughout all things by this theology
of ours, which alone is truly wisdom.

But we will speak about this locus in the next thesis, which the 1882 witness
misnumbers XXXII.[^p84-thesis] It reads: **Thus this theology is one, eternal,
and immutable. For that which is necessarily true is also necessarily one;
that which is just and holy can never fail to be just and holy; and finally,
that which is perfect according to God is altogether and always immutable.**

We attached this conclusion to the question concerning the form of our
theology because these three things necessarily fit its nature and condition.
First, this theology is unique; it does not have several forms. Second, it is
eternal in duration, not temporal as very often happens to every human cognition
and science. Third, it is immutable both in itself and in all its parts, even
its individual ones; it is not assailed by changes from any contingency.

As we said above, concrete things composed with some admixture vary in number,
time, and a thousand changes, but abstract things, or forms of things spoken in
the abstract, are raised exceedingly far above every change. A true person
changes, but truth never does; a theologian changes, but theology never does.
When a human being is called true, a theologian, just, holy, and so forth, as a
human being he possesses the rationale of mutable matter, but he possesses only
the accidental form of truth, theology, holiness, justice, and so forth. Those
pure forms from which true people, theologians, holy people, and just people
receive their names are incorrupt from every defect. Each is one thing in
itself; each is eternal and immutable.

The rationale of this affirmation must be sought from the nature of the form
which we ascribed to theology and the conditions joined to it. As we said
above, we attribute divine truth as the form of our theology and added three
necessary conditions of that truth: justice, holiness, and perfection. From
each of these are drawn arguments confirming the unity, eternity, and
immutability of this theology. From truth the following argument is drawn: that
which is necessarily true is necessarily one and belongs to eternal and
immutable truth; truth is necessarily true; therefore it is one, eternal, and
immutable. What is truer than truth? Or what is so necessarily true as truth?

To use Aristotle's words, we call necessary that which is always as it is and
cannot stand or happen otherwise, for the necessary is opposed to the
contingent (*tō endechomenō*, τῷ ἐνδεχομένῳ). We call true whatever exists
insofar as it is in our intellect, either actually understood or, so to speak,
intelligible. But here we deal not with the truth which can be perceived by the
human intellect, but with divine truth, which infinitely surpasses the measure
of our intellect. Divine things insofar as they are divine altogether possess
in themselves the necessity of divine truth. They therefore claim for
themselves all the more what is established concerning natural or created
truth: that whatever is necessarily true in this genus of divine and
supernatural truth is necessarily one, eternal, and immutable with the simplest
unity, eternity, and immutability, because it is divine. Just as this account of
our theology is

[Printed page 85; PDF page 115]

confirmed by the argument from divine truth, so too it is confirmed by justice
and holiness. That which is absolutely just and holy can never fail to be just
and holy. Justice and holiness are absolutely just and holy. It can therefore
never happen that they are not just and holy, and consequently one, eternal,
and immutable. But if any justice and holiness is true, only that can be called
true which is the justice and holiness [*Opera*, fol. 1409] of divine truth,
which is supernatural in itself and all its attributes, and which is the divine
form that God expressed as one, eternal, and immutable in this theology of ours.

Finally, since that condition of perfection of which we spoke above is also
necessary to all these things, I judge that the certain unity, eternity, and
immutability of that perfection—not human or formed by human means, but
divinely expressed in the truth of theology—is surely demonstrated by the
perfection which we assign to our theology. Whatever is perfect according to
God is altogether and always one, eternal, and immutable, especially since
this theology of ours is nothing other than a certain expressed delineation of
that one, eternal, and immutable perfection by which God is most perfect. God
is not only perfect but essential perfection itself, the cause and exemplar of
all perfection, and its most constant end.

It remains for us to conclude from what has just been said that this theology
is one, eternal, and immutable, and that it is the most perfect representation
of just, holy, and perfect truth, which God willed to communicate with the
human race according to the exemplar of his truth for his glory and the
salvation of our souls in Christ Jesus.

    1882 running text prints *Thesi XXXII*, which the Latin transcription
    preserves.

## Chapter XV. The Efficient Cause of Our Theology

Thus far we have spoken about what are commonly called the essential parts and
internal causes of our theology. We must now come to the causes customarily
called external or extrinsic. They are the efficient cause and the end, which
we will pursue in proper order as briefly as possible. **Thesis XXVIII. We
posit a twofold efficient cause of our theology: one principal and the other
instrumental.**

Whenever the efficient cause of anything is considered, we are especially
accustomed to observe whether the efficient cause effects by its own power or
not, whether it effects the whole or only a part, and finally whether it
effects the thing essentially or accidentally. We must observe these things in
all natural and human matters so that we may attain true knowledge of the
things which their causes effect. Here, however, we are dealing with theology,
that wisdom of divine truth which no creature—not even the universe itself—can
comprehend or attain. We must therefore observe this same mode with the
greatest care in examining our theology and see what effected it by its own
power, wholly or in part, essentially or accidentally, and what assistance
contributed to effecting it.

We call the wisdom now under discussion “our theology.” The first of these
words is the name of the genus; the second distinguishes the genus and
constitutes its species. The surest rationale for discovering its efficient
cause therefore appears to be one which can be ascertained from the very
rationale of the genus and species. The genus is theology, that is, wisdom or
an account of divine things. But divine

[Printed page 86; PDF page 116]

things in every way surpass our nature and that of this whole universe. Since
they cannot be comprehended by us or by nature and the entirety of nature, the
efficient cause of theology certainly must not be sought in a human being, any
created thing, or even this whole universe. Why seek it in a thing in which it
neither exists nor can ever inhere through the thing's own internal power or
faculty? The genus is supernatural and therefore cannot proceed from a natural
cause or even from nature itself. Indeed, it is supernatural to such a degree
that it can be produced only by God as its efficient cause and principle,
because God alone produces supernatural forms; one form does not produce
another. When we speak of theology according to its genus, therefore, it has a
single and particular efficient cause.

But because we seek the delimited species of theology and its efficient cause
insofar as we call it our theology, **we say that the principal cause of our
absolute theology is one and the other cause instrumental**. By a fixed
instrument, as it were, that theology which proceeded purely from God becomes
ours and, as it were, passes into the family of the human race [*Opera*, fol.
1410]. Thus we assert that theology has two efficient causes. The principal
cause formed theology by its power alone. The instrumental cause fitted and
accommodated that divinely formed wisdom to our capacity. We will therefore
deal with each in order.

**Thesis XXIX. The principal efficient and absolute cause of our theology is
God the Father inspiring it in the Son through his Spirit, for he is the sole
author and the supreme and most perfect maker of this wisdom in his servants.**

Whenever the causes of effected things are weighed in order—and an order must
be observed among several causes acting at the same time—the cause which is
principal must always be regarded principally, whatever the causes concurring
in one effect may be. In created things, however, it very often happens that
when several causes are coordinated with one another, each is partial, as they
say, and works its own part in the common effect. By contrast, causes that are
alone sufficient and efficient in themselves are called absolute. Because we
are to deal with divine things which differ in their whole genus and mode from
the effecting of created things, we joined two attributes when we called the
efficient cause of our theology both **principal and absolute**.

We call it principal or chief by comparison of one cause with another
pertaining to the same effect. We call it absolute because without any other
cause assisting, it accomplishes its effect by its power and faculty alone.
Although these two things cannot easily concur in created things, we join both
together in divine things. We believe that God as the absolute efficient cause
works in everything, including subordinate causes; and if he employs any
further causes, he always occupies the principal place among all the causes
which he uses most wisely and justly to accomplish his will.

If anything has required a marvelous and most powerful cause, it is certainly
theology, which human beings can possess not from nature but only from the
communication of grace, from which it was called our theology, that is, human
theology. If no one knows the things of a human being except the human spirit
within him, then certainly no human being would have known this wisdom, the
wisdom of the divine Spirit, unless revelation from elsewhere had come upon
human beings. We therefore establish the efficient cause of this theology as
God the Father in the Son through his Spirit: the one true and eternal God,
three persons in the unity of essence, working conjointly in this communication
of grace. For who reveals the things of God, even the deepest things, except
the Spirit of God? And in whom or from whom would that same Spirit reveal them
except in the Son our mediator and from the Father, who gave the Son as the one
prophet and teacher of his church?

[Printed page 87; PDF page 117]

The divine mode of this communication is inspiration. Although God uses many
modes of teaching toward created things, and all created things teach according
to God, a spiritual mode had to be observed in the communication of this
heavenly and spiritual grace, both because of the nature of the thing under
discussion and because of the nature of the one who communicates it. Since this
spiritual good proceeds from a spiritual author, only a spiritual communication
was fitting. Indeed, just as nature permits a bodily thing to be communicated
only in a bodily mode, so grace requires a spiritual thing to be communicated
through the mode of spirit.

Above all, the fact that God is the author of this communication and claims its
supreme and perfect effecting for himself alone as a work proper to his divine
glory shows that a spiritual communication, which we call inspiration, was
needed. Who but the one God who was its author could confer so great a spiritual
thing? Who but the author of nature himself, overflowing as vindicator through
grace, could set forth the highest of all things, which surpasses the nature of
the universe in its dignity? Who but the most perfect could ever teach a
perfect thing which is indeed the saving representation of all heavenly
perfection? He, therefore, is the author of our theology and heavenly wisdom.
He effected and begot it in his servants according to the measure of his gift,
so that individuals received their parts from God, the author of theology,
while God nevertheless set before everyone in common the perfect form of this
theology.

We call him the supreme author and maker of this wisdom when we contemplate him
employing intermediate causes for this communication of grace [*Opera*, fol.
1411]. We affirm that he is its sole and perfect author and maker because he is
the one who works all things in all, both in us, the elect, and in the
intermediate causes assumed for our calling. If he works all things, then he
works perfectly; otherwise we would have to say there is no perfection
anywhere if it is not in and from God, something piety forbids us to declare.

Teaching that even the slightest rudiments of this heavenly wisdom are to be
held in esteem and cherished and worshiped by everyone as divine, the apostle
wrote to the Corinthians in support of this teaching: “I make known to you that
no one speaking by the Spirit of God says Jesus is accursed, and no one can say
Jesus is Lord except by the Holy Spirit,” 1 Corinthians 12:3–4 and following.
Then, rising from those smallest principles to greater ones, or rather to all
things universally, he adds: “There are distinctions of gifts, but the same
Spirit; distinctions of ministries, but the same Lord; and distinctions of
operations, but the same God who effects all these things in all.” A little
later: “The one and same Spirit effects all these things, distributing them
privately to each as he wills.” Similarly, Christ had earlier said while
exulting in the Spirit, “All things have been handed to me by my Father, and no
one knows who the Son is except the Father, or who the Father is except the Son
and anyone to whom the Son wills to reveal him.” By this title he attributed
glory to God the Father, Matthew 11:27; Luke 10:21.

Let us now consider the other cause, which they call instrumental. **Thesis
XXX. The instrumental cause of this wisdom is the *logos prophorikos* (λόγος
προφορικός), or declarative speech of God: both spiritual speech perceived
spiritually and bodily speech perceived bodily.**

I am not unaware that artisans properly call an instrumental cause that which
the principal agent, as they call it, employs externally as an instrument to
produce the effect that will proceed from the action undertaken. Consequently,
declarative speech cannot properly be called an instrumental cause when God
employs it immediately. But because spiritual and bodily things have a certain
common analogy according to which spiritual things are also called
analogically by words that properly belong to bodily things, we fittingly refer
both spiritual and bodily speech to the genus of instrumental cause. For these
share a certain analogy

[Printed page 88; PDF page 118]

with one another,

according to which God uses speech with us spiritually and bodily, as it has
seemed good to him, for our instruction. “Instrumental cause” is fittingly
predicated of both as a certain analogical genus. Nor does the fact that bodily
speech employs outward instruments, by reason of which it seems to be called
instrumental, oppose our account. That same speech also employs its instruments
inwardly, from which it equally is and is called instrumental.

Of the instruments ordinarily employed for our actions, some are conjoined to
the principal cause and others separate. In acting, for example, the hand is a
conjoined instrument and a staff a separate one. In speaking, the tongue,
palate, and teeth are conjoined instruments; the air which we strike is
separate. God analogically employs both modes in his spiritual speech, whether
you wish him to use his own speech alone as an analogous instrument, or inwardly
to employ the speech of angels or our own speech.

The axiom therefore stands firm: the instrumental cause of this wisdom is the
speech of God, just as in human matters the instrumental cause of wisdom is the
speech of one human being teaching and instructing another. Human speech is
manifold. One kind is inborn, which the orthodox fathers called **implanted**
or **natural** (*emphyton* or *physikon*, ἔμφυτον or φυσικόν). This speech is
our intellect with itself. Another kind is imparted, which they called
**internal** (*endiatheton*, ἐνδιάθετον). Reason conceives this speech from the
combination and division of things perceived outwardly, from reasoning, and
from the conclusions which arise from them. Finally, the third kind is
declarative, commonly called **uttered** (*prophorikos*, προφορικός), by which a
human being communicates with others outwardly the conception of his mind,
whether inborn in the intellect or perceived by reason.

The first of these kinds of speech is natural and therefore always exists
actually in its subject. The second is effected in the subject by another. The
third effects something in another. Since the question here concerns speech
which is an efficient and quasi-instrumental cause, or, as we said a little
above, instrumental analogically, the implanted speech (*emphyton*, ἔμφυτον)
cannot be understood here. Implanted speech (*emphytos*, ἔμφυτος) is immanent,
as the scholastics say, and does not pass to another [*Opera*, fol. 1412]. Nor
should internal speech (*endiatheton*, ἐνδιάθετον) be understood, since it is
passive rather than active speech and can never truthfully be attributed to
God. Absolutely nothing happens accidentally to God, much less passion, which
is utterly unworthy of God's majesty.

It remains, then, for us to define that speech of God which we call the
instrumental cause of this wisdom as **uttered** (*prophorikon*, προφορικόν) or
declarative, as we stated above in our thesis. This is right. If no one knows
the things of a human being except the human spirit within him and anyone to
whom a human being wills to set them forth through his declarative speech or
some sign of it, how much more did we need God's speech in order somehow to
grasp divine, heavenly, and supernatural things which a human being, the earth,
and the whole nature of the universe cannot grasp? This uttered speech
(*prophorikos*, προφορικός) flows from God himself and, by a certain outflow or
procession from him, so to speak, produces its effect in those who hear.

This declarative speech of God is distinguished into two genera according to
its rationale and mode: the speech God uses is either spiritual or bodily. We
call speech spiritual when it is declared without a bodily instrument, and
bodily when it sounds with a bodily instrument also being employed. These two
genera of speech are distinguished from each other in three ways: by their
principle, object, and mode. By principle: spiritual speech flows only from a
spiritual essence, while bodily speech flows from a body, or also from a spirit
through a body as its instrument—either conjoined, like a human body which is
the personal organ of its soul, or separate, like air, a pipe, and similar
things, as we showed above. By object: spiritual speech is borne to a spirit and
can never be perceived by any essence other than a spiritual one; bodily speech
is perceived by a body, or also by a spirit through a body as its vessel,
drinking in things infused and cast into it from elsewhere. Finally, by mode:
spiritual

[Printed page 89; PDF page 119]

speech is uttered only spiritually by the one who possesses it and perceived
spiritually by the one who hears. Bodily speech, though it flows from a spirit
as its principal agent, is possessed and comprehended only bodily, so that
through the instrument of the body, as through a funnel, it may be directed
into the recesses and inner chambers of the spirit.

God most holily employed both these modes when he saw fit to pour this heavenly
wisdom from above into the human race. He spoke to the fathers in many portions
and in many ways through the Spirit, through visions, through very many other
instruments, and through his prophets. Then in these times he displayed and
communicated through his Son the fullness of that wisdom, which was afterward
recorded with most certain faith in written monuments through the apostles and
evangelists. But with the Lord's help we will speak about these things in their
proper place.


## Chapter XVI

### On the end of our theology

Thus far we have set forth those things concerning the matter, form, and
efficient cause of our theology which seem pertinent to our purpose. We have
deliberately deferred the question of its end until this point because we
thought that this route would provide the most convenient passage to that
other mode of theology which is commonly called theology in a qualified sense.
For the nature of things ordered to a definite end is such that the greater
their proportion and analogy to that end, the greater their power and dignity.
We must therefore rank this theology of ours, spoken of absolutely, above every
wisdom and science, because by the benefit of divine grace it has the most
perfect agreement with its end and orders its entire discipline to the divine
end in the most perfect way. This does not mean that it unfolds the whole
divine perfection of divine wisdom according to that wisdom's own mode and
condition. Rather, it truthfully sets forth an account in some measure adapted
to our frail condition: as much of that truth and wisdom as it is expedient for
us to have revealed here for the glory of God and the good of the elect, as we
established above in Chapter 12. We shall therefore bring what is needed about
the end to a close in a few definite axioms [*Opera*, fol. 1413], and so at
last bring this argument to an end.

The first axiom is as follows, Thesis XXXI: **The end of theology is twofold:
one is remote, or supreme; the other is proximate and secondary to it, and (as
they say) subordinate to it.** Nature teaches this order for distinguishing
ends with the greatest clarity. In all things and actions, whether of nature
or of the will, it always holds that they strive toward some end. Nature
constantly sets one end before itself; the will makes a changeable choice. The
things which are done solely by the instinct of nature without any motion of
the will are such that they never of themselves stray or turn aside from the
end which nature established. Things done not only by the instinct of their
nature but by the motion of appetite and will ordinarily strive toward several
ends according to the principles by which they are moved. Thus each thing
pursues a natural end from nature, but from the will a voluntary end—whether
deliberate or undeliberate. The deliberate end arises through the just ordering
of nature and will in agreement; the undeliberate through an unjust disturbance
which intervenes.

Here, however, since we have undertaken to speak of the end of our theology
apart from consideration of its subject,

[Printed page 90; PDF page 120]

we contemplate it in such a way that the nature of theology alone must be
furnished with its own proper end or ends, an end which properly belongs to
the nature of theology. Since this theology is supernatural, it also requires
a supernatural end agreeing with its own nature. We must necessarily attribute
either one end or at most two ends to theology. It does not flow apart toward
various termini, as though it were an uncertain and unstable wisdom. Rather,
it either establishes its one end in its own subject, with which it is
occupied, or—if a second end must also be added—this one end will nevertheless
be the utterly constant end in all the other things ordered to its proper
subject; there will never be several. The subject common to all and proper to
our theology is God, and human beings, theology's pupils, are ordered to him.
But an entirely different judgment must be made concerning the human beings
called by God's singular benefit to participation in it. They strive toward
the end set before them not only by nature or by a natural will reformed by
grace, but indiscriminately by a deliberate or undeliberate will as well, as we
shall say in its proper place.

Two ends, then (if one pleases), are assigned to theology: one primary end,
which regards God, and another secondary end, which regards us and the whole
human race. We must now treat them in order. This axiom concerns the first end,
Thesis XXXII: **The primary or supreme end of theology is the glory of God. For
theology sets this before everyone to behold, and all good people, by a lawful
use of this wisdom, also bear witness to it, insofar as wisdom is justified by
her children.** Matthew 11:19. No one can doubt, as we said before, that the
ends of things are more worthy in proportion as the objects toward which one
rightly strives as a just end are more worthy—especially when the certainty of
the end is added to its dignity. The excellence and authority of every science
and action depend chiefly upon these two things.

Here the object of theology is God, for that is why it is called theology—and
indeed the true God, for that is why it is called revealed or inspired
theology. As we showed above, the theology of revelation and our theology are
the same. Since, therefore, that object with which our theology is occupied is
the greatest of all, we rightly declare the glory of God to be its primary or
supreme end. Nothing else can be added to him through us; only his glory can be
made known to us and displayed among created things by the revelation of this
wisdom.

We demonstrate as clearly as possible that this is so by two arguments. One is
drawn from the nature of theology itself; the other from the consequent effects
in those whom God has been pleased to sprinkle with a share in this theology.
It belongs to the nature of theology that it sets its end—the glory of God—
before everyone to be known. Like a sun surveying this lower habitation of ours
from the summit of heaven, it sets that glory before every face and every eye.
If any do not believe, therefore, we must judge of them what the apostle says
in 2 Corinthians 4:3–4: if theology is veiled, it is veiled in those who are
perishing, in whom the god of this age has blinded their minds—that is, among
unbelievers—so that the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the
image of God, may not shine upon them. Or, as he says elsewhere in Hebrews 1:3,
Christ is the radiance of God's glory. If by nature color and light are not at
all perceived by the blind, sound by the deaf [*Opera*, fol. 1414], or odor by
the dead—even though these are natural things—because they lack nature's
faculty for perceiving them, then by the same and indeed a stronger argument we
must believe human beings unequal and unfit to perceive divine and supernatural
things. Not only they but the whole nature of things lacks every faculty by
which those divine things that transcend the totality of nature could be
grasped.

The other argument, which we taught was to be drawn from the effects, shines
far more clearly in the human race. Those supernatural and divine things cannot
be seen by human beings either in themselves or in their gracious communication

[Printed page 91; PDF page 121]

to the elect. But when those things which the human eye cannot grasp have been
rooted within by God's grace, they put forth their spiritual fruits outwardly
and display them like shining torches of good works, so that those who see them
may glorify our Father in heaven (Matthew 5:16). Then indeed there is the
greatest and most effective proof of that theology living and acting in the
elect. Those who have obtained a share in theology are themselves conscious in
their souls of the light kindled within by the power of the Spirit. Then, by
the free and open use of and obedience to that same light, they awaken and
illuminate the consciences of the brothers observing them, so that the brothers
may contemplate God's work in them, theology's efficacy in the work, and God
himself in that efficacy.

For it is God's surest work, and far the greatest of all the works which can be
performed here, that by participation in this theology the blind see, the deaf
hear, and even the dead revive at the voice of the Son of God and attain true
and eternal life in communion with him. John 5:15.[^p91-ref] In this work there is the
most magnificent efficacy of that most holy theology: from a corpse, as from a
dead trunk destined for the fire (for such is the natural or **soulish** human
being, *psychikos*, ψυχικός), theology draws out the richest fruits of that life
amid such masses of obstacles as the flesh, sin, the world, and Satan throw in
the way, and amid such great weakness. Finally, it is the clearest proof of
God's presence and assistance that he perfects all these things in his own by
the communication of his Spirit, and by this grace grants the godly to work out
their own salvation with fear and trembling (Philippians 2:12). For God is the
one who works in us both to will and to accomplish according to his gracious
good pleasure. Through it he so powerfully makes his glory attested to all that
his manifold wisdom is justified by her children, as Christ said in Matthew
11:19.

Thus it pleased God the Father that the unsearchable riches of Christ contained
in the treasury of that theology should be proclaimed among human beings, and
that everyone should have brought to light for them the fellowship of the
mystery hidden for ages in God, who created all these things through Jesus
Christ. In this way, through the church, the manifold wisdom of God would be
made known to the principalities and powers in heaven according to the eternal
purpose which he established in Christ Jesus our Lord and the apostle of our
confession—a confession which we have drawn from the fullness of that theology
to the glory of God. Ephesians 3:9–11. There follows now the other end, which we
called secondary and subordinate. Because the argument is straightforward, we
shall outline it briefly.

Thesis XXXIII: **The secondary or subordinate end of our theology is the
present and future good of the elect. For promises of this life and the life to
come have been made to their godliness, and this theology is the wisdom of true
godliness.** Having spoken of the ultimate or supreme end, which surpasses the
condition of every created thing and will endure forever, we now at last come
to the end joined to us, which will remain within us without ceasing for our
greatest good. Nature properly and of itself always seeks good: common nature
seeks it in common and individual nature individually. The natural order of
this desire is so arranged that each seeks its own good as proximate and the
common good as supreme, for the common good of individuals consists in it. In
the same way, within the order of saving grace God has shown by the light of
theology both the common good and our individual good which consists in that
common good.

The common good is God's glory. Our individual good is situated within this
common good: we are to be glorified in God's glory and shall receive every good
from his glory and for his glory. Therefore, just as God's glory constantly
remains in this age and the age to come and is the perennial fountain of good
belonging to all created things, so we must also settle in our minds that the
effect of that supernatural cause [*Opera*, fol. 1415]—always active, utterly
free, and never hindered—always exists and will never, once

[Printed page 92; PDF page 122]

any effect has been produced, die out. We therefore conclude that the good of
created things whom God, the sole author of salvation, has effectually called
to participate in his glory can never perish. Since these creatures come from
no other source than God's election and are distinguished by the purpose of
grace, we establish that the good of the elect—whether angels in heaven or
human beings on earth—is eternal and immortal, because it has its foundation
in God's glory and rests upon it. Thus, in striving toward the common good,
that is, God's glory, we are led to our individual good, that is, our salvation.
But why and how? By the promises of grace and the revelation of theology.

God's grace alone, and the promises of grace, are the cause for our being led
to both these goods. That same grace, by communicating theology to us, shows
everyone the mode. Moreover, when the apostle supplied the cause, saying in 2
Timothy 4:8 that promises of this life and of the life to come have been made
to godliness,[^p92-ref] he foretold two effects which would come from those
divine promises. One is that God himself graciously forms us into the just
condition fitting for his heirs. The other is that he arranges the hereditary
goods. The just condition of the heirs consists in true godliness, which is
defined by love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and an unfeigned faith
(1 Timothy 1:5). The hereditary goods indeed belong to the life to come, but
already in this very life they are communicated in fact and received through
faith until we attain full possession in that future life. God effects both:
godliness in us, the heirs, and the inheritance in heaven for those who will
be heirs of salvation. I add that he also effects the advantages needed to
take possession of the inheritance for as long as we are on the way, striving
toward the Lord.

No saving mode of providing and receiving this cause can exist except the
revelation of that divine wisdom. This wisdom is not of this age or of the
rulers of this age, who are passing away, but the wisdom of God in a mystery,
that hidden wisdom which God predestined before the ages for our glory. No ruler
of this age knew it, but God alone reveals it through the Spirit (1 Corinthians
2:7–8). This is the one wisdom which both produces godliness in us, the future
heirs of the kingdom of heaven, and teaches the godly the promises of every
good, and sets before their faith all the hereditary goods and eternal
salvation, to the glory of God. So much for our theology in itself, as the
incorrupt form of heavenly wisdom presented by God. It remains for us to add a
few things in the same order and manner about theology according to us, which
some call theology in a qualified sense and others theology in its subject.

## Chapter XVII

### On theology in its subject

Our theology, which inheres in a human subject insofar as it can inhere in that
subject, is therefore a certain imperfect form of that theology which God
perfectly presents to us by gracious communication. All those actions of God
which are purely from him obtain their perfect form and constitution in a
single moment from their very origin. Conversely, whatever actions proceed
jointly from God and human beings, in which God has in some fashion willed
human beings to be his **co-workers** (*synergous*, συνεργούς), inhere in their
subjects in a certain imperfect form for as long as we are in this life; they
do not attain their perfection in this age. And just as

[Printed page 93; PDF page 123]

nature, beginning from the most imperfect degree, gradually strives toward its own
perfection, so these actions too are gradually led by God's power from the
frailest and most imperfect beginning, through their degrees and increases,
into some image of their perfection. Consequently, this so-called theology in
its subject can never be defined according to degrees of perfection or
imperfection. It is as varied as the subjects in which it inheres through the
grace of God, according to the several degrees of each subject's imperfection.
Nevertheless, wherever this theology exists, it is not more truly theology in
one person than in another; it is considered only greater or lesser according
to the capacity of each [*Opera*, fol. 1416]. By way of example, we say that a
short oak is not less truly an oak but a smaller oak than another which is
tall. Theology in its subject can therefore be defined in some measure
according to its essence without comparison to this varying imperfection.

For Christ also taught us that faith, which has a necessary relation to this
theology, is not more or less truly faith but greater or smaller according to
its subjects. He told the apostles, “If you have faith the size of a mustard
seed, you will say to this mountain, ‘Move from here to there,’ and it will
move,” and so forth (Matthew 17:20). Prudently observing this account, therefore,
we define this theology of ours, which is called theology in its subject, as
follows. Thesis XXXIV: **Our theology in a qualified sense, or what is called
theology in its subject, is that same wisdom of divine things, modified
according to the capacity of the human beings in whom it inheres and from which
they are called theologians.**

From this definition one can easily recognize what this theology has in common
with our theology spoken of absolutely and how it differs from it. Two common
things are assigned: its genus and what they call its common difference. Its
genus is wisdom, which we discussed above in Chapter 2. Its common difference
is that it is wisdom concerning divine things; in Chapter 13 we briefly
outlined the nature, constitution, forms, and order of those things insofar as
they seemed pertinent to our purpose.

We must now come to its proper difference. For clarity we present this in two
ways: according to its nature or essence, and according to its sign. These
words concern the difference of this theology according to its essence: it is
said to be **modified according to the capacity of the human beings in whom it
inheres.** We denoted the difference from its sign by the words, **from which
they are called theologians.** In order properly to observe the essential
difference, so to speak, of this theology of ours, these two things must concur:
the subject and its capacity, and the gift of wisdom inhering in that subject.
Because the nature of this subject is always imperfect, it cannot of itself
receive anything truly perfect; or, if by God's grace it receives something
perfect—as it does—it nevertheless cannot receive it perfectly. This is the
capacity which we wish always to be observed in the subject, so that we may
judge properly of the gift or grace added to it.

For the subject is twofold according to its condition and the way it is
considered: one subject in nature and another in grace. The subject in its
nature cannot receive a perfect thing either actually, potentially, or by any
disposition toward that potentiality; nor can it receive anything perfectly.
The same subject, considered in grace—or, as they say, in the state of grace
while it is here—does receive a perfect gift and a good bestowal coming from
God, as James says in 1:17. Yet because it always remains in some imperfection
of nature and is moved only by the inception of grace, it cannot perfectly
receive that perfect gift and good bestowal proceeding from God, although by a
singular grace it has sometimes happened that the man of God received that
perfect gift.

From this capacity of the subject, therefore, proceeds that modification of our
theology. Although theology is perfect in itself, in its subject—however
perfect it may be—it is in some fashion mutilated by the subject's imperfection.
Accordingly, the form of theology in itself is perfect, but the form of theology
in its subject is imperfect, because it is a form of sheer

[Printed page 94; PDF page 124]

inception in us through grace. The argument for this modification is drawn from
the sign which we supplied at the end of the definition. Just as they hold a
quality to be that from which we are called qualified in a certain way, so
theology too must be called that from which theologians receive their name.
The condition of theologians is such that they agree with one another in their
subject insofar as they are human beings, but differ greatly in capacity,
because we see that more theology inheres in some and less in others. If anyone
were to think that a beginner knows theology as thoroughly as John or Paul did,
or that the same theologian can perform just as much while learning the first
elements of theology as when, through long habituation, his senses have been
trained by the canon of true theology to discern good and evil, then in the
judgment of good people he should surely be remanded, as they say, to his family
and kinsmen. It was therefore necessary to append to the preceding definition
a few observations and precepts concerning the mode of this theology and its
causes, so that by sure religion and prudence one might guard against both
opposite extreme errors, into one or the other of which the crowd readily
falls.

Some, as soon as they have drunk even the smallest taste [*Opera*, fol. 1417]
of this wisdom, think themselves theologians pure and simple and imagine that
everything they say proclaims the theologian. In view of their deserts, one
could more truthfully call them **enthusiasts** (*enthousiastas*,
ἐνθουσιαστάς). Others, on the contrary, after observing the meagerness of this
theology of ours in its subject and—alas—the vanity of many theologians, think
everything **vain talk** (*mataiologon*, ματαιολόγον). While they become futile
in these thoughts, these people race headlong into wicked **godlessness**
(*atheotēta*, ἀθεότητα). To forestall these evils reverently and holily, it has
seemed necessary to add a few headings about the mode of this theology, so that
the truth of the divine matter, however obscure, may be vindicated from human
falsehood.

The first is as follows, Thesis XXXV: **The mode of this theology in its subject
cannot be circumscribed, because it varies both in each person and is most
diverse among them all.** Here by “mode” we mean neither the form of the thing,
for the form of theology is always divine in all its degrees, nor the way it is
comprehended and perceived, for this too is a divine work and the seal of
divine grace. We mean instead the quantity, so to speak, of its comprehension
and perception which has reached a human being. Just as more or less liquid is
poured into vessels according to their capacity, so human souls are expanded
more or less by the power of God to become capable both of other spiritual
gifts and of this wisdom itself. We therefore rightly affirm that the mode of
this theology—or rather, of the human beings who participate in this theology—
cannot be circumscribed. Knowledge deals with universals, while this mode
belongs to individual particulars, as they call them. You would sooner drain
the ocean with a sieve than attain any certain knowledge of this matter.

We must first examine the causes of this innumerable variety, and then (if it
can be done) demonstrate certain headings by which at least some account of the
matter at hand may be shown for the service of our purpose. The causes do not
reside in theology itself, since by itself it is wholly uniform and cannot
exist in diverse modes. Whatever those causes may be, therefore, we must find
them in the subject itself. There are two causes proceeding altogether from
the subject's condition. One is that each individual subject—that is, every
human being—varies exceedingly within himself and is beset by innumerable
changes. The other is that different subjects differ from one another to the
greatest degree. Since the character of the subjects is so varied and manifold,
both within themselves and in relation to one another, the mode of this wisdom
inhering in subjects so changeable in themselves and so different from one
another must also vary.

We establish the following concerning the former mode of change, Thesis XXXVI:
**Theology varies in each person because a twofold principle inheres in each—
nature and grace. The former must diminish and the latter increase from glory
to glory by the power of the Spirit and the effectual communication of
theology.** Common experience most clearly

[Printed page 95; PDF page 125]

attests—and everyone sees even by the testimony of common sense—that each
person changes within himself and that the mode of theology therefore changes
in that same person. But not everyone sees the causes equally well. The causes
of change lie in the principles of this wisdom from which human beings who know
theology are constituted. There are two genera of those principles. The first,
namely nature, is their principle insofar as they are human beings. The second,
namely grace, is their principle insofar as they are endowed with this wisdom
above the mode of their nature. It is therefore utterly impossible for them
not to be led by various changes and through degrees of change to a
comprehension of this wisdom. Because nature is the common principle of all
human beings, everyone perceives that it is mutable and as it were revolved in
perpetual cycles and changes. But only those whose eyes God has illuminated by
grace properly see grace acting in human beings through degrees of change,
although people who are utterly estranged from that grace can sometimes see
its signs.

The effect of these principles concurring together in one and the same subject
is wholly diverse. Because the subject's nature is borne of itself toward
destruction, our natural or outward person must diminish and decay, while the
inward person whom grace moves increases and is renewed day by day (2
Corinthians 4:16). Thus all of us whom God has graciously made pupils of his
theology, beholding the glory of God as in a mirror with unveiled face, are
transformed into that same image from glory to glory, as by the Spirit of the
Lord (2 Corinthians 3:18). Nature's weakness thus declines of itself day by day
in proportion as the efficacy of grace advances. The author of this grace by
his power is the Spirit [*Opera*, fol. 1418]—that Spirit of God, I say, who
effects all these things in everyone, distributing his gifts individually to
each as he wills (1 Corinthians 12:11), and most effectually communicates
theology, or this divine wisdom, not only in common but also separately and
individually to every heir of divine grace.

Just as no fixed mode of theology can be given in any one member of Christ who
participates in this heavenly wisdom, so we must also establish that its mode
is wholly indeterminate if we compare with one another the condition of the
many human beings whom God has made participants in theology. We have thought
it best to explain this in the next thesis, Thesis XXXVII: **This theology
varies among everyone because in some nature is diminished and grace grows
stronger more than in others, even though no one here perfectly comprehends the
entire form of our theology in every respect.** The rationale of this
proposition is so evident that it hardly appears to need explanation. Just as
individuals experience change in themselves from the two principles we
established above, so those same principles also cause the mode of this wisdom
to be most diverse among all human beings.

To speak first of nature, we must consider it in two ways: as common to all and
as individual and proper to each, or, as the fathers called it,
**characteristic** (*charaktēristikēn*, χαρακτηριστικήν). If every human being
possesses his own proper, characteristic, and individual nature, who does not
see from this that all agree with one another by common nature, while by the
necessity of nature a difference intervenes through each one's individual and
proper nature? Thus, according to the changes of common nature, all human
beings undergo the laws of change in common; according to the character of each
one's particular and proper nature, each also undergoes his changes
individually. In bodily matters the nature of each human being gradually
diminishes as it passes through its turns and successive conditions, and at
last perishes. By the same principle we acknowledge change and diminution of
nature in matters belonging to our soul. Yet what we see in individual nature
striving toward its own diminution and destruction must conversely be
contemplated in the grace which increases in God's servants and all the godly
and happily rises toward its own perfection: nature's decrease is grace's
increase. There is only this difference in the matter: those decreases and
diminutions of nature, by the power of grace opposing and at last

[Printed page 96; PDF page 126]

going to conquer and overcome it, proceed from grace; but the power and efficacy of grace
alone accomplish its increases. From this follows another difference—or rather,
if one prefers to call it that, an effect arising from the preceding
difference: as grace is raised up and ascends in godly minds, nature is pressed
down by the same measure and, as they say, by contrary degrees. The two proceed
at an equal pace, nature declining as grace advances. It is just as if someone
were to drain from below a cask pierced at both ends while filling it from above
with another liquid which drove out the lower one, or were to dislodge some
impure liquid settled at the bottom by pouring in pure Spirit from above.

Yet however far human beings instructed by God's Spirit advance in the
communication of that grace, all good people and lovers of truth have always
settled this point: in all recorded human history no one has ever comprehended
the entire form of this theology of ours perfectly in every respect; no one
comprehends it in this life now; and no one will comprehend it in any age while
we walk in this miserable station by faith rather than sight and attain the end
of this theology and our faith. As the apostle says in 1 Corinthians 13:12, we
now see through a mirror and in a riddle, but then we shall see face to face.
Now we know in part, but then we shall know as we have been known.

There are consequently innumerable degrees in the mode of our theology—so great
an interval and difference of degree extends from its lowest degree, which is
nearest to nothing, to the highest degree, which no human being can ever touch.
Yet not even that person, whoever among human beings might be such a one, in
whom nature has been diminished to the greatest extent and grace has increased
and grown as strong as possible—not even the person whom God's grace has raised
to that point—has attained the entire form of this theology of ours so as to
perceive it in a perfect mode according to the proper rule of comprehension,
or in every respect according to all the parts of our theology, with a
perfection agreeing with the divine form of our theology [*Opera*, fol. 1419].

If there are any who approach this—and here we say nothing of the person of
Christ, whose proper theology of union is like the inexhaustible fountain from
which this theology of ours is derived—that person must certainly have been
one of the prophets or apostles, upon whom the Lord conferred singular gifts
for his singular calling and administration, to demonstrate that Spirit and
divine wisdom. But consider Elijah or Elisha from the company of the prophets;
John the Baptist, whom Christ himself pronounced greater than the prophets; or
Peter, James, John, or Paul. Not even in them, nor in any one of these men of
God, should that perfection be said to have existed. We must instead establish
that just as, with respect to life, God willed his grace to be sufficient and
his power to be perfected in our weakness, so also with respect to faith his
purpose was that his grace should suffice for each and his truth should rest
content in that frailty of his servants.

We have drawn this matter to a close in a few words, Thesis XXXVIII: **The
prophets and apostles indeed received the whole and perfect form of this
theology, but did not receive it perfectly in themselves; and by the singular
power of the Spirit they delivered the whole of it. All the rest receive
neither the whole nor perfectly.** By the “whole” form of theology we mean one
which embraces all its essential parts, so to speak. By “perfect” we mean one
which possesses the true and proper mode, lacking imperfection, both in itself
and in each of its individual parts. We therefore deny that most people have
either received the whole of this theology of ours, called whole and perfect
in this sense, or received it perfectly. If any did receive it, they were only
the prophets and apostles, to whom the perfect communication of this theology
of ours came by singular inspiration and revelation. Thus, when we grant that
those most holy servants of God, the prophets and apostles, received the whole
form of this theology, it must be understood according to the principles of
theology itself. In the same way,

[Printed page 97; PDF page 127]

Moses, God's faithful servant in his whole house, received from God this whole form
which had been delivered to him; that he recorded the same perfect form in
writing as he had received it from the Lord; and that he handed down the divine
canon to the ancient church. The entire nature of theology is contained
virtually in these principles, and those greatest men grasped those principles
by a certain singular and extraordinary power imparted to them through the
Spirit. In this respect we do not evade the conclusion that they attained the
whole and perfect form of this theology. Yet we by no means establish that they
possessed it perfectly in themselves. They possessed it according to their own
mode and the mode of the revelation made to them; these two qualifications are
enough to demonstrate the imperfection of this communication.

If we consider their own mode, they were human beings beset by the same
weaknesses as we are and confined by the same narrow bounds of frailty, in
keeping with the human lot. While that human lot sojourns here away from the
Lord it is so imperfect that it cannot receive a perfect thing perfectly. For
when, I ask, does an imperfect efficient cause produce effects which are
perfect or deserve to be regarded as perfect in their own right? It is surely
absurd for anyone to suppose that perfect effects could be produced or follow
from an imperfect cause. Someone may say that they did not perform this
perfectly according to their own mode, but that God worked this perfection in
them. We answered this in the second clause too, when we said that they did not
comprehend this theology perfectly even according to the mode of revelation.

The author of revelation is indeed perfect, his work is perfect in itself, and
therefore every revelation God makes is perfect in itself. Yet the character
of divine revelation in itself is far different from its character in an owl,
a night-blind creature or bat, or a bleary-eyed person who can receive only
some small portion of that light. The power of the divine Spirit did indeed as
it were enlarge the minds of the prophets and apostles and illuminate them
beyond our mode and its common measure. Yet it cannot be concluded from this
that their mind was enlarged to infinity or with a certain infinite perfection
so that they could perfectly receive the perfection of this wisdom. We believe
that they received the whole, perfect wisdom, free from every imperfection. But
we by no means affirm that they received it perfectly or with so great a
perfection that in themselves they equaled the perfection of this wisdom in
itself; it would not be pious to affirm it. Why should we think in our souls or
proclaim among others anything about those elect instruments [*Opera*, fol.
1420] of God other than what they declared concerning themselves with divine
truth and proper humility?

Speaking of himself in common with others, Paul said, “We know in part and we
prophesy in part. Now we see through a mirror and in a riddle, but then face to
face” (1 Corinthians 13:9, 12). Writing to Timothy, his true son in the faith,
he repeatedly commended the reading of sacred Scripture for no other purpose
than to show that this frailty of ours, unequal to receiving the perfection of
divine things, must be fought throughout the whole course of this life by holy
reading, prayer, and meditation, until by a blessed migration to the Lord we
attain complete victory over ourselves and our weakness.

Just as they received this entire wisdom, so the Lord also provided for his
church that this same whole and perfect wisdom should be handed down by the
singular power of the Spirit. He did not use the work of only one of them to
hand down this whole and perfect doctrine in the church. Rather, from the whole
and perfect doctrine of them all he caused us to possess the perfect body of
theology recorded in the sacred Scriptures through the labor of all those
servants of God, and to have the entire, perfect form of this wisdom perfectly
set forth. Thus the apostle most fittingly says that all Scripture is divinely
inspired so that the man of God may be perfect, perfectly equipped for every
good work (2 Timothy 3:16–17), because Scripture is the perfect instrument
provided to us by God, even though we individual workers are imperfect in every
respect.

[Printed page 98; PDF page 128]

Here, however, a question arises which might perhaps trouble those less experienced
students who devote themselves to sacred Scripture. We call Scripture the
perfect instrument of theology, yet we see that the prophets and apostles
sometimes said and wrote things differing from the truth which God reveals.
How, then, can we call this Scripture a perfect instrument? For example, when
Samuel saw Eliab, David's eldest brother, he said, “Surely the Lord's anointed
is before him” (1 Samuel 16:6). Nathan likewise told the king when he was
planning to build the sacred house, “Do whatever is in your heart, for the Lord
is with you” (2 Samuel 7:3). Paul wrote that he would travel to Spain and on
that journey come to the Romans (Romans 15:24), and he made similar statements
to the Corinthians and Thessalonians. Since they therefore spoke or even wrote
what proved false, someone may ask, how can Scripture be called a perfect
instrument when answers and promises in it are found to be false?

Anyone who diligently attends to our definition of theology can recognize the
solution to this doubt from the definition itself. Yet so that the less skilled
may not be delayed, we shall present it briefly as follows. Knowledge of divine
things is one thing; knowledge of our human counsels, will, or judgment is
another. Among divine things we number not only the things God does but also
those to which he bears witness by his speech, whether by the mode of the common
word or by singular revelation. Human counsels or judgments, by contrast,
embrace everything uttered by human beings without God's common or singular
testimony, however holy those people and their purpose may be, or by any merely
human judgment. Such were those judgments of Samuel and Nathan when, without
consulting God, they formed a conclusion according to their own by no means
evil opinion contrary to what God had determined by his secret judgment. They
did not cease to be human beings because they were prophets. They exercised
both genera of action: the human according to nature, and the prophetic
according to the relation of grace which had been added to nature. Scripture
truthfully narrates this false judgment of theirs, as the faithfulness of
history requires.

Paul's example appears more difficult because every godly person is convinced
in his soul that the epistles of the holy apostles, which were received into
the canon of Christ's church, were written by the sure dictation of the Holy
Spirit. Yet Paul promised one thing in his speech, while another followed in
the event by God's decree. If we look more deeply into the matter, however, we
shall see that it makes little difference whether they spoke or wrote things
which they judged to pertain to the exercise of their ministry. In both their
speeches and writings, insofar as they performed the ministry of the
apostleship, it is certain that the Spirit's power presided over them and
governed everything. Thus nothing in the epistles can be reproached on this
account, nor does it seem that anything rightly could be.

First, nothing pertaining to the doctrine and rule [*Opera*, fol. 1421] of
faith can be reproached anywhere. Nor does it immediately follow that if
something human is said or written which in no way opposes godliness, the truth
of faith, or holiness, the authority of the divine things handed down is
destroyed. What is said in a human way is not itself theology. It is no more
foreign to a theologian to speak or write in a human way than it is foreign to
a human being to be engaged in this wisdom which we call theology. Indeed, it
seems all the more fitting. Insofar as he is a human being by nature, he acts
by nature according to what belongs to a human being by the property of nature;
insofar as he is a theologian by grace, he does not act as a theologian except
by the singularity of grace. Moreover, it seemed good to God's wisdom to bear
open witness in this way that human opinions, judgments, and wills which pertain
to godliness and love are approved and sanctified by God, even though by God's
secret will their outcome will occur otherwise. Yet if no examples of these
things existed, the audacity of certain superstitious people,

[Printed page 99; PDF page 129]

and the imprudence of many would doubtless judge this kind of writing and counsel
unfairly. Which of these Catos would not condemn the human and affectionate
greetings which the apostles appended to the ends of their epistles, or the
commendation of Onesimus to Philemon, unless he reflected that the apostles—and
Paul by name—wrote in this way, and that God by his authority deposited these
humble proofs of human kindness in the treasury of his church?

In this manner, then, out of devotion to human kindness and with an awareness
of particular revelation, Paul promised the Romans, Corinthians, and
Thessalonians that he would come. He did so with a sure confidence in divine
grace, which approves these human promises even though it by no means ensures
that they will be fulfilled. God alone promises with the certainty of truth;
a human being promises with truthfulness of mind, leaving the certainty of the
whole matter to God. James warns us of this in 4:15, and Paul expressly states
it when writing to the Romans in 15:32. This is also why Paul so carefully
vindicates God's promises in Christ from all such uncertainty (2 Corinthians
1:17–20), while demonstrating that human promises made by himself and others
are always joined to their own uncertainty, which does not touch God's promises
at all.

He reasons as follows in that passage to excuse the fact that he had raised the
hope of his coming and yet had not come: “Our boast is this,” he says, “the
testimony of our conscience that we have conducted ourselves in the world—and
especially among you—with simplicity and godly sincerity, not with fleshly
wisdom but with the grace of God.” Then, “With this confidence I formerly
wanted to come to you” (for he had written that he wished to do so) “so that
you might have a second benefit, and through you to pass into Macedonia, and
again to come to you from Macedonia, and to be sent by you into Judea.” When
I planned this, then, did I act with levity—that is, when I made the promise,
did I promise thoughtlessly? Or do I plan the things I now plan—that is, at the
very time when I enter upon a different purpose—according to the flesh, so that
with me there is both “Yes” and “No”? In sum, was I frivolous then, or am I
frivolous now? He answers: on the contrary, the faithful God vouches that our
speech among you was not “Yes” and “No”; that is, I have always remained
consistent with myself in my statements.

Since there are two genera of speech from the apostle—as God's envoy, and as a
human being speaking humanly without God's definite and express command—he next
treats each distinctly. First he declares magnificently concerning the
ministerial speech of his apostleship: “For the Son of God, Jesus Christ, who
was preached among you through us—through me, Silvanus, and Timothy—was not
‘Yes’ and ‘No,’ but in him was ‘Yes.’” He proves this affirmation by a universal
statement: “For however many promises of God there are, in him they are ‘Yes,’
and in him ‘Amen,’ so that glory may be given to God through us.” A twofold
confirmation follows. The first is, “The one who establishes us with you in
Christ, and who anointed us, is God.” The other is, “He also sealed us and put
the earnest of the Spirit in our hearts.” Afterward, passing to the other genus
of speech, he clears himself by a solemn appeal to God: “But I call God as a
witness upon my soul that I have not yet come to Corinth in order to spare
you,” together with what follows in that chapter and the latter part of the
second.

Since these things are so, we establish that the apostles were ministers of
that heavenly wisdom in such a way that they handed it down perfectly to God's
church. This did not prevent them from humanly declaring and writing, from the
duty of love, counsels directed to that end [*Opera*, fol. 1422], even though
the outcome was otherwise. Nor can they be blamed for not knowing everything
God knows or for simply willing things which God does not will by his absolute
will. To know and will in that way belongs to divinity. It belongs to humanity
to know no more about those things, or to will them by a determinate will, than
God has revealed to them through his Spirit for his glory.

## Chapter XVIII

### Conclusion of the work

From everything said above we conclude this whole argument with a kind of
corollary as follows, Thesis XXXIX: **Thus the form of our theology is indeed
one in itself, as we said before, but in us its mode is now and will continue
to be manifold until, all coming together into the unity of faith and the
knowledge of the Son of God, we become a perfect man and are raised together
to the measure of the stature of the church, which is the fullness of Christ.**

In these words, from the apostle's own language, we establish first the nature
and then the end of our theology. As concerns its nature, as explained above,
we demonstrate that our theology, which God graciously reveals to us, is as
follows. In itself, theology—perfectly comprehended through the Spirit of God in
its instrument, sacred Scripture—has one essential form: wholly perfect, wholly
and perfectly set forth, and wholly constant and immutable in itself and in all
its parts. But in us, who are transformed day by day by the Spirit of the Lord,
neither the whole mode of that entire form, nor that mode perfectly, nor the
form perfectly inheres or dwells. Only its parts inhere; those parts are
imperfect, and we perceive them imperfectly. For God adapts the mode of his
revelations to the mode of our frailty, to declare his marvelous light and to
praise his glorious grace.

Although the present communication of this wisdom is of such a kind that it
advances through degrees and increases, we must not suppose that while we
remain in this mortal life we could ever climb to its summit or that the
perfection of this wisdom will reach us. We should think instead that as our
weakness and ignorance diminish by their successive stages throughout the
entire course of this life, this wisdom increases in us in the contrary order
and degree. Nor can it happen otherwise for us. This theology of ours which can
inhere in us is a small portion of that theology which God perfectly expressed
in the Scriptures. Yet that theology revealed in the Scriptures is itself a
symbol and riddle of the theology we shall attain in heaven by seeing God face
to face. It is therefore impossible that throughout the course of this
miserable life, the farther we advance, the more we should not perceive our
ignorance and frailty and become weary of that frailty, so that by true humility,
which holds the first and indeed every place in theology, we may rise toward
the height of heavenly and divine things and direct the whole gaze of our mind
most earnestly toward that end.

There are two ends most closely joined to us—for we will say nothing here about
the supreme end. We touch one in some measure in this age; we shall attain the
other most perfectly in the age to come. The end set before us in this life is
**the unity of faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God.** We must all come
together toward that unity in the communion of saints and strain every sinew
as much as possible to receive its fruit. Faith is that by which we apprehend
Christ with a will and most religious zeal, and through which we are borne to
him by our whole motion [*Opera*, fol. 1423]. Knowledge is that by which Christ
presents himself to be seen by us and dwells inwardly in our minds. The unity
of these gifts among all of us who are Christians is the proximate end most
closely joined to us, toward which we must aim in this life: that by knowledge
we may perceive Christ, and by faith be moved, borne, and swept toward Christ
with a most holy motion, so that Christ's saying may be true in us, that the
kingdom of heaven suffers violence and violent people seize it (Matthew 11:12).
From this proximate end, to which godliness toward God commands each of us to
attend, that other end common to all cannot fail by God's power

[Printed page 101; PDF page 131]

to exist in the age to come: namely, that **all together we are made one perfect man**
in Christ Jesus (Ephesians 4:13). Christ is our head, into whom we must grow in
every respect and from whom the whole body, properly joined together through
every joint of his supply, receives according to the effective power measured
out to each member the growth appropriate to this body, for building itself up
in love. Surely, then, according to his own ordering God will furnish for this
mystical body—established by his marvelous grace—the same thing which he willed
to furnish by nature for the natural body. We must therefore believe both that
we shall attain this benefit in Christ our head and that throughout the whole
course of this mortal life we must so employ the holy preaching of the gospel
that we contribute everything to this fitting together of the saints, to this
work of ministry, and to the building up of Christ's body, until we are raised
together to that proper measure of spiritual stature which God has prepared in
the most glorious way for his church in Christ. In this respect the whole
church, which is Christ's body, is called his fullness or complement (Ephesians
1 and 4).

This indeed is the greatest glory, which God has revealed to us by the word of
his theology and will consummate by the power of his Spirit: Christ, that
eternal Son of God, in whom the Father willed all the fullness of deity to dwell
bodily, has deigned by his divine benefit to establish so great a communion
with us that we are completed in him, who is the head of every principality and
power, and he in turn obtains his fullness in us in that glory which he will
preserve eternally for himself and for us in himself.

Since these things are so, we must value most highly that saving wisdom which
we call theology and keep the apostle's splendid example constantly before our
eyes, so that with the Spirit's sure testimony we may all say with him: “The
things which were gain to me I counted loss because of Christ. Indeed, I
certainly count everything loss because of the surpassing worth of knowing
Christ Jesus my Lord. For his sake I impose upon myself the loss of all those
things which the world esteems and count them as refuse, so that I may gain
Christ and be found in him, not having my own righteousness which comes from
works but that which comes through faith in Christ—the righteousness, I say,
which is from God in faith—so that I may know him, the power of his resurrection,
and the fellowship of his suffering while I am conformed to his death, if by
any means I may attain the resurrection of the dead, into which Christ went
before us for our justification” (Philippians 3:7–8 and following).

May that best and greatest God, who begot his eternal Son Jesus Christ our
Redeemer by eternal generation and sanctifies him for us by eternal
predestination so that he may be our wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and
redemption, also grant us the Spirit of wisdom, so that as we advance by his
power in the saving treasures of this knowledge and wisdom, we may grow into
the unity of faith and the knowledge of him, until we become a perfect man at
the proper measure of that stature which befits that greatest and most glorious
body in Christ Jesus our head and Savior, to his glory. Amen. [*Opera*, fol.
1424]
