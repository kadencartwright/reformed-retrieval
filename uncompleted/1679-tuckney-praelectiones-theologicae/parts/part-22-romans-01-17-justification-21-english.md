There remain only certain testimonies of the Fathers which Bellarmine
adduces to establish his opinion: Jerome's letter to Ctesiphon; Augustine's
*On Grace and Free Choice*, chapter 7; *On the Predestination of the Saints*,
also chapter 7; the preface to Psalm 31; and, as the Jesuit adds, wherever
Augustine discusses this subject. Bellarmine claims that they teach that the
works opposed to faith and excluded from justification mean works preceding
faith, performed by the powers of free choice alone.

[1679 printed p. 145 | IA leaf 153]

My general answer to all these testimonies is that we are reluctant, in
matters of faith resting upon divine testimony alone, to be pressed too
heavily by testimonies from the Fathers or any human beings, however learned
and holy. Many spurious writings of doubtful credit have been attributed to
them. Even their genuine writings have been wretchedly interpolated,
shamefully mutilated, altered, and corrupted—by the wickedness and cunning of
heretics, the negligence and ignorance of scribes, and especially the
Expurgatory Indexes, which deserve purging only by the fire of hell—in
defiance of every respect for antiquity, truth, faithfulness, and candor.
Finally, because the Fathers were human, they freely confessed as humbly as
truly that they could be deceived and sometimes had actually been mistaken.
They ask to be believed only insofar as they agree with the truth revealed
in Holy Scripture.

Passing over many others, consider the two witnesses Bellarmine employs
here, Jerome and Augustine. Jerome says on Psalm 86, “Although someone after
the apostles may be holy and eloquent, he does not possess their authority.”
Elsewhere he advises that the Fathers be read “so that you judge rather than
follow them.” In his *Apology Against Jovinian* he says of himself, “Wherever
I do not interpret Scripture but freely speak my own judgment, let anyone
refute me.” That may be especially necessary in his expositions of Holy
Scripture, which are often more subtle and allegorical than is useful.

As for Augustine, passing over many similar statements, I will transcribe
only what he says in Letter 19 to Jerome, whom Bellarmine cites here
alongside Augustine himself. The familiar words are these: “I have learned
to render this fear and honor only to those writers now called canonical,
so that I dare not believe any of them erred in writing. But I read the
others in such a way that, however eminent in holiness and doctrine, I do
not consider something true merely because they thought it, but because
they have persuaded me—either through the canonical authors themselves or
by probable reasoning—that it does not depart from the truth. Nor do I
suppose, my brother, that you think otherwise. I certainly do not imagine
that you want your books to be read like those of the prophets and apostles,
concerning whose writings it is impious to doubt that they are free from
every error.”

Since the Fathers candidly profess this about themselves, some ought not
imperiously to thrust them upon us to create faith concerning matters of
faith—least of all the Romanists, who esteem the Fathers and their
testimonies as nothing when they openly oppose Roman doctrine.

I do not say this as though I dared detract anything from their proper
dignity and authority; I rise in respect at their names, antiquity,
learning, and piety. Nor do I envy or diminish the heroic undertakings and
excellent achievements of Jewel and the other Reformed theologians, both
British and foreign. From the first four ecumenical councils and the ancient
Fathers they undertook to confirm our doctrine, refute the Roman doctrine,
and answer step by step all testimonies drawn from them to establish it.
They labored successfully, and I venerate and admire their monuments and
trophies in this field. Conversely, I would not blame our Ames for generally
passing over testimonies of this kind in his *Bellarmine Enervated*.

[1679 printed p. 146 | IA leaf 154]

Nor do I greatly differ from those who think that this method of debating
the Romanists elevates human authority excessively, detracts something from
the divine authority and complete sufficiency of Scripture, gives our
adversaries more than is proper, and is less conducive to the salvation of
some—at least the weaker—when it draws them far away from the strongest
fortress of Scripture.

I pity godly souls when I see our martyrology press them more with testimonies
of the Fathers than with Scripture. I also tire of Aquinas throughout his
*Summa*, and the other Scholastics, taking support for their opinions more
often from the Doctors and Aristotle than from the Sacred Page, as though
our chief concern were not what Christ, the supreme Teacher and Father of
the age, said, but what other doctors thought. I certainly have neither so
servile a faith nor so servile a disposition that the mere “he himself said
it” of any Father, however holy and learned, is enough for divine faith. Yet
I do not wish to be numbered among those peevish and excessively unfair
evaluators of the Holy Spirit's gifts who, offended by excessive veneration
of the Fathers, cannot bear to hear or say anything about them.

The best course in all these matters is moderation. We do not reject the
writings and testimonies of the Fathers but venerate them. Yet we want them
subjected to the rule and authority of divine Scripture, examined and judged
according to it. We must always preserve this twofold distinction between
prophetic and apostolic writings and the writings of any other catholic and
holy men, ancient or recent:

1. Holy Scripture alone possesses infallible testimony. Other teachers can
fail, be deceived, and deceive.

2. Divinely inspired Scripture alone must be believed for its own sake. All
others are believed because of Scripture and their agreement with it.

I have now discussed this incidental point at greater length so that it may
serve as my apology if, in the remaining course of these lectures, I abstain
from explaining and vindicating such testimonies except when the matter
necessarily requires it.

This restraint is especially appropriate because in the controversy over
faith and justification the Fathers plainly agree with us on the substance
and chief point. They reject every confidence in our works and merits and
ascribe our justification and salvation wholly to God's grace and Christ's
merits applied to us. Yet before Pelagius arose and brought this and related
controversies with him, they sometimes spoke less carefully and loosely.
Even after the poison of Pelagius had spread too widely, Augustine and
others treated faith as mere knowledge and assent, and often confused
justification with sanctification in their use of the word. Consequently,
they did not always write or think as precisely according to the analogy of
Scripture as Luther, Chemnitz, and others of our writers have rightly noted,
however much the Romanists rage and gnash their teeth.

Having said more than enough in advance, let us briefly weigh the testimonies
of Jerome and Augustine which Bellarmine cites for his opinion.

First, as to Jerome, I wonder what moved Bellarmine to cite his letter to
Ctesiphon as though it supported his opinion.

[1679 printed p. 147 | IA leaf 155]

Nothing in it asserts any such thing at all, much less “openly,” as
Bellarmine claims. The nearest passage occurs in chapter 4. Although Jerome
does not deny that “God's commandments are possible,” he immediately adds:
“The Vessel of Election most openly teaches how this statement should be
understood: What the law could not do, in that it was weakened through the
flesh, God, sending his Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, condemned sin
in the flesh. And again, No flesh will be justified by works of the law. So
that you do not think this was said only of the law of Moses rather than all
the commandments comprehended under the single name ‘law,’ the same apostle
writes, ‘I consent to the law,’” and so forth.

All Jerome asserts here is that our entire capacity and ability to fulfill
God's commandments is attributed to Christ and his grace, while the law
universally is denied this power, because “no flesh will be justified by
works of the law.” Who can chisel justification by works of faith out of
this? Or at least the justification we are debating, which Paul discusses
in the cited passage—the justification consisting in forgiveness of sins
and the imputation of faith, as Paul describes it in the immediately
following chapter?

Perhaps they object that this proves Jerome interpreted Paul in Bellarmine's
way, which is sufficient for his proof. Bellarmine had undertaken to show
that Jerome openly teaches that “works” opposed to faith and excluded from
justification mean works preceding faith, performed by the powers of free
choice alone. I answer that nothing here is less open. If Jerome teaches it
at all, he teaches it very obscurely. He openly teaches only that by Christ's
grace we can fulfill God's commandments, not by works of law, and supports
this with Paul's words that no flesh is justified by works of law. If he
uses “justification” contrary to Paul's meaning for sanctification, he
follows the terminology of his age, when “justification” was dangerously
used for sanctification. The Romanists eventually dared, at the greatest
cost to this most precious article of faith, to confuse the things
themselves, producing such tragedies in the church.

Nearly the same answer applies to the three testimonies from Augustine:
*On Grace and Free Choice* 7; *On the Predestination of the Saints* 1.7; and
the preface to Psalm 31. In these passages, especially the last, Augustine
principally seeks to avoid two abysses and precipices, one on the left and
one on the right, between the confession of weakness and presumptuous
boldness.

First, while confessing our weakness we must not believe that faith will
save us if we persevere in evil works, merely because we believe. Augustine
rebukes this in the first passage, chapter 7 of *On Grace and Free Choice*;
this is the precipice on the left. Second, no one must presume upon his own
powers and attribute to himself—or, as Augustine says, to the dominion of
his own will—“that he can fulfill righteousness and perform everything
commanded in the law without offending in anything, and that he has his
life in his own power so that he never slips, fails, stumbles, or grows
dark.” This is the precipice on the right. Augustine warns against it when
he shows in *On the Predestination of the Saints* 1.7 that faith is required
before good works: “The apostle says that a person is justified by faith,
not by works, because

[1679 printed p. 148 | IA leaf 156]

faith itself is given first, and from it are obtained the remaining things
properly called works, in which one lives righteously.”

Augustine attacks both errors more broadly and sharply in his preface to
Psalm 31. Several statements of this kind occur there: “Do not presume to
reach God's kingdom by your righteousness; do not presume to sin because of
God's mercy.” “Let no one be proud outside the way; let no one be lazy in
the way.” “Therefore one must not believe without works, nor do works avail
without faith.” “Believe in the one who justifies the ungodly, so that your
good works can be good works, for I would not call them good so long as they
do not proceed from a good root.” He finally concludes, “Let no one boast of
his good works before faith; let no one be lazy in works after receiving
faith.”

We embrace both truths wholeheartedly and freely profess both that faith
precedes good works, which the Romanists contend together with us, and that
good works necessarily follow faith, which we assert with Augustine against
the Romanists' denial. But how does this prejudice us or the present
position? Does it follow that we are justified by works, which we deny with
Paul? Or that according to Augustine only works preceding faith are excluded
from justification, as Bellarmine contends? Not in the least.

1. Even if in Augustine's interpretation the apostle's words, “A person is
justified by faith without works,” exclude works preceding faith, it cannot
be concluded that in Paul's and Augustine's view works following faith are
not also excluded. Both can be excluded and, as we proved above, actually
are. Works before and after faith differ as widely as heaven and earth in
their nature and origin. Yet when applied to justify a person they agree in
this: their mode and account of justification makes works the material cause
of justification according to the old covenant of works, as Bellarmine's
party affirms, instead of making forgiveness of sins and the imputation of
Christ's righteousness the formal cause, with faith as the means or
instrument, according to the order of the covenant of grace which we affirm
with Paul.

2. Let us grant that Augustine, in common with his age, took “faith” as bare
assent to divine truth or an external profession, and used “justification”
in the broader sense common to his age, comprehending the whole state of
righteousness: both imputed righteousness in forgiveness of sins and
inherent righteousness in the infusion and operation of righteousness and
holiness. Nothing harmful to the substance of our position follows.

If justifying faith is improperly taken as bare assent or profession, we
readily confess that we are by no means justified by it alone and that it
can be separated from love and good works, and consequently from salvation.
If “justification” is improperly used for sanctification or the infusion of
righteousness and grace, we likewise confess that only works preceding faith
are excluded from such improperly named justification—that is, from
sanctification considered in itself—not the works that follow faith, which
are contained in sanctification and in which sanctification consists or at
least is exercised. But if we speak of Pauline justification

[1679 printed p. 149 | IA leaf 157]

and the works Paul excludes from it, we earnestly affirm that this
justification consists in forgiveness of sins and the imputation of Christ's
righteousness. Paul excludes all works, both preceding and following faith,
from its formal and material account. The very words of the psalm which
Augustine expounds there openly proclaim this, and Paul adduces them to prove
the same thing in Romans 4: “To the one who does not work but believes in
him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is imputed for righteousness. So
also David declares blessed the person to whom God imputes righteousness
without works, saying, Blessed are those whose iniquities are forgiven and
whose sins are covered; blessed is the man to whom the Lord does not impute
sin.”

Augustine himself asserts and presses this point there. In all the passages
Bellarmine adduces, his whole purpose is to show that God's grace precedes
and produces both good works and faith itself, no less freely than
effectively. Would that the Romanists and other defenders of free choice
asserted this as firmly as Augustine did.

Let this suffice concerning the testimonies of the Fathers which Bellarmine
adduced to prove that Paul excludes from justification only works preceding
faith, not those following it, from which Bellarmine and his fellow Romanists
maintain that a person is justified.
