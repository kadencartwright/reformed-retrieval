## QUESTION III

### On the month and day of the Nativity

There are four principal opinions here. The first fixes the Nativity on December 25; the second, at the end of September; the third, at the beginning of April; and the fourth judges both month and day indeterminable.

The principal opinion is that of the papists, who bind this decree of the Roman Church for celebrating the Nativity on December 25 upon Christian consciences with such tight bonds that Protestants are thereby compelled to examine its foundations more closely. When we ask for the arguments and reasons for this Roman faith, we are referred before all others to Baronius in his *Annals* and commentary on the *Roman Martyrology*. There we read that the entire Church of East and West, and all the Greek and Latin fathers, always believed so. Yet since the now familiar papist Gorgon of “the entire Church and all the fathers” does not frighten us, we wish to descend to the individual witnesses. The first introduced by Baronius is Clement of Rome, who in book 5, chapter 13, of the *Constitutions* allegedly affirms from the mouth of the apostle Peter that the Lord was born on December 25. Although I have gone through that entire fifth book, I can find nothing even similar. But granting that it is written at the passage cited, the reply is ready: that Clement is an openly spurious author, stitched together from various ravings, errors, and heresies, and never possessed any authority among sensible people, as Baronius himself, Sixtus of Siena, Possevino, and others of the papists acknowledge. Rivetus rightly observed this in *Sacred Criticism* 1.1.

Baronius’ second witness is Augustine in his exposition of Psalm 132 near the end. Reply. We acknowledge that by the days of Augustine, who died after the year 430 in the fifth Christian century, various errors and superstitions had crept into the Church. The very passage before us can serve as witness. There Augustine grounds his opinion about a December 25 Nativity on John 3:31, “He must increase, but I must decrease,” as though John, who had to decrease, was born at the summer solstice, after which the days begin to shorten, while Christ, who had to increase, was born at the winter solstice, after which the days begin to lengthen. The Jesuits themselves do not conceal that this foundation is empty and vain. Petavius, at the end of *The Doctrine of Times* 2.7, expressly acknowledges the nullity of this foundation. He would indeed have preferred that the fathers had built their opinion of the Nativity upon no reasons but the Church’s tradition, but their mind was very different. Augustine certainly speaks not a word about the Church’s tradition either in the passage before us or in the others cited by Baronius, but grounds the Church’s practice on the stated reasoning. Whenever the foundation is empty and null, as the adversaries themselves acknowledge it to be here, the entire structure built upon it must fall.

Baronius’ third witness is Gregory Nazianzen. Reply. I acknowledge that he delivered a most splendid panegyric at Constantinople for the feast of the Nativity. But apart—

—from overwhelming in it with all the floods of his eloquence—which was indeed supreme—the profane and superstitious Roman Church practices at that feast of the Nativity, practices employed at Rome to this day under the pope’s gaze and approval, to the great disgrace of the Christian name, I read in that lengthy panegyric, and even in Nicetas’ lengthy commentary upon it, not one word about December 25 or about that specific day being dedicated to the feast of the Theophany.

Baronius’ fourth witness is Chrysostom in his 31st discourse on various passages of the New Testament. Reply. We acknowledge that the entire discourse concerns the Nativity and that Chrysostom quite expressly celebrated the feast of the Nativity at Constantinople on December 25. Yet he also declares with utmost clarity—as the Jesuit Fronton du Duc notes in his Latin margin—that this feast had been introduced into the Greek Church only very recently, namely ten years before, and that until that day many doctors of the Greek Church had customarily condemned this innovation as vain and unjust. He himself professes that he embraces the innovation quite gladly, but let the adversaries state honestly whether he builds his judgment upon firm reasons.

First, he says, this new feast must be from God, because for ten full years the people have delighted to celebrate it in the greatest crowds. Surely the common people’s superstition persisting for many more than ten years does not prove a divine decree, much less a divine institution.

Second, he says, although this feast was new in the East, it existed “from the beginning” in the West, and at ancient Rome one could then read the very day of Christ’s birth in the archives in the census of Quirinius. Reply. Fronton du Duc falsely translated *anōthen* here as “from the beginning,” since it means nothing other than “above” or “previously” there. Chrysostom next assumes gratuitously and without any witness that in his own century anyone could have read the very day of Christ’s birth in the archives of ancient Rome. Tertullian, Justin Martyr, and others affirm that the year of Quirinius’ census, and thus the year of the Nativity, was preserved in the Roman archives, yet they said not a word about the day of the Nativity.

Third, Chrysostom’s chief argument, which he presses at greatest length, comes from Luke 1. He argues as follows: Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, was the high priest, who alone was permitted on Tishri 10, or Julian September 27, to enter the Holy of Holies and burn incense before the ark. After completing this rite Zechariah went home, and Elizabeth conceived at the beginning of October. Six months later, at the beginning of April, Christ was conceived by the Holy Spirit, according to Luke 1:21. He was therefore born nine months after the beginning of April. Reply. This demonstration of Chrysostom and the other fathers is so defective that Petavius himself acknowledges it is vain. Besides moving the Nativity from December into January, its single foundation is plainly false. Zechariah, the Baptist’s father, was not the high priest but, as Luke expressly states, one of the common priests who served at the altar in rotations and returned home after their weekly ministry. They offered incense only when chosen by lot—not all of them, certainly, but the few upon whom the lot imposed this part of the ministry. The angel also appeared to Zechariah not inside the veil at the ark, but outside it beside the altar of incense, where the priest selected by lot burned incense every morning and evening.

Baronius adduces no more witnesses except Anastasius, a later man of little authority, whom I believe has not yet been printed. From his manuscript Heinsius cites two fragments in his commentary on Clement of Alexandria, page 340. In each Anastasius quite expressly places the Nativity on December 25, but he errs so grossly about the world-year of the Nativity—as though it were beyond the six-thousandth—that no chronologist should give his testimony any authority.

Various of the most learned fathers can be adduced for the contrary opinion. Baronius concedes Clement of Alexandria, who in the first book of the *Stromata*, after its midpoint, places Christ’s birth on May 25, but replies that Clement speaks according to the heretics’ view and not his own. Pammelius replies differently, as can be seen in Heinsius’ notes at page 340: namely, that Clement’s “26 Pachon” is December 25, not May 25. This is certainly a great blunder by the very learned Pammelius. I marvel that Heinsius did not censure it, especially since in that same passage he correctly compares the Egyptian and Latin months from Bede, making Pachon correspond to May, Choiak to December, and Tybi to January.

As for Baronius’ reply, it is plainly false. Clement does not speak there according to the heretics’ opinion, but professes in his own name that it is a work not necessary but subtle and curious—as he says, *periergoteron*—to inquire not only into the year but also the day of the Nativity. Furthermore, those whom he says place the Nativity on Pachon 25 are by no means heretics. What he affirms concerning the Basilidian heretics is entirely different: namely, that they celebrated Christ’s baptism on Tybi 15, or on the eleventh of that month, which falls on January 6. Baronius assigns this opinion not to the heretics but to the orthodox in Egypt, both in his *Martyrology* and in his *Annals*.

Baronius also concedes Epiphanius to us in the same place. Although Epiphanius places Christ’s baptism at the beginning of November, he consistently—

—places the Nativity on Tybi 6 or 11 in January. Baronius does not deny these things, but affirms that Jerome overturns Epiphanius in Ezekiel 1. It is true that Jerome applies the first verse of that chapter to Christ’s baptism in his thirtieth year, on January 5, which according to the easterners is the fifth day of the fourth month. There in one word he censures Epiphanius’ opinion, which placed the feast of Epiphany on January 5. He also censures that of his own teacher Nazianzen, who with Epiphanius called Christ’s Nativity the Epiphany, when God appeared on earth, rather than, as Jerome had it, being darkened and hindered from appearing. Yet Jerome says not a word about the Nativity on December 25.

From what has been said it appears how vainly the papists openly proclaim here that all the fathers and the entire Church support the greatest of their feasts, the Nativity on December 25—which truly coincides with the pagans’ December Saturnalia. The Greek, Asian, and Egyptian churches and the patriarchal sees of Antioch, Alexandria, and Byzantium heard absolutely nothing about a December 25 Nativity until very late, namely after Chrysostom’s translation from his presbyterate at Antioch to the episcopate of Byzantium. Nor, if the spurious writers are excluded, do the papists cite from the Latins anyone earlier than Augustine who affirms that Christ was born on December 25.

The second opinion is that of Scaliger, Calvisius, and others, who profess that they demonstrate with elegant subtlety from the priests’ rotations that Christ was born at the end of September.

First Chronicles 24 makes clear that David divided the priestly families into twenty-four courses. Each in its turn, called a rotation, devoted itself to divine worship in the temple for a whole week, from Sabbath to Sabbath. Twenty-four weeks make 168 days. When this circuit was complete, the first priestly course began its rotation again, then the second and thereafter the rest in their firmly fixed order, unless some public calamity interrupted all the sacred rites. By the lot David cast, the first course fell to the family of Jehoiarib, the eighth to Abijah’s family, and the rest to the other families named in that passage. In 224 years all the rotations returned to the same days. Thus, on whatever day of the month the family of Jehoiarib began its rotation—for example, Adar 29 when Solomon dedicated the temple—the same family would begin its rotation again on that same day after 224 years. By means of this sacred cycle, therefore, the rotations of the individual families can be investigated with utmost certainty and clarity.

Besides the calamity of the Babylonian captivity, what brought confusion into this order was the idolatry of various kings of Judah and the neglect of divine worship and the priestly cycles in the temple, mentioned quite often in the reforms of Hezekiah, Josiah, and other kings. Yet Josephus testifies that from Judas Maccabaeus’ cleansing of the temple from Antiochus’ pollutions until the final siege of Jerusalem by Vespasian, the order of rotations was never interrupted. Judas restored the temple on Chislev 25 in the 148th year of the Greeks, according to 1 Maccabees 4:52. According to Scaliger, this was November 22 in Julian-period year 4,549. On that day, therefore, the first family, Jehoiarib’s, began its rotation; and according to the priestly cycle the eighth family, Abijah’s, did so fifty days later, namely January 10 in year 4,550. Once this starting point is fixed, the rotation of any one of the twenty-four families can be found with certainty at any future time according to the computation of the priestly cycle. Indeed, on July 14 in Julian-period year 4,783, the twenty-fourth family, Maaziah’s, should have begun its rotation. Owing to a lack of men consumed in war, it could not perform it, as Josephus observes at the final breakdown of the rotation, 233 years and 234 days after Judas Maccabaeus’ feast of dedication.

According to this computation of the priestly cycle, Zechariah entered upon the rotation of Abijah’s eighth course on July 21, 160 years and 192 days after Judas Maccabaeus’ feast of dedication, in Julian-period year 4,710. Zechariah’s rotation and liturgy were completed on July 28. After that day Zechariah returned home and Elizabeth conceived John the Baptist at the beginning of August. According to Luke she hid for five months, and when the sixth began, namely at the beginning of January, Mary conceived Christ. Nine months later, at the end of September or beginning of October, Christ was born near the autumnal equinox.

This subtle demonstration of Scaliger rests upon so many hypotheses that, if one falls, the whole structure collapses. It therefore appears sufficiently doubtful to me. Yet what Petavius or Spanhemius object against it is by no means of great weight. Both pronounce Scaliger’s principal hypothesis infirm: namely, that at his restoration of the temple Judas Maccabaeus first employed in sacred service the first priestly course, from Jehoiarib’s family. Reply. Scaliger’s reasons here are more than probable. In the solemn reformation of deeply corrupted divine worship, to which priestly course should he have assigned the first turns of ministry rather than to the one to which David by divine inspiration—

—had assigned them and to which that prerogative had belonged without dispute until that day? Furthermore, at the breakdown of the rotations’ continuing cycle, the order had reached the final, twenty-fourth course then failing on Tammuz 17, or July 15. Counting the rotations backward from that date to Chislev 25, or November 22, the feast of dedication of Judas Maccabaeus, it is found that the rotation belonged to the family of Jehoiarib.

Petavius’ second objection has the same value. He claims from Kepler that even if Scaliger’s computation through the priestly cycles stands, Zechariah’s ministry fell on September 18 and consequently Christ’s Nativity on December 25. Reply. This assertion of Petavius and Kepler is gratuitous and without proof. Indeed, if Scaliger’s computation stands—which Petavius grants is correct and makes no attempt to amend—it is impossible that Zechariah’s ministry, completed on July 28, began no earlier than September 18.

To those objections of Petavius Spanhemius adds four others. First, that Scaliger supposes Christ was born in Julian-period year 4,711 and baptized in the 31st year of his age, which are not certain. Reply. Those suppositions enter only incidentally. The argument proceeds from the days of the months, which hold in any years whatever.

Second, Spanhemius objects that Scaliger supposes Abijah’s family always held the eighth turn; yet at Nehemiah 12:4, 17, it sometimes held the eleventh and sometimes the twelfth turn of ministry. Reply. Spanhemius rashly confuses the order of the priestly families from this catalogue in Nehemiah. Will the family of Jehoiarib then fall from the first turn to the seventeenth because Joarib is numbered in seventeenth place at verse 6 of that chapter? Certainly the Joarib and Abijah in Nehemiah are entirely different from David’s Jehoiarib and Abijah, to whose families he assigned the first and eighth turns in the temple rotations. The former lived in Nehemiah’s days, just as the latter lived in David’s. To which families Nehemiah’s Joarib and Abijah belonged is not established, for Nehemiah is silent about it. Moreover, Nehemiah numbers the heads of only twenty-two priestly families, and does so without any relation to the temple ministry but only to the dedication of Jerusalem’s wall.

Third, Spanhemius objects that even if Zechariah returned home on July 28, it does not follow that John was conceived immediately. Reply. Scaliger does not press the precise day of either John’s or Christ’s conception. Indeed, he professes the greatest uncertainty about the day. He only maintains that John was conceived soon after Zechariah’s rotation was complete, and no cause for a great latitude or delay can reasonably be supposed here.

Finally Spanhemius objects that, granting John was conceived at the end of July, it would follow that Christ was born not at the end of September, as Scaliger holds, but at the end of October. Reply. If Christ was born in October, Scaliger will not be far from his purpose. Yet from what Spanhemius grants here, Scaliger exactly attains his aim. For if John was conceived at the end of July or beginning of August, and Christ was conceived when the sixth following month began, namely at the beginning of January, Christ must have been born at the end of September or beginning of October.

The third opinion is Nisbet’s. He holds that Christ was born on Passover day itself and at the very hour when the Passover was sacrificed, on page 274 of his little book. For this paradox the learned man names no author. His principal reasons are these. First, that in Luke 2:41–42 Christ is said to be twelve years old at the feast of Passover. He must therefore have been born at the feast of Passover. Reply. Neither the antecedent nor the consequence should be conceded. Not the antecedent, because Luke does not there assert that Christ became twelve years old at the feast of Passover, but that when he had become twelve years old, he went up to Jerusalem at Passover. Then there is no consequence. Even if it had been said that he became twelve years old at Passover, it would not follow that either his twelfth or his first year began at Passover. In Christ’s twelfth year his parents celebrated Passover, and indeed also Pentecost, the Feast of Tabernacles, and all the other solemn feasts. Yet it does not follow that his twelfth year began either at Pentecost, at the Feast of Tabernacles, or at any other feast that he or his parents observed during that twelfth year.

Nisbet’s second argument. The end of Daniel’s seventy weeks, and consequently Christ’s death and the close of his final year, was at Passover. Therefore the beginning of that final year of Christ and of all the years of Daniel’s seventy weeks was at the feast of Passover or on a day close to it. Reply. This overly subtle argument is not solid. Besides what we demonstrated above—that Christ died not at the end of Daniel’s weeks but in the middle of the final week—even if it is granted that he died at the end and that the final year of the weeks closed at Passover, it does not follow that any of the years either of Christ or of Daniel’s weeks began at Passover. Scripture is silent about the day or month when the revelation of the weeks was made to Daniel, nor is the beginning of the weeks taken from the time—

—of their revelation. Nor did Passover begin any Jewish year or month. The learned man needed to prove many things that he did not touch before he could establish this argument.

He acknowledges that his remaining arguments are only probable. Indeed, the foremost chronologists have pronounced them so improbable that I do not think I should delay to examine them.

The fourth opinion, which we embrace, is Spanhemius’: the month and day of the Nativity ought not and cannot be determined by anyone, since Scripture is silent about them and nothing certain about them was put forward by anyone in the first centuries of the Christian Church. To establish this opinion nothing needs to be added to what has been said above. We depart from Spanhemius only in this: although he holds that Christ’s Nativity is buried by Scripture and earliest antiquity, he nevertheless thinks that December 25, or even another day, may usefully and piously be set apart by the Church to celebrate it. Surely on those feast days it does not become Protestants to embrace the superstitious remnants of the papists, nor to be wise beyond Holy Scripture in rites. The excessive accommodation of many most excellent men in trifles of this kind has disturbed the churches with many deeply grievous controversies.
