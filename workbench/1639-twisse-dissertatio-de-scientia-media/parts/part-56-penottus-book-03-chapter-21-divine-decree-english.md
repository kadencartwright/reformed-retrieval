ON

GABRIEL PENOTTUS’S

*Bulwark of Human Liberty*,

BOOK THREE, CHAPTER XXI.

Whether the ground of infallible knowledge of future contingents can be gathered from the efficacious
decree of the divine will.

It will be worthwhile to observe how Penottus conducts himself when he reviews the various opinions of
the theologians in this argument. “The first opinion,” he says, “affirms absolutely that all future
contingents, both those which arise from free secondary causes and those which arise from other natural
causes, are certainly and infallibly known in the divine decree alone by which he decreed that they
would come to pass. Nevertheless,” he says, “the authors of this opinion differ among themselves,
because some affirm that this alone is a sufficient ground for knowing future contingents and that no
other is sufficient, and so forth. The second opinion concerning the same doubt absolutely denies that
the divine decree, however efficacious, is a sufficient ground for knowing the future contingents
themselves if the presence of future contingents in the now of eternity is prescinded from.”

Indeed, the man of Novara wishes by some distinction to break the force of the preceding opinion, which
he hates insofar as he is wholly engaged in defending human liberty, and perhaps all the more because he
observes that the preceding opinion pleased Calvin in book 3 of the *Institutes*, chapter 23, § 6, as
Penottus notes. Thus he would render that opinion more hateful to his Papists even on this account. For
Calvin professes that GOD foresees the things which are future for no other reason than that he decreed
that they should come to pass. Therefore, lest too many Papists appear to agree with the same opinion
which Calvin also embraces, he conducts himself so as to shake the authority of this opinion, as much
as he can, by the disagreement of the Papist theologians. Accordingly, he says that not a few of those
who acknowledge the divine decree, however efficacious, nevertheless deny that this decree is a
sufficient ground for knowing future contingents, and so forth. How truly and modestly Penottus has said
this we shall presently examine.

For now it will suffice to show that this diversity of opinions does nothing to obstruct the authority
of the preceding opinion, at least in Penottus’s own judgment, since Penottus himself professes in his
second proposition that “the divine decree is a sufficient and even proximate ground for knowing all
future contingents whose cause is either the divine will alone, or whose cause is created insofar as it
is physically predetermined by the divine will for producing them.” Therefore, all who acknowledge
predetermination of this sort—as they must, provided they acknowledge the decree of God to be
efficacious, however efficacious—and yet deny that this decree is a sufficient ground for knowing future
contingents, do not judge rightly even in Penottus’s opinion but pass over into an opinion alien to the
truth. Nor is it at all fair that by their error they should detract anything from the weight of the
preceding opinion. Yet how truly and modestly Penottus invents such a distinction we shall presently
show. But first

[Book I, printed p. 124; BSB canvas 00150]

another artifice of Penottus must be observed, for the man of Novara accomplishes everything by devices,
not by arguments.

Observe how slender a piper of votes he represents as supporting the preceding opinion. “Scotus thinks
so,” he says, “as he is explained by some. Rada explains him thus in controversy 30, article 3, notable
5, at the end and in conclusion 5; Luis de Molina, q. 14, art. 13, disputation 15; and Aquarius in his
additions to Capreolus on 1, distinction 38.” But does not almost the whole school of Scotists and
Dominicans also explain Scotus in this way? “But,” says Penottus, “if Scotus’s words are carefully
inspected, he does not say that future contingents are known in the divine decree, but in the divine
essence, which nevertheless does not represent them unless the divine decree is added.”

But, I say, what else is it for future contingents to be known in the divine decree than for them to be
known in the divine essence as it is willing, as Durandus speaks—that is, with the divine decree added;
or insofar as the divine essence is partly the likeness of things, according as they are known by God
with respect to their possible quiddity, and partly their active principle, according as they are known
by God under the class of future things? Aquinas expressly sets out these modes of knowing singulars in
God’s very essence in the first part, q. 14, art. 11.

Certainly, when it is asked how GOD knows future contingents, the only thing asked is how the things
which are contingent by their nature—that is, which hold themselves indifferently toward opposites—become
future, and consequently become knowable under the class of future things. Once this has been
ascertained, we shall have no doubt that by that very fact they are comprehended by an infinite
intellect, and this without any discourse or reasoning which proceeds in this manner: “Whatever has been
decreed by God is future; this has been decreed by God; therefore it is future.” To remove reasoning of
this sort from GOD, Scotus says that, when God’s decree is added, the divine essence represents
contingents as future, and thus they are inspected by GOD as future.

“Having seen,” he says, “the contingency of things with respect to existence, and having considered this
in relation to the divine will, it remains to see the second principal matter: how the certainty of his
knowledge stands together with this. This can be posited in two ways. In one way, by the divine intellect
seeing the determination of the divine will—namely that a thing will be because the will determines that
it will be, for it knows that will to be immutable and unimpedible. Or it may be posited in another way,
since the former way appears to posit a certain discourse in the divine intellect, as though from an
intuition of the will’s determination and its immutability it concluded that this will be. The divine
intellect either offers to the will simple things whose union is contingent in reality, or, if it offers
a complex, it offers it as neutral to itself; and the will, choosing one side—namely the conjunction of
these things for some now in reality—makes this determinately true: ‘This will be.’ But when this is
determinately true, the essence is the ground for the divine intellect’s understanding that truth; and
this is so naturally, so far as concerns the essence, once the determination of the divine will by
which they become true is posited.”

Therefore Scotus retracts nothing from his opinion when he maintains that future contingents cannot be
known except through the determination of the divine will and that this determination is a sufficient
ground for knowing future things. He only shows how, through the determination of the divine will, they
are represented to the divine intellect as future. Nor would any sober person expect the determination
of the divine will to be called a ground for knowing future contingents in any other manner than by
constituting them knowable under the class of future things, from which they would be represented to the
divine intellect through God’s essence as future.

Penottus adds: “The same opinion is attributed to Durandus on distinction 38, q. 3; Richard there, q. 1;
Hervaeus, John Major, and certain others, concerning whom, however, he says that it is not so certain
that they speak prescisively”—that is, as Penottus adds in the next number, with “the presence of future
contingents in the now of eternity prescinded from.” But it is most certain that Durandus expressly
overthrows this presence of future contingents, 1, distinction 38, q. 3, nos. 12–14.

“Some say,” he writes, “that for this cognition it is required that created things be present to GOD
with respect to their own proper and actual existences. But they explain in this way how future
contingents can be present to GOD from eternity according to their proper and actual existences:
eternity is an indivisible duration existing all at once, and therefore whatever coexists with eternity
coexists with the whole of eternity. But it is evident that all things which come to pass temporally
coexist with eternity,” and so forth. “But here there appears to be a twofold defect. One concerns the
claim that future things are present to God from eternity according to their actual being, because what
is nothing in itself cannot really and actually coexist with anything else. For to coexist presupposes
to exist,” and so forth.

Penottus chiefly relies on this very argument to overthrow that presence of things in the now of
eternity, and he also professes that he borrowed it from Durandus, as may be seen above in Chapter XVI,
number 5: “That this doctrine is not true in itself”—namely the doctrine of the presence of things in
eternity—“I prove by an argument virtually suggested by Durandus which plainly convinces my intellect
and which does not yet appear to have been specifically answered by any author. The argument is this:
that which really does not exist in itself cannot truly coexist with anything else.” With what face,
then, does he number Durandus among those concerning whom he says that it is “not so certain whether they
speak precisively”?

Again, although Hervaeus was a Thomist, he is nevertheless commonly alleged by the Jesuits as one who
wished to vindicate Thomas himself from an opinion of this sort—namely the presence of things in eternity,
which he judges heterodox and erroneous—as may be seen in Suárez, *On the Absolute Knowledge of Future
Contingents*, book 1, chapter 7, number 19, and also in Vázquez, disputation 64 on the first part, q. 14,
chapter 5, number 33. Finally, Penottus himself numbers Hervaeus among those who thought that this opinion,
so understood, never entered Thomas’s mind, but that “he spoke of the presence of things to GOD in
eternity according to their objective being. Hervaeus and Hispalensis,” he says, “thought thus,” and so
forth, above in Chapter XVI, number 2. But Penottus also acknowledges others who, although they say by
other modes too that GOD knows

[Book I, printed p. 125; BSB canvas 00151]

future contingents—namely in their presence or in
the ideas—nevertheless acknowledge that “in the efficacious decree by which from eternity he predefined
all future contingents, GOD can sufficiently know the same things. So think,” he says, “the more recent
interpreters of Thomas: Báñez on the first part, q. 19, art. 8; Nazarius on q. 14, art. 23, controversy 2,
conclusion; and Alvarez, disputations 10 and 11, *On Aids*.” Navarrete may be added to these; indeed, may
not almost all the other Dominicans be added too?

But observe, I ask, how they also defend the other modes—whether in the presence of the future things
themselves, in the ideas, or in the determination of secondary causes—only upon presupposing the
determination of the divine will. Thus it is true not only that the determination of the divine will is
a sufficient ground for knowing future contingents, but that it is so necessary that, if it is excluded,
no other mode exhibits a sufficient ground for knowing them.

But I cannot sufficiently wonder why he has numbered Vázquez the Jesuit, a patron of middle knowledge,
among those Dominicans. Why should Vázquez not return equal favor and judge that Penottus too should be
added to the Dominicans? But I smell the devices of that Italian. Perhaps he wishes to cast into his
readers the suspicion that defenders of human liberty have no more to fear from the opinion of the
Dominicans on this point than from the Jesuits themselves, the principal assertors of middle knowledge.

It remains to examine the good faith with which he proceeds in assigning the assertors of the second
opinion, by which it is denied that the divine decree, however efficacious, is a sufficient ground for
knowing the future contingents themselves if the presence of future contingents in the now of eternity
is prescinded from. First, he writes that Ariminensis, Ockham, and Gabriel hold this opinion. Gabriel is
at hand, and in him I see Ockham’s opinion represented, from which, unless my conjecture deceives me,
Ariminensis’s opinion does not differ. Yet in my judgment nothing of this sort can be gathered either
from Gabriel or from Ockham’s opinion as Gabriel reports it.

After Scotus’s opinion is proposed, Gabriel says, “Against this position Ockham argues.” I undertook to
overthrow in a certain digression the arguments he exhibits from Ockham in my examination of the
Pelagian history compiled by Gerardus Vossius. But he also adds Ockham’s opinion, which—if we believe
Gabriel—does not require the presence of things with God in the now of eternity altogether for the
cognition of future contingents. “Therefore,” he says, “Ockham speaks otherwise, although he says that
the mode by which GOD evidently and with certitude knows all future contingents cannot be sufficiently
expressed for the condition of this life.” Let anyone consider whether this is to establish God’s
cognition of future contingents from their presence with God in eternity.

Gabriel continues communicating Ockham’s opinion to us: “He says that God himself, or the divine
essence, is an intuitive cognition both of himself and of all future and present things, so perfect and
clear that it is also evident knowledge of past, future, and present things. Thus, just as from intuitive
knowledge of the extremes our intellect can evidently know certain contingent propositions, so the
divine essence itself is a certain cognition and knowledge by which not only a necessary and contingent
truth about the present is known, but it is also known which side of a contradiction will be true and
which false. And perhaps this is not on account of the determination of his will. Rather, posit the
impossible: that with divine cognition itself existing just as it now does, the will were not the
efficient cause, either total or partial, of contingent effects.”

Here no mention occurs of the presence of things in eternity as required in any respect to establish
God’s cognition of future contingents. I acknowledge that they teach that future contingents would be
known to God even if, impossibly, he were not willing. Nor would I consider this to be denied, upon the
supposition that his intellect is infinite and that the contingents were future. But since contingents
hold themselves indifferently toward opposites and by their nature are no more future than nonfuture,
Ockham by no means explained how, when God’s will is excluded, contingents would pass from things merely
possible into the condition of future things. But neither could this have been explained by Ockham, nor
can it be explained today by the Jesuits, who philosophize about future things without any foundation.
They attribute determinate truth to them, which clearly cannot be had from themselves, yet they do not
refer it to the divine will as its cause, lest in this way they prejudice human liberty.

I say nothing of Capreolus, because he is not at hand, but it does not seem probable to me that anything
of the sort slipped from Capreolus. Yet I see from Navarrete whence an appearance of such an opinion may
arise—if not one by which a learned man may come to harbor such a suspicion concerning Capreolus’s
opinion, at least one by which trust might be given to an impostor and simple and unwary readers could
be mocked by these devices. For Navarrete too acknowledges that a presence of things of this sort in
eternity is required to constitute divine cognition under the class of intuitive cognition, not simply
under the class of cognition, as though the determination of the divine will were not sufficient to
establish cognition as belonging to God. Even as concerns the formal ground of intuitive knowledge, I
by no means accede to this opinion of Navarrete, which I have already taken pains to explode above.

From an inspection of Capreolus, who came into my hands after I had dictated these things, it becomes
manifest that Penottus wrongs Capreolus. For the first conclusion of the second article in Capreolus is
this: “A contingent as it is future cannot be infallibly known, but only according as it is in the
present.” But if a future contingent becomes present to God through God’s will, then according to
Capreolus—whose contrary Penottus has not yet demonstrated—it will follow that God’s decree is a
sufficient ground for knowing future things, and this from Capreolus’s own opinion. When Capreolus
responds to Durandus, he indeed denies that future contingents, for example future free actions, can be
known in a cause; but by “cause” there he understands the secondary, not the first, cause—the created,
not the uncreated, will—as is manifest from the text itself. It also seems likely that Palatius, Francis
of Christ, and Calarinus, all Dominicans unless I am mistaken, agree with Capreolus in the same opinion.
Ferrariensis, Hispalensis, and Aquarius are likewise Dominicans. As for Cajetan,

[Book I, printed p. 126; BSB canvas 00152]

Penottus merely has him inclining to it, and he does not hold even this for established, but says that
Cajetan appears to incline. Yet from inspecting the passage in Cajetan, I perceive no trace of this
conjecture. “Future contingents,” he says, “are known by God as they are actually in themselves and as
they are in their causes; but certainly in the first mode, and in the second mode only insofar as they
are knowable.” Perhaps Penottus relies on this as intimating that Cajetan thinks future contingents are
not certainly known from causes, among which God’s will must be numbered. Let that be granted;
nevertheless, from Cajetan in the same place Penottus knows very well that this uncertainty arises only
from the indetermination of the causes. If the causes are determinate, future contingents can be known
no less certainly as they are in causes—provided, I say, that they are determinate—than when they are
known as they are in themselves.

But one may also wonder here that he again places Suárez the Jesuit together with these Dominicans, as
though the Jesuit doctrine of middle knowledge, in which all the fortunes of Penottus’s entire
dissertation are placed, could have stood together with the doctrine of the Dominicans, who reject that
middle knowledge and refer the foundation of divine cognition of future contingents to the
determination of the divine will.

He makes the third opinion that of those who deny that effects freely depending upon the created will
can be known in the divine decree. But he wholly conceals the authors of this opinion, since they must
all be Jesuits, or at least Pelagians. From here he proceeds to his propositions.

PENOTTUS.

“The divine decree is a ground without which all future contingents cannot be known. For among all wise
men it is certain that the principles of being and of knowing are the same. But the divine decree is
the principle of being of all future contingents, as Augustine says in the *Enchiridion*, chapter 95,
and Prosper in the book of sentences excerpted from Augustine, proposition 58. Hence the argument for
this assertion can be formed thus: Nothing is certainly knowable by God which does not have true being
and determinate truth. But no contingent, from whatever cause it proceeds, can have true and determinate
being without the divine decree. Therefore without the divine decree no future contingent is certainly
knowable.”

RESPONSE.

There is no reason for me to resist Penottus in this proposition, unless perhaps I bring a suit against
him over his rather inexact method. He says it is certain among all wise men that the principles of
being and knowing are the same. But, I say, this maxim both needs exposition and, when rightly
expounded, can be so applied that it does nothing for the present purpose. The word “knowing” has an
active signification; therefore a principle of knowing is the principle by which someone knows. Again,
cognition is twofold, sensory and intellectual. We know the principle of sensory cognition to be the
sensitive soul, and the principle of intellectual cognition to be rational nature. But we grant that
both the sensitive and the rational soul are principles of being—but for what? Only for animals and
human beings. Yet Penottus philosophizes about principles of being which are principles of being for
all things.

But I interpret Penottus in this sense: that by “principles of knowing” he wishes to understand the
principles through which things are known. Yet here too there is room for a multiple distinction: the
principle through which a thing is known can be understood either in the class of an efficient cause,
and such principles are nothing other than the principles of cognizing natures; or in the class of a
formal cause, so that those things are called principles of cognition through cognition of which we
arrive at cognition of the things themselves. I judge that Penottus must be understood in this manner.

Again, cognition of a thing is manifold. There is cognition that it is, cognition of what it is, and
cognition on account of what it is. Yet cognition of a future thing is distinguished from all these.
The man of Novara passes over all these things without distinction. For me to know that a thing is
present, it is not altogether required that I know its principles of being. For cognition of this sort
comes about by simple intuition of the thing. Penottus wishes future things to be known by God in this
way, namely each one in its presence, and that from eternity. Moreover, cognition of a thing through
causes really distinct from the thing itself—such as cognition of a future thing through the divine
decree—appears to be discursive cognition, a kind of cognition which by no means falls within God or can
be proved of him.

Further, the proposition merely professes that future contingents cannot be known without the divine
decree, but the argument immediately subjected to it has the appearance of proving affirmatively that
future contingents are known through the divine decree. For your premises have been expressly posited:
“The principles of being and knowing are the same; but the divine decree is the principle of being of
all future contingents.” From these you could have added a conclusion of the same kind in this form:
“Therefore the divine decree is the principle of knowing all future contingents.” Although this appears
favorable to our opinion, it is by no means approved by me. For I hold that through the divine decree
contingents are only made knowable under the class of future things. But once it is posited that a thing
is knowable, it must be comprehended by an infinite intellect under whatever class it is knowable.

The passage you cite from Augustine’s *Enchiridion*, chapter 95, is this: “Therefore nothing comes to
pass unless the Almighty wills it to come to pass, either by permitting that it come to pass or by
bringing it about himself.” From this you infer, and rightly, that the divine decree is the principle of
being of all future contingents. Indeed, I congratulate you for this reasoning of yours from this
passage of Augustine, since you acknowledge that according to Augustine nothing comes to pass unless
God wills it—not only the things which God does but also the things which he permits to come to pass do
not come to pass unless God wills it. The same is true of Prosper’s statement from the book of sentences
excerpted from Augustine, proposition 58: “The will of God is the first and highest cause of all bodily
and spiritual motions.”

But I acknowledge that your latter argument, which you form thus, is more suited to the present purpose:
“Nothing is certainly knowable by God which does not have true and determinate being; but no contingent,
from whatever cause it proceeds, can have true and determinate being without the divine decree.
Therefore without the divine decree no future contingent is certainly knowable.” Nevertheless, I have
a few things to oppose even against this syllogism. First, it is said too loosely that no contingent has
determinate being without the divine decree. For this clause, “without the divine decree,” admits a
twofold sense, according as “without” can be expounded either as an adjunct or as a cause. If it proceeds
of “without the decree” only as an adjunct, it is also true that no necessary thing has

[Book I, printed p. 127; BSB canvas 00153]

determinate being without God’s decree, for God’s decree is also eternal and coeternal from eternity
with every truth, however necessary. Therefore, for the argument to proceed suitably, it must proceed
from the exclusion of the decree not merely as an adjunct but as a cause. And since you do not wish
God’s decree to be the total but only a partial cause of that determinate truth which contingents have
from eternity, it follows from your opinion that besides God’s decree another cause must be invented
which causes from eternity that determinate truth, although it itself by no means existed from eternity.
For beyond all controversy nothing outside God himself is eternal.

Second, a contingent also has determinate truth without the divine decree as its cause, for a thing to
be possible by its nature is for it to have determinate truth, just as God is able to produce that thing.
But the divine decree is by no means the cause of this determinate truth. You ought to have said that no
contingent has determinate being under the class of a future thing apart from the divine decree as the
cause of that determination. But without doubt it has determinate truth under the class of a possible
thing without God’s decree as its cause.

II. Let the second proposition pass as undoubted, namely: “If in time God efficaciously and physically
predetermines all contingent and free secondary causes toward their effects, the eternal decree itself
so to predetermine secondary causes in time toward contingent effects is a sufficient and even proximate
ground for the divine intellect’s certainly and infallibly knowing all future contingents.” I also
acknowledge that the argument which you add is demonstrative, proceeding in this form: “Every effect is
sufficiently and perfectly known in its proper, determinate, efficacious, and infallible cause. But the
divine will is the proper, infallible, and altogether efficacious cause with respect to all those
contingents. Therefore,” and so forth. Then why, I ask, will it not be equally true that God infallibly
knows contingent effects also in the determination of secondary causes, provided it is not to be
impeded? Again, unless I am mistaken, all the philosophers to a man acknowledge every cognition of this
sort to be discursive, which neither does nor can fall within God.

III. The third proposition: “Certain and infallible knowledge cannot be had of those effects which were
not efficaciously predefined from eternity by an antecedent divine decree and whose causes were not
predefined antecedently in time, physically, by the divine decree itself. Rather, it must be sought from
other causes.” I readily acknowledge that things which were not decreed at all cannot be known through
the divine decree. In our judgment such are all things which are not predefined by the divine decree,
for in our judgment the conceit that God’s decree is subordinated to foresight of the disposition of
secondary causes is conceived beyond all sobriety. Yet we do not grant that cognition of those things
must be sought from other causes, as you slyly whisper after your fashion. Rather, we deny that any
cognition of that kind is to be attributed to God which is sought from secondary causes apart from
their subordination to the first cause.

IIII. The fourth proposition: “Speaking absolutely and simply, just as the divine decree is not the
total and in every way adequate cause and ground of being for contingent effects which depend upon free
secondary causes, so the divine decree is not an adequate and by itself sufficient ground in every way
for knowing them certainly and infallibly while prescinding from their actual presence. This proposition
persuades me first on account of Thomas’s authority. For although in so many places he mentions knowledge
and the mode of knowing future contingents, he never said that they are known by God in the divine
decree, but recurs to other modes and other grounds. And I think everyone grants that he never said this
explicitly and in formal words, since no one has yet cited a passage in which he says it. But that he
does not say it implicitly either, we shall show below in resolving the arguments.”

RESPONSE.

He completes this proposition by making a comparison between the divine operation in producing the
effects of secondary causes and the divine decree. I do not understand to what end this is done, unless
he would covertly suggest an argument taken from the comparison to establish the latter member of this
proposition, in which all its weight is placed. But that argument is utterly unsuited to prove this.
First, it applies equally to effects of necessary and free causes. For just as God does not produce an
act of willing in a human being unless the human being concurs in eliciting the same act, so too God
does not produce an act of burning unless he cooperates with fire to produce the burning. Yet I do not
believe Penottus would say that this in any way prevents God from knowing necessary future things from
his decree.

Second, although God is not a total but a partial cause when he concurs in producing a free act in a
human being, nevertheless, if not only the free act but also the human will’s determination of itself is
subjected to divine predefinition, the partiality of God’s concurrence toward the free effect will in no
way prevent God from knowing that same effect in his decree. But since they are accustomed to proceed
cautiously, because they conduct the matter more craftily than solidly, it will be worthwhile to observe
the qualification according to which this proposition of the man of Novara proceeds. “The divine
decree,” he says, “is not a sufficient ground for infallibly knowing future contingents while prescinding
from their actual presence.”

But, I say, to know future contingents is nothing other than to know their presence. And what sane person
would expect someone to say that from his decree God knows future contingents according to their
presence while prescinding from their presence? It is as if someone were to say that God cannot know
that this old trickster Navarrete will be, while prescinding from his trickster’s craft. Perhaps someone
will reply here that this proposition holds concerning presence premised to divine cognition, but not
concerning presence as the subject of divine cognition. But anyone who argues thus does so in vain. For
since cognition of future contingents belongs to God from eternity, according to Penottus’s opinion no
presence of them antecedent to divine cognition is posited. For the presence of future things which
Penottus makes the object of divine cognition is their presence in time, not in the now of eternity, as
the Dominicans philosophize. And beyond all controversy,

[Book I, printed p. 128; BSB canvas 00154]

the actual presence of future things in the measure of time by no means prevents them from being known
in God’s decree. For the things which depend upon the divine will alone for their production in the
nature of things also actually exist at some time, and yet even in Penottus’s judgment this in no way
prevents them from being known in the divine decree.

But I readily see what you wish to press, on account of the favor with you which the Jesuit doctrine of
middle knowledge has attained. You wish to say that the future actions of the human will were by no means
sufficiently known by God from eternity in his decree: neither Pharaoh’s release of the Israelites from
Egypt, nor Cyrus’s release of the Jews from Babylonian captivity; neither Peter’s repentance after his
denial of the Lord, nor Paul’s conversion to the faith, nor any good work proceeding from grace however
great was sufficiently foreknown by God in God’s decree either to move creatures naturally toward
natural acts or to move them morally through efficacious grace toward gracious acts.

And, as you say, this proposition is principally rendered probable to you by Thomas’s authority—not so
much because he affirms this very thing as because he is silent about the contrary. But, I say, it used
to be said that a negative argument from authority, especially human authority, has no force. Come,
then, let us see how true Penottus’s statement is that Aquinas never said that future contingents are
known by God in the divine decree.

First, Aquinas’s opinion is most explicit: God sees all other things in himself and in his essence, and
that as in their cause, which is the same as if he had said that God sees all things in his will—that is,
in his decree. In the first part, q. 14, art. 5, in the body: “God understands himself perfectly. But if
something is known perfectly, its power must be known perfectly. Yet the power of any thing cannot be
known perfectly unless the things to which its power extends are known. Hence, since the divine power
extends to other things because it is the first efficient cause of all beings, God must know things
other than himself. And this becomes even more evident if it is added that the very being of the first
agent cause, namely God, is his understanding. Hence whatever effects preexist in God as in the first
cause must be in his very act of understanding.” And a little afterward: “God sees himself in himself,
because he sees himself through his essence. But he sees things other than himself not in themselves
but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the likeness of things other than himself.”

From these things observe that God sees things other than himself not by seeing them in themselves but
by seeing them in himself. This includes future contingents, and that because they preexist in him,
namely before they exist—a phrase used only with respect to future things. Again, he sees all things in
himself as in their cause, and as they preexist in God as in their first cause. But God’s essence as
essence is not the cause of things, nor is it as knowing when the will is excluded. For we do not make
the things which we know unless we will them, first part, q. 19, art. 11. Rather, he is the cause of
things as willing. Therefore to see things in himself as in their cause is nothing other than to see
things in his will—that is, in his decree.

But God sees his effects in himself as in their cause, and all contingents are God’s effects, as
Penottus acknowledges in number 9: “The divine decree,” he says, “is the principle of being of all future
contingents.” Therefore he also sees all future contingents in himself as in their cause—that is, in his
will or in the divine decree.

See also what Aquinas has in the first part, q. 16, art. 7, reply 3: “That which now is was future before
it existed because it was in its cause that it would come to be.” But, I say, this holds no more of that
which now is than of that which once was or will someday be. Therefore it is true of all contingents that
they were future before they existed because it was in their cause that they would come to be. But this
cause can be imagined as nothing other than God’s will, for he immediately signifies that this cause is
just as everlasting as God himself when he says: “Hence it does not follow from this that it was always
true that the things which are were future, except insofar as it was in an everlasting cause that they
would be future, and that cause is God alone.” Therefore the cause why something was future from eternity
is not to be sought outside God but in God himself. Yet in God himself nothing can be imagined as the
cause why things were future from eternity except God’s will—that is, the divine decree.

Again, first part, q. 14, art. 11: “Since God is the cause of things through his knowledge”—but knowledge
is not the cause of things except according as it has the will conjoined, q. 14, art. 8, and knowledge
is not the cause of the things which come to pass except through the will, q. 19, art. 11—“God’s knowledge
extends as far as his causality extends. Hence, since God’s active power extends not only to the forms
from which the account of the universal is taken but even to matter, God’s knowledge must extend to
singulars, which are individuated through matter. For since he knows things other than himself through
his essence, insofar as it is the likeness of things or insofar as it is their active principle, his
essence must be a sufficient principle of knowing all things which are made through him, not only in the
universal but also in the singular.”

Here let it be observed that the sufficiency of the principle for knowing things outside God is sought
from God’s essence as it is their cause or principle, partly exemplary and partly active. This active
principle of things can be nothing other than God’s will. Anyone who replied that Aquinas says these
things about cognition of present, not future, singulars would labor in vain, since by everyone’s
confession God knows things done no otherwise than things to be done.

But let Penottus proceed. “And that this is Thomas’s mind is confirmed because, since Thomas labored so
greatly to discover the ground by which the divine intellect infallibly knows future contingents, he
would not have omitted the easiest ground, accessible to everyone, which can be taken from the divine
decree, if he had believed it sufficient for knowing those future contingents infallibly. For what is
more easily perceived than that in the efficacious decree of his will God can understand the things
which he decreed to do?”

RESPONSE.

Evidently we shall by no means satisfy Penottus unless we also break his suspicions. Therefore we shall
take pains, if it can be done, to shake even these suspicions from him. From the things already said we
leave it for the prudent reader to consider whether Aquinas omitted this ground rather than expressing
it quite explicitly. “But,” you say, “he does not mention this ground when he inquires concerning future
contingents how they are infallibly foreknown by God.” Nor is it at all astonishing, since Aquinas was
about to

[Book I, printed p. 129; BSB canvas 00155]

demonstrate that the infallibility of divine knowledge conspires very well with the contingency of
future things; and, I acknowledge, he could not accomplish this better and more easily than by founding
the infallibility of divine knowledge in the presence of things with God. For a necessity consequent upon
the existence of a thing is judged to prejudice nothing at all in that thing’s contingency. Again, by
seeing things as present, a way plainly lies open to infallible comprehension of them. And from the
objections which he proposes to himself for resolution in q. 14, art. 13, it becomes manifest that
Aquinas has this purpose: that God’s very foreknowledge should not place an obstacle before the
contingency of things. How much more would an obstacle be placed by God’s determining will, one not so
easily removed?

See how Cajetan comments on this article: “A contingent effect can be compared with its causes in two
ways: in one way, as indeterminate toward producing the effect; in the other, as determinate toward its
production. Future things in the first mode are not taken as future but as entirely contingent future
things. Future things in the second mode are indeed taken as future, but they depart from contingency.”
And it is probable that Aquinas was moved by this to rest in the presence of all things with God when he
dealt with foreknowledge of future things while preserving their contingency. For in *On Truth*, q. 6,
art. 3, in the body, he also professes that it appears difficult to reconcile the infallibility of
predestination with the liberty of choice.

PENOTTUS.

“Second, that this is Thomas’s mind is proved as follows. If there were an efficacious decree in which
alone, prescinding from the actual presence of things, future contingents could be infallibly known, it
would be the decree of divine predestination. But according to Thomas’s opinion the decree of divine
predestination, prescinding from actual presence, is not a sufficient ground for knowing future
contingents. Thomas expressly teaches this in *On Truth*, q. 6, art. 3, reply 6. There he proposes to
himself this argument against the certainty of predestination: ‘According to Anselm, the truth of
predestination and that of a proposition about the future are the same. But a proposition about the
future does not have certain and determinate truth but can be varied, as is clear from the Philosopher
in *On Interpretation* and in book 2 of *On Generation*. Therefore the truth of predestination has no
certainty either.’

“He responds to the same argument, saying: ‘To the sixth it must be said that Anselm’s similitude holds
in this respect: just as the truth of a proposition about the future does not remove the contingency of
future things, neither does the truth of predestination. But it differs in this respect: a proposition
about the future regards the future only as it is future, and in this way it cannot have certainty. But
the truth of predestination and foreknowledge regards the future as it is present, as was said in the
question on God’s knowledge, and therefore it has certainty.’ These are his words. Hence it is evident
that Thomas thinks that, if the presence of future contingents themselves is prescinded from, those
future contingents cannot be known through the decree of divine predestination. And indeed, if Thomas
had thought that God could know future contingents in the decree alone, prescinding from the presence
of things, there was no occasion more opportune for expressing it than the occasion of resolving that
argument. Indeed, if he had even shadowily suspected that the certainty of divine foreknowledge could
be taken from the divine decree, he could not have concealed it in that place.”

RESPONSE.

But, I say, what Penottus says is false when he says that Thomas expressly teaches that “the decree of
predestination, prescinding from actual presence, is not a sufficient ground for knowing future
contingents.” He says only, as concerns predestination, that its truth and the truth of foreknowledge
regard the future as it is present. But beyond controversy this holds universally of all future
contingents, even those whose cause is the divine will alone. For he nowhere distinguishes among future
contingents whenever he founds the certainty of cognition of them in their presence with God from
eternity.

Yet notwithstanding this, we have already heard Penottus himself profess that the divine decree is a
sufficient and even proximate ground for knowing contingents of that sort. Concerning these we could
equally contend that, according to Thomas’s opinion, they cannot be known through the divine decree if
their presence is prescinded from. It would likewise be permissible to expatiate rhetorically just as
Penottus does: “If Thomas had thought that in the decree alone, prescinding from the presence of things,
God could know even contingents of this sort, he would by no means have recurred to their presence
whenever he speaks of the infallible certainty of cognition of future contingents, and that without any
distinction among future contingents.”

But Penottus wishes Aquinas to be like himself, obtruding light and shadowy suspicions upon his readers
as solid arguments. Yet I have now exhibited the ground why Aquinas, in asserting infallible divine
cognition of future contingents, preferred to recur to the presence of things rather than to the divine
decree: the presence of things appears both to accommodate the infallibility of cognition and to be no
obstacle at all to the contingency of things. But although the divine decree also confirms the
infallibility of divine cognition, it nevertheless appears greatly to obstruct the contingency of
things. Therefore in this very article he openly professes that “it appears difficult to reconcile the
infallibility of predestination with the liberty of choice,” which is the same as if he had said, “with
the contingency of future things depending upon free choice.”

Moreover, Penottus conducts himself in interpreting Aquinas as if the certainty of predestination
depended upon the certainty of foreknowledge, and not the certainty of foreknowledge upon predestination.
Yet nothing more contradictory to Thomas’s opinion can be affirmed. As the fullest testimony to this
matter I cite this very article, over which Penottus nevertheless glories so greatly as favorable to his
cause. Thomas has it thus: “For it cannot be said that predestination adds nothing to the certainty of
providence except the certainty of foreknowledge, as though it were said that God orders a predestined
person to salvation just as he does any other, but with this difference: he knows that the predestined
person will not fail of salvation. For on this account the predestined person would not be said to
differ from the non-predestined on the part of the order, but only on the part of foreknowledge of the
outcome. Thus foreknowledge would be the cause of predestination, and predestination would not be through
the choice of the one predestining, which is contrary to the authority of Scripture. Hence, beyond the
certainty of foreknowledge, the very order of predestination has infallible certainty; yet the cause of
salvation, namely free choice, is not ordered to salvation necessarily but contingently.”

Finally, he concludes in this manner: “Therefore, if we consider salvation with respect to its proximate
cause, namely free choice, it does not have certainty but contingency. But with respect to its first
cause, which is predestination, it has certainty.” And observe in passing that Thomas by no means adds
that salvation

[Book I, printed p. 130; BSB canvas 00156]

has contingency also with respect to predestination, but says only that it has certainty, omitting every
mention of contingency. For divine predestination appears to favor nothing of the contingency of the
things subjected to it. Therefore, in reconciling certainty with contingency, it is not at all
astonishing that Thomas, omitting mention of the decree, betook himself to the presence of things. Yet
at the beginning of his response to the question proposed in this article, he also acknowledges that a
certain estimation is had of a thing especially through its cause.

Finally, what is this presence of things according to Penottus? Is it anything other than the presence
of things in time? But how, I ask, can the temporal presence of things be premised to divine cognition
otherwise than in willed being? There he contemplates future contingents, namely in his essence as it is
willing, and nowhere else does he ever contemplate them than in his essence insofar as it is willing,
although they exist in time. For God knows things done no otherwise than things to be done. Nor is it at
all astonishing that, if this presence is prescinded from, God by no means knows future contingents,
since to know future contingents according to their presence with God in willed being—or in God’s
essence insofar as it is willing—is nothing other than to know future contingents in the divine decree.
Thus, by the same account in every respect by which the presence of things with God is a sufficient
cause for knowing future contingents, the divine decree must also be a sufficient cause for knowing
future contingents.

That this is also Aquinas’s opinion—namely that the presence of things with God is nothing other than
their willed being—is manifestly established from his doctrine of the book of life. For what establishes
the presence of things with God more aptly than their inscription as though in a book before God? But
according to Aquinas’s doctrine, this book of life is nothing other than the inscription of
predestination. And since it is a metaphorical expression, Aquinas, in reducing it to its proper sense,
professes that the book of life is nothing other than God’s cognition of his predestination, *On Truth*,
q. 7, on the book of life, art. 4. Hence it becomes manifest that according to Aquinas things are in
willed being with God before they are in known being with him.

PENOTTUS.

“Third, this proposition can be proved from innumerable authorities of the Fathers who say that God
foreknows many things which he does not predefine or predestine, but that he predefines or predestines
nothing which he did not foreknow.”

RESPONSE.

I acknowledge this to be so. Yet it is also clear that “to predestine” is used by those Fathers only in
the sense that it concerns things which God himself will do, not those which God will permit. Aquinas
takes predestination thus in *On Truth*, q. 12, art. 10, in the body: “Predestination is called as it
were a certain preparation of God; but someone prepares that which he himself will do.”

PENOTTUS.

“Fourth, it can also be proved from all those authorities of the Fathers by which we are taught that God
knows future contingents because they are future, but that they are not future because God knows them.”

RESPONSE.

But Augustine most explicitly teaches that they are future because God knows them, not the reverse.
Aquinas too proposes an opinion of Origen supporting Penottus, which he tempers with an interpretation.
Not only the Dominicans teach the same but also Thomas Bradwardine.

II. But grant that God knows future things because they are future. Then whence, I ask, has it come about
that, since by their nature they are merely possible, they have passed from the condition of things
merely possible into the condition of future things, and that from eternity? The cause of this matter
cannot be sought outside God, for what does not exist cannot cause a transition of this sort. In God
himself nothing at all occurs which is its cause except God’s own will—that is, God’s decree.

PENOTTUS.

“Finally, it is proved by reason. Future contingents are known either in an efficacious divine decree
antecedent to the free determination of the created will—and this cannot be said, because such a decree
would remove human liberty—or in a decree concomitant with the free determination of the created will.
And thus the divine decree by itself is not a sufficient ground for knowing future contingents.”

RESPONSE.

Indeed, lest this work of Penottus entitled *The Bulwark of Human Liberty* come to nothing—unless perhaps
it should be of use to sellers of incense and perfumes, from which in his opinion a great benefit would
hereafter be taken away from the Christian world by his lucubrations—this favor appears to be owed him:
that future contingents be said to be known by God in a decree, though not an antecedent decree but one
concomitant with the free determination of the human will. For God did not decree to determine the human
will toward faith and repentance and perseverance in both. Rather, God had seen that the human will,
placed in such an order, would determine itself toward every good work when divine determination was
excluded. Otherwise this bulwark of human liberty of Penottus would be worth nothing. But I am pleased
to linger somewhat more carefully in examining this argument, especially since Penottus places in it
the bow and stern of the confirmation of his opinion.

II. Therefore I answer that, although the decree in which God infallibly sees future contingents
antecedes the free determination of the created will, the created will will not thereby cease to be
free. For if it ceased to be free, that decree would by no means antecede a free determination of the
will, but rather a necessary one.

III. Nor will this determination of the created will, however you indicate it, cease to be free because
of God’s determination. For we say that God decreed to determine the human will freely to believe,
repent, and perform every good work. Will you deny that it is in God’s power either to decree or to
bring it about that someone believes freely? You will say: “Yet once such a decree or divine
determination is posited, a human being must believe and repent.” Certainly he must believe, but how?
Not necessarily but freely, provided God has determined him to believe freely.

IIII. Meanwhile, we freely acknowledge that neither a human being nor an angel is free in the same way
that God is, or has the same command over his action that God has. We even acknowledge that God
necessitates the will to

[Book I, printed p. 131; BSB canvas 00157]

do freely whatever it does. And we say this with good authors: not Luther, who once professed that free
choice is proper and incommunicable to God, or Calvin, but Thomas Bradwardine, a scholastic author above
every exception as though he were partial to our side. As often as I compare his memorable work *On the
Cause of God against Pelagius* with the spirit of your work, I seem to see how rightly either your work
could have been entitled *On the Cause of Pelagius against God*, or his work could have been entitled
*The Bulwark of God’s Grace against Pelagian Human Liberty*. In the same way, your writing can truly be
marked *The Bulwark of Pelagian Human Liberty against God’s Grace*.

V. But we proceed further, and by manifest reasoning we prove that God’s decree must antecede the free
determination of the created will. For the free determination of the created will comes about in time,
but every decree of God is from eternity. And that which is eternal must antecede that which is temporal.

VI. Here I know it is customary for you to recur to the free determination of the created will in God’s
foreknowledge. This is habitual both for our Arminians and your Jesuits, and universally for all
defenders of middle knowledge. But this refuge will profit you no more than the support of a broken reed.
For foreknowledge of the free determination of the created will as merely possible profits you nothing.
But foreknowledge of the free determination of the created will as future is altogether impossible
before God’s decree. For it is impossible that a thing which by its nature is merely contingent and
holds itself indifferently toward either side should lay aside that indifference and migrate from the
condition of a thing merely possible into the condition of a future thing apart from a cause.

But no tolerable cause of this migration can be imagined except God’s decree. For the cause of this
migration is sought outside God in vain, since this migration was made from eternity, nothing outside
God occurs which is eternal, and what is not eternal cannot produce an effect which is eternal. It
remains that the cause of this eternal change be sought only in God himself. But it is not found in
God’s knowledge, since according to your opinion God’s knowledge presupposes future things rather than
making them. Nor does God’s essence, when God’s will is excluded, change anything concerning the nature
of possible things. It remains, therefore, that God’s will alone is the cause why from eternity all
contingents were made future out of things merely possible, such as they are by their nature.

PENOTTUS.

“Against this final conclusion is the first argument. Every effect is sufficiently knowable in its
efficacious cause which is determined to produce it and which can be impeded by no other cause. But the
divine will is such a cause with respect to contingent effects. Therefore all contingent effects can be
known in its decree. I answer that the major proposition is true of a total and in every way adequate
cause, but not of a cause which is not adequate in every way without the concurrence of another cause,
such as the divine will is with respect to contingent effects, especially those which proceed from the
free created will.”

RESPONSE.

I. Scripture everywhere teaches that the Holy Spirit is the adequate cause why someone believes,
repents, or performs any good work. For if he were only an inadequate and partial cause, it would no
more be said that “it is not of him who wills or runs but of God who shows mercy” than that “it is not
of God who shows mercy but of the person who wills and runs.” Again, Scripture assigns both faith and
repentance to the gift of God, but removes them from us ourselves who believe and repent as their causes.

II. Again, although faith and repentance themselves are the work not of God alone but also of the human
being who believes and repents, nevertheless the human will’s determination of itself to believe and
repent is of God alone and not of the human being. What, then? Will you say that the human will
determines itself to determine itself? This cannot be said without gross absurdity. Rather, it is God
who brings it about that the human will determines itself toward the good, according to Ezekiel 36: “I
will bring it about that you walk in my statutes and keep my judgments.” Likewise Augustine’s statement:
“We are acted upon that we may act, not that we may do nothing.”

III. Aquinas himself expressly professes this in the very article from which Penottus scrapes together
what he judged to conduce especially to his cause. Namely, he openly asserts that a secondary cause in
its operation is subject to divine predestination. This is the same as if he had said that it is
subordinated, not coordinated, to divine action—although Penottus together with the Jesuits imagines the
latter order. Aquinas also acknowledges a subjection of the human will of this sort to divine
predestination, from which the certainty of predestination is established—and indeed a certainty of
such a sort that it advances beyond the certainty of foreknowledge. I say this to cut the sinews of a
certain rejoinder toward which I see that you are quite inclined, but with no advantage at all to your
cause.

Thus, when Aquinas had objected in the fourth place, “Every certainty which exists with the supposition
of something is not absolute but conditional certainty. Thus it is not certain that the sun causes
fruit in a plant unless with this condition, that the generative power in the plant is well disposed,
because the disposition of the sun toward the aforesaid effect presupposes the power of the plant as a
secondary cause. Therefore, if the certainty of predestination exists with the presupposition of a
secondary cause, it is not absolute but only conditional certainty, just as in me there is certainty
that Socrates moves if he runs and that this person will be saved if he prepares himself. Thus in divine
predestination there is no other certainty concerning those to be saved than there is in me, which is
absurd”—this was the objection; Aquinas’s answer follows.

“To the fourth it must be said that the secondary cause which must be presupposed for introducing the
effect of predestination is itself also subject to the order of predestination. But it is not so among
inferior powers with respect to some superior agent power. Therefore, although the order of divine
predestination exists with the presupposition of the human will, it nevertheless has absolute certainty,
even though the contrary appears in the example adduced.”

PENOTTUS.

“The second argument is taken from certain statements extorted by force. As for the first authority,
from book 1 of *Against the Gentiles*, chapter 66, it has been said a thousand times that the opposite
is evidently had from that passage. Let the words be read. Thomas says that future contingents and all
things which are or were are known by God in his power, in their causes, and in themselves. But he does
not say that all things are certainly and infallibly known either in his power alone or in secondary
causes alone. Indeed, he denies this both there and always elsewhere.”

[Book I, printed p. 132; BSB canvas 00158]

“II. To the second: in the same work, chapter 68, Thomas says that future contingents are known in their
causes by knowing the order of those causes toward their effects. But he does not say that this knowledge
alone is sufficient for them to be called certainly and infallibly known. Indeed, he denies this both
there and elsewhere, especially in the first book of the *Sentences*, distinction 38, q. 1, art. 5; the
first part, q. 14, art. 13; *On Truth*, q. 12, art. 10; *On Evil*, q. 16, art. 16; and other passages
cited above.

“III. To the third, from the *Secunda Secundae*, q. 171, art. 6, reply 2: It is more than false to say
that Thomas there says what the arguer pretends.

“IV. To the fourth, from the first part, q. 14, art. 13, where he says that God knows future contingents
not only because, as certain men say, he has the grounds of things with himself, but also because he
sees all things in their presence: I answer that nothing certain can be gathered from that manner of
speaking, for in the same article he says that God knows all future contingents not only in contingent
secondary causes but also in themselves. Yet no sane person gathers from this manner of speaking that
he meant God to know future contingents in secondary causes alone. And grant that Thomas proved the
opinion of those who say that creatures are known by God in the ideas: the answer is to deny that
knowing them in the ideas and in the decree is the same, just as it is denied that the ideas and the
divine decree are the same—which, nevertheless, the arguer supposes.”

RESPONSE.

I acknowledge that this response belongs to a foaming mind indignant at being restrained. But we do not
linger over the man’s clamors; let us examine what worthy thing he has produced with so great a gape. He
says that it has been said a thousand times that the opposite is evidently had from that passage, but
he has nowhere exhibited a particle of Aquinas’s text in which the opposite appears, much less evidently.
He thinks it sufficient that this has been said a thousand times, but let anyone consider whether to
beat the air even a thousand times is to contend by any legitimate argument.

Finally, in a more subdued tone he says that although Thomas acknowledges that all future contingents
are known by God in his power, in their causes, and in themselves, nevertheless Thomas does not say that
“all are certainly and infallibly known either in his power alone or in secondary causes alone. Indeed,”
he says, “he denies this both there and elsewhere.” But he establishes by no indication, however slight,
that Thomas denies this there. Moreover, Thomas’s own text, which Penottus acknowledges, sufficiently
indicates that in Thomas’s opinion God infallibly knows future contingents in his power and in their
causes just as he does in themselves. Otherwise we would attribute an exceedingly absurd opinion to
him, as though he acknowledged that God knows future contingents not only certainly and infallibly,
namely in themselves, but also uncertainly and fallibly, namely in his power and in their causes.

Penottus saw this disadvantage and appears to have wished to avoid it by saying that Thomas “does not
say that all are certainly and infallibly known by God either in his power alone or in secondary causes
alone,” as though the controversy were whether by these modes alone God knows future contingents
certainly and infallibly. On the contrary, the Dominicans make it known that God knows future
contingents certainly and infallibly not in one mode alone but in various modes. Yet I see another sense
of this exclusive particle “alone” which Penottus has invented for himself: namely, that it comes to this,
that neither God’s power alone nor cognition of secondary causes alone is sufficient for knowing future
contingents certainly and infallibly.

Thus Penottus puts on various forms to extricate himself from these testimonies of Thomas, but he is
held entangled even against his will. For according to this sense he must acknowledge that cognition of
the divine power and likewise cognition of the secondary causes, although neither is solitary, suffice
when conjoined with contemplation of future contingents in themselves for certain and infallible
cognition of them. But, I say, this opinion is too absurd on two counts for Penottus fittingly to
attribute it to Thomas. For a mode of knowing something fallibly does nothing at all to complete an
infallible mode of knowing, any more than adding a cipher contributes to increasing a number. Again, if
these modes of knowing, when conjoined with contemplation of things in themselves, contributed to
rendering God’s cognition infallible, it would follow that contemplation of future contingents in
themselves is by no means sufficient to constitute certain and infallible divine cognition of future
contingents.

Therefore it must either be acknowledged that, according to Aquinas, God can know future contingents
certainly and infallibly in various modes, or an absurd opinion must be attributed to Thomas, as though
he said that God knows future contingents in various modes: certainly and infallibly insofar as he knows
them in themselves, but not certainly and infallibly insofar as he knows them either in his power or in
their secondary causes. I have already shown from Aquinas, first part, q. 14, art. 11, that “God knows
things other than himself through his essence insofar as it is the likeness of things or as their active
principle”—that is, in his active power over them. Do you wish this to be understood of fallible cognition?
What sober person would obtrude an opinion of this kind upon Aquinas? And what other active principle of
things is there in the divine essence besides the divine will itself, or the divine decree itself? So
too, if secondary causes are considered as determinate or as to be infallibly determined, why would
their effects not be certainly and infallibly known?

II. If any knowledge of things is attributed to God which does not suffice for certain and infallible
cognition of them, it follows that by that very fact fallible and uncertain cognition of things is
attributed to God. And what fair appraiser of things would obtrude upon Aquinas an opinion of this sort,
by which he maintained that God knows some things uncertainly and fallibly? In the preceding chapter you
said that Aquinas speaks in this place of things future in a qualified respect. Suárez interprets the
passage of natural effects and professes that the same interpretation is approved by Ferrariensis. You
say Thomas denies that things are known certainly in this way, and that in this very passage. But you
bring forward no testimony or shadow of testimony for this claim. You also say that Aquinas denies this
elsewhere. Will you, then, prove anywhere that Aquinas attributes to God cognition of this sort which is
fallible? Perhaps Aquinas says that things considered in a certain mode cannot be subjected to certain
cognition. But that Aquinas anywhere attributed to God a mode of knowing according to which things
cannot be certainly known is wholly incredible to me. The passage from book 1, distinction 38, q. 1,
art. 5, in the body, commonly alleged by the Dominicans to establish

[Book I, printed p. 133; BSB canvas 00159]

divine cognition of future contingents from their presence, is this: “God not only knows from eternity concerning things
that he knows their causes, which is for them to be in his cognition, but he also sees at one intuition
all times from eternity, and that such a thing exists at this time and fails to exist at that time.”
Here he by no means denies cognition of future things from causes for establishing the certainty of
divine cognition, although he employs the presence of things to establish the certainty of divine
cognition.

And to interpose something in passing: when Thomas says that “for God to know the causes of things from
eternity is for them to be in his cognition,” do you wish to interpret this of fallible cognition? It
appears to me that it should rather be interpreted of infallible cognition, but a cognition which—since
it is knowledge of a thing through its cause—he distinguishes from cognition of things through their
presence. Aquinas relies on this latter ground of knowing whenever he discusses infallible cognition of
things notwithstanding their contingency. This is not because cognition of things through their causes
is not equally infallible, but because it does not equally serve to illustrate the friendly conspiracy
of God’s infallible cognition with the contingency of the things known.

Likewise, in the first part, q. 14, art. 13, in the body, he distinguishes a twofold consideration of a
contingent. “In one way, in itself, according as it now actually exists. Thus,” he says, “it is not
considered as future but as present; nor as contingent toward either side but as determined toward one.
On this account it can thus be infallibly subjected to certain cognition, for example to the sense of
sight, as when I see Socrates sitting. In another way a contingent can be considered as it exists in its
cause, and thus it is considered as future and as a contingent not yet determined toward one thing,
because a contingent cause holds itself toward opposites. Thus the contingent is not subjected with
certainty to any cognition.”

Nor is this astonishing, for to consider something as future and as a contingent which holds itself
indifferently toward opposites is not to consider it as future simply, but only as contingently future—
that is, as no more future than nonfuture, but as holding itself indifferently toward either. But
observe, I ask: Does Aquinas attribute any cognition of this sort to God? By no means, for this would be
to attribute error rather than cognition to God.

Yet I do not deny that God also knows how future things stand in causes which do not operate certainly.
That is, he knows what order the causes have toward effects, whether their production of those effects
is certain or uncertain. According to this sense Aquinas says in *On Truth*, q. 12, art. 10, in the body,
that “God knows future things by reason of the order of the causes, even those causes whose effects can
be impeded.” But this is not to know future things, or to foreknow, but rather to foreknow that by reason
of this order of causes it is uncertain whether they will be future or not. Question 16 of *On Evil*
contains only twelve articles, but Penottus sends us to article 16.

III. We have already subjected this response of Penottus to examination in the preceding chapter.

IV. It certainly does not follow that God knows future contingents in the ideas alone or in secondary
causes alone. Yet unless we wish to fasten an absurd opinion upon Thomas, as though he ascribed to God
some fallible cognition of future things, it must be acknowledged that each of these modes which he
attributes to God is sufficient by itself to constitute infallible divine cognition of future
contingents. Although knowing future contingents in the ideas and in the decree is not the same, it will
come to the same if the ideas themselves and the certainty of their representation rest only upon God’s
decree. I think you are not ignorant that this is the Dominicans’ opinion: divine cognition of future
contingents—whether it comes about in the ideas, in determinate secondary causes, or in the presence of
the future things themselves—presupposes in every respect the determination of the divine will. Nor does
the arguer suppose that the ideas and the divine decree are the same, but that the ideas cannot
represent future contingents unless God’s will is presupposed. For God sees things other than himself in
his essence only as it is the likeness of things or as their active principle, as Thomas has it in the
first part, q. 14, art. 11.

Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 18: “Saint Thomas,” he says, “in *On the Christian Religion*, or *On Faith,
Hope, and Charity*, book 3, says that the contingency of the effects of secondary causes cannot disturb the
certainty of divine providence, because the efficacy of the divine will furnishes certainty to it. The
same author above in the same work, 133, says that contingents, as they exist virtually in their causes,
are not determined toward one thing so that certain cognition of them can be had. Yet as they exist
actually in their being, in God’s eternity, they are already determined toward one thing and certain
cognition can be had of them. Boethius says the same in book 5 of *The Consolation of Philosophy*, prose
6: God is not properly said to have foreknowledge of future things but rather knowledge of present
things, because all future things are present to him.” This appears incapable of being understood
otherwise than that God knows all future things because they are present to him—that is, through his
insuperable and immutable will they are presently determined and decreed with certainty to come to pass
in the future.

At the end observe that Penottus had undertaken to prove that Aquinas never signified, either explicitly
and in formal words or implicitly, that God knows future contingents in his decree. He had undertaken
to demonstrate the latter at the end in resolving the arguments. But these arguments are such that in
them he appeared to the Dominicans to have asserted this explicitly. He is also believed to have taught
it implicitly in all the places in which he teaches that the created will is determined toward every
act of its own through the divine operation.

He concludes by proposing to himself a certain argument from Vázquez, to which he responds, concerning
the divine decree adding nothing above the bare essence except a relation of reason. Penottus denies
this. But when he comes to explain what the decree adds above God’s bare essence, he professes that the
matter is most difficult to explain and, so far as he knows, has to this point been perfectly explained
by no one—although, to note this in passing, Suárez the Jesuit and Navarrete the Dominican have devoted
their labor to its explanation. Yet he adds that it can be said without error that it is the divine
essence itself as it assumes the mode of one willing. I cannot but praise this writer’s prudence, since
he did not in the least doubt that it could be said without error. Indeed, in my judgment it is most
truly said; but how accurately and satisfactorily, let anyone who wishes judge. Could he not equally
have said that God’s decree is the divine essence as it assumes the mode of one decreeing? Again,
Penottus does not explain what this mode of one willing or decreeing is, or teach what it adds above the
bare essence, which is the single point in controversy. Certainly, if divine liberty were

similar to our liberty—namely, with respect to various acts—there would be room for this scruple. But
since it is not with respect to acts but objects, in my judgment it is asked in vain what it adds above
God’s bare essence. For in the simple divine essence nothing occurs added above another according to a
real ground, but only according to diverse formal grounds. Thus omnipotence, intellect, and will
represent to us different formal notions, which nevertheless differ among themselves by no reality.

If the divine decree is not a sufficient ground for knowing contingent effects certainly and
infallibly, then certainly God will not be able to know future things certainly and infallibly through
the ideas either, since their efficacy in representing future things depends upon God’s decree. But
according to Penottus himself, God can know future contingents certainly through the ideas when God’s
decree is added. In Chapter 10 he says: “Through the ideas, when God’s free decree is prescinded from, no
certain cognition can be had of future contingents insofar as they are future. But when the free
determination of the will is added, certain knowledge of future contingents can very well be had through
them. Thomas appears openly to grant this in book 1, distinction 39, q. 1, art. 2, reply 1, and more
clearly in *On Truth*, q. 2, art. 7, reply 6.” Penottus has statements of this sort in Chapter 24,
number 2.
