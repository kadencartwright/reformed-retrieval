One thing therefore remains for Navarrete to resolve: he must set out for us an explanation of
these refinements. God’s side and the thing’s side do not appear capable of being distinguished so
far that they are separated in the same vision of God. Wherever God plays his part as one who sees
some thing, the thing must also play its part as something seen by God. For example, if the
production of things on the side of God who produces is measured by eternity, it is equitable that
it should also be measured by eternity on the side of the thing produced. And if God intuits the
thing, on the side of the thing seen, as future in time, it is equitable that on the side of God who
sees he should intuit the thing as future in time.

Moreover, when he says that it does not belong to the nature of intuitive knowledge to presuppose
the thing at which it is terminated, and that it suffices for the thing to exist simultaneously
with it, these claims are true if they concern the order of duration, but false if they concern the
order of reason. Without doubt, the thing is understood as knowable before it is understood as
actually known. The distinction concerning a thing seen by God as simultaneously existing with
God’s vision—considered not as produced but as the terminus of knowledge—is conceived beyond every
measure of sobriety. No thing can be the terminus of God’s intuitive knowledge unless it passes
from the condition of a possible thing into that of a future thing, or from the condition of a
thing not present in eternity into that of a thing present in eternity. Consequently, it cannot
fail to be considered by God in that way. We call this transition of the thing its production, but
it does not arise from the nature of the thing itself. A possible thing not present in eternity
cannot make itself future or present in eternity. God must therefore bring about this transition
which we call the production of the thing from nonbeing to being—not to the real being which you
imagine in eternity and from eternity, but to intentional being, and that in two ways: first, by
which it is present before

God in willed being, and second, by which it is present before God in known being. When you say
that God’s intuitive knowledge does not presuppose the existence of the known thing but requires a
thing existing simultaneously with it, these words readily impress that the presence of things
which you imagine in eternity differs in no respect from the intentional being by which they are
before God in known being. Otherwise, the thing is undoubtedly understood as knowable before it is
understood as known. Yet the existence of a thing simultaneous with its cognition appears to be
nothing at all except that according to which it is said to be known. I readily admit that the
cognition of any thing, at the same moment of nature and reason, denominates both the thing known
and the person knowing.

Finally, when you add that a thing terminates intuitive knowledge in eternity for the time at which
it is to be produced, your words signify either that the thing does not exist in eternity before it
exists in time, or merely that it is known by God in eternity not as present in eternity but as to
be produced in time—that is, as future. This is just as if you said that God knows some thing as
future before the time of its production arrives, as present when it arrives, and as past after it
has passed. I judge this to be most true. God’s cognition of all these is one and the same, most
simple and unchangeable cognition, although the things known admit no slight variation according to
the various differences of time.
