CHAPTER XVI

The Contrary Arguments of the Opponents Are Weighed;
First, Those of Dr. Walaeus

This assertion of ours, now explained and confirmed, is opposed not only by
the Anti-Sabbatarians but also by certain others who agree sufficiently with
us as regards the principal question. Foremost is the most distinguished Dr.
Walaeus, in his *Dissertation on the Fourth Precept*, chapter 4, p. 95, and
following.

In that chapter the most distinguished man proves that one day of the week
must be observed by force of the fourth precept. At length he comes to examine
the objections of those who think that the septenary number of days should not
be adhered to. The first and principal of their objections is this: *From this
doctrine it appears to follow that not every distinction of days was abolished
after Christ's advent. For if one of the seven days of the week must be
consecrated to divine worship, that day will be far distinguished from the
other six days; yet the apostle in Romans 14:5 and Galatians 4:10 appears openly
to condemn that discrimination of days as conflicting with Christian liberty.*
It is not difficult to answer this objection. And the most distinguished
Walaeus turns the objection very effectively against the adversaries
themselves, because they concede that it pertains to the substance and
morality of the precept that certain days be appointed for divine worship; and
it cannot be denied that those certain days are distinguished from the others.
But when he proceeds to the matter itself on p. 93, *he acknowledges that it
follows from those passages that every distinction of any days was abolished
through Christ's advent, insofar, namely, as something ceremonial or properly
called religious was contained in them.* We indeed gladly concede that every
ceremonial distinction of days was removed through Christ's advent. That is,
no day is now to be preferred to another or distinguished from another because
of anything ceremonial. But because of the things stated in the previous
chapter, we cannot concede that every distinction of any days was removed
through Christ's advent insofar, namely, as something religious is in them. And
I certainly do not see how Dr. Walaeus himself can concede this, much less
assert it, since he asserts and proves that by force of the fourth precept it
is moral for one day in the cycle of the week to be constantly observed, and
acknowledges the Lord's Day to be of apostolic institution. For if, by force of
the fourth precept, one day must be observed in the week, and the day now is the
Lord's Day by apostolic institution, that day is undoubtedly distinguished
from the others and has in itself something properly called religious, since it
is commanded in the first table of the Decalogue.

*For the Jews* (he continues) *were bound under the Old Testament to the
observation of the Sabbath day and of the other feast days as a part of divine
worship and as sacramental signs of our regeneration, so that not only were the
days religious for the sake of worship, but the very rest and celebration of
the day was in itself religious for them.* Answer. If the Jews were bound by
force of the fourth precept to the observation of the Sabbath day—as a part of
divine worship; we are not speaking here of the other feasts—why not we also?
The precept is moral, binds perpetually, and is always of the same efficacy.
Therefore it appears to bind us no less than the Jews to this observance as a
part of worship. If

[1674 logical printed p. 342 | IA leaf 360 | PDF p. 360]

it is said that they were bound to the observance of the Sabbath as a part of
worship, not by force of the Moral Law but by force of some ceremonial law,
answer: then Dr. Walaeus will yield to his adversaries. And, I ask, what was
that ceremonial law? And what reason is there why ceremonial law can make
something a part of divine worship when Moral Law cannot bring this about? Is
ceremonial law more efficacious than Moral Law? If it is said that they were
bound to the observance of the Sabbath as a part of ceremonial divine worship,
answer: then the adversaries will prevail once again and nothing will be
answered to their objection. Therefore the celebration of the day appears to
be no less religious in itself for us than for the Jews.

He then shows that *it does not follow from this that some distinction of days
cannot be observed for other ends, just as some distinction of foods can also
be maintained under the New Testament. For just as intemperance in food and
drink is never lawful, so also in a time of mourning another kind of food and
drink must be used than in a time of gladness and prosperity.* He cites in
proof of this Isaiah 22:12, Daniel 10:3, and Luke 10:33. *It is evident from
those passages* (he says) *that although the distinction of foods which had to
be carefully observed under the Old Testament on account of ceremony was
removed after Christ's advent, nevertheless not every distinction instituted
for other moral, even religious, causes was therefore unlawful.* Answer. It is
true that not every distinction either of foods or of days is removed;
especially not the distinction which is founded in Moral Law and therefore
remains perpetually. Thus temperance in food and drink, commanded by Moral Law,
is always lawful, and intemperance is always unlawful. Therefore the
distinction of one day in the cycle of the week from the others always remains,
because it is founded in Moral Law. For the observance of that day is commanded
in the fourth precept of the Decalogue, as he himself proves. These things
therefore make for us and clearly prove that the Moral and Religious
distinction of days is not removed through Christ's advent, but only the
ceremonial distinction.

He adds on p. 95: *And thus we also say that although the ceremonial distinction
of days was removed after Christ's advent, not therefore was every distinction
of days whatsoever which is instituted for a religious cause abolished.*
Answer. Correct, if the words *for a religious cause* designate an immediately
religious end. For it clearly follows from this that the religious distinction
of the Sabbath from the other days of the week is not removed through Christ's
advent, since it is instituted as a part of moral worship by force of the
fourth precept. But—

He appears to correct this in what follows, saying: *Not because the very
observance of one of the seven days is either typical or in itself a part of
divine worship, but because it is a necessary circumstance of divine worship
under the New Testament no less than under the Old, seeing that without it the
public worship instituted by God in the church of the New Testament also
cannot be performed.* Answer. We gladly acknowledge that the observance of one
of the seven days is not typical, but we cannot say that it is not a Part of
divine worship. 2. We concede that it is a physically necessary circumstance
of divine worship, but we cannot concede that besides this it is nothing but a
mere circumstance. For here we are not speaking of indeterminate Time, which
naturally and physically is a circumstance necessary for this or that exercise
of worship, but of Time determined—and this by God—both as regards
its Duration and Frequency and as regards the particular day. This is a
circumstance

[1674 logical printed p. 343 | IA leaf 361 | PDF p. 361]

morally and theologically necessary for worship in general. I say *worship in
general*, because there were certain typical exercises of religion which had their own
morally and theologically necessary times: for example, the eighth day for
circumcision; the fourteenth day of the first month, or in a certain case the
fourteenth of the second month, was necessary for Passover. Yet this determined
solemn time is not necessary in this way to any act of Religion, for all the
duties of worship performed on that day can also be performed on any other
day. Nevertheless it is necessary morally and theologically for worship in
general, and therefore a part of worship.

He saw that it could sufficiently be replied that this same thing can be said
of Place, namely, that it is a necessary circumstance, since without it the
public worship instituted by God also in the church of the New Testament
cannot be performed. Therefore, a little afterward he has this: *But there is
a different account of sacred Places, which God left wholly free under the New
Testament because no certain precept concerning the same place could be given
throughout the whole world, since it must vary according to the church's size
or constitution, and its account is different in a time of the cross than in a
time of liberty.* Answer. 1. It is entirely true that the account of Places is
different from that of Time. But if this Time is nothing other than a mere
circumstance, I do not see how it differs from place as regards the matter now
in question. For each is a mere circumstance and a necessary circumstance, so
that without either the public worship now instituted by God under the New
Testament cannot be performed. Therefore, if it is commanded by a moral
precept that a certain time be appointed for divine worship because the
exercises of public worship cannot be performed without it, why should some
place not also have to be commanded by a moral precept, since the duties of
divine worship can no more be performed without it? 2. Although some precept
concerning the same Place throughout the whole world could not be given—just
as one cannot be given concerning the same point of Time—a precept concerning
different places nevertheless could have been given, which would have been
sufficient for the distinction of one place from another. 3. It therefore
appears that Time is distinguished from Place even from the fact that some
moral precept exists concerning the former, but none concerning the latter.
Now all obedience rendered to God's moral command in the first table is
religious, or a part of worship. Consequently, since he himself proves that one
day out of seven must be observed by God's command—by the Fourth precept,
namely—that observance will be Religious, or an act of worship.

To this he says on p. 96: *This no more presses us than those with whom we are
dealing here; for if some day must be kept by force of the fourth precept, that
day will be religious in the same way also.* Answer. We truly think that this
knot binds both parties equally, and we do not see what can be replied either
by the Anti-Sabbatarians or by Walaeus himself.

Yet he proceeds: *We say that the religious character of this day is to be
measured by the intention of God who commands it. For if God commands some day
to be observed as a part of public worship or as a sacramental type, then it
must be observed from the very religious character and with the intention with
which it is commanded. But if it is commanded only as a necessary and
determinate circumstance of public worship, or as a polity in the church
necessary for the people's edification, as many of our men speak, then it too
must be done only in that manner and with that intention.* Answer. 1. We concede
that the religious character of this day is to be measured by the intention of
God who commands it. But how will it be proved that God did not command this
day as a part of worship? He concedes that God commanded this day to be
observed by the Jews as a Part of public worship, and that in the fourth
precept of the Decalogue. But the same precept of the Decalogue commands us,
by his own testimony, to observe one day in the cycle of the week. Therefore it
also commands us to observe the day as a part of public worship. For it cannot
be proved by any argument that the same moral precept which enjoins that the
day be observed commands that day to be observed by us differently

[1674 logical printed p. 344 | IA leaf 362 | PDF p. 362]

from the manner in which it was observed by the Jews. In no way can such a
distinction be shown in the fourth precept of the Decalogue itself. Therefore,
if formerly one day in the cycle of the week had to be observed as a part of
worship by reason of the fourth precept of the Decalogue, then that one day out
of seven which is enjoined by the same precept must also be observed now as a
part of worship. And if one day out of seven must now be observed only as a
necessary circumstance, it had to be observed no differently formerly as
well, because the Moral Precept was the same then as it is now. 2. How will it
be proved that God commands by a moral precept, primarily and immediately,
something which is merely a circumstance of public worship or only a polity in
the church? Or how will it be proved that God commands such a thing by the
fourth precept of the Decalogue, which is a precept of the first table? 3. It
was said and explained above that that is a Part of worship which God requires
by his command to be religiously observed, to his immediate honor, and to be
exhibited to him as a sign of honor. Indeed, it is that which he enjoins to be
observed by a moral command of the first table. And the learned man has not yet
satisfied this argument. 4. Although one day in the cycle of seven days is not
now observed as a sacramental type—and I doubt whether it was ever observed in
that way by force of the fourth precept—it nevertheless can be observed as a
part of worship, because, by his own admission, the Sabbath was instituted
from the beginning, before the fall, when a sacramental type had no place, and
even then it was observed as a part of worship. No one will say that nothing
can be a part of divine worship unless it is a sacramental type. 5. Was the
Sabbath formerly a circumstance necessary for worship? We heard him say this
very thing just now. Yet it was a part of worship. Therefore, although it is
now a necessary circumstance of worship, it can also be a Part of worship,
because it is commanded by God. For whatever he commands to be given to
himself as a sign of immediate honor is, by that very fact, a Part of worship.
Indeed, a circumstance of worship commanded by God himself becomes a part of
worship, as was explained above. 6. Will he say that one day in the weekly
cycle is a necessary circumstance of every religious act, so that it cannot be
performed on another day or time? Or will he say that It is a necessary
circumstance of public worship, so that public worship cannot be performed on
any other day, nor can that day be piously spent unless God is publicly
worshiped on it? Then indeed no Sabbath could be observed in a time of
persecution, which is nevertheless false.

He proceeds: *Nor does this infer such a distinction of days as Paul forbids,
as though the worship exhibited on that day were more efficacious because of
the observance of the time than on any other day, or as though it had greater
blessings attached to it, just as the observance of types or sacraments has
blessings attached to it.* Answer. 1. It is not established that Paul forbids
every religious distinction of days, any more than that he forbids every
religious distinction of foods. Therefore, just as the use of food and drink
in the Eucharist is a part of worship notwithstanding Paul's doctrine, so,
notwithstanding the same doctrine of Paul, the observance of one day in the
cycle of the week will be a part of worship because, namely, of the divine
precept. 2. Just as the use of bread and wine in the Eucharist is more
efficacious for spiritual uses because of Christ's institution, so also the
worship exhibited on that day is more efficacious because of the observance of
the time than on any other day—if there is any efficacy in the divine precept.
And 3. it has greater blessings attached to it, as was indicated above. This
would undoubtedly be seen to be proved by experience by anyone who, passing
over the Sabbath or one day in the cycle of the week, publicly worshiped God on
any other day of the week, or on one day in a cycle of twenty, forty, or sixty
days. 4. Since the observance of types and sacraments which

[1674 logical printed p. 345 | IA leaf 363 | PDF p. 363]

are enjoined by positive precepts has blessings attached to it, why will the
observance of a day instituted by a moral precept not also have them attached?

He explains this as follows: *It is no different* (he says) *from the way in
which cheaper food or cheaper clothing in a time of mourning has no greater
holiness in itself than another kind, but only serves worship and holiness; in
the same way also a day prescribed for a time of fasting, though otherwise
altogether equal to the other days, is not in itself holier than another, but
serves holiness and sanctifies the day—that is, applies it to holy uses.*
Answer. These instances are not apt as they are proposed here, because it is
now admitted that God determined one day in the cycle of the week to be
perpetually dedicated to divine worship and precisely instituted the first day
of the week. But it is not said that God commanded by any moral precept cheaper
food or cheaper clothing in a time of mourning, or precisely instituted that
day of fasting which people may happen to observe here and now. Hence it is
clear how unlike the account of these instances is to that of the Sabbath day
or Solemn Time, about which the disputation is being conducted. 2. So long as
the ceremonial law was in force, it is sufficiently well known that there was
a certain ceremonial holiness even in certain garments and foods, that the
obedience rendered to those precepts was religious, and that the observance of
an annual fast prescribed by God was a part of worship.

He continues on p. 97: *Moreover, we have a clear example of this matter in
those morning and evening hours which under the Old Testament were appointed
for the continual sacrifice and prayers. By divine prescription they were
observed as a certain circumstance of divine worship, and yet they were not
therefore in themselves holier than the other hours of the day, except with
respect to their use.* Answer. If those morning and evening hours, though
appointed by divine prescription for the continual sacrifice and prayers,
were only some circumstance of divine worship, why not the Sabbath itself and
the Feast days also? And if the Feasts and Sabbath were a part of worship, why
not those morning and evening hours also? Can any distinction of this kind be
elicited from the text? Does Scripture say anywhere that God instituted the
Sabbath and other Feasts to be a Part of worship, but the morning and evening
time to be a mere circumstance of worship? This is therefore said
gratuitously. Indeed, it appears to us that this Morning and Evening Time was
precisely prescribed so that it would be precisely observed—not indeed the
moment of time, but at least the hour—so that it was not lawful to offer those
sacrifices at any morning and evening hour other than the one which had been
determined. And the observance of this determined time was a part of worship,
a part of the homage required, and a part of the honor immediately exhibited
to God. And although those times were not in themselves holier than the other
hours of the day, they were nevertheless held to be holier with respect to
God's precept, since they had been specially prescribed by God. Thus if they
had rejected those hours and offered the continual sacrifice at another hour,
they would have been guilty of violating God's worship. And from God's precept
rightly observed they could expect God's greater blessing and be certain that
the sacrifices offered then would be more pleasing to God than others offered
at another time devised by themselves. See Psalm 141:2.

*And therefore* (he continues) *under the New Testament no such promises are
attached to the observance of the first day out of seven as were attached to
the observance of the Seventh day in the Old Testament, but only to the
exercises and rites of piety and public worship.* Answer. 1. He acknowledges
that the Sabbath was instituted from the beginning, and its observance was
undoubtedly a part of worship. Yet we nowhere read of particular promises then
attached to its observance. 2. Those promises attached to the observance of
the seventh day were not attached

[1674 logical printed p. 346 | IA leaf 364 | PDF p. 364]

to that day because it had been prescribed by some ceremonial command, but
rather because it had been enjoined by a moral command. And therefore, by
reason of the same moral precept, those promises still have a place, just as
the promises made to the tribe of Levi, insofar as it was peculiarly separated
to God, pertain to the Ministers of the New Testament. 3. The adversaries can
argue in the same way: because the observance of the Sabbath is not enjoined
under the New Testament as it was enjoined under the Old Testament, but only
the exercises of public worship are enjoined, therefore it has no place at all
under the New Testament. But what would the most distinguished man answer?
Would he not answer that the precepts formerly given concerning observing the
Sabbath still retain their force? Why could the same not also be said of the
promises? 4. Especially since those promises were not made to the observance
of the seventh day *as such*, but insofar as it was the Sabbath, and therefore
to every Sabbath—namely, to ours no less than to the former one. 5. Obedience
rendered to God's commands always has promises attached, whether expressed or
understood. Promises of every kind are attached to piety. Therefore promises
are not lacking to this act of piety either. 6. The promises formerly made to
the Sabbath in Genesis 2, Exodus 20, Isaiah 58, and elsewhere also concern the
Sabbath for the future.

He then cites the words of Jerome's commentary, book 2, on Galatians 4. They
contain two answers to this objection: If it is not lawful to observe days,
months, times, and years, we too incur a similar charge by observing the fourth
day of the Sabbath, the Parasceve, and the Lord's Day, and so forth. The first
of these answers is this: *The one who answers this simply will say that the
days of Jewish observance are not the same as ours. For we do not celebrate
the Passover of unleavened bread, but that of the resurrection and the cross;
nor at Pentecost do we number seven weeks according to the custom of Israel,
but we venerate the advent of the Holy Spirit. And lest a disorderly assembly
of the people diminish faith in Christ, certain days were therefore appointed
so that we all might come together in one place—not so that the day on which
we assemble might be more celebrated, but so that, on whatever day we must
assemble, greater gladness might arise from seeing one another.* In this, as
the most distinguished man says, Jerome teaches that the end of the
observance of a day is that the Jewish distinction of days should not be
observed by us. But who does not see that these words oppose the most
distinguished man himself? For Jerome appears to admit no institution of the
Sabbath day other than that of Passover, Pentecost, and so forth, that is, a
merely human one. Yet the most distinguished man acknowledges that the Lord's
Day is of Apostolic institution. Therefore this distinction, resting on a
foundation which destroys the most distinguished man's own assertion, does
not prove the distinguished man's point. Nor does it do anything against us,
who say that the observation of the Sabbath rests on an altogether different
foundation, namely a divine, moral precept, indeed a precept of the first
table.

The second answer is of the same class, and it is this: *But the one who tries
to answer the question on the other side more vigorously affirms that all days
are equal, that Christ is not crucified only on the Parasceve and raised on
the Lord's Day, but that the day of the resurrection is always holy and that
he always feeds on the Lord's flesh; and that fasts and assemblies were
appointed by prudent men on account of those who are more at leisure for the
world than for God and cannot—indeed, do not wish—to assemble in the church
throughout the whole time of their lives and offer to God the sacrifices of
their prayers before human affairs. For how very few there are who always
practice even these few appointed things, whether the times for praying or for
fasting! And so, just as it is lawful for us either always to fast or always to
pray, and joyfully to celebrate the Lord's Day unceasingly after
receiving the Lord's body, it was not likewise lawful for the Jews

[1674 logical printed p. 347 | IA leaf 365 | PDF p. 365]

at every time to sacrifice a lamb, keep Pentecost, pitch tabernacles, or fast
daily. Thus Jerome.* Let us hear Dr. Walaeus's appended words. They are these:
*By these words Jerome also indicates this distinction between our observance
and that of the Jews: the Jews were bound by divine command to certain times
as parts of divine worship, but we keep this day of the week or year rather
than that other one only for the sake of maintaining order, since otherwise it
is lawful
to worship God in this way at all other times, just as is customarily done at
a time appointed by us to sharpen our negligence and preserve order.*

Answer. 1. As was said, Jerome relies on a foundation which the most
distinguished man does not approve but condemns, namely that the observance of
the Sabbath is not enjoined by any divine precept. But the most distinguished
Walaeus proves by firm arguments that by reason of the fourth precept of the
Decalogue we are bound to observe one day in the cycle of the week, and that
our Sabbath is of divine right. Jerome's reasoning therefore does nothing
against us. 2. I cannot sufficiently marvel that the learned man insinuates
that this day of the week is observed rather than that other one only for the
sake of maintaining order, and not by divine command. For this is openly to
contradict himself. 3. He likewise contradicts himself when he insinuates that the
observance of any annual day rests on the same foundation. For he will not say
that the fourth precept of the Decalogue requires us to observe some annual
day, as it requires us to observe one day in the cycle of the week. 4. What?
Do we worship God on the Sabbath day for no other end than to sharpen our
negligence and preserve order? Is it not because of the authority of God, who
commands this by his Moral Law?

It is now clear from what has been said that this most distinguished man cannot
defend himself unless he not only weakens but overthrows what he previously
proved and asserted. Indeed, unless my judgment deceives me, I believe everyone
who affirms that the fourth precept of the Decalogue commands that one day out
of seven be dedicated to God's worship and that the Lord's Day is of divine
right, yet does not wish the observance of this day determined by God—or of
Principal and Solemn, Necessary and Sufficient Time—to be a part of worship,
will do this. For if the observance of this Time is not a part of worship, all
days under the New Testament will be equal, and no day will be holier than
another by force of divine precept and institution. And if all days are thus
equal, no day will now be observed before another by force of a divine precept;
and consequently the fourth precept of the Decalogue will not now bind us to
observe one day in the cycle of the week before another. Nor will the Lord's
Day be of divine institution.

Someone objects: Time under the Old Testament was a Part of worship, not moral
but ceremonial. Therefore when that worship was abolished, Time will no longer
be a Part of worship. Answer. Although the other feast days which the Jews
celebrated pertained to ceremonial worship, this nevertheless cannot be said
of the Sabbath, whose observance was commanded in Moral Law. Much less can it
be said of Principal and Solemn, Necessary and Sufficient Time, which does not
regard the Jews alone but is enjoined upon everyone for observance until the
end of the world—that is, one whole day in the cycle of the week. We are
speaking of this Time, not of the other feast days which have now been
abrogated. We regard as a part of worship everything which God has commanded
to be offered to him as an indication of immediate honor, or which is a part
of the homage to be rendered to God. And if this is commanded by some moral
command, it will be a part of moral worship; if by a ceremonial precept,

[1674 logical printed p. 348 | IA leaf 366 | PDF p. 366]

it will be a Part of ceremonial worship. Therefore it will never be proved
that this Time is a part of ceremonial worship until it is proved that this
time is not commanded in Moral Law but only in ceremonial law; that is, until
it is proved that the fourth precept of the Decalogue is only ceremonial and
not moral. That will happen at the Greek Kalends, as will be evident from the
things to be said in books 3 and 4.

Another objects that the Sabbath is only a circumstance of the thing, or an
aid to worship—not a virtue, not a part of worship. Answer. Nathanael Jonson
refuted this objection at greater length on pp. 85 and 87, saying: *Meanwhile
these things do not conflict, nor are they opposites, because both can be
predicated of the same third thing. 1. Ames, a theoretical and practical
theologian of no small name, distinguishes the circumstances of God's worship
in book 2 of his* Marrow, *chapter 14—where he places the manner of worshiping
God among the circumstances—into inward and outward circumstances. And he
subdivides the inward ones into Antecedent, Concomitant, and Consequent, such as
the stirring of the mind, preparation, reverence, and devotion. But such
circumstances participate in the nature of worship, or virtue, as Ames says in
thesis 2 at that place. Indeed, they themselves are also worship and particular
virtues, although they are not improperly called circumstances with respect to
certain solemn exercises, since none of them is an essential constituent of
those exercises and yet they contribute to their practice antecedently,
concomitantly, and consequently. Such things among the Rhetoricians generally
come under the name of circumstances. 2. Nor is this uncommon among the
Philosophers, at least the Ethicists, when they call who, what, where, with what
aids, why, how, and when circumstances. And thus they comprehend the impulsive
causes, means, and manner of human actions within the compass of circumstances.
But in the exercises of piety, the impulsive causes, means, and manner of divine
worship have the character of virtue. 3. This especially holds with respect to
the fourth precept, where the circumstance of time—that, namely, one day out of
the number seven be consecrated to divine worship—is of the essence of the
precept and of the worship in question, since it is the essential circumstance
prescribed by that precept. But as for that which is of the essence of a
precept or of commanded worship, who would not deem it worthy of the name of
virtue?* — *But it can also easily be proved. For that which is specially
contained in this precept, and by which this precept is distinguished from the
others, is of the essence, or the essential character, of this precept. But the
determination of time, or time determined and expressly defined for performing
all God's public and private worship, is that which is specially contained in
this precept and by which it is distinguished from the others. Therefore.* —
*The aids of God's worship claim the title of virtue for themselves with no
less right. For an aid to worship either removes an impediment—as abstaining
from food on a day of supplication and resting from labors on the Sabbath—or it
denotes an instrument, a means, and inward causes cooperating in a solemn
exercise of piety. But such means or instruments of virtues are themselves
virtues in and of themselves. Thus acts of repentance, faith, and so forth
serve as means to prayer and meditation.* — *Nor are the aids which remove

premise is proved, because in that case the observance of the Sabbath or the

celebration of a Eucharistic day, as also of a day of supplication and fasting;
the dispensation of the divine word; the reading of Holy Scripture, insofar as
it is employed at the morning and evening time as an aid preparatory to prayers
