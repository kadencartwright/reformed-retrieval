# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* — Part 24

Fresh English translation of the paired normalized Latin transcription from
the 1623 first edition. The Princeton/Internet Archive page images for
`altared00cald` are the controlling witness; OCR and the 1708 Leiden reset are
reading aids only. This part begins in the lower portion of printed p. 287
with `Sed negamus id factum esse primis illis saeculis`. It contains the
historical argument about Roman cardinal presbyters, presbyters’ common
government after the division into parishes, and Roman stations. It ends in
the upper portion of p. 290 after the conclusion `ut ante divisam massam.
Ecclesiae totalis`; the immediately following inquiry into the bishop’s power
in the adjacent region is excluded. All quotations, marginalia, and citations
in the paired Latin are retained. This AI-assisted draft has not received
human review and is not publication-ready.

But we deny that this was done in those first centuries. No presbyters of the
Roman church were set over other presbyters for many centuries, as Bellarmine
himself bears witness. For although the cardinal presbyters have three
offices—one common to all other presbyters, namely, to consecrate the
Eucharist and to remit and retain sins in the forum of penance, while the
other two are peculiar to themselves, namely, the office of electing the
supreme pontiff and of assisting him continually and helping him by counsel
and industry in governing the universal church—Bellarmine says that *it is
not so ancient for the cardinals alone to discharge the*

[1623 printed p. 288 | IA leaf 300 | PDF p. 300]

*latter two offices. For from the time of the apostles, for many years and
even for some centuries, because presbyters were few, all the presbyters and
deacons were summoned together to the election of the bishop and to councils,
nor was it
then necessary to distinguish non-cardinals from cardinals, just as in other
churches canons were not distinguished from non-canons. Therefore, when
Cyprian writes several times to the Roman clergy, he does not write to the
cardinals alone but to all the presbyters and deacons of the Roman church.
And similarly, when they reply to Cyprian, all the presbyters and deacons
reply. See book 2, letter 7, and book 3, letters 5 and 21.* Bellarmine says
that when the number of the clergy had increased, all were indeed summoned
to the election but not to councils; rather, certain principal men, *who at
Rome and in some other places were called cardinals,* were summoned. But he
produces no record of this change before the time of Gregory. *This is
established,* he says, *from the synod in Gregory’s Register, book 4, chapter
88. For only thirty-four of the presbyters attended the council, and all of
them were titulars, that is, cardinals. Yet it is certain that there were then
many more presbyters at Rome than thirty-four. For if in the time of St.
Cornelius, around the year of the Lord 250, in the midst of persecutions while
idolaters reigned, there were nevertheless forty-six presbyters at Rome, as
Eusebius reports from the letter of Cornelius himself in History, book 6,
chapter 33, there were certainly many more in the time of Gregory, around the
year of the Lord 600, when there was no pagan in the city.* Dr. Field, though
a hierarchist, says the same thing: all presbyters without any distinction
participated in the election and consultations of bishops. He doubts whether
all participated in the consultations in Gregory’s time, but says it is
certain that all participated in the election of the bishop. *Finally, for
the last five hundred years,* Bellarmine says, *not all the clergy have been
admitted either to councils or to the election, because they would be too
many, but only the cardinals.* The true cause was the desire to amplify the
dignity of certain men in order to secure greater majesty for the Roman see.
They were never so numerous that they could not come together into one
consistory; and by what right were they to be excluded from the election more
in the last five hundred years than in the preceding thousand? *For divine
law has not determined that these clerics rather than those should elect,* as
Bellarmine himself admits. And why should they have been excluded from common
consultations in Gregory’s time—if that is true, though we adduced testimony
from Gregory himself above to the contrary—more than throughout so many
centuries before his age? Therefore, if in the Roman church for several
centuries, as Bellarmine says, cardinal presbyters were not distinguished
from non-cardinals, distribution among titles or parishes did not abolish the
presbyters’ common government. If this is true of the Roman church, it is much
more true of others. For, as Bellarmine says, in other churches too, canons
were not distinguished from non-canons for several centuries. Indeed, who
dares affirm as certain that the division into parishes was introduced into
the churches of other cities immediately from the time of the apostles? Just
as Siricius,

the Roman bishop, said in his letter to Ambrose, *when a presbytery had been
formed,* so Cyprian said, *it was resolved that the presbytery be convened.* Is
it likely that Cyprian, who resolved from the beginning of his episcopate to
do nothing without consulting the people, excluded some presbyters? The
bishop not only governed in common with the presbyters but also taught
indiscriminately, as did the other presbyters before the mass was divided into
particular churches, and after the division in his own separate church,
although as president of the presbytery he had oversight of the other
distinct parishes. For we do not read that the bishop ministered the word and
sacraments at his own discretion in this or that parish of the city and as
the common pastor of them all. Yet this is what ought to have happened if the
bishop were not only the universal overseer but also the pastor of all the
distinct parishes. He ought not remain in one part if he is pastor of them
all. For if individual parishes and portions of the Lord’s flock have been
assigned to individual presbyters, then either he has a separate portion of
the flock, like a presbyter, to which he too is assigned, or he has none.
If none, he is either a pastor without a flock or ought to feed the whole
flock equally; one part would not be dearer to him than another. If the
cathedral church is not peculiar to the parish in which it is situated but is
common to all, it is an idol. For if the individual parishioners attend their
own parishes, as the canons bind them to do, as Filesacus proves at length,
the bishop will preach from the cathedral as one beating the air and striking
the walls. Field adduces from Onuphrius that in the individual titles there
were certain principal churches in which baptism was administered and which
were therefore called baptismal; that in these churches the bishop preached
at his discretion and brought a numerous company with him; and that those
gatherings were called stations because they stood while praying, and hence
those churches were called stational churches or churches of the stations.
But when this ought to have been proved by examples, they produce none before
the age of Gregory, who is said to have preached in these churches. *A
station,* Bellarmine says, *was nothing other than a daytime vigil during a
solemn fast. For when people fasted publicly and solemnly, they kept vigil in
prayers, and vigils of this sort, like the watches of those praying and
fasting, were called stations, a term taken from military service. Again, St.
Gregory assigned certain places in the city of Rome to the stations and,
coming to those places at the times of the stations, delivered twenty
homilies in various churches.* Not only Gregory himself but other presbyters
also came to the places of the stations, nor is the bishop alone said to have
preached in those places. Otherwise, why would John the Deacon have said in
his Life of Gregory, book 2, chapter 18, that Gregory delivered twenty
homilies in those churches, when he ought instead to have said that Gregory,
by virtue of his office, gave homilies in the places he had assigned to the
stations? Not only the people but also the presbyters came together for those
solemn gatherings; and since the basilica at which the station was announced

could not contain so great a multitude of people, while all nevertheless
wished to take part in the station, the custom was introduced that now these,
now those would go to the place of the station. Thus from early morning until
evening there were never lacking those who prayed to God and praised him in
the church. And the bishop alone, of course, presided over these gatherings in
divine worship from early morning until evening. Just as the distinct
parishes came together as one on these more solemn occasions, so too their
presbyters fed and governed in common as they had before the mass of the whole
church was divided.
