THE THESES TO BE DISCUSSED AT PRESENT ARE AS FOLLOWS:

Articles of faith are not to be measured by the standard of human reason.

Christ truly and properly satisfied divine justice for the sins of the
elect.

ON THE FORMER.

Since Justin Martyr honored reason with the title “the divine Word,” while,
on Saint Augustine's authority, no sober person has disputed against it, an
attempt to detract from it in even the smallest degree may appear not only
atheological but altogether unworthy of that philosophy which is called
sober. Especially in an academy—the most celebrated workshop of universal
reason and, so to speak, its court—even to mutter such a thing secretly, much
less to declare it openly and clearly before the public assembly of all, is
very far removed from propriety. But our institution belongs to Solomon's
porch, as Tertullian once said: we are engaged in the school of Christ, not
of Aristotle, as a recent writer put it. We meet today not at Athens but at
Jerusalem, not in Zeno's Stoa or Plato's Academy but in a temple sacred to
Christ. Allow me, then, with your kind permission, excellent hearers, not to
cast this Hagar out of the house but to subject her to her mistress. I mean
reason, which I would not wish to remove from her place, but would gladly
compel to keep her proper rank, so that she might once learn not to rule
over faith but to serve it, to render obedience and not arrogate dominion to
herself. For, to confess the truth frankly, Philip Melanchthon's very grave
complaint has always carried the greatest weight with me: although Christian
doctrine differs entirely from philosophy, philosophy nevertheless crept
gradually into Christianity, and Christ's beneficence was obscured by that
profane and animal wisdom of our reason. The term *free will* was taken up
from divine literature in a sense and with a judgment utterly foreign to

[1679 new-series fol. 2 | IA leaf 322]

the Spirit. The equally pernicious term *reason* was added from Platonic
philosophy. Aristotle was embraced with the greatest ardor in place of
Christ; Christ's doctrine was undermined by Platonic philosophy, and so on.
These are his words. By this charge Bellarmine indeed takes a beating, but
Melanchthon touches a present-day ulcer, for which the thesis now to be
discussed provides a remedy.

Lest I appear to be staging only a battle with shadows, I must briefly
explain which adversaries I face in this arena.

1. First are the pagan philosophers, formerly the supreme arbiters and
champions of reason and, on that account, in Tertullian's phrase, the
patriarchs of the heresiarchs. Yet they were the most hostile enemies of the
Christian faith. When that faith surpassed or overthrew their philosophical
dogmas, they called it foolishness while becoming fools themselves above
all, 1 Corinthians 1:23. Thus Averroes said that to believe something true
which reason cannot grasp was a sign of levity. Likewise, we read that the
word-crafting Athenians called Paul a seed-picker in Acts 17:18. Celsus in
Origen similarly called faith, if it lacked a rational guide and believed
something beyond or above reason, the repository of every error—indeed, of
all deception.

2. Second are the ancient heretics. Some of them preferred reason to divine
authority; Augustine speaks of them in Epistle 56 and Bernard in Epistle
189. Alfonso de Castro also notes that Felicianus said arguments should be
used in a cause concerning faith more than testimonies of Scripture.

Others, despising faith, were wholly occupied with syllogisms and, having
abandoned the study of Sacred Scripture, applied themselves to geometry, as
we read in Eusebius, book 5, final chapter. Against these we set Augustine's
statement: “The Paraclete Spirit does not teach these truths, for he wishes
to make Christians, not mathematicians.” We also set against them the words
of Epiphanius: “God does not reveal the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit
to those who reason about him by syllogisms.”

3. Third are the papal scholastics. Having routed the more ancient and purer
theology resting on Scripture and the sounder doctrine of the Fathers, they
often appeal to philosophers but very rarely to the apostles, as Sadeel
says of them. They lay down not passages of Scripture but, for the most
part, logical axioms, topical maxims, and sayings of Aristotle—often poorly
read and still more often poorly understood—as the principles of an
irrefutable demonstration in a matter of faith.

4. Fourth are the Remonstrants and their followers, men sharp enough but
far too audacious in applying their measuring rods to the divine decrees.
As Saint Athanasius puts it, they “measure Christ's flesh geometrically.” If
they discover that God's decrees do not conform at every point to the
standard of their own reason, they rashly accuse those decrees—to say the
least—of crookedness, injustice, and tyranny, not without blasphemy.

5. Fifth are the Photinians, Socinians, and the rest of the Gnostics of the
same stamp. Since they are not consistent with themselves—or rather, so that
they may more cunningly conceal what they truly think—they boast that they
themselves attribute everything to Scripture, and say that all articles of
faith are contained literally in Scripture. Thus Smalcius. Indeed, Jacob
Palaeologus in Bellarmine says that something may be false according to
Scripture which is true in philosophy. But when the curtain is drawn aside
and they declare the opinions of their heart, they insist that every truth
of faith be deduced from things simply prior and better known. They employ
universal notions to be applied univocally and equally to God and creatures;
and, in order to rave with reason, they adopt the exploded position of both
our theologians and the papists concerning the judge of controversies:

[1679 new-series fol. 3 | IA leaf 323]

that human reason is not subordinated but coordinated with God's Word, and
that it is both the rule and judge of faith. Hence come these and similar
pronouncements of theirs: “Possibility or impossibility of the articles of
faith can be judged by reason alone.” “Things which seem impossible to the
mind are not to be believed.” “The sum of religion is reason.” Thus someone
recently even blurted out from the judicial bench that “the best law of God
is the law of reason.”

Our present thesis fights principally against these adversaries and plainly
denies that the articles of faith are to be measured by the standard of
human reason.

As for the explanation of the terms, when I say *articles of faith*:

1. First, I mean the theological articles themselves, not their
connections. With Augustine, I distinguish the truth of connections from
the truth of propositions. The school of dialecticians teaches the former;
the school of Christ teaches the latter. Human reason rises to the former
of itself, but not to the latter without divine revelation, Ephesians
1:17–18.

2. Second, the articles of faith in a broad sense contain all theological
truths which may be believed and accord with the analogy of faith; in the
strict sense, they contain only those which are simply necessary for
salvation and therefore must necessarily be believed by everyone. We do not
strongly object to granting reason somewhat more scope in discerning the
former, but in the latter we depend wholly on the guiding star of the Word
and faith. “To the law and to the testimony,” Isaiah 8:20.

3. Third, we further add that articles of faith strictly understood either
belong to theology and natural law and are shared with sound philosophy—for
example, that God is omnipotent, eternal, just, truthful, good, and therefore
to be worshiped—or they belong to theology and purely supernatural truth,
as do those concerning the holy Trinity, Christ's incarnation, his and our
resurrection, and the like. Although in the former matters faith reaches
many things beyond and above reason, it nevertheless defers greatly to
reason. These matters, since they constitute the presupposed object of
faith, are not our present question. We are concerned instead with the most
formal and proper object of faith—namely, supernatural truths of faith which
must not be brought before the tribunal of human reason.

4. Fourth, it must also be added that in the articles of faith we should
carefully distinguish *that it is* from *what it is*, and *why it is* from
*what sort of thing it is*. In matters belonging to natural theology, nature
establishes *that it is*, Psalm 19:1–2 and Romans 1:21; in supernatural
matters this becomes known by revelation alone, John 1:18. Yet in both, at
least in some of their subjects, *what it is*, *why it is*, and *what sort
of thing it is* exceed every capacity of reason, Romans 11:33–34.

Let this suffice concerning the articles of faith.

As for *reason*, it is either objective on the side of the thing or
subjective on the side of the intellect. The question is not primarily
concerned with objective reason. For that reason of a thing is nothing
other in itself than God's own wisdom, as the apostle calls it in 1
Corinthians 1:21, implanted in things, according to which God first ordered
both his decrees and his works and then executes and governs them. It must
therefore be granted that just as the truth and reason of things in natural
matters is the measure of the intellect, so in these supernatural mysteries
this divine reason may safely be called the rule of faith. This must,
however, be understood as follows:

1. This divine reason is the rule insofar as it has been revealed by the
Spirit in the Word. Faith

[1679 new-series fol. 4 | IA leaf 324]

rests upon divine testimony, just as among the Hebrews *to be faithful* and
*to believe* are mutually related as correlatives.

2. Faith must hold the matter and certainly believe it, provided that it is
revealed in Sacred Scripture, although in some cases the reason of the
thing is not equally known. For, as Bonaventure says somewhere, it belongs
to the rational character of virtue that faith should believe without a
reason. This was characteristic of Abraham's faith: “Against hope he
believed in hope,” Romans 4:18, and again, “By faith, when called, he went
out, not knowing where he was going,” Hebrews 11:8.

The thesis, however, concerns reason taken subjectively on the side of the
intellect and therefore calls it *human reason*. This may be considered
according to the fourfold state of humanity: whole at creation, corrupted
by the fall, partly healed by grace, and eventually perfected by glory.

We are not now treating the first and last of those states. For many things
disputed concerning the state of innocence are altogether unknown, and the
ultimate reach of the beatific vision in heaven must be placed among the
things which no eye has seen and which have not entered into the human
heart. We therefore remain silent about them and speak of human reason as
it stands either in the state of sin or of grace. Thus the term means:

1. First and properly, that faculty of the rational soul in a human being
by which it apprehends and judges intelligible things.

2. Second and metonymically, in this controversy it embraces every light of
knowledge by whose help the human mind understands and perceives
theological matters. This light may be:

First, innate and implanted by nature, consisting in those common notions,
both in things to be believed, Romans 1:19–21, and in things to be done,
Romans 2:14–15.

Second, acquired apart from revelation by instruction, meditation,
industry, and the like through common assistance, as formerly among the
pagan philosophers and today among educated and more learned people.

Third, divinely revealed in the church, whether merely externally through
the Word and outward means, Romans 2:17–18, or also inwardly and savingly
through the Holy Spirit, 1 Corinthians 2:12. I mean the “Spirit of wisdom
and revelation,” as the apostle says in Ephesians 1:17: not only of
*revelation*, by revealing and presenting the matter on the side of the
object, as the Pelagians of old acknowledged, but also of *wisdom*, by
illuminating the faculty on the side of the subject and, as it were,
producing the power of sight in the mind. The Pelagians, Semi-Pelagians,
Ticonius, Cassian, and at one time Augustine himself denied this.

These points especially form the hinge of this controversy. To explain its
state more clearly, since we deny that articles of faith are to be measured
by the standard of human reason, we must state distinctly and demonstratively
what is to be granted and what is wholly to be denied in this matter.
