QUESTION XXI.

ON THE HOLY SUPPER.

What is the Holy Supper, and by what principal names is it distinguished both
in Scripture and among the ancients?

[Marginalia: The mystery of the Holy Supper entangled in various
controversies.]

I. The other sacrament of the New Testament is the Holy Supper, in which,
through the distribution and reception of broken bread and poured-out wine,
communion in the body of Christ broken on the cross for believers and in his
blood poured out for them unto eternal life is declared and sealed to them.
The more illustrious and important for believers’

[1847 vol. III printed p. 360 | IA leaf 394 | PDF p. 394]

consolation this mystery of the Christian religion is, the greater the
violence with which Satan has
always attempted to obscure and corrupt it, so as to deprive the church of
this inestimable treasure. Hence it has come about that in no part of religion
have disputes been stirred up at such length and with such vehemence. Indeed,
the papists finally appear to have placed the sum of theology in this head, so
thoroughly have they left nothing in either theology or philosophy that they
have not brought here and compelled, once collected, to serve their tenets.

[Marginalia: The various names by which it is commonly expressed. 1. The
Lord’s Supper. 1 Cor. 11:20.]

II. It is distinguished by various names both in Scripture and among ancient
and more recent writers. First, Scripture calls it *deipnon kyriakon* (δεῖπνον
Κυριακόν), the Lord’s Supper (1 Cor. 11:20). It is called a supper with
respect to the time, because it was first instituted and celebrated in the
evening and substituted for the paschal supper. Hence the name was retained,
although in subsequent times it was celebrated not in the evening but in the
morning before any food had been taken. So also feasts in pagan sacred rites
were called *deipna* (δεῖπνα) rather than *arista* (ἄριστα). It is called the
Lord’s because of the Lord, who is its author, matter, and end, since Christ
immediately instituted it to be celebrated in memory of his death and to give
believers communion in his body and blood.

III. Our opponents wrongly raise a dispute with us here over this name, as
though we improperly refer it to the Eucharist when it pertains instead to
the Christian love feasts that were commonly celebrated at that time.
Maldonatus does this in his comments on Matthew 26:26, where he tries to show
that “this term for the sacrament neither occurs anywhere in Scripture nor was
ever used by the fathers.” He even affirms that “the fathers considered it
unlawful to call this sacrament a supper, but the Calvinists,” he says, “call
it a supper without the authority of Scripture, without the example of
ancient authors, without reason, and without any judgment.” Yet how
disingenuously he acts through an “immoderate counter-pulling” (*ametrian tēs
antholkēs*, ἀμετρίαν τῆς ἀνθολκῆς) is evident even from the fact that the
Tridentine Catechism retains the word and acknowledges that it was used by the
fathers. It says, “The most ancient fathers, following the authority of the
apostle, sometimes also called the sacred Eucharist by the name of the Supper,
because it was instituted by Christ the Lord in the mystery of that last
supper.” Baronius admits that “the term was used by the ancients, but the
innovators abuse it.” Vasques likewise acknowledges that “the ancients used
it, but now it must be avoided, lest our terms be shared with heretics.” How
falsely he asserts that the fathers never called the Eucharist this can be
shown by Chrysostom alone. On this passage he interprets *kyriakon deipnon*
(Κυριακὸν δεῖπνον) as *to despotikon* (τὸ δεσποτικὸν), “the Master’s supper,”
and maintains that “the awesome mysteries” (*ta phrikta mystēria*, τὰφρικτὰ
μυστήρια) are intimated by this name, thus designating the Lord’s Supper.

IV. Although we do not deny that love feasts, through which Christians
attested their mutual love, were added to the celebration of the Holy Supper,
it does not follow that the Eucharist itself was not properly designated by
this name. A comparison with verse 25 establishes that Christ celebrated the
Eucharist while eating supper, for he is said to have taken the sacramental
cup *meta to deipnēsai* (μετὰ τὸ δειπνῆσαι), “after supper.” Nor, if the
apostle reproves abuses that arose among the Corinthians in the meals added to
the celebration of the Eucharist—such as a lack of love, contempt for the
poor, drunkenness, and gluttony—can it be inferred from this that by the name
“supper” he wished to designate only such meals and not the Eucharist itself.
His purpose is nothing other than to prescribe to them the manner of rightly
celebrating this mystery.

[Marginalia: 2. Thanksgiving and blessing.]

V. Second, it is called *eulogia* (εὐλογία), “blessing,” and *eucharistia*
(εὐχαριστία), “thanksgiving,” by Paul (1 Cor. 10:16), where he calls the
sacramental cup *to potērion tēs eulogias ho eulogoumen* (τὸ ποτήριον τῆς
εὐλογίας ὃ εὐλογοῦμεν), “the cup of blessing which we bless.” For when Christ
had taken the bread and wine, he is said *eulogēsai* (εὐλογῆσαι), “to have
blessed,” and *eucharistēsai* (εὐχαριστῆσαι), “to have given thanks.” Hence
the entire action was called *eulogia* and *eucharistia*. The *cup of blessing*
answers to the paschal cup with which

[1847 vol. III printed p. 361 | IA leaf 395 | PDF p. 395]

the meal was concluded, called כוס הלל, *potērion hymnēseōs* (ποτήριον
ὑμνήσεως), “the cup of praise.” Hence in the Greek liturgies this is called
*thysia aineseōs* (θυσία αἰνέσεως), “a sacrifice of praise.” Now this
*eulogia*, or blessing, has regard to two things: God and the sacred symbols.
1. God is blessed and thanks are given to him for the excellent benefit with
which he has honored us in Christ. Chrysostom explains this very well on this
passage: “When I say ‘blessing,’ I unfold the whole treasure of God’s
beneficence and call to mind those great gifts and ineffable benefits. For we
also approach this cup recalling God’s ineffable benefits and everything we
have received; thus we make mention of it and share it with one another,
giving thanks that he has delivered the human race from error.” Theophylact
says on the same passage: “The cup of blessing—that is, of thanksgiving—for
when we hold it in our hands, we praise him and give him thanks, because he
poured out his blood for us and deigned to confer ineffable good things upon
us.” 2. The symbols themselves are blessed, that is, consecrated and
sanctified for sacred uses. Christ *eulogēsas eklase* (εὐλογήσας ἔκλασε) the
bread, “blessed and broke it.” Therefore *to eulogein* (τὸ εὐλογεῖν), “to
bless,” and *to hagiazein* (τὸ ἁγιάζειν), “to sanctify,” are the same in this
sense. Throughout the fathers *to hagiazein* is said of the signs when they
are consecrated for sacramental use. Hence *ta hagiasmata* (τὰ ἁγιάσματα),
“the sanctified things,” is among their names for the Eucharist. *Eulogia*
sometimes denotes the entire Eucharistic action, while at other times
individual wafers or portions given to communicants are called *eulogiai*
(εὐλογίαι). They also called by this name portions of consecrated bread that
it was customary in the primitive church to send to other parishes, as well
as to travelers from abroad, to declare love and agreement in the faith. This
practice was later changed at the Council of Laodicea, canon 14.

[Marginalia: 3. Table.]

VI. Third, it is called *Trapeza Kyriou* (Τράπεζα Κυρίου), “the table of the
Lord” (1 Cor. 10:21): “You cannot partake of the table of the Lord and the
table of demons.” “Table” is said by a metonymy of the container for the
contents, because the Supper was celebrated at or upon a table, where the
sacred symbols were set out. From this it is inferred that altars were still
unknown at that time. It is called “the Lord’s table” both because the Lord is
the author who prepares it (Prov. 9:1–2) and because he is the very food set
out on that table, namely, the Lord’s own flesh. It is opposed to “the table
of demons,” which was spread in an idol’s temple for the idolatrous feasts of
the pagans, in which the flesh of victims sacrificed to idols and, beneath
them, to demons was set out. Paul says that believers cannot partake of both
tables, that is, that it is not lawful or fitting to do so rightly and with a
good conscience because of the greatest opposition between Christ and demons
(2 Cor. 6:16). In this sense he said that they cannot “drink the cup of the
Lord,” that is, the cup instituted by the Lord and drunk in honor of the Lord,
and the cup of demons, which is drunk in honor of demons. Moreover, this
sacred table is distinguished by various epithets among the ancients:
Chrysostom calls it “the awesome and mystical table” in homily 1 on Genesis
and “the royal table” in homily 24 on 1 Corinthians; he also frequently calls
it “immortal and divine.” Theodoret calls it “the rational table” in discourse
6 *On Providence*, and there are other terms of this kind.

[Marginalia: 4. Communion.]

VII. Fourth, it is called *Koinōnia* (Κοινωνία), “Communion,” from Paul (1
Cor. 10:16): “The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of
the blood of Christ?” and so forth. Here Paul does not properly give this name
to the Supper but explains the nature and end of this mystery when he says
that the sacred symbols are the communion of the body and blood of Christ,
that is, that they were instituted to seal to us communion in Christ’s body
and blood. Hence the fathers everywhere came to signify this entire action by
the name *Koinōnia*. In Dionysius the Areopagite, *Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*,
chapter 3, this title is prefixed: *Mystērion synaxeōs eitoun koinōnias*
(Μυστήριον συνάξεως εἴτουν κοινωνίας), “the mystery of synaxis, or communion.”
Chrysostom in homily 10 on John calls it “the spiritual and awesome communion
of the mysteries.” It is rightly called this: 1. because of the union of
believers

[1847 vol. III printed p. 362 | IA leaf 396 | PDF p. 396]

with Christ and with one another, which is sealed in this sacrament; 2.
because of communion in his death and benefits; 3. because it was formerly
offered in common to all believers in the assemblies.

[Marginalia: 5. Breaking of bread. Acts 2:42.]

VIII. Fifth, it is called *Klasis artou* (Κλάσις ἄρτου), “the breaking of
bread,” from Acts 2:42, where the disciples are said to have persevered in the
apostles’ doctrine, *tē koinōnia, kai tē klasei tou artou* (τῇ κοινωνίᾳ, καὶ
τῇ κλάσει τοῦ ἄρτου), “in communion and in the breaking of bread,” that is, in
the communion of broken bread, or the communion that occurs through the
breaking of bread. That not common but sacred and Eucharistic bread is denoted
is evident both from the Syriac interpreter, who translates it “in the
breaking of the Eucharist,” and from comparison with Acts 20:7, where the
disciples are said to have assembled *tou klasai arton* (τοῦ κλάσαι ἄρτον),
“to break bread,” that is, to celebrate the Lord’s Supper. It is so called
from the rite of Christ, who “broke bread,” not because it was administered
under only the one species, as the papists today maintain. That this is false
can be established by several arguments, as will be proved in its proper
place. Rather, it is a synecdoche from the idiom of the Hebrews, who by a
customary expression commonly denote a meal by the breaking of bread. “To
break bread with someone” is to *syndeipnein* (συνδειπνεῖν), “eat with him.”
The Greeks, for their part, commonly designate the whole action of a meal by
the drink, namely by *to symposion* (τὸ συμπόσιον), “the drinking party.”
Furthermore, bread is said to be “broken” rather than cut because the Hebrews
used flat cakes, which were broken rather than cut with a knife. This name
occurs frequently among the fathers.

[Marginalia: 6. Synaxis.]

IX. In addition to these names drawn from Scripture, several others occur
among the fathers and ecclesiastical writers. Among them is *Synaxis*
(Σύναξις), “assembly,” because this sacrament is ordinarily administered in
the church’s public gatherings.

[Marginalia: 7. Liturgy.]

Another is *Leitourgia* (Λειτουργία), “liturgy,” because it is no insignificant
part of the public sacred ministry. In general, *leitourgia* is a public work,
or a work done publicly, and *leitourgein* (λειτουργεῖν) means to render public
service. From the church’s usage, this common word is transferred to divine
things and has (1) at times a broader and improper meaning for any work of
piety, as in Romans 15:27, *leitourgein en tois sarkikois* (λειτουργεῖν ἐν
τοῖς σαρκικοῖς), “to minister in carnal things,” that is, to administer aid
to the poor; and in 2 Corinthians 9:12 it denotes beneficence toward the poor.
(2) It has a narrower and proper meaning in general for discharging a sacred
function in the church. Thus it is used in Hebrews 10:11 of the priests of the
Old Testament, who are said *leitourgein*, “to minister.” So Christ is called
*leitourgos tōn hagiōn* (λειτουργὸς τῶν ἁγίων), “a minister of the holy
things” (Heb. 8:2). In this sense Paul calls the preaching of the gospel a
*leitourgia* (Rom. 15:16). Hence among the ancients certain descriptions of
the order to be observed in celebrating sacred rites were called
*leitourgiai*. Such are those circulated today under the names of Peter,
James, Andrew, and so forth. Later the word was used specifically to denote
the whole ministry performed in connection with the Holy Supper. Hence in
Novel 7 Justinian, when describing the *hiera leitourgia* (ἱερὰν λειτουργίαν),
“sacred liturgy,” enumerates its two parts: the recitation of the Holy Supper
and communion. Although this word is sometimes referred by the ancients to the
Holy Supper, our opponents wrongly draw from this an argument for the sacrifice
of the Mass, as though it occurred in Scripture in Acts 13:2. Cotonus presses
this argument, followed by Veronus and other missionary sophists. 1. Nowhere
in Scripture is the word *leitourgia* restricted to the celebration of the
Holy Supper; it is referred generally to sacred ministry. 2. Luke’s words do
not concern a sacrifice but the ministry of the gospel to which Barnabas and
Paul were being called, and the fasting and prayers by which they consecrated
them to it. 3. The Vulgate reads, “As they ministered to the Lord.” Lyranus
explains this as “in good works according to their ranks and offices.” The
Salamanca Bible says, “As they worked for the Lord,” which the notes explain
as “to announce the gospel.” Cajetan says, “The kind of ministry is not
explained, but from the preceding statement that there were prophets and
teachers in that church, it is intimated that they ministered to the Lord

[1847 vol. III printed p. 363 | IA leaf 397 | PDF p. 397]

by prophesying and teaching.” Justinian says on this passage, “I cannot approve
what I see very many vehemently embrace: that the Greek word *leitourgein*,
when used specifically in the sacred writings, is to be understood of sacred
ministry and sacrifice.”

[Marginalia: 8. Sacrifice.]

X. It is also often called *Thysia* (Θυσια), “sacrifice,” and with various
epithets: sometimes *hagia thysia* (ἁγία θυσία), “holy sacrifice”; sometimes
*phriktē kai mystikē thysia* (φρικτὴ καὶ μυστικὴ θυσία), “awesome and mystical
sacrifice”; sometimes *anaimaktos thysia* (ἀναίμακτος θυσία), “bloodless
sacrifice”; and sometimes *logikē thysia* (λογικὴ θυσία), “rational
sacrifice.” The reasons are not that they considered it to be a properly so
called *hilastikon* (ἱλαστικόν), or propitiatory sacrifice, for that was very
far from their thought, as will be proved in its proper place. Rather, there
are chiefly these four reasons. 1. There was in it a collection and offering
of alms, bread, wine, and so forth, which are a spiritual sacrifice. From
these offerings the symbols used in Eucharistic communion were taken, and
alms were distributed to the poor. 2. It is a representation and
commemoration of the sacrifice offered on the cross, and therefore they call
it *anaimakton thysian* (ἀναίμακτον θυσίαν), a bloodless sacrifice, because it
represents the bloody sacrifice. See Eusebius, *Demonstration of the Gospel*,
book 1, chapter 10, and Theodoret, *Epistle to the Hebrews*, chapter 8. 3. It
is a Eucharistic sacrifice of praise and blessing, which we offer to God as a
testimony of gratitude for the work of redemption. Every religious action by
which we consecrate something to God for his glory and our salvation is called
a sacrifice in the usage of the ancients. Augustine says in *The City of
God*, book 10, chapter 6: “A true sacrifice is every work we perform in order
that we may remain united to God and be perfectly blessed.”

[Marginalia: 9. Love feast.]

XI. The name “Agape” is also given to it. Tertullian says in the *Apology*,
chapter 39: “Our supper shows its rationale by its name, for it is called
*Agapē* (Αγάπη), which among the Greeks means love.” Agapes were the fraternal
meals of an ecclesiastical gathering. Jude 12 speaks of profane seducers in
this connection: “These are *en tais agapais hymōn spilades* (ἐν ταῖς ἀγάπαις
ὑμῶν σπιλάδες), stains in your feasts.” On this passage Œcumenius says, “At
that time there were still tables prepared in the churches, which they also
called agapes.” Their end was either general, that mutual love among Christians
might be fostered through them, as the name itself shows, or special, that the
poor might be assisted by this refreshment, as Chrysostom observes on this
passage. But since celebration of the Holy Supper was joined with them, the
name “Agape” was therefore given to the Supper. At first they preceded the use
of the Holy Supper, as is established from 1 Corinthians 11:20, where Paul
reproves an abuse that had gained ground among the Corinthians: those meals
were not communal, but each person privately ate what he had brought from
home, while the rich despised the poor. Paul instead wishes all to eat in
common, so that the rich might refresh the poor from their abundance;
otherwise, he teaches, they would sin grievously against Christ’s command and
would not celebrate the Lord’s Supper rightly and as they ought. In subsequent
times, however, the agapes were celebrated after the Holy Supper. This appears
from the Council of Carthage celebrated in the time of Aurelius, which decreed
in canon 4 that “the sacraments of the altar are to be celebrated only by
those who are fasting.” See also Chrysostom, homily 27 on 1 Corinthians 11.
These public agapes were removed from churches because of various abuses, as
canon 28 of the Council of Laodicea records: “It is not fitting to hold the
so-called agapes in basilicas or churches and to eat in the house of God.” The
name remained in ecclesiastical use, so that meals prepared to refresh the
poor and travelers were called *agapai* (ἀγάπαι). It is said that the Greeks
preserve traces of this custom even today. On the sacred day of the Lord’s
resurrection, after holy communion in the mysteries, they bring food into the
church, feast together, and sing a solemn song in approximately these words:
*Christos aneste ek nekrōn, thanatō thanaton patēsas, kai tēn en tois mnēmasi
zōēn charisamenos* (Χριστος ἀνέστε ἐκ νεκρων, θανάτῳ θάνατον πατήσας, καὶ την
ἐν τοῖς μνήμασι ζωην χαρισάμενος): “Christ has risen from the dead, trampling
death by death and giving life to those who were in the tombs.” This is
gathered from Cassander’s liturgies. Our most closely associated colleague,
the reverend and celebrated Antonius Legerus, *ho makaritēs* (ὁ μακαρίτης),
“of blessed memory,” who was formerly a most faithful pastor in this

[1847 vol. III printed p. 364 | IA leaf 398 | PDF p. 398]

church and a most worthy professor of theology in the academy, related that
when he lived in Constantinople at the court of the most excellent Belgic
ambassador, he observed this more than once, especially “in the distribution
of *tōn eulogiōn* (τῶν εὐλογιῶν), that is, blessed bread and wine.”

[Marginalia: 10. Mystery.]

XII. *Mystērion* (Μυστήριον), “mystery,” is also very frequently used to
designate this sacrament. Although it is said in general of all the heads of
the Christian religion, and specifically of the sacraments, which are
sometimes expressed by *hagia kai achranta mystēria* (ἅγια καὶ ἄχραντα
μυστήρια), “holy and undefiled mysteries,” it is said in a special sense of
the Supper because of the mystical participation in the body and blood of
Christ, which far surpasses our comprehension. Hence among them *metalambanein
kai metechein mystēriōn* (μεταλαμβάνειν et μετέχειν μυστηρίων) means to
partake of the Holy Supper. They are not only called mysteries, but are also
given various epithets: *theia* (θεῖα), “divine”; *thaumastika* (θαυμαστικά),
“admirable”; *zōopoia* (ζωοποιά), “life-giving”; *hagia* (ἅγια), “holy”;
*telestika* (τελεστικά), “perfecting”; *phrikta phrikōdestata* (φρικτὰ
φρικωδέστατα), “most awesome”; *aporrēta* (ἀπόῤῥητα), “secret”; and the like.

XIII. Several other terms occur among the Greeks by which this sacrament is
designated. Among these are *ta hagiasmata* (τα ἁγιάσματα), “the sanctified
things,” in Basil’s liturgy; *metechein tōn hagiasmatōn* (μετέχειν τῶν
ἁγιασμάτων), “to partake of the sacrament”; *hai hagiai merides* (αἱ ἅγιαι
μερίδες), “the sacred portions,” into which the bread to be distributed was
broken; and *ta dōra* (τὰ δῶρα), “the gifts,” because offerings of bread and
wine were commonly made for Holy Communion in the ancient church, so that as
much as was sufficient for consecration to Eucharistic use might be taken from
them. Another term is *Ephodion* (Ἐφόδιον), “provision for the journey.” In
various liturgies God is asked that *genētai hē metalēpsis tōn hagiasmatōn eis
ephodion zōēs aiōniou* (γένηται ἡ μετάληψις τῶν ἁγιασμάτων εἰς ἐφόδιον ζωῆς
αἰωνίου), “the participation in the sanctified things may serve as provision
for the journey to eternal life.” We pass over listing other terms of this
kind, as well as the other epithets by which the Holy Supper is commonly
distinguished among them, such as *pharmakon athanasias* (φάρμακον
ἀθανασίας), “medicine of immortality”; *kathartērion alexikakon* (καθαρτήριον
ἀλεξίκακον), “a cleansing antidote to evil”; *teletē teletōn* (τελετὴ
τελετῶν), “the rite of rites”; *blabēs pantodapous amyntērion* (βλάβης
παντοδαποῦς ἀμυντήριον), “a defense against every kind of harm”; and the like.

[Marginalia: Masses.]

XIV. Among the names by which Latin writers designated this sacrament, the
principal one is “Mass.” Baronius asserts far too confidently, but most
falsely, that it is the most ancient name of all. He maintains that James the
brother of the Lord handed down this designation to the Jerusalemites and
Paul to the Romans.

[Marginalia: Its etymology.]

There are various opinions about its etymology. Some, in order to commend it
with greater authority from antiquity, maintain that it is of Hebrew origin,
as though derived from the word מס, which means “tribute.” Hence מסת נדבה,
which is treated in Deuteronomy 16:10; or from משתה, which means “banquet,”
from Isaiah 25:6, where God is said to make a משתה, a banquet, for the peoples
on this mountain, and so forth. Others maintain that it is Greek, drawn from
the word *myēsis* (μύησις), which denotes initiation. But learned men among
our opponents themselves long ago observed that it is Latin. They do not,
however, agree among themselves about its rationale and cause. Hugh of St.
Victor maintains that it is called “Mass” because in it the offering and
prayers are sent to God. The Master of the Sentences says it is because an
angel is sent by God to assist at the sacrifice and carry it to God. But this
is less probable to Bellarmine. Others derive it from the gifts sent by which
the Supper and the agape were furnished and which were shared with the poor.
Far more certain, however, is the opinion of those who maintain that it is
called “Mass” from the sending away or dismissal of the people, just as
*remissa* is often used for *remissio*, “dismissal,” by Tertullian and
Cyprian. This custom was derived from the pagans themselves. In certain of
their sacrifices the practice had taken hold for the lictor to cry, “Let the
enemy, the prisoner, the woman, and the virgin depart,” as Festus notes. So
also at the initiation of the Eleusinian rites, the impious and wicked were
removed at the herald’s call, as Suetonius notes in *Nero*. This custom was
turned against the Christians themselves, who at the beginning of the
mysteries were ordered to leave the temple with the Epicureans. Imitating this
custom, as they did many others, the ancient Christians had their own masses,
or dismissals of the people. Polydore Vergil observes in *On the Inventors of
Things*, book 5, chapter 12, that when their sacred rites had ended, the pagan
priests

[1847 vol. III printed p. 365 | IA leaf 399 | PDF p. 399]

cried *tois laois aphesis* (τοῖς λαοῖς ἄφεσις), “dismissal for the peoples.”
The same custom is therefore also observed by us: when the sacred rites have
ended, the deacon pronounces, “*Ite, missa est*,” which is the same as “*I,
licet*,” that is, “You may go.” In Roman courts the herald cried “*I,
licet*,” and thus it was understood that the judges had been given permission
to depart.

[Marginalia: The twofold Mass, of the catechumens and of the faithful.]

XV. Moreover, a twofold Mass is distinguished among the ancients: one of the
catechumens and the other of the faithful. By the first the catechumens were
dismissed with the possessed and the uninitiated before the celebration of
the mysteries, after the reading of Scripture, because no one who was not
baptized was permitted to be present. Augustine says in sermon 237 *On
Time*: “Behold, after the sermon the Mass of the catechumens occurs; the
faithful will remain.” Isidore says in *Origins*, book 6, chapter 19: “Mass is
at the time of the sacrifice, when the catechumens are sent outside as the
Levite cries, ‘If any catechumen remains, let him go outside.’ Hence it is
called Mass, because those who are known not yet to have been regenerated
cannot be present at the sacraments of the altar.” By the other Mass the
faithful were dismissed after the sacred rites had been completed. When the
celebration of the Eucharist had ended, after the *euchēn apolytikēn* (εὐχὴν
ἀπολυτικὴν), or dismissal prayer, the deacon pronounced, “*Ite, missa est*,”
and the people were thereby given permission to depart. Alcuin says in *On
the Divine Offices*, chapter 40: “When everything has ended, release is given
to the people standing by as the deacon cries, ‘*Ite, missa est*.’ Mass is
therefore understood as nothing other than dismissal, that is, the release
which, once everything has been celebrated, the deacon then declares has
taken place when the people are dismissed from the solemn observance, and so
forth. The Mass of the catechumens therefore occurred before the action of the
sacraments, and the Mass of the faithful after the consecration and
participation of those sacraments.” Hence the name “Mass” came to be given to
the whole action, whence the phrases “to hear Mass” and “to attend Mass.” And
as terms are gradually drawn into another meaning over the course of time,
the name “Mass” began to be used for the prayers composed before or after the
dismissal of the catechumens. Finally, after many stages, the word “Mass”
settled into signifying the propitiatory sacrifice of Christ’s body for the
living and the dead under the species of bread and wine. Thus this term,
unheard of in the primitive church, did not become prevalent until the purity
of the Latin language and the simplicity of the Christian religion were
declining, and barbarism occupied languages and superstition occupied minds.

XVI. From these things it is easily inferred how unreasonably they contend so
vehemently to retain this name and judge it preferable to all others. Yet it
is the least suitable name for designating this sacrament, being drawn from no
part of this mystery: neither its matter nor form, nor even the rites added to
either, nor its efficient cause or effects, but from the most accidental of
all things, namely, the dismissal of the people. And how much more justly do
we use the terms handed down by Scripture itself, such as the Lord’s Supper,
Communion, Eucharist, and others of this kind.
