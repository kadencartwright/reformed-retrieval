CHAPTER SIX.

Verse 1. My son, if you have pledged yourself for your friend, if you have
struck hands with a stranger.

Verse 2. You have ensnared yourself by the words of your mouth; you have been
caught by the words of your mouth.

[Analytical diagram]

- Through verse 6 he shows:
  - the danger of inconsiderate suretyship, verses 1–2;
  - the remedy to be used by one entangled in it, verses 3–5.

“My son,” and so forth. The sense is this: if, as happens, you have become
surety for your neighbor and struck hands with one who is a stranger—that is,
one to whom you owe nothing and are bound by no tie or claim—do not consider
this light or contemptible because you transacted the matter only by words of
promise or by hand and did not disburse money. Whatever you may think, the
matter is just as though

[1632 witness | PDF p. 58 | printed columns 90–91]

you were held entangled in snares. Indeed, what is serious, your own words are
like nets and traps; your own hand, with which you struck his, tightens the
snares woven by your mouth and makes you, previously free, a captive no longer
under your own authority.

Here it must be observed that people often sin by giving too little thought to
the force inherent in their words. While they remain mere words, they value
them little. Therefore anyone solicited to become surety should reckon what it
would cost to pay immediately the money for which he makes himself sponsor,
and should be no quicker to enter the pledge than to count out the money whose
payment he binds himself to make. So long as nothing is required beyond
sealing a bond, striking hands, or undertaking the matter by a word, it is
counted a trifle. But if he considered the payment day already present, the
money immediately drawn from the chest, a lawsuit imposed upon him, the
sheriff surrounding his house, and prison already prepared, he would not fly
so quickly to suretyships. We deal similarly with God: we freely make vows and
promises, but do not so easily perform what we promised, Ecclesiastes 5. It is
therefore useful to measure the mouth and words by the dimensions of the arm,
and in this respect to imitate God, who executes by his arm what he pronounces
with his mouth.

Next it must be observed that becoming surety for another is a kind of
captivity and slavery. This is excellently confirmed by Proverbs 22:7. Let us
remember the apostle’s saying that we were bought with a price and therefore
ought not lightly and without weightier cause become servants of men, but
rather use liberty when it is given. We must consider that not only this kind
of pledge is forbidden here, but other things of the same class which have the
appearance of duty and charity but are not true and genuine charity. All must
avoid them, and ministers of the word especially. Jeremiah 19.

Two difficulties arise. The first is that Solomon, a prophet of God and doctor
of the church, devotes his discourse to these matters. They seem to concern
private houses rather than the church, a domestic chair rather than the public
pulpit, and a parent’s warning rather than a pastor’s instruction. But first,
what parents teach is not unworthy of the pulpit, for the minister often
handles the same things as a parent, though in another way. Next, since the
eighth commandment prescribes the lawful use of goods, one who makes people
cautious against squandering goods clearly does not depart from theology.
Furthermore, since the church, like the commonwealth, consists of families, it
is manifest that as much is taken from the church as from families, so that a
family’s collapse is the church’s diminution. Finally, religion acquires a bad
name from the dissipation of families born from the luxury of those professing
the truth. Hence 1 Thessalonians 4:12 teaches the godly to behave decently and
without offense toward the church’s enemies if no need oppresses them. Those
who do not provide for their families are counted worse than pagans, 1 Timothy
5. Add that the father of a household should teach in his family the same
things the minister teaches in church.

The other difficulty is that this passage seems to remove every pledge for
others, although an office of charity sometimes appears in one. God’s dearest
servants have sometimes fallen into times when they had to pledge even their
children and their children’s lives, much more money, Genesis 42:37. Judah in
Genesis 43 pledges himself to his father. We must therefore know that not every
pledge is forbidden, but one rashly undertaken without due consideration both
of oneself and of the person for whom one gives it.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 59 | printed columns 92–93]

If someone is indebted through his own carelessness and waste, it does not
seem prudent to pledge for him. But if someone suffers loss through a sudden,
unforeseen storm, especially if poverty presses him because of faith in Christ,
far be it from us not to aid him even by this office of suretyship.

Verse 3. Do this now, my son, and rescue yourself, since you have come into the
hand of your neighbor: go, humble yourself, and press your neighbor.

Verse 4. Grant no sleep to your eyes, nor slumber to your eyelids.

Verse 5. Rescue yourself like a roe from the hunter’s hand and like a bird
from the fowler’s hand.

There was the danger of inconsiderate suretyship; the remedy follows. It has
two parts. The first concerns what the surety must do toward the creditor—to
whom he should supplicate for an extension—and toward the person for whom he
made the pledge—to whom he should also plead to free his credit. The other
concerns what he must do among his friends: arousing and soliciting them, and
almost behaving imperiously toward them by urging and appealing that they aid
him by work, substance, or words.

Here is an anticipated objection whose substance appeared above: “It is
unworthy that I should offer myself to be trampled by my equals.” The answer
comes from the relations involved: by this pledge you seem to have come under
the power of both men, so that it is no less fitting to do this than for a
servant to offer himself as a suppliant to his master. This is adorned by the
circumstance of time: it must be done without delay. It is further confirmed
by a great promise that he will probably be rescued by this agreement. The
first and highest prudence of a Christian is therefore to keep himself intact
and immune from rash pledges of this kind; the next is, once entangled, to
extricate himself as quickly as possible. How diligently this must be done is
taught by the comparison with a roe and bird, which, when captured respectively
by hunter and fowler, strive with all their strength to escape at once.

If we must beware contracting debts before men, much more before God; if a
debt for another’s sake must not be rashly admitted among men, much less for
another’s sake before God. If we must so anxiously arrange to discharge a debt
contracted among men, much more before God. If we must labor so greatly where
no sure promise or outcome exists, much more before God, who has fortified us
with surest promises. Finally, if we offer ourselves to be trampled by our
equals to whom we are liable, much more by the immortal and most high God.

Verse 6. Go to the ant, sluggard; consider her ways and become wise.

[Analytical diagram]

- He shows:
  - the folly and dangers of the idle person through verse 14;
  - the effects following it, from there through verse 16.

He argues the folly from the lesser. You have teachers at home and abroad,
civil and ecclesiastical, some informing you by reason and others instructing
you by the Spirit’s lessons. You have examples of many people whose industry
might move you to work. Yet the matter to which you are urged is such that
even an ant, a lowly little creature, can plainly teach you your duty and justly
condemn you for rejecting its teaching. Much more, then, are you condemned

[1632 witness | PDF p. 60 | printed columns 94–95]

if all these excellent lessons do not profit you.

Here we see a lowly worm raised into the pulpit by Solomon to teach idle people
industry, appointed not merely as a teacher to instruct them but as judge to
pronounce sentence against them. Thus Christ sends people to the birds of
heaven and lilies of the field to learn to rest in God’s providence. The chief
use of creatures is that through investigating their nature we advance in
godliness, learning both God’s virtues shining in them and our corruptions in
comparison with their praiseworthy dispositions. Someone may think the ant
useless and suppose it more fitting to divine providence never to have created
it or immediately to destroy it, especially since this voracious creature
devours so much grain suited to nourishing humans. But even if it served no
other use, it should be fed on the richness of wheat, even kings’ delicacies,
for this reason alone: that all might keep it continually before their eyes;
that the industrious might remain at duty and the idle sharpen their diligence
and correct themselves. Those are rebuked who seek nothing in beasts, plants,
and other creatures except advantage for the present life. Among herbs they
do not know even the names of any but those useful for food; in horses, most
noble animals, they seek nothing celebrating the Creator’s magnificence and
majesty. As there is no creature from which some assistance toward godliness
cannot be sought, those which the Spirit has placed, as it were, in the chair
must especially be observed. It is as if one transferred wild flowers into
his garden when God chooses creatures by whose work he wishes to teach people
in a special manner. As wild flowers moved into a garden become more
conspicuous, these creatures set apart from philosophers’ common consideration
by a special lesson of the Holy Spirit must be examined more attentively and
accurately. These, finally, are the true books and images of the laity by which
they are taught knowledge of God.

Verse 7. Although she has no guide, overseer, or ruler.

Verse 8. She prepares her food in summer and gathers her sustenance at harvest.

He takes another argument from the lesser, from the accompanying governance of
each, pursuing particularly what was said generally. It divides into three
members. If the ant, having no guide to go before her, no inspector or
taskmaster (ἐργοδιώκτην), and no ruler to command or punish her, voluntarily
devotes herself to labor by natural instinct, how much more this senseless
man, who has examples before his eyes, overseers to demand an account, and at
least God, inspector of all; who has master or prince as teacher to command his
duties and punish neglect. The ant’s example is most fitting, since some
animals—cranes and sheep—have leaders they follow; oxen have keepers who drive
the hesitant with goads; bees have a king whose command or nod they obey.

Let us note that idleness in some respect fights against nature, since nature
has implanted in each creature a way to preserve itself and its life, and to
prepare food, clothing, and shelter. Thus one who sits idle is worse than
pagans. Even nursing infants do not draw milk without labor and difficulty.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 61 | printed columns 96–97]

Next let us note that every parent or master must drive subordinates to some
work and not allow them to grow sluggish in idleness, making them not merely
useless to the commonwealth but corrupting their dispositions like contracted
rust. Those are therefore censured who maintain a numerous retinue without
accustoming them to honest labor or exercise of mind or body. While serving
their own ambition they harm the commonwealth and ruin their attendants.

Finally, the more a person conducts his life contrary to commandments,
examples, punishments, and corrections that instruct, confirm, and drive him,
the more gravely he sins. If people were imbued with no commands or warnings,
they would still justly be blamed and punished here; but if cultivation brings
no progress, a heavier judgment is summoned.

To verse 8 he adds a third argument from the ant’s effects: as if foreknowing
winter, she prepares sustenance in summer, when calm air, mild weather, and
level, clean roads allow her to carry a proper burden. She considers not only
the time for labor but the opportunity harvest supplies, when food is
obtainable. She can prepare not merely what she presently eats but what will
nourish her in winter, when food nowhere appears publicly, and carefully
stores what will later be useful. Here the idle man is censured in many ways:
first, he thinks only of the present moment, little or not at all concerned
for the future; next, like a naked man gathering nuts, he devours everything
and stores nothing; moreover, he lets youth pass fruitlessly like summer;
finally, he neglects harvest and an opportunity for gain. Though separated
from beasts by so great an interval, the sluggard is more brutish than they,
yet in his own judgment he seems wiser than other people. He thinks it the
highest wisdom to live without labor while others work for food.

If it is foolish and inconsiderate not to account for advancing age and old
age, how much more if someone is so intent upon present life’s business that
he has no concern for eternal life and gathers nothing in heaven to fortify a
way to eternal dwellings!

In 30:25 ants are called a weak people. This designation answers the evasions
by which the idle try to exempt themselves from labor, for nothing is more
common than to plead their weakness.

Verse 9. How long, sluggard, will you lie there? When will you rise from sleep?

He locates another part of idleness in excessive sleep, tacitly rebuked by the
ant’s example. That little creature is very wakeful, carrying grain to its
refuge not only by day but by night under a full moon. The idle man rightly
belongs under the ant’s discipline in this respect too, to correct immoderate
sleep: she labors at night, while he does not even by day. By asking, “When
will you rise?” he declares that sleep has a limit. It is therefore worth
knowing the time for sleeping. Add Psalm 104:21, 23: God ordained that everyone
go to his work in the morning and return home in the evening. We must know not
only that we must labor and seek bread in the sweat of our face but that God
prescribes an ordinary time for labor,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 62 | printed columns 98–99]

unless a weightier cause such as illness prevents it. We need not much fear
that masters will fail to demand their task from servants; but those masters
also have a taskmaster (ἐργοδιώκτην) who demands a task from them no less than
they from their servants. This is not said as though we were strictly driven
to twelve hours’ labor. Time is left for taking food, recreation, and mental
relaxation. But we should know that food, sleep, amusements, and so forth are
directed to this end: better spending the day in some honorable calling. Nor
does the same amount of sleep fit everyone.

Our drowsiness should deservedly be shaken off because God himself calls
aloud, as though undertaking the labor of waking us. The one awakened prepares
to rise according to the awakener’s dignity. We see how Samuel, still a boy,
immediately ran when called at night by Eli the priest, as he thought, and
asked what he wanted. But when God himself knocks at the door, approaches the
bed, and calls you clearly, how absurd not to answer God calling and urging
you to rise in the morning!

He repeats in the same sense what he said before; as though not content to
call once, he knocks again and warns you to wake. Let this sound always strike
your ears like a clock you place by the bed so that you rise in time. If
someone calls you, think it is not so much a man as God himself who addresses
you whenever someone lawfully charged with doing so admonishes you to rise.

Verse 10. A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to
lie down.

By a kind of imitation he presents the idle man answering absurdly and
refusing to rise. His reason is the small amount of sleep either still lacking
or already received. Both interpretations return to the same point, but the
former better agrees with the next verse. The sum is that the nails of sloth
so fix him to bed that he cannot be torn away. See Proverbs 26:14, where one
tossing in bed is compared to a door turning upon hinges while never moving
from its place. Idleness itself causes such immoderate sleep: by doing nothing
an abundance of humors gathers and produces fuller, more copious sleep.

It is again asked whether sleep ends at a known time. Certainly night is
assigned to sleep; but just as no precise amount can be prescribed for food
and drink, because bodily size and the stomach require more or less, bodily
condition must also be considered in sleep. Though definite hours cannot
easily be fixed, this can readily be established: a person of any condition
can be limited to the span of night—twelve hours—so that his sleep does not
run into day unless illness or something unusual prevents him.

Verse 11. Meanwhile your poverty will come like a swift traveler, and your
need like a shield-bearing man.

[Analytical diagram]

- The idle man’s reasoning and life have been exposed; the effects follow,
  which are life’s disadvantages.

In this life the sluggard is punished with poverty. Thus Proverbs 13:4 and
19:15 teach that the sluggard’s soul will suffer hunger and starvation. It
follows that he will not merely be poor but suffer such poverty that he has
nothing to eat,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 63 | printed columns 100–101]

a misery greater because 13:4 says he greatly desires food. The contrary
illustrates it at 10:4. Here the emphasis is upon poverty’s violent invasion,
for which he has no remedy. His misery is increased because other poor people
often move pity and receive relief, whereas he receives none because his own
fault produced his poverty. Though one rashly entangled in suretyship also
becomes poor through his fault, others will pity him more than this drone who
does not work. Divine law even provides that those unwilling to work should
not eat. Thus he is commonly hateful not only to God but to people, though
other wicked persons are often acceptable to them.

This poverty has two circumstances: its unforeseen arrival and violent
invasion. Unexpected evils strike more grievously; such is this poverty because
the ignorant sluggard cares only for the present. This is illustrated by a
traveler unexpectedly entering someone’s house. While the sluggard reclines
and sits at home, poverty takes the road. The more he delays, the more she
hastens; the more he snores, the less she sleeps, until she meets and lodges
with him. The sluggard’s house is poverty’s lodging.

The other circumstance is violent invasion, which cannot be resisted. If
someone objects, “Once reduced to poverty, then I will work,” it is nothing.
One accustomed to idleness from youth is hardly ever brought to labor. Even if
he wishes, diseases have commonly exhausted him so he can accomplish nothing
worthwhile; or if diseases do not prevent him, scarcely anyone will hire him.

This is illustrated by arms. Poverty is introduced as armed, attacking the
sluggard as naked and unarmed and certainly crushing him. It is perfectly just
that he unwillingly yield to poverty who willingly yielded to sleep, and that
armed poverty overwhelm one who permitted idleness and sleep to conquer him
like an armed soldier. Whoever wishes to conquer poverty must first grapple
with sleep and idleness.

Verse 12. A wicked man, a worthless man, walks with a perverse mouth.

[Analytical diagram]

- Still concerning poverty in this life: disadvantages of the future life
  follow, which are:
  - sin;
  - sin’s punishment.

In the former he treats it first generally, then particularly. The Hebrew
words בליעל איש און אדם mark him as burdened with many sins. Although other
sins, like virtues, have their retinues, none is attended by more vices than
this one.

הולך עקשות פה is a man who speaks perversely not once or twice but is
wholly devoted to slander, walking and living in perversity of speech. See 1
Timothy 5:13. One who is nothing in deeds commonly prevails in words; whose
hands are idle, his tongue is very active. Hence he cannot conceal a secret
entrusted to him, Proverbs 20:19. David does not tolerate one slandering his
neighbor in his house, Psalm 101; how much less one both idle and slanderous!
Since a servant must secretly preserve his master’s secrets, it is plain an
idle man should not be received into a household, or, once received, retained.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 64 | printed columns 102–103]

Since he cannot gain favor through excellent deeds, he will try to please
people with words composed to their liking. If we hate detractors and
calumniators, let us remove the idle from our houses and villages. Wanderers,
hawkers, and roving monks are examples. Evil and idle ministers should also be
noted: none are more talkative when unjustly speaking about the godly.

Verse 13. He signals with his eyes, speaks with his feet, and teaches with his
fingers.

This verse seems to prove by an argument from the lesser that he is amazingly
devoted to perverse speech. He speaks it not only with his mouth but always
suggests something to companions by the other bodily members enumerated:
winking eyes, moving fingers, and so forth. When among those before whom he
cannot pour out folly, impatience to keep anything within makes him act by
signs—stamping feet, raising fingers, winking—thereby partly unloading himself
lest prolonged silence oppress him. Thus, as the apostle notes in 2
Thessalonians 3:11, those who are idle (ἄεργοι) are also busybodies
(περίεργοι). Those who will not move a little finger to any good work are very
ready and swift to accomplish evil. Those who will not step outside for labor
never cease running about to babble something harmful. The papal priesthood
provides an excellent example: passing over and despising study and
proclamation of divine Scripture, they turn to empty, childish gestures, wink
with eyes, speak with feet, and devise a thousand trifles to conceal laziness
and deceive the wretched common people. Many ministers under the gospel
imitate them, unable to teach but attempting to compensate for their want by
foolish observance of ceremonies.

Verse 14. Every kind of perversity resides in his soul; he devises wicked
thoughts and continually sends out contentions.

From effects he rises to the cause, a perverse heart from which those perverse
words proceed as from a rich treasury of perversity. He says not merely that
perversity resides in his heart, but *perversities*, as though his heart were
completely filled and packed with them, swelling until they could no longer be
contained. As an overflowing vessel pours liquid on the ground, his heart,
swollen with perversity, must unload itself in perverse sayings. This refutes
those who speak perversely but cry that they thought no evil. Christ’s judgment
is different: a good man brings good things from his heart’s good treasury.
We must therefore hold what we learned earlier: above all, guard the heart.

He assigns another cause to the former cause. These perversities do not merely
remain fixed in his mind as in all people whose heart is naturally depraved
and whose wicked thoughts exist from the first age of corrupt nature. Rather,
by continual practice of devising and, as it were, forging evil he has
increased and confirmed that fabric.

The word חרש, designating both farmer and artisan, marks his constancy in evil.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 65 | printed columns 104–105]

The metaphor aptly illustrates the matter. The idle man is as devoted to
devising wickedness as the farmer to cultivating fields or the artisan to
finishing work. Here at last the wicked are distinguished from the godly.
Though the godly sometimes draw a furrow of sin, they are not devoted to it as
a farmer and do not plow all day. Though they put hand to sin’s plow, they look
back to what lies behind. Though sin’s seed sometimes falls from them, they do
not carry it into the field to scatter everywhere. Though aiming at the anvil
of godliness and uprightness they fall contrary to it, they immediately
collect themselves. The wicked man is compared to artisan or farmer, his heart
to plow or tool, and perversity to the work he fashions.

This wickedness is magnified by the circumstance of time, illustrated from the
lesser. Farmer and artisan cease while eating, drinking, or preparing for
night; this man never stops, not even at night. See Micah 2:1 and the example
illustrated by a clear comparison in Hosea 7:6.

He then adds the effect, like a harvest or product of diabolical art: stirring
contentions. This commonly occurs through whispering and slander. He attacks
one and praises another, attacks before a companion one whom he praised to his
face, or the reverse. Indeed, he simultaneously praises and attacks the same
man—praising by words but disparaging him to an adversary by signs of feet,
fingers, and eyes. See Proverbs 1:18. If we desire peace in families or other
societies, the idle must be sent far away.

Verse 15. Therefore his calamity will come suddenly; he will abruptly be
broken so that there is no remedy.

The sin has been treated; punishment follows: destruction and inescapable ruin
by which he is so thoroughly crushed that he can never be repaired or restored.
This is illustrated by its sudden, abrupt invasion. It is noteworthy that the
wicked are unexpectedly removed from the world. See Job 21 and Psalm 73. This
must be noted against the wicked who promise themselves time to repent. The
wretches do not know, or refuse to know, that they will be suddenly seized as
by a whirlwind and driven to their place. Even if time is given, one may be
lacking to warn and call them to repentance. And even if he is present,
repentance is God’s gift and blessing; nothing will profit unless the saving
work of merciful God accompanies use of the means.

Verse 16. Jehovah hates these six things; indeed, seven are an abomination to
his soul.

Verse 17. Lofty eyes, a lying tongue, and hands shedding innocent blood.

Verse 18. A soul devising wicked thoughts, feet running swiftly to evil.

Verse 19. A false witness firmly testifying lies, and finally one sending
contentions among brothers.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:
  - God’s hatred of the idle man’s many crimes which summon certain ruin;
  - particular enumeration of those crimes.

In the former he announces that the idle man’s destruction will be certain
and most fearful. Two arguments confirm it: the accompanying multitude and
heap of crimes which vie to settle in his single little soul and body, and
God’s hatred and detestation of each, the cause of punishment.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 66 | printed columns 106–107]

He marks multitude by saying six, then argues from the greater by adding not
merely six but seven. Both proverbial numbers designate a great multitude of
crimes, indeed more than the number stated, as in Job 5:19. He makes the idle
man a cage or nest where innumerable ill-omened birds of crime are hatched.

Next the cause of ruin is marked: God’s hatred and abomination from his very
soul and inward parts. This is manifold anthropopathic speech
(ἀνθρωποπάθεια), attributing hatred and soul to God. The formula designates
extreme vengeance, 2 Samuel 5:1. Let us note that God hates and abominates
things contrary to his nature, who is wholly holiness. People persuade, or
wish to persuade, themselves otherwise: that God does not hate sin as much as
preachers assert, since his patience waits for their repentance and he not
only does not attack evildoers with judgments but loads them with benefits.
They then make God like themselves and somehow delighted by sin. Although God
pursues those indulging sins with benefits, let us never admit the thought
that God has no hostility against our sins, but hold certainly that he hates
them at that very moment as when he afflicts the sinner with gravest penalties.
When we detest something we immediately fly against it, and an hour seems a
year until we remove it. God is slow to vengeance and not powerless over anger
as man is. Mortals unable to avenge themselves conceal hatred until an
opportunity; but God, always able to avenge those he especially hates for sins,
often passes them untouched by outward plagues in this life.

The second part, particular enumeration, follows. First, are these most hated,
rather than sins directly opposing divine worship? Certainly sins directly
concerning God are most abominable, as 2 Kings 17 shows: passing over all else,
God accuses Israel only of idolatry and confirms that for it alone blindness
of one eye (μονοφθαλμία) was brought in. Scripture therefore portrays God’s
jealousy at admitted idolatry. For this one sin he hands people over to a mind
that entirely forgets the second-table commandments. The comparison here is
therefore not of all sins, but only those committed against people.

Second, are these seven the gravest committed against people? Adulteries,
incest, monstrous lusts, sacrileges, betrayal of country, and so forth are
fouler than most or all of these. Solomon treats not all sins, but those which
especially follow idleness and violate the commonwealth’s or family’s peace.
Having mentioned disseminated strife, he pursues what relates to it.

Third, in what order are they arranged? Scripture usually descends from the
greatest to least or ascends from least to greatest; neither occurs here.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 67 | printed columns 108–109]

Solomon regarded the order and position of bodily members used in these acts
rather than severity. He places eyes first, then tongue, hands after tongue,
heart after hands, and feet after heart: the chief members by which this
wickedness is committed. He also arranged the sins by the order in which one
begets another, except the heart’s depravity continually devising evil, placed
in the middle. As the heart in the body’s middle supplies life to members
above and below, the mind is the fountain of all sins springing everywhere.
The proud are raised by empty boasting and disparage others because they
falsely arrogate things to themselves. From venomous words they often leap to
fighting and bloodshed. One continually devising evil rushes incredibly
swiftly to perform it and, commonly lacking authority and power, accomplishes
it especially by a venomous tongue.

Why say God hates lofty eyes, a lying tongue, and so forth, rather than loftiness
of eyes and falsehood of tongue? It seems foreign to God to hate his creature,
as hatred of sin accords with his nature. He thereby answers those promising
immunity because God hates nothing he made: God pursues sin in itself, and the
creature too because of sin, with hatred.

Putting proud eyes first agrees with the apostle, who lays humility
(ταπεινοφροσύνην) as a foundation beneath every virtue of righteousness,
Ephesians 4. That pride especially sows strife is clear from Proverbs 13:10.
Nor should it surprise us that a sluggard, empty of every human ornament, is
proud. God appointed occupation, which he hates, to train people in humility,
Ecclesiastes 5. The idle man is expressly said to be wise in his own eyes,
Proverbs 26:16.

Why both a lying tongue and false witness, which seem identical? They mutually
follow, as Solomon teaches elsewhere, but differ. Lying anywhere is one thing,
lying while testifying another. Yet one devoted to lies in common speech will
bear false witness, and a false witness will not blush to lie commonly.

Verse 20. Keep, my son, your father’s commandment, and do not forsake your
mother’s doctrine.

From verses 20 to 30 Solomon generally exhorts the godly to keep the
commandments and dissuades them from habitual adultery, supplying reasons for
both.

[Analytical diagram]

- The parts are:

Verse 21. Bind them continually upon your heart; fasten them around your neck.

See 3:3 above. Only the order is reversed, and instead of writing upon the
mind another metaphor of bonds or tying is used. The Hebrew תמיד adds that the
mind must always rest upon God’s law, even when one cannot speak or meditate
upon it.

Verse 22. Wherever you go, she will lead you; when you lie down, she will guard
you; when you awake, she will converse with you.

See 3:23–24. Here only this is added: when we awaken, the commandment is a
ready counselor offering to discuss that day’s whole business. As masters
prescribe each person’s task each morning, the commandment declares the duty
God requires throughout life. It is the most faithful and wise counselor in
conducting affairs, always ready and at hand, present and speaking with you as
soon as you wake, before dressing gives opportunity to converse with others,
so that this conversation loses you no time for other business. It discusses
anything: dealings with yourself, another, the best and greatest God, or
people; it fails you in nothing, whereas people excellent in one matter are
commonly ignorant in others.

The doctrine’s sufficiency (αὐτάρκεια) is clear because it thoroughly informs
the intellect. It abundantly instructs the minister, who above all orders must
be illuminated by understanding truth; shapes the will to obey God, Psalm
119:2—for if it restrains youth, the age rushing most headlong into crimes, it
will more easily restrain others—and delivers a king from pride, to which he is
especially inclined. Thus it protects all professions and orders from evil.

Verse 23. For the commandment is a lamp and doctrine a light, and instructive
corrections are the way of life.

He confirms what he said of studying the law from its effects, distributed
across life’s duties and illustrated by comparison. Following Scripture’s
custom, he first takes what came later: “When I said God’s law would direct
you when going to bed at night and rising in the morning, I testified this
because it is like a lamp, very useful when going to bed or rising.” When he
said doctrine would lead by day, it is because the commandment is like light
or sun directing daytime labor, so that the sun never sets for the godly nor
their lamp goes out. For the wicked thick darkness exists at noon because of
nature’s corruption, making everything black night and wandering places;
without the commandment’s lamp and doctrine’s light we must stumble and fall
at every step.

The son may say, “I gladly receive my father’s command and mother’s doctrine
drawn from God’s word and embrace them with both arms, provided they do not
advance to harsh words and blows.” The answer is that instructive corrections,

those joined with instruction, are necessary as the way of life; those
rejecting them immediately reject life. See Proverbs 3 and Hebrews 12.

Verse 24. To preserve you from an evil woman and the flattery of a strange
woman’s tongue.

From the divine word’s general effects he descends to particulars, marking all
by synecdoche from one. He chose this advisedly: adultery arises from the
idleness attacked here and especially assails the youth he chiefly instructs;
the word’s power shines most in reforming it. It is objected that God’s word
alone does not rescue youth from adultery, since papists and other unbelievers
cultivate chastity. True chastity exists only among the godly, who abstain for
God’s sake and from consciousness of crime; the wicked cultivate it for honor,
fear of hell or civil punishment, or opinion of merit. The chastity celebrated
among Romans, desiring to appear free from shame, fell into greater evil.

To be delivered by God’s word from an adulteress, avoid what summons this
vice. First, do not allow sweet words to entice and lead you away. Let God’s
word rescue you from the prostitute’s words, his truth from her falsehood, his
faithful speech from her treacherous speech, his lips from her sharpened
tongue. Imitate Joseph, who refused to hear his mistress speak. Eve fared badly
because she exchanged words with the serpent.

Verse 25. Do not desire her beauty in your soul, nor let her capture you with
her eyelids.

Another medicine is not desiring the adulteress’s form. See Matthew 5: one who
desires her in mind commits adultery before God. This formula answers a tacit
objection: a youth might resolve to abstain from shameful prostitutes however
flattering and talkative, but think victory difficult and doubtful if a woman
is beautiful. Solomon says: however beautiful, elegant by natural and
unpainted beauty, do not desire her. It commends her no more than a gold ring
in a pig’s snout. Every beauty is fleeting and withers like a flower through
sun or wind, age or disease. Do not merely beware desiring her form; consider
her mind’s foul deformity. Remember her beauty is only bait by which you are
caught as by snare or hook; hence she is soon compared to hunters.

The youth again objects that amid such inclination of age and nature it is
difficult or impossible not to desire a beautiful woman. Solomon answers: to
avoid desire, do not fix your gaze upon her, since from seeing (τοῦ ὁρᾶν)
arises loving (τὸ ἐρᾶν). Potiphar’s wife is said to have raised her eyes;
David fixed his upon Bathsheba. Hence Psalm 119, “Turn my eyes away lest I see
vanity,” and Job 31.

Verse 26. For because of a prostitute a man comes down even to a morsel of
bread; the wife of a man also hunts the precious soul.

[Analytical diagram]

- The argument comes from accompanying disadvantages:
  - beggary;
  - death and infamy of name.

The first disadvantage, scantiness of food, signifies by synecdoche extreme
want of everything. Far from anything remaining in his chest, he will lack
what barely sustains life. The prostitute’s expenses, scarcely bearable even
by a wealthy man, and fellowship with gluttons and spendthrifts reduce him to
this. See the prodigal son, Luke 15, reduced to wishing to eat husks with pigs.
It is a parable, not history, but drawn from common life. Add the proverb,
“Without Ceres and Bacchus Venus grows cold”: this shame drags intemperance
with it, which alone easily supplies poverty, Proverbs 23:21. It will not help
to object that through adultery he receives rather than gives payment. Ill-got
things perish badly, and Solomon truly says wealth acquired by hand—that is,
honestly—endures. If he escapes poverty, it commonly goes worse because he
sleeps in sin when affliction might awaken him.

The second disadvantage is that a man’s wife hunts his soul, which should be
precious to him. She does this very rarely by intention, but by effect when he
is caught and punished with death. Thus one sparing the rod is said to hate
his son. He must value his soul and not squander it for the vile price of
momentary unlawful pleasure. Esau is condemned as profane for selling his
birthright for stew; how much more one exchanging life for wicked lust. David
refused Bethlehem’s water because it came at the price of others’ blood; much
less would he have bought these waters of Marah at his own blood’s price. Skin
for skin, and one gives all for life. It is holy to endanger life for one’s
country, especially the heavenly; but vile pleasure should not redeem even a
dog’s life.

Since this disadvantage arises from the justice of laws and magistrate, which
has no place among us here, one may say this threat has no use or terror. Yet
this should not make us secure: whatever is subtracted from present danger is
added to future danger, far greater than crucifixion of offenders. Those who
escape magistrates and human hands often fall into God’s severer judgment even
in this life, as Absalom and Eli’s sons did. Many condemned by men for theft
are thus justly carried to punishment by God for adulteries.

If human life must be so dear, much more that redeemed by Christ’s blood,
since adulterers are excluded from eternal life, 1 Corinthians 6.

Verse 29. So is one who goes in to his neighbor’s wife; no one who touches her
will be left unpunished.

He illustrates the preceding statement by a parallel comparison, likening
adultery to burning fire. See Job 31:12, where adultery produces the same two
fruits: the offender’s utter destruction and loss of every yield.

Verse 30. A thief who steals to fill his soul when hungry is not despised.

Verse 31. Yet if caught, he will repay sevenfold; he will give every possession
of his house.

Verse 32. But one committing adultery with a woman is senseless; the one doing
this will destroy himself.

Verse 33. He will obtain a wound and disgrace, and his reproach will not be
erased.

Verse 34. For jealousy is a husband’s angry passion; therefore he uses no
clemency on the day of vengeance.

Verse 35. He regards no ransom and does not rest although you offer many gifts.

These verses teach that adultery is a graver crime than theft, contrary to
most people’s judgment.

The greatest perversity calls good evil and sweet bitter. It is also corrupt
and depraved not to assign the true difference between good and evil, making a
lesser evil greater or preferring a lesser good to a better. The Corinthians
did the latter, 1 Corinthians 13–14; people of Solomon’s and our age do the
former. Everyone attacks a thief with gravest reproaches, calling him
*flagrieibam* (in English, *Gallows-clapper*, *Newgate-bird*, and so forth).
They refuse to eat or drink with him and almost shrink from his clothes, then
hand him to prison and death. Nothing like this occurs with adultery: they do
not reproach it or avoid the adulterer’s company. Who boasts of theft? Yet
many boast of adulteries, especially with a beautiful or noble woman. This
perversion has more place among us because adultery is almost immune from
punishment while theft is severely avenged. God has not changed his sentence
pronouncing adultery a greater fault, whatever people decide.

Consider the confirming reasons drawn from disgrace and civil punishment. In
the former, a thief stealing to satisfy hunger is not despised but moves pity.
Though some disgrace adheres, it is easily washed away; an adulterer’s never
is. Thus this sin especially makes a person vile and dishonored, as through
chastity people keep their bodies in honor. Other sins are said to be outside
the body, this against one’s own body. Perhaps relate the saying that David
was incorrupt in everything except Uriah’s wife, though he committed grievous
murder, as if disgrace from murder were erased while adultery’s remained.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 72 | printed columns 118–119 | ends above the ruled beginning of Chapter VII]

The other reason is civil punishment, harsher for adulterer than thief. A
thief can compensate loss and escape danger by repaying fourfold for small
livestock and fivefold for larger animals if killed or sold; the adulterer can
give no compensation. A thief can compensate with substance or wealth; an
adulterer cannot with all riches accumulated. If a thief steals seven or many
times from poverty, he restores each loss; an adulterer caught once is a son
of death. Thus the thief is said through theft partly to provide for extending
life, while the adulterer loses life by folly. Hence each sin is graver in
proportion to the harsher legal penalty.

Observe the distinction among thieves: indulgence is only for those compelled
by need. Want concerns not only hunger, as among us, but nakedness. It is as
unjust to endure death by cold from nakedness as from lack of food. This
distinction is confirmed in 2 Samuel 12.

In jealousy it is praiseworthy to accept no reward or gift for chastity; but
killing the adulterer is not equally approved. Vengeance must be left to God
and his appointed magistrate. If civil laws in many commonwealths permit the
husband, I answer that this is granted inconveniently. It is difficult for a
man, especially an angry or raging one, to keep measure in vengeance after
such injury. Furthermore, this confused, hasty killing closes the way and
entrance to repentance.
