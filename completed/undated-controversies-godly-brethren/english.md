# On Certain Controversies among Godly Brothers

*A fresh translation of the complete Latin corporate advice at CO X/1
229–230. The title and page notices are editorial rather than words silently
attributed to the historical body.*

[CO X/1, p. 229]

The calamity of the church at N. has affected us with no slight grief. But
because God now seems, in order to exercise his own people, to loosen the reins
of Satan and the wicked, our condition must be borne patiently. Meanwhile, we
must ask him in his kindness to spare the weakness of his tender and little
flock.

Those who think that things go badly with the church whenever it is attacked
by the fury of enemies or cruel violence have not yet grasped the rudiments of
Christian warfare. From this comes the doubt that has crept into the minds of
many and now occupies them: whether it is lawful for the godly to hold meetings
at night or in secret. For we see judgment being made chiefly from the outcome,
than which nothing is more perverse. Recently, when they met without trouble,
they also thought it beyond dispute that their service was pleasing to God.
Now that persecution has arisen, they debate whether they are paying the
penalty for their own rashness. By this reasoning, the confession of faith,
which inflames the rage of the wicked still more, will soon be condemned as
well. In short, according to these critics, it will not be advantageous to
follow Christ unless success is favorable.

Not without reason did Paul place Ishmael's mockery at the highest degree of
persecution (Galatians 4:29), because there is greater danger from derision and
reproaches than from armed savagery. If the wicked see us pressed by adversity,
they exult over our miseries. If our constancy falters at this point, they do
more harm with words than with the sword. Yet they ought to have held fast to
this saying of Paul: because we hope in the living God, not only persecution
but also insults must be endured.

Let the example of Christ therefore come to mind. Satan tried to wound him
with this venomous weapon: “He trusted in God; let him deliver him if he
wishes.” Let us not waver whenever we seem exposed to our enemies' caprice.

But if the brothers were stunned or disturbed by the first assault, it is your
task to support them so that they do not collapse altogether, or even to raise
them up so that they recover their courage. They must not abandon their holy
undertaking merely because it gave the wicked an occasion to rage. For if the
matter is evaluated in itself, the answer will be ready: private and
clandestine meetings were not imputed to the apostles as a fault, and the
meetings now held under the papacy differ from those in no respect. Malicious
people could likewise have objected then that it was not without grounds for
suspicion, at night and in secret,

[CO X/1, p. 230]

for men and women to assemble. Nor is there any doubt that they were burdened
with many slanders. And since garrison soldiers were stationed at Jerusalem,
this could have appeared contrary to public tranquility. Nevertheless, they
did not stop on that account. We see that, when Peter had been arrested, they
gathered at a widow's house (Acts 12:12).

There is no point in collecting examples here, because it is known that this
was their daily practice. In the same way, the histories testify that caves
served the godly martyrs as temples. Although this was exposed to no slight
reproach, so that they could be suspected of robbery or other crimes,
necessity compelled them to act in this way; with great courage they scorned
the insults that they knew were commonly hurled at them.

Our brothers are now armed with the best defense: if the magistrates permitted
them to invoke God's name freely and openly, they would gladly show themselves
in the light and would do nothing in secret. But because they are prevented by
the magistrates' tyranny, they follow a course that is as far removed from
tumult as possible.

The same judgment must be made about marriages. Their so-called blessing under
the papacy is sheer profanation. Yet, as far as the civil order is
concerned, a marriage for which documents have been drawn up cannot be called
clandestine. But it must in no way be tolerated that its holiness should be
polluted and defiled by superstitions.

Although burials at night are condemned by law with good reason, those who at
the funerals of their own people entangle themselves in accepted sacrileges
and participate in them vainly conceal their cowardice under this pretext.

The same rationale ought to prevail in baptism. For although the most immediate
danger presses upon them, that does not make it lawful to do what God plainly
disapproves. We know that under the papacy baptism has been corrupted by many
defilements and almost adulterated. If no fear stood in the way, all the godly
would readily agree that parents are not permitted to present their infant
children for a corrupted baptism. It is therefore exceedingly frivolous to
snatch an excuse from danger, as though the thing itself changed its nature on
that account.

For we know that testimony to godliness is more precious before God than to be
obliged to yield to threats or terrors, at least where fear compels us to go as
far as outward pretense, which would be a tacit approval of impieties.
Therefore, just as we share the grief of the godly brothers as the duty of love
requires, so it is by no means ours to release them from God's inviolable law.

If Jewish women in Egypt once did not hesitate to put their own lives at risk
to rescue other people's infants from Pharaoh's sword (Exodus 1:17), it is
shameful for parents to be so terrified by fear that, as far as lies in them,
they contaminate the souls of their own children.

I have written these things to you briefly in accordance with the judgment of
our collegium, excellent man and brother to be cherished from the heart. May
the Lord always govern you by his Spirit, bless your holy labors, and preserve
you unharmed together with the church entrusted to you. My fellow ministers
also send their warmest greetings. Geneva.

## Translator's notes

1. The `N.` in “the church at N.” suppresses a place name. Neither that church
   nor the individual addressee is identified in the CO main text.
2. The question concerns clandestine Christian assemblies under coercion.
   The defense rests both on apostolic precedent and on the brothers' stated
   willingness to worship openly if the magistrates permitted it.
3. The line introduced as “this saying of Paul” echoes 1 Timothy 4:10, while
   the taunt against Christ echoes Matthew 27:43. CO supplies no parenthetical
   references at those points, so they are noted here rather than inserted
   into the historical body.
4. Acts 12:12 names Mary the mother of John Mark; the Latin here calls the
   householder simply *vidua*, “a widow,” and the translation preserves that
   wording.
5. *Tabulae* are documentary instruments in the civil order. The writer
   distinguishes public legal documentation from participation in the papal
   rite he condemns.
6. *Simulatio* is rendered “outward pretense”: in context it is conduct that
   communicates tacit approval, not merely the concealment of an inward
   conviction.
7. “Our collegium” renders *collegium nostrum*, the Genevan ministerial body
   whose judgment the writer reports. The closing *symmystae* likewise means
   his fellow ministers.
8. The English was translated afresh from `latin.txt` and checked against the
   facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not consulted or used for
   wording.
