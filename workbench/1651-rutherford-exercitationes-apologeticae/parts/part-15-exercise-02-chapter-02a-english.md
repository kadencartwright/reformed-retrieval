CHAPTER II

*The proposition “What everyone is bound to believe is true” is answered. 2.
How God requires faith from the reprobate. 3. On the promises of the Gospel.*

The solution of an objection sheds considerable light on this dispute. In his
customarily boastful manner, Arminius said that this objection alone was
powerful enough to overthrow our position. It is this:

*What everyone is bound to believe is true.*

[Marginalia: *Against Perkins*, p. 735.]

[1651 logical printed p. 239 | IA leaf 257 | PDF p. 257]

*But everyone is bound to believe that Christ died for him.*

*Therefore it is true that Christ died for everyone.*

John Cameron replies by distinguishing the major premise. What everyone is
bound to believe is true either in the way that things narrated are true, in
the way that things predicted are true, or as things promised are true. In the
first and second ways it is not the case: things narrated and predicted are
true whether you believe or not. But things true as promised need not exist
unless you believe; indeed, they necessarily do not exist if you do not
believe. He teaches this because, most falsely and without any foundation, he
holds that Christ died conditionally for all, whether elect or reprobate, but
absolutely for the elect. This opinion seems to me closely allied to heresy.
For faith does not make its object, nor does our obedience give truth to the
divine promises; indeed, human unbelief would in that way abolish God's
faithfulness. 2. Cameron can concede the whole argument without any
distinction, since it smiles uniquely on his own error. Thus all mortals are
bound to believe that Christ died conditionally for them, and the Arminian
claim stands unshaken: that the grace of redemption is universal and common
to elect and reprobate. Therefore Christ paid the penalties owed by the
reprobate. By the order of justice, then, all the reprobate are exempt from
eternal punishment. O Carneadean subtlety! But more of this elsewhere.

[Marginalia: John Cameron, vol. 2, lecture on Matthew 16:27, pp. 274, 20.]

[1651 logical printed p. 240 | IA leaf 258 | PDF p. 258]

2. The most weighty and distinguished John Piscator, and another theologian
of no ordinary rank whom I will leave unnamed for honor's sake, reply that the
major is simply false. Indeed, they say that men are obliged to believe that
God intends or wills that what he commands us should occur, although perhaps,
as to the event, it was not true that God intended it, since perhaps it will
never occur. They even assert that the object of faith is sometimes false,
which neither our theology nor truth permits. For 1. faith rests upon divine
truth, beneath which nothing false can lie. Therefore its object is in no way
false. The antecedent is Hebrews 11:11 and Titus 1:2. Indeed, as William
Twisse—that unblemished flower of scholastic minds—rightly observed, God's
intention is not properly and infallibly revealed, but what God's intention
was is inferred from the command. 2. We would have to believe that God speaks
what can be false and wills us to believe falsehoods, which is absurd. For we
are bound to believe that the matter is as God speaks; but that God intends
Judas to believe is nothing in the nature of things. 3. Faith is ultimately
resolved into this: “Thus says Jehovah.” But that God intends the obedience of
all who are called is not a saying of Jehovah, nor can truth be resolved into
falsehood. 4. If from evangelical exhortations everyone in the visible church
were bound to believe that God intends their obedience, they would also be
bound to believe that God destined them for eternal glory and that Christ,
according to his and the Father's eternal counsel,

[Marginalia: Piscator, conference with Vorstius, p. 31.]

[Marginalia: Twisse, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius*, book 2, charge
4, section 18.]

[1651 logical printed p. 241 | IA leaf 259 | PDF p. 259]

expiated their sins on the cross. But the latter is absurd, and therefore so
is the former. I prove the major. If they were bound to believe that in his
commands God intended their faith, which is the means, they would be bound to
believe that God also intended the end. Otherwise they would have to believe
that God intended the means but not the end, contrary to Scripture. For those
whom he called, he glorified, Romans 8. Therefore those whom he intends to
call, he also intends to glorify. It is clear, however, that not everyone in
the visible church is bound to believe that God destined them for glory. If
all were bound to believe this, all would be bound to believe themselves
elect, and thus there would not be in the visible church reprobate and elect,
sheep and goats, chaff and wheat, tares and good crops—contrary to Matthew
22:14; 3:12; and 13:27. It would mean that God embraces everyone called by the
external calling with universal grace, and that God dictates false and
contradictory things in Scripture, than which nothing is more absurd. Nor can
this or that person be bound to believe that God intends his own salvation
and also to believe that God does not intend the obedience and salvation of
many. 5. All the hardened would be bound to believe that in evangelical
exhortations God does not intend their hardening, but their obedience, and
that Isaiah 6:9–10, Matthew 12:14–15, Acts 28, John 12, and similar Scriptures
speak falsely. 6. Peter in the praetorium was bound to believe that by the
saying

[1651 logical printed p. 242 | IA leaf 260 | PDF p. 260]

“Whoever confesses me before men, him I also will confess before my Father and
the angels, etc.,” Christ intended Peter to obey and confess him before
Christ's enemies. With what purpose, then, had Christ said that Peter would
deny him three times? Peter was bound either to believe that Christ had
foretold falsely or that by “Whoever confesses me, etc.,” Christ did not
intend Peter to obey. But Peter was not bound to believe that Christ spoke
falsehoods. 7. Even after the fall the law says to everyone, “Do this and you
will live.” Are we therefore bound to believe that by that legal command God
intends us to fulfill the law and covenant of works? This plainly contradicts
Scripture as well. 8. Since everyone is bound to believe that almighty God
cannot fail in his intention, because he fulfills it by irresistible power,
everyone would be bound to believe that God will always add the elect's
internal grace to the external calling, and thus that the grace of regeneration
will be common to all. But this opposes Scripture. 9. If all Adam's descendants
were bound by the law engraved on the heart and the gospel Word revealed to
believe that God wills and intends everyone's obedience, then they would not
be bound to believe that God intends or wills the permission of sin. For they
would then be bound to believe both that God intends the permission of sin and
that he does not intend it but intends obedience. Faith would thus contradict
faith, which is absurd. From Scripture and more than daily experience we know
that God wills and intends the permission of sin. But this volition

[1651 logical printed p. 243 | IA leaf 261 | PDF p. 261]

of permission cannot stand together with an intention of obedience. Therefore
faith in this cannot coexist with faith in that. 10. The act of believing that
God intends our obedience, if it is an act of faith as Piscator holds, will be
supernatural. It will therefore proceed from the grace of the Holy Spirit as
its principle. But who would believe that grace is the intrinsic and immediate
principle of believing a falsehood?

3. Let us hear Piscator's and others' arguments. 1. Argument. God commanded
Abraham to sacrifice his son. Therefore Abraham was bound to believe that God
intended his sacrifice, and consequently was bound to believe a falsehood, for
God intended no such thing. Reply. Abraham was bound to prepare himself for
the sacrifice and to believe that he was obliged to slay his son and that this
was pleasing and acceptable to God. But concerning God's intention or
non-intention of an actual sacrifice, Abraham's faith was bound to embrace
neither side of the contradiction, for in that case neither God's intention
nor his non-intention was revealed. But Paul Baynes, a theologian of
distinguished learning, objects that Abraham's faith rested upon this: that
God was able to raise Isaac from the dead, as Hebrews 11 says. Therefore he
truly believed that God intended Isaac's sacrifice. Reply. 1. With respect for
so great a man, let me deny the consequence. Hebrews 11:19 says that Abraham
believed God had power to raise the dead Isaac on the hypothesis that he had
been sacrificed. But the text does not say that Abraham believed

[Marginalia: Baynes, on Ephesians, chapter 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 244 | IA leaf 262 | PDF p. 262]

God intended his sacrifice. These things are as far apart as heaven and
earth. Insofar as the patriarch's faith rested upon divine omnipotence, I
readily concede it was true faith. Insofar as it touched God's intention
concerning Isaac's sacrifice, it was conjecture, not faith. Take an example.
Mary Magdalene encounters the gardener, supposes him—as I assume—to be the
Savior, falls on her knees, and humbly adores him. Insofar as the adoration
terminates in its material object, namely a creature, it is idolatry, not
adoration. But insofar as it terminates in that same object not under the
aspect of a creature but as Savior, it can be called adoration. 2. Argument.
Everyone is obliged to believe what, under the dictate of conscience, he
judges to be the meaning of any passage of sacred Scripture, although he may
meanwhile deviate somewhat from the passage's true meaning. On this
supposition he will be bound to believe as true what is in reality false.
Reply. Theologians rightly teach that an erroneous conscience binds but does
not oblige. It binds insofar as a person cannot without sin believe contrary
to what an erroneous conscience dictates, for he then sins against conscience.
But no one is obliged to follow the guidance of an erroneous conscience.
Rather, he must suspend assent, put away the erroneous conscience, and most
humbly search out the truth. Otherwise, one who through defective judgment
believes a heresy to be an article of faith would be bound to err. There is no
obligation to sin. 4. Someone will say,

[1651 logical printed p. 245 | IA leaf 263 | PDF p. 263]

since God has embraced only a few in gratuitous election, how can my wavering
conscience draw consolation from evangelical exhortations and promises
proposed indefinitely, unless on account of God's intention and decree it is
bound to believe that the promises were made to me by name? Reply.
Consolation will be vain if you believe falsehoods. It is far safer for each
person to examine himself and his conscience than to examine God's secret
intention, which has not yet been revealed but will be revealed by the event
and by signs. I illustrate it thus. A king scatters an abundance of gold among
a crowd of the needy with the intention that certain people whom he loves
above others should be enriched, not indiscriminately for everyone's sake. If
someone reasoned, “I refuse to gather the scattered gold until I know that the
king's love toward me is inclined and benevolent,” would he not be unworthy of
such royal munificence? A shipmaster also extends a rope to twenty men sinking
in the deep and commands them all to grasp it. Would not the reasoning be
insane and untimely of one who, tossed about in the waves and almost breathing
his last, resolved within himself: “I refuse to grasp the lowered rope until
the shipmaster addresses me by name and discloses his intention to me”? Is it
not safer for us beggars to gather our King's gold, and for those falling with
the devils into condemnation humbly to grasp the lowered rope and draw to
ourselves Christ offered to those

[1651 logical printed p. 246 | IA leaf 264 | PDF p. 264]

conscious of misery? If we do that, we may then infer that God's intention and
eternal decree were gracious toward us and toward me by name.

Reply 1. “What everyone is bound to believe is true” is a captious statement
arising from the equivocation of the word “believe,” which is used one way in
the proposition and another in the assumption. For “believe” is used in three
ways. 1. For assenting to every word of God, and thus the revealed Word of God
is the object of faith. Hence I formulate it thus: “That is true to which
everyone is bound to assent as revealed by God's Word. Therefore …” The major
is true and the minor false. It is not God's Word that Christ died for me by
name with the intention and decree to save me. The Word reveals God's
complacency and approval, but not properly God's intention and decree to save
this or that person—unless we except certain patriarchs and apostles who are
named in Scripture, and the elect who are designated not by names but by other,
more general conditions. In that way the will of sign discloses the decree,
but not as the will of sign distinguished from the will of good pleasure. What
God's intention toward each person by name was is inferred through the
application of each person's conscience, from sure signs fitted to the
general promises of the Word. But the Arminians incline toward a universal and
conditional election

[1651 logical printed p. 247 | IA leaf 265 | PDF p. 265]

and reprobation of all, just as the evangelical promises and threats are
universal. Indeed, Corvinus says, “All the promises are the very decree of God
revealed in the Word.” But who would deny that it is a decree? What kind of
decree, though? A decree of predestination? Not at all. Predestination is a
part and article of the gospel, not the whole gospel. Otherwise the gospel
would be that secret book of life in which the names of the elect are
inscribed. But that book is written in heaven, if we believe Scripture, and
the names of the elect do not appear in the gospel. Tell me where you have
read them.

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 4, folios 7–8.]

2. “Believe” means to rest by a fiducial act of the heart upon Christ as a
sufficient Savior. In that sense the major—“What everyone is bound to believe
is true”—is false. Such believing is neither true nor false, but a good and
saving gift of God which the heart and affections apprehend, and is in no way
an object of the mind. Thus the conclusion still does not follow.

3. “Believe” means to apprehend by the sense of faith and by consequence. In
that sense, what everyone is bound to believe—that is, what by the sense of
faith and from faith's fiducial act as a cause he is bound to infer—is true.
Let the major now stand. I assume: everyone is bound to believe—not by a
formal fiducial act of faith or by mental assent, but by the sense of faith and
an evident inference from the cause—and conclude that Christ, by his own and
God the Father's intention and counsel, died for him. The minor is now plainly
false.

[1651 logical printed p. 248 | IA leaf 266 | PDF p. 266]

Only believers are bound in this sense to infer God's intention and eternal
decree concerning themselves by name. The rest are not bound to believe—that
is, to infer—it. Indeed, they would then be obliged to nourish an empty and
vanishing presumption and a shadowy, chimerical conjecture. Thus the major
concerns the act of faith, both the mind's assent and the heart's fiducial
embrace, which is properly faith. The minor concerns the sense of faith and a
certain practical inference, which is improperly faith. There are therefore
four terms in the syllogism. For if the Word revealed to all that it was God's
intention and decree that everyone should believe he intends and decrees what
he commands, it would openly reveal false and contradictory things.

4. Second reply. I distinguish the word “true.” “What everyone is bound to
believe is true”: true metaphysically and fundamentally in itself and with
respect to the event, I concede the major, but utterly deny the minor. For it
is not true but false, fundamentally and metaphysically in itself, that Christ
died for everyone. 2. What everyone is bound to believe is true not only
metaphysically but morally and theologically, as revealed to the believer by
God's Word or by some evident τεκμήριον, proof, in the conscience. In this
sense the major is true but the minor still false. For it is not theologically
true or revealed to everyone that Christ died for everyone, but only to one
who is elect and indeed believing—that is, one who by a fiducial act

[1651 logical printed p. 249 | IA leaf 267 | PDF p. 267]

rests upon Christ the Savior. Now let the syllogism be constructed in this way,
and the Arminian vanity will appear at once:

*What everyone is bound to believe is true—namely, metaphysically and also
theologically revealed to each person who rests with his whole heart upon
Christ.*

*But that Christ died for everyone is metaphysically true and also
theologically revealed—namely, to every person who believes and rests with his
whole heart upon Christ.*

*Therefore.*

We concede the conclusion, namely: therefore Christ died for every believer.

5. Third reply. I also answer by distinguishing and limiting the word
“everyone.” What everyone—namely, everyone burdened and weighed down by the
weight of sins—is bound to believe is true. What everyone who abandons himself,
his own righteousness, and every created help from a humble desire for
salvation is bound to believe is true. Everyone can now see that not all, but
only those burdened with the weight of sins, are bound to believe that Christ
died for them; and this agrees with Scripture. To come to Christ is to
believe, but only the burdened and weary are commanded to come to Christ,
Matthew 11:28–29. Only those who thirst and are conscious of their dryness are
invited to the waters, Isaiah 55:1; John 7:37; Revelation 21:6–7. Only those
burdened with sins are immediately invited to Christ. Objection. Then are the
rest commanded in the evangelical precepts

[1651 logical printed p. 250 | IA leaf 268 | PDF p. 268]

to presume rather than believe? For all are commanded to believe. Reply. All
are commanded to believe, but in a definite order. Perhaps it may be said that
I do not include application in the essence of faith, contrary to the opinions
of orthodox theologians. Reply. I think it is an effect inseparably
accompanying faith, not its formal act; nor do I think that the more profound
theologians oppose me. Nor will a general assent be justifying faith, nor, as
Downham would have it, a free, living, true, and effectual assent. Rather, Paul
in 1 Timothy 1:15 seems to me to distinguish these three things. 1. Assent, by
reason of which the gospel is called a faithful saying. 2. Trust, the heart's
rest and reliance upon Christ as the object of trust, and also upon the
promises as the object by which it trusts. By reason of this Paul says that it
is λόγον ἄξιον πάσης ἀποδοχῆς, “worthy to be received with the whole embrace of the
heart”; and this is the formal nature of faith. Hence in Hebrew it is expressed
by אֶל and עַל נִשְׁעַן, Proverbs 3:5; Isaiah 50:10; Psalm 71:6; hence also πιστεύειν ἐπὶ, or εἰς τὸν
Θεόν; and more expressly by the Hebrew verb גָּלַל, “to roll,” Psalm 22:8;
גּוֹל עַל יְהוָה, Psalm 37:5. 3. Paul adds application, by which he believes
that Christ came to save him, the chief of sinners.

6. But it may be said that I cannot rest trustfully upon Christ as Savior in
general unless I am persuaded that he is my Savior. For either I rely upon
Christ as Savior indefinitely, without reference to those saved—which is
absurd,

[1651 logical printed p. 251 | IA leaf 269 | PDF p. 269]

for a relative would thus be separated from its correlate—or I rely upon him
as the Savior of other believers and thus apply him to others, not to myself,
which is absurd; or, third, I rely upon him as my Savior, and thus particular
application is enclosed within the essential concept of faith. I reply: by
the formal act of faith I trustfully rest upon Christ as the sufficient Savior
of all believers. Nor is it absurd to apply Christ to those to whom God
applies him in the Word. Then by the sense of faith I believe him to be my
Savior. Paul distinguishes these things in 1 Timothy 1:15. 2. I must first
rest upon Christ as the sufficient author of salvation, as he is described in
the Word—able to save those who come to him, merciful, compassionate, and
gracious to all who flee to him—before I rest upon him as my Savior. Otherwise
my faith will be entirely enthusiastic: relying on him as gracious toward me
before his gracious nature toward humankind has yet been established for me
from the Word. And this fiducial act may be said to contain that application
so often inculcated by our theologians, since it is opposed to the general
assent which the reprobate and devils give to God's Word. 3. That Christ died
with the intention and eternal decree to save me is not the object of faith,
nor written, nor present in the Word by consequence, unless some internal
τεκμήριον, proof, is added in my conscience. With that preceding, from a
fiducial act of faith in the word of promise and in Christ promised

[1651 logical printed p. 252 | IA leaf 270 | PDF p. 270]

and exhibited I infer it. 4. One guilty of treason first believes that the
prince is merciful to all who humbly deprecate his wrath, before he trusts
that the prince is merciful toward himself. A beggar seeing a sum of gold
first inclines toward it in his affection as the remedy for poverty, before
he draws it to himself with his heart's affection. 5. Christ first became
known to me, in the order of nature, as my Savior by right of promise because
I believe in him, before I was persuaded that he is my Savior. For faith
declares to me that I have a right in the Son of God; but having a right in a
thing precedes trusting that the thing belongs to me. For the faith by which I
know rests on the right by which I possess. Indeed, because I believe that
Christ died for me, it is therefore antecedently true that he died for me. But
it is not established that he died for me unless I have first believed in him
with the heart's fiducial embrace as a sufficient Savior; and this is the
specific act of faith.
