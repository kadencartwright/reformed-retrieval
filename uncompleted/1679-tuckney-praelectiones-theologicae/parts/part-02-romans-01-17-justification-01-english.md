ROMANS 1:17.

“For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith, as it is
written, ‘But the righteous shall live by faith.’” Vulgate: “For through it
the righteousness of God is disclosed from faith to faith, as it is written,
‘But he who is righteous by faith shall live.’” Beza.

When at last I had brought my mind, which had long been wholly averse, to
undertake this office of lecturing for a time at least, to the best of my
ability—reluctantly indeed, and all but under compulsion—I found myself just
as uncertain whenever I was about to enter the arena, anxiously considering
these two matters:

1. What Rhodes and what leap—that is, what head of theology I should treat
first of all.

2. By what road I should proceed: by that more pleasant and royal road of
interpreting Holy Scripture, or through the thickets of controversies and the
thorns of questions, which have grown far too plentifully in the Lord’s field,
in Christ’s vineyard, especially in the schools of theologians, since the
Scholastics became its cultivators.

As for the second matter, there are many things which persuade me, already
willing, and carry me headlong into the embraces of Holy Scripture.

First, its waters are the waters of Siloah: unmuddied, clear, flowing gently,
as those of Isaiah 8:6; they heal all things, Ezekiel 47:9; they make glad the
city of God, Psalm 46:4. Why, then, should we forsake the spring of living
waters, hew out broken cisterns for ourselves, and pursue pools, as we have it
in Jeremiah 2:13? Therefore let each of us regard what the same prophet says
to the Jews in verse 18 as spoken to himself in this matter: “And now, what
have you to do on the road to Egypt, to drink the water of Sihor”—that is,
black water, or, as the Vulgate interpreter renders it, turbid water—“or what
have you to do on the road to Assyria, to drink the water of the river,” that
is, the rushing river? Such are the waters of the Nile and Euphrates, and such
for the most part are the waters of disputants: rushing and turbulent, not
waters in which eyes are washed so that they may see more keenly, as that man
once did in the waters of Siloam, John 9:7, but waters by which they become
more blind—like the courtier who had learned to dispute so well that he had
unlearned the Apostles’ Creed.

Moreover, the words of God are “sound words,” 2 Timothy 1:13: both sound and
health-giving. But according to that saying of the same apostle in 1 Timothy
6:4, “diseased about questions and battles of words,” these altercations and
verbal combats—such as, on Cicero’s testimony, nearly all the disputes of the
Stoics were, and very many of the Scholastics’—produce disease rather than
medicine. The itch for disputing is the Church’s mange. You know whose motto
that was, and the man in whose authorship he so greatly glories.

Since, furthermore, Holy Scripture is “the end of every controversy,” as is
said of an oath, these disputes sow lawsuits rather than settle them,

[Marginalia: Isaiah 8:6; Ezekiel 47:9; Psalm 46:4; Jeremiah 2:13, 18; John
9:7; Dr. Henry Wotton; Hebrews 6:16.]

[1679 printed p. 21 | IA leaf 29]

so that one can deservedly say of them what Nazianzen once said of councils:
“I have seen no good end of any council, nor a remedy for evils so much as an
addition to them.” And what, I ask, is the outcome of the disputes and
altercations instituted everywhere today in schools and crossroads, except
that through unfinished battles each man departs more confirmed in his own
opinion? Thus the father of Francis Junius acted with the greatest prudence:
when his son had fallen into atheism and he wished to restore him to the fear
of God, he refused to dispute with him about it but set the sacred volume
before him to read. A happy outcome did not fail such a pious and prudent
plan. For Holy Scripture has this distinctive property: because it is
God-breathed, divinely inspired, it breathes God throughout. Beholding the
glory of God in it as in a mirror, we are transformed into the same image; we
are made either truly holy or at least less impious. The more we have to do
with Scripture and approach it more closely, the farther we withdraw from
error and begin to savor truth.

Hence it happens, as some of our men have also observed, that the same Roman
Catholic authors think more orthodoxly and come nearer both to us and to truth
itself when they write commentaries on Holy Scripture than when they fight in
their polemical works. Such is the reverence, majesty, and holiness of the
Scriptures that something of the truth is ordinarily rubbed off upon those
who approach them more closely.

We may also add this. The apostles, apostolic men, and other early Christians,
mindful of the apostolic precept concerning “sound speech that cannot be
condemned,” preached the Christian faith consonant with Holy Scripture. They
confirmed it not by the quarrels of disputations but rather by holiness of
life and martyrdom, and from the same Scriptures they successively refuted
the errors that arose. When Antichrist was now about to arise, men withdrew a
little from that simpler manner of preaching, writing, and believing, until,
when he had clearly arisen and been revealed, the whole matter came down
almost entirely to what is called Scholastic Theology, which Possevinus makes
synonymous with Christian Theology. Yet how enormously it differed from the
primitive theology is witnessed by Aristotle’s opinions being held almost as
a canon, the deep silence of Scripture—indeed, its plainly detestable neglect
and contempt—the fewer chairs and lectures in Scripture established in
schools and monasteries, and the very few commentaries on Scripture published
then in comparison with other theological and ecclesiastical writings. Those
commentaries too were generally inept enough. Once the new subtlety of
disputing was introduced in place of the ancient simplicity of believing, it
concerned little knots and thorns for the most part; or, if it dealt with the
more substantial articles of faith, they had been most shamefully befouled by
these Harpies.

Therefore let those thorns and nettles which have long covered the Lord’s
field, no less than that sluggard’s field in Proverbs 24, be rooted out. Let
those “oppositions of knowledge falsely so called,” which arose together with
Antichrist and, as I believe, are to be abolished with him, depart from our
churches and schools.

Only this must be added: the sole end and aim of all theological disputations
ought to be that the genuine sense of Holy Scripture be duly drawn out and
its most sacred doctrine properly explained and confirmed. For this there is
never any need of quarrels and “perverse disputings of corrupt men.” When this
has at last been accomplished by an orthodox exposition of Scripture, there
will be no further need

[Marginalia: 1 Timothy 6:4–5; Nazianzen, Epistle 42; in the life of Francis
Junius; 2 Timothy 3:16; 2 Corinthians 3:18; Chemnitz; Titus 2:8; Possevinus,
*Apparatus sacer*, prolegomena; Proverbs 24; 1 Timothy 6:20; 1 Timothy 6:5.]

[1679 printed p. 22 | IA leaf 30]

of endless controversies that can never be concluded. These, then, are the
considerations which perhaps make me too averse to treating controversies in
discharging this task and carry me, already running, toward the exposition of
the sacred volume.

Yet in the same sacred volume we have mention of the heresies and questions
of Paul—or of the apostles themselves.

Since heresies must exist, and since now, as formerly, there is no lack of
those who twist the Scriptures to their own destruction and that of others;

and since, finally, it rests upon the man who holds fast the faithful word not
only to exhort by sound doctrine but also to refute those who contradict;

I have judged that it will be most advantageous to you, and perhaps to me, if
I do not spend all my time expounding Scripture, but select certain passages
as either my mind or the subject may require. After first giving as clear and
genuine an exposition of them as I can, I shall furnish an explanation of any
doubts that arise from them, or, as occasion requires, treat the controversies
ordinarily debated about them, sometimes briefly and sometimes more fully if
the dignity or difficulty of the matters demands it.

Let this suffice, first, concerning the road and method which I have judged
should be followed in this course of lectures.

2. As for the other matter—namely, what head of theology I should choose to
explain, elucidate, and confirm chiefly by this labor of mine—although they
are all indeed splendid and far more worthy of the greatest possible acuity
than mine will be, nevertheless two considerations especially persuade me to
undertake, to the best of my ability, to preserve intact above the rest that
most delightful doctrine of the justification of a sinner—not the whole of
it, indeed, but its chief debated points:

1. The dignity of the subject.

2. The adversaries’ zeal to obscure and corrupt it.

1. Indeed, so great is the dignity and excellence of this doctrine that some
have deservedly called it the heart, soul, marrow, and pillar of all
Christianity. For Luther it is the article of the standing and falling
Church. By his own profession it reigned so fully in his heart and everywhere
filled both pages of his writings that Christ’s Church owes him much for
cultivating and establishing it, and will owe him forever. Nor will our
sloth be free of the foulest stain of ingratitude and impiety if we lose,
either by learning carelessly or teaching coldly, what he and the other
laborers in the Lord’s harvest transmitted to us their descendants with so
much zeal, sweat, and even blood, as a deposit from God sealed with Christ’s
own blood, and indeed committed to us to guard faithfully. Nor, under the
guise of a prudent moderation of spirit, if you please—that is, through a
degenerate softness—may we ever permit it either to be adulterated by the
sophistries of the heterodox or torn from us. That would bring no less
disgrace upon God’s truth and name than manifold loss upon the whole Church
and upon ourselves.

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 11:19; 2 Peter 3:16; Titus 1:9; Peter Martyr on
Romans 4 at the beginning; Spanheim, *Theological Disputations*, part 1, p.
277.]

[1679 printed p. 23 | IA leaf 31]

1. First, when this article is rightly understood and explained, the highest
glory of both divine mercy and divine justice shines in it as in the clearest
mirror. For according to its teaching, by a certain wonderful and ineffable
economy God makes equal satisfaction to mercy and justice: he freely forgives
the debt and at the same time exacts it in full, so that he is both just and
the justifier of the one who is of the faith of Jesus, Romans 3:26.

2. Our highest consolation in both life and death depends especially upon
this article. For, “being justified by faith, we have peace with God,” Romans
5:1. While all things within and without are calm and peaceful, Roman
Catholics can boast of their works and merits, as the Pharisee once did. But
when the soul is once bloodied by the goads of divine wrath, sin, and death,
there is absolutely nothing to stop the flow of blood or apply an anodyne
except Christ’s righteousness apprehended by faith and the pardon freely
granted for Christ’s sake. Cardinal Contarini voluntarily and piously
acknowledges this. Cardinal Bellarmine, compelled by the force of truth after
arguing extensively to the contrary, recognized it as the safest course and,
when he was at last on the border of death and breathing his last, confirmed
it by his own testimony.

3. This sweetest doctrine of free justification through Christ must be
asserted and vindicated all the more because—whatever Roman Catholics and
some from among us, if indeed they may be called ours, babble to the
contrary—it truly is the fountain of good works and of all holiness both of
heart and life. The apostle demonstrates this most abundantly in chapters 3,
6, 7, and 8 of the Epistle to the Romans. He expends no less effort refuting
that cavillation of the adversaries than he does confirming free
justification by faith throughout his extended treatment. Let those among
whom the daughter has devoured the mother set justification and
sanctification at war with each other. Let them regard the imputed
righteousness of Christ as imaginary and as an inducement to every kind of
carelessness and even baseness, while they market their own works and merits
far too insistently. But we have been better taught by Holy Scripture and by
our own most certain experience: “whatever is not of faith is sin,” and all
holiness properly so called, all righteousness internal and external, draws
both its true origin and by far its most effective motive from free
justification through faith in Christ. We must therefore take earnest care
that, in losing justifying faith, we do not thereby also lose all true piety
and the whole of evangelical righteousness.

4. Indeed, all evangelical truth is at stake. This is the fourth
consideration sharpening our diligence in asserting and vindicating this
article: its exceedingly close connection with the other chief doctrines of
the faith, such as God’s decree, Christ’s satisfaction and merit, original
sin, free will, and the obligation of the law. All these and other such
things will soon fall from their height the moment the foundations of free
justification are either overthrown or badly laid. Roman Catholics,
Socinians, Arminians, Antinomians, and Libertines have exhibited a most
lamentable example of this. Each group thinks wrongly, according to its own
opinion, about this free article of justification, and therefore also thinks
most badly about the doctrines just mentioned.

For Luther and Chemnitz this locus is, as it were, the citadel and chief
bulwark of all Christian doctrine and religion. If it is obscured,
adulterated, or overthrown, it is impossible to retain purity of doctrine in
other loci. Indeed, Calvin

[Marginalia: Romans 3:26; Romans 5:1; Contarini, *On Justification*;
Bellarmine, *On Justification*; Romans 14:23; Luther, vol. 1, fol. 13.]

[1679 printed p. 24 | IA leaf 32]

calls it the principal hinge by which religion is sustained. Pighius,
Bellarmine, and others freely confess that the matter of justification is the
hinge upon which all controversies between us and them depend and turn. Lest
we therefore fall with our cause utterly defeated, it demands greater caution
from us and more vigorous industry in asserting it.

5. We must labor at it all the more, finally, because this doctrine of free
justification is especially characteristic of the orthodox Church and, if I
may so speak, the most Christian doctrine.

First, it distinguishes Christians from the heathen. Among the latter some
report of justice and moral philosophy had spread, but so deep was the
silence concerning justification that its very word was unknown to them—or
at least it found less favor with the masters of their elegant language.

Even among Christians themselves, this justification which we defend through
Christ’s imputed righteousness apprehended by faith alone has always been a
Shibboleth, cross, and scandal to the heterodox, but the badge and symbol of
the orthodox.

2. This brings us from the first incentive for treating this subject—the
weight and dignity of the matter in hand—to the second: the adversaries’ zeal
and industry either to despise, obscure, or even corrupt it. The more a matter
of the greatest concern to us is either treated negligently by our own people
or attacked violently by enemies, the more vigilantly we must watch over it,
lest something of such value to us suffer harm.

We have already said how valuable the uncorrupted doctrine of justification
is, or at least ought to be, since our consolation, holiness, and salvation
chiefly turn upon it.

How fiercely throughout every age of the Church, from Abel down to the
present, men have fought against this Palladium of our salvation has been
explained by Chemnitz as piously and learnedly as fully, and I do not wish to
plunder him.

Under the Old Testament the prophets plainly had to deal with those who
placed all their hope of justification and salvation either in the ceremonies
of the law, in obedience rendered to the moral law, or finally in
superstitious acts of will-worship and works of supererogation invented by
themselves.

In the time of Christ and the apostles, the Gospels, Acts of the Apostles,
and ecclesiastical histories everywhere testify what the scribes, Pharisees,
and the rest of the Jews thought about the righteousness of works; what
Ebion, Cerinthus, and the pseudo-apostles thought about joining works of the
law with faith in the work of justification; what the Gnostics blasphemously
taught, under the pretext of free justification by faith, about utter neglect
of good works and entirely unrestrained license to sin; and what the
Carpocratians taught about the necessity of unspeakable defilements for
salvation. Against the former Paul painstakingly proves almost everywhere,
especially in the Epistles to the Romans and Galatians, that we are justified
by faith alone. Against the latter the other apostles—Peter, John, and
Jude—rise no less vigorously, urging repentance, love, and every holiness of
life, and especially James, who denies justification to a solitary,
historical faith empty of good works.

After these errors had thus been quieted or defeated, in the immediately
following centuries—when some matters were treated in their own right and
others, as occasion arose, chiefly in controversies about the Trinity,
Christ, and the articles connected with them—

[Marginalia: Calvin, *Institutes* 3.11.1; Chemnitz, *Examination*; Ephesians
5:12.]

[1679 printed p. 25 | IA leaf 33]

the doctrine of justification was little agitated at that time. It is not
mentioned very frequently by the writers of those ages; or if they treat it
at least incidentally, they often do so less properly and accurately.

But when Pelagius finally arose, Augustine, Jerome, Gregory, and then others,
awakened by him, labored to vindicate it from his corruptions and began to
speak of it more cautiously and treat it somewhat more accurately. Yet they
did not do so without saying many things less distinctly, and indeed rather
inconveniently. They take faith for knowledge and assent in the end. They
distinguish less accurately between Law and Gospel and between justification
and sanctification. In order to shake men from security and urge them more
sharply to pursue piety, they often mingled good works into the article of
justification itself.

Hence the Scholastics, when at last the matter came to them, seized an
occasion to confuse and corrupt everything. Among them there is nothing solid
or sincere: they prattle of preparations, works, and merits; they either
plainly know nothing of the righteousness of faith and hence free
justification, or stoutly oppose what they know of it.

Thus we have descended to our own times, in which human opinions are more
numerous or combative about hardly any article of the Christian faith than
about this primary article of justification. Osiander asserts that there are
twenty-five mutually dissenting opinions among the Lutherans. Bellarmine
deals more generously with us: he removes twenty of them and reduces them all
to five.

Leaving others aside, we too retain a fivefold number of those who contend
fiercely with each other about this article: Roman Catholics, Socinians,
Remonstrants, Antinomians, and finally Protestants who think orthodoxly about
the matter.

How many battles there are between them on this question will become clearer
later, when at least some of them are treated individually. For now let this
be said in general: the first three—Roman Catholics, Socinians, and
Remonstrants—unanimously reject the imputation of Christ’s righteousness for
our justification.

The Roman Catholics, who, by Pighius’s own confession, have obscured this
doctrine more than illuminated it in their writings, err here in several
ways. Their first and principal error is to place the formal nature of
justification not in the imputation of Christ’s righteousness apprehended by
faith, but in the infusion of inherent righteousness and in good works.

The Socinians wish it to consist in the remission of sins in such a way that
they exclude Christ’s satisfaction altogether.

The Arminians place it in the imputation of faith properly so called for
righteousness and in accepting it as payment for our justification.

The Antinomians and Libertines proclaim Gospel, grace, faith, and
justification in such a way that they refuse to admit the Law even as a rule
of life; they destroy repentance in their writings and deny holiness by their
deeds.

These are our chief adversaries in this arena. They devote themselves wholly
to undermining this most fortified bulwark of our faith with tunnels or
demolishing it with battering rams. In defending it, many of our men have
performed excellent labor. I too, though least of all, gladly join them,

[Marginalia: Bellarmine, *On Justification*, book 1, chapter 4.]

[1679 printed p. 26 | IA leaf 34]

“so that the truth of the gospel might remain,” as the apostle speaks about
this very matter in Galatians 2:5. May this primary truth of the Gospel, in
which our consolation and salvation itself consist, and which until now has
had the most vigorous champions and defenders among us, never finally perish
through either the craft or the force of the adversaries, or at some time
totter through our more drowsy negligence.

For this purpose the Epistle of the apostle Paul to the Romans first presented
itself to me for exposition. In it he accomplishes the whole task with plainly
divine artistry, acuteness, and strength of arguments. But since Augustine
himself, after undertaking an exposition of this epistle, abandoned it,
deterred by the magnitude and labor of the work, as he himself confesses in
*Retractations* 1.25, why should one so insignificant as I dare undertake so
great a task? Even if I wished to do so, the work has been taken from me by
the best ancient and more recent theologians, who have published most learned
commentaries on this entire epistle. To attempt anything here after them would
be to write the *Iliad* after Homer. It will be enough for me to select from
here and there certain passages for exposition, either those which contain
some greater difficulty or those which present to us more fully and plainly
the principal heads of this doctrine of justification. Let the first of these
be the passage I recited before, from chapter 1, verse 17:

“For in it the righteousness of God is revealed from faith to faith, as it is
written, ‘But the righteous shall live by faith.’”

Before I set about this work, however, I thought it worthwhile to premise two
introductory questions. A clearer treatment and solution of them will make the
whole subject to be discussed afterward more lucid, and the path through all
that remains less obstructed.

For the statement in chapter 3, verse 28—“We therefore conclude that a man is
justified by faith apart from works of the law”—is the sum of nearly the whole
disputation which Paul conducts elsewhere, but especially in this Epistle to
the Romans and the other to the Galatians, pursuing it with both the greatest
care in the work and the greatest earnestness of spirit. We shall therefore
lend light to everything that follows by explaining these two questions:

1. What it properly means to justify, or in what sense the word
*justification* is taken in this Pauline disputation.

2. What law the apostle means, and which works of that law he excludes from
this justification.

The sense in which faith is reckoned will have to be investigated later, when
we treat more particularly the things that contribute to the constitution of
justification.

As to the first question, Contarini, it seems to me, rightly and prudently
begins his epistolary treatise on justification in this way: “Since in every
disputation one must first inquire into and clearly understand what the terms
we use in the disputation signify—especially since their multiplicity and
ambiguity can easily confuse and overturn the meaning of the entire
matter—we shall first explain what justification signifies.” So it seemed to
him, and so it does to me. For if the trumpet gives an uncertain sound,

[Marginalia: Galatians 2:5; Augustine, *Retractations* 1.25; Romans 3:28; 1
Corinthians 14:8; Contarini, *Epistle on Justification*.]

[1679 printed p. 27 | IA leaf 35]

who will prepare himself for battle? Or at least who will fight lawfully if
he does not know whom he has as his enemy? This happens very often in the
battle over justification which the Roman Catholics undertake against us.
Since they confuse justification with sanctification, and since we locate it
in the imputation of Christ’s righteousness while they locate it in the
infusion of inherent grace, very many of their arguments labor almost
continually under this ambiguity of the word. It is therefore quite clear how
necessary it is, even at the threshold, to explain the genuine sense of the
terms, at least as they are used by the sacred authors in this controversy.

Nor are we dealing here with what they call a grammatical war. In the
principal loci of doctrine, as much as one errs in words, so much is lost in
the matters themselves, as Chemnitz rightly says in his *Commonplaces*, page
246. To carry out this explanation, take the following propositions.

1. Let the first be this: we need not labor much over either the derivation or
the composition of the Latin word *justification*. I know well enough that
the Roman Catholics will take offense at this. The Cologne censors were
annoyed that their Monheim, although a professor of grammar and Aristotle,
could not discover the derivation or true meaning of justification in either
science and therefore had to take refuge in a Hebraism. These cautious men,
of course, knew very well how greatly it would tell against their position if
the Hebrew and Greek words, which favor their cause less, were considered and
the Latin word *justificare* did not hold the field alone. From its etymology
alone they hope to win their case, since *justificare* is to make just, which
seems to speak plainly not of our imputed righteousness but of their inherent
righteousness. But with their leave, I would reply as follows.

1. It may be doubted whether all words with this ending—such as *justifico*,
*sanctifico*, *velifico*, and the rest of this kind—always derive their origin
from the verb *facio*, “I make.” If they do not, their argument in this case
from the etymology they assign to this word is futile.

2. Even if their derivation of the word, and the commonly received opinion,
be granted, so that *justifico* is the same as “I make just,” I nevertheless
deny that all words with this ending or composition always signify the making
of something which must inhere in the subject, or the acquisition of a new
quality inhering in it. Some of them denote only a new external relation or
declaration, as some of our writers clearly show in the words *lucrifacere*,
“to gain,” and *magnificare*, “to magnify.” *Lucrifacere* is derived from
*facere* far more plainly than *justificare* is; but one who gains money does
not thereby acquire a new quality inhering in the money, but a new ownership
and relation. And the blessed Virgin magnified the Lord not by making him
inherently great, but by declaring and proclaiming him so. The matter is
exactly parallel here. We acknowledge, and indeed freely profess, that a
justified man is made inherently righteous through sanctification, whether it
precedes or follows justification, or at least always inseparably accompanies
it. But justification itself, as such, makes a man just neither in the thing
itself, as will appear later, nor in the word, which is our present subject,
by infusing an inherent quality. That is the work of regeneration and
sanctification. Rather, since it is a relation, it introduces a new relation,
as we said before about the verb *lucrifacere*; and since it is forensic, it
presents a declaration of righteousness, in which it agrees well with that
other verb, “to magnify.”

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 14:8; Chemnitz, *Commonplaces*, p. 246; Selden,
*On the Syrian Gods*, prologue; Spanheim, *Theological Disputations*, part 1,
p. 277; Luke 1:46; Romans 5:17.]

[1679 printed p. 28 | IA leaf 36]

3. What I would say about this Latin word *justification* is what all our
writers object, with all Roman Catholics admitting it: the word is foreign to
Latin ears and appears nowhere in Caesar, Cicero, or other authors of older
and purer Latinity. Neither I nor any of our writers says this as though we
wished to bring forward Cicero or Terence as judge concerning *justifico* in
an ecclesiastical question, as Bellarmine in his candor has chosen to charge
us. We say it to weaken their argument for inherent righteousness drawn from
the word’s etymology, since the masters of Latinity do not support it or give
us assurance about either its derivation or its proper meaning.

4. Since they take no account here of pagan authors—which I wish they had
also done in many other theological doctrines—we appeal to Holy Scripture.
Since it was written entirely not in Latin but in Hebrew or Greek, Scripture
nowhere gives us, nor can give us, this Latin word *justification* word for
word. Thus it cannot be right that this primary theological doctrine should
have been established by etymological grammar applied to a foreign word,
especially by Scholastics who knew only Latin, and that none too happily.

Let this suffice for the first proposition: namely, that we need not labor so
greatly over this word *justification* and its etymology.

2. Let the second proposition be this. Justification, at least as regards the
word, must be determined from the original Hebrew or Greek words which the
Holy Spirit chose to use in Holy Scripture, either exclusively or chiefly and
generally, to designate this matter. Above the rest these are הִצְדִּיק in
the Old Testament and δικαιοῦν in the New, which Latin authors ordinarily
render by the verb *justificare*. It is entirely proper that the mind and
meaning of the Holy Spirit be understood from his own words rather than from
our borrowed ones. These words are therefore our Cynosure and the surest rule
for deciding the matter. Whatever הִצְדִּיק signifies among the Hebrews and
δικαιοῦν among the Greeks, let that at last be justification. Let us stand
before their tribunal, and by its sentence let our cause stand or fall.

Here the Roman Catholics evade the issue. Andradius in particular resents the
claim that the principal force of justification should be learned from the
idiom of the Hebrew language rather than from the clearest passages of
Scripture which teach the order and manner of justifying. But our writers do
not mean this; they do not set the idiom of the Hebrew language against the
plain testimonies of Scripture. How much those plain testimonies favor our
side will appear later, when we come to the matter itself. But here, while
dealing with the word and its meaning, we judge it not only equitable but
necessary that the matter be decided not from the idiom or etymology of the
Latin word *justification*, as the Roman Catholics do, but from the Hebrew and
Greek words which the Holy Spirit himself used in the sacred volume to
express his thoughts. To explain this more clearly, we think three things
must be observed.

1. We should be concerned not so much with the sense in which other authors
use these Hebrew and Greek words as with the sense in which they are employed
in the sacred volume, which is the canon of our faith. As for the latter of
the two, the verb δικαιοῦν has two principal meanings among secular authors,

The former meaning, “to judge just” or “to regard as just”—and that not by a
private judgment but publicly and before a tribunal—is indeed favorable, as
I note in passing, and supports our position. It is therefore wholly forensic,
which is our sense of justification. It does not mean to make or constitute a
person inherently just, which is the Roman Catholic sense but seems nowhere
to appear among approved Greek authors. In this first meaning of the word,
therefore, they vote with us and with Scripture, except that among them
δικαιοῦν, insofar as it means to judge just, is said rather of a thing or
cause, as when something is decreed to be just and equitable, while on the
sacred page, especially when justification is treated, it is applied to
persons when they are pronounced just. Even in this first meaning of the word,
then, there is not complete agreement between the writers of the sacred volume
and secular authors.

In the second meaning there is some agreement in the matter, but greater
disagreement in everything else. They agree with us in this: although among
them the word means to punish, they understand not penalties inflicted by a
private person but those imposed by a public judge, and thus they also regard
it as a forensic word. This is the very point we chiefly press against the
Roman Catholics in this controversy.

Yet the fact that among them κολάζειν means to inflict punishment establishes
a sense plainly contrary to that in which the sacred authors, especially
Paul, use the word in the matter of justification. In Paul, although the word
δικαίωμα sometimes has regard to punishment, as in the final verse of Romans
1—“who, knowing the judgment of God, that those who do such things are worthy
of death”—δικαιοῦν and δικαίωσις denote absolution and deliverance from guilt
and punishment. Among secular authors, by contrast, δικαιοῦν, κολάζειν, and
θανάτῳ ζημιοῦν, “to inflict punishment,” denote condemnation. Among the
Greeks δικαίωσις thus signifies not acquittal but condemnation, to which it
is often opposed on the sacred page. Even in Cicero’s *Verrines*,
ἐδικαιώθησαν is explained as “they were punished.” So also today, among the
Germans and Scots, when guilty men are punished, they are said to be
“justified,” because by paying the penalty for their crimes they satisfy the
law to some extent. The meaning of this word in the present theological
controversy must therefore be sought not from its use among profane authors
but from its use on the sacred page. This was the first point.

2. A second must be added to it: in this matter these scriptural words must
be taken in the sense in which they are used more or most frequently in Holy
Scripture, not in the sense in which they are used most rarely. Since they
occur there very frequently in this controversy, it would be unfair to abandon
their commonest meaning and the general rule, and restrict them to the anomaly
of one or two examples—even if we grant that a few such anomalous examples
may be found there, a matter to be discussed later.

3. It must also rightly be laid down that in expounding Holy Scripture these
words are to be interpreted in the sense in which Scripture uses them when
treating the matter of justification, not in a sense in which, while treating
another subject, they perhaps can or even must be interpreted differently as
the sense of the passage allows or requires. Words, like things, have their
proper seats, according to which they are to be explained.

So much for the second proposition.
