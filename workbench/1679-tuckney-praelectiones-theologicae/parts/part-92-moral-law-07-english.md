Let us attend, if you please, to the emphases of the passage.

1. “Do not think” intimates that by our natural disposition we are rather inclined toward the Antinomian opinion, and therefore blocks the path even to the first such thought.

2. “Do not think,” and “I did not come”: so that we might finally believe, he employs the negation twice.

3. In saying more emphatically, “Truly I say,” he demonstrates both the certainty and the evidentness of the matter, so that, if we will not trust Christ merely affirming, we may trust him asseverating and, as some hold, swearing.

4. “Until heaven and earth pass away” indicates eternal stability.

5. “One jot or one stroke.” See how he pursues the smallest details, taking more exact care that not even the least thing should be taken from the law.

But what is it that he puts forward with so much ceremony? This one thing, namely, that he came not to dissolve the law but to fulfill it. He does not mean the ceremonial law. For by fulfilling that law he dissolved it, but he means especially the moral law, which he expounded in the following chapter and vindicated from the Pharisees' corruptions.

He came not to dissolve but to fulfill this law:

1. With respect to active obedience, by performing the duties owed far more perfectly than Adam himself ever could.

[Marginalia: Of men.]

2. With respect to passive obedience, by paying the penalties owed for its violation far more fully than if a thousand men of the world had endured the same.

[1679 new-series fol. 31 | IA leaf 351]

3. With respect to the operation of his Spirit in believers' hearts, through which he both wills and brings it about that they render obedience to the law.

4. Finally, with respect to his teaching, by which he establishes the law's authority. He rebukes the Antinomian teachers more sharply in verse 19 and shows that he is not a new lawgiver who, after abolishing the law, would impose new and more perfect human duties, so far as morals are concerned, as the Socinians, Antinomians, and in part even the Papists dream. Rather, he showed himself the true interpreter of the ancient law. Moreover, Christ's more exact exposition of this law openly demonstrates that it pertains to Christians. For why would the law need to be interpreted by Christ if it were to be abrogated by him? We do not, I think, take care to mend a worn-out garment that we intend to cast away.

[Marginalia: *De legibus*, book 9, chapter 11, sections 14–15.]

But although this opinion is somewhat agreeable to Suarez, this passage, as he says, proves nothing in his judgment.

And why exactly? Because the statement, “I did not come to dissolve the law,” speaks indefinitely of the whole law, whereas with respect to its obligation Christ was dissolving the ceremonial law.

Therefore, Suarez says, “to dissolve” in this passage means not to abrogate but to transgress, which is what the Jews accused him of doing.

I answer:

1. Although he speaks indefinitely of the law, he nevertheless means chiefly the moral law, as is clear from his subsequent exposition and vindication of it in the same chapter.

2. Granted that the words are understood of the whole law, they nevertheless refer to the ceremonial law in one way and to the moral law in another. He did not dissolve the ceremonial law in the sense of treating it, while it remained in force, as though it contained nothing true in itself; instead, did he not fulfill it by exhibiting the reality it signified and so bearing witness to its truth? Yet by this fulfillment, once its time had elapsed, he dissolved it by abrogating it. But he in no way dissolved the moral law as the rule of right and good; instead, he fulfilled it in every way, both by performing and by teaching it. This above all serves our present purpose.

3. The Jews charged Christ not only with transgressing the law but also with abrogating it. Therefore this testimony of the Savior, whatever Suarez has advanced to the contrary, still retains its very great force for asserting our cause.
