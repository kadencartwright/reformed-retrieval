CHAPTER XXXVII.

Fourth Corollary: The Dissension in England Must Be Imputed to the Bishops, Not
to the Puritans.

Thirdly, he extends the defense of God’s holy servants in England who suffer in
the cause of reformed discipline. Bancroft regards them as heretics, and all
the Hierarchists as schismatics, to such a degree that they continually
proclaim them even the authors and fathers of the separatists’ schism. To
answer this calumny, it must first be understood that not all who have attacked
Episcopal government in England are of one kind. Some devoutly preserve the
communion of the Church; others publicly desert it and are commonly called
separatists.

I would say that the same thing happened to these men as to Tertullian, if I
believed Jerome. For he says: “Although he had remained a presbyter in the
Church until middle age, he later slipped into Montanus’s doctrine because of
the envy and insults of the Roman Church.” Caesar Baronius contradicts Jerome
in this matter. But if, in Baronius’s judgment, Jerome is so unreliable in a
historical matter, why does Baronius press his authority as though it must
necessarily be believed if only he has said somewhere that the Episcopate was
instituted at Alexandria from the time of Mark? But to the point. Tertullian,
if Jerome is truthful, was harassed by the insults of the Roman Clergy and fell
into schism. So these schismatics today have fallen into their schism through
Episcopal persecution.

The same thing happened to Audius, according to Epiphanius. He suffered many
things from the Bishops because he inveighed against their luxury and pride.
Thus this order was swollen with pomp, impatient of reformation, harsh toward
admonishers, and finally prone to pride and luxury even from its very birth.
But it is better to append Epiphanius’s own words: “Audius, renowned in his own
country for sincerity of life and zeal, observed the things done in the
Churches. He opposed and reproved Bishops and presbyters to their faces, like a
man devoted to truth and living an exact life. If he saw any Bishop or
presbyter among the Clergy devoted to money or living in pleasures and
delights, he could not endure it. And this was very grievous to those who did
not live an approved life.

“For this reason he was treated with insults, endured contradictions, was
hated, and tolerated being driven about, thrust away, and afflicted with
disgrace. He dwelt together with them in the Church for a long time, until
certain men violently rushed upon him and expelled the man for such a cause.
He, however, did not submit to being expelled, but strove all the more to speak
the truth and did not depart from the bond of the Church. But when both he and
his companions were often beaten, and he suffered very grievous things, he
groaned and took the necessity of these injuries as his counselor. For he
separated himself from the Church, and many departed with him; and so he made a
division, although he held nothing different in the faith.” Here, reader, you
have the manners of this age represented as if upon a stage, in every respect
almost drawn from life. When you read what—

The rest found in Epiphanius concerns the separatists. They are all the more
inexcusable because they do not imitate the patience of the Puritans, who
suffer all the same things and yet have by no means shaken themselves out of
the Church’s bosom. Nor indeed do I fear that any fair-minded person will fail
to praise them on this account: although they suffered so much, they did not
violate fraternal charity or the bond of peace. As for the accusation about
vipers, I would have the Bishops remember that before Christ those who tear the
Church with inward bites are also called vipers, even if they do not depart
from its communion, Matthew 23:33. Hugh of Saint-Cher explains it in these
words: “Many Clerics are the offspring of vipers. For the offspring kills its
mother when it is born, because the mother bursts; and many, from the time they
are born in the Church, persecute the Church and Christ their Father and, so
far as lies in them, kill them.”

Therefore, when Bishops and Clerics persecute God’s holy servants in the
Church, they cannot even use the argument of Menenius Agrippa at the secession
on the Aventine against the schismatics. Nor could Pope Hadrian IV, about whom
we read in John of Salisbury: “When he was reproved for luxury, avarice,
idleness, and the wasting of the Church’s property, he confessed that the
Prelates of the Church could be regarded as bellies which devoured many
things. Yet they were not entirely idle and did not do nothing, since by the
necessary irrigation they imparted growth to the body.” But Scripture does not
usually compare shepherds to the belly, but rather to the head, eyes, and
nostrils, 1 Corinthians 12. A Bishop as Bishop supplies no necessary irrigation
to the body; and a Bishop who persecutes God’s holy servants should rather be
called a viper in the belly than the belly itself, since he makes the belly
burst.

However that may be, those ministers who suffer in England for reformed
discipline are so far from being vipers that I would rightly say they maintain
a common cause with all God’s holy servants who in every age have strenuously
and bravely fought against the corruptions of their own time. The pride of the
Western Bishops displeased Basil just as the pride of the English Bishops
displeases these men. Nor do they usually denounce them more severely than he
did when he declared that it was not fitting to oppress those humbled and
afflicted through temptations, nor “to regard rank as a matter of pride,”
since that sin alone is sufficient to establish enmity against God.

They also suffer in the same cause in which Jerome suffered. Baronius himself
does not deny that, because Jerome had spoken and written rather freely, the
Roman Clergy conspired against him, and therefore he was forced to withdraw to
Palestine. This freedom, however, was the same that is now English freedom. He
seemed to attack his Church’s Hierarchy, says Lord Mornay, who cites these words
of his, words which are also the ministers’ and fall upon the English Bishops.
“There is a holy Church there,” he says; the ministers now confess the same
thing about England. “But indeed ambition and power, and also the luxury of
belly and throat, seek to overthrow the strength of the cross, whose banner
the austerity of preaching had raised.” Yet Jerome never happened to see
harassments of those preaching the cross such as may now be seen in England,
where every preaching of the cross that is strong against vices is regarded as
harsh. Thus ambition, power, and luxury have prevailed.

The Council of Ephesus issued a Canon against Bishops who invaded another’s
province: first, lest the pride of power creep into the Church under the
pretext of the priesthood; second, lest the liberty Christ gave us be lost.
Whitaker cites and approves these reasons. The ministers in England must
therefore now be excused on two grounds. First, they know that the English
Bishops’ usurpation over their other colleagues savors of pride in power.
Second, they believe they must stand for the liberty which Christ gave each
pastor, and which is now in much greater danger from the English Episcopal
usurpation than from the invasion condemned by the Council of Ephesus.

When John of Constantinople wished to become Universal Bishop, Gregory not only
resisted him but also wrote to others so that they might defend the common
dignity of the priests. He gives almost the same reasons as the earlier Council
of Ephesus:

First, that it savors of Lucifer’s pride to exalt oneself above the Bishops, who
are clouds and stars.

Second, because when some private distinction is given to one, all the priests
are despoiled of their due honor. The ministers in England must again be
excused on two grounds. First, they know that all ministers are Bishops, God’s
clouds, and Christ’s stars, which he holds in his hand. The English Bishops who
exalt themselves above them must therefore swell with Luciferian pride.
Second, they see that if they granted the Bishops that private and singular
distinction which they now claim for themselves, they would despoil the other
ministers of God’s word of their due honor.

What should I say about Arnulph? He was hated and secretly killed by the Roman
Clergy in the time of Honorius, not on account of any heresy, but because he
was the Puritan of his time and rather freely reproved the Bishops’ idleness,
luxury, pride, and love of a more delicate life. Arnold of Brescia was not far

ministers in England suffer today. Around the year 1250 Robert Capito, Bishop
of Lincoln, was also a Puritan. He was harassed in many ways for assertions
then regarded as Puritan, excommunicated, and finally died under
excommunication. In his epitaph he is celebrated as “the corrector of Prelates
and the director of Presbyters.” He was, of course, free from that luxury,
pomp, and pride under which the English prelates now labor and, so far as those
times permitted, drew close only to the Puritan direction of the presbyters.

How like a Puritan he thundered in the sermon before the Pope which John Hus
recalls! It was directed against Bishops who were ignorant and negligent
pastors of the kind who are now not only tolerated but also approved in
England. “The cause of the lamentable contraction and diminution, and thereby
of the incomplete extension, of Christ’s kingdom is readily apparent,” he says:
“the failure of good pastors and the multiplication of evil ones. For one
thing has one cause, and opposite things have opposite causes. Since good
pastors and the liberty of pastoral power were the causes of the extension of
the Christian faith and religion, the causes of the contraction and alteration
of the same faith and religion are necessarily, on the other hand, evil
pastors, their multiplication, and the repression of pastoral power.

“And because the corruption of good necessarily generates its opposite evil,
evil pastors are the cause of unbelief, schism, heretical depravity, and
vicious conduct throughout the whole world. For, according to the voice of the
Prophets, they are the men who turn the Lord’s desirable portion into a desert

flock, ravage and demolish the Lord’s vineyard, and pour pollution over the
whole earth. But what is the first and original cause, fountain, and source of
so great an evil? The cause, fountain, and source is this Court. It is so not
only because it does not dispel these things or cleanse these abominations,
although it alone is especially able and supremely bound to do so, but still

One must also append Francis Petrarch’s testimony concerning his own age.

malice of conduct increases because those alone are held to be wise who so

possible from that despised company of the godly and Puritans. And now it may
