### Chapter III

#### Second argument

Page 180.

If Christ, in a certain peculiar manner, laid down his life for the sheep,
redeemed the church, gave himself for the church, gathered together by his
death the scattered children of God, saved his people, and gave himself to
redeem and purify for himself a peculiar people,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 120 | printed p. 110]

then he did not actually obtain reconciliation with God and remission of sins
for all and each of mankind—unbelievers as well as believers—equally and
indiscriminately. For all and each are not to be regarded as such people, as
is clear from Matthew 25:33; John 10:28; John 1:12; Psalms 33:12; 144:16;
Hosea 2:22. But the former is true: John 10:15; Acts 20:28; Ephesians 5:25;
John 11:51; Matthew 21; Ephesians 5:23; Titus 2:24. This is the force and
meaning of this reasoning reduced to syllogistic form.

Here the Remonstrants complain first that the conclusion is ambiguous, and so
they ask what it is that is proved. Is it that Christ’s death is sufficient
for all—which, they say, is beyond controversy—or that it is efficacious for
many only, so that they are restored to the state of grace? This too is beyond
controversy. Or is it instead the point placed in the conclusion? Then they
cry out that the state of the question has been wretchedly inverted, since
the controversy consists in whether Christ by his death obtained remission
of sins for all people, although for lack of faith they do not partake of it,
or not. How unbecoming to grave men is this playacting! They ask their
adversary what he concludes: this, or that, or perhaps what is placed in the
conclusion? Certainly, if the question is not asked for the sake of
dissimulation, it is stupefaction and senselessness. The matter itself cries
loudly enough and prevents them from crying out about a wretched and shameful
inversion in the conclusion. For there is absolutely nothing there which
they do not themselves cast into their statement of the controversy in
almost the same breath, as comparison will make clear—unless either they say
that Christ obtained what he did not actually obtain, or they discover some
weighty distinction between those who remain in unbelief and impiety and
those who, for lack of faith, do not actually become partakers of Christ.

They bring forward three general reasons against the confirmation of the
minor premise from Scripture, which must be weighed in order. 1. “All these
passages can be understood not of

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 121 | printed p. 111]

the procurement, which is made for all, but of real enjoyment, which falls to
believers alone.” Reply: If by real enjoyment they mean God’s firm intention
of it, then what they affirm in this part is true—namely, that these passages
can be understood of that intention—and it is contrary to the negation in
the other part, where they distinguished procurement from this intention, as
I think I have sufficiently proved before. But if they mean actual enjoyment
and application to those who are believers in actuality, what they assume is
utterly false. For the enjoyment of Christ is our action, whereas these
expressions—laying down his life for the sheep, giving himself for the church,
redeeming the church, and so forth—properly denote Christ’s actions, completed
outside us and without us. These propositions also possessed complete truth
at the very moment when Christ was laying down his life: “he laid down his
life for his sheep”; “he redeemed the church with his own blood”; “he gave
himself for the church”; and so forth. But actual enjoyment did not
immediately follow in all the members of the church. Finally, the difference
between the meaning of these expressions and actual enjoyment is as broad as
that between any appointed means and its external end—or, rather, the use for
which it is appointed. 2. Their second objection is that the particle “alone”
is nowhere found in the cited passages. “Christ,” they say, “gave himself for
Paul (Gal. 2:20); did he therefore give himself for no one else? All believers
will rise; therefore will believers alone? So Christ died for his own sheep,
but also for the ungodly (Rom. 5:6), for those who perish (Rom. 14:14–15; 1
Cor. 8:11), and for those who deny him (2 Pet. 2:1).” Here this point must be
observed above all: among the Remonstrants it is no error, nor foreign to
their own forms of speech, to say that Christ died for the ungodly, for the
lost or those who perish in unbelief, and for apostates. Yet when our men
attribute this to them—that on their position Christ died for the ungodly,
that is, unbelievers who remain and die in that state—they cry injury

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 122 | printed p. 112]

and hurl abuse as though from a cart, as we just saw concerning the conclusion
of this argument and also above when the principal question was being
defined. But as for the exclusive word “alone,” I say that when a difference
and disjunction is contained within a term, the exclusion or restriction is
no less evident than if it were marked by this word. Thus the supreme blessing
is promised to Abraham’s seed. “Alone” is not added, yet the Apostle no less
refers it to the children of Abraham alone (Rom. 4; Gal. 3) than if this word
had been added with emphasis. When they themselves say that believers are
elect, do they not mean believers alone? I can also return to them in this
cause what our theologians customarily reply to the papists when they concede
that we are justified by faith, but say that it is nowhere expressly read in
Scripture that this occurs by faith alone. For just as it is rightly answered
to them that “not by works, but by faith” means precisely the same as “by faith
alone,” so also against these men “not for the goats, but for the sheep,” and
“not for the world, but for those whom you have given me” (John 17:9), is
entirely the same as “for the sheep alone” or “for those alone whom you have
given me.” But if similar speech is made about one particular individual, as
in Galatians 2:20, it is made in a certain secondary sense. Socrates, for
example, is said to be capable of laughter because he is a human being; but a
human being is so primarily, and therefore this property belongs not to
Socrates alone, but nevertheless to the human being alone. So Paul is redeemed
and is elect, but he is elect primarily, and Paul insofar as he is elect;
therefore not Paul alone, and yet the elect alone. This objection, then, does
not fit. But I freely concede the other point if the same kind of resurrection
is meant: all believers will rise, that is, believers alone—namely, to the
resurrection of the just (Luke 14:14), or into the resurrection of life (John
5:29). But they affirm that Scripture expressly says he died for others
besides his own sheep. Let us hear the words: “for the ungodly,” they say,
Romans 5:6—that is, as the Apostle himself inter—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 123 | printed p. 113]

—prets it in verse 8, “for us” (his sheep) “while we were yet sinners.” “For
him who perishes” (Rom. 14:14–15; 1 Cor. 8:11). But these passages do not say
that the person for whom Christ died perishes. Those who are stronger in
understanding are only admonished not to destroy a weaker brother by an
offense. A person can destroy another as far as lies in himself even though
the other does not perish, just as he is said to violate a woman in Matthew
5:28 even though she remains chaste. Nor certainly can destruction here be
understood properly, unless we say that the person who is in some way offended
or affected with sorrow by another’s act perishes. “For those who deny him
and bring swift destruction upon themselves” (2 Pet. 2:1). But it is not said
that Christ died for these. For here no mention is made of death, blood, or
Christ; rather, it is said that the Lord—not *ton kyrion* but *ton despotēn*,
which is commonly attributed to the Father—bought them. That is because in
some manner he had delivered them and claimed them for himself through
external aggregation, and because they themselves had also acknowledged him
as Lord when they professed their name among his purchased servants. 3. They
propose this argument for our consideration: “If Christ died for his own
sheep, for his own church, and so forth, as such, then someone was a sheep of
Christ and belonged to his church prior in order to Christ’s dying for him,
and before Christ obtained him with his own blood. This is absurd and false.”
Reply: 1. The inference is not altogether necessary. For just as Israel
“served for a wife, and guarded a flock for a wife” (Hos. 12:13), and yet that
wife was not given to Israel before he served for her, so Christ could have
died for his bride—that is, his church—even though he did not have her
betrothed before he acquired her for himself by his death. And it is certain
that the church is the object of redemption in such a way that it also exists
as its effect. Similarly, those who are heirs of salvation as such have the
relation both of object and of effect

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 124 | printed p. 114]

to the ministry of angels (Heb. 1:14). And the children of the promise are
its object insofar as it was made to them, but also its effect insofar as by
its power they themselves were made children. 2. Nor is it absurd or false
to affirm that in the divine counsel and intention a person is prior in order
a sheep of Christ and a member of the church, or assembly of those to be
saved, to Christ’s dying for him. For these sheep were given to Christ before
they were in Christ’s hands (John 17:24), for whom he was intercessor (John
17:7). Indeed, all the salvation prepared for them in Christ, and Christ’s
own mission, has its first fountain and origin in that donation by the Father:
“This is the will of him who sent me”—that is, of the Father—“that I should
lose nothing of whatever he has given me, but should raise it up on the last
day.” The Apostle calls this donation predestination (Eph. 1:5), and declares
quite clearly that redemption through Christ depends upon the same donation
(verse 7). Indeed, either it must be admitted that some assembly of those to
be saved was present to the divine mind and purpose when God appointed Christ
to death, or else that death of Christ was of uncertain outcome, this whole
mystery of redemption could have been rendered void through the devil’s
cunning and human stubbornness, and even now it is not established with
respect to its outcome except by the power of human reception as the primary
principle of its application. I will add further: if the church was in no way
present to the divine mind when Christ was anointed and sanctified for his
office, then he was established as a head without a body and a king without
any subjects known in the present even to the omniscient God himself. I do not
need to say how unworthy that is of those treasures of divine wisdom which
were hidden in this mystery. Let the thoughtful reader weigh this one point:
that satisfaction of Christ undertaken for us guilty people could not have
had force unless some antecedent union existed between us and Christ—namely,
one in which he was appointed by God to be the head of the body

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 125 | printed p. 115]

whose members we are. The distinguished Hugo Grotius, having deserted the
Remonstrants whom he defends elsewhere, candidly concedes this in his *Defense
of the Catholic Faith*, page 66.

They make a particular observation on each testimony. These observations
must now be considered, but briefly.

On John 10:15 they deny that the elect are meant. 1. “Because the elect are
nowhere designated by the name ‘sheep.’” This assertion is no less false than
bold. For the Remonstrants themselves concede that eternal life is conferred
or appointed to no others than those elected to life; and in Matthew 25:33
and John 10:28 the heirs of eternal life are expressly designated by the name
“sheep.” 2. “Because Christ speaks of sheep of such a kind as can be recognized
by others, since they hear, know, and follow Christ’s voice. This cannot fit
elect people of such a kind as are known to no one but God.” As though the
Remonstrants did not know it to be the common position of our theologians that
election can be discerned just as justification and true sanctification can,
and indeed that believers are accustomed to be assured of both these things
together. This is remarkable confidence: not only to assume as false what our
theologians consistently hold as true, but also to propose the contrary
position, bare of every proof, as an argument to attack another assertion
which rests upon the same foundations.

On Acts 20:28, “God redeemed his own church,” they observe that the elect
alone are not meant, since the church spoken of there was visible; and though
things belonging to the invisible church are sometimes attributed to the
visible church, this is nevertheless properly done in matters which concern
real communion. But they cannot be ignorant that election itself is
attributed to visible churches collectively (Eph. 1:4; 2 Thess. 1:4; 1 Pet.
1:2), although they know that it must be distinguished from real
communication

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 126 | printed p. 116]

as a decree is distinguished from its execution.

On Ephesians 5:25, “Christ gave himself for his church,” they reply: 1. “The
word ‘alone’ is not found there, so that it might be concluded from it that
Christ gave himself for no others.” This is just as though some adulterer
wished to observe concerning the first part of that verse—“husbands, love
your wives”—that the word “alone” is not found there, so that it might be
concluded from it that husbands ought to love their own wives alone and not
other men’s wives at the same time. 2. “Here it treats only of the giving,
without any reference to the redemption which comes to believers by real
application.” Nothing more inconsiderate could have been said. For 1. the
claim that Christ gave himself without any reference to that redemption
conflicts both with logic, which teaches that no means is employed without
reference to an end, and with Scripture, which teaches that the giving itself
was made for many as the price of redemption (Matt. 20:28), and finally with
the concessions of Arminius himself, who teaches that the end of this giving
is that Christ may obtain and communicate salvation. 2. It also conflicts
with the passage’s perfectly plain meaning. For “to give himself for the
church” is to give himself, as the Apostle explains in verses 26–27, “that he
might sanctify her, having cleansed her by the washing of water through the
word, and might present her to himself glorious,” and so forth. Will anyone
now maintain that such things are said of a giving which has no reference to
redemption and its application? 3. Finally, it conflicts with the
Remonstrants’ own interpretation. In the previous article, page 114, they say
that the immediately following verses 26–27—which do not convey a meaning
different from the preceding verse but only expound and illustrate the same
one—cannot be understood otherwise than of the condition of believers in
heaven. For if they describe the condition of believers, they must also
denote the application of redemption. Therefore either verse 25 must be said
to have no reference to the following

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 127 | printed p. 117]

words, which no Christian will think, or it must be admitted that the giving
mentioned there also has some relation to redemption applied to believers.

On John 11:51, “Jesus died for that people.” The Remonstrants reply: 1. “They
were Jews, among whom were also reprobates; therefore he did not die for the
elect alone.” Here they admit two errors in order to exclude the simple truth.
The first is that they want “the Jews” to mean all and each of them. For the
Apostle contends in express words and also with firm arguments that, in
spiritual matters and in promises made to the Israelites which pertain to the
new covenant, all and each of the Israelites are not included (Rom. 6, 7, 8,
and 11:1, 2, 7). The second is that they connect an inference completely
contrary both to their own principles and to the Scriptures themselves: if
Jews, then reprobates. Do they think the angel also had this meaning in
Matthew 1:21: “He will save his people,” that is, he will save reprobate Jews
no less than the elect? Do they not themselves reject as absurd this
inference on page 171 of the *Collation*: “Christ died for all; therefore for
unbelievers or reprobates”? Reply 2. “To die so that he might gather is to
receive the power to gather.” Since this is outside the proposition—for
nothing was said in the argument about verse 52, where the gathering is
mentioned—it could rightly be passed over by me. Nevertheless, that sentence
must also be freed from this sophistical corruption, which appears to be
borrowed from the inventions of Socinus. He attributes no higher efficacy to
Christ’s death than that by this means Christ should receive the power to
gather and preserve believers. I would certainly dare affirm that among all
orthodox interpreters this interpretation is not found: that “to die so that
he might gather” contains nothing other than receiving the power to gather.
But everywhere in Socinus this very corruption occurs. See the Racovian
Catechism, page 294; Hugo Grotius’s *Defense*, page 32. It can, however, be
refuted very eas—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 128 | printed p. 118]

—ily from the context itself. For in these words, “Jesus was to die not only
for that people, but also that he might gather into one the scattered
children of God,” a comparison of unequal things is expressly indicated by
the proper marks “not only” and “but also.” In it the Jewish people and all
the scattered children are compared with each other as lesser and greater in
relation to Christ’s death—or, rather, dying for the Jewish people and dying
for all the children of God are so compared. Therefore, if Christ’s death
with respect to the children of God has no other character than that of
acquiring the power to call them, then nothing more can be contained in the
other part, which holds the place of the lesser: “to die for the people.” If
something more is denoted in that expression, no less is present in the part
which here holds the place of the greater. But the distinguished Hugo Grotius
has accurately demonstrated against Socinus in chapter 9 that something else
is denoted there beyond and above the acquisition of that power when Christ
is said “to die for the people”; and no one will deny it unless he is plainly
a Socinian. In this matter the word “gathering” denotes nothing other than
reconciling as one body, as the Apostle Paul explains in Ephesians 1:9–10;
2:16; and Colossians 1:20. They can also see a suitable explanation of those
passages in that same writing of Hugo Grotius, chapter 7. The Remonstrants
reply 3. “The children of God are here said to be either the children of the
covenant (Acts 3:25), among whom were also those who were called so that they
might believe and thus become children of God.” They bring this forward too
outside the limits of the argument. Yet I say briefly that those children of
the covenant whom Peter mentions in Acts 3:25 cannot be designated here, for
they have a place in the other part of this comparison: “he was to die for
that people.” Nor can all who are to be called in any manner sustain this
title, because then, on the Remonstrants’ position, all mortals would be on
this roll. But if they mean only those who are to be effectually called, that
is the very thing we affirm, and it makes most strongly against

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 129 | printed p. 119]

the Remonstrants. For in these words the widest bounds of that redemption
which Christ’s death would bring are indicated, as the force and use of the
comparison demonstrates; and yet those bounds extend no further than the
effectually called, or believers. Who, then, would dare extend the end of this
redemption to all and each of mortals?

On Matthew 1:21, “He will save his people from their sins,” they observe: 1.
from Calvin, that by Christ’s people the Jews are named. But from the same
Calvin they ought also to observe that this promise of salvation is extended
to all who by faith coalesce into one body of the church; and from the Apostle
in Romans 11:2, that the people of God properly are that people whom he
foreknew. 2. They observe that this passage and similar ones do not treat of
the procurement of salvation, but of salvation itself, or the act of saving.
Therefore, I say, not bare procurement but the application of salvation and
salvation itself was the aim and end which God had set before himself both in
the sending and in the giving of Christ. For the angel expressly designates
God’s intention in sending Christ and the end of the mission itself by the
imposition of the name—“you shall call his name Jesus”—and by its notation or
reason—“for he will save his people from their sins.” Arminius himself teaches
this not obscurely in *Private Disputation* 35, thesis 2.

On Titus 2:14, “He gave himself for us that he might redeem us from all
iniquity and purify for himself a peculiar people, zealous for good works.”
The Remonstrants reply: “These things cannot be referred to believers alone,”
although the Apostle expressly refers them to a peculiar people zealous for
good works—that is, faithful—and by this description of the people declares
plainly enough whom he meant by those earlier words, “He gave himself for
us.” But they say verse 11 intimates the contrary: “It appeared to all
people,” and 1 Timothy 2:6, “He gave himself for all.” Nevertheless, nothing

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 130 | printed p. 120]

contrary is taught in those passages. For in Titus 2:11 the Apostle admonishes
servants to perform their duty toward their masters because grace has shone
upon all—that is, upon every sort of person, masters as well as servants. So
also in 1 Timothy 2:6 he wills prayers to be made for kings and so forth,
because God wills to save some from every kind of people, and to this end he
also gave Christ for them. Nothing is taught there which does not agree
perfectly with the argument drawn from Titus 2:14.
