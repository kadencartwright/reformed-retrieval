Second, how much more is salt spoiled if it is wholly covered with the filth of vices and thoroughly polluted by every kind of foulness? What if those whose office is to season others reek of drunkenness, dissolve in luxury, and serve Venus? I no longer call them the salt of the earth, but what are commonly called the earth's entrails: sheer poison and pus—if I may employ a harsher misuse of language in designating those who most wickedly misuse sacred things—pus, I say, pure, unadulterated, and utterly putrid. Since these ulcers must be rubbed with sharper salt, “Here I will not spare you, so that God may spare you,” as Bernard said to Eugenius. Yet your most chaste ears must be spared; I see your nostrils stopped and your eyes suffused with shame at these public disgraces even to humanity itself, the truly foul dishonors of the holier ministry: men who perform at once Bacchus's filthiest orgies and Christ's most holy mysteries, and empty the stable of Augeas into the innermost shrine, into God's sanctuary. But if we read that the dunghill's

[1679 new-series fol. 43 | IA leaf 363]

gate was very far removed from the temple at Jerusalem, in what place shall we finally judge that this tasteless salt, which is not even fit for the dunghill, should be deposited? Luke 14:35. Let them be ashamed—let these men, I say, be ashamed of their vileness, which it is painful even to recount; let them earnestly repent of violated religion and polluted priesthood. Let them hear Ecebolius the apostate, prostrate at the temple's threshold, continually repeating, “Trample upon me, the tasteless salt.” Let them consider Origen, guilty of lesser offenses, when he was about to preach and had read aloud from the Psalm, “But to the wicked God said,” and the words that follow: he closed the book again, sat down, and wept. Let them too weep over their own crimes, and at last either cease acting impiously or cease handling sacred things. Yet it is fitting to wrap in silence these disgraces of the clergy, which the most holy emperor Constantine wished to cover with his own cloak. I would wish to be salt so that I might give savor, not bite.

2. The one point remains which was the second: those who are the salt of the earth should not only have savor themselves but also season others by virtue of their office, “not merely free from vice, but even communicative of goodness to others,” as Theophylact most excellently puts it. To be both rich and to enrich others is truly apostolic, 1 Timothy 6:18–19; to be not more good than beneficent is most like God himself, Psalm 119:68. Christ is both the Father's Wisdom and Word, by which he teaches others. The holy angels were both burning coals and lamps in Ezekiel 1:13. John the Baptist was a burning and shining lamp, so that he did not burn more within himself than he offered light to others. The same belongs equally to all ministers of the gospel. For they are fathers, who do not merely live to God themselves but also beget children for him in Christ's image. They are pastors, who not only belong to Christ's sheepfold but also feed the Lord's flock. They are not merely living stones that compose God's house, but builders who hew rough stones and build the hewn stones upon Christ the foundation. They are basins who receive the water of life within themselves and, further, channels who distribute it to others, so that what is within the cistern flows out into the street, and the oil in the vessel feeds the flame in the lamp.

Saint Chrysostom also noted that the virtues which the Savior wished especially to commend to his disciples in the earlier summary of this chapter serve not anyone's private advantage but the public good. He calls them the light of the world, which indeed shines in itself, yet does so in order to furnish a torch to others. He names them the salt of the earth, so that they should no more have savor themselves than imbue others with purity of doctrine, integrity of life, and a relish for divine things. For in expounding this metaphor of salt we must not scrape together everything that occurs here and there in philosophers' disputations, physicians' so-called counsels, poets' fictions, or finally commentators' allusions, which are as inept as they are innumerable. It is certain that in this passage the Savior was dealing not with the subtlety of philosophers but with the simplicity of fishermen. By this analogy drawn from an ordinary thing, he intended not what the wanton wits of clever triflers might devise, but what anyone from the common people could infer as soon as he heard the speaker.

It contains five things in particular by which salt not so much sketches as expresses the character of the gospel ministry, insofar as it bites, heals, purges, seasons, and preserves from decay.

These were the chief points upon which, when I first undertook this assigned task, I had hoped to dwell longer. But since I sense that, scarcely out of the starting gates,

[1679 new-series fol. 44 | IA leaf 364]

I have already advanced beyond the finish line, I gladly pass over them all. For I wish to measure out not a long-distance race but a stadium course.

Only this remains, so that I may finally make an end once and for all: I wish that what Christ said to the apostles might be said by me to our universities, “You are the salt of the earth.” They were salt for the whole earth, as we have said; these universities are salt for our sweetest homeland of England—the “land of the Angles,” as foreigners commonly call it. We read that at Rome a certain road was formerly called the Salt Road, and that the ancients called the Caspian Gates “rivers of salt.” But the Roman salt has long since become foolish, and that Caspian Sea—from which the Turks issued through those gates—has become utterly tasteless. In these most blessed islands of ours, since God has blessed us with a purer doctrine of the gospel, one may find a truly salt-bearing road and, in our far-famed universities, rivers of genuine salt. From these we joyfully and gratefully acknowledge that not only has this land of ours been well seasoned, but this salt has also been carried to the furthest shores of the earth.

[Marginalia: Pliny, book 31, chapter 7.]

Meanwhile, just as everyone ought earnestly to grieve that through this recent corruption, the foulest of all, the salt has become far too savorless, so we academics in particular must diligently beware that what Pliny says of every place in which salt is found—that it is barren and produces nothing—does not happen to us. Far be it that our Pious Mother should ever grow barren; may her indefatigable fruitfulness never miscarry, which our most august and most gracious James once prayed might be so. Let us be salt; let us ourselves have savor; and let us season others for eternity. Let this covenant of salt be perpetual. May the merciful Father bring this to pass through Christ Jesus, our only Savior. Amen.

[Marginalia: cited above; Numbers 18:19.]

To him, moreover, we render the greatest thanks we can and the eternal thanks we owe, because at first he formed us upright by his mighty hand and, when we fell, raised us by his tenderest hand; because he washed away the guilt of all our sins by his Son's blood and their filth by the most abundant grace of his Spirit; and because he has granted us not only daily bread, by which we may pleasantly sustain this earthly life, but also that most wholesome salt of the earth, by which we may live blessed forever. We humbly pray that he would more deeply and thoroughly imbue us all, season us with it, and keep us by God's power through faith unto salvation, through Jesus Christ our Lord and Savior, to whom, together with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be praise, honor, and thanksgiving through eternal ages. Amen.

Blessed are those who hear the Word of God and keep it.

The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit be with you all, both now and forever. Amen.
