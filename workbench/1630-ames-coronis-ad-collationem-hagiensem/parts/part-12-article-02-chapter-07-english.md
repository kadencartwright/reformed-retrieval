### Chapter VII

#### Sixth argument

Page 193.

Those for whom Christ properly died he also loved supremely (John 15:13; 1
John 2:16). But he does not love all and each supremely, because for whomever
he loves supremely he not only merits salvation but actually applies it to
them. Therefore he did not properly die for all and each.

In this place the Remonstrants wrap their answer in so many various
circumlocutions that it can scarcely be understood

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 146 | printed p. 136]

what that point is in which they wish to rest above the others.

If supreme love consists not only in meriting salvation but also in applying
it, as the confirmation of the minor premise professes, then we deny the major
premise.

But 1. love properly consists neither in the work of meriting nor in that of
applying. For love is an immediate act of the will by which someone wills for
another those things which are good, or at least seem to be. The good things
themselves are effects which arise from love; love does not properly consist
in them. The work of redemption commends, but does not constitute, the love
of God (Rom. 5:8). We proved above that Christ possessed this intention to
apply salvation to all those for whom it was procured; and the Apostle seems
expressly to confirm the one from the other in Romans 5:8–9.

They say that the love of God and Christ toward us is twofold: one which
precedes our faith and love (John 3:16; 1 John 4:9; 1 John 4:16; Eph. 5:2),
and another which follows our faith and love toward God (John 14:22–23; Acts
10:34; Heb. 11:6). By the former Christ loves all and each; by the latter he
does not supremely love all those for whom he died. But they at once
extinguish this distinction themselves, for they confess that this second
love is nothing other than the continuation of the former and its enlargement
through new benefits. If this is true, then there is no further difference
between the preceding and subsequent love than that which consists in
external benefits or effects. But these cannot vary the nature and species of
love itself, nor indeed, properly speaking, even its degree. The Father loved
Christ hanging on the cross equally as much as Christ ascending into heaven.
God loved Abraham, to whom he gave no inheritance in the land of Canaan, not
even a footstep of ground (Acts 7:5), no less than Abraham’s descendants, whom
he led into that region in his own time.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 147 | printed p. 137]

I confess that the manifestation and perception of love, and also complacency
or approval of a state pleasing to God, follow faith; this is the meaning of
the Holy Spirit in John 14:22, Acts 10:34, and Hebrews 11:6. But neither faith
nor reason admits that a new love is added, or that love itself is properly
increased with respect to its internal intention. Moreover, that procurement
which is separated from the intention and purpose of application can scarcely
even sustain the name of love. For no one loves unless he wills a good to be
applied to another. For to love, says Aristotle in *Rhetoric*, book 2, is “to
wish good things for someone and, so far as one has the power, to be active in
bringing them about” (βούλεσθαί τινι ἀγαθὰ, καὶ τὸ κατὰ δύναμιν πρακτικὸν
εἶναι τούτων).

Concerning John 15:13 they bring forward something convoluted: namely, that
Christ does not say that this is his greatest love—that he should lay down his
life for his friends—but only advances some common maxim which he nevertheless
does not apply to himself. This maxim indeed shows that the greatest love is
that which is found among men, and tacitly intimates that the love which he
exhibits to men is exceedingly and immeasurably great.

But it is astonishing to say that this maxim is not applied, since it is
plain that a comparison is made there between the love of men and that of
Christ, in precisely the same sense in which the same comparison is instituted
in Romans 5:7–8; and no comparison is made where there is no application of
that which supports the inference to that which it proves. But if the
application is sound, then this proportion is observed: just as among all the
effects of love there is none higher among men than to lay down one’s life for
another, so no more efficacious proof of Christ’s love can be conceived than
that he lays down his life for them. This also surpasses human love in this
respect: he did this for enemies, something men will scarcely perform for
their best friends. It must also be observed—as they confess—that Christ’s
purpose in this passage is to commend the love which he exhibits to men

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 148 | printed p. 138]

as immeasurably great. For if he intended this, he undoubtedly brought forward
the greatest proof of this love. Nor was that proof common to all and each of
mortal men, for he addressed his own, but was peculiar to those whom he
embraced with the highest love.

They say that the force of the comparison is placed in these words, “lays
down his life.” For since no one can furnish a more precious pledge for
another than life, laying down one’s life is rightly said to be the highest
love. This agrees with Christ’s aim, as it does also with other passages
(Eph. 1:2; 1 John 3:16). Nevertheless, the Apostle does not speak of the
highest love of God, nor of any enlargement of it.

Here they could easily have perceived that they contradict themselves. For
what is more contradictory than to confess that the highest pledge of love is
furnished, so that one is rightly said to have shown the highest love, and
that this is signified in express words, yet at the same time to affirm that
nothing is said about the highest love of God or any enlargement of it? There
is no need for other words; the latter part of this assertion is sufficiently
refuted by the former.

They add: If we wish to take it for the highest love, it must be acknowledged
that the word “us” does not look to the present condition of those who speak
there (John 3:16), but to what they were before, namely enemies. Here let the
reader observe how well the Remonstrants’ discourse agrees with itself. In the
preceding article, in answer to nearly every passage of Scripture which proved
that faith depends upon election and not election upon faith, they always
replied that “we” occurs in the context, or in the chapter, or in some part of
the same writing. They thought this sufficient to prove that election regards
believers as such. But now, on the contrary, when they see that a similar
reasoning opposes them, they say that it must be acknowledged that “we” has
regard to the past condition. Let them therefore acknowledge at the same time
that the argument which they sought from that word in the preceding question
was not serious,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 149 | printed p. 139]

and they will not find us more difficult in this request. What gain will they
make from it? This, namely: that it was the greatest love which he could
exhibit to enemies, but that a greater love has been exhibited to believers.
But 1. this hostile condition—“if, when we were enemies”—is mentioned by the
Apostle in Romans 5:10 not to distinguish this love from some greater love, as
they do, but to amplify it as much as possible and to illustrate it by a
comparison with our unworthiness. 2. If this love were not the highest, and
also the same as that which is exercised toward believers, it would not so
often be proposed to believers by the Holy Spirit as though it were the sole
nourishment and support of faith. 3. In Romans 5:8–10, where the Apostle
institutes a comparison between the work of reconciliation and that of
completed salvation, he does not set two loves against each other, but two
effects of the same love. He manifestly teaches us that both redemption and
salvation come from the same love. 4. In that comparison he teaches that the
greater benefit is that Christ died for us, his enemies, while the lesser is
that he preserves those who are now reconciled and friends: “He who did the
greater thing,” says the Apostle, “will much more do the lesser.” But Christ
did the greater thing in reconciling us to God. Much more, therefore, will he
do the lesser thing by preserving those now reconciled. “Much more,” then, is
pressed twice, in verses 9 and 10, lest it escape anyone that this latter
thing is lesser and therefore to be done more readily. 5. We have the highest
love to be observed in Christ’s death marked out by the Holy Spirit with such
signal marks that it ought not to be denied by any Christian: “God commends
his love toward us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us”
(Rom. 5:8); “In this is love” (1 John 4:10); “God so loved the world” (John
3:16); “If God so loved us”

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 150 | printed p. 140]

(1 John 4:11); “And not only so, but we also glory in God through our Lord
Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation” (Rom. 5:11);
“I bend my knees that you may be able to comprehend with all the saints what
is that breadth and length and depth and height, and to know that love of
Christ which surpasses all knowledge” (Eph. 3:18, 16). Finally, reason itself
confirms this truth. For no greater affection of love can be exhibited in any
way unless some effect of it can be brought forth which would either be more
excellent in itself or be less owed to us. But Christ could give nothing more
excellent than himself and his life; and nothing can be less owed to us than
that he should become sin on account of our sins so that we might be made
righteous on account of his righteousness.

They observe that in the assumption of the argument it is posited that Christ
not only merits salvation for those to whom he exhibits his highest love, but
also applies it. From this 1. they gather that the highest exhibition of that
love does not consist in his laying down his life for someone. I would not
deny that this follows if they could show us a procurement such as they
imagine for themselves, namely one severed from application and from the
intention or certain appointment of application. But since it is certain that
this redemption is applied to all and each for whom it was procured, and that
application depends upon procurement as an effect upon its cause, in arguing
thus they act just as if they said that the highest love does not shine forth
in election to life, because those whom God supremely loves he not only elects
to life but also brings into its possession. 2. They concede that in
application Christ exhibits the highest love to believers alone, but they say
it does not follow from this that he did not also exhibit the highest love to
all while they were still enemies, so far as he could according to God’s will
and justice. But that this does follow was sufficiently proven

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 151 | printed p. 141]

above. Yet this can be added here by way of increase: whatever it is that
takes place in application after the required condition has been performed by
man is, according to the Remonstrants’ position, conferred under the character
of a prize and a wage, not of an end. And therefore it ought to be referred to
justice rather than to grace and love. Love properly consists in wishing and
preparing good, not in conferring a reward on the basis of a prescribed
condition.

Nor indeed was God’s will any obstacle to Christ’s loving those enemies whom
he undertook to redeem just as much as any friends. For this was the Father’s
will: that he should seek the lost sheep with no less affection than he
protects them once gained (Luke 15:4). Justice, moreover, could not stand in
the way, because this love looked chiefly to this very thing: that injured
justice might be fully satisfied.
