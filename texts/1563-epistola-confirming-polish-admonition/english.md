# Letter Confirming His Recently Published Admonition among the Poles

John Calvin — Geneva, 30 April 1563

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
`latin.txt` and checked sentence by sentence against the 1870 *Calvini Opera*
facsimile. The address, complete letter, and closing are included. Material
supplied by the nineteenth-century editors is documented in the README but is
not silently translated as Calvin's. See the translator's notes after the
text.

**[CO IX, p. 645]**

## Address

To the noble and honorable lords, the magnates of Lesser Poland, and the most
distinguished gentlemen, citizens of the renowned city of Krakow, who
steadfastly follow the orthodox faith, John Calvin sends greetings.[^1]

Noble, honorable, and most distinguished gentlemen and lords: when, four
months ago, sad news was brought of the inconstancy of many among you who had
deserted the right and sincere faith for an impious and detestable fabrication—and
when they had even published a *Table* by which they attempted to abolish
Christ's true deity—I immediately composed a short admonition. Its purpose was
that sober, teachable, and modest people, instructed to guard against snares,
might be retained in the sound doctrine of godliness.[^2]

Afterward I learned from our excellent brother, Lord Christopher Trecy, that
prudent men of sound judgment had detected the deceits of those who had tried
to undermine the first principle of faith concerning three persons in God.
The faithful servants of Christ, Lords Stanislaus Sarnicius and Jacob Sylvius,
wrote the same thing to me. I was especially glad that by your diligence and
courage the church among you was being gathered again, for its wretched
dispersion had been a cause for fear. Meanwhile, however, I learned that,
because you have to deal with proud and unyielding men, you are being tried by
hard and continual conflicts. It would therefore not be unhelpful to you if
some assistance were offered by pious brothers to strengthen you. Since I
desire to be counted among their number, I did not wish to fail in my part.

Now this most hideous error, which is running rampant among you, won favor
through the immoderate heat of controversy. For when Stancaro, a foolish
sophist and utterly unscrupulous brawler, thrust his inventions upon you—namely,
that Christ is mediator only insofar as he is man and therefore intercedes
before the whole Trinity—some thought it the best shortcut simply to answer
that the Father alone is truly and properly God. Many seized upon that escape
far too eagerly, because they thought Stancaro's absurdities could thus be
refuted without any difficulty. As the old proverb has it,

**[CO IX, p. 646]**

truth was lost through too much wrangling. I do not doubt that some slipped
through ignorance or thoughtless pliability. At the same time, however, one
may infer that others craftily seized an opportunity to foist an execrable
delusion upon simple people with impunity, hoping that it would appear
plausible. For among you are filthy scoundrels—George Blandrata, Valentine
Gentile, John Paul Alciati, and others like them—whom a furious lust for
innovation and disturbance drives headlong. Leaving them aside, however, it
will be useful to touch upon the main point of the question itself.

They want the name *God*, whenever it occurs in Scripture, to be restricted to
the Father alone. But after the Holy Spirit pronounced that God is one, he
plainly teaches that Christ was that God. Nor could John's testimony stand in
any other way—“This is the true God and eternal life”—unless Christ were the
same God as the Father. Again, when Paul, in Luke's account, uses the name of
God without personal qualification, it can only be understood of the Son,
since he expressly declares that the church was redeemed by God's blood. It
ought to be more than enough for us that the apostles apply to the person of
Christ what the prophets teach concerning the one and eternal God. My
*Admonition* will supply you with clear proofs of this point.[^3]

As for their noisy objection that God is everywhere called the Father of
Christ, the solution is at hand. Whenever the two persons are compared with
one another, the name of God is assigned especially to the Father, because he
is the principle of deity; but where no comparison is being made, the Son is
included under the name of God.[^4] Indeed, if a relation is admitted within
the very essence of God, that essence will no longer be one and simple but
will be torn apart. I will explain it a little more clearly. If the Son is
compared with the Father with respect to essence, a manifold deity will emerge
from it, and nothing could be more absurd. Yet that thrice

**[CO IX, p. 647]**

faithless apostate Gentile is not ashamed to spew out this blasphemy: that
Christ was “essentiated” by the Father. In this you recognize a monstrous term
devised by a diabolical invention. In short, just as the Manichaeans once
invented the story that the human soul derives its origin by propagation from
God, so this trickster imagines the Son of God to be some unknown thing
detached from true deity.[^5] But if Christ was “essentiated,” he is not that
Jehovah who once appeared to the prophets, although the apostles testify that
he is. They certainly cannot escape tearing apart God's essence unless they
concede that Christ is essentially the one God.

Another part of their madness is also exposed: unless the Son is the same God
as the Father, several gods must necessarily be introduced. They gain nothing
by evasion, because their inventions plainly imply that Christ is a God other
than the Father. Their objection that he is called *another*, just as the
Spirit is called another Comforter by Christ himself, is dispelled without
difficulty. For nowhere does Scripture, when distinguishing the
persons, cut God himself into parts or pieces, as though his essence were in
conflict with itself. The Son, therefore, is another than the Father, but not
another God. The distinction can consequently be applied only to the persons.
In this way we do not have a compounded God, as they spitefully and foolishly
babble. For although there is one God for us, when the Son comes into view the
Father nevertheless holds his own rank, so that the distinction may stand
out.[^6]

The familiar manner of Scripture's speech must also be maintained when
Christ, insofar as he is mediator, is ranked below the Father. I am speaking
of the whole complex—that is, God manifested in the flesh—because the Father
is now the head until Christ restores to him the dominion he received, so that
God may be all in all.[^7] Stancaro has no reason to jeer here, as if Christ
were made less than the Father. The question here does not concern the nature
of the deity; it indicates only the economy or dispensation of God's wondrous
counsel, which detracts nothing from Christ's eternal glory.

From this the pure integrity of the faith is elicited: in the one God there
are three persons, so that the Son is not the Father but differs from the
Father, while the essence of both is one. It follows that, although *one God*
is not said comparatively, it is nevertheless said without personal
qualification.[^8] Whatever venomous tongues may babble, it will always be
safe to rest in this faith. It is likewise useful to cut off the occasion for
many thorny questions and at the same time to refrain from verbal formulas
that are either too harsh or too remote from Scriptural usage. The common
prayer, “Holy Trinity, one God, have mercy upon us,” does not please me and is
altogether barbarous in expression.[^9] I therefore do not want you to quarrel
over superfluous matters, provided that what I have said about three persons
in one essence remains unimpaired.

**[CO IX, p. 648]**

What Stancaro babbles does not concern me. Nor is it right for the pure and
genuine doctrine of godliness, to which his inventions have no affinity, to be
obscured by them. We therefore reject as not merely tasteless but also profane
what he says: “One God and one mediator” means the Trinity; likewise, “That
they may know you, the one God” means the Trinity; “You believe in God” means
the Trinity; and “Whatever you ask of the Father” means the Trinity.[^10] Away,
then, with those perverse speculations, which could bring sound doctrine into
odium among the unlearned and which are craftily advanced for this purpose by
wicked scoundrels.

For whenever the Son is mentioned, the name of God applies distinctly to the
Father. Yet it is intolerable for those two fabricators, Blandrata and Gentile,
shamefully to corrupt passages of Scripture in which Christ is called God
without qualification. They wish the following to be said of Christ alone:
“The Word was God”; likewise, “The church was acquired by God's blood”; “Who
is God blessed forever”; and “This is the true God and eternal life.” I indeed
acknowledge that these things are predicated of Christ alone, provided that
they do not invent a second God—which it is clear they do. For in what sense
do they restrict these praises to Christ alone, except that Christ is called
God in a different way from the Father? I maintain, however, that he is called
God because he is one God with the Father and the essence of both is one. For
if Christ, in calling the Father the only true God, does not exclude himself,
he certainly does not touch upon his own essence but regards only the
dispensation and his person as mediator.[^11]

They distort the testimonies of the Fathers no less ignorantly. Yet I am not
surprised that they so boldly seize from here and there whatever they think
helps their cause. They are unlearned men, unpracticed in the writings of the
ancients. As they are carried by a turbulent impulse to capture support, they
indiscriminately seize upon whatever seems to depart even a little from
accepted usage. A clear example is their proud boasting of Tertullian's words
as though those words opposed us. But what do they contain? “If the Begotten
were Unbegotten, when compared with him who was Unbegotten, by equality in
both he would make two Unbegottens—if one who was not begotten were set beside
him who is not begotten.”[^12]

Yet the pious man teaches nothing other than what I repeatedly insist: the Son
is distinguished from the Father only when the persons enter into comparison.
For what do the participles *Compared* and *Set Beside* signify, unless that a
distinction arises from the relation of one person to the other? But applying
relation to the essence is condemned by the unanimous consent of all the
ancients. What these counterfeiters twist to establish

**[CO IX, p. 649]**

their error is more than frivolous: “The Father and the Son is not one,”
according to Hilary—namely, not one person. For that pious doctor does not
cease, on almost every page, to assert the one and simple essence of the Father
and the Son, so that the Son is the same God as the Father and nevertheless
differs with respect to person. And how modestly they thrust Basil,
Athanasius, and others like them upon us, when their testimonies transfix this
detestable impiety a thousand times over! Moreover, because I think I have
explained this part adequately in my refutations of Servetus and Gentile, I
will not cause you annoyance and weariness by repeating what may be taken from
those works.

**[CO IX, p. 650]**

It remains now, noble, honorable, and most distinguished gentlemen, for you to
continue with unwearied zeal in the defense of this cause. It is a matter of
no little importance to maintain that Christ is the one and only God, from
whom life and salvation must be sought and in whom it is right to glory. These
sacrilegious wanderers are trying to rob you of that truth. You must therefore
fight no differently than if they were leading you away to a fictitious god.
I would exhort you at greater length if I were not confident that you are
already sufficiently resolute of your own accord.

Farewell, then, noble and honorable lords and most distinguished gentlemen.
May the Lord always govern you by the spirit of wisdom and sustain you with
invincible strength to the very end.

Geneva, the day before the Calends of May, 1563—that is, 30 April 1563.

## Translator's notes

[^1]: The final `S.` in the Latin address abbreviates a conventional form such
    as *salutem dicit*, “sends greetings.” *Proceres Minoris Poloniae* are the
    leading nobles or magnates of Lesser Poland; the second group comprises
    prominent citizens of Krakow.

[^2]: Calvin means his separately printed *Brevis admonitio ad fratres
    Polonos* of 1563. The *Table* was an antitrinitarian diagram or doctrinal
    table circulating in Poland, associated especially with Gregory Paul of
    Brzeziny and the circle Calvin attacks here.

[^3]: The statements are 1 John 5:20 and Acts 20:28. CO supplies those
    parenthetical references, but the 1563 first edition does not print them;
    they are therefore identified here rather than inserted into Calvin's
    Latin.

[^4]: *Deitatis principium* means the Father as the personal source or
    principle within the Trinitarian order, not a temporal beginning of the
    divine essence. Calvin immediately insists that the essence remains one,
    simple, and undivided.

[^5]: *Essentiatus* is the opponents' deliberately unusual term, represented
    literally as “essentiated.” Calvin treats it as implying that the Son's
    deity is a derived product rather than the one divine essence. *Ex Dei
    traduce* uses the vocabulary of propagation from a stock or substance.

[^6]: *Conflatum* can denote something cast, forged, or compounded. Calvin is
    rebutting the charge that the orthodox account manufactures one God by
    fusing the persons.

[^7]: *De toto complexo* identifies Christ in the complete incarnate and
    mediatorial reality—“God manifested in the flesh”—rather than isolating
    either nature. “God may be all in all” echoes 1 Corinthians 15:28.

[^8]: *Quamvis non comparative, tamen indefinite* is compressed logical
    terminology. The contrast is between language used in an explicit
    comparison of Father and Son and the unqualified use of *God* when no such
    personal comparison is in view.

[^9]: Calvin's next sentence reaffirms the three persons in one essence. His
    objection is therefore to the prayer's verbal or grammatical formula,
    which he calls *barbariem*, not to invocation of the Triune God as such.

[^10]: The four phrases evoke 1 Timothy 2:5, John 17:3, John 14:1, and John
    16:23. Calvin reports and rejects Stancaro's interpretive glosses; he is not
    offering the gloss “that is, the Trinity” as his own exegesis.

[^11]: The four testimonies are John 1:1, Acts 20:28, Romans 9:5, and 1 John
    5:20. Again, the references in parentheses are additions in CO, not text
    printed in the 1563 Latin edition. The “only true God” recalls John 17:3.

[^12]: The syntax of the excerpt from Tertullian is as compressed and
    repetitive in the Latin as it is in this close translation. Calvin's next
    sentence supplies his point: the participles express comparison between
    persons, not a division or relation within the divine essence itself.
