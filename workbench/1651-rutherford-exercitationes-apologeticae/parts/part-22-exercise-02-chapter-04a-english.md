CHAPTER IV

*On God's Antecedent and Consequent Will.*

1. What Chrysostom taught concerning God's will under these notions, and what
Damascenus also taught, is clear from their writings. Nor do I deny that the
Arminians follow in their footsteps. But Scotus says that God antecedently
wills what he wills not in itself and immediately, but in its antecedent
cause, from which, says Durandus, the effect follows, although not necessarily.
Thus God antecedently wills everyone to be saved insofar as he gave everyone
a nature capable of salvation and did not deny sufficient means. God
consequently wills what he wills not in its cause but in itself, as that
believers be saved.

[Marginalia: Chrysostom, homily 1 on Ephesians; Damascenus, book 1, *On the
Orthodox Faith*, chapter 29; Scotus, book 1, distinction 48, sole question;
Durandus, book 1, distinction 45, question 1, number 11.]

Estius, Bañez, and Cumel maintain that no such will is in God. And I believe
Scripture nowhere attributes a will to God in this sense. For in that case God
would have to be said to will both everyone's sins and their eternal
destruction, since he gave everyone a nature capable of obedience and
disobedience, salvation and destruction. Indeed, in that way God would have to
be said to will the obedience and salvation of devils and the disobedience and
destruction of good angels, for the angelic nature is capable of salvation and
destruction. 2. By willing a cause, God does not thereby will all the effects

[Marginalia: Estius, book 1, distinction 46; Bañez, volume 1, question 19,
article 6.]

[1651 logical printed p. 324 | IA leaf 342 | PDF p. 342]

which that cause can produce. For he wills the cause to exist for the
production of these and other effects. He expressly says that he willed
Pharaoh to exist so that the glory of his power and wrath might be declared
in him. But if by any act of will he willed Pharaoh to be saved, one asks: to
what end? So that the glory of mercy might be made manifest. Yet he intends
the opposite end, and he cannot confusedly intend every end. Our Beza
maintains that the antecedent will is εὐδοκίαν, good pleasure, and complacency
in a thing pleasing in itself. Thus it is God's approving will as
distinguished from his decreeing will, which can be granted in a sound sense.
Amandus Polanus and Hieronymus Zanchius rightly teach that it is improperly
called a will. Didacus Alvarez, in a sense not to be rejected, says that by
the antecedent will God wills something good absolutely, while by the
consequent will and in the individual case, according to all its attendant
circumstances, God does not will that same thing. Thus he antecedently wills
the salvation of the reprobate, for it is a thing lovable in itself and
absolutely; but consequently he does not will it, because according to all the
circumstances and the order of the universe it is not good. For the salvation
of the reprobate would deprive the world of the glorious providence which
manifests divine justice.

2. The more recent Jesuits, especially Gabriel Vasquez and Francisco Suarez,
and the Arminians teach that the antecedent will is a natural affection in God
by which, through an impulse of nature, he wills the good and salvation of
intelligent creatures insofar as they are creatures and have as yet done
nothing meritorious or demeritorious. But insofar as they are obedient

[Marginalia: Vasquez, volume 1, disputation 83, question 1, chapters 3–4;
Suarez, *On God's Freedom*, disputation 1, section 2, figure 11.]

[1651 logical printed p. 325 | IA leaf 343 | PDF p. 343]

or disobedient, he consequently wills some to be damned. Hence from antecedent
love he calls everyone and affects everyone with common grace; he gave Christ
to death for everyone and is prepared to offer the word of the gospel to
everyone unless they place an obstacle in their own way. Consequently,
according as some have merited by their sins, he denies them a more particular
calling, hardens them, and does not grant them the gospel, according to his
wisdom and justice. Thus Arminius, Corvinus, and Grevinchovius. But some prefer
to call the antecedent will conditional and to explain it by a verb in the
optative mood. This does not at all please Vasquez's palate, for he maintains
that it is a will of simple affection and absolute complacency. It is called
conditional, however, not because it is directed conditionally toward its
object, but because from the will which is now present another will would
arise if the condition were posited in the object. Our John Cameron favors an
antecedent love and a conditional will by which God the Father delivered
Christ to death for everyone, calls everyone, and wills everyone's salvation.
How much this differs from that antecedent will is not easy to gather from his
writings.

[Marginalia: John Cameron, *Miscellanies*, volume 3, p. 575.]

3. Yet this antecedent will is unworthy of the divine majesty on many grounds.
1. If there is in God a will to save each and every person which is never
fulfilled, there will be finite power and weakness in God, because he could
not fulfill his desires. Arminius replies, “It does not follow. For God's
power is not the instrument of a divine affection,

[1651 logical printed p. 326 | IA leaf 344 | PDF p. 344]

desire, or weak wish, but of a free volition which follows the final judgment
of divine wisdom, although God employs his power to obtain what he desires
only so far as is fitting.” Reply 1. There is a plain contradiction here.
God's power is not the instrument of a natural affection, although God employs
his power to fulfill his desire so far as is fitting. Therefore it is not an
instrument, and it is an instrument. 2. He tells a fable about a final
judgment of divine wisdom, by which he implies that there is in God a first
and preliminary judgment of divine wisdom, and consequently a sensitive and
rash judgment which is corrected by the final judgment. Here God is an
Anthropomorphite. 3. He says that there is in God a natural affection which
God fulfills by no power. But why is this? This is a brutish and merely
natural affection, like the appetite of a stone tending downward, which is
directed by the cognition of the first cause. Is that so? Who superior to God
directs him? For it is an affection devoid of cognition, since the final
judgment of wisdom has not yet arrived. Therefore this affection falls far
beneath the dignity even of the sensitive appetite in brute animals. For the
sensitive appetite of brutes is not devoid of cognition. God will therefore
love while ignorant of what he loves. 4. In what way would God act contrary to
his own fittingness if he actually saved everyone whom he naturally desires
to save? Does he act contrary to his fittingness in procuring the salvation
of the elect? Not at all. But why would it oppose divine fittingness for him
to save the reprobate? For by a congruous motion

[1651 logical printed p. 327 | IA leaf 345 | PDF p. 345]

granted by Arminius himself, although his disciples protest, God knows how to
move and effectually move everyone's will equally, infallibly, and with
freedom unimpaired. But I will disclose this shameful mystery: it conflicts
with God's power that he should convert any others than those who are
converted. For prior to God's decree and middle knowledge, those to be damned
freely resisted God's calling and those to be saved obeyed it, and God cannot
by any power save those who rejected the call before every act of the divine
decree. 5. I ask what he understands by God's fittingness. Is this fittingness
a manner of acting freely instituted by God? I believe this cannot be denied.
But I ask why he willed this manner of acting to be fitting with respect to
the elect but unfitting with respect to the reprobate. For he freely instituted
these manners of calling. Here they will remain stuck unless they flee to
God's absolute freedom, by which he absolutely determined to call these in a
manner agreeing with his own fittingness, but those in a manner plainly
opposed to his fittingness. Yet the Arminians will not grant this to the
Jesuits. 6. But this is what they say: that this fittingness is a mode
connatural with divine justice and that it is foreign to God's justice for
God to call those who resist in the same way as those who do not resist are
called. Then, however, by necessity of justice God cannot bring it about that
those to be damned, whom God wills to be saved, obey and do not sin by
resisting him who calls. This will displease Arminius himself, who says that
“besides those

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, Bern edition, p. 729.]

[1651 logical printed p. 328 | IA leaf 346 | PDF p. 346]

sufficient hindrances, God has in the treasury of his wisdom even effectual
hindrances which, if produced, would certainly and infallibly prevent sin.”
But listen, Jacob: those hindrances either oppose God's fittingness and
justice, or they do not. If the former, it cannot be said that there are in
the storehouse of divine wisdom hindrances which, if produced, would prevent
sin—for example, the resistance made to the divine calling by all who are not
converted. For God's wisdom cannot dictate to God anything which opposes
fittingness and justice. Would God's wisdom, I ask, prescribe to God something
unfitting and unjust? Away with such blasphemies. But if those hindrances do
not oppose God's fittingness and justice, God can, notwithstanding his
fittingness and justice, fulfill his desires concerning the conversion and
salvation of all who are neither converted nor saved. For justice does not
hinder him, and therefore neither does their sin. Thus God's absolute freedom
alone hinders him, which conflicts with the Arminian faith. Nor can it be said
with any sound reason that God's absolute will, resting upon no reason in
sinners or upon divine fittingness, wisdom, or justice, prevents God from
fulfilling his natural desires. Indeed, in that way God's absolute will would
sin against this antecedent will. What could be more absurd? 7. Let them say
why God does not employ his power to fulfill his natural affection no less
than to fulfill his consequent will. For the same reason holds in both places,
unless they say that God does not understand the rationale

[1651 logical printed p. 329 | IA leaf 347 | PDF p. 347]

of natural affections as he understands the rationale of the consequent will.
But this falls little short of blasphemy. For what would God desire which
conflicted with his own reason or fittingness? Without doubt, if God converted
and saved everyone, this would have been fitting and just with respect to God.
Otherwise he would have given the creature a free choice which could not
produce every possible effect without injury to divine fittingness and
justice, which is absurd. 8. William Twisse observes very well that although
God does not correct nature so as to fulfill natural desires by omnipotence,
it does not follow that it cannot be corrected in another manner, namely by
grace effectual from foreknowledge. Arminius craftily conceals this. But I ask
Arminius why God calls some according to the decree of predestination, with
certain knowledge that they will obey, and thus God's natural desire is
fulfilled; while he calls others according to the decree of more common
providence, from foreknowledge by which he knows they will never obey, and
thus the antecedent will is never fulfilled. Arminius says that this agrees
with Augustine's doctrine and perhaps even with Scripture. Yet by equal
reason, according to Arminius, perhaps it must even agree with Scripture that
God, from absolute freedom alone, employs such unequal callings toward equals.
For no reason from justice, God's fittingness, foreseen faith, or men's
obstinacy explains why from eternity he established within himself two such
unequal decrees concerning men. If they say that it is from God's justice

[1651 logical printed p. 330 | IA leaf 348 | PDF p. 348]

that the more compliant are called by a congruous motion, the question will
return to infinity. But they became compliant through a congruous motion, and
if the noncompliant had been called in that manner, they would without any
doubt have been most compliant.

4. But Jackson seems to me to have declared the Arminians' mind more clearly
than the rest in at least this matter. For he says: “The rules of eternal
equity do not allow God to extend his hands any farther to save men than he
actually extends them.” Reply 1. I ask whether any rule is prescribed to God
by which he converts some and not others, apart from God's free will by which
he has mercy upon whom he wills. Scripture assigns no other. 2. Does not
anyone see that this empties and exhausts the storehouse of God's wisdom, in
which, according to Arminius, he has stored infinite modes which, if produced,
would prevent the sin of neglecting the call? Does anyone not see that it
makes God unable, without the stain of injustice, to convert any others than
those who are actually converted, as though God would cease to be just if he
graciously gave everyone effectual converting grace? 3. It follows that by a
natural necessity of justice God converts those whom he converts and does not
convert those whom he does not convert. Indeed, from eternity God could not
have prevented the perishing of all who perish unless he willed to be unjust.
Thus by fatal necessity God saves the saved and destroys the damned, and by
fate they are so many—no more and no fewer. Believe me, Homer's Jupiter,
Aristotle's first mover, and the God of the Stoics cannot

[Marginalia: Jackson, *On the Divine Essence*, section 2, chapter 17, p. 195.]

[1651 logical printed p. 331 | IA leaf 349 | PDF p. 349]

be bound with tighter chains. 4. But the innovator himself, forgetful of
himself, says on p. 179, chapter 15: “The apostle intimates that it was an
argument of God's immense mercy that God permitted Pharaoh to survive longer
after he had already become a vessel of wrath destined for eternal punishment
in hell.” Therefore he grants that God extended his hands farther to save
Pharaoh than the rule of eternal equity allowed. For after the seventh plague
he was a vessel of wrath. Therefore according to the rule of justice God could
then have killed him, but he delayed the punishment and extended his hands
farther, for he granted more calls after the seventh plague. Therefore God did
this contrary to all justice and violated the rules of eternal equity. What
monstrous theology!

5. Nor do I see how the Remonstrants extricate themselves when they say that
“the supreme Lord and lawgiver, sworn to no one, does not lay a law upon
himself but always preserves his own right intact to act outside the order as
he pleases.” But I ask whether God has a right to act contrary to his justice
and fittingness in some cases but not in others. Then God certainly reserved
for himself a right to sin and act unjustly. It follows necessarily that God's
justice and fittingness are in no way the rule which God follows in fulfilling
the antecedent will, but that God is here regulated by his absolute freedom
alone. Since he does not exercise that freedom, it stands invincibly that God
is weak and cannot grant the fulfillment of his desires.

[1651 logical printed p. 332 | IA leaf 350 | PDF p. 350]

6. Our second argument. From this distinction God's freedom to have mercy
upon whom he wills perishes. Therefore it is unworthy of God. The antecedent
is proved. God wills the antecedent good of his creature not freely but by
necessity of nature, as Arminius says. “Indeed,” says Corvinus, “by such an act
God cannot fail to will good,” and according to Grevinchovius this will
precedes God's freedom and decree. Therefore there is no freedom in God's
antecedent will. But neither is there any freedom in the consequent will,
because the consequent will is necessarily regulated by God's natural justice,
by which he cannot fail to will the conversion of the one who is converted
and not will the conversion of the one who is not converted.

7. Third argument. Scripture acknowledges no natural affection of showing
mercy. For if by an impulse of nature God gave his Son to the world, then it
was necessary for the human race to be created and, once created, to fall into
sin. In that way God created the world and permitted man to fall by necessity
of nature, because God cannot fail to bring about the occurrence of the things
which he naturally wills. For by the same impulse of nature a father loves his
sons and begets sons whom he may love. If God has mercy upon the human race by
an impulse of nature, by that same impulse he must procure the object of his
mercy. 2. He would also have to prepare a remedy for the fallen angels, since
they are rational creatures and hence necessary objects of God's natural
desire. But they will say that God's justice prevents a remedy from being

[1651 logical printed p. 333 | IA leaf 351 | PDF p. 351]

prepared for fallen angels, who sinned most freely, with no one persuading or
tempting them. But I reply first: this justice either arose from God's free
decree or is in God naturally. If the former, it would not have been unjust,
had God so willed, if he had prepared a remedy for them. If the latter, they
must say that in the infinite storehouse of God's wisdom there are no possible
ways of saving every rational creature. This conflicts with Arminius, to say
nothing more severe. For we have proved that God's justice toward creatures is
in God contingently, not naturally. 2. It is shameful and unworthy of God that
with respect to common love he should will the conversion and salvation of
many upon whom he cannot have mercy with respect to justice, as though he
could love someone unjustly, or either exercise justice against his natural
love of someone or naturally love someone contrary to justice, when God's
freedom is the rule both of justice and of love toward creatures. 3. Indeed,
it follows from this that men are converted as much according to the rigor of
divine justice as according to rich mercy, and that men are debtors to divine
justice, and hence ought to sacrifice to their own net, as much as they are
debtors to God's rich mercy. Scripture vigorously contends that we owe our
conversion entirely to the latter and in no way to justice. Ephesians 2:4–6;
James 1:18; Titus 3:5–6; 2 Timothy 1:9; Romans 9:15. 3. Natural things in God
are eternal, just as God's nature itself is eternal. Hence it follows that for

[1651 logical printed p. 334 | IA leaf 352 | PDF p. 352]

eternity God wills damned men and reprobate angels to be saved. For nothing
natural accrues to or departs from God as foreign to his essence. Thus it is
natural to God to devise remedies by which he may redeem all the damned from
eternal torments, and here Origen's error, which asserts that the torments of
the damned are not eternal, will revive. Arminius foresaw this argument and
answers it in two ways. 1. He says that by the antecedent will God willed—note,
he does not say that God now wills—the salvation of demons. But why did he
will their salvation and now does not will it? For God's natural desire is
eternal. What could depart from the divine nature? Both now and eternally God
naturally loves his creatures as such, yet the demons have not ceased to be
God's creatures. Would that God's church perceived the foul atheism of the
Arminian sect: namely, that with Vorstius they maintain that God was changed
long ago and has grown obsolete, and that God's natural desire for the
salvation of demons has vanished. 2. The Arminians say that God's wisdom and
justice impose a determination or modification upon God's natural affections.
Come, then, let us see what kind of thing this determination is. God's wisdom
and justice either determine God's natural desires so that at last they cease
to exist, although they once existed, or so that they exist modified and
determined. I ask whether God's wisdom causes there now to be no natural desire
in God to save damned angels and men. But I ask the reason why he does not

[1651 logical printed p. 335 | IA leaf 353 | PDF p. 353]

now desire their salvation. The reason is either in God or in the damned
themselves. What reason can there be in God why he once desired the salvation
of the damned but does not now desire it? Is it because he absolutely does not
now will to desire it, although he once willed to? But since God's freedom is
a free cause, it can in no way be the cause of a natural and necessary desire.
Nor can there be any reason in God why God's volitions should change and
perish, for then God would be mutable from an internal principle and therefore
corruptible. Nor can the reason be in the damned themselves, because while the
formal object of a desire remains, the desire itself remains. But the damned
retain their existence and do not cease to be God's creatures because they are
damned. Therefore God must still will the damned to be saved insofar as they
are God's creatures. Thus God's wisdom does not cut away anything natural in
God. But if God's wisdom only determines God's desire so that it is fitting
and just, then there will be in God properly so-called passions which precede
the act of divine wisdom and are unjust and unfitting in themselves until they
are reduced to order by the command of right reason. For what is capable of
moderation is of itself naturally apt to deviate. In that way God will be no
different from the poets' Jupiter, subject to passions. Furthermore, when God
says, “I will the Jerusalemites to be gathered,” he wills this either justly
and wisely or unjustly. If the former, he wills it not by an act of the
antecedent will but by an antecedent act of a restricted and determined will.

[1651 logical printed p. 336 | IA leaf 354 | PDF p. 354]

But in that way he wills them to be gathered by an act of the consequent and
not the antecedent will. For an act of a will restricted and determined by
wisdom and justice is not an act of God's natural desire, which terminates in
the bare existence of an intellectual creature. For a will restricted by
justice presupposes the creature's acts of obedience and disobedience, which
the antecedent will in no way presupposes. But if he wills the Jerusalemites
to be gathered not justly but only by a natural inclination, we see here no
determination made by God's wisdom and justice, but a merely natural,
unmodified act. Yet I ask when it is modified, if not when God actually wills
them to obey and be saved. But the Arminians must incline to this: that God in
exercise never actually wills anything antecedently by the antecedent will,
but wills it only by a propensity or inclination. Corvinus says this
expressly. “Then certainly,” he says, “insofar as God desires men's salvation,
that desire is fulfilled because he desires it, except insofar as justice
permits it.” But this is to will the salvation of the reprobate by the
consequent will determined by justice, and this is not to will the reprobate
to be saved. For justice protests against their being saved. Therefore,
according to Arminius and Corvinus, God does not actually will the reprobate to
be saved by an act of the antecedent will.

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 5, section 9.]

8. It is blasphemous for there to be in God desires devoid of justice and
wisdom. “Indeed, it is enough,” says

[1651 logical printed p. 337 | IA leaf 355 | PDF p. 355]

Corvinus, “that they are benevolent.” But is not a blind and brutish love
condemned among intelligent creatures when they do not know whether they will
attain the things loved? Was not Corydon burning for Alexis, as Virgil says, while
having no hope to cherish? In the same manner God will burn with love for the
salvation of all men and devils, not knowing whether he will obtain it.

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 5, section 6.]

“Indeed,” says Corvinus, “when God intends and wills something, it is not
necessary that he consider whether he will obtain it or not. It is sufficient
that he consider that it is fitting for him to desire it, both from goodness
and from love toward justice.”

[Marginalia: Ibid., section 7.]

But 1. according to Grevinchovius, who teaches this with the Arminians, God's
natural desires precede free decrees and acts of divine wisdom. Corvinus,
however, swallows this back and maintains that God wills and intends the
creature's salvation from love toward justice and from his own fittingness, as
Corvinus invents. Therefore the acts of God's fittingness and wisdom by which
he considers it equitable and just that he should desire such things will be
prior to the antecedent will. But they believe natural things in God to be
prior to what is free, which is what they themselves teach. Behold an open
contradiction here. 2. No brutish appetite is devoid of sensitive cognition,
which is most perfect in its own kind. Therefore there is not in God an
affection devoid of infinite cognition which penetrates not only into the
fittingness and wisdom of the desire or affection, but also into the desire's
fulfillment. For the love of an intelligent creature is imperfect in this:
that it does not presuppose perfect knowledge by which it is known whether
that love is to be satisfied or not. 3.

[1651 logical printed p. 338 | IA leaf 356 | PDF p. 356]

All equitable and holy natural desires presuppose sufficient and suitable
means for attaining their intended ends, as is clear by induction in the
appetites for motion, generation, nourishment, and rational willing. Therefore
the antecedent will presupposes grace effectual from foreknowledge for saving
all men and angels. Consequently all men and angels must be saved.

9. Fourth argument. This antecedent will is indeterminate, uncertain, and
suspended, and it is changed into an absolute and peremptory will to save if
the condition is fulfilled, or to damn if it is not fulfilled. Therefore it
is not in God. Corvinus says that it is one and the same will which, when first
conditional, passes into an absolute will once the condition is posited. Reply
1. Once the condition is posited, he says, the conditional antecedent will
passes into an absolute will. But the condition is posited in time, for men
obey in time. Therefore in time the antecedent will is changed into an
absolute will, and from eternity the will to save Peter was suspended until
Peter finally believed in time. How absurd. But if he says that God foreknew
from eternity that the condition would be posited, then the antecedent will
passes into an absolute will from eternity. How then do the Arminians teach
that by the antecedent will God wills everyone to obey and be saved in time?
2. The Arminians teach that God wills everyone to obey. But how, or what
condition must be posited here, so that the conditional will

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 5, section 11.]

[1651 logical printed p. 339 | IA leaf 357 | PDF p. 357]

may pass into an absolute will? Namely, if they obey, or if they will to
believe. Behold the theology! He wills men to will to obey, provided they will
to obey. 3. A natural will cannot pass into a free will, because the Arminians
and Jesuits teach that the free and the natural are contradictorily opposed.
But if he means that the antecedent will so passes over that it does not remain
the antecedent will, behold, then the antecedent will has vanished and ceases
to exist. But this is to posit God as mutable. “Nor,” says Arminius, “can
uncertainty be attributed to the will of one who in his infinite wisdom has
all things present and certainly foreknows as future even all things that are
most contingent.” Reply 1. Yet the antecedent will must be uncertain and
suspended, because by that will God intends both the obedience and salvation
of each and every person without knowing whether everyone will obey and be
saved. 2. But if he does not employ his infinite knowledge and instead desires
by a blind impulse of nature, with no foreknowledge of whether what he desires
will occur or not, God's will here will be suspended in exactly the same way
that men's wills are called suspended, since they do not know whether they
will attain what they will. 3. Corvinus, citing Vorstius, manifestly asserts
mutability here “with respect to hypothetical decrees. Therefore these can be
said to change when accepted separately in parts, because with respect to man
the condition either is or is not performed, and thus it either exists or does
not exist; and when it has existed for a while, it also often ceases to exist,
and again, after it has ceased for a while, it begins

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 5, section 10.]

[1651 logical printed p. 340 | IA leaf 358 | PDF p. 358]

to exist.” Thus Vorstius himself. “I say that only some particular event
dependent upon a contingent second cause changes at times, in relation to
which God's decree, accepted separately in parts, may then be called mutable
in some respect.”

[Marginalia: Vorstius, *Friendly Discussion*, chapter 1, p. 6.]

Here we have the guilty confessing. Since a hundred times in one year a man
believes and does not believe, the condition exists and does not exist, and
therefore God's will changes in parts and in some respect. Thus God will be
God in some respect and immutable in some respect, and not God and mutable in
some respect; that is, not eternal in a certain respect and not God in a
certain respect. 2. If God's decree taken in parts and separately is mutable,
they give no reason why the whole decree should not be no less mutable. And
certainly, if God is mutable and not God for an hour, why should he not be
mutable and not God for eternity? No reason can be devised. 3. It is worth
observing how vigorously Corvinus in the cited place defends this mutability
in God and supplies an argument which especially pleases the Arminian school,
yet nevertheless denies the conclusion, namely that God's will is mutable in
any way. This is to vote generously for the premises while striving against
the conclusion. 4. Suarez, whom the Arminians follow everywhere, grants that
the antecedent will remains in God even toward the damned, by which God wills
them to be saved, even when there is in God an effectual will to inflict
punishment. The Remonstrants say the same in their Apology, and Corvinus says
the same against Molina, chapter 5.

[Marginalia: Suarez, *On Divine Freedom*, section 2, § 10, p. 295.]

[1651 logical printed p. 341 | IA leaf 359 | PDF p. 359]

And here Corvinus and Vorstius maintain that decrees arise or cease in God
from the condition's being posited or not posited, and therefore that decrees
change. But once the condition is removed, there will then be no reason why
the decree should be removed or cease. For when a man does not believe,
according to their doctrine God can decree that the man should be saved if he
were to believe. Therefore the decree will both remain after the condition of
the decree is removed and will not remain, which are contradictory.
