Lecture XXXIII.

Now that, by God’s help and grace, that first theological commonplace has been
completed which occurred for us to expound in the first part of chapter I of
the Epistle to the Ephesians, and which stood out in that part as, so to
speak, chief and primary above the rest, other commonplaces observed and
gathered from the same source would now follow, to be explained in like
manner. Especially there follows the one which, among the principal and most
conspicuous passages of this Epistle, I remember naming and proposing in the
second place when, as is customary and for your sake, hearers, I prefaced the
exegesis of this Epistle with a few remarks.

[Marginalia: See the preface.]

I mean the offices of the Holy
Spirit toward the elect and faithful, when through the proclamation of the
gospel he renders their calling to faith in and acknowledgment of Christ
effectual and saving for them. He does this by his power, inwardly opening and
illuminating their minds so that they can perceive and discern those mysteries
of their salvation and redemption disclosed in the gospel—spiritual matters
of this sort for which no one is otherwise capable by nature, much less
skilled or knowledgeable. By his brightness and holiness he renews, purifies,
and cleanses the judgment of their mind so that, with that spiritual and new
acuity of the renewed mind, it may discern, apprehend, and judge that wisdom
of God which seems sheer foolishness to our deranged nature.

[Marginalia: 1.]

By his power he softens and refashions their hearts so that he thereby
produces true and living faith in them. By that faith they eagerly and without
hesitation embrace the light and grace offered to them in the gospel and

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 180 | Caput I p. 136]

shining inwardly
upon their minds. With all their strength they apply to themselves Christ as
preached in the gospel and filled with every saving grace and blessing, as
the wisdom and power of God; they rest upon him alone as the fountain and
foundation of all blessedness, with full confidence of salvation; they lean
upon him alone with living faith, and in him alone gladly and willingly find
rest.

[Marginalia: 2.]

Finally, by this same power and efficacy he seals the gospel promises in their
hearts, and therefore the certainty not only of adoption and salvation but
also of eternal election. As a plainly heavenly and divine seal, he imprints
his image upon them and deeply engraves upon their souls the character of his
brightness, purity, and holiness—imperfect though it is in this life, to be
perfected in his own time and completed in every detail. He inwardly testifies
to their spirit that they are among the children of God’s purpose and have
been called to share in that heavenly inheritance in Jesus Christ, according
to God’s eternal purpose and that gratuitous grace and benevolence by which,
even before all secular ages, he resolved to favor and embrace them in Jesus
Christ. In the uniquely beloved one he pours that fatherly love of God into
their hearts with joy inexpressible and glorious, with an incredible peace of
conscience and that filial confidence and freedom.

[Marginalia: 3.]

And so he supplies them with the most certain earnest of that most blessed
completion and of the full enjoyment of those heavenly goods to which they
must at last be raised and introduced. From those firstfruits of faith and of
the saving gifts which follow faith in this life they take a most certain hope
of things to come and foretaste the glory of heaven even here on earth, just
as was declared above in the proper place with our apostle in the exposition
of the next two verses of this chapter, namely the thirteenth and fourteenth.
This commonplace, then, embracing under itself certain other particulars
concerning faith, hope, sanctification, and therefore the dispensation of our
salvation, would now have come to be explained by us next.

[Marginalia: 4.]

But since this matter has already been treated copiously in part, in our
discussion of the effects, signs, and evidences of election, where we
considered at sufficient length the gifts, operations, and efficacy which the
Holy Spirit peculiarly exerts and exercises in the elect and regenerate; and
since in part a great many statements about this same matter follow,
scattered by our apostle throughout the body of this Epistle—as may readily
be observed both in this latter part of the chapter, which we are now
undertaking, and in the fourth and sixth chapters which follow, where the
apostle mentions those same gifts and offices of the Holy Spirit in
illuminating and sealing the elect and faithful and in strengthening and
arming them with that spiritual panoply against every force of temptation—we
have therefore been content to touch and sketch this commonplace with the
brevity you have heard. It is now time, with God favoring us, to take back
into our hands for unraveling this precious and divine fabric of the apostolic
context, interrupted by us for perhaps longer than was fitting—or, if not
beyond due measure, certainly far beyond our intention.

[Marginalia: Setting aside, or postponement. Transition.]

Nor is a long course needed to connect what is contained in verse 15 and the
following verses, in the remaining part of this chapter, with the part already
expounded by us.

[Marginalia: The connection repeated.]

In so clear a matter it will suffice to recall to your memory in one or two
words that, apart from the inscription or salutation which stands as a common
preface set before the whole Epistle, the remainder of this first chapter was
divided by us into two parts. In the former, extending as far as the fifteenth
verse, the apostle sets forth and presents—briefly indeed, but magnificently
and with the highest majesty of the Spirit—the causes of our salvation, that
is, those spiritual benefits bestowed upon us in Jesus Christ by God the
Father. He regards them in three ways. First, as decreed and appointed from
eternity before God in the secret counsel of election and predestination.
Second, as actually accomplished for us by Christ the mediator incarnate for
our sake, and obtained and procured for us by his death and merit. Third, as
actually communicated and applied to all of us who have hoped in Christ
through the ministry of the gospel promulgated and preached, with the
effectual grace of the Holy Spirit intervening, and as sealed in our souls by
the power of the Spirit—first the souls of the believing Jews, and then also
of the Gentiles called to share in the same inheritance. Though formerly
scattered, Christ gathered them under himself as one head into the joined
structure of one mystical body.

[Marginalia: The division of chapter 1 repeated. Part 1.]

Thus in this former part the apostle proposes those saving benefits in
general by way of doxology; individually introduces and explains what he has
proposed; and applies in passing and distributively what he has introduced and
explained both to the Jews and to the Gentiles. But in the latter part, which
fills and occupies the rest of the chapter from the fifteenth verse, he takes
occasion from the effectual calling of the believing Ephesians and their
sealing unto the hope of the same heavenly inheritance just mentioned, and
digresses into a remarkable passage of benevolence. In it he signifies his
zeal for their salvation and continual advancement. He both indicates the joy
he conceived from the fruits of their calling, namely faith and love, for
which he testifies that he continually gives thanks to God on their behalf,
and pours out prayers without ceasing to the end that God may supply them day
by day with new increases of the same saving gifts. Since, I say, these two
parts of this chapter were established by us, we have thus far executed the
former.

[Marginalia: Part 2.]

With God’s favor, we now enter the latter. It certainly breathes not only a
truly apostolic heart—that is, one full of godliness, zeal for advancing
Christ’s kingdom and glory, and brotherly love—but also carries before us a
most brilliant torch which we rightly follow both in acts of thanksgiving and
in prayers conceived either for ourselves or for others, as will later appear
more distinctly in its proper place. Indeed, although this thanksgiving of
the apostle and the prayers conceived before God for the Ephesians may appear
to display a certain form and semblance of digression, they in fact have the
force of an indirect exhortation. By it the apostle exhorts them obliquely and
urges them not only to perseverance in the Christian faith and in the other
gifts which accompany and follow faith, but also both to make continual
progress in these same means, earnests, and beginnings of eternal salvation
and to request it from God by constant prayers. For when he shows himself so
zealous for these things on their behalf and in their name, how much more did
it befit them to be zealous for their own salvation? When Paul values so
highly these saving gifts of faith and love bestowed upon them, how much more

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 181 | Caput I p. 137]

did the Ephesians themselves need to give God thanks for them without ceasing
and to ask God continually for their confirmation and increase?

[Marginalia: Proposition. Argument from the lesser.]

Yet this whole context, which follows to the end of the chapter, coheres in
such an uninterrupted course that one can scarcely observe even a conspicuous
joint or, as it were, articulation at which our apostle’s discourse pauses.
Just as a craftsman fashioning a bracelet fits one loop to another, so the
apostle adapts and binds one clause to another throughout this whole period,
with such ease in the bonds that offer themselves, as it were, spontaneously
and to hand, that to resolve this most beautiful structure by the art of
logical analysis might seem a desire to dissolve and destroy a far more
elegant art, and to tear apart into its fragments and tesserae a work
variegated with a vermiculated mosaic—that is, to spoil all its elegance,
grace, and beauty. Yet because the grounds both for strengthening memory, for
easier teaching, and for improving our knowledge require some distinction
here, we may divide this entire discourse of the apostle into two parts. In
the former he sets forth both the subject of his thanksgiving and the content
of the prayers offered to God for the Ephesians; that is, he explains, as far
as the twentieth verse, what above all he asked God for them and what gifts of
the Holy Spirit he petitioned to be divinely conferred upon them or daily
increased and confirmed in them. Namely, he asks God to give them the Spirit
of wisdom and revelation: to disperse the remnants of natural darkness which
remain all too dense even in the souls of the regenerate, and so illuminate
them by the power of his Spirit that they may daily advance more and more in
that true wisdom which comes to us only by divine revelation and consists in
the acknowledgment of Christ the mediator. Thus with true and living faith
they may both perceive and apprehend those hoped-for goods stored up in
heaven for believers and the called—namely, the riches of that glorious
inheritance into fellowship with which they had been admitted together with
all the saints—and may also feel and experience more and more throughout
their whole life in themselves that singular power of God which exerts itself
so mightily in the conversion, vivification, and transformation of believers.

[Marginalia: The division of this consolatory part. Part 1.]

In the latter part, in the next four verses, there is incidentally attached a
somewhat fuller commendation or declaration of that effectual strength and
power of God from one of its effects, by far the greatest and most marvelous.
God first exercised it in Christ himself, the head of the faithful and elect,
by raising him from the dead and exalting him to that supreme glory and
sublimity which the apostle describes for us at the end of this chapter with
a magnificent and in some sense divine abundance of speech. In this way he
confirms believers more and more in the hope and faith they have conceived
concerning that same power of God which will at last be exerted in raising
them also from death and glorifying them with Christ their head. Although
Christ, raised to that glory, has absolutely everything subjected to himself,
he nevertheless has his Church subjected to himself in a peculiar way as his
body and bride. Like a leader, king, husband, and head, he cherishes, rules,
protects, animates, vivifies, and furnishes her with all the gifts necessary
for life and salvation until at last he makes her a sharer in his eternal
glory and blessedness—as if she were the completion of his own person. Thus,
although he lacks nothing for perfect blessedness, nothing for the supreme
majesty of glory, and no eminence can be added to that pinnacle of heavenly
honor; although he is not only entirely self-sufficient in himself but also
fills all others belonging to his body with his gifts and pours out every
saving grace abundantly upon all his own; he nevertheless appears in a
certain sense maimed and imperfect until he has taken that completion of his,
that is, his mystical body, to himself, perfectly united it to himself, and
crowned it with perfect glory and felicity. This is the sum of both parts.

[Marginalia: Part 2.]

For the sake of teaching and explanation we again distinguish the former part
in this way. First, in verses 15 and 16, he sets forth by thanksgiving and
prayers his joy and zeal toward the Ephesians, together with the causes or
occasion of that thanksgiving. Then in the next three verses he explains the
sum of his prayers or petition.

[Marginalia: Subdivision of the former part.]

As concerns the first part, just as we see it comprehended in two verses, so
in the former the apostle proposes the cause or occasion of his congratulation
and of the joy conceived concerning the Ephesians, while in the latter he
proposes and expresses its manner. He indicates the cause when he says,
“Because of this,” or “wherefore,” or “therefore, I also, having heard of the
faith in the Lord Jesus which is among you, and love toward all the saints,”
and so forth. Here “because of this” appears to be referred to what he had
said above concerning the Ephesians, when he applied specifically to them, as
he had to the believing Jews, that grace of effectual calling, saying that
they also had believed in Christ after hearing the word of truth, and, when
they believed, had been sealed with that Holy Spirit of promise who is the
earnest of our eternal inheritance. “Because of this cause,” therefore, he
says, “since I have thereby recognized”—as though he said—“that you too belong
to Christ’s Church and God’s election, and that you too have been admitted
with us to fellowship in the same heavenly inheritance, I cannot but
congratulate you from my heart on such a happy lot and condition, especially
since, though absent, I have also heard of your faith and love from other
believers.”

[Marginalia: The twofold cause of the apostolic congratulation: the Ephesians’ faith and love.]

Yet it should not therefore have been inferred with certain people that this
Epistle to the Ephesians was written by the apostle before he had seen them or
known them in person. For although he later received a report about their
faith from others while living at Rome, nothing prevents him from having
previously grounded and instructed them in the faith himself, as the sacred
history of Acts testifies. Someone can bring others to faith in Christ and
later, while far removed from them, be informed by others of their progress
in godliness and love, congratulate them on this very thing by letter, and
ask God in prayer for their perseverance and increase in the faith. Doctor
Zanchi, of godly and blessed memory, testifies this concerning himself.

[Marginalia: An incidental vindication. Zanchi.]

This being observed in passing, there is no difficulty in these words. As we
see, he adduces two most closely joined causes of his congratulation and joy:
the Ephesians’ faith in the Lord Jesus and their love toward all the saints.
These two comprehend the whole duty of the Christian person. Whatever is
referred directly and immediately to God himself and Jesus Christ the
mediator is completed by faith; whatever regards our neighbor, and especially
the saints and faithful, is contained under the name of love. Hence
Chrysostom neatly and elegantly calls these two things, which are necessarily
joined in every true Christian, “a certain marvelous pair,” as though you
might say, an admirable team by which the chariot of the Christian life is
continually drawn,

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 182 | Caput I p. 138]

until by the even, happy course and progress of these two
yoke-fellows it is at last brought to that goal of our calling above.

[Marginalia: Chrysostom’s elegant phrase.]

Yet when explaining the fourth verse of this chapter above in the proper
place, we used another figure to express the distinct offices and effects of
faith and love and therefore both the harmony and agreement of the virtues
themselves with one another and their difference from one another. We said
that they were like the two hands of the Christian person: with the one he
apprehends and applies Christ to himself, while with the other he embraces
all brothers and saints in Christ and for Christ’s sake.

[Marginalia: A reference to the observations on verse 4.]

Since, to confess candidly what is true, this comparison greatly pleases us,
we polish it still further. We suppose every Christian to stand upon Jacob’s
ladder, which extends from heaven to earth, and the whole life and pursuit of
the truly Christian person to be nothing other than a continual ascent along
that ladder, or at least a constant endeavor to ascend. Yet he has the two
hands divinely granted him occupied meanwhile in different ways. With one he
grasps the ladder itself above him, leans upon it, and steadies his steps so
that he does not fall and slip from it. But he reaches the other down to help
his brothers in every way he can: bearing their burdens, supporting them as
they ascend together with him, taking them by the hand, and, if it can be
done, drawing, urging, and advancing them with him into the same way of
salvation.

[Marginalia: Elaboration.]

Now the former is the hand of faith, as is plain in itself, for it grasps the
true ladder of salvation, Jesus Christ, and leans upon him with all its
strength. The latter is the hand of love, which strives to bring help to its
neighbors in every possible way, especially by procuring their salvation and
advancing them in its way. Indeed, it supplies them with these corporeal and
earthly things chiefly to the end that in this way it may advance the good of
their soul and their eternal salvation, which true love regards and desires
for its neighbors above all else. This is truly to love: not to applaud
someone in evil, not to indulge him to his own destruction, not to offer
oneself as a fellow worker and companion in his sinning, nor to supply the
instruments, art, or any assistance for sin—which is nothing other than to
kiss so that you may strangle, as those Egyptian robbers whom they therefore
called Philetæ did—but rather to take thought for the salvation of one’s
neighbors. Even when you provide and procure the necessities of the body and
physical life, you must nevertheless look beyond to that further life and
direct all the offices of your love especially toward it. Since we have spoken
about this above, we will add here only a few things more properly belonging
to this passage concerning the nature of faith and love.

[Marginalia: Application to the subject. Note well. Φιλήται, that is, kissers, Gen. Note 1.]

First, we must observe the order in which these two are proposed together not
only in this passage but elsewhere by the apostle in most of his Epistles,
particularly at the opening both of Colossians and of Thessalonians, where he
always sets first faith in Jesus Christ and second love toward the saints.
The reason is that the former is the cause, fountain, and foundation of the
latter, while the latter is the necessary effect and inseparable companion of
the former in the hearts of the faithful.

[Marginalia: An exegetical note on the order of these two.]

But the order and manner, so to speak, of that production or emanation must be
noted—that is, how love emanates from true faith. We may state it briefly as
follows. We do not think love flows immediately from faith, but by means of
the love of God by which we love him. That love flows from the sense of God’s
love by which he loves us in Christ as its own immediate and proximate cause.
Yet this is in fact nothing other than faith itself, because faith is God’s
love apprehended by us, or the apprehension of that love, which comes to the
same thing. For what else, I ask, is that saving or justifying faith peculiar
to the elect—the only faith under discussion here—than the acknowledgment,
apprehension, and full persuasion of God’s fatherly favor and mercy in Jesus
Christ? Its living and inward act is to draw to ourselves that love of God
poured and spread abroad in our hearts through the Holy Spirit, and to apply
it to ourselves as closely as possible.

[Marginalia: Its more distinct explanation.]

From this faith and apprehension, as we were also saying yesterday according
to the occasion and the argument then before us, these two effects necessarily
and, as it were, immediately follow. First comes confidence in God, which, as
you know, is closest and most nearly related to the character and nature of
true faith itself. I mean that very resting in God, entrusting and committing
oneself, one’s life, and one’s salvation wholly to him, resting upon him and
his promises made, confirmed, and sealed in Jesus Christ, and leaning upon
them with all one’s strength. It is impossible for a person who acknowledges,
apprehends, and senses God as a Father gracious to him in Jesus Christ, and as
truthful, constant, and powerful in most certainly fulfilling and performing
his promises—as we apprehend God by true and living faith—not immediately to
rest wholly in him and conceive in his soul this confidence in God in Christ
Jesus of which we speak.

[Marginalia: In the public exposition (namely, Section 32 of the Catechesis). The twofold effect of faith. Confidence in God.]

We may contemplate and observe its true character and nature in the example
of Abraham, the father of believers. This confidence is content with even the
bare word of God who promises, or, I say, with his promise alone, even if it
sees no manner or means either of escaping present difficulties or of
attaining the fulfillment of the promises in the future beyond God’s power
alone, his wisdom, might, and most constant faithfulness in performing his
promises toward the faithful. It trusts and leans upon these—as may be seen in
Abraham sacrificing and already on the point of slaying his Isaac unless he
is forbidden—although sense and human reason resist, the judgment of
distrustful flesh struggles against it, and all the circumstances of present
things cry out in opposition.

[Marginalia: The example of Abraham explained.]

It is consequently plain that, although the object of that historical and
general faith is the whole word of God in general—which historical faith
acknowledges and apprehends as true, indeed as divine, that is, proceeding
from God as author, and indeed, as regards the gospel promises, as true in
general toward the Church and its individual members, toward each and every
one of the elect—nevertheless the proper object of this saving faith is, as
the apostle speaks here, the Lord Jesus Christ, that is, the promises made by
God in Christ and for his sake in the gospel. For it is the peculiar nature
of this faith to apply to itself what it apprehends, “to appropriate” it and,
if I may speak thus, “to make it one’s own.” But it properly applies to itself
and makes its own only the gospel promises. It is therefore directed to these
in a peculiar way as its proper and peculiar object.

[Marginalia: An incidental corollary. On the proper object of true faith.]

This, then, is the first and most inward effect of faith. The other which
flows from the same source is love for God.

[Marginalia: And love for God.]

It arises from the sense and apprehension of divine love and mercy—that is,
from this very faith about which we are

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 183 | Caput I p. 139]

speaking. For it is impossible that anyone should sense and apprehend God as a Father
who loves him in Christ, as appeased and gracious toward him in Christ Jesus,
and as a Father kind and merciful toward him, without also loving him in
return above all things and being kindled reciprocally by love for him. The
greater a person’s inward sense and apprehension of divine love, the greater
his confidence in God, and the more and more vehemently he is kindled and
inflamed to reciprocal love for him. In a word, all our love by which we are
borne toward God is a certain reflection and reciprocation of God’s love
toward us.

[Marginalia: Which is declared by a description.]

You know that rays of sunlight striking or falling upon a solid and polished
body are reflected and thrown back by it, and are reflected more powerfully
the more directly and powerfully they strike the body placed before them. We
must think in this way about that intelligible Sun and about his Son Jesus
Christ, that Sun of righteousness. He must first send down from heaven upon
us those nourishing and life-giving rays of his love and spread them in our
hearts by his Holy Spirit. He must not only first love us, but by this
outpouring of his love also cause us to perceive that we are loved by him. As
soon as he does this, those rays are then reflected back upon him from the
mirror, as it were, of our hearts and are poured back by our reciprocal love
upon the Sun of our souls himself. This occurs with an impulse, ardor, and
vehemence as much greater as the acknowledgment, sense, and apprehension of
his own fatherly love toward us is greater—that is, as that act of saving
faith about which we speak is more vivid and vigorous.

[Marginalia: From a comparison. And from a comparison of degrees.]

The apostle John teaches us this very thing in chapter 4 of his catholic
Epistle, this order, I say, of our love toward God, when he says, “We love him
because he first loved us,” and above in the same chapter, “In this is love,
not that we loved God”—spontaneously, that is, and first, as is evident from
the antithesis which follows—“but that he loved us and sent his Son to be a
propitiation for our sins.” So in Psalm 31, “Love Jehovah,” says the psalmist,
“you who are loved by him,” or, “whomever he pursues with kindness, for
Jehovah preserves the faithful and repays excellently,” or in a preeminent
manner and degree, “and does magnificent things”—although our common version
translates these words differently. And in Psalm 116 he says, “I love Jehovah
because he has heard my voice and my supplications; because he inclined his
ear to me, therefore I will call upon him in my days.” Here the Holy Spirit
uncovers and reveals to us the first fountain and foundation of our love
toward God: God’s own love, first apprehended by us and perceived and gathered
by us from its various effects, especially the forgiveness of sins, the
hearing of our prayers, the peace made with us, and his return to favor with
us, or our reconciliation with him in Jesus Christ.

[Marginalia: And by testimonies of Scripture. 1 John 4:10. Psalm 31 v. 24. Psalm 116:1.]

In turn, this reciprocal love of ours toward God manifests and exercises
itself in two effects: namely, our union with God and the performance of new
obedience to his commandments. It therefore produces in us these two most
holy affections as its own two inseparable marks and properties. First, the
desire to be united to God, to cling most closely to him through Jesus Christ,
and at last to perfect and consummate the fellowship with him begun in us
here through faith, hope, and love. The Church testifies concerning this
inward desire for union with God and Christ in Song 2 when she says, “I greatly
desire to sit in his shadow”—namely, that of Christ her bridegroom—“for his
fruit is sweet to my palate,” and afterward professes herself sick with love;
that is, she languishes and collapses, or almost faints in soul, as she seeks
and awaits the completion and consummation of that most desired union with
her bridegroom. This is the former affection.

[Marginalia: And note either by its proper property or by a twofold effect. 1. Song 2:3 & 5.]

The latter is a sincere zeal to please him in everything, to do his will and
flee what is contrary to it, and above all to avoid offending him. “For he who
has my commandments and observes them is the one who loves me,” says the Lord
in John 14; and according to John himself in chapter 5 of his first Epistle,
“This is the love of God, that we observe his commandments.” The more sincere
and ardent this love for God is within us, the more vigorous will also be the
zeal to obey God gladly according to all the precepts of his law. But after
the love of God himself, the greatest and most important precept of the divine
law is love for one’s neighbor. This above all God enjoined in the law, that
we should love our neighbor as ourselves. This Christ in the gospel calls a
new commandment and his own, that we should embrace one another in mutual
love. It is therefore impossible that anyone should love God and consequently
be intent upon observing his commandments without heartily loving his
neighbor in God and for God’s sake, since this love alone brings the
fulfillment of the whole law, as the apostle clearly declares in Galatians 5.
From this it is also plain, unless I am mistaken, in what manner and order,
and through what intermediary, love toward one’s neighbor flows from faith in
Jesus Christ. Although this is a common saying among theologians, it was not,
I think, equally clear to us at the outset.

[Marginalia: Affection 2. John 14:21. 1 John 5:3. Galatians 5:14.]

We shall therefore embrace the whole matter in this summary. First of all is
God’s gratuitous love toward us, the fountain and foundation of all our grace,
which God manifests and attests to us especially in the gospel and its
promises. We then embrace and apprehend this love by faith, when faith is
poured into our hearts by the power and operation of the Holy Spirit. This
faith, or apprehension of God’s love, is thirdly followed immediately and
necessarily by these two things: confidence in God and reciprocal love for
God. Finally, our reciprocal love toward God is followed by union with him,
desire for the consummation of that union, and sincere zeal to perform the
divine law, whose fulfillment is love toward one’s neighbor itself. Love,
therefore, depends upon and flows from faith in God and Christ only through
the intermediary of that love for God by which we love him in return. This is
what we undertook to explain at the outset.

[Marginalia: Summary of the preceding exegesis.]

From this it may also be inferred that the greater faith in Christ is, the
greater love toward one’s neighbor will also be, from a greater zeal to
please and obey God. I shall endeavor to illustrate this by the same
comparison expounded above, namely of a person ascending a ladder. The more
firmly he grasps the ladder with one hand, the more power the other hand,
reserved for the aid and support of his neighbor, will have. Experience
itself makes this sufficiently known to each of us. For if in such a difficult
position and ascent you have nothing by which to steady and support yourself
and grasp nothing, you will be able neither to carry any burden, nor to help
and support another ascending with you, nor to bring any assistance to your
neighbor, because you yourself would fall at even the slightest movement. But
if with one hand you hold Christ and apprehend him by faith, then you will
have the
