## Part XLVII: Law, Gospel, and Assurance

[Part II | PDF page 227 | logical source scan 226 | printed page 225 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

[Marginal note: To 2.]

My adversary thunders nothing here but anathemas against even the angels themselves if they should happen to dissent from him. But I pray God that he rather may not someday hear from God’s own mouth, “With the measure you measured others, I will measure you.” But from where did such indignation finally arise? Namely, because from those words, “God shut up all under stubbornness,” I inferred that human beings fell into this misery neither by chance nor contrary to God’s will, yet by their own fault and not God’s. Meanwhile Andreae answers nothing to that phrase, nor to the countless other even more explicit ones that I adduced in their place and explained most fully and learnedly both by comparing other passages and from Augustine’s disputations against the Pelagians, which I refuse to repeat here. But what will become of you, poor Augustine—indeed, of you too, Dr. Luther—if you stand before Andreae’s tribunal?

But to the point. “Whatever,” he says, “God forbids, he does not will anyone to do.” Certainly whatever he forbids is evil and cannot please the supremely good God. No godly person will deny this. But these things differ very greatly: considering the law in itself and absolutely as it is in itself, and considering it insofar as it is or is not fulfilled by human beings. From God’s law we therefore always understand what he approves as right and disapproves as perverse—the will God disclosed in the law. But the end God intends by commanding his law and even his gospel to be declared to these or those people cannot be gathered from that external declaration alone; it is finally understood from the consequent effects. I trust I have abundantly proved this in the preceding disputations, but I will now touch on it again briefly.

If whenever God forbids something he does not will anyone to do it, he certainly wills what he commands to be done. Yet he never forbids anything without commanding its contrary, nor commands anything without forbidding its contrary. Tell me, then: when he commanded Abraham to slaughter Isaac and consequently forbade him to spare Isaac, did he will Isaac to be slaughtered and not will Isaac to be spared? Did he will the Ninevites destroyed, as he commanded Jonah to announce? Did he will Hezekiah to die, commanding him through Isaiah to prepare for death? Did he will Pharaoh to release the people when he commanded this through Moses, yet did not change Pharaoh’s wickedness but even, for the sake of his own glory, hardened Pharaoh’s heart in that malice through Pharaoh himself—

[Part II | PDF page 228 | logical source scan 227 | printed page 226]

—We discussed this above when we showed that the will of sign sometimes wholly differs from the will of good pleasure, yet God cannot be blamed for that. But to speak more generally, did he give his people the law and does he daily command it so that anyone might fulfill it completely? Yet he commands the whole law. And when we hear the legal promises, does he will that they be furnished by us? Far from it. He never determined to save his own through the law fulfilled by them. Thus he even commands something he does not will to occur and promises something he does not will to furnish, yet does not therefore mock human beings.

On the contrary, when human beings hear something demanded from them that they cannot fulfill and themselves forbidden what they cannot abstain from, and see a condition impossible for them added to the promises while, conversely, a condition they cannot escape coheres with the threats, they must necessarily recognize—unless utterly out of their minds—that the law is divinely set before them for no other purpose than that they be reproved and terrified even against their will. In this terror, unless brought as it were from hell by peculiar grace, they either plunge into despair or are hardened more and more through their own wickedness. Does this occur, I ask, without the counsel and voluntary decree of God the just judge? But if God transforms that bite of sin, lethal in itself, into a heart contrite and humbled before him, afterward to pour the oil of gospel grace into the wound, then the changed person hearing God’s commands experiences the law pressed upon him again with a purpose different from before: not any more than before that he be saved by fully fulfilling it, but that, resisting the remnants of his flesh according to the powers he has received from God, he walk according to that will of God.

But if Andreae thinks human beings possess these powers by nature, he is wholly Pelagian. If partly from nature and partly from grace, he is Semi-Pelagian. I would attribute neither to him, although I do not see how one or the other does not necessarily follow from his teaching. For if he wills this grace to be offered to every individual, the same question returns that I explained more fully before: why this grace is effectual in some but not so in others. The difference will arise either wholly from nature or from grace, but grace unequally distributed. Nor will God be free from blame in this inequality if the natural destitution of all powers for thinking and acting well does not suffice for a person’s just condemnation, but in addition, for God to be blameless, grace with some capacity to receive it must be offered to every individual. Otherwise sinners will answer God by asking why he demands from them what he knows they cannot furnish, and why he condemns them for what they did not furnish when he gave them no capacity to furnish it. This is just as that profane Castellio of unhappy memory said,

[Part II | PDF page 229 | logical source scan 228 | printed page 227]

as though God demanded oxen fly. Shameless mouth! As though this human impotence for good and captivity under evil came from the Creator, and not from the one who voluntarily deserted his Creator.

What then? Since sins too are counted among sin’s punishments, whenever God gives Satan and evil instruments not only some power to act—not by inspiring new malice in them, but by rightly moving what he finds in them—but also prescribes what they will do, rouses and incites them, and renders them effectual, does he not will them to do what they do? They do everything forbidden by the law’s commands. Nor does the distinction between the action and the action’s defect, and the two works in one work—one God’s, as one rightly employing an evil instrument; the other the instrument’s, no longer as instrument but as cause—avail here. It suffices now to have proved that God often does not at all will something forbidden in the law to be done through someone and, conversely, does not will something commanded in the law to be done, so that we are not bound by Andreae’s anathema.

What I said of the law must be thought much more manifestly of faith and the gospel’s grace. The law speaks to many more people than the gospel, since nature itself engraved it in the hearts of all endowed with reason as far as knowledge of it is concerned, and some natural desire for a measure of uprightness remains in those not altogether depraved. Yet all this is far removed from the Holy Spirit’s gift peculiar to the regenerate. The gospel’s grace is therefore much more special. If our adversary dreams it was ever offered, now is offered, or ever will be offered to every individual, mere experience, the teacher of fools, will refute him. If he compounds this most empty delusion with the further error of thinking that the heart is opened in everyone to whom it is offered so that they are willing to be able to attend, and ears are given to hear so that they can hear and learn, then let him teach that all are drawn by the Father, the Son reveals himself to all, and it was given to the crowds to know the mysteries of God’s kingdom.

But that twofold testimony he objects to me from page 551 of this colloquy out of the first volume of my treatises has been transcribed with my adversary’s usual faithfulness. I nowhere said what he ascribes to me, that “in a wonderful and incomprehensible manner it pleased God, and God willed, that our first parents sin.” I wrote in just these words on page 179 of that volume that “that fall”—elsewhere I explained why I said “fall” and not “sin”—“so proceeded from Adam’s voluntary motion that it nevertheless did not happen against God’s will, whom in a certain wonderful and incomprehensible manner it pleases that even what he does not approve insofar as it is sin nevertheless does not occur without his will.” I thus sought to explain more fully Augustine’s statement that “in a wonderful and ineffable manner, what occurs contrary to God’s will does not occur apart from God’s will.” The other passage from page 192 of the same volume was indeed copied more faithfully but cunningly distorted by him. I did not write simply and indefinitely, as he wishes, “in the reprobate,” but

[Part II | PDF page 230 | logical source scan 229 | printed page 228]

“in some of the reprobate, understanding is aroused to perceive and believe what they hear.” This is as true of general and historical faith, shared even by the demons themselves as I there explain, as it is utterly false that this grace is given to every reprobate, as my adversary contends.

Here indeed I would have needed to answer most fully the utterly shameless slander he pours upon me throughout pages 551 and 552, namely that Beza’s doctrine entirely abolishes the use of the gospel’s word and the sacraments. I would have needed, I say, to answer this slander most fully, had I not already shown both in the colloquy on baptism, pages 66 and 83, and in this treatise on predestination how consciences doubting their election should be counseled from true and solid doctrine. Yet the importunity of this man compels me to say something again on the subject.

“You make,” he says, “all the promises particular, that is, pertaining only to the elect, concerning whom God made the hidden eternal decree that he wills to save only them. Who then will tell you, or from where will you know whether you are comprehended in the number of the elect or the reprobate, when in those most grievous temptations the conscience doubts those very testimonies and motions of the Holy Spirit to which you refer them?” This is his summary, from which he concludes on page 558 that “all the godly must beware that doctrine, so fertile in generating new errors that it deprives human beings not only of the substance and true use of the sacraments but even of the whole gospel together with Christ, and deprives them of all consolation.” This, of course, is to dispute mildly rather than revile.

Tell me, what consolations does he oppose to temptations of this kind? Precisely those that Satan not only easily tolerates being proposed to those tempted concerning their election, but even himself suggests, so that they either rest upon false faith or plunge into a deeper abyss of despair. Andreae wills the particular decree of election, which he calls “hidden” in an invidious and false sense—as we will soon show—to be abolished once and for all, that is, election itself. What could be more desirable to Satan, when our entire faith and hope rests upon no other foundation than assurance of our eternal and immutable election? Paul testifies this everywhere, especially Romans 8:29 and 11:1; 1 Corinthians 2:16; Ephesians 1:5; 2 Timothy 2:19; and 1 Peter 1:2. Indeed, since one who draws every individual without exception does not “elect,” if Andreae’s doctrine stands, either that sweetest name of election must be erased from Holy Scripture, or if God is said finally to elect those who received that universal grace offered by external calling, human beings will be called before they are destined for election. Thus the sequence of all the causes of our salvation must be overturned. Furthermore, this election will depend upon human beings’ own choice if God is said to give every individual the power to believe or

not believe, so that faith belongs potentially to every human being without exception but actually only to those whom God pleases to believe—not by the power of some second particular grace confirming the first, but by cooperating with the power of that first universal grace. Since Scripture as a whole testifies all these things are utterly false, Satan will easily tolerate consolation against those terrors of conscience being sought from them, so that those tempted deceive themselves.

Then suppose these utterly certain truths come to the tempted person’s mind: not all who are outwardly called are drawn inwardly by the Father; not all are given to the Son, but only those who will believe in their time; faith does not belong to all; the Son does not reveal the Father to anyone whatever, but finally to those he wills; and, as is evident from the saddest example of the Israelites themselves, not even all who hear the promise with their ears are children of promise, that is, destined for the promise; many are called and few elected. It is no more possible that every individual outwardly baptized with water is baptized with the Spirit than that every individual circumcised in flesh was also circumcised in heart. Who could then persuade himself that the false grace of universal election—a thing ridiculous even to say, since one who rejects none cannot be said to elect—or universal calling, which I have so often said ought rather to be called indefinite unless restricted to the whole body of the elect, must be believed? Thus we know that all circumstances customarily understood in Scripture under the name *horothesia*—nation, sex, age, few or many, graver or lighter sins—cease when God’s mercy is in question and must in no way delay or hinder our faith and hope.

Finally, since Andreae teaches that this fabricated universal grace is not so firm that someone cannot fall from it, for he denies it is irrevocable and contends that the Spirit of adoption can be wholly lost, as we heard in the treatment of baptism, does he not himself overturn by this teaching whatever he formerly built, if he must deal with someone persuaded that he has fallen from faith and God’s grace? Everyone therefore sees how empty and futile these things are. We explained most fully in the treatise on baptism, as just noted, what must be opposed to them from God’s pure word, how the use of the doctrine of predestination must be applied, and whence assurance of election must be sought and how retained.

I only wish the Christian reader now to observe this man’s twofold error concerning what he calls particular election and God’s hidden decree. If he calls that election “particular” which pertains to every individual without exception, not I but he will be guilty of this particularity. But if he calls “particular” what, proposed universally, every hearer should apply particularly to himself, he does precisely what he censures in me, except that he falsely extends that whole body to the entire human race without any exception, while I restrict it with Holy Scripture to the elect alone, who themselves constitute their own whole body—

[Part II | PDF page 232 | logical source scan 231 | printed page 230 | ends before 2 Peter 3 item on shared page]

—And who would not marvel that this man is carried away by such zeal to defend his opinion that he has not noticed that what he holds concerning universal preaching pertaining to every individual without any exception cannot apply to the sacraments—unless perhaps he should even dare affirm that the grace of participating in baptism and the Lord’s Supper is offered to every individual in the entire world. But enough on this.

As for that eternal hidden decree of God which he cannot bear—although once it is removed all God’s providence and consequently God himself are necessarily either abolished or suspended from intermediate causes—we have often said it is called “hidden” from us, first because, preceding every intermediate cause without exception, indeed subordinating all those intermediate causes to itself, it depends upon God’s mere will, whose depth is inscrutable and wondrous even to the angels themselves. Second, because it is disclosed to us only *a posteriori*, that is, through its effects, and only then truly ceases to be hidden from us. This disclosure ought to suffice for us and fully does suffice: as we ascend from the fruits of faith and sanctification—that is, from displeasure at sin and the Spirit’s works—to justification and the irrevocable gift of true faith; from there to inward calling joined with outward calling, that is, the preaching of the word and administration of the sacraments; and then to predestination in Christ, utterly certain and impregnable against every temptation however violent, so that, certain of our glorification certainly to follow, we even now have peace in our consciences.

[Marginal note: To 3.]

How long will this fabricator of anathemas have enough leisure in so great a matter not to tire of attacking that monster which he alone imagined for himself? For who was ever so shameless as to hold that God sentences human beings to eternal punishments because it so pleased him, without the most just causes of damnation being found in the condemned themselves? Certainly to pass over the intermediate causes from the first to the last is not a descent but a plunge. We explained most fully in its place in this same treatise how far and in what sense the axiom “what is the cause of a cause is the cause of the effect” applies here.
