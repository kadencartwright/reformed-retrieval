Let us finally proceed, if you please, to the divine testimonies and the
arguments derived from them.

This was foreshadowed in the Old Law, where we often read that many who were
contaminated by some legal pollution remained unclean until evening:
“namely the shadow of death, when activity ceases and we are freed from
sin,” as someone observed on Leviticus 11:24.

But let us leave the shadows. This shines more clearly in the rays of the
New Testament: “He who has died has been freed from sin,” Romans 6:7. The
death in this passage must in its proper sense be understood as spiritual
death, or the mortification of sin, as verse 2 and the sequence of the
Apostle's argument establish. Yet by the interpreters' agreement the
Apostle here alludes to what occurs in bodily death and applies it by
analogy to one who has died to sin, as Grotius rightly observed.

Thus Theophylact and, before him, Chrysostom, whom he nearly transcribes,
say in substance: “This belongs naturally to every man, because once a man
has died he is released from what follows and has no occasion to sin,” and
so forth.

[1679 printed p. 273 | IA leaf 281]

“For a dead man does not sin at all; likewise, one born of God does not
sin.” Thus Pseudo-Jerome on the passage. Grotius holds that Paul's statement,
“he who has died has been justified from sin”—whose twin is Peter's
statement in 1 Peter 4:1, “he who has suffered in the flesh has ceased from
sin”—was taken from sayings of the rabbis, namely the Talmudic statement,
“As soon as someone has died, he is made free from the commandments, and
therefore from sin.” In this sense Aeschylus calls death a physician because
it frees us from every misery, and Anacreon says that it frees us from sins
and vices: “The dead man does not desire.”

I do not press this passage strongly. Even if it does not speak only of
spiritual death, as Piscator in his *Analysis* and Louis de Dieu in his
*Annotations* explain it, and even if an allusion to bodily death were
granted, it does not appear to concern the particular deliverance from sin
which occurs to the saints at death, but what happens to absolutely everyone
after death. Chrysostom, as noted above, and Theophylact after him say that
this belongs naturally to every person, insofar as death abolishes all the
actions of life. Thus, according to Estius, the argument is tacitly drawn
from the effect or property of death and should be explained as follows: a
dead person performs no living action but is released from the duties of
the life he formerly lived; similarly, we who have died to sin ought to be
freed from its actions. These words therefore appear to mean cessation from
the acts of sinning ordinarily exercised in this life, which cease in every
person after death, not the peculiar abolition through death of sin's
remnants in the saints, which is our present subject.

Some also adduce 1 Corinthians 15:26 here: “The last enemy to be abolished
is death.” If sin remained in the saints after death, they argue, death
would not be the last enemy abolished. But this does not appear to follow.
Even if sin remained in the dead for a time, it would not remain as long as
death. According to some, sin remains after death only until the body is
reduced to ashes, as we showed above; but death remains until the last day
of judgment. Only then, through the resurrection to eternal life, will death
be entirely abolished and thus be the last enemy destroyed, while sin was
abolished long before, if not in death itself, at least when the body became
ashes.

The following proves the point better. The Apostle Paul in Ephesians 5:27
and Jude in verse 24 say that “Christ presents us before the Father
blameless, without spot or wrinkle or any such thing.” Although this final
and perfect presentation of the whole Church together does not occur before
the last judgment, it occurs distributively to each particular believer at
his death. “When the dust returns to its earth from which it came, the
spirit returns to God who gave it,” Ecclesiastes 12:7. It returns to God as
Judge, to be driven from his presence into outer darkness if it is impious;
but if it is pious and faithful, it returns to God as Father and Rewarder,

[1679 printed p. 274 | IA leaf 282]

to reign with him continually in heaven. Nothing defiled enters there, as
we will show afterward. Sin must therefore depart from believers when they
are no longer exiles on earth but at last dwell with the Lord as inhabitants
of the heavenly homeland.

Thus Hebrews 12:23 calls them “the spirits of righteous persons made
perfect,” that is, consummated or perfected. The word “perfected” is added
not restrictively but emphatically—not to distinguish some spirits of
deceased godly people from others, but to indicate the perfection common to
them all. In a similar matter Gregory of Valencia impiously distorts Peter's
phrase in 1 Peter 4:3, “unlawful idolatries,” or “wicked and illicit forms
of idolatry.” Taking “unlawful” restrictively, he attempts to fashion some
forms of idolatry as illicit and others as licit. In the same way Estius,
if not impiously, at least ineptly, wishes to infer from the epithet
“perfected” added to the righteous dead that some—those fully purified from
sin in this life—are taken into heaven immediately at death and are wholly
perfect, while others, not so purified here, must still be purged in
purgatory for some period, sooner or later, and are meanwhile not yet fully
consummated or perfect. Certain Hebrews raved similarly before him. But, as
I have said, neither passage is to be explained restrictively; both are
emphatic and explanatory rather than discriminatory. When Peter calls
idolatries “unlawful,” he means idolatries which are all wicked and illicit.
Likewise, when Paul speaks of “righteous persons perfected,” he means
righteous persons who are all perfected and consummated at death.

Since he speaks of spirits, he must be understood as speaking of souls—as
earlier in clause 9 of this chapter—distinguished from flesh or body and,
in this passage, separated from the body by death, as all interpreters
agree. He declares that as soon as they are thus separated they are perfect
and consummated. But they would not be perfected in this way unless they
were separated from sin as well as from the body. The word “perfected” itself
suggests this, for in Holy Scripture it means consecrated no less than
perfect. The soul cannot possess full or principal perfection unless there
is also full consecration and perfect separation from sin. Only then are
they perfected—or, as the Codex Claromontanus reads, thoroughly perfected—
namely, perfectly consummated by full consecration and most firmly
established in it.
