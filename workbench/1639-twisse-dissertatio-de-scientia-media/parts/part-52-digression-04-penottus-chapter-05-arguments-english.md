FOURTH DIGRESSION.

Penottus is called to account regarding the things he discusses in Chapter 5 in response to certain
arguments brought against the second proposition proposed in Chapter 4.

We have already subjected to examination what Penottus discussed in Chapter 4 concerning the second
proposition. It will also be worthwhile to weigh what is appended by him in Chapter 5 in order to
vindicate that proposition from certain objections, so that we may explore with what good faith he
conducts himself in these matters too.

PENOTTUS.

The first argument against the second proposition is taken from the authority of St. Augustine,
book 83, *Questions*, q. 46, where he says that it is sacrilegious to say that God beholds something
placed outside himself. This manner of speaking indicates that God beholds creatures not as they are
outside God but as they are in God. Thomas agrees, 1, q. 14, art. 5, saying: “God sees himself in
himself, because he sees himself through his essence; but he sees things other than himself not in
themselves but in himself, insofar as his essence contains the likeness of things other than
himself.” Scotus subscribes to this in several places cited by Ioannes Rada, controversy 19, art. 1,
conclusion 7.

I answer that they must be expounded as speaking about that which is known by the divine intellect
as its primary and adequate object and through its proper species, not through an alien species.
Those particles “in himself” and “outside himself” denote the medium of knowing, not the terminus of
cognition. Thus the sense is that God knows nothing placed outside himself in a species which is
something outside God, but knows all things in himself, that is, in a species which is within him,
which is the divine essence. With particular regard to the authority of Augustine, which some abuse
to support senses never intended by the author, I say that the clear and open sense of his words in
that passage is that, in creating things, God looked upon nothing placed outside himself as an
exemplar in imitation of which he would create things.

RESPONSE.

Augustine explains his opinion in these words at the designated passage: “For he did not behold
anything placed outside himself so that he might fashion according to it what he was fashioning. To
suppose this is sacrilegious.” From these words it is manifest that they were spoken in no other
sense than to signify that God looked upon nothing placed outside himself as an exemplar in
imitation of which he would create things. It is altogether incredible that anyone devised an alien
interpretation of so open an opinion. Penottus nevertheless intimates that this was done when he
complains of certain senses formed by some people but never intended by the Author. And what, I ask,
was placed outside God before the creation of things, in imitation of which he might produce either
the world or any part of it? Yet since it is nevertheless received that God knows things made no
differently than he had known things to be made, it follows that it is no less sacrilegious even
today to affirm that God beholds things placed outside God differently than he once beheld them
before there were any things placed outside God for him to behold. The opinion of Aquinas is also
most explicit on this point, professing this very thing no less explicitly, not about the primary
and adequate object, which indeed is the divine essence itself, but about the secondary and
inadequate object, which the creatures themselves constitute. Thus Penottus’s first distinction is
of no use whatever. As for the mode of cognition, which Penottus notes to take place through a
species, I do not yet understand what foundation it rests upon. God himself understands his essence
not through any species, either proper or alien, but through the essence itself. Likewise, God knew
all future creatures perfectly well from eternity and thus beheld them (for the knowledge of vision
has as its object not only present things but also future and past things), yet not through either a
proper or an alien species, but through his essence. For God had nothing besides his essence through
which he might understand future things. When I say “essence,” I include the will, and I consider the
essence as willing. From this alone it comes about that things which by their nature were merely
possible (represented by the essence of God considered simply) passed from the condition of
possibles into the condition of futures, so that they thereby became a suitable object of the
knowledge of vision. I gladly embrace Penottus’s explanation of the particles “in himself” and
“outside himself,” namely that the medium of knowing is denoted by them and not the terminus of
cognition. For without doubt GOD beheld all future things,

[Book I, printed p. 99; BSB canvas 00125]

and that from eternity, but in himself and not in themselves, as Aquinas explicitly teaches. How
this agrees with Penottus’s opinion he himself has not yet explained. From eternity God had seen that
each creature would exist in itself at the definite difference of time he had decreed. That is, he
saw that they would exist in themselves, not that they were existing. For they did not exist in
themselves from eternity, that is, really; but they did indeed exist in God from eternity, and that
in willed being, in which God beheld them from eternity. This, however, is to exist only
intentionally, not really, and consequently to be seen by God not as existing in themselves but
rather as going to exist.

PENOTTUS.

The second argument: What is seen in a mirror is not seen in itself except by reflected cognition.
Creatures are seen in the divine essence as in a mirror, as the Council of Sens says in its decrees
on the faith, c. 13; Augustine in the last chapter of the book *On the Threefold Dwelling*; and
Thomas, 1, q. 12, art. 8, reply 2, and q. 14, art. 5, in the body. And this seems to be the common
manner of speaking of the holy Fathers, who call the divine essence a voluntary mirror. Indeed,
divine Scripture does so in Wisdom 7, where uncreated Wisdom is called a spotless mirror.

I answer that, if we speak properly, creatures are called a mirror of divinity or of divine things
rather than God a mirror of creatures. Hence in 1 Corinthians 13 Paul calls the knowledge we have
here of God through the mediation of creatures knowledge through a mirror, for the reasons to be
adduced in Chapter 8. Therefore, the authorities brought to the contrary must be expounded according
to a metaphorical sense, not according to propriety. The metaphor is regarded in this way: just as
things are seen clearly and distinctly in a mirror, so creatures are represented most clearly and
most distinctly in the divine essence. The Son, however, is called a spotless mirror because through
generation he receives the essence which represents all things.

RESPONSE.

I am not pleased by Penottus’s statement that things seen in a mirror are seen only by reflected
cognition. In my judgment, he should rather have said that things seen in a mirror are seen by a
reflected species rather than by reflected cognition. For it is not our cognition that is reflected
by a mirror; rather, the species of the object are reflected.

But in my judgment creatures are no more properly called a mirror of divinity than God is called a
mirror of creatures, although creatures shine forth far more clearly in God than God does in
creatures. This is no wonder, since creatures are only a mirror of divinity for us, whereas the
divine essence is a mirror of creatures for God himself. This further difference must also be added:
we behold things in a mirror more obscurely than things in themselves. But God by no means beholds
creatures more obscurely in his essence than in themselves. Indeed, God does not behold creatures
in themselves in any other way—not merely not more clearly, but not at all. For when each thing
exists in itself, God beholds it no differently than in himself. Thus the fiction of this Novarian
concerning an intuition of things attributed to GOD from eternity as though they existed in
themselves utterly vanishes, unless perhaps recourse is made to intentional existence, namely in
willed being before God, not to real existence. I readily understand, however, how little this
pleases Penottus, the foremost champion of middle knowledge.

PENOTTUS.

The third argument: Intuitive knowledge necessarily requires the actual existence of the thing at
which it terminates. But GOD knows many things which do not actually exist. Therefore, his knowledge
of them does not terminate at them as they are in themselves. This seems to be the principal motive
of Ioannes Rada.

I answer that the major can have two senses. The first is that intuitive knowledge requires the
actual existence of the thing in such a way that both are posited and removed in the same difference
of time. The objector seems to accept this sense, but it is false, since it would follow that GOD
does not have intuitive knowledge of many things which have existed or which are still future. The
other sense is that intuitive knowledge must reach not only the future being which a thing has in
its cause, but also the present being of actual existence which the thing will have outside its
causes. This sense is true. In this way God is said to have intuitive knowledge even of things which
do not coexist with him, because, although Antichrist does not now exist, the divine gaze, which in
a single act in the *now* of eternity is borne over all the differences of time and in a certain way
anticipates the event of the thing, sees that being which Antichrist will have when he exists in the
nature of things, as Thomas says in 1, q. 14, art. 13.

RESPONSE.

Penottus’s distinction is devised beyond all sobriety. For the proposition to which he devises a
reply openly professes that the condition of intuitive knowledge requires the actual existence of
the thing to be posited together with the positing of intuitive knowledge. For to say that some
thing is seen both connotes that some thing actually exists and signifies that this actually
existing thing is seen by someone. From this it follows, I admit, that when the actual existence of
the thing ceases, the vision of it also ceases. Since Penottus denies this, it would have amounted to
the same thing for him to rest content with a simple denial of the opposing proposition. But he does
not do so. Instead, he invents an empty distinction by whose byways he may take care of himself,
which is evidence that his own conscience suggests to him that the proposition has such evidentness
that he utterly despaired of making its direct and untricked denial sufficiently marketable.
Therefore, since it becomes manifest to me that Penottus did not lack the will explicitly to deny
this proof, let us observe the reasoning by which he does it. “This sense,” he says, “is false, since
it would follow that God does not have intuitive knowledge of future or past things.” He could just
as well have said without circumlocution that the proposition itself is false. For its authors
undoubtedly hold that God does not have intuitive knowledge of future or past things. Therefore, it
was hardly fitting for Penottus to let this matter pass without confirmation. Perhaps he wished to
instill in his readers the suspicion that this was rejected by everyone. But such a fiction is
scarcely sober. Yet I do not deny that God is commonly said to know by the knowledge of vision not
only present things but also future and past things. Nevertheless, all who require the actual
existence of things for God’s intuitive knowledge will say that the knowledge of future or past
things attributed to GOD is called intuitive not properly but figuratively. For without doubt
intuitive knowledge is properly accommodated to human or angelic intuitive knowledge. Among humans
and angels the cognition of existing things is said to be more certain than that of things not yet
existing, and they also understand

[Book I, printed p. 100; BSB canvas 00126]

future things differently from present things. Neither of these, however, can be said of God, since
he beholds most clearly the things which are still future, as Penottus acknowledges, and, according
to the received opinion of the theologians, knows things made no differently than things to be made.
As for what our response to this argument is, we shall set it out as soon as we have finished what
must be said against this reply of Penottus. Let us therefore approach the other sense which
Penottus invents—and he truly does invent it, by a plainly chimerical fiction. For he holds that
intuitive knowledge reaches not only a thing’s future being but also the present being which the
thing will have outside its causes. But, I say, the being which a thing will have outside its causes
is not present being but future being. He nevertheless says that this sense alone is true, although
it is merely fictitious and plainly contradictory, and in this sense he attributes to God intuitive
knowledge of all things—that is, in a plainly chimerical and contradictory sense. At times Penottus
himself is conscious of this incongruity, and so more than once he has explained his meaning in this
way: God reaches from eternity the present being of the actual existence of some thing—not the being
which it will have, which is said of the future, but the being which it has, which is affirmed of the
present. But if he had stated his opinion here in that way, he saw that a storm difficult for him to
dispel would arise. (For the adversary would immediately retort: Grant that God reaches the present
being which the thing has; therefore, actual existence of the thing is required, so that I may
convict you from your own mouth.) Hence, to avoid this difficulty, he now says that GOD by his
knowledge reaches the present being which the thing will have, although elsewhere, when he seems to
be situated outside danger, he professes everywhere that God sees the present being which the thing
has. For this Novarian conducts himself everywhere as though he had approached the defense of human
liberty only so that by any artifice he might obscure and weaken the grace of God, to which he is a
foremost enemy.

And who asks whether it is guile or valor in an enemy?

I, however, would answer the proposed argument as follows. Divine intuitive knowledge must by no
means be conformed to the image of human intuitive knowledge. Nevertheless, I say that God has
beheld all future things from eternity just as he beholds them while they are present. For even when
they are present, he beholds all things only in himself and in his essence. I acknowledge that the
constitution of this intuition requires and posits a certain actual existence of things—not real,
however, but intentional—through which they are present to God from eternity in willed being. For
this too is a certain actual existence, insofar as, although by their nature they were only
possible, through the determination of the divine will they passed from the condition of possible
things into the condition of future things. Yet this condition of future things is also the
condition of present and past things (lest someone perhaps object that in this way intuitive
knowledge is not at all of things present or past in time, but only of future things). This
threefold condition in name is in reality simple and unique, so that it can be known by God through
one and the same most simple cognition. For to be future until some definite difference of time has
arrived is to be present as soon as it arrives and to be past as soon as that time assigned to the
thing in question has ceased to be.

PENOTTUS.

The fourth argument: Intuitive knowledge by which a thing is seen in itself necessarily depends
upon the object, and when the object varies the knowledge itself also varies. But the knowledge God
has of creatures does not depend upon them and does not vary with their variation. Therefore, God
does not behold them as they are in themselves.

I answer that the major proposition is true only of the knowledge of the primary and adequate
object, not of the knowledge of a secondary and inadequate object, such as creatures are. For when
the secondary object varies, the knowledge does not vary; or if it varies, it does not vary as to
the substance of the act, but only as to a relation of reason. So that this reply may be understood
better, it must be noted that, in 1, q. 14, art. 5, in reply to Aureolus’s first argument, Luis de
Molina admits that divine knowledge, as to those relations of reason which it adds to creatures
over and above the substance of the act insofar as they are known together by the divine intellect,
depends in some way upon creatures as upon the terminus of the relation, and that when the being of
creatures varies those same relations of reason also vary. Some more recent writers attack this
doctrine in many ways. Nevertheless, it can be said without scruple that divine knowledge, as to
the relations of reason which it bears toward creatures, depends in some way upon them—not indeed
as an effect upon a cause, but as a relative upon a correlative and a relation upon its terminus—and
that when the terminus of the relation varies, the relations themselves also vary. For to me it is
more than certain, without sophistries, that now GOD does not see Adam as future but as past; that
he now sees Antichrist as future; and that when Antichrist has existed he will no longer see him as
future.

But if anyone shrinks from admitting that divine knowledge depends upon creatures according to
those relations of reason, let him say that, although divine knowledge does not depend upon
creatures, the being of the creature itself is nevertheless a condition necessarily required in
order that the creature can be the terminus of certain and infallible divine knowledge. This will
be to say the same thing as some more recent writers say so that we may not seem to agree with
Molina.

RESPONSE.

Those who hold intuitive knowledge to be such that it too admits variation when the object varies
altogether presuppose that the object about which they philosophize admits variation. But it is
beyond controversy that the primary and adequate object of divine intuition, since it is the very
essence of God, is wholly incapable of every kind of variation. Therefore, the proposition should
rather have been denied simply than dispatched by so weak and incongruous a distinction. Who does
not know that intuitive cognition primarily and properly concerns human intuitive knowledge as it
is distinguished from abstractive knowledge? But who has ever distinguished the object of our
vision into a primary or adequate object and a secondary, inadequate object? Therefore, you would
have answered far more suitably that the major proposition concerns human intuitive knowledge, not
divine knowledge. This does not please Penottus so well, because it would make intuitive knowledge
in God of a different kind from the intuitive knowledge attributed to a human being. Whether he
wills it or not, however, he must concede it. Again, observe, I ask, how unseemly his conduct is when
he says that intuitive knowledge admits variation from variation of the primary object but not from
variation of the secondary object. For who among them

[Book I, printed p. 101; BSB canvas 00127]

would not suspect that, if any variation should enter divine knowledge, it would certainly proceed
from variation of the primary rather than the secondary object? But Penottus knows very well that,
if variation comes to divine knowledge only from variation of its primary object, no variation
whatever will come to it. For the primary object—namely, the divine essence itself—is wholly
invariable. Therefore, if any variation is attributed to God’s knowledge, it must necessarily arise
only from variation of the secondary object. But Penottus denies that God’s intuitive knowledge
varies from variation of the secondary object. Yet when he noticed that not even a color of
variation could arise in God’s knowledge except from variation of that secondary object, he
immediately applied a qualification in this form: “Or if it varies, it does not vary as to the
substance of the act, but only as to relations of reason.” This must be reckoned among the actions
of that Italian, unredeemed by any candor because of his crafty and most insincere manner of
argument. For when he perceived that the opinion of those who profess that God’s knowledge varies
with variation of the object had a bad reputation, and when he himself adhered to that opinion, as
will be manifest in what follows, he did not dare profess it plainly and openly. Instead, to break
the odium and avoid offense, he outwardly asserts the contrary—that God’s knowledge by no means
varies from variation of the secondary object—so that by throwing them this bone, as it were, he
might pacify his adversaries. But immediately afterward, as though hiding behind first principles,
he substitutes his own opinion in what seems a more opportune place, saying: “Or if it varies, it
does not vary as to the substance of the act” (which no one has ever called into controversy; for
since God’s knowledge as to the substance of the act is nothing other than God himself, it must be
judged no less invariable than God himself), “but only as to relations of reason.” Yet he presents no
instance of this variation. It can, however, be extracted from what follows, namely where he says:
“To me it is more than certain, without sophistries, that now God does not see Adam as going to be
future, but as past; and that he now sees Antichrist as future; and that when Antichrist has existed
he will no longer see him as future.” This is the variation of divine knowledge which Penottus
acknowledges, at least according to relations of reason: namely, insofar as God does not see as
future something which he formerly saw to be future. By the same reasoning, a transmutation of this
kind can also be admitted in divine knowledge insofar as he now knows something to be past which he
did not previously know to be past, but either present or future; and likewise insofar as he now
knows something to be present which he previously knew only to be future and afterward will know to
be past. But, I say, no variation or transmutation of this kind must be attributed to divine
knowledge, whether according to the substance of the act or only according to relations of reason.
I shall confirm this by testimony greater than every exception—namely, that of Penottus himself—so
that all may know with what dizziness he undertook to erect this stronghold of his for human
liberty. For observe, I ask, how he argues at chapter 16, number 4, while confirming his opinion
about the presence of things before God according to a new invention, and that by a reason which he
calls demonstrative: “Either creatures are at some time present to God according to their actual
existences or never. The latter cannot be said. If the former is said, then they have always been
present to God; otherwise, divine cognition would be mutable and subject to vicissitude, which is
contrary to the catholic faith.” Observe from comparison of these passages how serenely our
Novarian establishes contradictions. For if it must not be said that God now knows something to be
present which he did not previously know to be present, lest divine cognition thereby be mutable,
by the same reasoning it must not be said that God once knew something as future which he now does
not know to be future; or that he was once going to know something as past which he now does not
know as past but rather as present or future, lest divine cognition thereby be held mutable. These
are the Novarian’s dictates in chapter 16. But here in chapter 5 of the same book he does not
hesitate to profess that “to him it is more than certain, without sophistries, that God now does not
see Adam as future, although he once saw Adam as future” (for he acknowledges this everywhere when
he asserts that from eternity God saw not only the future being but also the present being of
future contingents), “but as past; and that he now sees Antichrist as future; and that when
Antichrist has existed he will no longer see him as future.” Thus far he admits that God’s cognition
is mutable—not indeed as to the substance of the act, but only as to relations of reason. The
suspicion also enters my mind that he framed the reason proposed in chapter 16 only against the
resistance of his conscience, although he markets it as demonstrative. For in chapter 5 he places
his instance only in the condition of future and past things; but in chapter 16, while arguing in a
way suited to the present undertaking, he philosophizes only about the condition of a present
thing. Hence the appearance of a formal contradiction, and one in the terms themselves, as they
say, is avoided. Again, I ask, why is it not equally true that God now sees Adam as future and that
he sees Adam as present? For Penottus will not deny that God today sees Adam as present. And what
sober person does not perceive that a thing’s presence is as opposed to the thing’s pastness as is
its futurity? And although God today sees Antichrist as future, nevertheless, according to Penottus,
God also sees Antichrist today as present. And why should God not even see Antichrist today as past?
For a thing’s presence is no more opposed to its futurity than its pastness is. But since he admits
in passing that Molina’s opinion—that divine knowledge as to relations of reason depends in some way
upon creatures as the terminus of a relation, and that when the being of creatures varies those
same relations of reason also vary—is attacked in many ways by more recent writers, it can be seen
why Penottus conducted himself as he did in his response to this fourth argument: namely, with the
cunning of an old hand. While excluding variation of divine knowledge as though through the front
door, he nevertheless cherished and embraced the same variation admitted with Molina through a
false door. I, however, see no reason why any variation should be thought to accrue to divine
knowledge on the side of the knower. For although God does not today see Adam as future, though he
once saw him so, what do you conclude from this? That God’s cognition is therefore mutable? But I
will deny that consequence.

[Book I, printed p. 102; BSB canvas 00128]

The thing indeed is transmuted, but I deny that a transmutation of divine cognition follows from
the transmutation of the thing. I also prove this by reason. For God’s will is not transmuted by the
transmutation of a thing. If God’s will remains the same, why should his knowledge not also remain
the same despite the variation of things, especially if God beholds things only in himself, or in
his essence as it is willing—that is, if he beholds things as they are before God in willed being?
Let those who hold that God’s knowledge depends upon the things themselves see how they mean to
preserve it safe and sound from transmutation. But it will be as easy for us to show God’s knowledge
as his will to be immune from change, notwithstanding the transmutation of things. For by entirely
the same reasoning we shall show that God’s knowledge remains immutable despite the fact that he
once knew a thing to be future, now knows it to be present, and afterward will know it to be past;
and they will show that God’s will is free from every change, although he once willed some thing to
be future, today wills it to be present, and afterward will will it to be past. Yet to speak much
more precisely and more congruently with the Scholastic method, it must be said not so much that God
once willed a thing to be future, today wills it to be present, and soon will will it to be past, as
that from eternity he both willed, today wills, and eternally will will some thing to be future for
a definite time (whether you say it is or was makes no difference to a change of the divine will,
but serves only to signify the change of things), again to be present for another designated time
(again, whether you say it is or was makes no difference), and thereafter to be past (whether you
say it is or will be amounts to the same thing). In the same way, from eternity God knew, today
knows, and eternally will know that the same thing was so willed by God that it would be future for
a definite time, present for another difference of time, and thereafter eternally past. Again, if
God’s knowledge is transmuted, it is transmuted either by knowing what he did not previously know or
by not knowing what he previously knew. But God knows nothing he did not previously know, nor does
he fail to know anything which he previously knew. You will say: indeed, today he does not know Adam
to be future, though he once knew it.

I answer: that Adam is future today is not something knowable. How can he be said not to know by not
knowing what is not knowable? You will perhaps reply: knowledge of Adam as future was once in God,
but today it is not; therefore, something has departed from God’s knowledge. I answer that no act of
knowing was ever in God which is not in him today. For an act of knowing in God is not other than
God himself. Nor are diverse things known by God through diverse acts, but through one and the same
act, which is the very essence of God. And just as through the same act which is God it could have
come about that he willed another world very different from this one to be made, so it also could
have come about that he knew other things very different from those he now knows. Therefore, there
is nothing at all remarkable if by no other but one and the same act God both formerly knew Adam as
future and today knows him as past. This is especially so because God neither wills nor knows
anything to be future indefinitely, but for some definite time. Hence, unless God knows Adam to be
future for this time, he by no means formerly knew that Adam would be future for the time assigned
to him. Nor do I see any need here to invent distinctions about variation of God’s knowledge either
according to the substance of the act or according to relations of reason. You will perhaps say
that one and the same act of knowledge in God once had a relation to Adam’s futurity and now does
not. But as to the pastness of the same Adam I answer that God no more once had a relation to Adam’s
futurity than to his temporal presence and thus to his pastness. For he formerly saw Adam’s presence
for a definite time, and thus his pastness for a time following his presence, just as for a time or
duration preceding his actual existence he saw his futurity. But let it be as you please. I
nevertheless deny that any relation of reason accrues to God’s knowledge from the fact that he knows
some thing; it arises rather from the fact that the knowledge of things in God is considered by
some intellect and a comparison is made. Furthermore, I say that even today, by the same reasoning,
there is in God’s knowledge a relation to Adam’s futurity. For what? Does God not today know both
Adam’s futurity and Adam himself? From Penottus himself I prove that God knows this and even sees it
today. For if God today sees Adam to be future, he also sees his futurity today. But God today sees
Adam to be future, which I prove as follows. It is no more incongruous for God to see Adam to be
future when he is past than to see him to be past when he is future. But God sees Adam’s past being
when he is future; therefore, he also sees Adam’s future being when he is past. I prove the minor,
which alone is doubted, as follows: it is no more incongruous to see the past being of a thing when
it is future than to see the present being of the thing in time when it is future. But according to
Penottus, God sees the present being of the thing, and that in time, when it is future. Therefore,
he also sees the thing’s past being while it is future. The minor is Penottus’s own, used by him
everywhere in this argument and in this very chapter, number 10, in these words: “The other sense is
that intuitive knowledge must reach not only the future being which the thing has in its cause but
also the present being of actual existence which the thing will have outside its causes, and this
sense is true.” I prove the major in this way, although it is sufficiently manifest by its own
light. If it is not incongruous for God to see what is more remote from existing, it certainly is
not incongruous for him to see what is nearer to existing. But the past being of one thing can be
nearer to existing than the present being of another thing. For example, at the creation of the
world the past being of Noah’s flood was nearer to existing than the present being of the tower of
Babylon. But at the creation of the world, according to Penottus, God saw the present being of the
tower of Babylon, as we showed above. How much more, then, did he see the past being of Noah’s flood?
And therefore, by the reason that God sees the past being of a future thing, he can see the future
being of a past thing. Again, if God today sees Adam as future, Penottus will then readily grant that
there is no transmutation of divine knowledge even according to relations of reason. But God today
sees

Adam as future according to Penottus, which I prove as follows. It is no more incongruous to see
Adam as future today than to see Adam as present today. But it is not incongruous for God to see
Adam as present today according to Penottus, which I prove as follows. It is no more incongruous to
see Adam as present today when he is past than formerly to see Adam as present when he was future.
But according to Penottus, God formerly saw Adam as present when he was future, as he everywhere
discloses the opinion of his mind on this point. For he holds that God sees all things as they are
in their presence, just as Aquinas argues. Finally, I prove this very thing as follows: either God at
some time reaches by his gaze and the keenness of his intellect the future being of Adam, or he

8, which he professes is also supported by an evident reason. The sum is this: although God today
does not see that Adam is future, or see him as he is future, this by no means prevents him from
seeing today both Adam and his futurity. For although he does not see Adam’s futurity with respect
to the present time, it does not follow from this that God does not see his futurity. Adam’s
futurity is spoken of with respect to the duration preceding Adam. God saw Adam’s futurity from
eternity under this relation, sees it today, and will see it eternally. Therefore, there is nothing
God once saw which he does not both see today and will see eternally. So too God will eternally see,
sees today, and saw from eternity the pastness of Antichrist with respect to the existence of the
time which will follow him. So too God saw from eternity, sees today, and will eternally see the
presence of each of them intermediate between the differences of time respectively preceding and
following each. To contract everything into a few words: today God sees Adam’s futurity to be past;
today he sees both the presence and the pastness of future things to be future. Nor is this
remarkable, for a thing’s futurity is spoken of with respect to the present time, and a thing’s
pastness is also spoken of with respect to the present time. The former has a place before the thing
has begun to exist in time, the latter after it has ceased to exist in time. Penottus adds the
crowning point: “If anyone shrinks from admitting that divine knowledge depends upon a creature
according to those relations, it can be said that, although divine knowledge does not depend upon a
creature, the being of the creature itself is nevertheless a condition necessarily required in
order that the creature can be the terminus of certain and infallible divine knowledge.” “And this,”
he says, “will be to say the same thing as some more recent writers say so that they may not seem to
agree with Molina.” But in my judgment these things are advanced with too much license, since the
being of the creature as an object of divine cognition is manifold: namely, either future being,
present being, or past being. Accordingly, God’s certain and infallible knowledge also bears itself
in manifold ways. For God knows certainly and infallibly that things will be future; likewise, God
knows certainly and infallibly that things are present; finally, God knows certainly and infallibly
that things are past. All these, however, are reduced to one. For to know a thing for a definite
time, or to know that its existence will be joined to a definite difference of time, is both to know
that the same thing will be future until that time arrives, will be present as soon as that time
arrives, and will be past after that time has elapsed. In accordance with his infinite knowledge,
God must know all these things, provided they are knowable in this genus. But if it is asked how they
become knowable in this genus when by their nature they are only possible, nothing precise can be
answered here unless recourse is made to the determination of the divine will. For from it those
things which by their nature had been only possible and purely indifferent both to not existing in
the future and to existing become future. And thus God beholds all future things as they are before
God in willed being. But God also beholds them in willed being while they are present. Finally, God
beholds those same things only in willed being after they have become past.
