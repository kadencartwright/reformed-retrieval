QUESTION XV.

CHRIST’S DESCENT TO HELL.

*Was Christ’s soul taken to Paradise immediately after its separation from
the body? Or did it descend locally to hell? We affirm the former and deny the
latter against the Roman Catholics and Lutherans.*

I. The question about Christ’s descent to hell is twofold. One controversy is
with the Roman Catholics and certain Lutherans, who urge Christ’s local
descent. The other is among the orthodox themselves about the true sense of
this article: whether it should be referred to Christ’s spiritual anguish or
to his burial and most abject state under the dominion of death. The latter
will be discussed next; the former must now be examined.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The Roman Catholics maintain that Christ’s soul, once separated from the
body, immediately descended locally to hell until the resurrection. The
Tridentine Catechism proposes for belief that “when Christ had died, his soul
descended to hell and remained there as long as his body was in the tomb.”
And lest this be understood as done only by virtue and power and not by
essence, as Durandus thought, it adds, “It must be believed altogether that
the soul itself descended to hell in reality and presence.” They hold that it
descended there to free the souls of the fathers of the Old Testament detained
in limbo and carry them with itself into heaven.

III. The Lutherans approach them by asserting Christ’s substantial descent to
hell, not only into limbo but into the very place of the damned, in order to
display his victory and exhibit his triumph there. Thus they wish it to be
referred to the state of exaltation and not the state of emptying, as
Brochmannus maintains in section 14, questions 6 and 7.

IV. Hence the question arises: Did Christ descend locally to hell, either only
to the limbo of the fathers and purgatory to bring out the souls of the godly,
or to the very place of the damned to display his victory? The adversaries
would affirm this; we deny it.

V. First, Christ’s soul passed into Paradise immediately after its release
from the body, according to the promise made to the thief in Luke 23:43:
“Today you will be with me in Paradise.” In vain: 1. The adversaries would
have these words understood only of the deity, with which the thief’s soul was
to be in Paradise. For they involve the future presence in Paradise, or the
translation, not only of the thief’s soul but also of Christ’s, so that just as
Christ and the thief truly suffered, they would also be transferred together
into heaven, and both conditions would be common to both. Thus they are words
of consolation not only in relation to the thief but also to the Lord himself,
that both would soon emerge from these straits.

[Printed p. 314 | Internet Archive PDF 342 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

In this way they would be together in heaven with respect to their souls just
as they had been together on the cross with respect to their bodies. Hence
Cyprian says in *On the Passion,* “The thief was made a participant in the
kingdom; through confession he was made a colleague in martyrdom.” The words
themselves prove this. Christ does not say, “I will be with you,” which would
have designated the presence of the deity in Paradise, but “you will be with
me,” promising him fellowship with his humanity. Fellowship with Christ in
his kingdom is promised. But this cannot be understood of the deity, which
grants fellowship in the kingdom to no one, but of Christ θεανθρώπῳ, *the
God-man,* who calls believers into communion with his kingdom. 2. Others fare
no better when they wish the word *today* to be referred to Christ’s speech,
not to the ascent into Paradise, so that the sense is, “Today I tell you that
you will be with me in Paradise.” As Suarez rightly observes, this is evasion,
not interpretation. There was no need for Christ to indicate what the
present-tense verb and Christ’s speech itself sufficiently indicated. Rather,
he wills to raise up by this consolation the thief who was placed in agony and
longing for Christ’s grace, because his petition would be fulfilled that very
day. 3. Thomas also gratuitously imagines in part 3, question 12, article 3,
that “Paradise here generally denotes the place of blessedness, wherever it
may be, in which all who enjoy divine glory are said to be. Hence the thief
was with Christ in hell as to place but in Paradise as to reward, so that
Paradise is wherever Christ is and from wherever God is seen.” But what is
this except to mingle heaven not only with earth but also with hell? Thus the
thief would already have been in Paradise on the cross, because he was there
with Christ. Yet Christ speaks of the Paradise where he was not then. Further,
Paradise must be understood no differently than Scripture speaks of it
elsewhere, as the abode of the blessed (2 Cor. 12:4; Rev. 2:7), which
Bellarmine acknowledges in controversy 7, book 1, chapter 3, and Suarez on
Thomas 3, question 52. The matter itself proves this, because Christ’s promise
must answer the thief’s petition, “Remember me when you come into your
kingdom,” to which Christ responds, “Today you will be with me in Paradise,”
that is, in my kingdom.

VI. Second, Christ’s soul was in the Father’s hand (Luke 23:46): “Father, into
your hands I commit my spirit.” Therefore, it was not in hell. In the style of
Scripture, the Father’s hand must be understood not with respect to power,
according to which God’s hand is everywhere (Ps. 139), nor as the terrible
hand of God the Judge, but as the consoling hand of the merciful Father with
respect to grace and glory, or the condition of the blessed, which is
customarily described as being in God’s hand. This is confirmed because by
this phrase Christ wished to indicate that nothing remained for him to do,
either with respect to freeing others or enduring new torments. Rather, just
as his body was to have its sabbath in the tomb, so also his soul was to rest
in heaven from all its labors and be flooded with the greatest joys. For τὸ,
*the act of commending or laying down the soul,* bears a relation to the
labors which have gone before. Christ could not have said, “I
commit my spirit,” if after death he was still to descend into hell and
undergo the most grievous labors. 3. He commends his soul into the Father’s
hands in the same way David and Stephen commended their souls, because these
very words were David’s before (Ps. 31:5), from whom Christ borrows them, and
Stephen’s afterward (Acts 7:59), who imitated Christ himself. Indeed, it is
also how Peter in chapter 4, verse 19, commands all believers to commend their
souls to the faithful Creator—not, certainly, so that they might descend into
hell, but so that they might be received into heaven, as the Ordinary Gloss
has it: “Into your hands I commend my spirit, so that you may receive it as it
departs from the body.” Hence among the fathers the Father’s hands and hell are
opposed. Cyril of Alexandria says in the homily *On the Soul’s Departure,*
“The innocent are above and the guilty below; the innocent in heaven and the
guilty in the deep; the innocent in God’s hand and the guilty in the devil’s
hand.”

VII. Third, if Christ according to his soul truly and locally descended

[Printed p. 315 | Internet Archive PDF 343 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to hell, he did so either to suffer something there, to free the fathers, to
preach the gospel to the dead, or to display his victory to the devils. But
the first cannot be said, because he completed all things on the cross (John
19:30) and by one offering perfected forever those who are sanctified (Heb.
10:14). Nor the second, because they had already been received into heaven and
had never been in the fictitious limbo, as was proved above. Nor the third,
because the preaching of the gospel pertains only to the state of this life,
not to the condition of death. If Peter in chapter 4, verse 6, says that *the
gospel was preached to the dead,* this must not be understood in the composite
sense, as though it was preached to them as dead, because they are not in the
state of the way and no longer need any proclamation, but in the divided
sense, that is, to those who are now dead but were once living when the gospel
was preached to them. Nor the fourth, because this descent had to be penal,
not triumphal, and pertains to the state of emptying, not exaltation.

VIII. Various considerations teach that Christ’s descent was of this kind. 1.
In the style of Scripture, descent to hell signifies the most grievous
adversities and most exquisite pains (Gen. 37:35; Job 14:13; Ps. 6:5; 86:13;
130:1). 2. The passages which treat the descent to hell denote his extreme
misery, not triumph, in which he is not therefore to be left but freed by the
Father (Acts 2:30–31). 3. The descent into the lowest parts of the earth is
opposed to the ascent above all the heavens, which is a part of exaltation
(Eph. 4:9). Therefore, it must be a part of emptying.

IX. In Hebrew usage *the heart of the earth* signifies nothing other than
what is within the earth. For לב, *heart,* is put for תוך, *the midst,* and
what is internal is often called the midst, whether it is at the exact center
or not. In Ezekiel 27:4 the boundaries of Tyre are said to have been *in the
heart of the sea* because it was washed by the sea on every side, and in
Deuteronomy 4:11 the mountain is said to burn *to the heart of heaven,* that
is, to the middle region of the air. Thus to be *in the heart of the earth*
(Matt. 12:40) denotes nothing other than being within the earth, whether
nearer to or further from its surface. In this way is indicated the state of
Christ’s body in the tomb, which was in the earth and in which it rested until
the third day.

X. When Christ is said to have descended “εἰς τὰ κατώτερα μέρη τῆς γῆς, *into
the lower parts of the earth*” (Eph. 4:9), a local descent is not denoted, but
his emptying and manifestation in the flesh which he assumed on earth, so that
it is nothing other than descending to the earth, which is the lowest part of
the world. This is a construction quite familiar to the Hebrews, in which a
governing expression is put for an apposition, as in Psalm 139:15, “in the
lowest parts of the earth,” that is, in the earth, which is the lower part in
relation to heaven. Thus the parts of the earth are not compared with one
another, but the parts of the world, heaven and earth, are compared. The words
of the text intimate that there can be no other sense, for the opposition is
between ascent from earth into heaven and descent from heaven. Just as ascent
has earth as the point from which and heaven as the point to which, so in turn
descent has heaven as the point from which and earth as the point to which.
Cajetan saw this, wishing “the lower parts of the earth” to be understood as
“the earth, which is the lowest part of the world, in distinction from the
lower parts of heaven which are in the air. It is as if he had said more
plainly, ‘Because he first descended to the lower part, the earth.’”

XI. When Peter says in Acts 2 from Psalm 16 that “Christ’s soul was not to be
left in hell,” a local descent cannot be understood, but detention in the
tomb, because Peter refers this to the resurrection. This is gathered: 1. From
the connection: “My flesh,” he says, “will rest,” namely in the tomb, “in hope,
because you will not abandon it.” 2. From the phrase added by way of
explanation: “You will not permit your Holy One,” that is, me, “to see
corruption,” namely in the body, which otherwise, if left in the tomb, he would
experience.

[Printed p. 316 | Internet Archive PDF 344 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

3. From comparison with Acts 13:34–35, where God is said to have raised Christ
from the dead so that he would not see corruption. Neither is the word ψυχῆς,
*soul,* an objection. As Emmanuel Sa notes in *Phrases of Scripture,* by a
synecdoche of the part for the whole it is put for the whole person, as not
infrequently elsewhere (Ps. 3:2; 17:13; Acts 7:14), or by the part for the part
it is put for the body itself (Lev. 19:28; 21:1, 11; Num. 5:1; Luke 6:9), as
Virgil says in *Aeneid* 3, “We lay the soul in the tomb.” Nor is the word
ᾅδης, *hades,* an objection, for it is often taken for the tomb, as will be
proved below.

XII. Peter’s passage in 1 Peter 3:19, where “Christ, having gone in the Spirit,”
is said “to have preached to the spirits τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ, *in prison,*” does not
favor a local descent into hell. 1. Peter does not speak of the soul, but of
the Spirit. Therefore, it cannot be understood of any descent of the soul.
That Spirit cannot be taken here for the soul but for the deity is gathered
from the preceding verse. No other Spirit is understood than that by which he
was made alive, which cannot be said of the soul, either subjectively, because
only what can die is made alive, which does not befall the soul, or
efficiently, because making alive is a work of infinite power. Therefore, the
deity itself must necessarily be understood, which is designated in this way
more than once elsewhere (Rom. 1:4; Heb. 9:14; 1 Tim. 3:16), where he is said
to have been *justified in the Spirit,* which Cajetan, Gagnaeus, Thomas,
Lyranus, and others understand of the deity and the Holy Spirit. 2. The
passage concerns spirits ἀπειθήσασι, *disobedient,* who did not obey good
admonitions. They cannot be called the fathers whom the Roman Catholics hold
Christ led into heaven after their detention in limbo. Nor can it be said that
they were indeed unbelieving at first but afterward repented, because this is
not in the text and is rather opposed to it: only eight are said to have been
saved and the rest to have perished. If some had repented, Peter would not
call them ἀπειθεῖς, *disobedient.* 3. There is no mention here of liberation,
but only of preaching. 4. Φυλακή, *guard or prison,* of which the passage
speaks, is taken in only two ways in Scripture: either for a night watch or
for the prison in which the guilty are detained (Luke 3:20). Since it cannot
be used here in the former sense, it must necessarily be taken for prison, as
the Interlinear Gloss says, “a prison of darkness and unbelief.” But nowhere
in Scripture is any place in which blessed spirits are confined called a
prison. 5. The preaching is not said to have been made to spirits existing in
prison as though they were in prison at the time of preaching—for why would
it occur, since there is no return from it? Rather, it is said to have been
made long ago in Noah’s time, when God’s patience waited for men, to those who
at the present time when Peter writes are in prison. Hence Peter does not say
ἐκήρυξε τοῖς πνεύμασι ἐν φυλακῇ, “he preached to the spirits in prison,” but
τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ πνεύμασι ἐκήρυξε, “to the spirits in prison he preached,” so
that the substantive verb must necessarily be supplied. It is not as the
Vulgate renders it, “those who *were* in prison,” as though they were in prison
at the time of the preaching, but “those who *are,*” τοῖς οὖσι, at the time
when the apostle was writing. For Peter joins ποτέ, *once,* not with the words
ἐν φυλακῇ, *in prison,* but with ἀπειθήσασι, *disobedient,* in this manner:
τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ πνεύμασι ἀπειθήσασι ποτέ, “to the spirits in prison who once
were disobedient,” clearly distinguishing the times in which they were
rebellious in Noah’s days and in which they were thrust into prison because of
their rebellion. Thus the plain sense of the passage is as our Beza most
successfully expounded it: “Christ, whom I said was made alive by the Spirit,
having gone—not by a change of place, but by a certain singular testimony of
his presence through his revelation and operation, just as God is often said
to come in Scripture not literally but figuratively and metaphorically; not in
the body, which he had not yet assumed, but in that very Spirit or divine power
by which he rose and was made alive and by which the inspired prophets spoke
(1 Pet. 1:11)—preached to those spirits who are now in the prison of hell,
where they pay the deserved punishments of the rebellion which they displayed
toward his preaching in Noah’s time.” Andradius saw this in book 2 of the
*Defense of Trent,* calling this the plain sense of the passage: “In this
Spirit he himself, coming long ago, preached to those spirits who now in prison
pay the deserved punishments of their unbelief, for they never wished to have

faith in Noah as he rightly admonished them and built the ark at God’s
command.”

XIII. If Christ is said in Acts 2:24 to have been *released through the
resurrection from the pains of death,* it does not follow that he sustained
pains until the moment of resurrection and that his soul went into hell where
it could be affected by such pains. The passage can be understood in two ways.
1. By the grammatical figure ἓν διὰ δυοῖν, *hendiadys,* “the pains of death”
can be put for a painful death, as in Matthew 3:11 Christ is said to baptize
with Spirit and fire, that is, spiritual fire, and in Virgil, *Aeneid* 1, “I
sing of arms and the man,” that is, an armed man. For it is certain that
Christ’s death was conjoined with the most exquisite pains, and that this death
was not loosed until the moment of resurrection. 2. The passage to which Peter
alludes, Psalm 18:5, uses the expression חבלי מות, *cords of death,* which
properly denotes the ropes and bonds by which a man is held as a captive in
death and from which he is released through resurrection. Hence there is no
need to imagine any passion of the soul after death. Yet Peter, following the
Septuagint, retains the word ὠδῖνας, *pains,* which can connote both the
torments which he suffered in death and the bonds of death by which he was in
some manner bound in the tomb. Thus the sense is, “God raised him, namely
Christ, by loosing the bonds of death, since it was not possible for him to be
κρατεῖσθαι, *held,* by it forever like a captive.”

XIV. The triumphal song which Paul sings after Hosea in 1 Corinthians
15:54–55 is rightly referred to Christ’s resurrection, by which he began to
carry off victory over sin, death, and hell. But it cannot pertain to the
descent to hell, which was the final degree of his emptying, in which he seemed
almost to be swallowed up by death.
