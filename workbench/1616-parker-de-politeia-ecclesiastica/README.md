# Robert Parker, *De politeia ecclesiastica* (1616/1638)

> **Translation preparation:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,
> diplomatic-normalized Latin transcription and a close English translation.
> Neither has been independently reviewed. Both must be checked by a qualified

## Research-inventory identification

This is the Robert Parker item recorded in
[`research/untranslated-latin-treatises.md`](../../research/untranslated-latin-treatises.md):
the posthumous *De politeia ecclesiastica Christi, et hierarchica opposita,
libri tres* (Frankfurt, Godefrid Basson, 1616), with the later two-book 1638
witness flagged there for collation rather than silently treated as an
equivalent edition. No complete English translation was identified in the
inventory.

## Edition decision and explicit collation caveat

Parts 01–16 are controlled by the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek copy of *Roberti
Parkeri De politeia ecclesiastica libri duo* (1638), digitized as
[`bsb10507786`](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb10507786). Its
[IIIF manifest](https://api.digitale-sammlungen.de/iiif/presentation/v2/bsb10507786/manifest)
provides stable, complete images through the present stopping point. Page
images, not the BSB OCR, govern the transcription.

This is a **local control decision for the present parts**, not an edition-level
claim. The 1616 witness is a three-book publication (`[18], 368, 456` pages),
whereas the 1638 title explicitly announces two books. The two Google copies
of 1616 consulted—[`VgoNE2Lg9RUC`](https://books.google.com/books?id=VgoNE2Lg9RUC)
and [`hQc4mb6DDMQC`](https://books.google.com/books?id=hQc4mb6DDMQC)—do not
supply a clean, continuous opening: printed pages 1–2 were not available in
the inspected opening sequences, and one sequence duplicates opening images.
A page-by-page sample collation of the available 1616 pages 3–21 against the
1638 pages 3–21 found the same chapter order and argument, with compositorial
spelling, punctuation, and setting differences but no intentionally adopted
substantive variant in this unit. That sample does **not** establish that 1638
is a duplicate, revision, continuation, or replacement of 1616. Every later
unit requires renewed collation, and the two-book 1638 witness cannot control
the third book unique to the 1616 publication.

Parts 02–16 continue under the provisional 1638 control decision but have not
been represented as a renewed full collation against 1616. Their wording must
therefore not be used to infer agreement between the editions. The current work
remains within Book I, which is present in the 1638 witness.

## Part 01: exact authorial unit and source map

Part 01 contains Book I, chapters 1–7: Parker's definition of ecclesiastical
polity followed by the complete first branch of its “excellence,” namely its
dignity as part of Christ's kingdom. It begins at the book title on printed
page 1 and ends with Parker's explicit *finis esto* on the upper portion of
printed page 21, immediately before `CAP. 8. De Disciplina Ecclesiastica
utilitate`.

The unit crosses twenty-one printed page labels but occupies approximately
twenty pages of authorial space: page 1 includes the book and chapter display,
and page 21 is shared with the excluded chapter 8. Stopping at printed page 20
would cut Parker's seventh argument and his concluding sentence; stopping at
chapter 6 would leave his refutation without his positive case. The
chapter-defined endpoint is therefore the exact coherent boundary.

| Chapter 2, polity as part of Christ's kingdom | 3–6 (upper) | 11–14 |

| Chapter 4, opponents' hidden motives | 8–10 | 16–18 |
| Chapter 5, Bancroft's arguments | 11–14 (upper) | 19–22 |
| Chapter 6, Bilson's arguments | 14–16 | 22–23, 28 |
| Chapter 7, Parker's positive arguments | 17–21 (upper) | 29–33 |

The BSB sequence contains duplicate captures between printed pages 15 and 16:
canvases 24–27 repeat the textual leaves represented by canvases 22–23 and
28–29. They are excluded; no authorial text is omitted. Source markers use
the retained canvas sequence and are identical in the paired files.

Part 04 treats the second and third alleged delays to reform, identifies
self-satisfaction as the third delay, and develops Parker's first two defects
in the English reformation: lack of a scriptural rule and failure to conform to
the primitive Church. Printed page 63 is shared with the excluded chapter 25;
the source marker is retained in both paired files, but no text of chapter 25 is
included in Part 04. Part 05 resumes with that heading and develops the next
three defects: retention of Roman leaven, conformity of ecclesiastical polity
to civil polity, and failure to reform the source of the Church's evils. It ends
at the complete chapter 27 boundary.

In the Part 05 sequence, the visible printed numerals `71` and `70` occur in
that order on BSB canvases 80 and 81. The prose and catchword sequence is
continuous in that same canvas order, so the paired files preserve the textual
sequence and identify the transposed visible numerals explicitly in otherwise
identical source markers. No text is reordered to make the numerals ascend.

Part 06 develops Parker's sixth and last defect in the English reformation:
the Hierarchists' attack on the discipline of the reformed Churches. It answers
the claim that most reformed Churches are governed by bishops or equivalent
superintendents, distinguishes German superintendents from English bishops,
and treats Lutheran, Swiss, French, and Belgian examples. Chapter 29 begins on
the lower portion of printed page 85 and is excluded. The source marker for
that shared page is retained in both paired files, but no text from chapter 29
is included.

Part 07 resumes with the exact chapter 29 heading on the lower portion of
printed page 85 and completes the opponents’ second and third objections in
chapters 29–30. Chapter 29 argues that England must take seriously the example
of the reformed Churches; chapter 30 answers the claim that reformed discipline
was introduced perversely, distinguishing Geneva’s Evangelical preaching from
its later ecclesiastical settlement. Chapter 31 begins on the lower portion of
printed page 90 and is excluded. The source marker for that shared page is
retained in both paired files, but no text from chapter 31 is included.

Part 08 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 31. Reformatio Genevensis a Calumnijs vindicatur.`; under the standing
`i/j` practice the normalized text reads
`CAP. 31. Reformatio Genevensis a Calumniis vindicatur.` Chapter 31 answers
charges that Calvin used resistance and cunning to establish Geneva's
ecclesiastical polity. Chapter 32 extends the vindication to the other reformed
Churches, answering the claim that they adopted presbyterial discipline through
craftiness or `κακοζηλία` and treating England and Scotland in particular.
Chapter 33 begins on the lower portion of printed page 96 and is excluded. The
source marker for that shared page is retained in both paired files, but no text
from chapter 33 is included.

Part 09 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 33. Quarta Calumnia adversariorum, disciplinam esse malorum fontem,
diluitur.` Chapter 33 answers the charge that reformed discipline is the source
of ecclesiastical evils. After rebutting the general accusation, Parker treats
six alleged stains in sequence: Korahism; Novatianism, Donatism, and Puritanism;
Aerianism; Acephalism; Anabaptism; and Papism. Chapter 34 begins on the lower
portion of printed page 106 and is excluded. The source marker for that shared
page is retained in both paired files, but no text from chapter 34 is included.

Part 10 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 34. Ex præmissis infert, primò Magistratuum esse disciplinam
restituere.` Under the standing normalization practice the transcription reads
`CAP. 34. Ex praemissis infert, primo Magistratuum esse disciplinam
restituere.` Chapter 34 draws the first corollary from the preceding defense of
reformed discipline: magistrates themselves must restore it rather than
delegate the whole work to bishops. Chapter 35 addresses the bishops and their
supporters, arguing that they must cease defending episcopacy by possession
alone lest their conduct prove obstinate, impatient, proud, destructive, and
persecuting. Chapter 36 begins on the lower portion of printed page 113 and is
excluded. The source marker for that shared page is retained in both paired
files, but no text from chapter 36 is included.

Part 11 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 36. Porisma tertium; Episcopis de possessione cedendum esse.` Chapter 36
continues Parker's address to the English bishops: after requiring them to stop
contending by possession in chapter 35, he now argues that they must yield that
possession for the Church's peace. Gregory Nazianzen, Hierax, John of Beverley,
and Gregory the Great supply the principal examples. Chapter 37 begins on the
next printed page, 117, and is excluded; the new part therefore ends at the
complete chapter 36 boundary on printed page 116.

Part 12 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 37. Porisma quartum; Episcopis non Puritanis dissidium Anglicanum
imputandum esse.` Chapter 37 argues that England's ecclesiastical dissension
must be imputed to the bishops who persecute reforming ministers rather than to
the Puritans who remain within the Church. Parker first contrasts the ministers
with separatists, then places their sufferings in a long historical succession
from Audius and Jerome through the Waldenses, John of Salisbury, the Bangor
monks, Robert of Lincoln, and later witnesses. Chapter 38 begins on the lower
portion of printed page 125 and is excluded. Both paired files retain the
shared-page source marker and stop immediately before its heading.

Part 13 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 38. Maculae generales diluuntur.` Chapter 38 answers three general
objections to the English ministers' cause: that civil and ecclesiastical
tribunals have condemned them, that their controversy concerns a slight
matter, and that they suffer at the hands of fellow Christians rather than
pagans. It ends on the upper portion of printed page 127 by declaring that the
suffering ministers should be praised and crowned rather than condemned and
punished. The conclusion visibly reads `porisma tertium` in the control
witness; it has not been silently changed, although chapter 39 is headed
`Porisma quintum`. Chapter 39 begins on the lower portion of that shared page
and is excluded. Both paired files retain the shared-page source marker and
stop immediately before its heading.

Part 14 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 39. Porisma quintum, fratribus non esse silendum in causa disciplinae,
etsi Episcopi ea conditione conniverent.` Chapter 39 addresses ministers who
secure episcopal toleration by promising silence about reformed discipline.
Parker first questions the lawfulness and spiritual dangers of that bargain,
then argues from gratitude, the integrity of the Gospel, fidelity, resistance
to episcopal stratagems, love for the Church, ecclesiastical liberty, and
solidarity with fellow ministers in conflict. The opening prose visibly begins
`QUARTUM`, continuing an argument sequence from the preceding context; later
the chapter visibly repeats `Quinto` and prints `5. Sexto`, all of which are
preserved rather than silently renumbered. Chapter 40 begins on the lower
portion of printed page 136 and is excluded. Both paired files retain the
shared-page source marker and stop immediately before its heading.

Part 15 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 40. Rationes spondentium silentium tolluntur.` Its prose begins visibly
with `PRIMO illud Salomonis citare soletis, tempus silendi perinde ac loquendi
esse: Eccles. 3.7.` Chapter 40 answers four defenses of the ministers' promised
silence: Solomon's distinction between times for silence and speech; Paul's
conduct toward Ephesian idolatry; the claim that silence preserves an
opportunity to preach the Gospel; and the charge that preaching discipline is
fruitless labor. The witness visibly cites `Isai. cap. 93.5`, which is preserved
rather than silently corrected. The pair contains four identical source
markers for printed pages 136–139 / BSB canvases 146–149. Chapter 41 begins on
the lower portion of printed page 139 and is excluded; both paired files retain
the shared-page source marker and stop immediately before its heading.

Part 16 resumes with the exact printed heading
`CAP. 41. Porisma sextum, notitia disciplinae est populo necessaria.` Chapter
41 argues in six stages that the English people need knowledge of reformed
discipline: for the sanctification of understanding, repentance, duties of
piety, informed obedience, works of charity, and their own participation in
ecclesiastical affairs. The text visibly cites `Luc. 25:5`, begins its fourth
stage with the solecistic `ab obedientiam`, and prints `3. Quinto` before the
fifth stage; these readings are preserved rather than silently repaired. A
horizontal defect obscures part of one line on printed page 143 in the BSB
control image. The corresponding passage in the 1616 Google witness
`VgoNE2Lg9RUC` was checked only to confirm the obscured words; this narrowly
reported check is not a renewed full collation. Chapter 42 begins on the lower
portion of printed page 144 and is excluded. Both paired files retain the
shared-page source marker and stop immediately before its heading.

## Editorial method and apparatus

Latin long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are
normalized. Substantive wording and spelling are retained. Running heads,
signatures, catchwords, and bare page numbers are omitted from prose but
represented by source markers. Printed headings, numbered arguments, Greek,
biblical citations, quotations, and discernible marginal references are
retained. Marginal matter is placed in square-bracketed apparatus notes near
the text it annotates; expanded or uncertain bibliographical forms are not
silently invented.

The English is a full close translation of the paired Latin, not a summary.
Names and polemical labels are retained; *politeia* is normally rendered
“polity,” *regimen* “government,” and *administri* “ministers” or
“administrators” according to context. Square-bracketed apparatus is
translated or explained but not promoted into Parker's prose.
