# Laelius Socinus to John Calvin

Letter 1231, 25 July 1549

This is a new English translation from the normalized Latin transcription of *Calvini Opera* XIII, 337–340. It is an AI-assisted draft checked against the printed page images, not an independently reviewed critical translation. The 1875 editors warn that Socinus’s Latin is highly involved and that they supplied all punctuation; the notes below identify clauses in which the English consequently remains uncertain.

[CO XIII, p. 337]

To the most distinguished Master John Calvin, most vigilant bishop among the Genevans, always most highly esteemed.

Geneva.

I give you immense thanks for not having answered thus far—not because your answer would not have been most welcome to me, but because it will now be all the more welcome, the more ardently I burn with desire to read yours.[^1] Driven by that desire, I am now compelled to ask you, and to entreat you in every way, that you deign to reply at once, as soon as your many duties permit. Otherwise, just as my longings cannot grow any further, so, if I must wait long, I shall necessarily fall into some serious illness. I know that you would most gladly free me and everyone from every affliction whenever it were granted you; and since you can forestall any affliction from striking me, shall I doubt whether you will go to meet it? Your love is so long and so broad that it most kindly embraces not only friends who are present and from your own country, but also foreigners who are very far away, and even enemies, and wishes to provide well for everyone to the best of its ability. And shall I—absent, certainly, but not very far away, a foreigner but a friend, indeed one most devoted to you—not obtain help after many entreaties, though it has been promised more than once? I am therefore fully confident that in this too, as in many other matters, you will surpass my expectation.

But so that you may do this with still greater credit, I expect you most graciously to answer not only my earlier requests (you have a copy of them on the other side of the sheet, in case that first one should at some point have been lost), but also those that I will append shortly. For although we must continually and reverently handle all matters of this kind, chiefly with a view to the greater glory of God—and in what way, I ask, is that glory rendered more illustrious than if, paying deeper attention to God’s works and oracles, we grasp more exactly how magnificently things have been promised to us?—yet what could be more excellent in the meantime, or what could fail to be directed to this end: that one day, made most blessed, we should render eternal glory to the author of ourselves and, in short, of every good?

But now the question is who we are—or rather, what we will be, and of what things we will consist. Will our soul alone exist in that blessedness, of which it becomes a participant as soon as it flies out of this prison? Or will this body also, although endowed with a different nature, likewise exist one day? For not a few passages of Scripture seem to confirm the latter, while reason seems entirely to confirm the former. I do not say this because I hold the opinion that nothing contrary to reason should be believed, but so that, as in many matters, the weak may be helped in this one too.[^7] It is hard, I admit, for one rightly instructed in the way of the Lord not to place simple faith in the Word of God. But in turn it is no less hard to persuade oneself that impossibilities will occur, and to force the will to

[CO XIII, p. 338]

desire what the understanding continually suggests will never occur. This is not because the mind is otherwise badly disposed or wishes to detract anything from divine power. Rather, it is because Scripture is thought, when it promises the resurrection of bodies so magnificently and repeatedly sets our hope almost before our eyes by other anthropopathic expressions, to have aimed at nothing other than rendering that indescribable blessedness—and indeed ourselves, otherwise invisible and incomprehensible to our own selves—almost perceptible to the senses by portraying them in a certain fleshly manner. This would last until, endowed with a more perfect light, we see ourselves and God himself in Christ as he is “bodily,” that is, truly and not in shadow.

Hence also the saying: “I appoint you a kingdom, so that you may eat and drink and sit upon thrones,” and so forth. And what would be absurd if it were believed that we will have no more need of bodies than those bodies themselves will have of food and drink and of the other conditions equally promised for their being suffused with every happiness?[^2] I entertain all these thoughts with trembling: just as they are uncertain and suspect, if not dreadful, to me, so they are entrusted to you alone, most prudent man. I want you to render them more certain and secure. Accordingly, show me the matter of this hand by whose work I write to you, and of this whole body to which this arm is attached: by what means can it, at one and the same time, be restored to and benefit me and the others of whom it was and will be the substrate?[^3] Or, if a body will be fitted to us from somewhere else, demonstrate where divine justice, where resurrection, and where that transformation of the lowly body will be. For then, unless I am mistaken, we would have to expect an exchange of bodies, not of the body’s qualities, and a kind of *metempsychosis*, not a return to the same dwelling. If this is so, I ask and beg you again and again to lead me to the truth. Fare most happily. Basel, in the house of Master Münster, 25 July 1549.

Your most devoted,

Laelius Sozinus

The question is whether marriage with a papist must be avoided just as much as marriage with a Jewish woman. There is a man who thinks he may marry a woman who indeed places all righteousness in Christ and is not intractable, but who is still held by other superstitions, falls down before idols, and so forth. Another denies that she may be married, even if she agrees in the whole body of doctrine, until, by a public confession or some other manifest approval of the truth, she sends Antichrist away and joins herself to Christ’s church—just as it is forbidden to contract marriage with a Jewish woman, even one who thinks rightly in all matters, until she becomes known through baptism.[^4]

Another man is deeply perplexed. Although he is otherwise free to

[CO XIII, p. 339]

live devoutly, he asks whether, because he is compelled to present an infant, he must abandon everything, or whether he may instead provide for his son and the rest of his household at the cost of some contamination of himself. This is especially pressing since Melanchthon, whom you endorse, favors this, and Peter Martyr likewise concedes it, provided they detest the things that must be detested. If the man does that, it is surely not baptism but life that will be denied him.

Next, do you regard as valid even a baptism administered in play, since you recognize the efficacy of the papists’ baptism? They suppose a person to have been baptized through what is not merely play but blasphemy—through their exorcisms and incantations and so forth—not by God’s power and not by the word, except perhaps insofar as they dream that some peculiar power is enclosed in those words.[^5] You undoubtedly do not attribute such power to those words. Otherwise, you would not rebaptize someone who had once been baptized with those same words, in the utmost simplicity and good faith, by a private friend or separately by his parents. For you say—and say very well—that the action is to be celebrated in the sight of the church. If you deny that this action takes place in the house of even the most faithful man, much less will it take place in a gathering of idolaters. To these points I add this one thing:

**Addition.** When a Turk is baptized, do you judge that his entire household staff should likewise be baptized?[^8] And if it is not permissible to imitate circumcision in this case, why should it be permissible in the case of sons who were circumcised with no better right?

As for the Mass, they say first that the approval inferred from our presence is blameless,[^6] unless you also condemn a person who does not in fact attend Masses, yet lives so cautiously that he is regarded as one of their approvers, and unless you require him, whenever conversation turns to the Mass, immediately and openly to curse it, even with the gravest danger looming and no hope at all appearing. As for the offense to the weak that you press so strongly, and in the absence of which Paul seems even to have permitted the eating of food offered to idols, they reply as follows and think that they have thus escaped rather cleverly:

“Look, I know with complete certainty,” one says, “that absolutely no weak person is present who could be led by my example to worship idols. On the contrary, everyone thinks that I came solely for pleasure, so that I might have people with whom I could chat and could walk about for a while—and this practice is certainly all too common among us. Not one suspects that I came in order somehow to approve what most of them do not believe in the least. Rather, for the sake of exercise they assemble in this way once a week and dance. Indeed, my companions would laugh if they could perceive that in my heart I placed even the slightest faith in such trifles. They are so well instructed that there are few who are even persuaded that God exists.”

They contend that attendance of this sort ought especially to be permitted to foreigners who, although they have testified to their faith in more than one place, are nevertheless compelled by necessity—as they themselves allege—to stand once or twice at that abominable spectacle in a foreign country. But it is plainly the sort of necessity by which they prefer their own interests to Christ’s. There would be no need for so many questions if, as is fitting, we had resolved to undergo any kind of

[CO XIII, p. 340]

punishment before offending our most excellent Father in even the smallest degree. Nevertheless, please write back; I beg you not to frustrate my hope.

## Translator’s notes

[^1]: The Latin has *legendi tuas* (“to read yours”), with the feminine noun *literas* (“letters”) understood.

[^2]: The likely antecedent of *ipsa* is *corpora* (“the bodies themselves”). Because Socinus’s syntax is compressed, the precise relation of the final purpose clause (*ut sint omni felicitate perfusa*) to the promised “conditions” is not certain.

[^3]: The clause *quorum fuit et erit subiectum* is grammatically tangled. The translation takes neuter *subiectum* as referring back to *corpus* and renders it in the philosophical sense “substrate.” Socinus appears to be posing the familiar problem of matter that belongs successively to more than one human body, but the exact construction is uncertain.

[^4]: Here *Iudaea* is read as the ablative of the feminine noun “Jewish woman,” not as the place-name “Judea.” The final *innotescat* literally means “becomes known”; “[as a Christian]” is implicit in the reference to baptism.

[^5]: Because the autograph had no punctuation, the scope of *credunt* (“they believe” or “suppose”) in this sentence depends on the 1875 editors’ punctuation. The English follows their construction. “What is not merely play but blasphemy” expresses Socinus’s evaluation of the papal rites; it need not mean that papists describe their own actions as blasphemy.

[^6]: *Indemnem* literally means “unharmed” or “without loss.” In this moral argument it appears to mean “innocent” or “blameless,” but the usage is unusual.

[^7]: The printed *succurratur imbecillis* is not standard agreement: classical Latin would expect a dative such as *imbecillibus* for “the weak.” The translation treats *imbecillis* as Socinus’s irregular dative plural, in keeping with the editors’ warning about his Latin.

[^8]: *Famulitium universum* denotes the convert’s collective servants or household staff, not every member of his family; Socinus raises sons separately in the next clause.
