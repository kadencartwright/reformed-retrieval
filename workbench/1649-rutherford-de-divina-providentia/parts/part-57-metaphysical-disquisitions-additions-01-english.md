ADDITIONS

Question. Is there any such conflict in GOD’s will whereby God must be said to
will something which he by no means approves; to approve something which he by
no means wills; to will something by the will of sign which he does not will by
the will of good pleasure; or to will something by the will of good pleasure
which he by no means wills by the will of sign? I answer: I have no idea what
entered the Disputant’s or the Arminians’ mind that led them to impute to us a
conflict or contradiction between the will of good pleasure, which decrees
either effectively or permissively, and the will which we call the will of sign
and approval. For I believe that God wills permissively and decrees many things
by a permissive decree—for example, the crucifixion of GOD’s Son and the sale
of Joseph—which he nevertheless does not approve or will by approving them as
if they conformed to GOD’s law. Indeed, he disapproves, forbids, detests, and
punishes them, and yet permissively decrees their existence or occurrence. If
the Disputant says that such things occur by GOD’s permissive and approving
will—that is, his commanding will—let him sing to his own Muses. I do not think
he will have Protestant theologians concurring with him, for in that case there
would be a conflict between GOD’s wills.

[Marginalia: There is no conflict between GOD’s wills.]

Nor is what the Disputant adds true: *For God to approve something occurring
with his permission is nothing other than for God to approve that permission.*
First, when we use the expression “approving will,” for God to approve something
occurring with his permission is a signal contradiction. It is for God to
approve something as sin, which is for God to command sin; nothing could be
more contradictory. For to us the approving will is the commanding and
promising will, just as the disapproving will is the prohibiting and threatening
will. We use the term in this way from Romans 12:2 and Psalm 5:4.

Second, this also is false: *For God to approve something occurring with his
permission is nothing other than for God to approve that permission.* For if
“approve” is taken for an act of willing or decreeing by the will of good
pleasure, then GOD’s approval bears not only upon the permission but also upon
the permitted thing—namely, sin. For GOD’s will, however it is considered
insofar as it flows from the will of good pleasure, does not stand negatively
toward the permitted thing and approvingly or positively toward the permission
itself. The will of human beings and angels bears in this way upon permitted
sin, yet neither humans nor angels exercise any acts of providence concerning

[Marginalia: The Disputant wrongly says that God approves something while
permitting it.]

[Additions, Part I; printed page 614; IA PDF page 688; BSB canvas 664]

permitted sin. But if “approve” is taken for willing a thing as morally
consonant with GOD’s law, or for willing and approving the morality of the thing
itself, then God is absurdly said to will or approve the permission of sin—as
though some god were to impose upon our infinite God a law which regulated or
morally circumscribed him in permitting or not permitting sins.

Question. Does the Disputant rightly say: *Where the knowledge called simple
intelligence, by which God knows all possible things, is attributed to God,
must a will by which he wills all possible good things be attributed to God on
the same ground? For GOD approves and wills every good, and nothing is in God
potentially which is not in God actually; consequently God can will nothing
which he does not actually will.* I answer: I do not sufficiently grasp the
Disputant’s metaphysics, for, unless I am mistaken, the Disputant reveals
himself as a disciple of Arminius. He maintains that God wills every possible
good. Does God, then, will the obedience and salvation of all reprobate human
beings and evil angels, the repentance of Judas the betrayer, and of thousands
of people capable of being created but never to be created? For these are
possible goods and lie within the power of GOD’s omnipotence. But how did it
happen that all reprobates and demons do not obey? Did God, so far as lay in
him, will the obedience of all the damned? Did he decree it, I ask, by the will
of good pleasure? Did he approve it because he willed it? But was God defeated
in his own will, decree, and intention—that is, in the thing willed, decreed,
and intended by God—by the perversity of creatures? In this matter Arminius and
the Disputant agree against Calvin and our theologians.

[Marginalia: How God actually wills what he is able to will.]

Second, what prevents God from attaining what he wills in thousands of other
worlds, angels, and human beings, since these things are possible within GOD’s
creative omnipotence? Nothing prevents it except GOD’s free will, which willed
all these possible things to be created but did not will them to be created,
because he freely did not will it. Behold what a conflict of wills this would
be! For, if the Disputant’s metaphysics is to be trusted, *whatever God is able
actually to will to occur, he actually wills.* But God wills all possible
things to occur—namely, thousands of additional worlds. Perhaps, however, the
Disputant must be understood as speaking of GOD’s natural will. I certainly
would not resist the view that God has a certain speculative pleasure in his
omnipotence and all the possible things contained in this productive
omnipotence. But this is not the kind of

[Additions, Part I; printed page 615; IA PDF page 689; BSB canvas 665]

free will by which the Arminians dream that God wills all demons and reprobate
human beings to obey God and be saved. For this will has vanished with respect
to the demons and most of the damned, whom God now cannot fail to will to be
damned and to have perished. Thus this inference stands by no metaphysical
consequence: *All possible things are good; therefore God wills all possible
things.* For goodness, as also unity and truth, are properties of created being.
Yet no one would say that all possible things are created beings. They are
creatable and possible, but they are neither real beings nor good, one, true,
or actually existent.

But the Disputant is asked: how does God will future things, and how possible
things? Does he will them by the same will? A future thing adds something to a
possible one. Yet the Disputant believes a future thing is absolutely nothing,
because it is neither God the Creator nor a creature, as he taught above. What,
then, I ask, will a possible thing be? For a possible thing is neither Creator
nor creature. Nevertheless, for the Disputant it is good and therefore willed
by God, because God can will it, and *whatever GOD can will, GOD actually
wills.* But God certainly can will very many future things. He can will all
future things which were predicted by the prophets, such as the incarnation,
birth, miracles, crucifixion, burial, resurrection, and ascension into heaven
of CHRIST, which once were future; and those things which now are future, such
as the last judgment, the resurrection of the dead, the glorification of the
saints, the ruin of Antichrist, and so forth. Did God will and decree these
things by the very same will by which he willed thousands of other worlds and
the obedience and salvation of all reprobates?

[Marginalia: Whether God wills possible things.]

But *if God can will and actually wills whatever he can do,* as the Disputant
says, he can free CHRIST from death by more than twelve legions of angels; he
can raise children for Abraham from stones. Therefore, he actually wills
CHRIST’s deliverance from death and the creation of Abraham’s children from
stones. Yet he does not will CHRIST’s deliverance from death or the raising or
creation of such children. He can make all possible things; therefore, he
actually wills the production of all possible things—namely, myriads of other
worlds. Yet he does not will the creation of all possible things. Are there,
then, contradictory wills in God? The Disputant sees this, and therefore tries
to resolve it as follows: *I speak,* he says, *of an intrinsic act; otherwise,
if the denomination of will is taken from GOD’s extrinsic operation, it could
be said that GOD could have willed something by an effective will*

[Additions, Part I; printed page 616; IA PDF page 690; BSB canvas 666]

*which he does not actually will by an effective will, because he could produce
more things than he wills to produce. But willing and approving something is
plainly and simply intrinsic to God.* I answer: even so the Disputant does not
escape. For this statement holds true in God: *God can will nothing which is
not actually in God,* and consequently, *God can will nothing which he does not
actually will.* For that most blessed God and infinite, most pure actuality is
devoid of all potency or potentiality, which always tastes and smells of
imperfection and an admixture with nonbeing, both in extrinsic and intrinsic
operations. For *whatever God can be, he actually is* in every way; and in
extrinsic operations, *whatever he can will, he actually wills.* For GOD’s will,
as demonstrated above, is most perfect and most actual, determined inwardly
most perfectly by GOD’s infinite wisdom, as it is also determined toward
everything he can produce or will outwardly. Yet he never produces and never
actually wills many things of this kind, because by a perfect act God
determinately and positively decreed and established from eternity not to
produce all the things which he never produces. Nothing external determines,
circumscribes, or either efficiently or objectively actualizes the power of
willing or producing in God, though this happens to us wretched creatures in
both intrinsic and extrinsic operations.

[Marginalia: That GOD’s will is determined from eternity both in intrinsic and
extrinsic actions.]

Accordingly, GOD’s will was most actually determined inwardly by his infinite
wisdom toward all the things which he can create but which, from infinite
wisdom, he actually does not create and does not will to create—or whose
noncreation he positively and most actually decreed. Yet he can create them,
or could have created and willed them, if infinite wisdom had so deemed from
eternity. No new determination or new spark of reason can shine upon God by
which he might today be determined to will something or create something
outside himself toward the willing or positive nonwilling, creating or not
creating, of which he was not most actually and perfectly determined by his
infinite eternal wisdom. Unless the Disputant admits this, we must assert,
first, that GOD’s will in extrinsic operations was not determined from eternity,
but that a new determination and a new, as it were, ultimate dictate of the
practical intellect arises daily in God. Second, that a new ground of things is
presented to God every day and that he properly grows in his knowledge of
external things. But I believe that *his understanding is infinite, and there
is no searching of his wisdom.* This is my faith.
