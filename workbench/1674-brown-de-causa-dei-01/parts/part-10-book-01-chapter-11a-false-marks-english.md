## CHAPTER XI

### On the false marks of the Moral Law proposed by the adversaries

IN order that what has now been said about the *Moral Law* may become clearer,
and that the road may be made smooth for deciding the principal question, it
will be useful to propose and explain certain κριτήρια, “criteria,” of *Moral Laws*.
For by this means we will be able more easily to discern whether the precept
concerning the *Sabbath* is *Moral* or not. But first we will call back for
examination those indications of the *Moral Law* which are found proposed and
inculcated by our adversaries.

There are some who propose to us the following characteristic of the *Moral
Law* simply so called: “Namely, that the actions prohibited or commanded by
that law are evil or good in their own internal nature and quality before any
external constitution was established, or considered abstractly from the
external imposition of the legislator.” Thus Bishop White of England in his
vernacular little book *On the Sabbath*.

Concerning this I have the following things to say. First, if by that external
constitution or imposition of the Legislator this Author understands the
Mosaic promulgation (as he indeed seems to understand it; for later he treats
the following expressions as synonyms: “before the Law of the Decalogue was
given on Mount Sinai,” “from the beginning,” and “before the external Law”; and
the passage from Irenaeus, book 4, chapter 30, which he cites in the Margin,
confirms this: “The just Fathers, having the virtue of the Decalogue inscribed
in their own hearts and souls, namely, loving God,

[1674 logical printed p. 123 | IA leaf 141 | PDF p. 141]

who made them, and refraining from injustices toward their neighbor—for which
reason it was not necessary to admonish them with letters of correction,
because they had the justice of the law within themselves”), he asserts what
is true. For beyond doubt the matter of the Moral Law was good and just before
the Mosaic promulgation, since it was commanded by God, although it had not
been ordered and enjoined with the solemnity with which it later was in the
time of *Moses*. For those holy Fathers who lived before the time of *Moses*
cultivated piety and practiced holiness and justice, and thus rendered
obedience to God. Therefore they had a law enacted for them, obedience to which
was virtue. But I do not understand who could then say that those things
were “good or evil in their own internal quality and nature.” For these words
imply that no Divine Law concerning those moral actions, whether good or evil,
had been enacted in any manner before the time of *Moses*. Nevertheless, God
had signified his will to the Patriarchs in various ways before that Mosaic
dispensation, and they had this law in part inscribed in their hearts and
souls. And if there had then been no law, there would have been no
transgression of law, as *Paul* testifies.

Further, if the meaning of this statement is that the things commanded or
prohibited by the Moral Law possess, before the law was enacted, an intrinsic,
foundational goodness or evil—that is, an agreement or conformity with a
rational soul or with right reason—it can be admitted. But if this was what he
intended, why did he understand no legislation other than the Mosaic?

Next, if he understands some other legislation as well—namely, every manner in
which God, whether mediately or immediately, makes his will concerning a duty
owed known—and if by that intrinsic goodness and evil he understands moral
goodness and evil, then this assertion must certainly not be admitted. For
moral evil is sin and transgression of law; but where there is no law, there
is no transgression and, consequently, nothing morally evil. And moral good is
obedience rendered to a law; but where there is no law, neither will there be
any obedience rendered to a law. Therefore nothing is morally good. See what
was said above in chapter 3.

Finally, I would like to inquire further what he understands by “good and evil
by reason of their own internal nature and quality.” He explains this to us in
some measure when he describes the things which are good or evil positively,
or by reason of an external constitution commanding or prohibiting, namely,
that they are “things which before the enactment of a law are indifferent in
their own internal nature and quality, or are good or evil only by reason of
some circumstance.” It is therefore plain that he limits more narrowly than is
right the things which he calls moral simply. For some of the things which he
wishes to be merely positive are called moral and are undoubtedly moral
simply. Homicide in general, for instance, is indifferent in its own nature
and, by reason of circumstances, can be licit or illicit: licit, namely, in a
defensive war and in the execution of justice upon a guilty person; but
illicit when it is done from private vengeance. Who, however, will say that
the sixth commandment is therefore not to be referred to the Moral Law simply
so called? In this way he himself includes among these positive matters, or
moral matters arising merely from an external constitution, the law against
Polygamy, citing the following from Augustine, *Against Faustus*, book 22,
chapter 47: “Some sins are against nature, others against morals, and others
against precepts. Since these things are so, what crime, then, is there in the
charge brought against the holy man Jacob that he had several wives at once?
If you consult nature, he used those women not for the sake of lust but of
begetting. If you consider the custom at that time and in those lands. If you
consider the precept,

[1674 logical printed p. 124 | IA leaf 142 | PDF p. 142]

it was prohibited by no law”; and marriage within the degrees prohibited in
Leviticus 20:10, 21. Yet these are nevertheless called simply moral.

Next, they commonly propose the following mark of the Moral Law: namely, “that
only those Laws are Moral which become known to everyone, at least by the
light of corrupted nature.” But neither is this adequate.

1. Our natural light is now corrupted and very imperfect. It is therefore
unfit to perceive all the moral precepts of God, and is by no means suitable to
be established as the adequate rule and measure of moral duties. “The law of
nature,” Ames says in *Cases of Conscience*, book 2, chapter 2, “implanted and
inscribed, containing the rules of natural uprightness and justice, is almost
entirely buried by original corruption and almost entirely covered over by
custom as though by a mound of evil desires placed upon it. The very light of
the inward sight has also been enveloped and obscured by manifold darkness, so
that neither does the book of the mind display the rules of uprightness
inscribed in it as fully, plainly, and legibly, nor can our intellect read them
distinctly and clearly in that book.”

2. Who will deny that a law can be just, equitable, and good, and suited and
agreeable to rational nature, yet that the light of nature, now corrupted and
vitiated, may be unable to perceive it distinctly and perfectly?

3. If this rule is to be admitted, we will have no need of Sacred Scripture in
order to discern what that good, pleasing, and perfect will of God is. Instead,
leaving aside the written word of God, which is our perfect and adequate rule,
a lamp to our feet and a light to our path, Psalm 119:105 and Proverbs 6:23, as
our writers prove against the Papists, we will have to go to the most imperfect
rule of all and to a glowworm, so that we may know well what our duty is.

4. Further, on this reasoning it will follow that the covetousness prohibited
by the tenth commandment of the Decalogue is not a moral evil. For without the
law this did not become known even to *Paul*, Romans 7:7, and from this he
proves in the same place that he did not know sin except through the law: “For
I would not have known covetousness itself,” he says, “unless the law had said,
You shall not covet.” But by this law he understands no other law than the
Moral Law, as is evident. And covetousness is undoubtedly a moral sin, even if
it is only an indeterminate desire, or a first-first motion not yet approved,
whatever the Papists or lax casuists may say. But what can the Gentiles know
about this covetousness when it remained hidden so long from *Paul* himself?

5. From this characteristic the same may be concluded concerning any evil
desire not yet determined by a purpose. For none of the Gentiles condemned it
as evil. Let *Seneca* himself be heard, book 2 of *On Anger*, chapter 3: “That
first agitation which the appearance of an injury arouses,” he says, “is no
more anger than is the appearance of the injury itself. But that subsequent
impulse which not only received the appearance of the injury but approved it
is anger.”

6. What then? If that which is not known to be such by natural light is not a
moral good or evil, it will be no less true that whatever this obscured light
teaches to be such is certainly a moral good or evil. But who does not see how
dangerous this is? Further, what this natural light teaches can best be seen
from the universal practice of the Gentiles. Yet by their universal practice
the Gentiles approved polytheism, εἰδωλοθρησκείαν, “idolatry,” sacrifices, and
similar things, which no one calls morally good.

[1674 logical printed p. 125 | IA leaf 143 | PDF p. 143]

7. Finally, if it is sufficient to say that what some, although not all, can
perceive by the light of corrupted nature is moral, then all their labor here
is in vain. For by the light of corrupted nature certain Gentiles perceived
that one day in the week should be celebrated and distinguished from the
others, as we will demonstrate later. Therefore, this mark of the Moral Law
notwithstanding, the precept concerning celebrating the Sabbath every seventh
day will be moral. If the adversaries say that this became known to the
Gentiles not through nature or the light of nature but through the writings of
the Jews, since they all wrote long after the time of *Moses*, why could the
same not also be said concerning all the other actions approved or condemned
by them, since all the writers of better reputation came after *Moses* and,
therefore, we cannot know from their writings what nature teaches and what it
does not?

Let what has been said here suffice on this subject, since this assertion can
also be refuted abundantly from what was said above in chapter 5, page 64 and
following. I will therefore append the full refutation of this assertion which
Nathanael Jonson has in *The Socinian Opinion concerning the Sabbath*, page 73
and following, since perhaps the book itself is not available to everyone. [“A
person who contends,” he says, “that all moral matters are known by nature and
are common to us with every human being overthrows the practice of true piety.
For everything absolutely necessary for rightly regulating conduct before God
and human beings, or necessary to its very existence, is moral. Hence the
negative conclusion follows by conversion of the propositions: What is not
known by nature and is not common to us with every human being is not moral
(for in every kind of conversion the converting proposition correctly follows
from the converted one, as logicians speak), and thus it possesses no
character of virtue or vice, is not worship of God, is not piety, and is not a
good work. For among Christians these are equivalent in a broader sense,
according to the word of God in Philippians 4:8 and the author of the
*Apology*, page 55, where the terms *Virtue*, *Good Work*, *Worship*, and
*Moral* are convertible with one another. And yet this is the Author’s opinion
in the *Apology*, page 9: What binds everyone must in some manner be known to
everyone, and something of it must exist in every human being. Page 21: The law
concerning the Sabbath is not a matter of natural or moral right, because it
is not known to the Gentiles and is not common to the Jews together with all
human beings; for they could not know it through nature. Page 21: Moral Right
is common to all human beings. Page 39: Why is it moral? Is it because nature
teaches it? Page 28: The nations would be wrongly reproved unless they could
have reached the conclusion from right reason, and so forth. It is therefore
plain that for the Author only that which the nations could know from the
light of Nature is moral, and so forth. In all these statements, the Major
premise, whether expressed or suppressed in the manner of an enthymeme, must
be the following: Only that which is known by nature and is common to us with
every human being, including the Gentiles, is moral.—Once this is posited, it
is all over with the true worship of God, or the practice of piety. I pass over
all the arguments by which I earlier proved at sufficient length that not all
moral matters are implanted in us and known by nature, and consequently are
not common to us with every human being, since the true worship of God, the
practice of piety, or all moral matters become known and are grasped through
the supernatural revelation of God’s Word. Hence the Apostle teaches in
Romans 3:17 that the Gentiles did not know the way of peace, that is, the true
worship of God and the practice of piety, by comparison with Jeremiah 6:16 and
Matthew 11:28–30.—I add only instances from which the conclusion follows
specifically, if anyone is willing merely to form the induction. 1. Knowledge

[1674 logical printed p. 126 | IA leaf 144 | PDF p. 144]

of the triune God and of the way of salvation after the fall is not known by
nature or common to us with the Gentiles. Yet this knowledge is part of the
worship of God, or the practice of piety, and thus pertains to morals, since it
is commanded by the first precept—unless the author, with the Remonstrants,
denies that the knowledge of God is the worship of God. From this it would
follow by the law of opposites that neither the errors nor the heresies and so
forth of the Jews, Mohammedans, and Gentiles possess the character of vice and
sin. 2. The practice of conversion according to its proper and formal concept
is not known by nature or common to us with all Gentiles. Therefore it does not
possess the character of virtue, or it does not pertain to moral matters—far be
it! The proof is that the Gentiles know neither the point from which conversion
proceeds, spiritual death, nor the point toward which it proceeds, nor the
proper adjuncts of conversion, nor its formal character. For to this end the
Word of God and supernatural illumination are required: ‘I send you,’ Acts
26:18, and so forth; Ephesians 2:17–18; ‘sorrow according to God,’ 2
Corinthians 7:9, which is proper to conversion. They are ignorant of the Spirit
of bondage, which is the proximate cause of conversion, Romans 8:15. They lack
a sense of sin, because it is from the written law, Romans 7:7 and 3:20;
Revelation 3:17. Consequently they are blinded, hardened, and dead, Ephesians
2:1, so that they cannot discern what conduces to their peace, Luke 19:42. 3. I
say the same about the practice of faith and its formal character, which is
not known by nature or common to us with the Gentiles. Indeed, their faith is
mere carnal security and presumption concerning God’s mercy, without
satisfaction of God’s justice and without divorce from sin. Nor can nature
teach anything else on this matter, because it cannot show the way of peace,
Matthew 11:28–29. It does not know the proper object of faith and the things
faith requires, nor can it instruct us to count all things contemptible in
comparison with Christ, Philippians 3:7, and to despair in ourselves. 4. What
shall I say about the love of God? Can anything except the sense of God’s grace
in Christ and of our redemption through his blood engender the true love of
God in us, according to Luke 7:47? Nature is inclined to hatred of God; and
whatever we have in common with the Gentiles on this point can be called love
of God only equivocally. 5. Hope itself, if any falls to the Gentiles, is mere
presumption, since nature does not know either the certainty of salvation,
perseverance, and God’s special providence concerning his own, which occupies
the subject of hope, or election together with the formal object of hope,
which is God’s promise. 6. How far sanctification—that is, the mortification of
sin—is from the Gentiles and from nature, since nature cannot bring us to the
knowledge of sin, Romans 7! 7. As for what is the object of mortification, what
shall I say? Is taking heaven by force, Matthew 11, and worshiping God in spirit
and truth, or sincerity, common to us with Gentiles who are destitute of the
Spirit, or known by nature? Both are proper effects of regeneration, concerning
the knowledge of which nature is as blind as a mole, John 3:4–5 and following.
Let either paganism or the philosophy of the Gentiles teach me what devotion
is according to its proper and formal character, from principles not alien to
it but its own. What is Zeal? What is ἀκριβῶς περιπατεῖν, ‘to walk carefully,’
before God? Indeed, what is patience, unless someone should wish to
μεταμορφῶσαι, ‘transform,’ a Gentile into a Christian and Natural light into
Supernatural light? I pass over humility of mind: from where will Nature,
puffed up by carnal pride and deluding itself with vain imagination, bring it
forth? Revelation 3:16.—Plato saw the pride of *Diogenes* through the ragged
patches of his cloak. Paganism indeed contrived such a denial of oneself that
we are scarcely moved by the enjoyment or loss of the advantages which

than this practice: both ordinary or fixed daily practice, morning and evening,

See what follows, where the same man demonstrates this concerning the practice

person who teaches that nature, even after the fall, can supply a human being
with all moral matters necessary for such an upright life as is needed to
attain the human being’s final end, so that he may be blessed in God,
overthrows the practice of true piety. The reason is that he ascribes it to the
Gentiles. The proof is that whatever is a suitable and sufficient means for
