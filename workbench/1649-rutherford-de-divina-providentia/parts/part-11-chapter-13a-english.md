# CHAPTER XIII

## How some people answer passages which appear to support middle knowledge;
whether predetermination overturns that knowledge; and God's most actual will,
in which he knows future contingents.

Many people exhaust themselves beyond measure in untangling 1 Samuel 23:9–10.

Verse 9: “When David learned that Saul was devising this evil against him, he
said to Abiathar the priest, ‘Bring the ephod.’”

10. “And David said, ‘O Jehovah God of Israel, your servant has certainly heard
that Saul seeks to enter Keilah to destroy the city because of me.’”

11. “‘Will the people of Keilah deliver me into his hand? Will Saul come down,
as your servant has heard? O Jehovah God of Israel, tell your servant, I pray.’
And Jehovah said, ‘He will come down.’”

12. “Then David said, ‘Will the people of Keilah deliver me and my men into
Saul's hand?’ And Jehovah said, ‘They will deliver you.’”

Conrad Vorstius comments on this passage: “There remains the final class of
things which are necessary neither simply nor hypothetically, but are either
only possible or even entirely impossible and contradictory. This class is
therefore divided in two. The first kind contains things which exist only in
potentiality and never in actuality—that is, things which neither exist, nor
have existed, nor ever will exist, but which nevertheless can exist through
some power, whether created or at least divine. God therefore understands
these things through a certain simple or bare idea constructed upon some
hypothesis of possible existence, as though they were future, although in fact
they are not future. Thus, for example, God foresaw that David would be
betrayed by the citizens of Keilah if he remained in that place (1 Samuel
23:11), and that the Tyrians would believe if the gospel were preached to them.
Yet neither ever happened nor ever will. In this way God knows that he can do
or permit very many things which he will nevertheless never definitely do or
permit. Therefore, although such things are not known in themselves as actual,
since in actuality they are nothing, yet insofar as they possess some exis-*

indefinite causes—something can be known about them to that extent: namely,
that they are possible beings, and that when such causes are placed in the act
of causing, those things too will certainly emerge.”

Thus, according to Vorstius, a conditional contingent future is only possible

2. On our account, the linkage between the condition and the conditioned thing
is actual in God's decree. They cohere not merely possibly but actually through
God's decree.

3. “The essence of a possible being which will never be future, lying hidden
in its causes and certain to emerge when the causes are posited,” is a
chimerical essence certain to emerge, and therefore certainly future and known
as future, “yet existing only in potentiality, which never actually exists and
never will.” Thus, in Vorstius's judgment, it is never future. “But it is a
future essence conditionally.” I answer: the condition is eternally a
nonexistent thing in actuality; therefore it will simply never emerge. From
this follows what that most learned and celebrated theologian Gisbertus
Voetius says with his customary solidity and acuteness: “Either eternity is
not wholly simultaneous but, like time, possesses a threefold difference in
its duration; or the futurition of things in time and an age is not eternal but
temporal, and even now new futuritions of things are produced daily.” Nothing
could be more absurd.

270.]

Let us hear Molina: “God knew and revealed to David those two contingent
futures arising from human choice, although they never existed or will exist
in the nature of things, and therefore not in eternity either.” In the same
place, letter f: “Through natural knowledge God comprehends himself and in
himself all things which are eminently in him, and therefore the free choice
of every creature which he can create by his omnipotence. Hence, before his
will's free determination, from the loftiness of his natural knowledge by which
he infinitely surpasses individual things (that is, he knows as future things
which will never be future, and knows things unknowable and contradictory), he
penetrates what the free choice of each creature would do from its innate
liberty, given the hypothesis that he willed to create it in this or that order
of things with these or those circumstances or aids. Yet if it wished, the
creature could withhold the act or do the opposite; and if it were going to do
that, as it freely can, God would foresee the very thing which he foresees it
will actually do.”

I answer: Yet God's free will is always determined in this way by creatures.
Given that it appeared better to foresee this world rather than another and no
other order of things than the one he in fact established, if he created this
world rather than another and this order of things, all the wills of angels and
human beings would have to be determined in precisely the same manner in which
they are now in fact determined. Therefore, before conditional foreknowledge
and the will, all wills had to be determined in the way they are now in fact
determined. For the object of this foreknowledge is prior to the act of
foreknowledge, and much more prior to the act of will. And since all acts of
finally obeying God-*

freely arrange things otherwise than they had all been predisposed and defined

to middle knowledge and therefore to God's free will, were in fact going to be
saved—even through God's absolute power—once it is posited that he decreed to
create a free choice so pompously proud and independent.

2. The possible existence of this free object prior to every decree of God
also depends upon this conjecture of Molina, Fonseca, Suárez, Diego Ruiz, and
Hieronymus Fasolus: “The concurrence of the created will with God and its own
determination are independent of God's will and concurrence. For God's
concurrence is directed to the effect, not to created choice. Therefore,
abstracting from God's concurrence—that is, before every decree—it can be known
what a free choice placed in these or those circumstances will do.” But that
precise man and, among us, singular ornament and glory of scholastic minds,
Gisbertus Voetius, answers admirably: “This is to prove one doubtful claim
through another equally doubtful.” To the claim that the order of operation
toward a second cause does not depend upon the first cause, he says: “Although
it does not depend upon the first cause terminatively—because as the termini
differ, so the orders also differ with respect to the termini—it does not
follow that it does not depend upon it effectively, that is, with respect to
its being and production.”

4. Among many possible things, why is this one future and by no means
nonfuture, while that one will never be future? There can be no cause except
either in the possible creature itself or in the Creator. The cause is not in
the creature, for it would have to be either in the creature insofar as it is
possible or insofar as it is future. The cause cannot be in the creature
insofar as it is possible, for then there would be a cause from eternity why
all possible things were future and all eventually present. But what prevents
all possible things from being future and eventually present at some time? On
that account infinite worlds, more angels, countless thousands of people, free
actions, species of things, and worlds other than those which actually exist
will exist at some time—which is false. For a possible thing cannot itself
prevent its breaking forth into future being. Nor can a possible creature,
insofar as it is future, be the cause why it is future, for whatever is the
cause of a future thing is the cause of a present thing; but it is impossible
for something to be the cause of itself or the cause why it eventually becomes
present. God will therefore be the cause of the future thing. But he is either
a necessary or a free cause of the future. If necessary, possible creatures
would flow from God by a necessity of nature, as light flows from the sun. Yet
if the sun had existed from eternity, so would light. There is therefore no
cause why all possible things did not actually exist from eternity; hence
there is no necessity for the world's creation or for things to arise in time,
which is absurd. But if God is the free cause of future things, nothing was
truly future—whether contingently, freely, necessarily, absolutely, or
conditionally—except by God's antecedent free will. The object of middle
knowledge is therefore a nonexistent thing.

5. The sole cause why many things possible in themselves will nevertheless
never be future is God's free will.

1. Many possible good things never exist because God did not will them to
exist. Otherwise there would be no reason why we, granted existence by God in
creation, should thank him because in his goodness he freely created us rather
than the countless thousands of possible worlds and creatures which were never
created. But the latter is contrary to Scripture. Revelation 4:11: “You are
worthy, Lord, to receive glory, ὅτι σὺ ἔκτισας τὰ πάντα, because you created all
things.” Likewise Romans 11:36–37.

2. God averts and drives away from us many possible evils which could happen,
and does so from sheer gracious goodness and indulgence: for example, he keeps
away attacks of evil angels, plague, famine,-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 147; PDF page 198]

*sterility, childlessness, diseases, premature death, infamy, and those sins of
final hardening into which the reprobate have fallen. But if God in his freedom
did not at all restrain these, so that they remained within the bounds of
merely possible things and never arrived at the state of future and eventually
present things, there would be no reason for us to pray to God, to place our
hope and confidence in him, or to thank him for having deigned to avert such
things; nor would there be reason why it should be right for those oppressed by
them to submit themselves to God.

3. God created all things by judgment—in number, weight, and measure. It is
therefore owing to his free will, not to fortune or chance, that one world was
created rather than four or a thousand; and that exactly so many stars, angels,
human beings, species and individual specimens of brute animals, reptiles,
little flowers, and herbs were created—neither more nor fewer. He numbers the
stars (Psalm 147:4), the hairs of people's heads (Matthew 10:30), and knows most
precisely the drops of the ocean and rivers and the particles of dust and sand
(Isaiah 40:12; Proverbs 30:4). A prudent potter deliberately makes exactly so
many clay vessels, neither more nor fewer; a commander-in-chief prepares a
fixed number of soldiers; a builder erects a fixed number of buildings of a
given size.

For there is no middle between something happening apart from intention—that
is, outside every decree of God—and its happening according to God's intention
and decree, except what those atheists who deny God's providence call happening
by fortune or chance.

all future things from eternity. Otherwise a new volition and decree would

comes there, the Keilites will deliver David to Saul. If Christ prayed to the
Father, he would send more than twelve legions of angels to deliver him from
death. If the Israelites married foreign wives, they would be led astray into
idolatry. If someone raised from the dead preached to the rich man's five
brothers, they would nevertheless never repent.” Although the consequent terms
of these propositions never exist, all these things would truly—indeed,
unavoidably—have been future on the hypothesis that the condition had been
posited, although it never was. Therefore these propositions possess
determinate truth, for a hypothetical truth, if the hypothesis were posited,
is a determinate truth. God's free will therefore either wills such
connections, positively does not will them, or “negatively wills” them—the
printed text apparently omits *does not* in this third alternative. If he wills
them, then he decreed such connections, for by the nature of the thing there
is no more a connection in these cases than between their contradictory
propositions; and the point at issue is established. If God positively does
not will such connections, he would never have foretold that they would be
true, for then he would have foretold something false, which is blasphemous to
say. If he negatively does not will such connections, he would still have
foretold something false; and it would follow that God's will was suspended
with respect to such a connection, just as a stone does not will such
connections. But God's most actual will is determinate from eternity toward
everything that can be rejected or willed. For God is not in potentiality to
will or reject something new; if he were, God's will would begin to be more
actual, noble, and perfect than it had been from eternity.

[Marginal summary: God's most actual will is positively directed toward every
object that can be willed or rejected.]

For, as Suárez observes, “when God is said to be able to will or not will,
‘being able’ does not signify an active or passive potency, but only the
indifference of the same act as it is terminated upon this object or that one.”
God therefore decreed from eternity all such connections, which are merely
contingent in themselves, although the connected terms never exist. Otherwise
those connections which express God's marvelous wisdom, justice, mercy,
freedom, patience, forbearance, grace, and power happen entirely by chance—than
which nothing more injurious to God's providence can be devised—or they happen
by God's decree.

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *Metaphysics*.]

[Marginal summary: Let middle knowledge depart: God foreknows conditional
futures in his decree.]

2. Let us also examine in the Scriptures all the promises of the law and most
of the gospel promises,-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 149; PDF page 200]

*made to the reprobate, to show the same thing. For these promises are proposed
to us who are now fallen: “God wills all Adam's descendants to live forever if
they have perfectly fulfilled the law.” Again: “God decreed that whoever
exactly fulfills the law should enjoy all temporal goods—children, riches,
reputation, freedom, and health” (Deuteronomy 28). Yet the connected terms
never exist. Apart from the Savior, there never is, was, or will be anyone who
perfectly fulfills the law; nor has eternal life or temporal prosperity ever
been given to any mortal by title and right of obedience rendered absolutely
and in every respect to God's law. Yet they will not say that these connections
—“If a person keeps the law, he will be preserved and live by the law”; and
others such as “If Cain does well, he will be accepted by God” (Genesis 4), and
“If Judas believes, Christ's righteousness will be imputed to him”—precede
every free decree of God and objectively terminate middle knowledge. It is as
though one had to fear that God would destroy human freedom if by his absolute
decree he assigned eternal life to those perfectly observing the law, and
justification, forgiveness of sins, and eternal life to those repenting and
believing. But that is a vain and shadowy fear.

3. Scripture ascribes to God knowledge of his own works. But how would God
foreknow what he himself is going to do except in his decree by which he
resolved to do it, and how would he foretell the things he foreknows? For the
prophet Micaiah predicts the scattering of Israel's army and Ahab's death in
the battle at Ramoth-gilead, because God had revealed to him his decree,
pronounced from the throne, to send a lying spirit into the mouth of all the
prophets who would entice the king into that battle. When Jehovah predicts
that he will do this—devastating the Babylonians by the Medes, Assyrians, and
others—he does so because he himself formed that plan for their destruction
(Isaiah 14:24–26). He predicts that he will deliver the people from captivity
because he decreed to use Cyrus as the agent of that deliverance (Isaiah
46:10). If the certainty of prediction depends upon the decree, so too does
the infallibility of foreknowledge. Hence all predictions resting upon a decree
by which God resolved to do those things and unfailingly bring them about will
prove that the certainty of foreknowledge depends upon the decree. Nearly all
prophecies will therefore prove this very point; one finds it on almost every
page of the prophets. Thus Jehovah says, in effect: “I predict that this will
happen under the Messiah's kingdom and in those last days because I promise
that it will happen,” if the matter concerns gospel promises; “and I threaten
that I will inflict this evil,” if it concerns threats, “because-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 150; PDF page 201]

*I myself am going to do it, I will it, and from eternity I decreed within
myself to do it.” Prudent people likewise foreknow whatever they are going to
do because they have resolved to do those very things. Why should this be
denied of the infinitely wise God?

What do the Jesuits say to all this? Didacus Ruiz “contends that God foreknows
all free operations, including his own, whether good or evil, natural or
supernatural, of all human beings and angels—those to be created, those merely
possible, and those never to be created—not only generally and vaguely, but
also determinately and particularly: either according to an inferential sense,
or one of simple coexistence, or in wholly disparate cases; or which operations
are inferred from a hypothesis as determinately future or nonfuture because of
the will's greater inclination. And he knows this antecedently to his own
predetermination or efficacious decree, yet in such a way that there is
presupposed a will of God by which he would will to produce the created power
of choice in the circumstances presupposed in the hypothesis. This will of God
is not presupposed as though it already existed from eternity, nor as though it
were absolutely future, but as a condition which would be required for such a
supposition to exist and for such an act to be joined to the hypothesis.”

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, volume *On the Will of God*, disputation 62,
section 3.]

Here the reader has, in brief, twelve articles from Ruiz. Francisco de Oviedo
thinks he can demonstrate the matter philosophically as follows: “Suppose,” he
says, “two men, one of whom says, ‘If Peter had such a thought, he would
consent,’ while the other says no—‘If Peter had such a thought, he would
consent.’ One of these men would state the matter as it was. Therefore one of
them would speak the truth. Consequently one of these propositions would be
true and the other false—since, in any given case, either consent or the
withholding of consent would occur determinately. This is confirmed: this
proposition is true, ‘Peter, when called, answers.’ Therefore this was
previously true: ‘If Peter were called, he would answer.’ And all the arguments
which establish the truth and falsity of a proposition about free absolute
futures likewise establish it concerning a conditional future.”

[Marginal reference: Oviedo, volume 2, philosophical course, *On the Soul*,
controversy 7, point 6, question 3, no. 10.]

Arriaga raises this objection against himself: “Every antecedent supposition
from which an effect necessarily follows takes away that effect's freedom. But
Peter's sin being now future and known by God is an antecedent supposition,
because today the proposition is true and today God knows the sin which will
be future tomorrow. Moreover, once this knowledge of God is posited, the
effect cannot fail to follow, because that knowledge cannot be mistaken. ——
What exists determinately exists necessarily, for determination is opposed to
existence [so the printed text]; and for a proposition now to be determinately
true, its object must be determinately going to exist. Therefore it will exist
neces-*

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, philosophical course, disputation 14, logic,
section 5, subsection 2, nos. 91, 90, 91 (as printed).]

[Chapter XIII; printed page 151; PDF page 202]

*sarily. As a solution,” he says, “I posit two kinds of determination of an
effect. One arises formally from the effect's determinate existence, as that
is contrasted with a vague and indeterminate existence. This determination
does not take away freedom, for it is taken from the effect itself, just as I
said about consequent necessity, which coincides with this determination. The
other determination is antecedent, from a cause determined to one outcome,
and this does take away freedom, because every necessity arising from
something antecedent introduces a necessity from the effect. —— Therefore,
for the determinate truth of a proposition, its object need not exist
determinately from a cause—that is, from a cause determined to one outcome—or
arise in such a way that it could not fail to arise. It is enough that it exist
determinately, with the determination arising from the effect itself as it
actually exists, as in propositions about the present and the past.”

I know that Suárez and others devise a determinate truth in such conditional
futures without any inference or syllogistic consequence. But, as I have often
insisted, I cannot accept this; with the very learned Jacobus Revius I say:
“Truth is founded in being. The question is where conditional futures obtain
determinate being apart from every act of the divine will. They do not obtain
it from themselves, for they do not actually exist; indeed, antecedently to
every decree they are no more future than nonfuture. And if they have no
determinate being before the decree, from where, I ask, do they have necessity,
and from where knowability?” The very eminent Bradwardine adduces arguments by
which he maintains that “things known are not causes of divine knowledge.”
From Dionysius he proves that they are neither real nor objective causes
capable of grounding the certainty of God's infallible knowledge.

[Marginal reference: Suárez, *Metaphysics*, disputation 30, nos. 1, 2, etc.]

p. 632.]

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 15.]

“Therefore,” he says, “the divine wisdom, knowing by itself, knows all things
immaterially. For if the known thing were the cause of divine knowledge, God
would not be the first cause of all things. He would cause external things
(those outside himself) only with wisdom disposing them, and there would be a
cause prior to this—namely the known thing itself, on your Pelagian view. This
is still clearer concerning future things. For A's being future is the cause
of God's foreknowledge, while God is not the cause why A is future, since in
that case he would cause it in utter ignorance. Therefore A's being future is
simply the first cause here. Indeed, since God's foreknowledge is here an
effect in such a way that it is in no way a cause, it is caused by some prior
cause which is not God. All philosophers and theologians unanimously condemn
all these claims.”

[Marginal reference: Dionysius, *On the Divine Names*, chapter 7.]

Again: “Contingents open to either alternative are not of themselves more
determined to one side than to the other, for then they would not be open to
either alternative. Therefore, of themselves they cannot deter-*

[Chapter XIII; printed page 152; PDF page 203]

*minately cause in God knowledge of one alternative rather than the other
(listen and answer, Jesuits!). Again, if known things were causes of divine
knowledge, then, since a cause is naturally prior to its effect, known things
would be naturally prior to God's knowledge. This appears absurd, since he is
the first and the last, the beginning and the end. The Pelagians say that
something will happen not because providence foresaw that it would be future,
but rather the reverse: because something is future, it cannot escape divine
providence. Furthermore, if things known were causes of divine knowledge, they
would greatly assist God in knowing, give him counsel, and show him what must
be done. Thus a foolish man or a donkey would be a necessary assistant,
counselor, and teacher of our all-wise God, contrary to Isaiah 40: “Who is his
counselor?”*

Certainly, if known things objectively terminated God's knowledge, God would
first have to consult things outside himself as to whether this or that was
fitting to be done. He would not dare decide anything categorically until the
things themselves supplied wise counsel: a wise connection between fire and
heating, snow and whiteness, fish swimming in water rather than air, and birds
in the air rather than rivers and the depths of the sea would determine God's
wisdom. This is indeed true among human beings, for an object's extrinsic
goodness and suitability determine the artisan's knowledge. The artisan
borrows and begs wisdom from the known things, because things are not regulated
in their fitness by the artisan's knowledge; on the contrary, the artisan's
knowledge is regulated objectively and truly causally by the known things. An
artisan knows future things less perfectly and the same things more perfectly
once they exist. But it would be utterly absurd to affirm this of God. God
knows with equal perfection from eternity the world as future and to be
created, and the world as existing and created today. For he knows nothing new
today which he was ignorant of yesterday or from eternity; today he knows
nothing more exactly or perfectly which from eternity he knew less exactly and
more crudely. Known things are not the measure of the divine intellect; indeed,
they are not properly its object, except in this sense: first they are ideally
future and present in their archetype in God's intellect—outlines and forms
from eternity in that decretive and intellectual workshop of the divine mind,
if it is permissible to speak this way. There is a complete and exact
conformity between God's intellect and external things, whether possible,
future, present, or past, just as there is between knowl-*

*edge and our object.

2. Known things terminate the divine intellect in some fashion, but they do
not measure, limit, or perfect it. In this case the known things are an object
which does not pass outside God's mind. For all known things are presented to
God's practical intellect: they are known to him insofar as they have practical
being from him—whether by his bringing it about that they exist, bringing it
about that they do not exist, permitting them, or through their being possible
in relation to his power. This internal and objective existence of things in
the divine intellect is the first rule and active measure of external things.
God's knowledge receives from those known things no measuring, potential and
passive determination, or conformity with external things; for known things
are regulated and measured by God's practical knowledge as by a rule. Nor
should God's knowledge be said to conform to known things; known things conform
to God's knowledge. Future contingents cannot of themselves terminate or
determine God's infallible knowledge to one opposite rather than the other.
For it involves a contradiction that future contingents, before God's decree
which gives them futurition and removes mere possibility, are determinate or
determinately known as future, when in reality they are merely possible. I am
not speaking here about known uncreated realities, such as God's being eternal
and so forth, which are prior to God's knowledge, but about created things
which are known by God.
