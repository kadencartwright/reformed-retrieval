QUESTION XIII

Is There Any Use of Philosophy in Theology? Affirmative.

[Marginal heading: The pseudo-apostles erred by excess.]

I. People err in this matter by excess and by defect. Those who confuse
philosophy with theology err by excess. The pseudo-apostles did this in
ancient times by mixing various unhealthy philosophical doctrines into
Christian doctrine, for which reason the apostle reproves them in Colossians
2:8.

[Marginal heading: Certain fathers.]

Certain fathers who came from among the philosophers retained some of their
erroneous opinions and tried to bring the Gentiles to Christianity by mixing
philosophical and theological doctrines. Such were Justin Martyr, Origen, and
Clement of Alexandria.

[Marginal heading: The scholastics.]

The scholastics did so as well. Their doctrine is more philosophical than
theological, since it rests more on the reasonings of Aristotle and other
philosophers than on the testimonies of the prophets and apostles.

[Marginal heading: The Socinians.]

The Socinians today stumble against the same stone. They place philosophy in
the citadel as a principle of faith and an interpreter of Scripture. The
paradoxical disputant especially urges this in the impious treatise *On
Philosophy as the Interpreter of Scripture*, published not long ago.

[Marginal heading: By defect.]

[Marginal heading: Fanatics, Anabaptists, Weigelians.]

Those who consider philosophy contrary to theology and therefore think it
must be entirely eliminated from theology err by defect. They regard it not
only as useless but also as harmful. The fanatics and enthusiasts maintained
this in previous centuries, and the Anabaptists and Weigelians retain it
today. They appear to have formally declared war on philosophy and the
liberal arts.

[Marginal heading: The orthodox hold the middle course.]

II. The orthodox hold the middle course. They neither confuse theology with
sound philosophy as though they were parts of one whole nor oppose them as
contraries. Instead, they subordinate and arrange them as things in a
subordinate relation which do not conflict with each other but render
service. Philo Judaeus, and after him the fathers, aptly expressed this
through the allegory of Sarah and Hagar, the mistress and the servant: theology
is to rule over philosophy, while philosophy is to serve and minister to
theology.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 41 | IA leaf 95 | PDF p. 95]

[Marginal heading: And they distinguish its use from its abuse.]

They acknowledge that philosophy has varied and manifold use in theology,
but this must be carefully distinguished from its manifold abuse.

III. Although not everything true can be demonstrated by reason, since the
bounds of truth extend much farther than the bounds of reason, nevertheless
no falsehood can rely upon any patronage of true reason against the truth.
Nor can one truth destroy another, although one may transcend and surpass the
other. For whatever kind of truth it may be—whether below reason and
perceived by sense, alongside reason and perceived by the intellect, or above
reason and perceived by faith—it has proceeded from no other source than God,
the father of truth. Thus grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, and
supernatural revelation does not abolish natural revelation but purifies it.

IV. Philosophy is taken either properly and in the abstract for the knowledge
of divine and human things insofar as they can be known by the light of
nature, or improperly and in the concrete for a collection of conflicting
opinions held by the philosophers of different sects. We acknowledge that in
the latter sense it contains many errors and has no use but only the greatest
abuse, and in this sense Paul condemns it in Colossians 2:8. In the former
sense, however, it cannot be denied that philosophy has manifold uses. We
will indicate only the general ones in passing.

[Marginal heading: Uses of philosophy.]

V. First, it serves as a means both for convicting the Gentiles and for
preparing them for the Christian faith. Hence Clement of Alexandria,
*Stromata* 1, says that it “prepares the road for the most royal teaching,” as
is evident from Paul's sermons in Acts 14 and 17 and from the fathers'
writings against the Gentiles. To the same point belongs the saying of Julian
the Apostate when he saw the Christians overthrowing the errors of the
Gentiles through the study of philosophy and humane letters: “We are caught
by our own wings.” Thus God wills that we apply to theology whatever truth
lies hidden in the lower sciences. By taking it away from the pagans as
possessors in bad faith, we must claim and vindicate it for Christ, who is the
truth, for the building of the mystical temple. So in ancient times Moses
adorned the tabernacle with Egyptian gold, and Solomon used the labor of the
Sidonians and Tyrians in constructing the temple. 2. Philosophy serves as a
testimony of agreement in matters known by nature, so that the truth and
certainty of those things may be more firmly confirmed by the twofold
revelation. 3. It serves as an instrument for perceiving things clearly and
discussing them correctly, judging what is true and false and what follows or
does not follow according to the rules of sound and necessary consequence
impressed by God upon rational nature, with the light of the divine Word
leading the way. This was treated above in Question X. For although reason
takes the principles of religion from the light of faith, with that light
going before it reason must judge from these principles how the parts of
heavenly doctrine cohere with and mutually establish each other, what agrees
with them, and what disagrees with them. 4. The mind is cultivated in the
lower disciplines and prepared for handling and receiving the higher science.
Yet this must be done soberly, lest too great a love of philosophy seize us.
We must always regard it as a servant, not a mistress, according to that
saying of Clement of Alexandria: “Let philosophy submit itself to theology as
Hagar to Sarah and allow itself to be admonished and corrected; but if it
will not obey, cast out the servant.”

[Marginal heading: And its abuses.]

VI. Its manifold abuse can also be observed. 1. Things that philosophy truly
pronounces about matters subject to it and within a lower order are
transferred to the mysteries of theology—for example, that nothing comes from
nothing, that there is no return from privation to possession, that a virgin
does not become a mother, and so forth. This makes a transition into a
different category, for what philosophy teaches must be understood within
its own realm and about natural causes, not within the realm of grace and the
supernatural order.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 42 | IA leaf 96 | PDF p. 96]

Therefore, those who use these claims to argue against the creation of the
world, the incarnation, and the resurrection of the dead err, because
Scripture teaches that these things occur not by the power of natural causes
but by the omnipotence of God. 2. Under the pretext of philosophy, false
opinions of philosophers are adopted, and errors in theology are constructed
or defended from them. Such are Aristotle's opinion about the eternity of the
world, Plato's about purgatorial fire, and the Stoics' about the fated
necessity of things. But the errors of philosophers are not the decrees of
philosophy, just as the mistakes of craftsmen must not be imputed to the art
itself. Clement says in *Stromata* 1: “Philosophy must not be called Stoic,
Platonic, Epicurean, or Aristotelian. Rather, the whole selection of whatever
has been rightly said by these sects must be called philosophy.” 3. Philosophy
assumes mastery over the articles of faith rather than being content with a
ministry. The scholastics did this by placing Aristotle on the throne, as do
the Socinians, who refuse to admit the mysteries of the Trinity, the
incarnation, and so forth because they do not appear to agree with the
principles of philosophy. 4. New terms and phrases are introduced from
philosophy into theology without necessity, and new and often dangerous
doctrines lie hidden under them.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

VII. For something to be denied by philosophy is one thing; for it not to be
taught by philosophy is another. We do not deny that various theological
mysteries are not taught in philosophy, but it does not follow that
philosophy denies them. The boundaries of the sciences must not be confused.
Just as a physician does not treat geometrical matters, nor a jurist physical
matters, so philosophy must be contained within its own boundaries and not
put its sickle into another's harvest. Therefore, although it delivers
nothing about the Trinity and the incarnation, it must not on that account be
thought to deny those mysteries.

VIII. In Colossians 2:8 Paul does not condemn true philosophy considered in
itself, but the vain and false philosophy found in the philosophers of that
age, by whom the doctrine of the gospel was being corrupted. Such philosophy
is always found in the concrete when it is carried beyond its proper limits
and assumes judgment over supernatural and divine things. The following lines
of Prudentius, *Against Symmachus* 2, refer to this:

> For if our lesser nature tries to strain its sight
> Too sharply and to penetrate the supreme God's decrees,
> Who can doubt that frail nature wearies when its sight is overcome,
> And that the power of the exhausted mind within its little breast
> Is thrown into disorder and sinks, dulled, beneath feeble cares?
> But the way of faith is easy, and so forth.

That Paul understands philosophy of this kind is evident, 1. from the added
description, since he calls it an “empty deception”; 2. from the example of
the worship of angels in verse 18, which is not a doctrine of true philosophy
but a cancerous growth; 3. from a parallel, because in verse 4 Paul condemns
specious reasoning—not every kind, since he himself is read to have used it
more than once, but the kind that misleads. Thus he does not condemn every
kind of philosophy, but only one that is unhealthy and deceptive. This is the
kind the pseudo-apostles were trying to introduce so that they might draw the
faithful away from the simplicity and truth of the gospel. Under the pretext
of a certain secret wisdom, they were striving to press upon the Colossians
new and erroneous doctrines about the worship of angels, perhaps drawn from
the pools of the Platonists. Or through false reasonings and sophisms they
endeavored to commend the use and necessity of the legal ceremonies and
human traditions and so gradually lead the Colossians back from Christ to
Moses.

IX. In Romans 1:21–22 the apostle does not condemn true philosophy, but only
its abuse. Nor does he speak about philosophy, but about philosophers who,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 43 | IA leaf 97 | PDF p. 97]

swollen with a vain opinion of their own wisdom, became vain in their
reasonings. Likewise, when he disputes at Athens against the Stoics and
Epicureans in Acts 17, he does not thereby reject philosophy in itself, but
only the doctrines of those philosophers who opposed faith in the one true
God and Jesus Christ and the resurrection of the dead.

X. It is absurdly inferred from the fact that God is the author of philosophy
and natural reason that philosophy is therefore the interpreter of Scripture.
For God is the author of the former through natural manifestation in the
state of fallen humanity, as far as truths still known by nature are
concerned. But he is the author of the latter through gracious revelation in
the state of restoration by grace, as far as mysteries inaccessible to reason
are concerned. Nor, because God is the author of philosophy, is he therefore
immediately the author of the interpretations elicited by this or that
philosopher.

XI. If the fathers sometimes spoke more harshly against philosophy—as when
Tertullian, *Against Hermogenes* 8, says that philosophers are the patriarchs
of heretics, and in *Prescription Against Heretics* 7 says, “What has Athens
to do with Jerusalem? What has the Academy to do with the church? What have
heretics to do with Christians? Our instruction is from Solomon's porch, not
Zeno's. Let those who have produced a Stoic, Platonic, and dialectical
Christianity look to it. We have no need of curiosity after Jesus Christ, nor
of inquiry after the gospel,” and so forth—and if Cyprian, book 4, epistle 2,
Lactantius, book 3, chapter 13, and others did likewise, they do not condemn
true philosophy that remains within its bounds. They condemn false and rash
philosophy that dares to intrude itself into matters above its comprehension,
and the philosophers of that time, who were the most bitter enemies of the
Christian faith.

XII. Although the apostles taught theology without the aid of philosophy, it
does not follow that we can do so as well. An inference is not valid from the
extraordinary and immediate instruction of God, which was necessary at the
first beginnings of the church's birth, to ordinary and mediate instruction,
which occurs through study and the aid of the lower disciplines.

XIII. Although theology delivers many things that philosophy does not know,
it does not follow that something false in philosophy is true theologically,
because truth does not conflict with truth, nor is one light contrary to
another. Care must be taken, however, that philosophical truths are not
extended beyond their own sphere and the ordinary powers of nature to matters
of supernatural revelation or power, and that physical things are not
confused with things beyond nature, nor human things with divine. For example,
in philosophy it is true that a virgin does not give birth, a heavy body is
carried downward, fire burns when material is placed near it, and nothing
comes from nothing, while theology establishes the contrary. Yet philosophy
and theology do not thereby conflict with each other, because these statements
are made according to different respects. In philosophy the contrary events
are denied according to the laws of nature; in theology they are affirmed
with respect to divine omnipotence and in a supernatural manner.

XIV. Although a philosopher might be permitted to begin with doubt for the
safer investigation of natural things, it would be wrong to extend this to
theological matters and matters of faith. These rest upon certain and
indubitable principles and self-evident truths which it is sinful to doubt,
such as the existence of God—unless we wish to strip ourselves of conscience
and renounce our moral dependence upon the Creator, which cannot be shaken
off without sin even for a moment. That would introduce the Academic
suspension of judgment into religion and render the whole of theology
skeptical.
