# CHAPTER XXIX

## The fourth precept of the Decalogue bound the Gentiles

From what has now been said and proved, it further follows as a corollary
flowing of its own accord that the Fourth Precept of the Decalogue formerly
bound, and even now binds, the Gentiles. For above in *Chapters VII and VIII*
we proved that all the precepts of the Decalogue are moral. We also proved in
*Chapters XXI and XXIII* that the Fourth precept is moral; in *Chapter XXVII*,
that it is not purely and simply ceremonial; and in *Chapters XXV and XXVI*,
that it is not partly moral and partly ceremonial. From all these things it is
manifest that this fourth precept bound, and even now binds, the Gentiles,
that is, people other than the Jews. Further, in *Chapters II and III* we
proved that the entire Decalogue bound people other than the Jews. From this
it also plainly follows that this Fourth precept bound the Gentiles, so that
there is no need to prove this at length, since it follows further from what
has already been proved. Yet because some make this a distinct controversy, I
would also like to discuss this question briefly.

There are some who hold that the entire Decalogue did not pertain to the
Gentiles because the Fourth precept did not pertain to them. Thus, indeed, the
most celebrated *Zanchi* argues. Others hold that the fourth precept does not
bind the Gentiles because the Decalogue itself, of which the fourth precept is
a part, did not bind them. And thus they argue in a circle. *Zanchi* indeed
contends that the law of the Decalogue, insofar as it was given by *Moses*,
not only did not bind the Gentiles formerly, but also does not bind Christians
now, except insofar as it accords with the law of nature. His arguments,
however, were examined by us above in *Chapter III*.

The *Manichaeans* formerly expunged the whole Old Testament and rejected the
law as something proceeding from someone other than the good God. And the
*Antinomians* free all Christians even from obligation to the moral law, as we
saw above. Others, who favor neither the *Antinomians* nor the *Manichaeans*,
deny that we are subject to the law insofar as it was given by *Moses*. This
differs little from the opinion of the *Antinomians*, for they too sometimes
explain themselves in this way. But we examined this opinion above.

We now come to discuss the present question, namely, whether or not the fourth
precept binds the Gentiles. We prove the affirmative side of this question as
follows.

*Argument 1.* What is moral binds the Gentiles no less than the Jews. But the
thing commanded in the fourth precept is moral. Therefore it binds the
Gentiles equally with the Jews. The *major premise* needs no proof, since the
Adversaries freely concede that everything moral pertains to everyone and was
not peculiar and proper to the Jews. For from this they wish to conclude that
the precept concerning the Sabbath is not moral, because it was given only to
the Jews. The *minor premise* is manifest from what was said before. See *Book
III, Chapters XXIII and XXIV*.

*Argument 2.* If the Decalogue binds the Nations, the fourth precept will also
bind them, because it is part of the Decalogue. But the former is true, as is
manifest above in

[Printed p. 308 | IA PDF 314 | IA leaf 314 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Chapters II and III*. Therefore the latter is also true. The force of this
argument is not removed by saying that the Decalogue in itself indeed binds
the Gentiles, but not insofar as it was given through *Moses*. For even if
this were true, our argument would remain firm, because it would follow from
this that the fourth precept, no less than the others, binds the Gentiles in
itself, although not insofar as it was given by *Moses*. *Instance.* The
Decalogue binds the Nations with respect to precepts that are purely and
naturally moral, but not with respect to ceremonial and positive precepts.
*Response 1.* It is assumed here that some ceremonial precept is given in the
Decalogue, which is false. 2. It is assumed that positive and ceremonial are
the same, which is likewise false. 3. It is assumed that what is positive,
that is, not natural, cannot be moral. We demonstrated long ago that this is
false. 4. We showed above how the precept concerning the Sabbath can be said
to be natural. See *Book III, Chapter XXVIII*. Hence it follows that if the
Decalogue binds the Gentiles with respect to precepts that are purely and
naturally moral, it will also bind them with respect to the fourth precept.

*Argument 3.* It is part of that law with whose binding authority *Paul*
pressed even the Gentiles, *Romans 13 and Ephesians 6*. Therefore it also
bound the Gentiles.

*Argument 4.* It is part of that law whose summary bound the Gentiles.
Therefore it itself also binds the Gentiles. See *Chapter XXII, Argument 9*.

*Argument 5.* The general scope of the fourth precept pertains to the
Gentiles. Therefore the precept itself also pertains to them. See above,
*Chapter XXII, Argument 14*.

*Argument 6.* What was given to *Adam* as the common head and root of all
people binds the Gentiles equally with the Jews. But the fourth precept is
such a thing. The Adversaries concede the *major premise*. We proved the
*minor premise* above in *Book III, Chapters IX, X, XI, XII, and XIII*.

*Argument 7.* The Royal Law, *νόμος βασιλικός*, binds all the King's subjects.
Therefore the fourth precept binds the Gentiles. The reason for the
*consequence* is: 1. because the Gentiles are subjects of God equally with the
Jews; and 2. because the fourth precept is part of that royal law of which
*James speaks in James 2:8*. For it is part of the Decalogue, and no other law
could then have been urged, nor could it have been said of any other law what
we see said there of the Decalogue, namely, that *whoever offends in one has
become guilty of all*. Dr. *Rivet gives us the following sense of this passage
in his Explanation of the Decalogue, Works, volume 1, page 1231, column 2*:
[that is, he who transgresses one precept of the law has become a transgressor
of the law, all of which he ought to have fulfilled. *Paul of Burgos* offers
this interpretation. He likewise notes that it was the error of the Pharisees
to think that one who observed the law for the greater part was not condemned,
as though people were saved who had a greater heap of good works than of sins.
Against them *James* says that even the person who fails in one precept is to
be punished, because he is in fact a transgressor of the law, all of which he
ought to have fulfilled. The following words which we have cited agree most
plainly with this sense, as if he were saying: since the lawgiver is the same,
one must keep one command in the same manner as another, and consequently the
whole law, which is violated by the violation of one precept. This entire
discussion would be useless unless we were bound to obey the law of which he
speaks.]

*Argument 8.* Either the Gentiles were bound to observe one day in the weekly
cycle as a solemn and sufficient time of divine worship, or they were bound
to observe some other

[Printed p. 309 | IA PDF 315 | IA leaf 315 | IA decausadei02brow]

time. The latter cannot be said. Therefore the former must be said. The *major
premise* is certain, because otherwise it would have to be said that they were
not bound to consecrate any solemn time to God and, consequently, were not
bound to worship God solemnly. This is absurd and utterly false. The *minor
premise* can be confirmed as follows. If they were bound to observe some
other time, then it was either a time determined by God or one determined by
people. If the former is said, it follows that God determined another
proportion of time. But what is it? We read that God determined one out of
seven, but that he instituted no other division of time for the celebration
of solemn worship. If the latter is said, then it is asked: by whom should
that determination have been made? By the Jews or by the Gentiles? Not by the
Jews, for they did not determine this for themselves, and a determination made
among them is rejected. If God's determination does not bind the Nations, is
it probable that a determination made by the Jews themselves could bind them?
Not by the Gentiles themselves, for then it will follow that the Nations were
not bound to consecrate to God a greater portion of time than they themselves
pleased. This is sufficiently absurd.

*Argument 9.* There are some who argue as follows, both for the whole
Decalogue and for the fourth precept of the Decalogue. If the Gentiles were
bound in some respect to observe the ceremonial law, and God intended this,
then much more were they bound to observe the moral law. But the former is
true. Therefore, and so forth. The consequence of the *major premise* is
evident. They confirm the *minor premise* as follows. If the Gentiles were not
bound to observe the ceremonial law, but could be saved without it, their
condition would then be better than that of the Jews, who, although they could
not be saved through that law, nevertheless could not be saved without it.
But *this is absurd*; therefore so is *the other claim*. The argument is
confirmed by the fact that the ceremonial law was a sufficiently burdensome
yoke. Therefore, if the Gentiles could be saved without the yoke but the Jews
could not, the condition of the former would have been better than that of the
latter. Further, if the Gentiles were not bound to observe the ceremonial
law, their admission into the Jewish Church would have been useless, because
they could have obtained salvation without that admission. But no one who
acknowledges God to be most wise will say that this admission was useless. It
is said in vain that they could have been saved more safely and perfectly
through that law, because then it must be said: 1. that the Gentiles could
have been saved through the law of nature, contrary to the Apostle; 2. that
salvation is granted even ordinarily outside the Church; 3. that the Jews
were saved through ceremonies as if by the work performed. Next, the Jews
were bound to worship God with the worship prescribed by him. Therefore they
were bound to observe the ceremonial law, because that alone was the worship
prescribed by God. That God intended this is evident from the fact that he
prescribed the manner of admitting Gentiles, *Exodus 12:48, 49; 1 Kings 8:41
and following, and 9:3*. If someone says that the Gentiles were not bound to
observe this ceremonial law before it was promulgated and made known to them,
they respond with the following argument, which they propose as follows.

*Argument 10.* If unbelievers are now bound, by force of the Gospel
promulgated by Christ and the Apostles, to believe in Christ and to observe
baptism, the sacred supper, and so forth, then the Gentiles were formerly
bound to observe the Decalogue and the fourth precept of the Decalogue. But
the former is true. Therefore the latter is also true. The consequence of the
*major premise*

[Printed p. 310 | IA PDF 316 | IA leaf 316 | IA decausadei02brow]

is confirmed by the fact that the reasoning is the same and no difference can
be assigned. The *minor premise* is evident, because God is to be worshiped
with the worship prescribed by him. And in the New Testament the Lord has
declared that he is to be worshiped only in this manner. Therefore they are
either bound to observe this manner of worship or none. See *Mark 16:16*.

*Exception 1.* [Negative unbelief is not a sin and therefore cannot condemn,
whether under the law or under the Gospel. Those who sin without the law will
perish without the law, and those who sin outside the Gospel will perish
without it. How will they believe in Him of whom they have not heard?]

*Response 1.* It is doubtful whether, after the law was given and the Gospel
announced, there is any *negative* unbelief, but only *privative* unbelief,
which is sin. The whole world had heard of the Jewish people, and the report
of the miracles performed among them had reached everyone's ears, *1 Kings
8:42*. And the Apostle says of the Gospel that the sound of those proclaiming
the Gospel went out into all the earth, *Romans 10:18*. 2. But let it be
granted that they could not be condemned because of what they had not heard.
They could nevertheless be condemned, and still can be, because they did not
and do not make an effort to understand those means of salvation. They cannot
indeed believe in him of whom they have not yet heard. Yet they are bound to
make an effort to understand who he is in whom alone they may be saved by
believing.

*Exception 2.* [The law was given only to the Jews, but the Gospel to
everyone. Therefore the reasoning is not equal.]

*Response.* The former claim begs the question. Nor is it absolutely true,
although in a certain respect it is most true, *Psalm 147:19, 20*. Likewise,
what is said in the second place is not absolutely true, for at first the
Gospel was announced only to the Jews. And the Apostles had been told: *Do not
go into the way of the Gentiles, and do not enter a city of the Samaritans*.

*Exception 3.* [Nevertheless, after the resurrection Christ at last commanded
the Apostles *to preach the Gospel to the whole created world, Mark 16:15*, and
*to teach all nations, Matthew 28:19*. But Moses and the Prophets were never
commanded to make the law known to another nation. Therefore the reasoning is
unequal.]

*Response 1.* That commission given to the Apostles did not immediately
become known to all nations, and therefore it did not instantly bind them all,
because the Adversaries' rule is that *a law not enacted does not bind*.
Indeed, the Apostles had to begin the proclamation of the Gospel at Jerusalem.
2. This commission did indeed indicate the Apostles' duty, but it did not
bring about the nations' actual obligation. For it no more follows from this
that the nations were bound to believe the Gospel before it was announced to
them than that they were formerly bound to observe the Decalogue before it had
been made known to them. 3. Even today, notwithstanding this commission, very
many nations have never heard the Gospel. Therefore they cannot be condemned
for unbelief. If someone says that they are bound to investigate and search
out those means of salvation, it can be answered that the nations were
likewise formerly bound to seek out the law's meaning and the means of divine
worship. 4. Any difference in the reasoning favors us, insofar as the law of
the Decalogue was the same as the law inscribed upon hearts. The Gospel,
however, is a mystery which does not become known without revelation.

The sum of this argument is that, just as now nothing else is required

[Printed p. 311 | IA PDF 317 | IA leaf 317 | IA decausadei02brow]

to bind the nations than the making known of the Gospel, so formerly no new
and solemn promulgation of the law was necessary to bind the nations, but only
its being made known. And just as the nations are now bound to seek and
investigate the Gospel, so formerly the nations were bound to inquire into
the law and join themselves to the Church of God.
