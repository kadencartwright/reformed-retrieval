## The objections of the Antinomians are answered

Now that the truth has been confirmed and established in the preceding
chapter, it follows that we should vindicate it from the opponents' objections
and refute the arguments by which they try to maintain the contrary. These are
indeed not few, nor can they be answered in a single word; nevertheless, we
shall pursue brevity as far as possible.

*Objection 1.* Believers are freed from the condemnation of the law, for
*“there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ”* (Romans 8:1).
Therefore they are freed from the law itself, for a law that neither curses
nor condemns is no law.

*Answer 1.* We readily grant that believers are freed from the condemnation
of the law as regards their persons—that is, so that they will never in fact
be condemned for having violated the law (Galatians 3:13; Romans 8:33–34; 1
Corinthians 15:55, 56, 56, 57). This notwithstanding, the law can and does have
power to condemn—insofar as such power can belong to a law—all erring actions,
even those of the regenerate. For the law announces God's wrath against all
transgressions, whether they belong to the regenerate or the unregenerate.
That is, it shows the penalty owed because the law has been violated, or says
that the violator, whoever he may be, deserves the penalty imposed, namely
eternal death. See Psalms 130:3 and 143:2. Yet the law remains law even if the
transgressor is not actually punished, for it belongs not to the law but to
the lawgiver to punish the guilty person in fact. It does belong to the law to
establish or declare instrumentally the penalty owed; and the divine law does
this even though believers, because of the death of Christ their surety, do
not pay the penalties of the violated law. Therefore, although believers are
not condemned by the law as regards their persons, their sinful deeds are
nevertheless condemned, because they are violations of the law, and
accordingly they deserve eternal death.

2. Just as God's law establishes the penalty owed by transgressors, so it
establishes the reward owed to those who observe it. And it is no less true
that a law which does not reward is no law than that a law which does not
condemn is no law. From this it is clear that the wicked would be as exempt
from obligation to the law as believers are, because just as believers are
not condemned, so the wicked are not rewarded. Therefore, if some people can
be bound to obey the law even though it is not a rewarding law for them,
others can likewise be obligated to render obedience to the law even though
it is not a condemning law for them. For *rewarding* is said of God's law no
less than *punishing* or *condemning*.

3. Believers under the Old Testament were also freed from the condemnation of
the law. Therefore they too were free from this yoke of the law, and it will
be no less true of them than of New Testament believers that the moral law was
abrogated for them. The opponents, however, do not concede this. This
foundation, therefore, is not solid, and this argument has no force.

4. Adam before the fall was not under the condemnation of the law, yet he was
under obligation to the law. So too were the angels before the fall.
Therefore a law that does not condemn can be

[Printed p. 56 | IA PDF 64 | IA leaf 64 | IA decausadei02brow]

a law and can establish a duty, or declare what duty the Lord and Lawgiver
requires of us. Furthermore, these actions or effects of a law are not
inseparable from one another. There can be a law that merely rewards and does
not establish a penalty for the disobedient. There can also be a law which has
attached to it the threat of a penalty to be inflicted upon transgressors but
does not establish a reward that is owed; and yet each prescribes a duty to
the subject, or declares the Lawgiver's will concerning the subject's duty.

*Objection 2.* Believers are led by the Spirit of God; therefore they do not
need any external law to regulate their external actions.

*Answer 1.* Did believers under the Old Testament have the Spirit as their
guide? See Psalm 51:11; Isaiah 59:21; 44:3; 63:11; and Psalm 143:10. Therefore
this argument proves no more that this is a privilege of those justified under
the New Testament than of those justified under the Old Testament.

2. The Spirit is indeed the principal source and cause of obedience: he
enlightens the eyes of the mind, inclines the heart to obedience, and arouses,
moves, urges, and gently impels it to perform the various things that conform
to the law. See Isaiah 30:21; Matthew 10:19; Romans 8:26; Revelation 7:17. Yet
he is not the rule or standard of our actions. Rather, he presupposes a law
that exists and actually binds, because he always directs, moves, inclines,
and urges us to observe God's law. Whatever helps us to obey a ruler is not
for that reason the ruler's command itself: the shipmaster steers the ship,
but he is not therefore the mariner's compass. The Spirit does indeed make us
fit to obey the law, but he is not the law itself. The law is law even if no
one observes it.

3. The inference is therefore utterly bad: believers have the Spirit as their
author and impelling cause; therefore they do not need the law. For
subordinate things do not conflict, and an efficient cause does not remove an
exemplary cause. The same Spirit who leads along the right way dictated the
rule. The Spirit who works inwardly moves, urges, inclines, and effectually
leads the soul. He dictated the Scriptures themselves, which contain our rule
and standard. Therefore the Spirit who is in believers does not contradict
the Spirit who is in Scripture: the Spirit does not oppose himself.

4. It is therefore absurd to reject the Spirit speaking in the word and
setting forth there the rule of life and conduct for us because of the Spirit
who moves and leads within. Those who reject God's law therefore reject the
Spirit, because the Spirit is in the law. They reject that Spirit who is the
supreme judge of all controversies, against whose judgment pronounced in the
Scriptures all private spirits must be tested, and indeed also all the
motions, effects, words, and pronouncements of the true Spirit dwelling in us,
according to 1 John 4:1; John 5:39; and Isaiah 8:20.

*Reply.* But that Spirit of life is a rule or law and is called a law in
Romans 8:2. What need is there, then, of an external rule where this internal
rule is possessed?

*Answer 1.* That Spirit of life, or life-giving Spirit, is called a law by
reason of his efficacy, because he works authoritatively and effectually; and
thus he is improperly called a law, just as sin and death are in the same
passage. This is not because he is our standard or directs us perfectly, for
this efficacy of Christ's Spirit in believers is imperfect and is greater in
some but less in others. God's law, however,

[Printed p. 57 | IA PDF 65 | IA leaf 65 | IA decausadei02brow]

is perfect and the same with respect to everyone. This law of the Spirit of
life is called imperfect with respect to the effects which it produces in
believers, for it works according to the capacity of the recipient and
produces greater holiness and conformity to God's will in some than in
others. But God's law perfectly reveals God's mind and equally declares or
displays God's will to everyone. The law of sin of which the apostle speaks in
the same passage is not the law by which we judge concerning sin or which
makes sins known to us. Rather, it is called a law metaphorically, because
just as a law moves, impels, urges, and as it were compels us to render
obedience, so this power of sin and corruption in us moves, urges, impels, or
compels us to sin no less effectually than the law moves us to obey. Thus this
law of the Spirit of life is called a law because it moves, impels, and urges
us to obey as if it were a law.

2. No privilege peculiar to believers under the New Testament can be inferred
from this. For this same law of the Spirit of life freed believers under the
Old Testament from the law of sin and death. They did not walk according to
the flesh but according to the Spirit; and consequently that righteous
requirement of the law was fulfilled in them, for even then Christ condemned
sin by virtue of the price that was eventually to be paid.

*Objection 3.* The Old Testament administration differs from the Gospel
administration in this: the former was an external ministry, but the latter
an internal one. Thus Holy Scripture, or that letter, is not our rule or law;
rather, the law of life written in our hearts is.

*Answer 1.* This distinction between the Old or Mosaic administration and the
New or Gospel administration has no foundation in Scripture. For we see that
the Gospel dispensation is also in part external. We too have external
institutions, namely the proclamation of the word and administration of the
sacraments. Christ himself proclaimed the Gospel publicly and with his living
voice; his apostles, in accordance with his command, both preached and sent
others to preach; and they committed the teaching of the Gospel to writing so
that others might believe that Jesus is the Christ and, believing, might have
eternal life through his name (John 20:31; 1 John 5:13).

*Reply.* But that dispensation pertains to Christ in the flesh, not to Christ
in the Spirit.

*Answer.* This distinction too is fabricated without any reason and rests on
no testimony of Scripture. But let us grant that such a distinction exists.
We ask, when did that dispensation of Christ in the Spirit begin? Was it not
after Christ's ascension and session at the Father's right hand? Yet the
following were external effects of that ascension, as may be seen in Acts
2:33 and Ephesians 4:8, 11 compared with Psalm 68:18. Indeed, Christ himself,
speaking of the Spirit and promising him, says in John 16:13 that the Spirit
would guide them on the way into all truth; and in John 14:26 he says that the
Advocate, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father was going to send in his name,
would teach them all things and bring to their remembrance what he himself had
told them. He also says in John 15:26 that the Spirit would bear witness about
him. And in John 16:14–15 he says something also worthy of notice: *“He will
glorify me, because he will take what is mine and announce it to you. All
things the Father has are mine; therefore I said that he will take what is
mine and announce it to you.”*

2. Was there nothing internal in the Mosaic administration? Was no internal
grace then granted, no operation of the Spirit in the souls of believers, no

[Printed p. 58 | IA PDF 66 | IA leaf 66 | IA decausadei02brow]

effect of the indwelling Holy Spirit? Since this cannot be said, why should
they not also have had to acknowledge this internal law of life as their only
rule of life and conduct?

3. The law of life written in hearts is imperfect, sometimes obscure; it
increases, and sometimes decreases and fails; and it admits of varying degrees
in different people. These things cannot be said about the divine law. That
law is the same with respect to everyone, binds everyone equally, and is
always perfect and complete in every respect. It does not increase today and
diminish tomorrow, as our internal grace does.

4. Christ and his apostles nowhere direct us to this rule or law, but to the
written law: John 5:39; Colossians 4:16; Luke 10:26–27; Ephesians 6:1; 2
Timothy 3:15–16; 2 Peter 1:18–19; Romans 15:4. As is clear, they commend this
alone for us to learn and show by their practice that it is to be employed.
For from it Christ himself refuted Satan's temptations (Matthew 4:4, 10) and
confirmed his office and teaching (Luke 24:27, 32). The apostles did the same.
See Acts 22:20; Romans 3:10 and following; 4:1 and following; 9:7, 12 and
following, 33; 10:8, 11, 20; 11:26; together with many others, all of which it
would take too long to cite.
