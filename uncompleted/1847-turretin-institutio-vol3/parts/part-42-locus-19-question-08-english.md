QUESTION VIII.

ON THE EFFICACY OF THE SACRAMENTS.

Do the sacraments of the New Testament physically work grace in such a way
that they effect and contain it *ex opere operato*, whether faith and devotion
are present or not? Or are they rather efficacious signs and seals of grace?
We deny the former and affirm the latter against the papists.

[Marginalia: In this question the Socinians err by defect.]

I. In the question about the efficacy of the sacraments, error occurs both by
defect and by excess. The Socinians err by defect, maintaining that the
sacraments are only external marks of profession by which we are distinguished
from others. We disputed against them above. They are joined by those who wish
the sacraments to be bare signs and mere figures that only arouse and nourish
faith through the representation of the object, but not through an efficacious
sealing and exhibition in lawful use.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 309 | IA leaf 343 | PDF p. 343]

The papists and Lutherans wrongly ascribe this opinion to us, because, as was
observed above, we maintain that they are efficacious and practical signs that
truly seal the thing signified to us and exhibit it in their own manner.

[Marginalia: By excess.]

II. All those err by excess who attribute to the sacraments that they are
vehicles and vessels containing grace, or who make them real and subordinate
causes of grace. Such are

[Marginalia: The Lutherans.]

those among the Lutherans who wish the sacraments to be *ochēmata*, or channels
of grace, and physical means, or real and instrumental causes, through which
the benefits obtained by Christ’s death are exhibited and conferred. They
therefore charge us with saying that the sacraments are only seals signifying
and sealing grace, as Gerhard does on page 500 of *On the Sacraments*.

[Marginalia: And the papists.]

But especially such are the papists, who contend that the sacraments of the
New Testament are true and proper physical causes that effect and confer grace
*ex opere operato*. This was defined at the Council of Trent, Session 7, Canons
6, 7, and 8, where three things are decreed. First, they contain the grace that
they signify. Second, they always give grace to everyone who places no
obstacle. Third, they give it *ex opere operato*, that is, by the force of the
external action, without any movement, devotion, piety, faith, or any other
preparation of the recipient. Canon 6 says: “If anyone says that the sacraments
of the new law do not contain the grace that they signify or do not confer
grace itself upon those placing no obstacle, etc., let him be anathema.” And
Canon 8: “If anyone says that grace is not conferred *ex opere operato* through
the sacraments themselves of the new law, but that faith alone in the divine
promise suffices to obtain grace, let him be anathema.”

III. There is indeed a great division of opinions among the papists about this
efficacy. Some wish the sacraments to contain grace as an effect in its
instrumental and efficient cause, which works in the soul a disposition to
receive grace. Others say that they contain it through an inherent created
quality, as medicine is contained in a vessel; according to them, the
sacraments are physical causes of grace. Others say it is through an assisting
power, because when the sacraments are applied, God produces and confers grace
by covenant; thus they are conditions and causes without which it is not
given. Still others explain it in other ways. On these, see Chamier, *On the
Sacraments*, book 2, chapter 1, and Forbes, *Historical-Theological
Instruction*, book 9, chapter 2. Yet they all agree that the sacraments contain
grace and are its vehicles. They assert that when the sacraments are lawfully
administered, they actually work grace and confer it proximately, immediately,
and *ex opere operato*. For this reason the council wished to remain at a more
general level, affirming that the sacraments contain and produce grace but
defining nothing about the mode, so that it might conceal the scholastics’
diverse opinions and so balance its words that no party could complain that
it had been prejudged, playing in buskins after the usual fashion.

IV. By *opus operatum*, however, the fathers of the council understand the
external celebration of the sacraments, which is the cause and instrument of
grace for those who place no obstacle of mortal sin, although otherwise they
are devoid of faith and piety. They distinguish between actions by which piety
is aroused and which produce grace *ex opere operantis*, that is, not by the
powers of the work itself but by those of the person acting and by his
devotion, and actions that procure grace *ex opere operato*, that is, not from
the devotion of the one acting or receiving the work, but by the power of the
work itself. They wish the sacraments to be of the latter kind. In this they
follow the opinion of the old scholastics, such as Biel, Paludanus, Scotus,
Albertus, and others, who exclude a good movement of the recipient. Biel says
in chapter 6 on John: “The *opus operatum* is the completion of the external
work without an internal movement.” Biel says in chapter 3: “A sacrament is
said to confer grace *ex opere operato* in such a way that, beyond the outward
exhibition of the sign, no good movement is required in the recipient.” More
recent authors such as Bellarmine, Konink, and Valentia do not wish good
movements, a disposition of faith,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 310 | IA leaf 344 | PDF p. 344]

and the exclusion of an obstacle to be excluded by this phrase, but only merit.
Thus, for grace to be conferred *ex opere operato* is the same as for it to be
conferred by the force of the sacramental action itself instituted by God, not
by the merit of the one acting or receiving. But here the adversaries practice
deception and do not depart from the scholastics’ mind, whatever distinction
they seek. (1) The Bellarminian definition maintains that grace is actively
and proximately effected by the external sacramental action. Indeed, against
those who interpose an act of faith, Bellarmine argues that it is effected
immediately, and he excludes faith and an internal movement from the efficacy
of sacramental grace. (2) Moreover, his distinction of the new sacraments from
the old in this way—that the old did not properly work grace, but either the
recipient’s faith and devotion or those of the whole synagogue did so—proves
that a movement of faith is not required, because a sacrament administered to
infants is valid even though they lack faith. (3) Finally, whether they wish
it or not, in these matters they teach the same as the ancients, whose opinion
our theologians deservedly assail. Since they exclude all merit, by that very
fact they exclude every good movement and good thought, which they wish to be
meritorious.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the orthodox.]

V. Our opinion is that the sacraments do not work grace physically and *ex
opere operato*, as though they had an imparted and inherent power to confer
and effect grace. They work only morally and hyperphysically, insofar as they
are signs and seals that, in lawful use, seal and exhibit grace to believers,
while God, through the power of the Holy Spirit, truly performs and fulfills
in believers whatever he promises and portrays in the signs. Thus, according
to us, a twofold efficacy is attributed to the sacraments. One is moral and
objective, by which the sacraments present to our mind the object that they
are appointed to signify and seal. In this way faith is either aroused or
confirmed, and through it hope and sanctification are increased. The other is
covenantal, by which God, in sealing his promise or covenant through the
sacraments, exhibits the promised things themselves to the believing soul, or
else a greater sense and perception of things already exhibited, and through
both produces greater operations. Hence the sacraments are rightly called
exhibitive, provided the exhibition is understood not as physical through
some real containment, real conjunction, or power really implanted in the
element, but as moral: that grace is objectively exhibited to the mind and,
together with it, really to the believing soul.

VI. Therefore the question here is not (1) whether the sacraments are
efficacious. Both sides acknowledge this. Rather, how do they exercise their
efficacy? Is it as physical causes that confer and effect grace by a power
imparted and implanted in them, as our opponents contend? Although some deny
that the sacraments are physical rather than moral causes, when they defend
the *opus operatum*, they nevertheless make this efficiency physical in fact.
Or are they moral causes in whose presence God works, as we maintain? (2) The
question is not whether faith and devotion are required in the use of a
sacrament, which the adversaries do not deny. Rather, does the external action
of the sacrament produce grace *ex opere operato*, whether faith is present or
not? Or does this depend solely upon the Holy Spirit accompanying the external
sign in lawful use, as we assert? Thus the question may be reduced to these
terms: are the sacraments of the New Testament true, proper, and immediate
causes of justification that, by an efficacy implanted in the signs
themselves, effect and confer holiness and righteousness upon everyone who
receives only the sacrament’s external action, even though otherwise devoid
of faith and piety, provided that they place no obstacle of mortal sin? The
papists affirm; we deny.

[Marginalia: It is proved that the sacraments are not physical causes. 1.
Because they are seals.]

VII. First, the sacraments are nowhere called vehicles or receptacles of grace,
but are expressly called signs and seals of the covenant of grace (Rom. 4:11;
Gen. 17:10; Ex. 12:13). The nature of signs and seals is that they

[1847 vol. III printed p. 311 | IA leaf 345 | PDF p. 345]

contain nothing in themselves or confer anything by an inherent power, but
signify grace, whether already conferred or to be conferred later, and seal it
to those to whom it belongs.
Just as efficiency differs in its entire genus from signification, so the
principle for instituting efficient and signifying instruments differs. This
is evident by induction, both in miraculous signs such as the rainbow,
Gideon’s fleece, and Hezekiah’s sundial, which contained or worked nothing in
themselves but only signified and sealed, and in ordinary signs, to which
signification, not efficiency, is attributed.

[Marginalia: 2. Because they presuppose grace.]

VIII. Second, the sacraments cannot work or confer by a power implanted in
themselves what, according to God’s institution, they presuppose or require
beforehand in the subject, such as the grace of justification and regeneration.
For God does not wish the sacraments to be administered indiscriminately to
adults, but to those who, having first been instructed, believe and are
partakers of grace through faith. Nor does he wish them to be administered
indiscriminately to the infants of unbelievers, but to the infants of
believers. Thus Abraham received the sign of circumcision, “a seal of the
righteousness of faith received in uncircumcision” (Rom. 4:11). The eunuch
(Acts 8:37–38), Paul (Acts 9:11, 17–18), Cornelius (Acts 10:34–35, 37), and
others, once they had believed and received the remission of sins through
faith (Acts 26:18), were baptized for the remission of sins, that is, so that
they might obtain God’s testimony of it (Acts 2:38).

[Marginalia: 3. Because the Word is not a physical cause.]

IX. Third, the Word does not confer grace through any power implanted in its
sound or letters, but solely through the power of the Spirit who accompanies
it. The principle of the Word and of the sacraments is the same here, and they
confer grace in the same manner, insofar as both are external means that God
uses to confer grace upon us. Moreover, the principle of the sound in the Word
is the same as that of the external action in the sacrament. Now there is no
power in the sound, because the Word does not benefit unless mixed with faith,
without which the sound affects the ears, not the heart. It is said in vain
that the Word and the sacraments differ because the Word is the hand offering
and the sacraments the hand applying. (1) Both the hand offering and the hand
applying require a receiving hand, that is, our faith. Therefore, just as the
spoken Word does not justify except through the mediation of faith, and not by
the power of the external sound, so the sacraments, which are the visible Word,
do not justify except through the mediation of faith and consequently not *ex
opere operato*, or by the power of the external action alone. (2) Every hand
applying is also an offering hand, because that hand is called applying which
offers particularly.

[Marginalia: 4. Because grace is not bound to the sacraments.]

X. Fourth, if the sacraments physically contained grace in themselves and
conferred it by an implanted power, grace would be bound to the sacraments.
This cannot be said without absurdity: (1) because God is a perfectly free
agent; (2) because it belongs to God alone to confer grace by an implanted
power; and (3) because many are saved without the sacraments, such as the
thief on the cross, and many are condemned with them, such as Judas and Simon
Magus.

[Marginalia: 5. Because what is corporeal cannot act upon the soul.]

XI. Fifth, nothing corporeal can by its own power effect something spiritual
or act upon the soul. But the sacrament’s *opus operatum*, or external action,
is something corporeal, which by its own power cannot effect grace, which is
spiritual. Nor should it be said that a corporeal thing cannot act upon a
spiritual thing of itself but can do so by the power of God, who elevates the
instrumental cause when God uses it for this end. For that power would have to
constitute in the corporeal thing some quality proportioned to the object upon
which it acts, but God infuses into the signs themselves or the external
sacramental action no power or quality proportioned to the object upon which
the sacraments ought to act, namely, the soul. Otherwise it will have to be
said that, just as the soul is spiritual, so

[1847 vol. III printed p. 312 | IA leaf 346 | PDF p. 346]

a spiritual quality is infused by God’s power into the sacramental signs or
the *opus operatum*, which is absurd. The sacraments are only instrumental
causes, not physical, but objective and moral causes, in whose presence God
works that spiritual grace.

[Marginalia: 6. Because faith is required for the efficacy of a sacrament.]

XII. Sixth, faith, devotion, and an internal movement of the soul are required
for the efficacy of a sacrament. Scripture expressly asserts this (Mark 16:16;
1 Cor. 11:27; Acts 2:38); nothing can please God without faith (Heb. 11:6);
the promise contained in the sacraments and faith are correlates; and the
papists themselves acknowledge that the state of grace and a conscience
cleansed of mortal sin are required for the efficacy of a sacrament in adults,
which cannot exist without faith and devotion. Indeed, the very act of placing
no obstacle that they require is an effect of grace and faith, since a person
devoid of grace and faith cannot help placing an obstacle. Although faith does
not belong to the essence of the sacrament, just as it does not belong to the
essence of the preaching of the Word, nevertheless, just as the latter does
no good without faith (Heb. 4:2), so the efficacy of the sacraments is not
saving in unbelievers. They are washed in body with water, but not in heart
with grace; they eat the Lord’s bread, but not the bread who is the Lord. Thus
in former times it was customary to require faith and its confession from
those admitted to the sacrament of initiation (Acts 8:36–37). The creed chiefly
served this purpose.

XIII. Seventh, since the sacraments cannot be efficacious except under the
aspect of signs and seals, all their efficacy must depend upon two things:
first, what the sign objectively offers to the mind; and second, the
disposition of the faculty to which the sign is offered. A sign effects
nothing and is not naturally suited to move objectively unless it encounters
a well-disposed faculty. This is the difference between physical and moral
causes: the power of physical causes is such that they neither know themselves
nor need the intervention of knowledge in the thing upon which they act in
order to unfold and exercise themselves. But signs, like all other objective
causes, although unaware of themselves, do nothing except where and because
they are known. The sacraments can be said to act upon us objectively in three
ways. (1) They remind us of their author, which moves us to piety and reverence.
(2) They recall to mind the reasons by which God was moved to institute the
sacraments, which instills a powerful sense of gratitude in the soul. (3) They
make the thing itself that they are appointed to seal present to the soul as
far as a sign can, which confirms faith, increases hope, and advances
sanctification.

[Marginalia: 8. Because no effects can arise from the signs.]

XIV. Eighth, the sacraments can have no effects arising from the signs
themselves through an inherent efficacy, whether effects produced in us
without us or effects produced in us through us. The former, such as adoption,
love, reconciliation, and the like, cannot arise from them, both because water
baptism comes after this kind of effect and because no cause of them apart
from God can be given; otherwise his grace would not be the first and sole
cause of our salvation. Nor can the latter, such as a sense of justification
and sanctification, arise from them. For if, for example, a sense of
justification were physically produced through the sacraments, a person would
not be justified by faith, contrary to Paul, since in the papists’ opinion the
sacraments’ operation through faith is opposed to their physical operation.
Nor can sanctification arise from them, because it is produced by the Holy
Spirit and faith. But no physically acting instrument is applied to an effect
already produced.

[Marginalia: 9. From 1 Peter 3:21.]

XV. Ninth, if the sacramental signs effected what they signify by some power
inherent in themselves, the washing away of the filth of the flesh in baptism
would save the person baptized, contrary to Peter (1 Pet. 2:

[1847 vol. III printed p. 313 | IA leaf 347 | PDF p. 347]

21): “The corresponding antitype, baptism, now saves us also, not by putting
away the filth of the flesh but by the pledge of a good conscience, etc.” Here
a twofold ground is given: (1) salvation is denied to arise from washing away
filth, and (2) it is ascribed to the answer of a good conscience in opposition
to the outward washing. However the *eperōtēma* of a good conscience is
understood—whether as the pledge of faith that was made in prescribed words
before baptism when the catechumen was questioned about the matters belonging
to the inward sense of faith, “Do you believe?” “I believe,” etc.; as the
pledge of eternal life; or as amendment of the soul explained through its
effect—it is certain that an inward cleansing of the soul is thus designated,
which is not effected by that external application of the visible element.

[Marginalia: 10. From the fathers.]

XVI. Tenth, the fathers repeatedly inculcate this by distinguishing the
efficiency of the Spirit and of the element. Cyprian, or whoever is the author
of the sermon *On the Baptism of Christ*, says: “Whether the remission of sins
is given through baptism or through other sacraments, it properly belongs to
the Holy Spirit, and the privilege of this efficiency remains his alone, etc.”
Gregory of Nyssa says in his oration *On the Baptism of Christ*: “Water does
not bestow this benefit, for it is more sublime than every creature; rather,
God’s command and the approach of the Spirit, who comes mystically to our
liberation, bestow it. Water, however, serves to demonstrate the cleansing.”
Augustine says in Tractate 6 on the Epistle of John: “The water of the sacrament
is one thing; the water that signifies God’s Spirit is another. The water of
the sacrament is visible; the water of the Spirit is invisible. The former
washes the body and signifies what is done in the soul; through the Spirit
the soul itself is cleansed and nourished.” See the same author in question 84
on Leviticus, Letter 23, and Tractate 80 on John; Bernard in his sermon *On the
Lord’s Supper*; Nazianzen in Oration 40; and others in Chamier, chapters 10
and 21.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVII. If the sacraments are said to forgive sins, save, and regenerate (Acts
2:38; 22:16; Eph. 5:26; Titus 3:5; 1 Pet. 3:21), they do not perform this by
a proper, immediate, and internal power, as is evident from 1 Peter 3:21, but
sacramentally and improperly. They are efficacious signs by which those
benefits are signified and sealed and are exhibited in lawful use.

XVIII. The sacraments confer what is promised objectively and morally by
arousing faith to embrace grace and by sealing that grace, not efficiently by
newly producing, drawing forth, or impressing some quality. They are rightly
called practical signs, not because by some imparted power they truly,
properly, and immediately attain the effect, but because in their presence and
lawful use God certainly works to exhibit the thing itself. Thus the striking
of the rock with the staff (Ex. 17) was a practical sign of what followed,
even though it did not bring the water forth immediately and by its own power.

XIX. When the remission of sins is attributed to baptism (Acts 22:16), it is
attributed to it only under the aspect of a sign and seal, not under the aspect
of a true, proper, and immediate physical cause. Remission consists in the
judgment of the offended God and therefore cannot be attributed to an external
and corporeal thing except under the aspect of a sign and seal signifying and
sealing that judgment. Moreover, although this remission is attributed to
baptism, it does not belong to baptism by reason of the external action
properly, but by reason of the recipient’s internal grace and faith, without
which every external washing is in vain.

XX. It is one thing to speak of baptism and another to speak of baptism’s
external action. It does not follow that because saving power is attributed to
baptism (Mark 16:16), that power therefore inheres in its external action.
Whatever saving power is attributed to baptism cannot belong to it except
through the mediation of faith: “Whoever believes and is baptized will be
saved.” This is evident from the opposite. It does not say that the person who
does not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 314 | IA leaf 348 | PDF p. 348]

believe and is not baptized will be condemned, but simply, “the person who
does not believe,” indicating that the absence of faith alone, not of baptism,
condemns. Therefore life-giving power must be attributed not to external
baptism, but to the internal grace that produces faith.

XXI. When John the Baptist says of himself (Matt. 3:11), “I baptize you with
water,” and of Christ, “He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit,” he does not
compare baptism with baptism, but person with person, to demonstrate Christ’s
excellence above himself. To Christ alone it belongs to administer internal
baptism, or confer the thing signified, whereas John was only the administrator
of the external sign. The same can be said of all ministers, who can indeed
give water, but not the Spirit. Yet nothing can be inferred from this for the
efficiency of the sacrament or external sign. Indeed, external water baptism
is opposed to the internal baptism of the Spirit, indicating that the
conferring of the Spirit cannot belong to the former, but only to the latter.
We will speak of this below when the baptism of John is treated.

XXII. The water from which we must be born again (John 3:5) does not signify
external baptism, because salvation can be obtained without it, but the power
of the Holy Spirit acting after the manner of water in the cleansing and
renewal of the soul. Thus the word *Spirit* is added after *water* by way of
explanation, so that the figurative word *water* may be explained by the
literal word, through a hendiadys: that is, by spiritual water, or the Spirit
acting after the manner of water.

XXIII. When Paul says in 1 Corinthians 10:17, “We who are many are all one
body, because we partake of one bread,” he does not wish participation in one
bread to be the cause of our being one body. He does not say, “We who are many
are one body because we partake of one bread,” but “who partake of one bread.”
Therefore participation in one bread indeed proves that we are one body, but
not by an argument drawn from the cause of that unity, only from its sign and
testimony. This is confirmed by comparison: in corporeal things we rightly
prove that members belong to the same body because they are nourished by the
same food, but it is false that the same food effects the unity of the members
in the body. They must be members of one body before they are nourished by the
same food.

XXIV. Although Peter compares the ark in which Noah and his family were saved
with baptism (1 Pet. 3:21), (1) it does not follow that the likeness holds in
all things, but only in some: namely, as the ark efficaciously saved Noah from
the flood, so also baptism saves. But it does not follow that, as the ark saved
physically and without divine institution, so does baptism. (2) The correction
added by Peter expressly teaches that he speaks not of external but internal
baptism, and therefore that salvation is not to be ascribed to the former but
to the latter. He says that the baptism that saves us is not “the putting away
of the filth of the flesh,” that is, external water baptism, but “the answer
of a good conscience,” that is, internal baptism or spiritual cleansing, which
he calls from its effect “the answer of a good conscience before God.” The
person who is inwardly pure and cleansed by Christ’s blood answers the
questioning spirit with a good conscience and filial confidence, and in turn
asks God and requests necessary things from him with the most certain
confidence of being heard. But it is wrongly said that Peter compares not
internal and external baptism with one another, but Jewish baptisms with the
baptism of Christians. The apostle’s purpose was not to teach that Christians
are not saved by Jewish baptisms, because there was no reason to inculcate
this when no one either affirmed or suspected that Christians were saved by
Jewish washings. And since Bellarmine proves by no argument his assertion that
“the putting away of the filth of the flesh” means Jewish washings, it is
rejected with the same ease with which it is proposed.

XXV. Although the sacraments are applied to those who do not understand, such
as infants

[1847 vol. III printed p. 315 | IA leaf 349 | PDF p. 349]

and the mentally impaired, it does not follow that they are true and proper
causes effecting holiness rather than signs and seals. 1. This is evident from
circumcision, which was administered to infants, although according to the
papists’ own opinion it could not confer grace *ex opere operato*, because
according to them this is a prerogative of the sacraments of the New Testament.
2. Those who understand are of two kinds. Some possess both first and second
act, namely, those who actually conceive the thing proposed in the present.
Others understand only in first act, namely, those who by natural capacity are
capable of understanding. It is foolish to apply the sacraments to those who
do not understand in first act, but we affirm that they can be applied to
those who, although capable of understanding, do not understand in second act.
With respect to the mentally impaired and those asleep, no example exists of
their being baptized. With respect to the deaf, we say that they can use sight
and touch and are not devoid of mind. Thus the sacraments, which move the
intellect through sight and touch, can be applied to them.

XXVI. Although the preached Word might arouse faith better than the sacraments,
it would not follow that their institution should be neglected. With respect
to knowledge, the spoken Word indeed arouses faith better than the sacraments,
but not with respect to confidence or certainty, for pledges and seals possess
greater certainty than a bare promise.

XXVII. It is absurd to say that a picture of baptism depicted on a panel can
do the same as baptism and make the promise visible. A panel depicting baptism
does not correspond to the institution, which commands water and washing with
water. No picture of washing is a washing or washes anyone.

XXVIII. Certainty about the nature and truth of the sacrament is one thing;
certainty about its fruit is another. There is no doubt that the latter depends
upon the recipient’s faith no less than upon Christ’s institution. The former,
however, arises solely from Christ’s institution. If the sacrament is
administered according to it, it is certain that the thing itself is sealed
and exhibited in lawful use.
