### CHAPTER VI

#### On the Sufficiency of Scripture, Book 4

##### THESIS I

Bell.: *The controversy consists in two points, though he nevertheless makes
three divisions. 1. We assert that the whole doctrine necessary either for
faith or for morals is not expressly contained in the Scriptures, and that
therefore, besides the written Word of God, the unwritten Word of God is also
required—that is, divine and Apostolic traditions. But they teach that
everything necessary for faith and morals is contained in Scripture, and that
therefore there is no need for any unwritten word. 2. They think that the
Apostles instituted certain things besides Scripture which pertain to the
rites and order of the Church, yet are not necessary or commanded, but free;
but that they handed down nothing besides Scripture which necessarily
pertains to faith or morals. We, however, acknowledge Apostolic traditions of
every kind: concerning faith, morals, permanent and temporary matters,
universal and particular matters, and things necessary and free. 3. They think
that Apostolic traditions, if there were any, cannot now be certainly
demonstrated. We assert, on the contrary, that certain ways and methods by
which Apostolic traditions may be shown are not lacking.*

Prot.: In this statement of the question we find almost nothing wanting,
except that in the first division the word *expressly* is used ambiguously.
For the question is not whether everything necessary is contained in
Scripture in so many words, but whether it is contained either in an express
statement or to be gathered from it by necessary consequence. In the second
division also, those who deny that certain things instituted by the Apostles
are *necessary* must be understood as speaking of perpetual and universal
necessity. For whatever was instituted by the Apostles was also necessary for
that time and for those Churches for which it was instituted. What is said in
the third division about *certainty* must likewise be understood as certainty
of divine faith, not another kind.

##### The Roman Catholics' Arguments

2. Bell.: *For two thousand years, from Adam to Moses,*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 70 | printed p. 60 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*religion was preserved by tradition alone. Scripture is therefore not simply
necessary.*

Prot.: 1. By similar reasoning one may argue that religion was preserved for
a long time not only without the Roman Pontiff, but also without Baptism, the
Lord's Supper, and similar institutions; therefore these are not simply
necessary. 2. It is false that throughout that time religion was preserved by
ordinary tradition alone, as if from one person's hand into another's. The
living voice of God was almost continually sounding in the Church. As it
gradually ceased, writing afterward succeeded it and now has the same
necessity which the living voice formerly had.

3. Bell.: *When Scriptures afterward existed, they belonged only to the Jews,
while others belonging to the Church used tradition alone, as Job and his
friends did. The Jews themselves also used tradition more than Scripture.*

Prot.: 1. If any godly people lived outside the visible Church, God dealt with
them outside the ordinary order. The question, however, concerns the ordinary
necessity of Scripture in God's Church. 2. Job and his friends lived before
the written Law and had the living voice of God, as is evident from the book
of Job. 3. Others who joined themselves to the Jewish Church after the written
Law had the use of the Scriptures, as is evident from the history of the
Eunuch, Acts 8. 4. It is manifestly false that the Jews used tradition more
than Scripture. Their duty was continually to have the words of the Law before
their eyes, in their heart, and in their mouth, Deuteronomy 6:6–9. The accounts
frequently mentioned in the Scriptures intimately belong to the use of
Scripture, so far are they from excluding it.

4. Bell.: *For many years after Christ's advent, the Church existed without
the Scriptures.*

Prot.: 1. The Church was not without all Scripture. 2. That it was for a time
without Apostolic writings arose from this: the Apostles had first to preach
the Gospel by the living voice and afterward to write. 3. The fact that the
most wise God willed the Apostles to commit to writing as soon as possible the
Gospel preached by the living voice itself demonstrates the necessity of
Scripture.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 71 | printed p. 61 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Thus far Bellarmine has tried to show that the Scriptures are not necessary,
but traditions are. Now he wishes to prove that the Scriptures are not
sufficient without traditions, although traditions were sufficient without
the Scriptures. Thus by a twofold blasphemy he exalts traditions above the
Scriptures.

5. Bell.: *Either the whole canon taken together is sufficient, or the
individual books are sufficient by themselves. The individual books are not,
as is conceded; nor is the whole canon, because many truly canonical books
have perished.*

Prot.: 1. The enumeration in this disjunction is not exhaustive. For the
extant books of Scripture considered together are neither one individual book
nor, as Bellarmine judges, the whole canon; yet we affirm these books to be
sufficient. 2. Even if one or another of those which now exist had perished,
such as the Second and Third Epistles of John, the rest would nevertheless be
sufficient for the Church's salvation. For in the whole canon God has provided
not merely sufficiently but abundantly for our salvation. 3. We deny that any
book of the canon has perished, and do so for those reasons by which Bellarmine
himself, book 2, chapters 2 and 7, proved that the Hebrew and Greek copies were
not corrupted universally and altogether. For not everything the Prophets or
Apostles wrote is immediately canonical, but what God set apart for that end.
4. If canonical books have perished, this was the broad fault of the Pontiffs,
who also can and ought to restore them to the Church for us out of the
fullness of infallible knowledge which they are said to possess.

6. Bell.: *If Christ and the Apostles had intended to restrict the Word of God
to Scripture, then it would be evident from the Scriptures that the Apostles
wrote at the Lord's command. But we nowhere read this; indeed, they applied
their minds to writing only when compelled by some necessity.*

Prot.: 1. The consequence is denied if an outward, express command is meant.
2. That the Apostles had an inward command follows from what Bellarmine himself
admits in chapter 3, namely, that they wrote because God willed, prompted, and
inspired them. 3. John is commanded twelve times in the Apocalypse to *write*.
What is expressed concerning one is understood concerning the others, as in
the Old Testament. Thus Augustine also affirms the same

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 72 | printed p. 62 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

concerning them all in *On the Harmony of the Evangelists*, book 1, chapter
35: *Whatever Christ willed us to read concerning his deeds and sayings, he
commanded them to write as though they were his own hands.* 4. The Apostles
were no more compelled to write than to preach. 5. The instance of Churches
which Bellarmine says entreated certain Apostles to write sufficiently shows
that the necessity of Scripture was known to the Church at that time. 6.
Neither on account of God's command nor on account of the writer's intention,
both of which cannot be denied in Moses, does Bellarmine concede the
sufficiency of the Scripture written by him. This whole argument therefore has
nothing to do with the question.

7. Bell.: *If they had wished to commit their doctrine to writing, they would
certainly have composed a Catechism or a similar book, or all together would
have published some common book.*

Prot.: 1. Who will arrogate this to himself, that he should counsel the Holy
Spirit and prescribe his manner of acting? 2. Moses wished to commit his
doctrine to writing, yet he wrote historically, as did the Evangelists. 3.
Bellarmine speaks here so absurdly, as though he wished to conclude that the
Apostles did not wish to commit their doctrine to writing. 4. He also acts
foolishly, if not blasphemously, when he implies that there would have been
greater sufficiency in one Catechism than is now found in all the Apostles'
writings.

8. Bell.: *There are many things which cannot be unknown and yet are not
contained in the Scriptures.* Prot.: Bellarmine has nothing of that kind.

1. *In the time of the Old Testament, women had some remedy by which they were
cleansed from original sin, just as circumcision was instituted for males,
according to the opinion of many. But Scripture nowhere contains what existed
for women.* Prot.: 1. If there was some remedy necessary for all women for
salvation, then it is contained in the Scriptures by Bellarmine's confession
in chapter 11, where he says, *All things necessary for everyone have been
written.* For a remedy necessary for women is just as necessary for everyone
as that remedy is necessary for males. 2. If there was a singular sacramental
remedy peculiar to women, no reason can even be imagined why it should not
have been placed in the written Law just as much as those more minute things
which pertain to

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 73 | printed p. 63 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the purification of a woman after childbirth, Leviticus 12. 3. According to
Bellarmine's opinion in *On the Effect of the Sacraments*, book 2, chapter 13,
neither circumcision nor any sacrament of the Old Testament cleansed from sin
or justified. His sophistical impudence is therefore intolerable when in this
place he says that *without doubt such a remedy cleansing from sin was
necessary for women*, while affirming only *according to the opinion of many*
that circumcision was instituted for men for that end—especially since he
himself admits that many of the ancients thought otherwise, *On the Sacrament
of Baptism*, book 1, chapter 4. 4. It is false that circumcision was instituted
only for males. Although it was performed only on males, it was not for males
alone, but for the whole seed of Abraham, Genesis 17:9–10. 5. Bellarmine was
not unaware that the Scholastics, with Lombard, book 4, distinction 1,
referred the cleansing of women from sin principally and properly to faith
alone. Scripture is not silent concerning the cleansing and justifying power
of faith.

Bell. 2: *It is in no way credible that there was no remedy for males dying
before the eighth day, yet nothing about this matter exists in Scripture.*
Prot.: 1. It is just as credible that there was no remedy for Jewish infants
dying before the eighth day as what the Roman Catholics believe, that there
is no remedy for Christian infants dying before they can be baptized. 2.
Elsewhere Bellarmine himself calls uncertain what he here affirms is in no way
credible. For these are his words in *On the Sacrament of Baptism*, book 1,
chapter 4, in reply to argument 4, which is drawn from those infants: *The
whole argument consists of uncertain matters. We do not know whether Jewish
infants before the eighth day had some remedy.* 3. Alexander of Hales, in the
*Summa*, part 4, question 4, article 3, asserts that little children dying
before the eighth day were saved through their parents' faith, together with
offerings and sacrifices. Thomas also, part 3, question 70, article 4, does not
hesitate to affirm that *faith alone in Christ saved both those little
children and other adults, both before and after circumcision was given.*

Bell. 3: *Nothing is contained in the Scriptures concerning the justification
of the Gentiles in the time of the Old Testament.* Prot.: As though it were
not

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 74 | printed p. 64 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

expressly written that all the families of the earth were to be blessed in
Abraham, Genesis 11:35—a promise which was to be received through faith,
Galatians 3:14, and which availed for justification to those receiving it,
Romans 4:16.

Bell. 4: *It is necessary to know that certain truly divine books exist, but
this can in no way be obtained from the Scriptures. For the Quran affirms the
same about itself as Scripture does about itself.*

Prot.: 1. It is well that at last, from zeal for contention, Bellarmine
concedes that it is necessary to know the Scriptures. 2. Bellarmine himself,
book 1, chapter 2, proves by many arguments that the Holy Scriptures are truly
and certainly divine. He draws these arguments from the Scriptures themselves
without any mention of traditions, nor does he deny here that this doctrine is
contained in Scripture. This argument therefore contains nothing about
unwritten doctrine. 3. The comparison of Holy Scripture with the Quran is
plainly impious and unworthy of a Christian. But since the Roman Catholics
are plainly like the Muslims in this, that they boast of traditions and accuse
the Scriptures of being corrupted and imperfect, it is no wonder if they have
the same sort of faith concerning the Scriptures as those people have
concerning the Quran.

Bell. 5: *It is necessary to know what Scripture is.* Prot.: By the same
arguments which show certain books to be divine Scripture, it is also shown at
the same time, at least for the most part, which books they are. Nor is this
unwritten doctrine, at least concerning the whole Old Testament and a large
part of the New.

Bell. 6: *It is necessary to know that the books in our hands are those
books.* Prot.: This too is confirmed by the same arguments, with the common
evidence of all ancient writings added.

Bell. 7: *It is necessary to understand the Scriptures.* Prot.: 1. The sense
of Scripture is in Scripture and is not unwritten doctrine. 2. It is not
necessary for salvation to understand everything in the Scriptures. The Roman
Catholics, with their traditions, will not claim this for themselves. The
necessary things can be understood from the Scriptures themselves. Yet
Bellarmine admits in chapter 11 that *the most obscure questions generally do
not have the testimony of a tradition received throughout the whole Church.*

Bell. 8: *It must be believed that the Blessed Mary was always a Virgin.*
Prot.: It must be believed as Basil believed, in *On the Human Generation of
Christ*: *While*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 75 | printed p. 65 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*Christ's generation was being accomplished, virginity was necessary. But
what happened afterward is not to be anxiously joined to the doctrine of this
mystery.*

Bell. 9: *It must be believed that in the New Testament Easter is to be
celebrated on the Lord's Day.* Prot.: Concerning the necessity of Easter in
the New Testament, absolutely nothing is to be believed; human institutions
are not objects of divine faith.

Bell. 10: *It is necessary to believe that the baptism of infants is valid.*
Prot.: Bellarmine himself proves this from the Scriptures in *On the Sacrament
of Baptism*, book 1, chapter 8. He also admits in chapter 11 of this book that
*all things simply necessary for everyone were written by the Apostles*, while
he holds Baptism to be supremely necessary.

9. Bell.: *From the Scriptures it is demonstrated that there are some true
traditions.* Prot.: Namely, those which are handed down in the Scriptures
themselves; the Scriptures offer no testimony to others.

Bell. 1: *John 16: I have many things to say to you, but you cannot bear them
now. And the last chapter of John: There are also many other things which
Jesus did, and so forth.* Prot.: 1. The former passage was once almost a
commonplace of the heretics. Thus Augustine, *Tractate 97 on John*: *All the
most foolish heretics who wish to be called Christians try to color their
boldest inventions, which human sense most greatly abhors, by this occasion of
the Gospel saying, where the Lord says, I have yet many things, and so forth.*
2. It favors the Roman Catholics no more than those ancient heretics, because
the former can no more show than the latter that the things they hand down
besides Scripture are those which the Lord then deferred teaching. Nor can
they in any way prove that those same things were not written by the Apostles.
This reasoning has no appearance of a valid consequence: before the
resurrection the Apostles could not bear certain things; therefore, they did
not write those things afterward. 3. Christ does not say, I have many things
to say to you which I have in no way said, for he had said all necessary
things, John 15:15 and 17:8. Rather, he says, you cannot bear them now—that is,
you cannot grasp their meaning fruitfully before you receive the Spirit. He
therefore teaches that fuller instruction is to be expected from the Spirit,
but concerning those things which he himself had taught, verse 13 and chapter
14:26. 4. Yet if it is conceded that other things were to be

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 76 | printed p. 66 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

revealed, then we say that they all exist in the Apostles' writings. 5. John,
last chapter, verse 30, expressly speaks of deeds, not dogmas, and also adds in
verse 31 that all those deeds whose knowledge is necessary for life have been
written.

Bell. 2: *1 Corinthians 11:2: You keep my precepts as I delivered them to you;
and verse 23: I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you.* Prot.:
Unless the Roman Catholics can demonstrate: 1. That all things which the
Apostle commanded the Corinthians were universally necessary for salvation.
2. That the things he commanded and handed down are nowhere written. 3. That
they now know certainly what those things are which the Apostle handed down
and did not write—they can wring nothing from these words in defense of their
traditions.

Bell. 3: *Hold fast the traditions which you received, whether by speech or by
our epistle, 2 Thessalonians 2.* Prot.: There is no appearance of a valid
consequence in this reasoning. The Apostle taught the Thessalonians certain
things by speech and certain things by epistle; therefore, he taught certain
necessary things which are contained in no part of Scripture.

Bell. 4: *The last chapter of 1 Timothy: O Timothy, guard the deposit. There
the deposit is understood as the sense of the Scriptures and of other dogmas.*
Prot.: The sense of Scripture is not denied. But to add other dogmas is to beg
the question. The sense of Scripture is preserved: 1. By preserving the
Scriptures themselves without addition or subtraction; in this matter the
Roman Pontiffs acknowledge that they have been faithless when they contend
that the Scriptures are corrupted. 2. By retaining faith in them, 1 Timothy
1:18–19. 3. By defending, promoting, and propagating the same faith among
others, 2 Timothy 2:2. These words therefore do not pertain to traditions, but
those immediately following do: *to turn away from profane clamors about
empty things and the additions of falsely named knowledge.* The same applies
to what he says elsewhere about avoiding fables.

Bell. 5: *In the Second and Third Epistles of John: Having many things to write
to you, I did not wish to do so with paper and ink.* Prot.: By this manner of
inference one may conclude that no doctrine is contained in all Scripture
which John did not set down in those two later epistles. Those who argue in
this way do violence not only to Scripture but also to reason.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 77 | printed p. 67 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

10. Bell.: *The same is shown by the testimonies of Pontiffs, Councils, and
Fathers.* Prot.: The very thing which had to be proved is that *some necessary
doctrine exists which is not contained in the Scriptures*. But this is not
proved by these testimonies and cannot be proved by them. The ancients often
approve traditions which are contained in the Scriptures, according to
Cyprian's statement to Pompeius: *If it is commanded in the Gospel or
contained in the Apostles' Epistles or Acts, let this holy tradition certainly
be observed*—especially if these things are not expressed in so many words in
Holy Writ. They attribute certain verbal formulas to tradition, while they
themselves show their sense from the Scriptures. For the most part, however,
they designate by traditions rites and ceremonies received without Scripture,
about which there is no dispute. In these they were more than sufficiently
easy and liberal, according to Jerome's words to Lucius: *Let every Province
abound in its own understanding and reckon its forebears' precepts Apostolic
laws.* Yet when they render judgment from the Scriptures, they plainly condemn
unwritten traditions, as the same Jerome says on Haggai, chapter 1: *The sword
of God strikes the things which they discover and invent of their own accord,
without the authority and testimonies of Scripture, as though by Apostolic
tradition.*

11. Bell.: *The same is proved by four other arguments.* Prot.: Arguments
which would prevail against the Scriptures ought to be firm.

Bell. 1: *Heretics of every age rejected traditions; therefore, they are not
to be rejected.* Prot.: 1. Consider similar reasoning: heretics of every age
rejected the idolatry of the nations; therefore, it is not to be rejected. 2.
It is manifestly false that all heretics rejected all traditions. Irenaeus,
book 3, chapter 2: *When they are convicted from the Scriptures, they turn to
accusing the Scriptures themselves, and so forth, because the truth cannot be
found from them by those who do not know tradition.* Tertullian, *On the
Resurrection*: *Take away from the heretics what they think with the heathen,
so that they may establish their questions from the Scriptures alone, and
they will not be able to stand.* Eusebius testifies that a certain Papias set
forth various errors *as though from unwritten tradition*, book 3, last
chapter. Marcion also used to boast that he had his inventions not from
himself but from the Apostles'

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 78 | printed p. 68 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

own disciples, namely, those who had been hearers of Matthias. Clement, book
7 of the *Stromata*: while the Apostles were still living, impostors began to
invent discourses and Epistles as though they had proceeded from the Apostles,
2 Thessalonians 2:2. How much Montanus attributed to traditions is evident
from Tertullian's *On the Soldier's Crown*, written after he had defected to
Montanus's heresy.

Bell. 2: *The customs of all nations and of profane affairs approve unwritten
laws.* Prot.: 1. The most ancient laws of the Romans were written on tablets.
2. It is the common opinion of interpreters of civil law that writing is
necessarily required for human Law. Albericus, Salicetus, the Gloss, and the
Archdeacon also agree, as the Jesuit Azorius testifies in *Moral Institutes*,
book 5, chapter 2. Indeed, the definition of law in Canon Law, chapter *Lex*,
distinction 1, taken from Isidore, is: *Law is a written constitution.* 3. The
customs of nations and profane people do not always agree with God's ways.
Nor is the danger in ignorance of human laws as great as that in ignorance of
God's will.

Bell. 3: *If everything were openly written, the Church would have no
privilege over heretics, Pagans, and Jews, although the oracles of God were
entrusted to the Church, Romans 3.*

Prot.: The objection is ridiculous and profane at once. For Scripture is in
the Church in a far different manner than among others: to the Church it is
the law of life and life itself, Deuteronomy 32:47. Moreover, Moses expressly
says that the written Law was the Church's wisdom, prudence, and dignity above
all nations, before the eyes of peoples who would hear its statutes,
Deuteronomy 4:6.

Bell. 4: *The dignity of many mysteries requires silence, nor is it fitting
that they should be explained in the Scriptures, such as the dreadful
mysteries of the Mass.*

Prot.: 1. By this reasoning the dignity of traditions is exalted above the
dignity of Scripture, and unwritten doctrines are pronounced more divine than
written doctrines. The secrets of the Mass are more worthy of reverence than
that great mystery of godliness, 1 Timothy 3:16. 2. All the mysteries of the
Old Testament, even those which none but the Priests were permitted to see,
were nevertheless written and described in the Law. 3. Christ's command in

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 79 | printed p. 69 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the New Testament is that the disciples should teach openly what they had
learned from him, *in the light and upon the housetops*, Matthew 10:27. The
dreadful mysteries of the Mass which must be concealed are therefore mysteries
of neither the Old nor New Testament, but part of that mystery of iniquity
mentioned in 2 Thessalonians 2:7 and of the depths of Satan, Revelation 2:24.
Yet these very dreadful mysteries of the Mass are found explained in the Roman
Missal and similar most trifling books. What, then, prevents them from existing
in Holy Writ if they were from God?

12. Bell.: *There are five rules by which one arrives at knowledge of the true
traditions of the Apostles. 1. When the universal Church embraces as a dogma
of faith something not found in the divine writings. 2. When the universal
Church observes something which no one but God could have established. 3.
When something has been observed in the universal Church throughout all past
times, even if it is of such a kind that the Church could have instituted it.
4. When all the Doctors of the Church teach by common consent that something
descends from Apostolic tradition. 5. When something is held as Apostolic
tradition in those Churches where there is an unbroken and continued
succession from the Apostles—a succession which has now failed in all Churches
except the Roman.*

Prot.: 1. The basis of all these rules is one thing: namely, that the Church
can in no way slip or be deceived in defining Apostolic traditions. Azorius,
in *Moral Institutes*, book 8, chapter 5, therefore not undeservedly referred
these five special rules to that one general rule. But this is nothing other
than proving the uncertain by something more uncertain. 2. Moreover, it is a
most shameful and at once most impudent begging of the question to thrust
forward as the rule of traditions the Roman Church, which stands accused of
the greatest crime because of its fabricated and counterfeit traditions. But
this is how the matter truly stands. The names of Councils, Fathers, and the
Primitive Church are produced by the Roman Catholics for show and pomp, while
they meanwhile wish the present Roman Church to be the rule of them all. Thus
Bellarmine expressly says in *On the Sacraments*, book 2, chapter 25: *The
firmness of all ancient Councils and of all dogmas depends upon the authority
of the present Church.* 3. None of these rules could have had a place in
former times, for

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 80 | printed p. 70 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

different Churches contended that plainly contrary things had been handed
down to them by the Apostles. Azorius, *Moral Institutes*, book 8, chapter 4,
defends that they truly made this assertion: *Different traditions*, he says,
*flowed from the Apostles. For throughout the East, by tradition derived from
the Apostles, the Sabbath was piously and religiously observed just like the
Lord's Day; and yet throughout the West a fast was observed on the Sabbath by
a tradition which, as I think, was received from the Apostle Peter. Hence
different rites and customs were retained and observed throughout East and
West.* 4. Absolutely nothing not contained in Holy Writ was received by the
universal Church throughout all ages as necessary to believe or observe. 5. If
traditions are to be received with the same faith as the things contained in
the Scriptures because of the Church's testimony and the Fathers' consent,
then these testimonies of the Church and Fathers have the same certainty and
authority as Holy Scripture; then the Fathers' writings must be believed with
the same faith by which we believe the Prophetic and Apostolic writings; then
whether the Fathers speak from Scripture or without Scripture, there is no
difference with respect to the faith to be given them. All these things are
plainly absurd and altogether foreign to piety.

##### Our Arguments

13. Prot.: Deuteronomy 4:2: Do not add to that word which I command you, nor
subtract from it. Bell. 1: *The passage does not concern the written word, but
the word handed down by the living voice.* Prot.: 1. It therefore does not
concern the Decalogue, which was written by God himself, nor those laws which
Moses had received from the Lord's mouth, written in the book of the covenant,
and read while the people listened, Exodus 24:4, 7. 2. If the passage concerns
the word then handed down by Moses's living voice, it concerns the written
word. For Moses *finished writing in a book, to their very end*, the same words
of the Law which he handed down by the living voice, Deuteronomy 31:24. Bell.
2: *The true exposition of that passage is that God does not mean, You shall
observe nothing other than what I now command, but, You shall change nothing
in what I command, by adding or subtracting. Otherwise the Prophets and
Apostles, who afterward added so many things, would have sinned.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 81 | printed p. 71 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot.: 1. If it was not lawful to add anything to the individual precepts of
the Law, certainly nothing was to be added to the whole Law. 2. No reason can
be given why an addition within a precept should be no more lawful than an
addition outside the precept. 3. Moses himself, Deuteronomy 12:30–31 and 22,
manifestly shows that this law pertains to additions outside or beyond the
Law: *Do not act toward Jehovah your God as the nations acted toward their
gods. Every matter which I command you, be careful to do it; do not add to it.*
Additions of this kind are also condemned by the Prophets for the same reason:
*what I did not command, nor did it enter my mind*, Jeremiah 7:31. 4. As for
the Apostles, whatever they added was done not so much by them as by God
through them, and at the same time as through those same men he introduced the
abrogation of many laws of Moses. The Prophets, however, added no entirely new
command to the Law, but only explanations and applications. The Prophets'
writings are not called interpretations of Moses because Moses commends the
Prophets' authority, as Bellarmine says that traditions are praised in the
Scriptures and commended by the Church's authority, and are therefore in some
manner explanations of the written Word. Rather, they are called
interpretations because the Prophets expounded the sense of the Law and
applied it to the people's use in each generation.

14. Prot.: The last chapter of Revelation: If anyone adds to these things,
and so forth. Bell.: *It is certain that John there forbids the corruption of
that book.* Prot.: 1. Although it is not so certain that John had regard only
to that book—indeed, it is very probable that by this most grave denunciation
he sealed the whole canon—yet, even granting this, since the principle is the
same for the other canonical books, and those who hand down doctrines not
contained in these books add something to them, that threat also pertains to
the authors of traditions. 2. If the Roman Pontiffs produced certain
prophecies about the state of the Church or the mystery of Antichrist, said
that they had received them from John, and declared them of the same faith as
the prophecies contained in Revelation, they would manifestly be guilty of
adding to that book. They therefore cannot be excused from a similar crime
when

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 82 | printed p. 72 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

they produce certain dogmas as Apostolic which are not contained in the
Apostles' writings.

15. Prot.: Galatians 1: If an angel from heaven preaches to you beyond what we
have preached to you, let him be accursed. Bell. 1: *The Apostle speaks of
every word, whether written or unwritten.* Prot.: Paul speaks of the word
preached by himself, but he preached nothing which was not contained in
Scripture, Acts 26:22. Nor will any Christian deny that the whole Gospel which
Paul preached to the Galatians exists written in the New Testament. Bell. 2:
*By beyond, the Apostle means contrary to. For the Apostle himself afterward
taught many other things, and so did John in Revelation. Here he also acts
against contrary doctrine. Thus beyond is taken for contrary to in the last
chapter of Romans, verse 17.* Prot.: 1. In theology it is true that *beyond the
Scriptures is contrary to the Scriptures*, because Scripture forbids
additions. Although what is added is *contrary to* that general command, it is
nevertheless only *beyond* those particular dogmas to which it is added. 2.
Elsewhere the Apostles wrote absolutely nothing in the class of dogma which
Paul had not preached among the Galatians. 3. Here the Apostle acts against
contrary doctrine by reasoning either from the lesser to the greater or from
the genus to the species. For *beyond*, strictly taken, is *less*, and broadly
taken is *more general* than *contrary to*. 4. For the same reason *beyond*
includes *contrary to* in the last chapter of Romans, verse 17.

16. Prot.: John 20:3: These things are written so that you may believe and,
believing, have life. Bell. 1: *John there speaks only of Christ's miracles,
not of the dogmas of faith and precepts of morals.* Prot.: That miracles alone
are not meant is evident from the fact that miracles without doctrine are not
sufficient *that we may believe and, believing, have life*. Nor had John
written in his Gospel only, or even chiefly, about miracles. Bell. 2: *If the
things written by John are sufficient, all the other Scriptures will be
superfluous.* Prot.: 1. By the same reasoning he could conclude that if the
other Evangelists had written sufficiently about Christ's passion, then
whatever John wrote about it was superfluous. But it thus seemed good to
divine wisdom to provide not merely sufficiently, but also abundantly, for our
faith and

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 83 | printed p. 73 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

salvation. 2. They are not excluded. For John, writing last, wished to show
that no other canonical books were to be expected until the end of the world,
and that nothing necessary was absent from his Gospel together with the other
books previously written by the Prophets and Apostles.

17. Prot.: 2 Timothy 3:15–17: The Holy Writings can make you wise for
salvation; all Scripture is useful, and so forth, so that the person of God may
be perfect, perfectly equipped for every good work.

Bell. 1: *Scripture sufficiently instructs and perfects the person of God
because it expressly contains many things and, for what it does not contain,
shows all the places from which they are to be sought.*

Prot.: 1. The Apostle shows that all things can and ought to be sought from
the Scriptures, because they can make a person wise for salvation and
perfectly equipped for every good work. Bellarmine plainly reverses the sense
and says that Scripture perfects a person insofar as it shows that the things
which it does not itself have are to be sought elsewhere, namely, from the
Church or Roman Pontiff. 2. By this reasoning the First Epistle to Timothy, or
that third chapter alone, would perfectly equip one for every good work. For
it expressly contains many things and, for those it does not contain, shows,
according to Bellarmine's interpretation, where they are to be sought: *from
the pillar and support of the truth.* 3. If this is *to perfect*—to show from
where perfection is to be sought—then John the Baptist perfected his
disciples, John 3. Indeed, the Law perfected Christians, Galatians 3:24, which
the Apostle expressly denies, Hebrews 7:19.

Bell. 2: *Paul does not attribute even this sufficiency to Scripture, because
when he says every Scripture, he attributes this praise not only to the whole
body of Scripture, but also to the individual books.* Prot.: It is most
correctly said that by these words some praise is attributed both to the whole
body of Scripture and to its individual books. But nothing follows less from
this than that not even this sufficiency is attributed to Scripture, which I
have just shown belongs in its own manner to the various books. Indeed, it
rather follows that every sufficiency is attributed to the whole Scripture
and, proportionately, its own sufficiency to each book. For if someone said
that Logic could make a person skilled in reasoning well because

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 84 | printed p. 74 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

all its precepts are useful for discovery and judgment, so that one who
exercises himself in them is perfectly instructed in reasoning well, he would
attribute great sufficiency to that art and its parts. So it is with what the
Apostle here affirms about the Scriptures. But Bellarmine wants something
else, namely, that the whole body of Scripture is not understood here, but
each book—a claim he tries to prove by the emptiest reasons. 1. *In the
judgment of everyone who knows Latin, what is said of every Scripture is said
of the individual books.* But in the judgment of everyone who knows Greek,
the word πᾶσα, here rendered *every* in the old version, often means the same
as ὅλη, *whole*, as in Matthew 8:32, 34; Ephesians 4:16; and Acts 20:27. Also,
in the judgment of everyone who has carefully studied the common translation,
*every* often denotes the same as *whole*, as in Matthew 26:54; Mark 1:33 and
14:55; Acts 1:47, 7:10, and 15:32; Philippians 1:3; Hebrews 3:2: *all the
people, the whole house, the whole city, the whole Church, the whole Council,
the whole praetorium.* In this passage, moreover, by τὰ ἱερὰ γράμματα, *the
Holy Writings*, the body of Holy Scripture is necessarily understood; and in
the judgment of all who know theology, no one book of Scripture is understood
by that Scripture which *perfectly equips the person of God for every good
work.* 2. *When this Epistle was written, something was still lacking from the
body of the Scriptures.* But the whole Scripture existed with respect to the
essential parts of Law and Gospel, Old and New Testament, although Revelation
and John's Gospel were still lacking as integral parts; and even those books
which were lacking are included by analogy. Thus when Paul says that *the
whole mystical body of Christ is animated and nourished by his Holy Spirit*,
he also includes those members which neither existed then nor yet exist. 3.
*From what the Apostle attributes to every Scripture, he concludes in
particular about the Scriptures of the Old Testament.* At least, then, in the
premises of this reasoning he treats of another Scripture besides the Old
Testament; and it is evident that he does so in the conclusion as well, from
those words in verse 15, *through faith which is in Jesus Christ.* But if we
grant this whole point, the argument binds more strongly. For if the writings
of the Old Testament could make people wise for salvation and perfectly

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 85 | printed p. 75 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

equip them for every good work, how much more will the whole Scripture of the
Old and New Testaments together accomplish this?

Bell. 3: *Useful does not mean sufficient, at least not sufficient alone, but
that it has this use, is directed and ordered to this, or something similar.
Thus one who says food is useful for nourishing a person says that food is
appointed to nourish, but does not say that food alone is sufficient. For if
natural heat or some bodily instrument necessary for nourishment is lacking,
food will not nourish.* Prot.: 1. Although *is useful* does not mean *is
sufficient*, nevertheless, *is useful for all necessary things and perfectly
equips for all good things* means at least as much as *is sufficient*. This
greatness of usefulness which Paul attributes to Scripture in the highest
degree—this usefulness, I say—is what we press. 2. If Scripture is ordered by
God to this end, that it may perfectly equip for every good work and make any
person wise for salvation, as Bellarmine concedes, then certainly anyone can
be content with Scripture as the sufficient rule of faith and morals. 3. The
example of food is employed sophistically and ridiculously. For just as one
who says that food can nourish a person and is useful for perfect nourishment
signifies that food is the sufficient matter of nourishment, although natural
heat and other things concur as efficient causes, so Paul, saying Scripture
can make a person wise for salvation and is useful for perfect equipping,
signifies that Scripture is the sufficient matter of spiritual nourishment,
although the Holy Spirit and faith must necessarily concur as efficient
causes of salvation. It is well that Bellarmine concedes Scripture to be
sufficient for salvation as food is for nourishment, for nothing besides food
is to be eaten; therefore, nothing besides Scripture is to be believed. But
among the Roman Catholics, when people ask for Scripture as bread, they give
them tradition as a stone or serpent—something Christ denies that fathers will
do to their children, Matthew 7:9–10.

18. Prot.: Traditions are everywhere condemned in the Scriptures: Isaiah 29;
Matthew 15; Galatians 1; Colossians 2; 1 Timothy 1; 1 Peter 1. Bell.: *Christ
and the Apostles do not rebuke the traditions*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 86 | printed p. 76 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*which the Jews had received from Moses and the Prophets, but those received
from certain later people, some of which were empty, some pernicious and
contrary to the Scriptures.* Prot.: 1. Nowhere in all Scripture do we read
that anyone was rebuked on the ground that he neglected unwritten traditions.
Indeed, life is promised to those doing what is written, Galatians 3:10, then
12. 2. Those traditions are condemned which the Scribes and Pharisees said
were from Moses and the Prophets. For it cannot be doubted that from the later
people from whom they had received those observances, they also received that
they were from Moses, either in particular or at least universally. 3. *Moses
and the Prophets*, among Christ and the Apostles, signifies the same as the
Scripture of the Old Testament. They therefore acknowledged no Word of God
handed down by them to the Church besides the written Word. It is thus no
wonder if they never censure their traditions by name. 4. No reason can be
imagined why the Jews' traditions were either pernicious or empty if all or
most of the Roman Catholics' traditions are to be approved.

19. The testimonies usually brought from the Fathers by our writers are so
clear that, when Bellarmine was compelled to answer them in some measure, he
had to hand the whole case over to us. For besides the many most shameful
evasions which he uses, this is his confession in chapter 11:

1. *The Apostles were accustomed to preach to everyone the things simply
necessary for everyone, and other things useful for everyone.*

2. *All those things necessary for everyone, and those which the Apostles
themselves openly preached to everyone generally, were written by the
Apostles.*

3. *The most obscure questions are generally not those which have the
testimony of a tradition received throughout the whole Church.*

4. *There exist in the Scriptures testimonies of all things pertaining to the
nature of God or to indicating the union of natures in Christ; and concerning
these dogmas we can be plainly and fully instructed from the Scriptures if we
understand them rightly.*

5. *There is no vice for the healing of which some remedy is not found in
Scripture.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 87 | printed p. 77 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

6. *The things contained in the Apostles' Creed and Decalogue are openly set
forth in Scripture.*

7. *The Apostle sufficiently explained what kind of good the profession of
widowhood is.*

8. *The clearest testimonies of the Scriptures must without doubt be placed
before all testimonies of Councils.*

20. From the first and second confessions we argue as follows: 1. If all the
things which the Apostles openly preached to everyone have been written, then
the Apostle's *discourses*, about which he speaks in 2 Thessalonians 2:15, are
written; for they were preached to the whole Church just as the Epistle was
written to everyone. Bellarmine therefore acts wickedly when he so often
presses that little word to establish unwritten traditions.

2. If everything either necessary or useful for everyone is written, then the
whole will of God revealed for human salvation is written; for all of it is
useful to everyone because it contributes to everyone's salvation.

3. If the things useful for everyone are written, then so too are the things
necessary for Bishops and Presbyters. For besides the fact that the chief part
of ministers' office is to teach the things necessary and useful for everyone,
all know that the whole Scripture everywhere impresses their duties no less
than those of the people, and that the Apostle Paul devoted three entire
Epistles chiefly to that subject. In the Old Testament also, the duties of
Priests and Levites are described more precisely than those of private people.

4. From 2 and 3 thus: If traditions do not concern matters generally necessary
or useful, nor the most obscure questions, then certainly there are none. For
no reason can be devised why both the matters necessary or useful for everyone
and the most obscure questions should be committed to writing by the Apostles,
while certain other things of I know not what kind should be omitted—unless
perhaps they blasphemously say that Scripture was written rashly and not by
God's supreme providence.

5. From 4 thus: If everything pertaining to God's nature is contained in the
Scriptures, then Bellarmine falsely posited earlier in chapter 8 as the

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 88 | printed p. 78 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

foundation of traditions that certain mysteries are too worthy to be
explained in the Scriptures; for there are no greater mysteries than those in
God's nature and the union of natures in Christ.

6. From 5 thus: If remedies against all vices are described in the
Scriptures, then the means for acquiring all virtues are also found there.
Nothing is therefore lacking which pertains either to faith or morals, for
everything must be referred to one or the other.

7. From 6 thus: If everything in the Creed and Decalogue is openly set forth
in Scripture, then there we have the perfect rule of faith and morals.

8. From 7 thus: If such minute matters as the profession of widowhood are
explained in the Scriptures, certainly nothing necessary is omitted.

9. From 8 thus: If the testimonies of Scripture must without doubt be placed
before the testimonies of Councils, then what Bellarmine posited earlier is
false: that the Church's word is always God's Word. For every Word of God has
one and the same authority. Certainly Councils do not have in decreeing the
same direction of the Spirit that the Prophets and Apostles had in writing.
Faith which rests upon clear testimonies of Scripture is thus also more
certain than faith which rests upon the authority of Councils.

21. It seems impossible that pure unwritten traditions could have been
preserved so long, because of human forgetfulness, ignorance, negligence, and
perversity. Bell. 1: *This care falls chiefly not upon people but God, because
by his Providence he preserved the Church, Scripture, and also traditions from
Adam to Moses.* Prot.: 1. No care falls upon God which he himself did not
undertake. But nowhere in the Scriptures has he revealed that he undertook
care for unwritten traditions; for then he would also have revealed what those
traditions were, and so unwritten traditions would be written. 2. We have
clear promises about preserving the Church and Scripture, but not traditions.
3. Bellarmine contends in chapter 4 that the whole Scripture was not
preserved, but that many of its parts perished. 4. From Adam, as long as pure
unwritten doctrine was preserved, it was preserved by God's living voice and
extraordinary Prophets,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 89 | printed p. 79 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

a dispensation which has not existed since the Apostles' times. But it was not
preserved pure until Moses once the living voice ceased; and it was therefore
committed to writing by Moses, and so thereafter, that it might be vindicated
from those human corruptions to which traditions are liable.

Bell. 2: *There were four assisting causes for preserving traditions: 1.
Ecclesiastical books, in which they are written. 2. Their continual use. 3.
External monuments, such as temples and the like. 4. Heresies, which stirred
learned people to investigate ancient traditions and commend them to others.*
Prot.: 1. All these things are liable to the forgetfulness, negligence,
ignorance, and perversity which they ought to oppose. 2. If God had willed
those unwritten doctrines to be preserved as sacredly as those in Scripture,
he would undoubtedly have taken care to have the holy Apostles write them and
would not have left them to be written by lying people according to their own
discretion. 3. No one unwritten tradition exists in all Ecclesiastical books;
nor do all exist in any one book; nor are all those which exist in some books
approved by the Roman Catholics. 4. Continual use cannot preserve pure even a
common language, whose use is most frequent. *Common languages*, says
Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 15, *change greatly in each century.* Nor can the
continued use of these traditions from the Apostles' time be shown any more
than the traditions themselves. 5. Neither use nor the stones and walls of
temples can preserve dogmas or doctrines. 6. Heresies ought to stir godly and
learned people to search the Scriptures, which contain God's undoubted will.

22. Prot.: The divine Writings were written so that we might have a perfect
rule and standard of faith and morals. Bell. 1: *Scripture's proper and chief
end was not to be a rule of faith, for then it would contain everything and
only those things which of themselves pertain to faith, and it would be one
continuous work. Rather, its end was to be a certain useful reminder for
preserving and fostering doctrine received from preaching.* Prot.: 1. A rule

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 90 | printed p. 80 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

and a reminder are not opposites; Scripture is an admonitory rule. 2. The
proper and chief end of law is to be a rule, and the Roman Catholics concede
that Scripture is law. 3. God's will was revealed in the Scriptures so that
we might live according to it, as Moses, the first among its writers,
testifies everywhere. 4. To prescribe laws to God—that he should deliver that
rule nakedly, in one continuous work without any appendices, applications, or
declarations by which he might provide for our weakness—is the act of a person
answering God back. 5. If Scripture is merely a reminder serving preaching,
what will be the rule of the preaching itself? 6. As a sufficient reminder it
cannot preserve the doctrine received from the Apostles' preaching unless it
contains the whole doctrine preached by them.

Bell. 2: *Scripture is a rule, not total but partial.* Prot.: 1. Absolutely no
reason can even be imagined why certain necessary doctrines should have a
written rule and other equally necessary doctrines should not. 2. If it is a
partial rule, then it does not direct perfectly, and one who perfectly did the
will of God revealed in the Scriptures would not yet be perfect. 2. If
Scripture is a partial rule, people are bound to be wise above what is
written—that is, above the Law and Gospel.

23. Prot.: If unwritten dogmas are to be received, many will be given occasion
to invent and introduce into the Church many falsehoods under the name of
tradition. Bell. 1: *This argument avails equally against the Scriptures,
because many false books were once adorned with the title of canonical books.*
Prot.: The principle is not the same. Canonical Scriptures are distinguished
by other arguments, not merely by the inscription of titles. Experience
itself teaches that various false traditions were introduced among the Jews,
yet no false Scripture was admitted into the Canon. Nor afterward in the
Christian Church would any Apocryphal book have been admitted as Holy
Scripture unless the authority of traditions had made a way for it. Common
sense also shows that it is not as easy to thrust a false book upon the Church
as canonical

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 91 | printed p. 81 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

as it is to thrust forward a false tradition as true. Bell. 2: *The Church
both has authority and also a certain way of discerning true traditions from
false.* Prot.: It has no way or method of discerning true, unwritten dogmatic
traditions besides its own authority, and it cannot show any suitable author
of that authority.

24. Prot.: It is characteristic of heretics to hide their dogmas and to say
that the Apostles preached certain things openly to everyone, but handed down
others secretly to certain people alone. Bell.: *Heretics hide their mysteries
because they are shameful; but ours are hidden from certain people either
because there is no need for everyone to know them or because not everyone is
capable of them.* Prot.: If any heretic said that his mysteries were shameful,
or did not say that everyone was incapable of them, then this is an answer and
not mere prattle.
