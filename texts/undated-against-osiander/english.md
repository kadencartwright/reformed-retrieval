# Against Osiander

*An undated consilium; a fresh translation from CO X/1 165–167.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 165]

If he had restrained himself of his own accord, he could have benefited the
church of God. But while he burns with eagerness to display his cleverness,
no reverence, modesty, or even sense of shame restrained him from trying to
overturn everything. And so, while indulging his speculations, he corrupted
the genuine and simple doctrine of godliness in various ways with perverse
delusions. But I shall not now touch his other inventions.

### [CO X/1, p. 166]

In explaining justification by faith, he bewitched himself with sheer
illusions and deceived others. He indeed admits what he did not dare deny:
that remission of sins intervenes when a person is justified. But by placing
in the first and highest rank the phantom of essential righteousness which
he himself invented, he leaves nothing else for God's free acceptance than
to be a kind of subordinate appendix.

He imagined essential righteousness for himself without any reason. For
although he drags in that direction the testimonies of Scripture which
declare that God dwells in us and that we become one with him, they prove
nothing beyond this: that by the secret power of the Spirit, while we grow
together into Christ's body, we are at the same time united to God. Add that
this essential communication was taken from the delusions of the Manichaeans.
Nor do I see how this absurdity can be excused: that God's essential
righteousness should be an accident which may now be present in a person and
now absent.

Yet there is no need of a long refutation if we maintain this principle: at
creation, human righteousness was the rectitude to which the human being was
formed, so that God's glory shone in every part of the soul and indeed in the
body itself. Since the human being was endowed with the light of
understanding, had a will ordered toward obedience to God, and possessed the
same harmony in every faculty, we say that the human being was righteous.

Not content with this description, Osiander imagines that Christ was not only
the Idea but the primary exemplar, so that Adam was his essential image.
Hence this invention, with which he so intoxicated himself that he guarded
against no absurdity.

Now, after the human being has been alienated from God, we say that he is
restored by righteousness and sanctification. The former signifies free
reconciliation; the latter, human renewal. Therefore, as far as the terms are
concerned, Osiander wrongly confuses regeneration with righteousness. For
Paul's principle remains fixed: David gives the true definition of
righteousness in these words, “Blessed is the man to whom God does not impute
sins” (Romans 4:6).

Osiander says that we are justified not only by the obedience Christ
rendered and the satisfaction he discharged to expiate sins, but by his
divine and eternal righteousness. Paul speaks very differently. He simply
affirms that we are justified by the obedience of one man (Romans 5:19), and
elsewhere similarly declares that Christ was given to us for redemption and
righteousness (1 Corinthians 1:30). Likewise, he was made under the law in
order to redeem us from the curse of the law (Galatians 4:4).

Moreover, this is the righteousness he discusses everywhere: with guilt
blotted out, we are received by God into favor. And certainly the statement,
“For their sake I sanctify myself” (John 17:19), is referred to Christ's human
nature. Besides this, if Christ's flesh truly is food and his blood truly is
drink, we gather that we are justified in him only insofar as he is the one
who makes propitiation.

How ignorantly and perversely Osiander speaks about reconciliation is more
than sufficiently plain from one passage of Paul (2 Corinthians

### [CO X/1, p. 167]

5:19): “God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, not imputing sins
to human beings.” The account of righteousness added immediately afterward
must also be noted: “He made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that we
might be the righteousness of God in him” (verse 21).

This brief admonition, I hope, will suffice to arm sound and moderate readers
against Osiander's illusions. For there is no leisure now to write a long
volume, nor do I judge it expedient.

## Translator's notes

1. Andreas Osiander taught that believers are justified through the
   indwelling of Christ's divine, essential righteousness. Calvin instead
   distinguishes God's free acceptance and Christ's mediatorial obedience
   from the Spirit's work of renewal.
2. *Praestigiae* combines deceptive tricks, illusion, and enchantment. The
   translation uses “illusions” in most places and “bewitched” for Calvin's
   related verb *fascinavit*.
3. In scholastic terminology an “accident” can be present or absent without
   belonging to a thing's essence. Calvin argues that Osiander makes the
   contradiction of treating God's essential righteousness as such a
   removable accident in a human being.
4. *Idea* and “primary exemplar” are Osiander's account of Christ as the
   archetype according to whose essential image Adam was made. Calvin's
   capitalization of *Ideam* is retained in English because it signals this
   conceptual use.
5. “Righteousness” and “sanctification” translate *iustitia* and
   *sanctificatio*. Calvin assigns free reconciliation to the first and human
   renovation to the second; smoothing both into a general moral change
   would erase his central objection.
6. *Satisfactione qua defunctus est* uses *defungi* for discharging or fully
   performing an obligation. “The satisfaction he discharged” preserves its
   forensic force.
7. *Propitiator* is the person who effects propitiation. The translation
   expands the uncommon English noun because Calvin is emphasizing Christ's
   mediatorial work in his human nature.
