VERSE 10. Is there anything of which it is said, Look, this is new? It already
existed throughout the ages which were before us.

VERSE 11. There is no remembrance of former things; nor will there be any
remembrance among those who come afterward of the later things that are yet
to be.

This is a prolepsis. Its anticipation arises from the associated notion of
something new and unusual; the reply is drawn from the efficient cause of the
supposed novelty, namely, that past things have been handed over to oblivion
by those who come afterward. This is illustrated by a division: present
things are buried in oblivion by those who succeed them, so that nothing is
new except through the false imagination of human beings. Hence let us learn
not to be inflamed by so great and so immoderate a desire for new things (for
we are all Athenians, that is, devotees of novelty). First, indeed, we are
never satisfied by them, nor is there anything new under the Sun by the sight
or hearing of which the mind rests and is calmed. Once one surge of our desire
has been stilled by the report of something new, another is aroused, and it
torments the mind until we have understood what there is in it. Then, in
reality and fact, nothing is new under the Sun except in the false and
imaginary opinion of human beings. Therefore, the more we are moved by zeal
for novelty, the graver the testimony of our ignorance of things that have
passed which we give against ourselves. By that very fact we declare ourselves
to be boys and simpletons reared and educated in the country, to whom
everything—even things otherwise commonly known—seems new, while those who
live at court or in the city judge otherwise. Here the greatest Intellects of
the world are sharply rebuked: greatness of mind incites them to attempt some
great thing, the like of which did not exist before. Next, the
fact that these undertakings, however ingenious, are worn away by oblivion
among posterity ought to restrain this vanity of mind, though they nevertheless
take these things upon themselves in order to acquire a name for
themselves. How much better it would be to devote effort to righteousness,
which alone can rescue a person from oblivion! For the righteous will be in
everlasting remembrance. Proverbs 10. But if we desire to know what is done in
our absence, let us imitate Nehemiah, who earnestly inquired about the state
of the Church from those who had come from a far country, with this purpose:
that we may give thanks to God for things excellently done, but mourn with him
over adversities and pour forth prayers, joined with fasting if the
circumstances so demand. Then, so that we may be truly blessed, our mind and
pursuits—away from those worldly and obsolete things and toward the works of
God, especially the Gospel—

let us turn. In it truly new things are found which, once done, neither existed
before nor will exist afterward, such as the birth, death, resurrection, and
ascension of Christ the Savior. As they are new, so they are so astonishing
that, if we spent our whole life weighing and reflecting upon them, something
new would daily shine forth in them which had not been comprehended before.
Of all other things it can truly be said, as the common saying has it: that
which people admire comes to an end within nine days. And just as in the
works, so in the Law of God, wonderful and new things shine forth daily for those whose
eyes have been opened. Psalm 119. Then the mind must be lifted up, and we must
aspire to the heavenly kingdom, where all things will be new: a new heaven, a
new earth, a new sea, and whatever fills them will be new, a new Jerusalem,
where no weariness and no disgust will steal upon us.

Thus far he has proved the vanity of all things by more general reasons drawn
from the very nature of things. Arguments drawn from the testimony of
experience follow, which are of two kinds: namely, the things he experienced
in himself and in others.
