QUESTION XXI

ON THE AUTHORITY OF THE FATHERS

Are the writings of the Fathers the rule of truth in doctrines of faith and
the interpretation of Scripture? We deny, against the Roman Catholics.

I. Although it can already be sufficiently gathered from the preceding
question that the Fathers cannot sit as judges in controversies of faith,
nevertheless, because the Roman Catholics repeatedly have recourse to them and
are accustomed to press the consensus of the Fathers upon us as the rule of
truth, this subject, which is of the greatest importance for present-day
controversies, must be treated in a separate question.

[Marginal heading: Who are called Fathers.]

II. By the name *Fathers* we do not understand, with Augustine on Psalm 45,
the apostles as the first founders and patriarchs of the Christian church.
Rather, in accordance with the usage now established from ancient custom, we
understand the teachers of the ancient church who, after the departure of the
apostles, taught and illustrated the doctrine of salvation both by the living
voice and in writing. They are so called on account of their age, because they
preceded our times by many years, and on account of their teaching, because by
inculcating it in their disciples they begot children for God in the church.

III. Although there are those who extend their age as far as the tenth
century, we nevertheless do not think it should be extended beyond the sixth.
For it is certain that after the year 600, when Antichrist raised his head, the
purity of doctrine and worship suffered a great stain as errors and
superstitions increased by God's just judgment. In the first century after the
death of the apostles the principal figures were Ignatius and Polycarp, of
whom fragments survive. In the second were Justin Martyr and Irenaeus. In the
third were Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Cyprian, Arnobius, and
Lactantius. In the fourth were Athanasius, Eusebius of Caesarea, Hilary of
Poitiers, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus, Ambrose, Jerome, Gregory of Nyssa,
Epiphanius, and John Chrysostom. In the fifth were Augustine, Cyril of
Alexandria, Theodoret, Hilary of Arles, Prosper of Aquitaine, and Leo I. In the
sixth were Fulgentius the African, Gelasius, Gregory the Great, and others.

IV. There are three Roman Catholic opinions concerning the authority of the
Fathers. The first is that of those who make them equal to Scripture. To this
belong the words of the glossator on the *Decrees*, distinction 9, asserting
that “the writings of the Fathers, both of the individuals and of them all,
are authentic.” The second, opposed to the first, is that of those who
acknowledge their writings to be merely human and therefore incapable of being
a rule of faith. This was the view of Cajetan in his preface to the five books
of Moses and of the sounder Roman Catholics. The third is that of those who
take a middle course: they grant that the authority of the individual Fathers
is human and fallible, but judge the common and universal consensus of the
Fathers in controversies to be infallible and divine. This was the position
of the Council of Trent, session 4, canon 1, when it decreed that the
traditions of the Fathers pertaining both to faith and to morals are to be
received with the same feeling of piety as the Old and New Testaments. In the
same place it forbids anyone to dare to interpret Scripture contrary to the
sense which holy mother church has held or holds, and so forth, or even
contrary to the unanimous consensus of the Fathers. Most Roman Catholics,
including Stapleton, Bellarmine, Cano, Valencia, and others, agree with this.

V. The orthodox, however, although they hold the Fathers in great esteem and
judge them most useful for a knowledge of the history of the ancient church
and for establishing our agreement with it in the heads of faith, nevertheless

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 147 | IA leaf 201 | PDF p. 201]

deny that the authority of either individual Fathers or all of them can be
called authentic in matters of faith and the interpretation of Scripture, so
that one must stand or fall by their judgment. Rather, their authority is only
ecclesiastical and human, subordinate to Scripture, and has no weight except
insofar as it agrees with Scripture.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

VI. The question is not whether the Fathers are witnesses who testify about
the consensus of the ancient church or the position of the church in the
centuries in which they lived. It is whether they are judges who can settle
controversies of faith with infallible authority. The Roman Catholics maintain
the latter; we establish the former. Whenever we argue from the Fathers
against our adversaries, we use them only as witnesses who confirm with their
vote the truth we believe and declare the faith of the church of their own
time, not as judges whose rulings must be accepted absolutely and without
examination and are the measure of truth in doctrines of faith or the
interpretation of Scripture.

[Marginal heading: The Fathers cannot be judges. 1. Because they were
fallible.]

VII. The reasons are these. 1. Whether the Fathers are considered individually
and separately or all together, they were not prophets or apostles who,
equipped with an immediate calling and extraordinary gifts, possessed the
privilege of infallibility. They were fallible human beings liable to error,
whose knowledge was imperfect and who could be led by party spirit and carried
astray by passions. Nor did the mediated calling with which they were equipped
place them beyond danger of error. They not only could err, but it is
established that they often did err, whether individually or several together,
on various heads. This could easily be proved if we did not already have Roman
Catholics who agree with us here. Bellarmine confesses that even the most
learned Fathers have erred, and not slightly, in many matters, *On the Word of
God*, book 3, chapters 3 and 10; that they contradict themselves, *On Christ*,
book 2, chapter 2; and that there is no one who is not at times blind, in the
same place, chapter 2. Sixtus of Siena confirms this in the preface to book 5
of the *Bibliotheca*, as does Salmeron, part 3, book 1, disputation 6 on the
epistles of Paul.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because their writings are corrupt.]

VIII. 2. The writings of the Fathers have been corrupted and adulterated in
different ways. This has happened partly through the various pseudepigrapha
circulated under the Fathers' names, which, in the judgment of the more
learned, are known to be spurious offspring rashly ascribed and attributed to
the Fathers—whether through the tricks of calumniators, the frauds and deceits
of heretics, or the shameful pursuit of gain (αἰσχροκέρδειαν) by printers or
booksellers. It has happened partly through the corruption and falsification
inflicted on the Fathers' genuine writings, which are known to have been
variously adulterated through the wrongdoing of copyists, the audacity of
monks, and especially the wickedness of Jesuits in correcting, expurgating,
and mutilating them. Learned men have long complained about this, and our
writers have proved it with innumerable examples, as may be seen in Rivet's
*Sacred Criticism*, Dallaeus's *On the Use of the Fathers*, and others who have
treated this subject.

[Marginal heading: 3. Because the Fathers themselves do not want this.]

IX. 3. The Fathers themselves acknowledge that their writings ought not to be
authentic in such a way that one must stand absolutely by their bare
assertions in the things they propose about religion. Augustine says in letter
19 to Jerome: “I confess to your charity that I have learned to render this
fear and honor only to those books of Scripture which are now called
canonical: to believe most firmly that none of their authors made any error in
writing, and so forth. But I read others in such a way that, however much they
excel in sanctity of doctrine, I do not think a thing true merely because they
judged so, but because they were able to persuade me—either through those
canonical authors or through probable reason—that it does not

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 148 | IA leaf 202 | PDF p. 202]

depart from truth. Nor, my brother, do I think you hold anything differently.
Indeed, I do not think you wish your books to be read as those of the prophets
or apostles, concerning whose writings it is impious to doubt that they are
free from every error.” And in letter 3 to Fortunatus he says: “We ought not
treat the discussions of any persons, although they are catholic and
commendable men, as canonical Scriptures, so that, while preserving the honor
due those men, we would not be permitted to disapprove and reject something in
their writings if we should perhaps find that they thought differently from
the truth. This is how I approach the writings of others, and this is how I
want readers to approach my own.” And in *Against Cresconius*, book 2, chapter
31, he says: “We do Cyprian no injury when we distinguish any of his letters
from the authority of the canonical divine Scriptures. For the ecclesiastical
canon was not established with such salutary vigilance without cause, so that
certain books of the prophets and apostles would belong to it—books which we
would not dare to judge at all and according to which we freely judge all
other writings, whether of believers or unbelievers.” And in chapter 32 he
says: “I am not bound by the authority of this letter, because I do not regard
Cyprian's letters as canonical. Rather, I consider them from the canonical
writings, and whatever in them agrees with the authority of the divine
Scriptures I receive with praise for him; whatever does not agree, I reject
with peace toward him.” He confirms the same point more fully and forcefully
in *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, book 11, chapter 5: “There,” namely in the
canonical Scriptures, “if something seemingly absurd causes difficulty, one
may not say, ‘The author of this book did not hold to the truth.’ Instead,
either the manuscript is defective, the translator has erred, or you do not
understand. But in the works of later writers, which are contained in
innumerable books yet are in no way equal to the most sacred excellence of the
canonical Scriptures, even wherever equal truth is found in them, the
authority is nevertheless far from equal.” Jerome says similarly in letter 62
to Theophilus of Alexandria: “I regard the apostles in one way and other
expositors in another: the former always speak truth, while the latter, being
human, err in some things.” And in letter 76 he says: “I judge that Origen,
because of his learning, should sometimes be read in the same way as
Tertullian, Novatus, Arnobius, and certain ecclesiastical writers: we should
choose their good things and avoid the contrary, according to the apostle's
saying, ‘Test everything; hold fast what is good.’” He inculcates this
throughout and with great freedom repeatedly applies the censor's rod to the
opinions and expositions of his predecessors. Indeed, he even speaks this way
about his own writings: “If someone speaks better—indeed, more truly—we too
will gladly acquiesce in what is better,” commentary on Habakkuk and
Zechariah, volume 5. Ambrose gives the same testimony in *On the Sacrament of
the Lord's Incarnation*, chapter 3: “I do not want you to believe us; let
Scripture be recited. I do not say on my own authority that in the beginning
was the Word; I hear it. I do not invent it; I read it,” and so forth. Cyril of
Jerusalem says in *Catechesis* 12: “Do not attend to my ingenious constructions
(εὑρεσιλογίαις), for you might perhaps be deceived. Unless you receive
testimonies from the prophets concerning every point, do not believe what is
said,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: 4. The Roman Catholics themselves reject the Fathers.]

X. 4. The Roman Catholics themselves reject the authority of the Fathers
whenever they recognize them as opposing their own position, and they freely
depart from them. So far are they from acknowledging them as judges in matters
of faith. Many passages pertinent to this can be produced besides those
already brought forward from Bellarmine, Sixtus of Siena, and Salmeron.
Cajetan, speaking about his commentaries on Scripture in his preface to the
Pentateuch, says: “If at some time a new sense occurs which agrees with the
text and disagrees neither with Holy Scripture nor with the church's doctrine,
although it is alien to the stream of sacred doctors, I ask all readers not to
detest it rashly, but to show themselves fair judges. This authority is
reserved to the authors of Holy Scripture alone: that we believe a thing to be
so because they wrote it so.” Augustine says, “But others

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 149 | IA leaf 203 | PDF p. 203]

I read in such a way that, however much they excel in sanctity and learning,
I do not believe a thing to be so because they wrote it so.” After Melchior
Cano says from Augustine in *Theological Topics*, book 7, chapter 3, “God
wished this felicity to reside only in the divine volumes, that there should
be no error in them,” he adds: “Otherwise, no one, however learned and holy,
fails sometimes to be mistaken, to be blind somewhere, and at times to slip.”
And afterward, in section 3: “Therefore the ancient Fathers will be read by us
with reverence indeed, but as human beings, with selection and judgment.” And
in section 10: “But in truth, to follow our predecessors in everything and to
plant our feet in their footprints, as children do in play, seems to be
nothing other than to condemn our intellects, deprive ourselves of our own
judgment, and take away the ability to inquire into truth. Let us follow the
ancients—very well—but as leaders, not as lords.” Maldonatus frequently says
in his commentaries on the Gospels, at Matthew 19:11, 16:18, and 20:22:
“Almost all the Fathers explain it this way, though I certainly do not agree
with them.” Petavius says in *On Epiphanius*, page 205: “With the moderation
that is proper, we do not so much search out the errors and lapses of divine
men—yet still men—as disclose those which present themselves of their own
accord, occurring even against our wishes, so that they may deceive no one. We
are no more obliged to protect and defend them than their vices, if they had
any.” He testifies to the same thing on pages 244 and 285. Throughout his work,
Baronius very freely censures and refutes the Fathers whenever they think
something other than what he approves. Therefore, if the adversaries
themselves are found to despise and trample upon the Fathers so casually—even
Fathers who agree with them—whenever they are less pleasing, with
what effrontery do they decree that the Fathers must be heard as judges in our
controversies?

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XI. Whatever all the teachers hand down with unanimous agreement according to
the Word of God, the entire church can and ought to believe. But if they do not
speak from the Word and instead speak contrary to it, the church is so far
from being obliged to receive it that it is instead bound to pronounce an
anathema upon them, Galatians 1:8.

XII. Although the Fathers who were nearer to the age of the apostles ought to
have been purer, it does not follow that their writings can be regarded
together with the apostolic writings as a norm of truth. For the gift of
infallibility is a special privilege (ἐξαίρεμα) proper to the apostolate and
cannot belong to their successors, who were not endowed with the same gifts.

XIII. The unity of the church is properly preserved through the unity of the
faith delivered in Scripture, not through the consensus of the Fathers, which
can scarcely—if indeed even scarcely—be established.

XIV. The obedience due to leaders according to Hebrews 13:17 is not blind and
brutish, as though we should rashly assent to everything said or written by
them. It must be rational: we are to hear them when they speak the oracles of
God and hand down the things which they themselves received from Christ,
Matthew 28:20; 1 Corinthians 11:23.

XV. Although we are unwilling to acknowledge the Fathers as judges in causes
of faith, we do not thereby say that they have no authority. They can be of
great use—if not for founding faith, at least for illustrating and confirming
it—so that by this testimony the faith of the ancient church may be
established, it may be evident that the Roman Catholics boast of the consensus
of the Fathers rather than follow it, and it may be evident that the dogmas
they press upon us from tradition beyond Scripture were unheard of in the
first centuries.

XVI. The Roman Catholics vainly invoke the consensus of the Fathers for the
judgment of controversies and the interpretation of Scripture. 1. Even if it
could be obtained, it would furnish only a human and probable argument, like
one obtained from the opinions of jurists, but not a necessary and
unanswerable one (ἀναντεύθυνον), since the Fathers themselves submit both
themselves and their writings to the judgment of Scripture. 2. For if it is
not impossible, it is at least

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 150 | IA leaf 204 | PDF p. 204]

exceedingly difficult to discover such a consensus. Nor would that very long
and intricate way, entangled in the labyrinths of so many volumes, be suited
to ending controversies. This is especially so because one can scarcely, if
indeed even scarcely, know with certainty what the ancients thought about our
controversies. First, we possess very few writings of the ancients, especially
of those who lived in the first, second, or third century, although greater
account must be taken of them than of the later Fathers, since they approach
most nearly to the age of the apostles. Second, the writings which survive
from the first three centuries treat for the most part matters far removed
from our controversies and touch upon the controversies only in passing while
doing something else. Third, the Fathers frequently disagree with one another,
and they themselves are found not always to have thought the same thing on the
same heads of faith. They frequently change their position as they advance
with age in the knowledge of truth, and as old men retract what they judged
when young.

XVII. It must not be thought that the Fathers are despised or injured when the
supreme power of judgment is taken away from them. Care must indeed be taken
that the honor due them is not taken away, but much greater care must be taken
that too much is not accorded them, since error in this direction is usually
more dangerous than in the other. Indeed, if they came to life again, they
could not bear having such authority attributed to themselves. They would
thunder at us the very thing the apostles cried out to the Lystrans as they
approached to worship them: “We too are human beings of like nature”
(ὁμοιοπαθεῖς), Acts 14:14–15. Throughout their works the Fathers teach that
they wrote not with authority to prescribe but as an exercise for progress,
so that their writings would be read not with the necessity of believing but
with liberty to judge. They profess that their works are in no way equal to
the authority of the most sacred Scriptures, as Augustine says in *Against
Faustus*, book 11, chapter 5, and *Against Cresconius*, book 2, chapter 31.

XVIII. From these points it is easily gathered that the Fathers neither can
nor ought to be employed as judges in our controversies, but as witnesses who
by their remarkable consensus bear testimony to the truth of Christianity and
who, by their silence or even by sound arguments, prove the vanity of the
dogmas introduced by the Roman Catholics beyond and against Scripture.
Therefore, however reverently their writings should be received and however
profitably they can be read, they can have no authority other than
ecclesiastical and human—that is, an authority subordinate to and dependent
upon Scripture.
