QUESTION XVIII.

CHRIST’S ASCENSION.

*Was Christ’s ascension effected properly by local motion from the lower
places to the highest heaven of the blessed? Or metaphorically by
disappearance? We affirm the former and deny the latter against the
Lutherans.*

I. This question is raised because of the Lutherans, specifically those who
attribute ubiquity to Christ’s body. For in order more easily to weaken the
argument drawn from Christ’s ascension to prove the spatial boundedness of his
body, they deny that Christ ascended truly and properly. They say he ascended
only figuratively, insofar as he withdrew his visible presence from the world,
although he still remains invisibly in the world. And they contend that the
heaven into which he ascended is not the highest heaven, or third heaven,
called the heaven of the blessed, but blessedness and the kingdom of God,
which is everywhere. See Brentius, *On Christ’s Ascension.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

[Printed p. 322 | Internet Archive PDF 350 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

II. It is true that they do not agree among themselves in every respect. Some,
such as Brochmann in section 16 of *On Christ,* do not deny that Christ’s
ascension consists in a true and real upward passage and motion from the lower
parts of this world and in a change of place. Indeed, he proves from various
passages of Scripture that Christ entered heaven. But he says that no mortal
can comprehend or explain what that heaven is. He wishes it to be certain that
it is not a corporeal place situated above the visible heavens and separated
from these lands by a local distance, and that it is defined not so much by a
particular local position as by joy and glory in the Holy Spirit.

III. We, however, maintain that Christ was carried locally, visibly, and
bodily from earth into the third heaven, or the seat of the blessed above the
visible heavens. This occurred not merely by the withdrawal of his visible
presence or familiar society, but by a true and local translation of his human
nature, which is to remain there until the day of judgment. Thus, although he
is always present with us by his grace, Spirit, and divinity, he is no longer
present with us by the bodily presence of his flesh.

[Marginal heading: Christ’s ascension is proved to have been effected by local
motion.]

IV. First, it is proved from the history of the ascension, which indicates a
true and real change of place. Mark 16:19 says that he “was taken up into
heaven” (ἀνελήφθη εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν). Luke 24:51 says that “he withdrew from
them and was being carried up into heaven” (διέστη ἀπ’ αὐτῶν, καὶ ἀνεφέρετο
εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν). Acts 1:9–10 says, “As they watched, he was lifted up, and a
cloud received him and took him from their eyes.” Here the point from which
this ascension began is described—namely, the earth, and specifically the
Mount of Olives; that through which it passed—the air and the cloud; and that
to which it proceeded—heaven. And the terms by which it is expressed—taking
up (ἀνάληψις), carrying up (ἀναφορά), departure (διάστασις), and elevation
(ἔπαρσις)—imply a real translation and change of place. Nor does it help to
reply that these things are said only of his familiar society and earthly
association, but not that a real invisible presence was taken away from
believers—a presence which Christ’s solemn promise confirms. For the words
themselves sufficiently teach that no less the real presence of his body on
earth than his familiar
association was taken away, since they necessarily suppose a translation from
a lower to a higher place.

V. Second, because he is said to have left the world and returned to the
Father, John 16:28, which could not be said if he had withdrawn only his
visible presence from the world. The circumstances of the places also show
one local presence, partly from the antithesis between the point from which
and the point to which: “I leave the world,” that is, the earth, he says, “and
go to the Father,” that is, I ascend into heaven; and partly from the
opposition between coming and departing. “I came into the world” through the
incarnation; now “I leave the world” with respect to the flesh, according to
which he is said to have been “taken up” (ἀναληφθεὶς) and was seen by the
disciples “going into heaven” (πορευόμενος εἰς τὸν οὐρανόν), Acts 1:10–11. It
does not follow from his being said to have come into the world as the Word
(Λόγος) merely by manifestation and not by a change of place that the same can
be said of his flesh. For the case of his divinity, which, being immeasurable,
cannot change place, is one thing; the case of his humanity, whose property it
is to pass from one place to another, is another.

VI. Third, the heaven into which Christ was carried is described to us as a
particular place which he entered and which we will enter after him. It is
called the Father’s house in which there are many dwellings, where Christ
ascended to prepare a place for us, John 14:2–3; the third heaven and Paradise,
2 Corinthians 12, which Christ entered as our forerunner, Hebrews 6; and the
place toward which we should lift our eyes by seeking the things above, where
Christ sits at God’s right hand, and not the things that are upon the earth,
Colossians 3:1–2. Therefore Brentius and other Lutherans wrongly contend that
this heaven is nothing other than blessedness itself, which is incomprehensible
to us. For it is one thing to ask what this heaven is and another to ask what
it is like.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Christ is said to have ascended above all the heavens, Ephesians 4:10,
namely the visible ones—the aerial heaven and the firmament—in order to enter
the third heaven, or Paradise. For he is said to have ascended above all the
heavens in such a way that he is nevertheless said to be in heaven, Ephesians
1:20. Nor, if he is said to have ascended there “that he might fill all
things,” should this be understood of filling every place by the omnipresence
of his body, but of filling all the members of the church by the outpouring of
the Spirit’s gifts, as is explained in the same passage.

VIII. Christ was in the midst of the seven lampstands and thus on earth,
Revelation 1:13, by his Spirit, grace, protection, and government, but not by
the presence of his body. Nor did John see him walking on earth by ocular
vision, but only by intellectual and ecstatic vision. This is indicated in
verse 10, where he is said to have been “in the Spirit” (ἐν πνεύματι), that
is, in an ecstasy, as in Acts 10:10. In this way Paul also beheld him standing
beside him, Acts 23:11.

IX. The same one who descended also ascended, Ephesians 4:10, with respect to
the person but not with respect to the nature. For the divine nature descended,
but the human nature ascended: the former metaphorically and figuratively,
because it could not do so otherwise, but the latter properly and locally. So
he is truly in heaven, where he was not before, just as he is no longer on
earth, where he was before. Although Christ did not descend properly and
locally from heaven, because as the Word (Λόγος) he is everywhere, it does not
follow that this heaven is not local but allegorical, since things are
attributed to it which can apply only to a true and proper place. In these
expressions, therefore, the figure is not in the word *heaven* but in the verbs
*coming* and *descending.*

X. Although Christ departed through the ascension to the Father, who is
everywhere, it does not follow that this ascension is nothing other than a
departure into supreme blessedness, which is everywhere in the Father and not
in some definite place. For he is said to have departed to the Father not so
that he might be everywhere with the Father, but so that he might be where he
was to be crowned with glory and honor—namely, in heaven, where God has his
throne and where Christ himself had to remain until the restoration of all
things. Thus, when he says that he is departing, he indicates a change of
place; when he adds that he is departing to the Father, he indicates a change
of condition appropriate to the human nature.

XI. Although believers are blessed wherever they are because they are always
with God, their blessedness is not perfect until they are received into
heaven, toward which Stephen looked as he longed for Christ. Hence Paul,
although he was already with Christ by faith, nevertheless desires to leave
the earth, depart into heaven, and be with him by sight, Philippians 1:23. And
believers who are already said to sit in heaven with Christ by faith and hope
are still absent from him as strangers, 2 Corinthians 5:6.

XII. Just as Stephen is said in Acts 7:56 to have seen Christ in heaven when
he saw the heavens opened and Christ standing at God’s right hand—having been
given extraordinary keenness of sight by the Holy Spirit—in the same way Paul
could see him, Acts 9:27. Hence in Acts 26:19 he calls it a heavenly
appearance. And the light said to have shone around him from heaven, verse 13,
was indeed a symbol of Christ’s glorious majesty, but not evidence that his
body was present on earth or in the air. Nor, if Paul is said to have been on
the road when he saw the Lord, does it follow that Christ, whom Paul saw, was
also on the road.
