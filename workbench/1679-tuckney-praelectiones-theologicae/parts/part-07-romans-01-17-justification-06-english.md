4. But in the fourth place, we are fully convinced that “all” is signified;
that is, that the apostle excludes all works—ceremonial as well as moral,
those done with faith as well as without faith, those done from the grace of
the Holy Spirit as well as from the powers of nature or free choice, internal
as well as external works, and works pertaining to the spiritual and truly
Christian and thus eternal life as well as to civil life. They are excluded
not indeed from those who have been justified, but from justification; nor,
to be sure, from justification itself, whose perpetual and inseparable
companions or attendants they are, but from the cause of justification,
whether material, formal, efficient, meritorious, or constitutive. For since
Paul excludes “works of the law” indefinitely and absolutely, “we ought not
to limit what he himself did not limit,” to use Salmerón’s words. Yet in
speaking so he said better than he acted. For after rightly saying that the
apostle’s words must not be limited, he nevertheless limited them—though not,
as others do most deplorably, to ceremonial works, still wrongly—to moral
works done without faith by the power of free choice. We most rightly limit
them to no species of works, but extend them to all works universally. That
is, we contend that, in the apostle’s intention, all works are excluded from
the nature and essence of justification according to Paul and properly so
called.

For if with Grotius and the Roman Catholics we understood justification as
the infusion of grace and its effects, it would be mere λογομαχία, a battle
over words, and we would be playing games—arguing, namely, whether inherent
grace and its works are excluded from themselves. But since the apostle
treats of that justification which we also debate and upon which, to speak
with Contarini, we ought

to depend and rest—namely, for the forgiveness of sins, the pardon of guilt,
and divine acceptance unto eternal life—the apostle excludes absolutely all
works from it. We also contend that they are utterly excluded from it, and do
so according to the apostle’s intention and even that of another Roman
Catholic himself, Cardinal Contarini of the Roman Church. In his *Book of
Letters*, in the letter on justification, he has these words, which agree
most closely with our position and the apostle’s: “It remains to inquire upon
which” (that is, either inherent righteousness or imputed and bestowed
righteousness) “we ought to rest and reckon ourselves justified. I judge it
entirely pious and Christian to say that we ought to rest—rest, I say, as
upon a stable thing which certainly sustains us—upon the righteousness of
Christ bestowed upon us, and not upon holiness and grace inhering in us,
because it is begun and imperfect and cannot protect us,” with the rest that
follows in the same vein. Nothing could be said more piously or more
orthodoxly than these words; nothing more truly conformable or serviceable to
the cause of all Protestants could have been said by Luther or Calvin
himself. The Tridentine faith on this matter had evidently not yet been so
firmly established that a cardinal of the Roman Church did not dare both to
profess and to confirm this most delightful doctrine of gratuitous
justification through Christ’s righteousness imputed to us. But he was not
permitted to do so for long, for he was shortly carried off by death. Whether
by the evil arts of certain men, I say nothing myself, though others suspect
it.

I return to the subject. The various opinions on the matter we are treating
now remain to be examined.

As for Grotius’s third fiction, there is no need for us to say anything
further, except insofar as it draws the same yoke with that twofold opinion
of the Roman Catholics.

For the better examination and refutation of each, these two foundations
must first be secured.

1. Let this be the first. That opinion is more probable, safer, and more
consonant with Paul’s argument and judgment which, if the other things agree,
assigns less to us and more to grace. For the apostle is wholly occupied,
everywhere but especially throughout this controversy over justification,
with snatching from us every occasion for boasting in ourselves and
assigning the whole matter, whatever it may be, entirely to God, Christ, and
grace.

But our opinion, which excludes not only ceremonial works and moral works
preceding faith but also those following faith, assigns less to us and more
to grace. The other things also agree, as will afterwards be evident from the
answer to the objections brought against it. It is therefore safer and more
consonant with the apostle’s intention.

Against the minor premise: 1. Reason appears to protest, since the opinion
which denies justification to works done from grace—which is ours—appears to
assign less to grace than the opinion which assigns justification to them,
which belongs to the Roman Catholics.

For how is an opinion opposed to grace when it assigns justification only to
those works which flow from grace?

2. Bellarmine protests still more strongly against the claim that his party’s
opinion assigns less to grace. For what is rendered to works done from grace
is not simply a reward but is also grace—indeed, more grace than reward. So
he argues in *On Justification*, book 1, chapter 19.

I answer:

1. To the first, by denying that the opinion which

[1679 printed p. 90 | IA leaf 98]

denies justification to works done from grace assigns less to grace than the
opinion which assigns it to them. For the apostle opposes works to grace, in
whatever sense the works are taken or from whatever source they proceed. “To
the one who works, the reward is not reckoned according to grace,” Romans
4:4; and in chapter 11:6, “If by grace, then it is no longer from works;
otherwise grace is no longer grace. But if from works, it is no longer grace;
otherwise work is no longer work.” Therefore, insofar as works proceed from
grace which first justifies and then sanctifies, they in their own place and
order illustrate and exalt grace. Insofar as they are works, however, if they
are elevated to constitute justification—higher than God ever intended
them—by the very fact that they are works and that they are in any way ours,
they conflict with the nature of justification and consequently with grace
insofar as it justifies. This will become still clearer from the answer.

2. To the second objection, which was Bellarmine’s: namely, that what is
rendered to works done from grace is not simply a reward but also grace,
indeed, more grace than reward. Take those trappings to the crowd! As though,
in truth:

1. The reward of those works should be credited to grace which, according to
the opinion of the Roman school, merit eternal life by condignity. That is,
as they have held, eternal life is owed to them by the rigor of justice—and
of commutative justice—according to arithmetical proportion, so that heaven,
on the assertion of the Cologne Jesuits, is offered for sale, and God, in
Cajetan’s judgment, would be unjust unless he rendered it to them. This is
because of the absolute equality between the reward and the merit, as
Bellarmine held. Do these things sound at all of grace? Do they not openly
cry out for reward alone?

But if they object that, although the reward of glory is by condignity, the
works nevertheless arise from grace alone with respect to their cause and
origin, I answer:

2. As though those very works of which Bellarmine speaks could be ascribed
wholly to grace, when at least in part—and that coordinately in the first
conversion—and indeed with respect to the determination of the act itself
toward the concurrence of divine grace, they antecedently depend upon and
flow from our free choice. But grace is in no way grace unless it is in every
way gratuitous. And our justification before God is, according to the
apostle in Romans 3:14 [the witness’s citation; the words are Romans 3:24],
δωρεὰν τῇ αὐτοῦ χάριτι, “freely by his grace.” Under this twofold title it is
in every way, in its whole extent, wholly gratuitous, according to the
apostle’s position and ours. Our position, therefore, which assigns this
whole matter to grace, is both safer and more consonant with Paul’s doctrine
and argument, according to the first foundation.
