Let this be the fourth argument. Works that follow justification do not
causally constitute justification so that we are justified by them. But
works following faith also follow justification. Therefore they do not
constitute justification so that we are justified by them. The major premise
is plain, since an effect neither constitutes nor precedes its cause. There
will be little controversy about the minor if we believe Augustine's famous
statement, “Good works follow one who has been justified; they do not precede
one who is to be justified,” or simply yield to the evident truth of the
matter.

Roman Catholics make faith the first preparation antecedent to
justification and say that in this preparation it produces fear, hope, and
similar things. They may therefore wish certain works or operations of this
kind to be performed after faith but before formal justification. Yet they
will concede that these are still preparatory works, not formally justifying
ones. They might instead say that works flowing from faith as an antecedent
preparation formally justify in the act of justification itself, so that
although they follow faith, they precede justification and do not follow it.
I answer:

We regard faith not as a work, but as a passive instrument insofar as it
apprehends Christ in the formal act of justification. We thus acknowledge it
as prior by nature, though not in time, to the accomplishment of actual
justification. But the Roman Catholics flatly deny and condemn this manner
of justification. They must therefore designate other works of faith that
can formally justify us. Yet all such works follow not only faith but
justification, since only one already justified can perform them, just as a
good fruit can come only from a good tree (Matthew 7:18) and from a branch
already engrafted into Christ (John 15:4–5). We are engrafted into Christ in
the very act of justification. Therefore only after we are actually
justified do we bear good fruits of this kind, and our good works follow
justification. Augustine likewise says that justification does not come to
those who do the law, but justification precedes those who do it: “A man
does not work righteousness unless he is justified.” These works therefore
follow justification, as was to be proved.

The adversaries may reply that these works indeed follow justification, but

[1679 printed p. 122 | IA leaf 130]

justification itself consists in the infusion of a habit or of grace. Since
this infusion constitutes justification, it does not follow it. I answer:

1. When we reach that controversy, it will appear that justification does
not consist in the infusion of grace. It will later be demonstrated that the
instrument through which we are justified is not the habit of faith but the
act of believing.

2. They wrongly confuse works flowing from a habit infused into us with the
infusion of the habit or the infused habit itself. The infusion and the
infused habit are the operation and work of God alone, whereas the works
flowing from it are also ours. The former finds justification—and, even on
their view, a person who is unrighteous (Romans 4:5)—while the latter,
performed after the habit has been infused, presuppose a person already
righteous and justified. They therefore follow justification and do not
causally effect it.

Fifth. Whatever their antecedent or consequent relation may be, these works
undoubtedly possess something else that takes away all their power to
justify: they are not perfect, but are more or less sinful in some degree.
All our righteousness is like a menstrual cloth (Isaiah 64:6): “Who can
bring a clean thing out of an unclean?” (Job 14:5).

Gregory says repeatedly that “all human righteousness is proved to be
unrighteousness if it is judged strictly. Prayer is therefore needed after
righteousness, so that what could succumb under examination may prevail by
the Judge's mercy alone. For our evils are pure evil, but the good things we
believe we possess cannot be pure good.” Bernard adds: “Unless perhaps we
believe ourselves better than our fathers, who said no less truly than
humbly that all our righteousness is like the cloth of a menstruating woman.
How can righteousness be pure where guilt still cannot be absent?”

These words plainly concern righteousness considered simply in itself and
in individual works, not righteousness compared with God or viewed over the
whole course of life, as the Roman Catholics quibble. More must be said about
this later. For now we take as indubitable that even the works of faith of
the holiest person possess some imperfection and hence some sin. We conclude
that not even the holiest person can be justified by works, however holy he
may make them. No one will ever be justified by things that require pardon
and deserve condemnation.

Someone may say that pardon is not so foreign to justification that the two
cannot be joined. A person might be justified by works that need pardon to
some extent, since Roman Catholics say that some venial sins may adhere to
works that justify nevertheless; some of our writers, moreover, make pardon
of sins the whole nature of justification, and others at least one part of
it. I answer:

1. No sins are venial by their nature in the Roman Catholic sense. All are
mortal, condemnatory, and obstacles to justification.

2. Among our writers, the preferable view denies that the entire essence of
justification is exhausted by pardon of sins. Besides the imputation of

[1679 printed p. 123 | IA leaf 131]

Christ's righteousness, it requires an accounting of righteousness and a
reputation as righteous. God willing, this will be discussed later.

3. Those who place the whole of justification in remission of sins intend
anything but that a person is justified by the very things or works that
need pardon. Although we obtain justification from pardon, we deserve
condemnation from works needing pardon, for such need itself implies prior
guilt. A prince's pardon of a traitor's crime justifies the traitor insofar
as it frees him from guilt and punishment. He does not obtain this from his
treason, which deserved exactly the opposite—not pardon but death. Thus,
although pardon and justification in the sense previously defined agree and
cooperate within the gracious economy of salvation and divine grace, in
this respect they are directly opposed. Pardon speaks of the personal guilt
of the pardoned man considered in himself, while justification declares not
his guilt but his righteousness. Since he has none in himself as a guilty
man, his Surety's righteousness is freely imputed to him, and by this
imputation together with the pardon of his sins he is truly and fully
justified.

Sixth. We are not justified by things on account of which we are not elected,
for grace is wholly gratuitous both in election and justification. We are
freely justified (Romans 3:24) just as we are freely elected (Ephesians
1:5–6), and a consideration of works is as opposed to grace in justification
as it is in election (Romans 4:4–5; 11:6).

We are not elected even by works done from faith. Romans 9:11 says that
God's purpose according to election stands, “not from works but from him who
calls.” Second Timothy 1:9 speaks of God “who saved us and called us with a
holy calling, not according to our works but according to his own purpose
and grace, which was given to us in Christ Jesus before the ages began.” In
the former passage election is expressly denied to works; in the latter our
works and God's gracious purpose are placed in direct opposition.

One may reply that these passages indeed mean works, but “our” works—that is,
as Paul explains himself in Titus 3:5, “which we ourselves did.” Bellarmine
takes this to mean works done by our own powers, not by Christ's grace and
faith.

I answer:

All these passages plainly say the opposite. Works are not combined with the
grace of election, but are directly opposed to it as causes of election:
“not from works, but from him who calls”; “not according to our works, but
according to his own purpose and grace.” These passages deny the whole
matter, whatever its extent, not merely to some of our works, as the
adversaries wish, but absolutely all of them. They refer it wholly to the
purpose and grace of the God who calls and elects, to whom alone it is due.

Nor does Paul's phrase “works of righteousness which we did” admit the Roman
Catholic gloss about works done by our own powers without faith. It appears
to mean just the opposite: works which we performed after we had been called
and renewed by the Holy Spirit poured abundantly upon us. Thus Clement writes
to the Romans: “We are justified not by ourselves, nor by our wisdom,
understanding, piety, or works performed in holiness of heart, but through
faith.” These works are themselves righteous and performed by us after being
constituted righteous or justified.

[1679 printed p. 124 | IA leaf 132]

They seem contrasted with the works done while people were still
unregenerate and described in the preceding verse 3 as disobedient, deceived,
serving various desires and pleasures, and acting in malice and envy.

But one may say that Paul speaks about himself and others who are now
regenerate while referring to works they did, or at least would have done,
before renewal. Calvin, Hyperius, and some of our writers agree here with the
Roman Catholics. The aorist verb “we did,” signifying past time, suggests
this, and the context compels it. Paul is exhorting believers to malign no
one but to show every gentleness toward all (verse 2). According to Piscator,
he supports this with two arguments. First, we ourselves were once such
people before conversion, so we must not do to others what we would then
have wished others not to do to us. Second, believers were delivered from
that miserable state by no righteous works of their own, since they then had
none, but by God's grace alone. They therefore have no reason to despise the
unconverted, but should instead hope that the same grace and mercy of God
will come to them also, however unworthy and unfit they may be.

I answer:

1. The aorist sometimes denotes present time, as in “I am well pleased”
(Matthew 3:17) and “they sit” (Matthew 23:2).

2. Even if it denotes the past here, it does not follow that it means only
works performed before conversion. When Paul says that God “saved us,” this
must not be restricted to their first conversion and regeneration, of which
verse 5 speaks. It extends through the whole course of grace and godliness
after regeneration to the inheritance of eternal life in heaven, as verse 7
shows. It may therefore embrace not only works before conversion but past
works after it. Indeed, as Estius observes, “good works” occurs six times in
the epistle and, by his own admission, must mean truly righteous works.

3. My third answer is that the contextual argument correctly proves that all
works before regeneration are included, as Calvin, Hyperius, and Piscator
hold. Yet I say with Beza that “the benefit of our salvation is denied to the
righteousness of subsequent works as well as antecedent ones.” This appears
from the antithesis between works and grace and from the two immediately
following verses contrasting our regeneration with our justification by
grace.

4. If they obstinately insist that works following faith and conversion
cannot be meant here, it is enough for us that other passages of Scripture
deny justification not only to works preceding faith but also to those that
follow it. This has already been proved.

Our seventh argument may be drawn from considering the people whom Paul
either rebukes or better instructs in this dispute about justification. They
were not pure Jews hostile to the faith, whom he sought to prove were not
justified by works done without the faith they rejected. If he dealt only
with such people, as Tilenius rightly observes, his argument in Galatians
2:21 would be inept and ridiculous: “If righteousness is through the law,
then Christ died in vain.” Pure Jews would readily concede this and indeed

[1679 printed p. 125 | IA leaf 133]

heartily assert it, blasphemous though it is. Rather, Paul refutes the
Judaizing deceivers, false apostles, and teachers, and better instructs the
deceived, half-Jewish Christians to whom he writes. They would never concede
that Christ died in vain or dare to say that a man is justified by works
done without faith. They wished only to join Moses with Christ, law with
gospel, and works with faith in the matter of justification.

The Roman Catholic Estius likewise writes on Galatians 2:16: “The doctrine
of the Judaizers against whom Paul acts was to mix the law with faith in
Christ and to expect salvation equally from both.” This is precisely the
cause Roman Catholics advance today when they contend that a man is
justified not by works without faith, but by works joined with or flowing
from faith. Thus with Origen they make a confused mixture of law and gospel.
Since Paul refuted this old opinion of the Judaizers, it follows that he also
simultaneously explodes the Roman Catholic invention, since it is precisely
the same.

Let the eighth argument be the one we previously used to prove that Paul
does not exclude ceremonial works alone. It applies equally here to prove
that he does not exclude only moral works preceding faith. Otherwise the
inference that Paul says his adversaries customarily drew from his doctrine
of justification would have no foundation whatever: “Let us do evil that
good may come” (Romans 3:8), and “Shall we continue in sin that grace may
abound?” (Romans 6:1). The latter is said of those already engrafted into
Christ and justified, as the following verses 2–3 show: “By no means! We who
died to sin,” and so forth.

Assuming that Paul speaks about regenerate people, I say that his doctrine
of justification would give his adversaries no occasion for this inference
if it were understood in the Roman Catholic sense—that only works preceding
faith are excluded from justification. I grant that if the subject were the
unregenerate, the inference would have some appearance of reason. An
unconverted, impious sinner might wickedly reason: Since works preceding
faith contribute nothing to my future justification, why should I perform
such works while unregenerate? Rather, let me continue and indulge more in
sin, so that God's indulgence may appear greater in my justification and the
pardon of these sins.

Yet Paul, as already proved, speaks of those dead to sin and implanted into
Christ. If he excluded only works preceding faith, as the Roman view holds,
there would be no reason for the inference, “Therefore let us continue in
sin.” Who would ever infer that sins should be indulged after faith because
works preceding faith do not justify? On the contrary, if works following
faith justify, as Roman Catholics maintain, anyone—and especially Paul—could
easily answer the adversaries' quibble by saying that he excluded from
justification only works preceding faith. Good works following faith would
be so far from excluded that they formally constitute justification and
must be performed precisely so that we may be justified by them, while all
sins must be banished.

But in refuting this profane quibble Paul makes no distinction of works
preceding and following faith. He urges the indivisible bond of justification
and sanctification, since he had excluded all works from justification,
whether following

[1679 printed p. 126 | IA leaf 134]

or preceding faith. We have sufficiently proved this by these arguments.
