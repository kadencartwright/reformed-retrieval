Now that Alfonso's argument has at last been deprived of authority, let us
see whether the arguments Domingo de Soto brings into battle perhaps fight
more effectively for this cause. He argues as follows.

Objection 3. After the apostle had concluded in chapter 3 that no one is
justified by works of the law, he immediately introduced Abraham's example at
the beginning of chapter 4 and labored to show that Abraham was justified
before circumcision. Why insist so much upon circumcision here unless to show
that, when he denied anyone was justified by works of the law, he meant only
the law of circumcision and the other ceremonies?

I answer:

1. This argument is drawn from the connection between chapters, as the former
was from the connection between clauses, to prove that precisely the same
matter is treated in both. We showed earlier that this does not always hold,
especially in Paul's writings and least of all in this epistle.

2. Chamier answers that Paul came to mention circumcision not to prove that
men are not justified by law, but rather that justification is not proper to
the Jews and is offered to the Gentiles. This answer deserves greater
approval because, as Chrysostom rightly says, two things principally troubled
Jewish minds: first, that some are justified by faith without works of the
law; second, that Gentiles share equally with Jews in this justification by
faith. Since the latter distressed them most, after proving the former in the
first eight verses of chapter 4, he pursues the latter more fully afterward.
He had acted similarly in the preceding third chapter: after a long argument
he concluded the former proposition in verse 28, that all are justified by
faith, not works of the law, and immediately approached the second
proposition, as Calvin calls it on that passage,

[Marginalia: *On Justification*, book 21, chapter 9; on Romans 3:28.]

[1679 printed p. 110 | IA leaf 118]

teaching that Gentiles as well as Jews were made participants in justification
by faith. Soto's argument therefore totters, since when Paul speaks there of
Abraham's circumcision he treats a question connected with the former one,
but not precisely the same question.

3. Even if it were conceded that the same matter is treated in both places,
and that Paul wishes to prove no one justified by works of the law through an
argument drawn from circumcision as an example—Abraham was not justified by
works of the law because he was not justified by circumcision, since he was
justified before being circumcised—it still by no means follows that “works
of the law” excluded from justification in that conclusion means ceremonial
works alone. Circumcision is presented only as an example and thus taken
synecdochically for the entire law; but the Mosaic law comprehends the moral
no less than the ceremonial law. Why Paul principally mentions circumcision
and takes his example from the ceremonial rather than moral law was explained
in the answer to the first objection.

4. Add that although circumcision in Abraham's time and under the law was
ceremonial, obedience rendered to it was moral, since the ceremonial law was
an appendix of the Decalogue's first table. Works of this kind at that time
were therefore ceremonial-moral. Thus when Paul denies justification to the
observance of ceremonies insofar as they were performed under law, he also
denies it to moral works.

Let us come fourthly to Soto's other argument. When Paul in Galatians 2
reproved Peter for excessive indulgence toward the Jews in a ceremonial
matter, he did so by this argument: a man is not justified by works of the
law. He pursues this proposition through nearly all the following epistle.
But Paul's argument would be entirely beside the point unless ceremonial
works were meant in the proposition he undertook to prove. What logic would
there be in the inference: “A man is not justified by moral works; therefore
Peter had to be reproved by Paul for being an excessive observer of
ceremonies”?

I answer:

1. We conceded earlier that, because of the occasion the Galatians supplied,
Paul attends more to ceremonial works in that epistle and deprives them of
all power to justify.

2. Yet he could take occasion from ceremonial works for a broader argument
which comprehended moral works.

3. He did so in that argument, as we showed earlier from Melanchthon.

4. He could rightly do this: beginning with circumcision and the ceremonial
law, he could then deprive the whole law, including the moral, of power to
justify, because Moses delivered the moral as well as the ceremonial law.
Indeed, the moral law is equally opposed to justification, and in one respect
more opposed insofar as it savors more of merit, whereas justification is
entirely from grace.

5. Finally, the same can be said about both Soto arguments as was said earlier
about Alfonso's: they do not compel Roman Catholics themselves to embrace the
opinion they are intended to establish. Alfonso's argument partly pleased
Bellarmine. Soto's arguments cannot persuade even Soto himself to approve the
opinion they seek to prove, namely, that in this argument Paul excludes only
ceremonial works from justification. Since that opinion is therefore hard to
defend, it has little credibility with us.
