QUESTION XX.

ON INFANT BAPTISM.

Should the infants of believing covenant members be baptized? Affirmed,
against the Anabaptists.

[Marginalia: Origin of the controversy.]

I. The controversy was renewed in the previous century on account of the
Anabaptists, who began to attack infant baptism. Hence they acquired the name
of Anabaptists or Catabaptists because they repudiated baptism received in
infancy and repeated it once they had reached adulthood. They are commonly
called Anabaptists from the repetition of baptism in those who have once been
baptized, whether with respect to infants already baptized or with respect to
adults. For when adults pass from one sect of their people to another, they
baptize them anew in order to receive them into the communion of their
assembly. They are called Catabaptists, however, from their rejection of
baptism, especially in infants. Under this more general name come all
despisers of baptism who maintain that its present use in the church of Christ
is either of no value or a matter of indifference.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

II. Observe, first, that the question does not concern infants of every kind,
including those of unbelievers and pagans, but only those of covenant members
and Christians. As for the former, we acknowledge that they neither can nor
should be baptized unless instruction has preceded and they have reached
adulthood, when they can give an account of their faith. Second, the question
does not concern the absolute necessity of baptism, such as the papists
assert, which we refuted above, but the hypothetical necessity of the command.
The question is therefore whether the infants of Christians, born to one or
both Christian parents, should be baptized because Christ so commanded and
because through baptism God is graciously, though freely, accustomed to work
effectively in attesting and sealing grace. The Anabaptists deny this; we
affirm it, supported by the following arguments.

[Marginalia: Infant baptism is proved. 1. From Christ’s command.]

III. (1) From Christ’s command. Christ commanded it, if not *kata to rhēton*
(κατὰ τὸ ῥητὸν), according to the express wording and in so many words, yet
equivalently and as to the sense (Matt. 28:19–20): “Go, teach all nations,
baptizing them, etc.” This is evident: (1) He who commands all nations to be
baptized also commands infants to be baptized, for a command concerning the
genus includes every species. (2) Christ’s purpose is to teach the manner of
gathering and preserving the church on earth until the consummation of the
ages and to prescribe it to the apostles and their successors. Now just as
the church consists of infants as well as adults, so that manner pertains to
both, but according to the condition of each: adults are to be taught before
they are baptized, but infants, as covenant members and Christians, are first
to be baptized and afterward taught at the proper time. (3) It is evident
from the antithesis, for “all nations” are opposed to all and only the Jews,
as the distinction between the Old and New Testaments requires. Among the
Jews both infants and adults were circumcised. Therefore, by parity of
reasoning, the rationale of baptism ought to be the same. (4) It is evident
from the deed of the apostles, by which they declared the meaning of the
command and confirmed it in practice when they baptized entire households,
that is, parents with their children (Acts 16:15, 33; 18:8; 1 Cor. 1:16).

IV. It should not be objected that Christ places instruction before baptism
and therefore speaks of adults who can be taught, not of infants: “Make
disciples” (*mathēteusate*, μαθητευσατε), he says. For although Christ placed
instruction before

[1847 vol. III printed p. 355 | IA leaf 389 | PDF p. 389]

baptism, this would have to be referred to the baptism of adults, which was
the first and chief concern in the original state of the church when it was
to be constituted from adults. Here, therefore, a distinction must be made
between the church being constituted and the church already constituted. In
the former, adults must be taught before being baptized, but in the church
already constituted, infants must be baptized before being taught.
Predications are such as their subjects permit them to be: infants are not
capable of instruction; therefore, it does not pertain to them. Meanwhile,
Christ’s command must be understood analogically of infants from the nature
of the covenant and the perpetual practice of the church from Abraham onward
(Gen. 17:20). The word *mathēteuein* (μαθητευειν), which Christ uses, properly
means not so much to teach by preaching as to make disciples. This is also
done by the administration of baptism, which is the sacrament of initiation
and the first entrance into the church and family of Christ. Thus in John 4:1,
*mathētas poiein* (μαθητὰς ποιεῖν) is not simply to teach, but to make
disciples and receive them into a profession of discipleship. In the same
way, disciples were often made among the Jews not after they had already been
taught, but in order that they might be taught. Hence the Gentile said to
Hillel, “Make me a proselyte so that you may teach me.” That this must be
understood in this way is proved from the added word *didaskein* (διδάσκειν),
which would appear tautological unless *to mathēteuein* (τὸ μαθητευειν) were
referred to something other than simple instruction and teaching.

[Marginalia: 2. From the covenant.]

V. (2) From the covenant. The seals of God’s covenant also pertain to those
to whom the covenant belongs. But it is evident from the covenant clause that
the covenant pertains to infants: “I will be your God and the God of your
offspring after you” (Gen. 17:7); “The promise was made to you and your
children” (Acts 2:39). I grant that the covenant of God primarily and chiefly
regards adults, because every such compact consists in a reciprocal agreement
that must be voluntary on both sides; and it accords with God’s wisdom to
covenant with mature persons who can exercise the operations of the moral
faculties.
But this does not prevent the covenant from also pertaining to infants. (1)
It does so by God’s ordering, because he willed to extend his grace from
parents to children. (2) It does so from the nature of the matter, because
children are part of their parents and have the same condition as they. (3)
Among human beings, compacts embrace the children of those who make them;
therefore, this is also so in the covenant of God. (4) The thing signified—
namely, remission of sins, regeneration, and the kingdom of heaven—pertains
to infants. Therefore, the sign also pertains to them, for if the greater is
communicated to them, on what ground could the lesser be denied them? Thus
Peter argues in Acts 10:47: “Can water be withheld from those to whom the
Spirit has been given?”

VI. It is objected in vain: (1) “It follows from this that the Supper should
also be administered to infants because the thing signified pertains to
them.” For although the thing signified is the same in each sacrament, the
manner of signifying is different. In the Supper Christ’s grace is sealed in
the manner of solid food, of which infants are not capable, but in baptism it
is sealed in the manner of the washing of regeneration, of which infants are
capable (John 3:5). Moreover, the Supper requires a twofold condition: a
preceding condition in self-examination and a concomitant condition in the
commemoration of Christ’s death. Each presupposes the use of reason. Neither
is required in baptism. In the same way, the Passover was formerly
distinguished from circumcision: circumcision was provided to infants, but
the Passover only to adults. (2) It is added: “Infants are not capable of
regenerating grace or of the other benefits of the covenant, which the Spirit
is accustomed to confer through the word.” But it is gratuitously assumed
that they are not capable of the covenant’s benefits. For who denies that
they are capable of the remission of sins, Christ’s redemption, and the other
benefits that depend upon the covenant into which infants are received? Who
doubts that baptism can be for them a distinguishing sign, an introduction
into the visible church, and a seal both of divine truth in the covenant
promises and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 356 | IA leaf 390 | PDF p. 390]

of our obligation to mutual duty? If they are not capable of the obligation
in the present, they can be in the future. As for regeneration, why could
infants not be capable of regeneration just as they are capable of sin,
unless we say that guilt can do more than grace? And since they are rational,
although they do not exercise an act of reason, what prevents them from being
called holy and believing by the Holy Spirit given to them, even though they
cannot yet exercise an act of faith?

[Marginalia: 3. From circumcision.]

VII. (3) By parity of reasoning, the necessity of baptism is the same as that
of circumcision. But circumcision had to be administered to infants by God’s
command. Therefore, infant baptism must be administered as well. The truth of
the major premise is proved: (1) Baptism was substituted for circumcision and
succeeded it in the New Testament. In Colossians 2:12, Paul finds
circumcision in baptism. (2) It is proved from the end of each: circumcision
was the sacrament of initiation by which people were received into the
covenant; baptism is the same. (3) It is proved from the thing signified by
each, which is the internal circumcision of the heart and the grace of
regeneration. (4) God’s grace was not diminished and restricted in the New
Testament, but rather enlarged. Therefore, if childhood is an obstacle to
baptism because of the lack of reason, the same obstacle was present in
circumcision. But if God’s benefits were not offered in vain through
circumcision even then, why should baptism not be judged in the same way?
Nor should it be said either that “the circumcision of infants was expressly
commanded, but infant baptism was not,” because it is one thing to be
contained expressly and another to be contained implicitly in a general
command, as was said above; or that “the argument does not proceed correctly,
because there are various differences between circumcision and baptism.” The
analogy of the two sacraments must be considered not in all things but in the
principal conditions related to the confirmation of the gracious covenant
and declared in the New Testament: namely, that each sacrament was a sign of
the covenant, a symbol of initiation, and a token of profession, by which the
Jews were formerly distinguished from the Gentiles and Christians today are
distinguished from unbelievers. The distinctions commonly brought forward
are of another kind and do not touch the very essence and end of the
sacrament.

[Marginalia: 4. Because infants belong to the kingdom of heaven.]

VIII. (4) The kingdom of heaven pertains to infants according to Christ’s
declaration (Matt. 19:14): “Little children were brought to Christ so that he
might lay his hands on them and pray.” But when the disciples drove them
away, Christ said, “Let the little children come to me, and do not forbid
them, because the kingdom of heaven belongs to such.” This is confirmed by
the word *enankalisasthai* (ἐναγκαλίσασθαι), “to take into his arms”: he
commended them to God with his blessing and prayers. Now if it is right that
infants be brought to Christ, why should they not also be received to baptism,
the symbol of our communion with Christ? Why should the church not receive
into its lap those whom Christ took to his bosom? How unjust we would be if we
drove away those whom he freely admits! Nor should those be heard who wish
“little children” to refer to humility, not age. For the ones meant are those
who were brought to Christ and whom he embraced in his arms. They were called
infants not mystically with respect to humility, but properly with respect to
age. It is true that in Matthew 18:2, when Christ calls a child and places him
in their midst, saying, “Unless you become like this child, you will not enter
the kingdom of God,” he is speaking of spiritual infancy, or humility and
innocence. But the history related in Matthew 19 is different, for it concerns
the bringing of children, as does Luke 18:15. In that place somewhat older
children cannot be meant, because they are called *brephē* (βρέφη). A
*brephos* (βρέφος) is an infant still in the cradle, tender, and usually
nursing (Luke 1:41, 44; 2:12; 1 Pet. 2:2). They are said to have been brought
to Christ, *prosepheron autō* (προσέφερον αὐτῷ), because Christ is said to
have taken them in his arms. Nor is it an obstacle that they are said to
“come,” because this is taken broadly for “approach.” Nor, if it is said only

[1847 vol. III printed p. 357 | IA leaf 391 | PDF p. 391]

that Christ prayed for them and laid his hands on them but did not formally
baptize them, does it follow that this is of no force for their baptism. By
parity of reasoning, if Christ could lay his hands on them, why could they not
also be baptized?

[Marginalia: 5. Because the children of believers are holy.]

IX. (5) The children of believers are holy; therefore, they are to be
baptized. For since they possess the thing signified, they cannot and should
not be deprived of the sign. The apostle says in 1 Corinthians 7:14, “The
unbelieving woman is sanctified in the believing husband; otherwise your
children would be unclean, but now they are holy” (cf. Acts 10:47). They are
called holy not because they are legitimate, as some think, because that was
not under discussion and thus nothing would be attributed to them above the
children of unbelievers, who can also be legitimate. Rather, they are called
holy with a covenantal holiness, that is, Christians and persons belonging to
the church. Thus *akatharton* (ἀκάθαρτον) and *hagion* (ἅγιον) here designate
a Gentile and a Christian, in which sense the word *qedushah* (קדושה) is
often used among the Talmudists. An offspring is said to be begotten “in
holiness” or “outside holiness,” that is, when the parents have become
proselytes or while they are still Gentiles. The *tekna* (τέκνα), children,
of Gentiles were *akatharta* (ἀκάθαρτα), unclean, in their eyes (from Acts
10:28), while those of Jews were *hagia* (ἅγια), holy. Paul alludes to this
meaning, which was very well known to that people.

[Marginalia: 6. Because nothing prevents the baptism of infants.]

X. (6) No impediment can be given why baptism should not be conferred upon
infants. There is none on God’s part, for he does not wish to exclude infants,
but calls them to himself and declares that the kingdom of heaven belongs to
such. There is none on the sacrament’s part, because all the essentials of
baptism can be given to them. There is none on the minister’s part, because
he is no less able to baptize an infant than an adult. There is none on the
recipient’s part, because an infant is a subject capable (*dektikon*,
δεκτικον) of grace. The objections are answered in the sources of solutions.

[Marginalia: 7. From the fathers.]

XI. (7) The fathers acknowledged the necessity of infant baptism and
confirmed its justice by their practice. Justin mentions it in question 56
*To the Orthodox*; Origen, on Romans 6, book 5; Cyprian, book 3, letter 8 *To
Fidus*; Jerome, book 3 *Against the Pelagians*; Cyril, book 8 on Leviticus,
chapter 8; and Augustine often—in book 10 *On the Literal Meaning of Genesis*,
chapter 23; book 2 *Against Pelagius and Cælestius*, *On Original Sin*, chapter
40; and book 1 *On the Merits and Remission of Sins*, chapters 5 and 26. In
the last, he reports that the Pelagians did not dare deny the baptism of
little children because they saw all too plainly that they would have to
fight the whole church over it. The same thing was also confirmed by no few
synodical decrees: Carthage in 407, Milevis in 420, Gerona in 470, and others
that Pamelius notes in his annotations on Cyprian, book 3, letter 8.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XII. There was no need to give a particular command to baptize infants,
because the disciples knew that infants were circumcised. The general command
to baptize all nations was therefore sufficient, since the particular is
contained under it.

XIII. The fact that Christ’s baptism occurred only in his thirtieth year
cannot overthrow infant baptism. Not all of Christ’s deeds that do not pertain
to doctrine are immediately to be drawn into imitation; otherwise baptism
would have to be received only in one’s thirtieth year, which the adversaries
nevertheless do not say. There was a special reason why he waited until this
time, not only because John’s baptism had been instituted only in the
preceding year, but also because of the type of the Levitical priests. Though
they were received into the priestly college in their twenty-fifth year, they
were not admitted to the use and exercise of their office before their
thirtieth. Christ, who did not need baptism, could defer it without danger,
but our case is not the same, since we are conceived and born in sins.

XIV. John admitted no one to baptism unless he confessed his sins, because
the matter concerned adults who were to be baptized and called to the
Christian church;

[1847 vol. III printed p. 358 | IA leaf 392 | PDF p. 392]

but it does not follow that the same thing applies to infants, who are already
in the church.

XV. It is one thing to be a sign whose power depends upon the disposition of
the subject receiving it and another to be a sign whose power depends upon
the promise of its institutor. The former sign is applied in vain to one who
does not understand, but the rationale of the latter, such as baptism and
circumcision, is not the same. Moreover, it is one thing to be a subject
receptive of a sign and another to be perceptive of it as well. Infants can be
the former, even if not the latter. Third, a sign is applied in vain to one
who neither understands nor ever will understand, which cannot be said of an
infant.

XVI. Baptism is called the sacrament of repentance not because it requires
repentance beforehand in everyone baptized, but because it obliges the
baptized to pursue repentance, whether in the present, when they are capable
of it, or in the future.

XVII. Sacraments without the faith of the recipient are called empty
ceremonies with respect to adults, not with respect to infants. In infants,
the promise and grace of the covenant sealed on God’s part suffice for the
salutary use of baptism, even though a response and reciprocal pledge on the
part of the person baptized are not yet given, as is evident in circumcision
(Gen. 17:7).

XVIII. The cause of infant baptism is not the actual faith of infants, of
which they are no more capable than of the instruction by which disciples of
Christ are taught (Matt. 28:19). Rather, it is both the universal command to
baptize all the church’s members and the covenant promise made to parents and
their children alike (Acts 2:38–39; Gen. 7:7 [sic]). The same thing must be
said about the faith of covenant infants as about their reason. Each is in
them in first actuality, not second; in the seed, not the harvest; in the
root, not the fruit; by the internal power of the Spirit, not an external
demonstration of the work.

XIX. An unbeliever is so called either negatively or positively. In the
former sense, one is called an unbeliever who, although he lacks the salutary
habit of active faith, is nevertheless not infected with the contrary habit
of unbelief. In the latter sense, an unbeliever is one who not only lacks true
faith but also suffers from a habit of unbelief, either because he does not
actually believe or because he elicits an act of unbelief. Or the distinction
is between antecedently and objectively, and consequently and terminatively;
between one who lacks an act of faith and one who also lacks its principle.
The infants of believers can be called unbelievers negatively, not
positively; antecedently and objectively according to the reality prior to
grace, but not consequently and terminatively. They can be called such with
respect to the act, because they cannot believe, but not with respect to the
principle and root, because they can possess the Holy Spirit by whom they may
believe at the proper time. But since in common usage unbelief is taken
positively, infants would less correctly be called unbelievers.

XX. Infants cannot be capable of active sanctification or conversion with
respect to the sense and knowledge of the benefit, but they can be capable of
passive regeneration with respect to its principle and the impressing of the
divine image, which ought to exercise itself at the proper time. Although we
cannot grasp how that grace operates in infants, its truth must not therefore
be denied. Likewise, although we cannot perceive how sin can exist in the
soul, it is nevertheless no less true. God’s work is not nonexistent merely
because it does not fall within our comprehension. If infants who are to be
saved bring innate corruption with them from their mother’s womb, they must
be purged of it before being admitted into the kingdom of God, into which
nothing polluted enters. The way is as easy and ready for God as it is
incomprehensible and wonderful to us.

XXI. With respect to true believers, baptism seals saving grace, because it
is joined with the internal baptism of the Spirit. But with respect to
hypocrites, it does not have the Spirit’s grace as its companion and is only
a token of profession by which they are distinguished from Jews, Turks, and
other unbelievers. It is then

[1847 vol. III printed p. 359 | IA leaf 393 | PDF p. 393]

administered not by the power of election, but according to the rationale of
the general dispensation by which God appointed that through the
administration of the word of the gospel and the sacraments he would gather
the church for himself and distinguish it from other profane assemblies by
certain signs and marks. Thus the doctrine of perseverance is in no danger
here, for it remains constant with respect to the elect.

XXII. No one is regenerated without his own act consequently—that is, without
the regenerated person eliciting the act of regeneration; I grant this. No
one is regenerated with his own act antecedently; I deny this, because we are
passive at the beginning of regeneration.

XXIII. Although the custom prevailed among many of the ancients of deferring
baptism until a more advanced age, indeed until death—because they were
deceived by the common error of that time that a life immune from every lapse
was required after baptism and no further expiation afterward remained for
those who sinned—it does not follow that infant baptism was not in use. The
contrary is evident from the church’s continual practice traced from the age
of the apostles. Nor should the practice of certain churches or the sanction
of certain councils, such as canon 7 of the Council of Neocæsarea, which
defines that “the baptism of infants is not only unnecessary but also
unlawful and illegitimate,” be opposed to the practice of all antiquity.

XXIV. It is absurd to wish to twist to infants what Scripture says about the
baptism of adults. It would be as if, when those who do not work are forbidden
to eat (2 Thess. 3:10), infants also were commanded to be killed because they
are no more able to seek their sustenance by working than to seek baptism by
a profession of faith. In the same way, Jewish infants would also have had to
be prohibited from circumcision, since each sacrament is a sign of faith and
repentance.

XXV. Those who proclaim that Christ’s perfect symbols must be given only to
the perfect or to those capable of perfection wrongly restrict the perfection
sacramentally presented in baptism to that one moment, though its power
extends through the course of the whole life and unto death. And they foolishly
seek on a person’s first day the perfection to which baptism invites us by
continual degrees throughout life.

XXVI. When it is said by some that “infants are baptized in the faith of
their parents,” this does not mean that the parents’ faith is imputed to the
infants or accomplishes in infants what it does in adults, both because each
person lives by his own faith and because, if the parents were hypocrites,
the infants would thereby be excluded from salvation. Rather, the parents’
faith serves this purpose: that they bring their children to be baptized from
the faith by which they believe the gospel promises pertain to them and their
children, and then that through this faith they obtain God’s grace for their
children (Mark 7:19 [sic]).
