Objections

Objection I. Whoever wills and decreed from eternity that evil works should
occur is the cause of sin. But God wills and decreed from eternity that evil
works should occur, Genesis 45:8 and 50:19; Acts 2:23. Therefore.

Response I. I deny the major premise. The efficient cause of evil works is not
the one who wills that evils should occur, but the one who does them; God,
however, does not do them.

Response II. As to the passages: God willed the selling of Joseph and the
killing of Christ, but not with the same manner and end as their enemies, as
Genesis 50:20 teaches.

Response III. Although God willed the selling of Joseph and the betrayal of
Christ insofar as they were actions, and insofar as these deeds were means for
executing the decree by which he determined to procure the Church's good, he
nevertheless neither produced nor approved the evil itself in the actions. He
therefore willed and approved those actions insofar as they were actions, with
respect to their outcome, and insofar as they were the means of his decree,
but not with respect to the evil joined to them. Bellarmine, however, responds
badly to this argument in chapter 11 of the book cited. He says that God in no
way willed those evil actions, but only the good sufferings of Joseph and
Christ; that he foresaw those evil actions and willed to permit them; and he
denies that God willed and determined that Judas should betray Christ. Yet God
wills evil actions in the ways stated and governs them: 1. with respect to
their underlying acts; 2. insofar as they serve the execution of God's
justice; 3. insofar as they are punishments of other sins; and 4. insofar as
he directs them to good ends. Augustine therefore rightly says in *City of
God* 14.26, “God is the

[1610 printed p. 37 | Google Books PA37]

helper and rewarder of good wills, but the forsaker and condemner of evil wills, and
the orderer of both.” And in book 5, chapter 9: “He assists good wills, judges
evil ones, and orders them all.” Fulgentius likewise says in book 1 *To
Monimus*: “Although God is not the author of evil actions, he is nevertheless
their orderer, and from anyone's evil work he himself does not cease to work
good.”

Instance. But God wills what devils and the ungodly execute; and what they
execute is sin. Therefore.

Response. He wills the sin, but not in the same manner and respect as they do.

Response II. He wills the actions: 1. their subjects and their outcomes. But
he does not will to produce the evil attached to them. Thus he willed Job to
be afflicted so that Job's faith and patience might be proved; but he neither
willed to produce nor produced the evil in the action of the devil and the
Chaldeans. Respond in this way to 2 Samuel 24:1 and 26:10; 1 Kings 22:20–21;
Isaiah 6:10; John 1:13, 27.

Objection II. God commanded sins, Genesis 22:2; Exodus 3:22 and 12:36.
Therefore.

Response. There was sin neither in the command nor in the obedience. Not in
the command, because God's will is the rule of all truth, and as supreme Lord
he can dispose at his pleasure of the death, life, and goods of individuals.
He commanded Abraham to sacrifice his son, not so that Abraham would sin by
doing so, but so that he would render divine worship and offer a sacrifice,
and so that God would prove his faith and obedience. Nor was there sin in the
obedience, because Abraham ought and willed to obey God's command, not from
superstition or hatred of his son, but from a believing eagerness to obey.
Likewise the Israelites did not act from wicked desire or against God's will,
but from an eagerness to obey God, when they carried away the vessels of the

[1610 printed p. 38 | Google Books PA38]

Egyptians.

Objection III. God works all things, Ephesians 1:11; 1 Corinthians 12:6, and
does so for himself—even the wicked man for the day of evil, Proverbs 16:4;
Romans 9:17. Therefore.

Response. The first two passages speak of the benefits bestowed upon the
Church in Christ, all of which God works, but not of sin. It is therefore a
fallacy from a statement made in a certain respect to one made simply.

The later passages speak of the wicked man, not of wickedness or of the evil
of guilt, which is sin, but of the day of evil, that is, punishment. The same
answer is given to Amos 3:6; Isaiah 45:7; Lamentations 3:38.

Objection IV. God is said to harden and blind, to give people over to a
reprobate mind, to send the efficacy of error, and to turn hearts so that they
do evil, and so forth, Exodus 7, 8, and 9; Deuteronomy 2:30; 1 Samuel 2:25; 1
Kings 12:15; Isaiah 19:14 and 63:3; Jeremiah 20:7; Psalm 105:25; John 12:39,
49; Romans 1:24 and 9:18; 2 Thessalonians 2:11. Therefore.

Response. God hardens and so forth not merely negatively, by bare permission
or desertion, as Bellarmine holds in the book cited, chapter 14; nor merely by
withdrawing his grace, although he sometimes acts in that way so that he may
punish the ungrateful and they may recognize their weakness; nor merely by
setting before them the external objects against which they stumble. He also
works inwardly, namely by sending new thoughts into the hearts of the
ungodly, which they abuse and from which they draw evil conclusions.

Response II. God hardens through means, whether good or evil, so that the
hardening is punishment and therefore good. For he uses every instrument
well. If a rider urges on a lame horse, the principle of the urging comes from
the rider, but the limping from the lame horse. So God uses the means of
hardening well, but the hardness and

[1610 printed p. 39 | Google Books PA39]

blindness come from us.

Response III. He hardens and so forth insofar as hardening, blindness, and so
forth are means of executing divine justice.

Response IV. He is said to incline hearts to evil, not by acting or moving
them toward evil, but by not preventing it. So Thomas, question 3, article 1.

Objection V. Sin occurs either against God's will or by his will. Not the
first, because he is omnipotent; if the second, then God is the author of sin.
Therefore.

Response. Sin occurs because God wills it to occur, but not because he wills
to do it. Nor does he will sin to occur insofar as it is such, but only with
respect to its underlying act, insofar as it is a punishment and a means for
executing the decree of both election and reprobation, and finally with
respect to its end.

Objection VI. Whoever wills the end wills the means. But God wills punishment
and the manifestation of his justice. Therefore.

Response I. God wills sin in the manner stated above.

Response II. Punishment and the manifestation of justice are not properly the
end of either sin or the sinner. One who sins does not sin in order to be
punished, but in order to fulfill his desires or harm others. Punishment and
the manifestation of justice are instead effects or consequences of sin,
which God accidentally draws from sin.

Objection VII. Whoever wills the consequent wills the antecedent. But God
wills the consequent, namely punishment. Therefore.

Response. Distinguish the major premise. Whoever wills the consequent wills
the antecedent in every respect: I deny it. He wills it insofar as it agrees
with his nature: this may stand. But sin as such does not agree with God's
nature. Moreover, whoever wills the antecedent wills the consequent which

[1610 printed p. 40 | Google Books PA40]

follows from it properly and through itself, whereas punishment and the
manifestation of justice follow sin accidentally.

Objection VIII. Whoever can prevent a sin from occurring and does not prevent
it is the author of the sin. But God is such a one. Therefore.

Response. This is true of one who can prevent something and does not prevent
it because he does not hate it, and if he is obligated to prevent it. But God
hates it perfectly and is not obligated to prevent it.

Objection IX. Whoever wills a man to be tempted when he knows that the man
will fall if tempted wills the sin. But God so willed. Therefore.

Response. If he wills this solely to the end that the man should fall, the
major premise is true. But God willed it for other ends, namely to test the
man's obedience.

Response II. He does not will it insofar as it is sin, but insofar as it is a
means of executing the decree of predestination.

Objection X. Whoever withdraws the grace without which sin cannot be avoided
is the author of sin. But God is such a one. Therefore.

Response. Distinguish the major premise: if he withdraws grace already given,
and if he is obligated to give new grace. But God did not withdraw the grace
already given; he allowed man freely to use the grace given, and he was not
obligated to give new grace.

Objection XI. A second cause does not act without the first cause. But our
will is a second cause and the cause of sin. Therefore the first cause,
namely God, is also a cause of sin.

Response. Distinguish the major premise. It is true that the second cause does
not act without the first cause sustaining and governing it, and doing this
well. But in the second cause there is a defect which is not from the first
cause, but is directed by God toward good ends. The second cause is good and
acts well insofar as it is from the first cause and follows it.

[1610 printed p. 41 | Google Books PA41]

Instance. What is the cause of a cause is the cause of what is caused. But God
is the cause of the will which is the cause of sin. Therefore.

Response. The major premise is true if the first and remote cause acts
through itself to produce that effect and not accidentally, and if it intends
that effect or is ordered to it. Otherwise it is not true.

Bellarmine's Objections by Which He Tries to Prove from Our Doctrine That We
Assert God to Be the Author of Sin, from Book 2 *On the Loss of Grace and the
State of Sin*, Chapter 4, Section 6

Objection I. Calvin, *Institutes* 1.17.11, says that the ungodly are driven by
God as though by a bridle to conceive their crimes; and in 1.4.4 he says that
by God's just impulse man does what is not lawful for him. Bellarmine argues
from this as follows: Whoever impels someone to perform a work is the
principal author of the work. But according to Calvin, God is such a one.
Therefore.

Response I. The citation of the passages is false.

Response II. God restrains the ungodly by a bridle so that they can attempt
nothing either without or beyond his will, and he impels them to do what he
wills. Therefore he is the author of the sins of the ungodly: the consequence
is denied. For God does not produce the evil that is in the actions; the
actions themselves are from God, but the evil in the actions is from the
ungodly themselves. God so governs that evil that it serves the execution of
his decree, and he brings good out of evil.

Response III. Calvin teaches from Augustine that in the very betrayal of
Christ, which was a most grievous sin, God was just and man guilty, because
God and man acted in different ways and had different ends set before them.

[1610 printed p. 42 | Google Books PA42]

Instance. But one who uses an instrument is the author of the work which he
does through the instrument. Therefore.

Response. This is true insofar as he uses the instrument. But God uses an evil
instrument for a good end. The evil of the instrument, and what comes from the
instrument, must not be imputed to God.

Instance II. Calvin, *Institutes* 3.23, says that God decreed that man should
sin. Therefore.

Response. Let it be so. He decreed that man's sin should occur, but not that
he himself should commit it. He willed sin to occur because of the good ends
stated above.

Objection II. Calvin says in *Institutes* 2.4.2 that the same deed can be
ascribed to God, Satan, and man. Therefore.

Response. The same deed is ascribed to God, but not insofar as it is a
misdeed, that is, an evil and a crime, but insofar as it is an action. In the
same place he teaches that God, the devil, and man are engaged in the same
action, but in different manners and with different ends. In Job's
affliction, God acted with one end and manner, and the devil and Chaldeans
with another.

Objection III. Calvin and others teach that man could not have avoided sin
but sinned necessarily. Bellarmine therefore argues as follows. No one sins
in what he cannot avoid, because what is necessary is not voluntary, while
every sin is voluntary. But our first parents could not avoid sin. Therefore.

Response. I deny the major premise. Its proof is, moreover, utterly false,
because necessity does not remove an act of the will; compulsion does. With
respect to our own will, we sin freely. See the locus on providence. The
remaining claims Bellarmine brings forward are answered in the same way and
are either false or unworthy of a response.

CHAPTER IV

On the Material Cause of Sin

Thus far we have discussed the efficient cause of sin. Its material cause
follows. Since sin is a privation, it properly has no matter from which it is
made. What most nearly approaches the character of such matter, however, is
the defect of obedience and holiness, or the disobedience in the rational
creature, which the privative particle *a-* in the Apostle's definition,
“sin is *anomia*,” indicates.

The matter in which sin exists, or the subject in which it inheres, is the
nature or action of a rational creature.

The subject occupied by sin is God's law, and therefore the Apostle's
definition is taken from the object.

Bellarmine, book 1 *On the Loss of Grace*, and others therefore err, first,
when they hold that the material element of sin is the substance or action,
but its formal element is departure from the rule. They err for the following
reasons.

Reason I. They confuse the subject with the matter from which something is
made.

II. Sin is a privation, as was proved above. But the subject of a privation
cannot be its matter, just as the eye is not the matter of blindness.

III. If substance or action is the matter of sin, then God will be the author
of sin, because every substance and action is a being and therefore from God.
If, then, sin is an action, God will be the author of sin. Far be it from us.

IV. Positive accidents do not have the subjects in which they inhere as their
matter from which they are made. A wall, for example, is not the matter of its
whiteness. Much less is a subject the matter of a privation.

Others err, secondly, who hold that sin has threefold matter:

[1610 printed p. 44 | Google Books PA44]

namely, a disordered action, the absence of original righteousness, and a
quality contrary to righteousness. A threefold form corresponds to this
matter, and in this positive quality contrary to righteousness there is a
threefold entity. This view is rejected for the following reasons.

I. God either will or will not be the author of this positive quality, which
has three entities in itself and is contrary to righteousness and holiness.
If he is its author, he is the author of sin, because what is contrary to
righteousness is unrighteousness and therefore sin. If God is not its author,
there will be some positive entities which are not from God. But this is
absurd, for it follows that there is some supreme principle of evil, as the
Manichaeans held.

II. If sin is a positive quality, it will be good. The proof is that a
positive quality is a positive being, but being and good are convertible.
Therefore.

Others err, thirdly, who hold that sin is a quality first created by the
devil, for the following reasons.

Reason I. Creation is God's proper action.

II. No men or angels can create even the least being.

Flacius Illyricus and his followers, who held sin to be a substance, err in
the fourth place.

The Flacians' position was this: taken generally, sin is something which
transcends every category; taken specifically, however, some sins belong to
the category of substance and others to other categories. They then held that
original sin is a substance, indeed that it is the substantial image of the
devil, and that after the fall human nature is substantially corrupt. This
position is refuted by the following arguments.

Argument I. Every sin is a privation. Therefore no

[1610 printed p. 45 | Google Books PA45]

sin is substantial.

Argument II. If sin is man's substance, then man's substance is not from God,
or God will be the author of sin. But neither can be said. Not the first,
because man's substance, whether one considers his body or the man himself,
is from God, Genesis 1; Deuteronomy 32:6; Job 10:8; Malachi 2:10. Not the
second, Psalm 5:5 and 45:8; 2 Corinthians 6:14, and so forth, as was proved
above. Therefore.

Sin is indeed from the devil as its first efficient cause and author, but when
he first sinned he did not create a new substance, because creating
substances belongs to God alone.

Argument III. If sin is man's substance, then before the fall man either had
sin or was not a perfect man because something belonging to his substance was
absent. But both are absurd. Therefore.

Argument IV. If sin is a substance, then either Christ did not assume our
nature and substance because he did not assume sin, or if he assumed our
nature, he assumed sin. But he assumed our nature, Hebrews 2:16–17 and 4:15,
and did not assume sin, Hebrews 5:9, for he was the spotless Lamb. Therefore.

Argument V. If Christ came to redeem and save man's nature and to destroy
sin, then sin is not man's substance, but the two differ greatly from each
other. But the first is true, Matthew 1:21; 1 John 1:7 and 3:8. Therefore.

Argument VI. If sin is man's substance, then Christ redeemed sin, because he
redeemed man's substance. But the latter is absurd. Therefore.

Argument VII. If Scripture testifies that it is not sin but man's nature
which is justified and sanctified, then

[1610 printed p. 46 | Google Books PA46]

sin is not man's substance. But the first is true, 1 Corinthians 6:11 and
elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument VIII. If sin were man's substance, then sin would be raised and
glorified on the last day, because man's substance will rise to glory, Job
19:26. But the latter is absurd and contradicts Scripture, Micah 7:10.

Argument IX. Scripture everywhere distinguishes between man's substance and
sin, Isaiah 51:1; Psalm 51:40; Romans 5:12 and Psalm 50:4; 1 John 1:7. Sin is
also said to dwell in us, Romans 7:18, and to surround us, Hebrews 12:1.
Therefore.

Argument X. If sin is a substance, then it is either the body, the soul, the
whole composite, or a part of the composite. But none of these can be said.
Therefore. Proof of the assumption:

For the body, soul, whole composite, and all its parts are of the same
character and species with respect to substance as Adam's body before the
fall and the body of Christ the Mediator, Hebrews 2 and 4. Therefore.

Argument XI. The Fathers—such as Basil, Damascene, and other Greeks, as well
as Augustine, Prosper, Bernard, and other Latins—call sin a privation.
Therefore it is neither a positive quality contrary to holiness nor a
substance.

Objections by Which It Is Proved That Sin Is Something Positive

Objection I. If sin is not a positive being, then it is nothing. If it is
nothing, it does not deserve punishment. But it deserves punishment.
Therefore.

Response. Distinguish the major premise. That it is a negative nothing is
false. It is a privation, just as the destruction of a house is not

[1610 printed p. 47 | Google Books PA47]

a negative nothing, yet is not a positive being.

Objection II. Scripture speaks of sin as a tyrant; in Romans 7 it “works,” and
misers, adulterers, and so forth are said to be carried off to sin. Therefore,
since sin has force, it is something positive.

Response. These and similar things are said by reason of the subject, for the
sinner is carried toward evil by the force of his corrupt will. Moreover,
these and similar things are said by way of likeness.

Objection III. What fights against the law of the mind is something positive,
namely an action. But sin fights against the law of the mind, Romans 7.
Therefore.

Response. No action as an action fights against the law; what fights against
the law is disorder. Moreover, it fights against its contrary in the manner
of a privation.

Objection IV. The commission of murder is a sin and is a positive being,
namely an action. Therefore.

Response. In the commission of murder, the action must be distinguished from
the disorder attached to it. Disorder is a privation.

Objection V. Sin is called corruption. But corruption is a motion into
substance, and motion is a positive being. Therefore.

Response. Corruption is properly not motion but change. Moreover, corruption
is change from being to nonbeing, whereas sin, on the contrary, is change
from a positive quality to its privation. Finally, corruption is neither an
action nor a motion but a privation, for the force of nature must be
distinguished from disorder.

Objection VI. Christ was made sin, 2 Corinthians 5:21. But he was not made a
privative but a positive being. Therefore.

Response. Christ was made sin neither in his essence nor

[1610 printed p. 48 | Google Books PA48]

by assuming a new being or positive quality. He is said to have been made sin
because he was a victim for sin, or because he was numbered with sinners as
though he had been a sinner, on account of the imputation of our sins.

The Flacians' Objections

Objection I. When Scripture speaks of sin, it uses words signifying a
substance, Genesis 3:5 and 8:21; Romans 6:6. Therefore.

Response. Those expressions are not proper but metonymic, for the subject is
put for its adjunct. Moreover, in Romans 6 the body of sin is said to be the
whole multitude of sins which we have contracted by living badly.

Finally, the old man is the likeness of sinful Adam in us. He reigns in the
unregenerate and is the one whom we put off in regeneration; he is not the
substance itself, and he is opposed to the new man, that is, to
righteousness.

Instance. Flesh is a substance, but flesh is sin. Therefore.

Response. This is a fallacy from equivocation. In the major premise “flesh”
is put for a part of the body, but in the minor for a carnal affection.

Instance II. A stony heart is a substance. But a stony heart is sin, Ezekiel
36:26. Therefore.

Response I. This is a fallacy from things conjoined, for it joins things which
must be divided. The heart is a substance, while “stony” signifies the
privation and defect of the obedience which ought to be in the heart.

Response. It is a fallacy from equivocation, because in the major premise the
heart is put for a bodily part, but in the minor it signifies sin in the
heart, or a heart hardened by sin.

[1610 printed p. 49 | Google Books PA49]

Objection II. The fountain of actual sins is original sin. But the heart is
the fountain of actual sins, Romans 7:8; Matthew 15:29. Therefore the heart is
original sin and consequently a substance.

Response. It is a fallacy from what is said in a certain respect to what is
said simply. The heart is the fountain of sin not simply, but by reason of the
disorder inhering in it. Otherwise Christ's heart would have been sin, which
is absurd.

Objection III. Whatever fights against the law is sin. But the heart, indeed
the whole man, fights against the law, Matthew 15:19; John 3:6; Galatians
5:19. Therefore.

Response. It is a fallacy from what is said in a certain respect. Man does not
fight against the law simply, but in a certain respect: 1. by reason of the
vice inhering in him. Accordingly, in the passages cited it is not the
substance but man insofar as he is corrupt that is meant. 2. It is a fallacy
from accident, because the subject is confused with sin itself.

Objection IV. The image of God in man was a substance. Therefore, if the
image of God was lost, the image of the devil which man put on through sin
will be a substance.

Response I. I deny the antecedent. The image was located not so much in
substance as in righteousness, holiness, and dominion.

Response II. I deny the consequence, because the privations of those gifts
followed in their place.

Objection V. What is guilty of death and what the law condemns is sin. But
man's substance is guilty of death, and the law condemns the whole man.

Response. I deny the major premise, because the law condemns not only the
disorder but also the subject because of the disorder. This proposition is
true: what the law condemns is either sin or something defiled by sin.

Response II. It is a fallacy from what is true in a certain respect, for the
law does not condemn man

[1610 printed p. 50 | Google Books PA50]

simply, but because he is a sinner. Likewise, man is said to be guilty of
death not simply, but because he is a sinner.

Objection VI. Through justification and regeneration God creates a new
heart, Psalm 51. Therefore.

Response. It is a fallacy from what is true in a certain respect. A new heart
is created not with respect to its substance, but with respect to the
qualities which take the place of sin and privations.

Objection VII. Man's whole nature is regenerated through baptism; therefore
the whole of it is sin.

Response. I deny the consequence. What follows is that the whole of it is
defiled by sin and guilty before God. Furthermore, regeneration is not a
change of essence but of accidents, as in John 3:5.

Objection VIII. What acts through substantial instruments is a substance.
But sin is such a thing, Romans 6:13. Therefore.

Response. I deny the major premise. Death, wounds, and so forth act through
substantial instruments when they diffuse their poison through the body by
means of the spirits and humors, yet they are not substances.

Response II. Sin properly and through itself does not act; only the subject
in which it exists acts. The action belongs to the members of the body, while
the evil in the action belongs to sin.

Objection IX. What lives and works is a substance. Sin is such a thing,
Romans 7. Therefore.

Response. I deny the minor premise, because sin neither lives nor works
through itself. Rather, it is human nature defiled by sin which, insofar as it
is defiled by sin, lives and works badly.

Objection X. Christ died for sin, but

[1610 printed p. 51 | Google Books PA51]

he died for man; therefore sin is a substance.

Response. There are more than three terms. Moreover, Christ died for sin in
order to expiate, remove, and cover it, and for the sinner in order to free
him from sin. That is, he died for the debt and the debtor, yet the debt and
the debtor are not the same. Finally, if sin is a substance, then as many as
there are different sins, so many will be man's substances.

CHAPTER V

On the Formal Cause of Sin

Since the form of sin is a privation in the sense we have explained, it
properly has no form. The formal element which can be assigned for the sake of
teaching, however, is guilt, or liability to punishment. For the law requires
either full obedience or punishment. Since sin is *anomia*, it is not only a
defect but also an obligation to punishment, and this will belong to sin's
essence. For liability to punishment is not a bare consequence of sin, as
some hold, because sin is not a simple defect but a defect bearing a relation
to punishment on account of the defect. I know that others think otherwise;
let everyone be free to judge what ought to be held.

CHAPTER VI

On the Final Cause of Sin

Sin properly has no end, whether in relation to God or man, because end and
good are convertible, and wherever there is an end, there is perfection.
Accidentally, however, in relation to God, the end is God's glory. For God
orders sin toward a just end for the sake of his glory. It is a means for
executing the decree of election and reprobation, and therefore a means by
which he is glorified in both justice and mercy. In relation to men, however,
there are two consequences: guilt and punishment.
