QUESTION XXVIII.

ON THE STIPENDS OF MINISTERS AND ECCLESIASTICAL GOODS.

Is some stipend owed to ministers of the church? We affirm this against the
Anabaptists.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

I. This question was formerly raised by the Anabaptists, who thought the
gospel ought to be preached without charge either by wealthier men, who have
an abundance of their own on which to live, or even by poorer men, who procure
food and necessities for themselves through some manual trade and their own
labor, after Paul’s example, who practiced tentmaking and labored with his own
hands (1 Cor. 4:12). They judged that Reformed ministers were not true
ministers, but hirelings who undertake the ministry as a form of commerce and
customarily bind and hire out their labor to those offering the highest
stipend. They indeed acknowledge in the Colloquy of Emden, act 78, section 4,
that the church is bound to provide necessities for ministers called by it,
but only in the case where ministers do not have enough to support a family
honorably, whether from an inheritance, commerce, a trade, or some other
calling of this age. Outside that case they wish ministers to be bound to
preach from charity and without charge.

II. The orthodox maintain the following: a minister not only can but also
ought to live from the ministry. In a case of necessity, when he can be
supported neither by the church he serves nor by others, he can indeed furnish
the gospel without expense. Even outside a case of necessity, for weighty
reasons of charity and Christian prudence, he can remit that debt to a
particular church and thus refresh it for a time or permanently by his own
alms. Nevertheless, he is not bound to do so by divine or human right. On the
contrary, the church is absolutely bound to procure and pay a just stipend.

[Marginalia: That a stipend is owed to ministers is proved: 1. From 1
Corinthians 9:4–6.]

III. The reasons are as follows. 1. From Paul’s passage in 1 Corinthians
9:4–19, which is the principal seat of this doctrine. There the apostle not
only claims for himself and all ministers this right to receive an owed
stipend and this power, but confirms it by various arguments from both divine
and natural right and from approved examples. The first is drawn from the
apostles (vv. 5–6): “Do we not have the right, as the other apostles do, to
take along a believing wife? Or do Barnabas and I alone have no right not to
work?” The second is from the analogy of a soldier, farmer, and shepherd, to
whom natural equity and reason dictate that stipends are owed (v. 7): “Who
serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat
of its fruit? Who shepherds a flock and does not drink of the sheep’s milk?”
The third is from the law’s prescription concerning an ox threshing grain,
arguing from the lesser to the greater, because the mouth of an ox threshing
grain must not be muzzled (Deut. 25:4). As God forbids working oxen to be kept
from fodder, how much less should ministers of the church be deprived of the
fruit of their labor? For in this law he has in view not so much oxen as human
beings, lest they be defrauded of the just wage for their work (vv. 9–10): “Is
God concerned about oxen? Are these things not said for us? Because he who
plows ought to plow in hope, etc.” The fourth is from the comparison between
unequal things, work and reward (v. 11): “Those who sow spiritual things ought
to reap material things.” The fifth is from the order divinely instituted in
both the Old and New Testaments

[1847 vol. III printed p. 229 | IA leaf 263 | PDF p. 263]

(v. 13): “Do you not know that those who perform sacred service eat from the
sacrifices, and that those who minister at the altar ought to partake from the
altar? So God commanded that those who preach the gospel should live from the
gospel.”

[Marginalia: 2. From Christ’s command.]

IV. 2. From Christ’s command (Matt. 10:10; Luke 10:7). When he sends the
apostles to preach the gospel in Judea, he forbids them to carry anything on
the journey, not even food, but wishes them to live at the expense of those to
whom they were sent. He gives a most just reason for this: “The worker is
worthy of his food and his wage.” He thereby shows that the things needed to
support themselves and their families are no less justly owed to ministers of
the gospel by their hearers than a wage is justly owed to a hired laborer for
his labor and work. Nor should Christ’s words in verse 8 be opposed: “Freely
you have received; freely give.” It is clear that there he is not treating the
preaching of the word, but the miraculous gifts and powers of healing, for
which he forbids payment to be accepted, much less that they should be sold,
as Simon Magus endeavored to do (Acts 8:18). “Heal the sick,” he says,
“cleanse lepers, raise the dead, cast out demons; freely you have received,
freely give.” The words quoted from verse 10 prove that this cannot be extended
to ministers’ just stipends. For why does he wish the apostles to be supported
and fed by their hearers, because the worker is worthy of his wage, if they
were obliged to preach without charge?

[Marginalia: 3. From the stipends of Old Testament priests.]

V. 3. From the stipends of sacred ministers in the Old Testament (Num.
18:8–12), to whom ordinarily fell the sacrifices, tithes, firstfruits, and
other things of that kind, besides certain cities and their surrounding fields
(Num. 35). Although in the New Testament we are not bound by those laws with
respect to the particular material from which stipends were given and the
manner in which they were given, they nevertheless remain in force with
respect to their general class and analogy, as is clear from the passage
quoted above (1 Cor. 9:13).

[Marginalia: 4. From the example of Christ and the apostles.]

VI. 4. From the example of Christ and the apostles, who did not refuse to
receive from the faithful the necessities for their support. Christ received
their supplies from the women (Luke 8:3), and Paul likewise received from the
Philippians (Phil. 4:10, 15–16). And in 2 Corinthians 11:8 he says that he
received wages from other churches to discharge his ministry to the
Corinthians, so that he could preach the gospel to them without charge. This
was evidently to avert the charge of greed and shameful gain with which the
false apostles attempted to burden him.

[Marginalia: 5. From the threats.]

VII. 5. From the threats which follow the denial of a stipend. Failure to pay
stipends to ministers is numbered among the gravest sins: injustice toward
one’s neighbor and impiety toward God. God threatens punishments for this and
promises his blessing to those who do the opposite (Mal. 3:8, 10, 12; Neh.
13:10–11; Gal. 6:6–7). “God is not mocked,” says the apostle; “whatever a man
sows, that he will reap.”

[Marginalia: 6. From absurdities.]

VIII. 6. From the absurdities and disadvantages which follow. If the
necessity of stipends is denied, it would follow: (1) that in the election of
ministers, fleshly qualities and advantages must be considered, and those who
could support themselves and preach without charge must be preferred. It
would thus often happen that the best and most deserving men would have to be
passed over in an election. (2) There would be a dangerous distinction among
ministers, some of whom would furnish the gospel without expense from charity
and conscience toward God, while others would burden the church because of
their need and thus be regarded as more contemptible, more odious, and less
welcome. (3) Stipends would thereby be changed into alms, and ministers would
be thought to live from alms, which is false, and their condition would be

[1847 vol. III printed p. 230 | IA leaf 264 | PDF p. 264]

rendered contemptible. Hence it was wisely provided in the discipline of the
Reformed churches of France that their own stipends should be assigned to
individual pastors and
that no one should be promoted to office without them, article 42, *On
Pastors*.

[Marginalia: 7. From natural justice.]

IX. 7. From natural justice and equity, whose rule the apostle indicates in 2
Corinthians 8:13–14. It does not permit an advantage to be obtained from
another’s loss or one person to be burdened while the whole community is
relieved. Indeed, equity requires that those who are taught support their
teachers from their own goods (Gal. 6:6; Titus 3:13), and that those who
receive spiritual things from someone at least impart temporal things to him.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

X. It is one thing not to have a right to request and demand a stipend; it is
another to be unwilling to use that right for certain reasons. While the right
remains, its exercise and use can be suspended here and now. Paul could do the
latter, and in fact did so for weighty reasons (Acts 20:33; 2 Thess. 3:8–9).
But he does not make for himself or other ministers a rule which they must
constantly follow, since elsewhere he is recorded as having used his right.

XI. Receiving wages is one thing; the love of money is another (Mic. 3:11).
The priests “who taught for a wage and the prophets who divined for money” are
rebuked. That is, they taught falsehoods in order to extort money from the
wealthier, spoke pleasing things, and promised a happy condition for the
commonwealth so that they might receive more generous stipends. Instead, they
ought to have sharply censured the sins of their hearers and exhorted them to
repentance by threatening punishments. But it cannot be inferred from this
that it is unlawful to accept stipends, because in his law God had instituted
a liberal support for the very priests who are accused of greed.

XII. It is one thing to embrace the ministry for the sake of a stipend and
profit; it is another to demand a stipend and make an agreement about it in
order to discharge the ministry suitably. The former belongs to hirelings,
whose sole business is to seek gain from the gospel. But the latter belongs to
true pastors. Certainly if no wage were owed to them for the work they do, and
if that collection were truly voluntary and gratuitous, as alms are, then it
would not be lawful to negotiate stipends and such a stipulation would be
sordid and simoniacal. But because stipends are most justly owed according to
God’s and Christ’s prescription, it is lawful to negotiate them when they
cannot otherwise be suitably obtained. In this there is no stain, blemish, or
suspicion of a mercenary spirit, provided that they are moderate, not so great
as to burden the flock, and sufficient for the honorable support of the
pastor and his family. The amount of such stipends ought to be established by
the counsel of prudent men and the common consent of the church. But if any,
obstinately and from impiety and ingratitude rather than poverty, deny them,
they are worthy not to have the word of God proclaimed to them and to be
abandoned by their pastors.

XIII. If some ministers can suitably and honorably support themselves and
their families from an inheritance, they must not for that reason be deprived
of the stipends of their ministry. Nor, unless some particular reason exists,
should they refuse the rewards of their labors (1 Cor. 9:18), lest they seem
to hunt after the reputation of some extraordinary holiness, burden their
poorer colleagues with resentment, or induce the people by this exemption to
cherish greed, to the great disadvantage of their successors and danger of
the whole church, which will not always obtain a ministry without expense. But
since each person is master of the stipend acquired by his labor, it is fair
that at his own discretion, not another’s, he should expend whatever he sees
fit upon the poor and other uses.

XIV. These stipends can be paid in various ways: from

[1847 vol. III printed p. 231 | IA leaf 265 | PDF p. 265]

the voluntary offerings of the faithful, which they generously contribute
from their goods for the common use of the church out of Christian charity
and justice, as was done by the first Christians in the time of the apostles
and for some centuries afterward; from mutual agreement and a contribution
collected from each person; from the public treasury, paid by a Christian
magistrate; from tithes; or finally from annual income and revenue gathered
from fields and estates donated and bequeathed to the church and from other
ecclesiastical goods. Whichever of these ways it is done makes little or no
difference to the matter itself, provided that sufficient stipends are paid to
ministers of the gospel and a proper measure is observed in them. They must
not be more meager than is fair and below what is sufficient to support the
pastor and his family honorably, nor so ample that they contribute more to
luxury and pomp than to what is sufficient and necessary. Each extreme is
equally dangerous and must be avoided, lest either ministers be held in
contempt by an ungrateful people because their domestic circumstances are too
straitened, or they sacrifice to and indulge luxury, idleness, and empty pomp
because their circumstances are too rich and splendid, as occurred in the
papacy.

XV. A pastor may be bound in certain cases to serve without charge: if he has
the means, while the church is poor, or newly planted and still tender and
would not receive the gospel unless it were free; or when sectarians do this
and reproach our ministers for their conduct. It does not follow that he does
not possess this right. But if a church is wealthy enough yet refuses from
greed or contempt and disdain for its minister to give him the necessary
stipend, it is lawful for him to leave the church, though only after every
other means has first been attempted, and offer himself for the ministry of
another church. For such resentment against a minister—to say nothing of the
crime—counsels and permits departure.

XVI. If it is asked what must be judged concerning the *gratis* of monks and
especially Jesuits, which is prescribed to them by Ignatius, the founder of
the order, in rule 17, the answer is ready: it is not only gross hypocrisy,
but also a bold and crafty license to scrape up everything. For however much
they pretend to teach without charge, they are nevertheless most ready to
receive choice fields, fertile estates, and immense revenues offered to them
as a free gift or alms. Thus they bestow their labor not *gratis* adverbially,
or “freely,” but *gratis* as a noun—that is, on grateful people who acknowledge
and repay the benefit.

XVII. As for ecclesiastical goods, to say something about their origin and
growth: it is certain that their beginning arose already in Christ’s time
through the pious and devout offerings of the faithful, especially of the
women whose names are commemorated in Luke 8. The administration of this
money was entrusted to Judas. It had to be applied partly to the use of Christ
and the apostles and partly to the use of the poor, as is explained in John
12. After Christ’s ascension, the first Christian church gathered at Jerusalem
brought its resources into a common treasury, partly to testify to charity
and partly so that it might be less vulnerable to the injuries of enemies and
more prepared for migrations and flight, with the wealthier relieving the
need of the poorer even at the cost of their own fields (Acts 2 and 4). Yet
this community of goods was not according to ownership but according to use.
It had no place in churches outside the Jerusalem church and did not last long
even there. Thus individual churches had their own treasuries into which each
person cast a contribution and in which the collections were deposited, as is
clear from the apologies of Justin Martyr and Tertullian.

XVIII. Writers do not agree on the time when the church first began to possess
fields and landed estates. It is certain that churches possessed lands and
estates even before Constantine, and that the Roman church above the others
had been enriched with very ample

[1847 vol. III printed p. 232 | IA leaf 266 | PDF p. 266]

offerings sufficient not only to support clerics and the poor but also to
assist other churches. Yet lavish benefactions, by which the church swelled
immensely as it was enriched, became prevalent only in Constantine’s age. For
this emperor,
out of his zeal for the Christian religion, restored to churches what had
been taken away by his predecessors, allotted to them a portion of taxes,
tributes, and public grain, and offered most splendid gifts made from the
melted gold and silver statues of idols. But as for the donation of the city
of Rome together with the western empire which is attributed to him in
distinction 96, chapter *Constantine*, it has long been observed and proved by
unanswerable arguments to be false and forged by learned men within the papacy
itself: Peter de Vincis, Dante, Marsilius of Padua, Cusanus, Valla, Otto of
Freising, Volaterranus, Nauclerus, and even Æneas Sylvius himself. Therefore,
whatever reality there is in the gift seems to be attributable to Pepin rather
than Constantine, although not even he lavished so many and such great things
as is commonly believed. It is far nearer the truth that, while Italy burned
with civil wars, the popes occupied quite a few towns and territories by force
and fraud and then sought a donation from one person or another.

XIX. As superstition later increased and the people were bewitched by its
goad, they believed there was no crime so atrocious or offense so shameful
that it could not be expiated by gifts of this kind. Thereafter the force of
the overflowing benefactions could be restrained by no embankments. By this,
as by a towering wave, the church was overwhelmed and ruined, and its
spiritual glory degenerated into the splendor and luxury of a profane
monarchy. It was all too truly said: “Religion gave birth to riches, but the
daughter devoured the mother.” Whoever wishes to know more about the origin
and growth of ecclesiastical goods should consult Hospinian’s *On
Ecclesiastical Goods*, Voetius’s *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 4,
volume 2, and the golden Italian treatise of Paolo Sarpi the Venetian, *On
Benefice Matters*.
