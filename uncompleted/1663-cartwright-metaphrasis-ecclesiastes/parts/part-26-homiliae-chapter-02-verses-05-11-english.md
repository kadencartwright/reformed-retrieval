VERSE 5. I made very great works; I built houses for myself and planted
vineyards for myself, etc.

There are many who seize the allurements of pleasure most eagerly but do not
possess the judgment and intelligence to devise those things through which
pleasure is experienced. There are others sufficiently inventive and
ingenious in devising the supports and attendants of pleasure, but because of
their poverty and slender means they cannot obtain the things they have
devised. Solomon, however, surpassed in both respects all mortals who ever
lived or ever will live: he was most fully equipped both with his head, so to
speak, to devise, and with his hand to execute, whatever tended toward the
enjoyment of pleasure. First, therefore, he describes in general what things
he acquired as attendants of pleasure, when he says that he made very great
works, from whose use he might receive the highest and greatest pleasure.
There are indeed some who experience great pleasure from trifles and rather
slight matters, such as the emperor Domitian, who was occupied daily in
catching flies. But Solomon's mind could take delight only in works that were
exceedingly magnificent and august. After stating the matter in general, he
next follows it out part by part.

First he places the construction of buildings. See 1 Kings 7 and 9.

Second, the planting of vineyards, gardens, and orchards. To irrigate these
gardens and orchards he dug pools that would have sufficed for a forest or
woodland, so immense were the plantations.

3. He acquired and bought slaves, male and female servants. This is amplified
by its opposite, although he had home-born servants in his household.

4. Beasts of burden, both flocks and herds—this is amplified by comparison
with lesser men—above all who had ever been at Jerusalem.

5. Gold, precious stones, and silver. See Isaiah 39:2.

6. Male singers, female singers, and every kind of the most exquisite music.

All these things are amplified by comparison with lesser men, because he
surpassed in every respect all who had ever been at Jerusalem. They are next
adorned by Solomon's accompanying wisdom, which had not left him when he did
these things. How great and magnificent they were is recognized from this:
not content with the splendor of his father David in all these things—though
that splendor was royal—he made all these things more august. Next he ascends
again from particulars to generals when he says that he denied his eyes
nothing they desired; here there is a synecdoche of one sense for all. For
just as all his works were delightful to the sight, so most of them affected
the taste by their pleasantness, such as the fruit of the trees; the sense of
smell, such as ointments and spices; and likewise the hearing, such as music.
Here someone may ask, What delight could he take from herds of cattle and
flocks of sheep, and what delight from slaves? As far as slaves are concerned,
the answer is ready at hand: it is well known that eunuchs have always been
counted among luxuries, and no one can fail to know how Ethiopians and dwarfs
are highly prized by princes. There is also in flocks and herds

a certain honorable and gentlemanly delight, and this among the greatest
minds; see 2 Chronicles 26:10. This is especially so since there is no doubt
that both his herds and his flocks consisted of the choicest beasts and were
arranged most orderly in their stalls and folds. The same thing must be
understood in the planting of the gardens, vineyards, and orchards, both with
respect to the temperateness of the locations and air and with respect to
their elegant arrangement. It should be noted that Solomon did not acquire
buffoons among the entertainments of this life, without whom many people
nowadays cannot cheer themselves. See the example of Achish, king of the
Philistines, 1 Samuel 1:21, who will condemn the frivolity and futility of our
people. The fact that Solomon declares that he first built houses and then
planted, and so forth, also seems to bring him into conflict with himself,
since in Proverbs 14:27 he commands as a precept that one should first provide
the things that are outside and in the fields, which may supply the expenses
for the construction of buildings, and only then commands him to build a
house. I answer that one principle applies when usefulness is sought, and
another when pleasure is considered. In Proverbs he gives precepts concerning
usefulness, but here he investigates pleasure; in the exploration of pleasure,
that ought to be first which has the greatest delight in itself. Verse 10. He
says that his mind was cheered by all these things and that he received
pleasure from them all. This is added most appropriately to the point he
undertook to teach, for there are many who, even when everything has been
acquired for pleasure and the desire of the mind, cannot enjoy so much as one
little pleasure: they are either hindered by war, carried off by premature
death, or fall into some grave disease from which they never recover; or,
finally, by a certain dryness and stinginess of mind they deprive their own
nature of its due. If this had happened to Solomon, he would never have become
a competent judge and assessor of what advantage or delight pleasure brings
with it. Therefore he quite appropriately adds that he received pleasure from
all these and other things of that kind. He also gives the reason for this:
because it is his portion and all that he can receive from his labor. By this
very statement he indicates that he had to undergo many labors before he could
come into possession of this pleasure, and that none of these labors would
have borne fruit unless he had received delight from them.

The transcription retains all three printed references, and the translation
renders them as printed in the body while identifying the discrepancies here.
