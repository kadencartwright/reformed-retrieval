CHAPTER THIRTEEN.

ON

The Perseverance of the Saints.

JOHN 10:28.

And I give eternal life to my sheep, and they shall never perish, nor shall
anyone snatch them out of my hand. Verse 29: That Father of mine, who gave them
to me, is greater than all.

From this context it is evident that Christ argues for the indefectible and
certain perseverance of the saints by an argument drawn from his Father's
omnipotence. He who is greater and more powerful than all cannot be robbed of
his sheep, and necessarily is greater and stronger than all who try to snatch
his sheep from his hands. But no one is greater and no one more powerful than
my Father, who gave me the sheep.

There are four better-known positions today concerning the perseverance of
the saints.

The first is that of the more recent Jesuits. They assert that the elect who
are congruently called according to the absolute decree of election can never
fall from grace totally and finally, but that many who have truly been
regenerated and justified can fall from grace finally and totally.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 550 | PDF p. 601]

The second position is that of certain pseudo-Lutherans, who deny the final
apostasy of the saints but admit a total one; that is, they defend a complete
interruption but oppose a final severance.

The third is that of the papists and Arminians, whom we in turn divide into
three classes.

1. The papists generally defend the apostasy of the saints along with their
doctrine of the uncertainty of salvation.

2. Arminius himself and the Hague Collocutors, page 401, say that they do not
call the doctrinal head concerning the perseverance of the saints into doubt,
but merely ask that they be instructed more fully. Nevertheless, they deny
that their opponents' arguments prove conclusively that the regenerate cannot
fall from grace.

3. The disciples of Jacob Arminius—the Remonstrants at the Synod of Dort;
Corvinus against Molina; Tilenus in his *Observations on the Canons of the
Synod of Dort*; and Peter Bertius in *On the Apostasy of the Saints*—teach that
those truly regenerated and justified can fall away finally and totally. They
do so, first, to avoid the Stoicism of a fatal and absolute election, as they
slanderously call it; second, to free God from causality with respect to sin,
as it seems to them; and third, after grace has been overthrown, to raise human
liberty—as a boastful mistress and the independent maker of election and
reprobation, salvation and damnation—to the throne of God.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 551 | PDF p. 602]

The fourth and true position is that those truly regenerated and justified
can neither finally nor totally fall from the state of grace, depart from God,
or perish eternally.

But certain slanders of our opponents must first be wiped away before we fix
the state of the question.

1. Bellarmine, Suárez, and Gregory of Valencia, having cast off all shame,
impute to Wycliffe, Calvin, and Luther the heresy of Jovinian and the Beghards,
namely, that some people are truly rendered incapable of sinning through the
grace of God. We deny this. We teach that the truly regenerate can fall into
horrible and grievous sins and very often incur damnable guilt. Indeed, this
very slander touches those—not us—who defend the article concerning the
possibility of fulfilling the Law through God's grace.

2. The Arminians slander us as teaching that true believers, whatever sins
they commit, are nevertheless bound to believe with absolute persuasion that
they are certain of their final perseverance. We teach a certainty of
persuasion concerning the final perseverance of every believer, but we do not
teach one so absolute and released from every condition of godly concern.
Indeed, we hold that the certainty here is not so absolute that someone
believes he will persevere whether he fears or does not fear, but hypothetical
and joined with the constant exercises of holy fear, diligent faith, and
effective love.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 552 | PDF p. 603]

3. They impute to us an impossibility of the saints' apostasy like the
impossibility of a change in God and in the divine decrees. But here we teach a
hypothetical impossibility of apostasy, given the decree of election and true
regeneration. It is limited and qualified, extrinsic and modal, not intrinsic
and pure. Indeed, as far as we ourselves are concerned, if account is taken of
our instability and God's grace is set aside, we can fall away and perish.

4. They spitefully fasten upon us an inevitable perseverance, as though, if it
is necessary that we persevere, we must be unwilling and resistant while we
are pressed and dragged by such a fated strength and power of God according to
God's immutable decree; and thus, no matter how carnally secure we are, we
would nevertheless persevere by any force and any injury. We reject this
entirely.

Indeed, we teach, first, that the baptized can fall away completely.

Second, we think a distinction must be made between the essence of the elect's
special grace and many things that are in it or accompany it. We reduce these
to the following six: 1. Habit. 2. Acts. 3. A pleasantness in working that
makes the agent's actions easier. 4. A sense of grace. 5. Solid certainty of
one's state. 6. Peace and joy of conscience. From this we teach: 1. That the
habit of grace can be diminished and reduced through graver sins. 2. That
gracious acts can be

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 553 | PDF p. 604 | split word joined: intermitti]

interrupted. 3. That the pleasant facility of acting piously can be lost. 4. That the sense
of special favor and mercy can be lost and all but destroyed. 5. That solid
and firm certainty concerning one's final perseverance can be struck down and
enfeebled. 6. We teach that consolation and peace of conscience, together
with godly security and *παῤῥησία*—confidence or boldness—can fade and wither
for a time.

The question, therefore, concerns: 1. Not infants, but adults. 2. Not
temporary believers, whom they call *πρόσκαιροι*, but those truly regenerated
and justified. Nor, 3, is it asked whether it is absolutely impossible for
believers to fall away, for if they are considered with respect to their
inherent corruption, they can finally fall away.

Hence it is asked whether: 1. Adults, 2. Truly regenerated and justified, 3.
Considered not with respect to their own weakness and corruption, but 4. Given
Christ's intercession, the vital principle of faith, God's immutable covenant,
election, keeping, and power, can fall away in such a manner that they not only
sin grievously in the actual event, but: 1. Lose every right to the kingdom of
God that they once possessed in Christ; 2. Fall from the favor and grace of
gratuitous election; and 3. Be totally and finally removed from and depart
from the state of God's children? The papists, Jesuits, pseudo-Lutherans,
Arminians, and Socinians affirm it. We deny it.
