[Marginalia: The moral law.]

The question therefore concerns only the moral law. Although the word
*moral* has generated disputes in almost every subject to which it is
applied—as is sufficiently evident in the doctrines of the Sabbath,
sacraments, conversion, and law—there is little reason for us to be anxious
about the name, since it is so called because it gives precepts concerning
conduct. But because ceremonial and forensic laws can also be called moral
in this general sense, a sharper controversy is waged among theologians
concerning the formal nature of the thing itself.

1. Some think the morality of the law consists in its binding all people in
every place and time. This is true but perhaps stated too generally: it sets
forth two inseparable attributes, perpetuity and universality, without
expressing the essential formality itself.

2. Others therefore maintain that a moral precept is one known from the light
of nature.

3. Others say it is one agreeable to right reason, to which people, even in
the corrupted state, assent from the law of nature, or of whose equity they
can at least be convinced apart from Scripture. But:

[1679 new-series fol. 20 | IA leaf 340]

1. Since morality is twofold, natural and positive, the law of nature would
not exhaust the whole moral law.

2. Since human reason after the fall is wholly blind concerning most of the
heights of divine law which regard the inward person and the law's
spirituality—as the exceptionally sharp-sighted Paul admits in Romans 7—we
must fear that, while human reason is enlarged beyond what is proper, a
matter about which Melanchthon complained earnestly, the moral law will be
far too severely restricted.

4. Others define something as morally good when it is good antecedently to a
command: it is commanded because it is good, not good consequently merely
because it is commanded. Thus it is first fundamentally good from the will
of decree and only then morally good from the will of command.

Here too it must be considered:

1. Whether ceremonial law would not become moral on this account. Before
every ceremonial precept there was the will of decree by which God willed
in himself that this should be done. At this point it is rationally good
insofar as God willed it. The will of command that we should do it then
followed. According to this account, what would it lack to make it moral?

2. It can also rightly be denied that every moral good is primarily good in
itself and antecedent to a divine command. This is beyond controversy
concerning natural morality, but not concerning that morality called
positive or disciplinary. Those who defend this opinion are consequently
compelled to take refuge in Scotus's distinction between morality belonging
to the law of nature strictly and broadly understood. Yet they could not
readily show how far this broadly understood natural law differs from
positive law, or whether the broadly understood natural moral good contains
within itself some primitively innate good antecedent to command more than
the positive moral good does.

5. Some consider that what is confirmed in the gospel is moral in the law. But
that is more extrinsic; whatever was truly moral in the law already
possessed that character before the gospel was written.

6. Finally, some locate the moral in its agreement with human nature and
maintain that the following things agree with human nature:

1. What flows from a natural relation either between God and a human being,
such as honoring God, Malachi 1:6, or between one human being and another,
such as loving one's wife, Ephesians 6:28, 29.

2. What expresses God's imitable attributes or works by imitation. A human
being was made after the divine image as an exemplar and created for God as his
proper and ultimate end. It is therefore fitting for him to become like God
so far as he can. Thus the precept in 1 Peter 1:15, “Be holy as he who called
you is holy,” is rightly placed among the moral precepts.

3. What natural reason, by either an innate light or one presented from
outside, judges to be good. Since a human being is a rational animal, what
appears right to natural reason is reckoned agreeable to human nature.

Yet corrupted natural reason as it now stands must not be taken as the
rule. Therefore:

4. Something is called agreeable to human nature when it is found to have
been inscribed on the human heart in the primeval state of innocence. The Savior
appeals to this in the matter of marriage, Matthew 19:8: “It was not so from
the beginning.” Thus in a similar matter he shows us

[1679 new-series fol. 21 | IA leaf 341]

the way: the first thing in each kind is the measure of the rest.

These terms circumscribe the moral good, and therefore the law which
commands such things is rightly and properly called the moral law. To say it
as plainly as possible, this is the law contained as if in summary in the
Decalogue.

Let this suffice concerning the subject of the proposition, namely the moral
law which binds.

The second task is to say something about the persons whom our thesis calls
believers under the gospel, not because it exempts the rest.

1. First, I affirm that absolutely all people are bound by this law. Though
in some circumstances it was proper to the Jews, whom Sacred Scripture
therefore pointedly calls “those under law,” 1 Corinthians 9:20, or “those
in the law,” Romans 3:19, in the substance of the matter it also embraces
the Gentiles and every person whatsoever, “so that every mouth may be
stopped, and the whole world become liable to God's condemnation,” as the
apostle says in the same place. Theologians have a sufficiently sharp
controversy about the mode of obligation: whether all people are bound by
this law only insofar as it belongs to the law of nature, or also as it was
delivered by Moses. Suarez and many others vigorously defend the former
position, while Vázquez sharply advocates the latter. Yet all agree
unanimously in the first point: the moral law delivered by Moses, insofar as
it belonged to the law of nature, binds everyone who participates in human
nature. Since God gave it as the author of rational nature, as Vázquez
rightly assumes, whoever withdraws himself from it must put off his nature
and reason at the same time.

2. I say that as all people generally, so unbelievers themselves—especially
if they have lived under either the law or the gospel—are in every way most
fully subject to this law, both its curse and its required obedience. Those
who despise the gospel perish by the law, according to the Savior's words:
“He who does not believe the Son shall not see life, but the wrath of God
remains upon him,” John 3, final verse.

3. Our adversaries think the case far different principally for believers
living under the gospel. Our thesis fights against them and maintains to the
contrary that even believers under the gospel are bound.

I say *believers under the gospel*.

1. I therefore affirm nothing here concerning the holy angels, though Saint
Basil and, among our theologians, Gerhard, Perkins, and others maintain that
they obey this law.

[Marginalia: See Taylor, p. 226; a *Commonplaces*, vol. 3, p. 157; b on
Galatians 4.]

2. Nor is the question about the saints now blessed in heaven. Nevertheless,
several of our theologians place this ark of the covenant in the very holy
of holies and think those heavenly citizens subject to this law. Suarez
likewise proves that this moral law is not merely relatively perpetual, as
the ceremonial law was, but absolutely so. Some cite in support Psalm
119:89, “Forever, O Lord, your Word remains in heaven,” though this perhaps
has less force. The better argument is that these heavenly inhabitants are
creatures and obey their Creator. They therefore keep this law, which, as
Melanchthon thought, binds every rational creature, at least as regards the
summary of the whole law in love for God and neighbor, which they fulfill
exactly.

[Marginalia: * Perkins, in the same place. Our Ursinus in the *Decalogue*, p.
37. Bucanus. It is abolished neither in this life nor in the future. Bucanus.
† *On Law*, book 9, chapter 10, §§ 17, 18.]

Several particular points of this law admittedly have little application to
that state,

[1679 new-series fol. 22 | IA leaf 342]

such as sanctifying the seventh day when they perpetually celebrate the eternal
Sabbath. Nor must they labor to avoid murder, adultery, theft, and similar
things, since they are immortal, equal to the angels, and in need of none of
these outward goods. Yet this does not prevent their being subject to the
law. Adam was subject to law in the state of innocence, although there are
many things to which the law binds us in our present state which he could
not then perform—mercy toward the wretched and vengeance upon evildoers, for
none then existed. This arose not from a defect in the law but from the
incompatibility of the state.

3. We therefore now treat the law as a lamp in this world's darkness and as
that pillar of fire in the wilderness, bearing a torch before every believer
who is still a traveler and making the journey to the heavenly Canaan,
without exception, not even the Libertines' *Macarii*. The error of the Beghards and
Beguines was to exempt their “perfect” people from human laws, yet not even
they dared assert this concerning natural and divine law. The Antinomians of
this and the preceding century dare it. David must be reckoned among the
most perfect and truly blessed; though a king, he acknowledges no less
frequently than humbly that he is subject to this law.

[Marginalia: Vázquez, disputation 166, chapter 1.]

4. The Antinomians customarily object that it is no wonder David and the
rest who lived under the law submitted to it: “Before faith came, we were
kept enclosed under the law, and the law was our pedagogue unto Christ. But
when faith came, we were no longer under a pedagogue. When the fullness of
time came, God sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the law, to redeem
those who were under the law,” as the apostle plainly testifies in Galatians
3:23–25 and 4:4–5. There will be a more suitable place later to discuss the
apostle's passage. Here in explaining our position it is enough to say that
not only believers, but believers under the gospel, in a sound sense remain
under law—that is, under its obligation—even now and forever. Faith does not
dissolve but strengthens the obligation to obey the law; likewise, the
gospel of faith does not remove the law but establishes and perfects it.
Thus every believer under the gospel, with Paul, the most celebrated herald
of both gospel and faith, is “not lawless toward God but under Christ's law,”
1 Corinthians 9:21.

Let this suffice concerning the persons bound.
