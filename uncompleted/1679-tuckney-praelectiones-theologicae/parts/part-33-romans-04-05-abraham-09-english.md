The second question concerns the meaning of the phrase λογίζεσθαι εἰς
δικαιοσύνην: what does Scripture mean when it says that something is
credited to someone as righteousness? We first read that faith was credited
to Abraham as righteousness in Genesis 15:6, as we do here and in many other
places. We also read that Phinehas's deed was reckoned to him as
righteousness in Psalm 106:31; that observing the commandments would be our
righteousness in Deuteronomy 6:25; and, concerning the return of a pledge,
“It shall be your righteousness before the Lord your God,” Deuteronomy
24:13.

To dispatch this as briefly as possible, we must first observe the
following.

The last two testimonies from Deuteronomy say that observing the
commandments and returning a pledge “shall be righteousness before God.” As
to the subject matter, they agree in part with the first two testimonies.
Yet since we are presently treating the apostolic phrase and its meaning,
we need not dwell now on explaining those passages. They do not contain the
Hebrew חשב or החשיב which Moses uses in Genesis 15:6 and to which the Greek
λογίζομαι used by Paul corresponds when he cites Genesis to confirm his
teaching.

Those Deuteronomy passages, I say, do not contain the word for crediting
about which we now ask. Moses did not say that observing the law or
returning the pledge “will be credited to you as righteousness,” as is said
here of faith, but “it shall be righteousness to you.” We shall later see
in what sense this is said of observing the commandments or returning a
pledge. Our present subject is the form of speech in which something is said
to be credited as righteousness.

Regarding this phrase's meaning, I answer:

First, the phrase generally has particular regard to the end and effect:
the person is regarded as righteous and acquitted as a result of this
crediting. Similarly, sin is said to be credited to someone not so that he
may possess it, but so that he may be punished because of it.

Second, righteousness is twofold. There is righteousness of a work, when
the work is righteous in itself; and righteousness of a person, when a
person is constituted righteous through works, a habit of righteousness, or
some other reason. Crediting righteousness is similarly twofold, of a work
and of a person: either an action or a person is regarded as righteous. From
this we say the following.

First, something can be said to be credited as righteousness when the thing
or action credited is principally regarded. By this form of speech we mean
that the thing or action is ratified, reckoned righteous, and accepted. If
it is plainly righteous, it is then more properly said to be righteousness,
as the Deuteronomy passage concerning the return of a pledge says. The
Arabic likewise renders “it shall be righteousness to you” as “and let it
be a good work to you,” Deuteronomy 24:13, as also in Deuteronomy 6:25. But
something is especially said to be reckoned as righteousness, or reckoned
for righteousness, when the thing

[1679 printed p. 216 | IA leaf 224]

so credited or reckoned appears unrighteous at first sight. Phinehas's deed
in transfixing the adulterers in Numbers 25 was liable to accusation under
several names. In order that it might be regarded as free from such an
accusation, it is said to have been credited as righteousness: it was
regarded and pronounced by God as pious and righteous.

Second, when the deed is said to have been credited to Phinehas as
righteousness, the expression does not regard only the work as righteous in
itself. It extends in some degree to his person, so that Phinehas himself is
pronounced righteous with respect to this act, which so pleased God that he
rewarded him in verse 13. Thus the Chaldean renders Deuteronomy 6:25, “it
shall be a reward for us”; and Deuteronomy 24:13, “it shall be righteousness
for a reward,” just as verse 15 says, “it shall be sin”—that is, for
punishment.

This was not, however, a universal crediting of righteousness, nor a first
and universal acceptance and justification of Phinehas's person. He had
already been justified. This is evident because “he was zealous for his
God” in Numbers 25:13, and with “God's zeal” in verse 11, as well as “in the
fear of the Lord,” as Sirach 45:23 says. Psalm 106:30 also says ויפלל: “he
prayed,” as the Chaldean and Syriac render it; “he judged and vindicated,”
as others render it; or ἐξιλάσατο, “he appeased and made atonement,” as the
Vulgate and Septuagint render it and as God himself testifies in Numbers
25:11, 13. All these things prove that he had already been justified with
respect to the universal acceptance of his person.

It was therefore only a particular crediting of righteousness—a
justification not so much of his person as of the deed. Insofar as it
extended to the person, it was the justification of his person with respect
to this deed: he had acted not rashly and unjustly, as it might at first
appear, but righteously. On this account God “gave him the covenant of
peace,” Numbers 25:12. As Hyperius rightly notes, this concerned a temporal
good: the continuation of the priesthood to Phinehas and his posterity.

In sum, the crediting of this work to Phinehas as righteousness means only
that the deed was righteous in itself and that Phinehas was righteous with
respect to it. It thus simultaneously expresses God's pleasure in it and
his intention to reward it as a righteous deed.

Third, therefore, something is said to be credited to someone as
righteousness when not only is the thing credited pronounced righteous, but
the judgment extends to the person, so that the one to whom it is credited
is declared righteous because of it. This concerns not merely a particular
deed—which according to the law's requirement constitutes no one
universally righteous—but the person's whole state, so that he never comes
into judgment, John 5:24, and there is no condemnation for him, Romans 8:1.
Phinehas's one deed was credited to him as righteousness in the first two
senses, but it could not be so credited in this third and final sense.

Under the old covenant of works, only a personal, full, and perpetual
observance of God's commandments can be credited to us as righteousness,
according to Deuteronomy 6:25: “This shall be our righteousness if we
observe and do all these commandments before the Lord our God, as he has
commanded us.” But this way of justification has been utterly closed to us
after the fall by the decree, “Cursed is everyone who does not continue in
all things written in the book of the law to do them,” Galatians 3:10. The
more distant we all are from that standard, the more distant we are from
this justification.

[1679 printed p. 217 | IA leaf 225]

There remains, therefore, one way alone to justify us universally and with
respect to our whole state under the new covenant of grace, in which
something else must be credited to us as righteousness. It is either:

The meritorious and material cause of our justification, namely the
righteousness of our surety Jesus Christ, according to Romans 4:6, where we
read of righteousness—Christ's righteousness—credited apart from works;

Or faith as the receiving and applying instrument. In this sense Moses in
Genesis 15:6 and Paul in Romans 4:3 said that faith was credited to Abraham
as righteousness, as Paul also says of all believers in verse 5.

So much for the second question, in what sense something is said to be
credited to someone as righteousness. Two matters remain: whether and in
what sense Christ's righteousness is credited to us, and in what sense Paul
here says that faith is credited as righteousness to Abraham and to us.
