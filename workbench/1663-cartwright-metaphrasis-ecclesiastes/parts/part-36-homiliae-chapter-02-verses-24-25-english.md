VERSE 24. Since pains and indignation occupied him throughout all his days,
his mind did not rest even at night; this too is vanity.

He further aggravates this evil from its continuous accompanying span of time,
during which a person—for he is not speaking about himself in these verses—
tormented himself with labor, care, and anxiety. This span is indicated by its
parts, namely, day and night. He ought to have borne the loss of his goods more
equably, because, when men passed beyond moderation in laboring, the splendor
of divine justice shone more brightly in dispersing those goods.

For besides the fact that after weightier business had been completed,
something ought to have been given to relaxation of mind, man's labor is
accustomed to close with the day and its light; night was appointed by God for
rest. One should indeed meditate upon the Law by night and by day, but it is
not permitted to be engaged so restlessly in the business of this world. From
this it follows that the more we failed to observe measure and moderation in
accumulating goods, the more we are distressed and tormented by their loss.
Thus it happens that, setting rest before their eyes and expecting it from
wicked and importunate

labor, they are consumed by anguish and torment of mind. Therefore, in order
that we may bear the loss of goods more equably and calmly, let us both join
honorable recreation of mind to sad and grievous labor and by no means pass
beyond the time appointed for labor by God.

In these words he describes the miser, who pricks himself with many pains and
never rests. Rather, while his body sleeps, his mind is distressed and anxious
about many things. The sleep of the godly is sweet and calm, Proverbs 3, but
the mind of the miser wanders here and there. For avarice produces not only
thorns by which piety is choked, but also thorns by which the miser himself is
pricked and wounded. Matthew 13:22; 1 Timothy 6:10.

VERSE 25. There is no good within man's power, that he should eat, drink, and
cause his soul to enjoy good from his labor; I saw that even this is from the
hand of God himself.

VERSE 26. (For who would be going to enjoy it, and who would enjoy it at once
except me?)

VERSE 27. For to the man who shall appear good in his sight, God gives wisdom
and knowledge with gladness; but to the sinner he gives the occupation of
gathering and heaping up, so that he may hand it over to the one who shall
appear good in his sight. Even that is vanity and affliction of spirit.

The sum is that it is not within the power of any mortal to enjoy—much less to
enjoy pleasantly—the goods he possesses.

Here the axiom is considered: it is not within mortals' power to enjoy goods
acquired and possessed by themselves. This is in the first half of verse 25.
The reasons upon which this axiom rests are in the remainder of the context.

He had said above that man's condition is wretched because he is compelled to
transmit to posterity things well acquired by himself, especially while
doubtful whether they will be foolish or wise. He amplifies this misery by the
fact that things justly acquired are often transferred to strangers. Now he
declares the culmination of human misery.

It is not within a mortal's power to enjoy things acquired by himself, not
even while he still possesses them and keeps them safely enclosed and guarded
in his own receptacles. What sort of thing this is must be examined. And it is
indeed evident that the matter stands this way: first, because through disease
and *a bad stomach* (`κακοστομαχίᾳ`) he cannot even taste those things which
have been set before him with great variety and an exquisite selection of
foods, since the stomach—as it is in Job—disdains and abominates every kind of
food. Job 33:19–20.

We are prevented by severe grief of mind from eating those things which are
at hand. Hence it is said in the Psalm that tears were his drink, and ashes or
dust his bread or food. Psalms 107:17–18; 42; 102. Sometimes a person struck
by sudden and unforeseen terror at the height of his eagerness to eat is
compelled, after leaving the table, to provide for himself and his life by
flight. 1 Samuel 30:16–17. At other times he is interrupted by sudden death. 2
Kings 7:2, 17; Luke 12:19–20.

Finally, there are men so greedy and avaricious that they defraud their own
appetite, and are so intent upon their possessions and have so evil an eye
that they deny themselves generous nourishment. More will be said later about
this kind of men, so that it might be evident from this how truly the proverb
says: “Many things intervene between the cup and the top of the lips, between
what is carved and what is served.”

From this it becomes clear how worthwhile it is to pray to God daily that God
give us our bread even when we have every kind of provision gathered and
stored in pantries, granaries, and other repositories. Indeed, it becomes
clear how we should pray not perfunctorily but ardently even when everything
is prepared and set upon the table. He further

amplifies this human inability by the fact that it is not within man's power
to cause himself to enjoy joy and gladness. Suppose, indeed, that he has eaten
and drunk his fill from the things he acquired by labor; nevertheless, it is
not placed within his power for his mind thereby to be suffused with joy.
There is indeed a certain power in bread to strengthen a man's mind, just as
there is a power in wine to cheer it. Psalm 104. Yet that power is not so
attached to these created things that it cannot be separated from them by
God's curse. Therefore in Acts 14:17 Paul distinguishes these things: filling
with food, and supplying gladness to minds from food.

For it happens not rarely that men swallow with a piece of bread a little
needle which is fatal, so that death is in the pot or the cup. When God rained
quails down, the Israelites ate to satiety; nevertheless, while the food was
still being pressed by their teeth, God's wrath rushed upon them and destroyed
and consumed all the strongest of them. Since, therefore, God's kindness and
blessing are necessary for nourishment and the maintenance of the body, they
are much more necessary for refreshing a man's mind. Without that blessing,
all the richest and sweetest things will be reckoned as bread and water of
sorrow.

Thus we see how many things we need for the preservation of this life,
especially in order to live comfortably. First, we need to acquire something
by labor with which to protect this life by food and clothing. Next, we need
to partake of the things acquired. Then, they need to be effective for
preserving us. Finally, we need to be cheered by the use of these supports.

So much for the first part. The second part follows, concerning the reasons
employed to prove the axiom. The first of these is taken from disparates. For
it is placed in the hand or power of God; it is therefore not in the hand and
power of man.

For this reason he reproves the arrogance and insolence of mind in what is
said almost universally by everyone: “Today or tomorrow, and so forth, I will
eat and drink.” What smacks of even greater pride is, “I will eat from this
food and drink from such-and-such wine.” Yet here the same modesty and
moderation must be employed which James prescribes in chapter 4, verses
13–17.

But if someone rises up here and says, “This is always understood by
everyone,” I answer that this is hardly credible unless we sometimes attest
it in words. Although, moreover, when called to account they have ready the
answer that the qualification which was not at all expressed is understood,
it often happens that when we utter these things, forgetfulness of this
inability of ours seizes us, so that neither our own inability nor divine
power comes to our mind even in a dream. For the Christians with whom James
was dealing were by no means ignorant either of their own inability or of
divine power, as is clearly established from verse 17.

This is also confirmed by the fact that when cheered, men pour themselves out
into every kind of luxury and ignoble joking. They would not do this if they
were touched by a serious awareness of God's gratuitous gift. Furthermore,
since suffusing minds with joy from food and drink is something greater and
more divine than eating and drinking, we nevertheless transfer this honor in
some measure to ourselves. The saying that we will cheer ourselves is repeated
in almost everyone's speech; and when a host bids us to compose ourselves and
our minds for gladness, we credit that same host with the fact that we are
glad.

When he says that even this is from the hand of God, it is as if he were
saying that although this may be ever so small and may seem to be placed
entirely within man's choice and power, it is nevertheless not within his
power, but depends upon God's providence and administration. Thus, in order
that we may partake of the Lord's Supper and be held worthy and suitable
fellow guests for sharing the Lord's bread and wine, we need God's singular
protection. How little some seek this protection can be established from the
fact that as the Supper approaches, they assail one another with insults and
challenge one another to single combat.

By saying that he had seen or experienced this, he thereby teaches that he
confirmed by use

and experience those things which he learned from the Word, turning his eyes
and mind here and there so that by diligent attention to the things which
happen in the life of men he might increase and strengthen his faith. If this
were performed by us, it is difficult to say how much fruit we would carry
away from it for the increase of faith.

1. The initial lemma block gives verses 25–27, but the exposition completed in
   this unit treats the axiom in verse 25. This unit therefore ends at the
   next repeated lemma, `VERS. 26`, on printed p. 145.
2. The edition prints the Greek `κακοστομαχίᾳ`, the dative of
   `κακοστομαχία`, literally a “bad condition of the stomach.” The English
   gives the contextual gloss “a bad stomach,” while the Latin retains the
   full Greek script and accentuation.
3. In the initial and repeated verse-26 lemmata the edition prints
   `confestim`, “at once” or “immediately.” The English renders that printed
   wording as “at once” rather than silently substituting the comparative
   sense found in some forms of the biblical verse.
4. The sentence beginning `Atque æquius ferre oportuerit` shifts from the
   impersonal singular construction to the plural participle `transeuntes`.
   The English makes the contextual plural subject explicit; the Latin
   preserves the construction as printed. The subsequent sentence likewise
   shifts from first-person plural `nos` to an implicit third-person plural
   subject in `conficiantur`, which the English retains.

   intelligi, in promptu sit respondere` is highly compressed. The English
   supplies “they have ready the answer” to express its contextual force
