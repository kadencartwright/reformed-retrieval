Verse 24. Do not be a friend to a master of anger! Nor approach a man of hot passions.

Verse 25. Lest you learn his paths, and receive a snare for your soul.

In הבעל אף (*master of anger*) and איש חמות (*man of hot passions*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In מוקש (*snare*) there is a metaphor from bird-catchers, who hold birds entangled in snares.

The purpose is to warn us, in choosing friends, to guard against an irascible man because of the danger to this present life and the life to come.

In forming friendships we must exercise careful choice, since a friend, if he is treacherous and deceitful, can do us very great harm; but if he is true and faithful, he provides a great consolation in life. Concerning this, a command is given in general, 2 Chronicles 19:2, that we should not enter into [friendship] with the wicked, or have any dealings with them, much less bind ourselves to them by a close relationship. Here, however, we are commanded more specifically not to cultivate friendship with an irascible man. From these things it follows that friendship must not be formed rashly and suddenly. For we must have thoroughly observed and tested people's disposition and character before we write them in the roll of our friends. The common saying fittingly warns us that we must eat several measures of salt with the person whom we are about to admit into the fellowship of friendship.

But perhaps someone will object that Jonathan is thereby charged with rashness, since almost at first sight he embraced David with the greatest love and friendship, 1 Samuel 18:1. But we must consider that, through that wonderful and plainly divine victory over Goliath, and through the wise speech which David had previously made before Saul, Jonathan had discerned David's piety no less clearly than if they had lived together for many years.

The precept is strengthened first by a comparison from the lesser. We must not associate with an irascible man, or keep frequent company with him; much less must we be bound to him by the bond of friendship. For friends must meet both to cherish and strengthen their friendship and to enjoy its sweet fruits. From this we gather that we should admit into the covenant of friendship those with whom, because of the proximity of our dwellings, we can conveniently maintain intercourse.

Another reason is drawn from the attendant danger of losing our patience and falling into the sin of irascibility. The proverb is common in ordinary speech that one who lives beside a lame man is apt to limp; and Solomon teaches, Proverbs 13:20, that one who associates with the wise will become wise, but one who makes himself the companion of fools will be made wicked by the contagion of their vices. We find this true in every other association, but especially in the closer union of friendship. For since in friendship, because of intense affection, there is a willing and an unwilling of the same things, it follows that we desire to be as like our friends as possible. If anyone here objects that through this companionship the irascible man can be brought to a gentle and peaceable disposition just as readily as the other can fall into irascibility, the answer is ready: through the corruption of our nature we are driven headlong into vices, but toward virtue we make slow and tardy progress.

He adds another argument from the attendant loss of this life. For although friends are chosen to provide assistance, yet an irascible man, as soon as he is seized by fury, despises the obligations of friendship, breaks its bond, and lays violent hands upon his friend. Then, even if that friend escapes his hands, he himself, made irascible by evil imitation, will provoke others whom he has wrongfully assailed, to his own destruction.

### Translator's note

The 1632 witness prints *inimicitiam* (enmity) in the sentence citing 2 Chronicles 19:2. The surrounding argument prohibits intimate association with the wicked and immediately speaks of cultivating *amicitiam* (friendship) with an irascible man. The English therefore supplies “[friendship]” as the probable intended reading; the paired Latin retains *inimicitiam* without silent correction.
