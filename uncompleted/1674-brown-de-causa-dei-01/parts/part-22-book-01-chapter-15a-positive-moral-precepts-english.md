CHAPTER XV

Can any positive precept be called moral?

Dr. Burmann’s doctrine on this matter is examined.

In order that we may know what answer can be given to this question, we must
explain the nature of positive law.

Above, in chapter 4, we distinguished between divine natural and positive law.
We said that everything belongs to divine positive law of which it cannot be
said that God was bound by the necessity of his nature to one of the two sides
of a contradiction—that is, those things which are so constituted by a free
determination of God’s will that they could either not have been constituted
at all or at least have been constituted differently, if that had seemed good
to God. It is therefore clear that certain laws founded upon this divine
positive law, and thus positive in this sense, must be called moral, because
many things which pertain not only to the fourth but also to the fifth, sixth,
seventh, eighth, and tenth precepts of the Decalogue are referred to this law.

We also said above that some distinguish between *natural morality* and
*positive morality*. By the latter they understand a law contained in the
Scriptures which even corrupt nature cannot deny to be good and just, and thus
fitted to bind perpetually according to the lawgiver’s good pleasure, although
before the lawgiver’s will was revealed it could not judge that law to be in
every respect necessary of itself. If this distinction is admitted, it is
certain that there are some precepts of this kind which can be called
positive, because they command things whose absolute necessity, before God’s
will concerning them was declared, is not apparent to nature now corrupted.
Natural precepts, by contrast, are those which are just and necessary according
to the testimony and judgment even of natural light now corrupted. And they
must be called moral because they bind universally and perpetually, as we will
prove more fully later.

There are others, I acknowledge, who explain both moral and positive law a
little differently, so that they are distinguished in opposition to each
other. Thus moral law, as such, cannot be called positive, nor can positive law,
insofar as it is such, be called moral. Nevertheless, they concede that one
and the same law can be called both moral and positive in different respects.
For, they say, a divine law can be founded upon and have reference to something
which is moral, and yet require some positive precept to be superadded for the
due observance of the thing commanded. They assert that the moral ground of
the thing commanded, which arises from its natural relation to God and from
the order of the universe, can sometimes scarcely be perceived without an
express positive precept. Consequently, without this superadded precept the
thing commanded, although antecedently necessary by force of the law of
creation, cannot be duly performed. In this sense, they say that a law can be
both positive and moral, because both grounds, the moral and the positive,
though distinct and separable in themselves, here concur and bind to one and
the same observance—an observance which is composite or mixed.

But because our adversaries attach little importance to how we explain these
terms unless we concede together with them that a moral and a natural precept
are the same, and unless we

[1674 logical printed p. 197 | IA leaf 215 | PDF p. 215]

explain a natural precept as commanding only those things which flow
necessarily from the very nature of God and of the human being, and therefore
bind even before they are commanded and are accordingly known from nature—and
thus say that whatever is positive cannot in any respect become moral—it is in
our interest first to bring their doctrine to the anvil and then to confirm
the truth by arguments.

Thus Dr. Burmann expresses himself in his *Apology*, chapter 1, section 10 and
following, page 9 and following:

“Whatever lies outside the scope of those moral precepts which flow from
natural divine and human law comes under the name of positive precepts.”

Reply: As we saw above, by these moral precepts he meant, strictly speaking,
nothing other than the precept concerning love of God and neighbor, because
he expressly said on page 8: “Strictly speaking, nothing is morally good or a
virtue except love of God and neighbor.” He attempted to establish this by
many arguments, as we have seen. Consequently, he expressly excludes all
external actions and every other internal action except these two. It follows
that he does not wish commands which enjoin such actions to be moral, properly
speaking, but only concomitantly, as he puts it. Thus these commands will be
either moral-positive or purely positive. And if they are purely positive, the
Decalogue will consist for the most part of purely positive precepts. But let
us admit his less exact manner of speaking and the explanation of moral
precepts which he proposed at the beginning of the chapter and briefly
presents to us again here. When, for the sake of explanation, he adds the
following remarks, he does not make the matter any clearer to us. For he
speaks as follows:

“What is properly positive, as the word itself indicates, is that which is
laid down by choice beyond the nature of the thing. Consequently it cannot be
confounded with moral precepts without the gravest inconvenience and the
greatest absurdity. For moral precepts flow from the very nature of God and of
the human being even before they are commanded, and accordingly they are known
from nature. Positive precepts possess neither characteristic. Moral precepts
are connected with the image of God and can be deduced from it, whether
immediately, as the precepts of the first table, or mediately and upon the
supposition of the human being and the order of things, as the precepts of the
second table, although some of them are deduced more clearly and others more
obscurely, according as they are nearer to or more remote from first
principles. From the ideas implanted in us by God we can elicit the duties
which by nature we owe to God and human beings. For every right and duty is
founded upon reason and elicited from it, since it is nothing other than what
we owe to another by reason. But this cannot take place in any positive
precept, which can never be deduced or known from natural reason but depends
upon and is known from the lawgiver’s will alone. Nevertheless, once it is
known, if it is equitable, it can easily be recognized as not contrary to
reason.”

Reply: I discern an enormous difference between these statements and those
made above. Here he wishes only those precepts to be moral which flow from the
very nature of God and the human being, or which can be deduced from the image
of God, either immediately, as those of the first table, or mediately and upon
the supposition of the human being and the order of things, as the precepts of
the second table, or which we can elicit from the ideas implanted in us by
God. Above, however, he said that those things were moral which flow from the
image of God, whether as it was created together with the human being before
the fall or as it was restored by grace after the fall. He therefore wished
more things to be moral there than here, for here there is no mention of the
image of God restored by grace after the fall. What he understands by this
image, and what he wishes to reduce to the category of moral things by reason
of it, he himself had taught earlier in the discussion on page 7, where he
offered the following explanation of what is moral:

[1674 logical printed p. 198 | IA leaf 216 | PDF p. 216 | ends before Audiamus primùm rationes]

“Since that is properly moral which flows from the image of God, whether as it
was created together with the first human being before the fall—of this kind
are all the precepts concerning love of God and neighbor—or as it was restored
in the human being after sin through God’s revelation and the covenant of
grace—of this kind are (note well) the precepts concerning faith in Christ,
repentance, and the like, in which all morality is comprised, both that which
belongs to the natural human being and that which belongs to the Christian.”

Here we see that precepts other than those which flow from the attributes and
nature of God and the human being, are known from nature, and can be elicited
from the ideas implanted in us are moral. For I hope he will not say that faith
in Christ, evangelical repentance, and the like flow either from God’s nature
or from human nature, or that these duties are known from nature and elicited
from the ideas implanted in us. He must acknowledge that they flow instead
from God’s revelation and the covenant of grace—that is, that they pertain to
and flow from the image of God restored through that revelation and covenant
of grace. Yet, to say this in passing, I do not adequately understand how faith
in Christ, repentance, and things of this kind can be said to flow from the
restored image. For on this account, as it appears, no one would be bound to
believe in Christ and repent except one in whom that image has been restored,
that is, one who already believes and repents.

It is therefore evident that Dr. Burmann is not sufficiently consistent with
himself in this matter and leaves us in doubt about what we should understand
by *moral*, and consequently by *positive*.

Furthermore, there is another difference to be observed here. He previously
said that what does not so flow from the attributes of God and the human being
as to be known of itself and deducible from them is not moral, even if it is
fitting for God and the human being once God has prescribed it. Here, however,
he denies that something is moral if it can never be deduced or known from
natural reason, although, once it is known, it can easily be recognized, if it
is equitable, as not contrary to reason. He thus appears to suppose that some
things which are not equitable can be prescribed by God. I do not adequately
understand what else the words “if it is equitable” can signify. But the
difference I wished to note is this: previously he said that things which do
not flow from the attributes of God and the human being are not moral, and so
forth, “even if they are fitting for God and the human being.” Here, however,
he says, “although they are not contrary to reason.” What then? Are all things
which seem contrary to our corrupt reason unfitting for God? Can we not say
that many things are not contrary to reason which nevertheless cannot be
called fitting for God and the human being? This positive expression signifies
something very different from the former negative one.

But leaving these matters aside, we see what he understands by a positive
precept: namely, everything which does not flow from the very nature of God
and the human being even before it is commanded; which is neither immediately
nor mediately deduced from or connected with the image of God upon the
supposition of the human being and the order of things; which we cannot elicit
from the ideas implanted in us; which can never be deduced or known from
natural reason; but which is laid down by choice beyond the nature of the
thing, and which depends upon and is known from the lawgiver’s will alone.
Accordingly, he wishes no such precept to be moral in any respect, for this is
how he speaks in section 11, as we will see.
