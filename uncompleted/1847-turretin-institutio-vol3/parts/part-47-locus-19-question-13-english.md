QUESTION XIII.

Is baptism absolutely necessary for salvation? We deny against the papists.

I. Just as the Socinians err by deficiency concerning the necessity of
baptism, so the papists err by excess in contending for the absolute necessity
of baptism. They maintain that its necessity is such and so great that all who
are deprived of baptism are excluded from the kingdom of heaven, even if this
happened through no fault of their own and no contempt of the divine command.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the papists.]

II. The Council of Trent explains its position in Session 7, Canon 5, in these
words: “If anyone says that baptism is optional, that is, not necessary for
salvation, let him be anathema.” Because this is expressed more generally, lest
anyone suppose that only the necessity of the divine command against the
Socinians is meant, the Tridentine Catechism discloses the meaning of the
anathema, which is ambiguous in itself: “Nothing can appear more necessary
than that people be taught that the Lord prescribed the law of baptism for all
human beings, so that unless they are reborn to God through the grace of
baptism, they are brought forth by their parents—whether those parents are
believers or unbelievers—to everlasting misery and destruction.” Hence the
scholastics, such as Suárez and others, contend that it is a conclusion of
faith that baptism is a means necessary for the remission of sins and for
obtaining grace and glory. And as one error is born from another, the dogma of
the necessity of baptism gave birth to the fiction of the limbo of infants,
into which they contend that infants who die without baptism descend. It must
nevertheless be acknowledged that others are not so strict but admit certain
exceptions: first, that this necessity had a place only after Christ’s death
and from the day of Pentecost; and then, that baptism with water can be
supplied by desire or by martyrdom. Hence Canon 4 of the council, on the
sacraments, pronounces anathema on those “who say that people receive the
grace of justification through faith alone, without the sacraments or the
desire for them.” Thomas, on John 3, says that “baptism is necessary either in
itself, in desire, or in figure.” Lombard agrees with him in book 4 of the
*Sentences*, distinction 4, letter D: “It is certain,” he says, “that some are
justified and saved without baptism.” But the more common opinion, according
to the intention of the Tridentine Catechism, favors absolute necessity.

[Marginalia: And the opinion of the orthodox.]

III. Our opinion, however, is that by divine institution baptism is indeed
necessary as an external means of salvation through which God is efficacious
in its lawful use, so that the one who despises it is guilty of a serious
crime and incurs eternal punishment. But we do not believe it so absolutely
necessary that someone deprived of it through no fault of his own must
immediately be excluded from the kingdom of heaven, or that salvation cannot
be obtained without it.

IV. Here a twofold necessity must be distinguished. One is absolute and
simple from the nature of the thing, which is called a necessity of means,
without which the end cannot be obtained;

[1847 vol. III printed p. 331 | IA leaf 365 | PDF p. 365]

the other is qualified, from God’s ordering, which is called a necessity of
command because it is founded wholly upon God’s command, not upon the thing
itself. Again, some means are internal and have in themselves a necessary
connection with the end, so that the end cannot be obtained without them;
such are faith, repentance, justification, and regeneration with respect to
salvation. Other means are external and are not connected with the end in
themselves, but serve it only by God’s ordering, yet not with such necessity
that the end cannot sometimes be obtained without them. Such are the
sacraments. They are therefore rightly said not to be absolutely necessary as
though they possessed the necessity of an internal means, strictly so called,
but necessary only in a qualified sense by virtue of the divine command and
his institution.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

V. Hence it is clear that the question does not concern every kind of
necessity of command, which we also do not deny but defend against the
Socinians. It concerns the absolute necessity of a means. The question is not
whether contempt for baptism condemns, which we do not deny, but whether mere
privation incurs liability to eternal punishment and exclusion from the
kingdom of heaven. Our adversaries affirm this; we deny it.

[Marginalia: It is proved that baptism is not absolutely necessary. 1. Because
many are saved without it, and many perish with it.]

VI. First, because not a few are saved without baptism and more perish with
baptism; therefore, it is not absolutely necessary for salvation. This is
clear from the converted thief, who was saved without baptism, and from
various martyrs converted to Christ in the very heat of persecution, who,
enlightened by Christ’s power alone, did not even have any knowledge of the
sacraments. To say that baptism was supplied by desire or martyrdom is to
abandon their hypothesis and not acknowledge the absolute necessity of
baptism. Likewise, no one can deny that many baptized people also perish,
such as Simon Magus and all the hypocrites and reprobates in the visible
church.

[Marginalia: 2. Because God’s grace is not bound to signs.]

VII. Second, if the absolute necessity of baptism were granted, God’s grace
would be bound to the sacraments and God would not be a supremely free agent.
Both God’s nature teaches that this is false and Scripture testifies to it more
than once, and we proved it above.

[Marginalia: 3. Because the necessity of faith and that of baptism are
unequal.]

VIII. Third, the necessity of baptism for salvation is not equal to that of
faith. Without faith no one can be saved at all, but this is not equally true
without baptism. This is clear from Mark 16:16: “He who believes and is
baptized will be saved, but he who does not believe will be condemned.” Why
does he not say, “He who does not believe and has not been baptized,” but only,
“he who does not believe,” unless it is because the relations of baptism and
faith to the business of salvation are different? Without faith there is no
salvation but most certain destruction. It is not equally so without baptism,
for without baptism, as has already been said, provided that contempt is
absent, all who are endowed with faith, such as adults, or who at least are in
the covenant, such as infants, can be saved.

[Marginalia: 4. Because circumcision was not absolutely necessary.]

IX. Fourth, circumcision was not absolutely necessary; therefore baptism is
not either. The condition of Christians should not be harsher than that of the
Jews, since Christians live under a milder economy. That circumcision was not
absolutely necessary is clear because the Israelites lacked circumcision
during the forty years they were in the wilderness, yet those who died without
it should not be thought to have perished. Circumcision was likewise not to be
performed before the eighth day, and it cannot be doubted that many died
before then. Nor should it be replied that the uncircumcised were to be cut off
according to God’s prescription (Gen. 17:14), because this is said of contempt,
not involuntary privation. The passage concerns an adult who despises his
circumcision, since it concerns one who violates the covenant, not an infant
who is deprived of baptism through no fault of his own.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 332 | IA leaf 366 | PDF p. 366]

[Marginalia: 5. Because the thing signified pertains to infants.]

X. Fifth, those to whom the thing signified by baptism—namely, God’s
promises—pertains before baptism cannot be excluded from the kingdom of heaven
because they are deprived of the sign. But the promises pertain to the infants
of believers according to Acts 2:39. Therefore they cannot be excluded.

[Marginalia: 6. Because otherwise a son would bear his father’s iniquity.]

XI. Sixth, if a son were condemned because he was deprived of baptism through
no fault of his own, he would bear the personal iniquity of his parent or of
the person through whose fault he was deprived of baptism, contrary to Ezekiel
18:20.

[Marginalia: 7. Because its necessity comes only from a command.]

XII. Seventh, the whole necessity of the sacrament of baptism comes from a
positive divine command, not a natural one. Therefore, its absence is fatal
only to those who transgress that command. But children, to whom the divine
institution is unknown and who by nature are incapable of actual obedience or
transgression, since that requires the use of reason and free will, cannot be
said to transgress a positive command.

XIII. Eighth, if baptism were equally necessary for salvation, the certainty
of election and salvation would depend on the will of a midwife, a Jew, a
pagan, or a priest. If the midwife chooses to confer baptism upon a dying
little child, he will be saved; if not, the child will perish eternally.

XIV. Ninth, if no one has grace before baptism, no one is loved before
baptism. But the love of God precedes not only birth (according to Rom. 9:11)
but also all time, because it is from eternity. And if no one whom God loves
can perish, infants whom God loved from eternity and from the womb cannot be
condemned, even if they die without baptism. Again, if baptism is absolutely
necessary for salvation, no one can be sanctified before baptism, because
holiness is necessarily connected with salvation. But many are recorded as
sanctified from the womb, and Paul says of the children of Christians that
they are holy (1 Cor. 7:14). Now if they are holy from birth, who will say that
they lose holiness through death? Or who will believe that those destined for
eternal destruction are called holy from birth?

XV. Tenth, not a few papists approach our position here. In his sermon on the
nativity of the Virgin Mary, consideration 2, Gerson denies “that God has so
bound grace to the sacraments that, without prejudice to his own law, he
cannot inwardly sanctify children not yet born outside the womb.” This was also
the opinion of Biel, Cajetan, Soto, Cassander, and others, whom Vega, *On
Justification*, book 5, chapter 16, therefore “denounces as excessively
merciful toward infants.” In book 4 of the *Sentences*, distinction 4, letter
D, Lombard defends his opinion with Augustine’s testimony that “God’s grace is
fulfilled invisibly where not contempt for religion but a constraint of
necessity excluded the mystery.” He concludes, “It is therefore certain that
some are justified and saved without baptism.”

XVI. More could be brought here from the fathers. Tertullian, *On Baptism*,
chapter 12, says: “Your faith has saved you, and your sins are forgiven you,
surely as one who believes and yet has not been baptized.” Cyprian teaches the
same in his epistle to Jubaianus. Particularly notable is the example of
Emperor Valentinian. Although many presbyters were present by whom he could
have been baptized whenever he pleased, he nevertheless died without baptism,
yet Ambrose says in his oration on Valentinian’s death that he was saved. Two
customs received in the ancient church pertain here and demonstrate that the
ancients did not believe in the absolute necessity of baptism. The first was
not to baptize solemnly on days other than the Sabbaths immediately before
Easter and Pentecost. We have various conciliar decrees concerning this that
are cited in distinction 4, *On Consecration*. This law would have been cruel
and impious if no one could be saved without the water of baptism. The second
was to defer baptism until old age or the point of death,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 333 | IA leaf 367 | PDF p. 367]

in order to avoid the rigor of the penitential canons to which the baptized
were subject, or for other reasons. This openly indicates that they did not
believe that no one is saved without baptism; otherwise they would have
hastened baptism for fear of sudden death.

XVII. It must nevertheless be acknowledged that not a few doctors of the
church pressed this necessity more than was right. Ambrose seems to have been
the first among them, for he denied that eternal life was open without the
saving washing, excepting only martyrs and those who died with a desire for
baptism, whom, as he says, “their faith” and the blood shed for Christ “wash,
and their request consecrates.” Augustine followed him and excluded unbaptized
infants from salvation, for which he was called a harsh father of infants. He
was driven to this by the occasion of the Pelagian controversy. Hence, as
usually happens, that error easily spread on the authority of so great a name,
especially in the Latin church, which received Augustine above all as its
doctor and professes to follow him, although it departs from him more than
once. This is clear even in the case of little children, to whom Augustine
wanted the Eucharist administered, which is rejected by the papists.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. It is granted, with respect to the commands of natural law, that what
has the necessity of obedience has the necessity of means; but it is denied
with respect to commands of positive law, such as baptism. Next, it is granted
that what is commanded as an absolutely necessary means, without which the end
or salvation is not attained, has a necessity of means. But what is commanded
as an external and qualifiedly necessary means does not have the necessity of
means strictly so called. Nor, if the end of baptism—namely, the remission of
sins—is absolutely necessary for salvation, does it immediately follow that
baptism also is absolutely necessary. The inference holds only for an internal
means without which the end does not exist, not for an external means taken
broadly, without which it can exist.

XIX. The passage in John 3:5, where Christ says that “no one can enter the
kingdom of God unless he has been born of water and the Spirit,” does not
support the absolute necessity of baptism. Water cannot designate baptism
with water but spiritual renewal, which is effected by the Holy Spirit. Thus
“water and spirit” means a “spiritual washing” or “spiritual water,” or the
Spirit washing and cleansing hearts, so that the cause of this regeneration,
namely the Spirit, and the mode of its effect, namely through renewal or
spiritual washing, may be denoted. This is through a figure very commonly used
by secular and sacred writers, which the Greeks call *hen dia dyoin* (ἓν διὰ
δυοῖν), when of two nouns one must be explained adjectivally. Thus “in the
region and shadow of death” (Matt. 4:6 [sic]) means “in the region of the shadow of
death”; the priest of Jupiter brought “bulls and garlands” (Acts 14:13), that
is, “garlanded bulls”; and “the lake of fire and sulfur” (Rev. 20:10) stands
for a lake of sulfurous fire. And, most pertinent to the present matter, John
the Baptist says that “Christ will baptize with Spirit and fire” (Matt. 3:11),
that is, with a fiery Spirit, or one who sets hearts aflame, as Toledo on this
passage and Stella on Luke 3 observed. Or the particle *kai* (καὶ) can be
taken not disjunctively but explanatorily, so that the sense is “water, which
is the Spirit,” as Scripture customarily explains what is obscure by what is
clearer, and what is tropical and figurative by what is proper. The basis of
this is that the Holy Spirit is frequently designated by water, especially as
he was to be dispensed under the New Testament (Isa. 43:19; Ezek. 36:25, 27;
Zech. 13:1; John 4). Nor can this therefore be called a tautological expression
in which one and the same thing is said twice. Although it is one and the same
with respect to the thing, it is not so with respect to the mode of the thing,
because either the Spirit is designated there by his operation, or the more
obscure term is explained by the clearer, as in Matthew 3:11. Augustine says
in *On the Unity of the Church against Petilian*, chapter 10: “Scripture does
not always intend the visible sacrament of baptism to be understood when it
names water; sometimes it intends that sacrament, and sometimes something
else.”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 334 | IA leaf 368 | PDF p. 368]

Moreover, many considerations prove that baptism with water cannot be meant
here. (1) Christ’s purpose toward Nicodemus, who as a Pharisee was swollen with
an opinion of his own righteousness and measured the worship of God by carnal
ceremonies and washings, was to remove the pride and superstition by which he
was bewitched. Christ taught him that he could not be saved unless he were
wholly renewed and declared that the true means of obtaining salvation was
nothing other than spiritual or supernatural grace. This belongs not to an
external element, from which Christ wished rather to call Nicodemus away, but
to the Holy Spirit alone. This is clear from verse 3, where only birth *from
above* (ἄνωθεν) is mentioned, and from verses 6 and 7, where Christ likewise
mentions only the Holy Spirit and “from above” (τὸ ἄνωθεν) is explained by “to
be born of the Spirit.” It is confirmed by verse 8, where this new birth is
compared with the blowing of the wind. This comparison is not suited to
designate bodily washing, because it concerns a hidden operation not bound to
an external element, but is most suitable to express the secret power of the
Holy Spirit breathing upon hearts. (2) It is not likely that Christ, when
instructing Nicodemus in the rudiments of religion, wished to begin with the
sacrament of baptism, which he had not yet instituted. (3) Christ would not
have accused Nicodemus of ignorance that would not have been culpable if he
had been speaking about baptism, since it had not yet been instituted. (4)
Christ would be treating baptism as presently binding, though according to our
adversaries it did not begin to be necessary until after Christ’s death. (5)
It would follow that the Spirit and water have the same necessity, and thus
that no one can be saved without baptism. The papists themselves deny this,
since they maintain that baptism can be supplied either by desire or by
martyrdom.

XX. The papists themselves, who contend for absolute necessity, are compelled
to attach various exceptions to this statement of Christ, and they labor
wonderfully in explaining this passage. Lombard, book 4, distinction 4,
chapter 2: “This must be understood of those who can be baptized and despise
it.” Bonaventure, on the same distinction: “It is understood of one who has
the opportunity and is not prevented by necessity.” Barradas explains it on
this passage as follows: “Unless someone is born of water and the Spirit—in
actual fact if he is able, but if he is not able, in desire.” Villagudus on
this passage: “It is not necessary in itself, so that if water is lacking, the
one who wishes to be baptized must perish.” Salmerón, tract 4, volume 8,
expressly restricts the law: “Only,” he says, “those who have learned the
necessity of baptism from preaching, and who are not prevented from receiving
it by want of time but have failed to receive it through either negligence or
contempt, are constrained by this law under penalty of sin to receive
baptism.” Hence it is clear, even by their own admission, that there are
different degrees of necessity corresponding to the twofold reality of the
sacrament. For as is the efficacy and contribution of the thing toward the
end, so also is its necessity. Although the same attribute is commonly
predicated of two subjects, it must nevertheless be explained differently
according to the nature and mode of each. The necessity of the Spirit is
indeed absolute and simple, applies to all times and all human beings, whether
adults or infants, and allows no exception. The necessity of water is only
qualified, applies to certain persons who are able to receive it, and can be
supplied either by desire or by martyrdom. Now if they admit that it can be
supplied in various cases among adults, and thus that adults can be excepted
from this necessity, why should a harsher law be imposed upon infants than
upon adults, so that because they can neither vow, desire, nor request baptism,
they perish absolutely from its absence, which an adult can supply by his own
desire and vow? Why should Christ’s statement not rather be limited with
respect to tender infancy than with respect to adults, who are so much less
worthy of any indulgence because they have added many actual sins to original
corruption, which are not found in infants?

XXI. If the heat of debate and confidence in a good cause carried Augustine,
when disputing against the Pelagians for the truth of original sin and the
necessity of regeneration and of baptism to be conferred even upon infants

[1847 vol. III printed p. 335 | IA leaf 369 | PDF p. 369]

for the remission of original sin, to the point that in various places he
spoke too incautiously about the necessity of baptism, assigning to eternal
death infants who had not been baptized or taken from this life by martyrdom,
elsewhere, nevertheless, he speaks more mildly in *On Baptism against the
Donatists*, book 4, chapter 22: “Faith and conversion of heart suffice if,
perhaps because of pressure of time, there is no opportunity to come to one’s
aid by celebrating the mystery of baptism, when not contempt for religion but
a constraint of necessity excludes the mystery of baptism.” He adds in
chapter 25: “Conversion of heart can indeed be present when baptism has not
been received, but it cannot be present when baptism has been despised.”

XXII. It is not absolutely true that every means God uses to justify and save
us is simply necessary for salvation. The Eucharist is a means God uses to
sanctify and save us, yet many are saved without it. The same can be said of
the church’s miracles and persecutions, which God often uses as aids to
salvation. The same is true of baptism with water. The claim therefore holds
only for an internal means, which is such by the nature of the thing, not for
an external and sacramental means, which exists by command, as has been said.
The passages brought from Ephesians 5:26 and Titus 3:5 concern not so much the
external washing of water as the internal washing of the Spirit. This can be
inferred even from the fact that Paul locates it in the word, not in the
element of water.

XXIII. Although we deny the absolute necessity of baptism and do not believe
that God’s supremely free grace is bound to any external signs, or think that
mere deprivation of this sacrament excludes a person from the kingdom of
heaven when someone is deprived of baptism through some unavoidable necessity
and a lawful intervening impediment—whether the absence of a lawful minister,
the illness of an infant, or some other cause—which ought to console those who
cannot obtain this seal of God’s grace and token of Christianity either for
themselves or for their children, nevertheless we hold that diligent care must
be taken lest, falling into the other extreme, we neglect this sacrament,
which God willed to be an efficacious symbol and means of his grace whenever
it can be used. We therefore judge those guilty of a serious crime who without
any necessity delay the administration and reception of so saving a sacrament,
whether in themselves or in others committed to their care, whenever the
opportunity to obtain it is present. This cannot be done without violating the
divine command and despising the grace God is accustomed to dispense through
such a symbol, and it is beyond doubt that this contempt brings a great
punishment upon those who despise it.
