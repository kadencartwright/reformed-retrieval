Here we must also examine that claim so often repeated by our adversary, which
is taken from Ezekiel 20:11, 12, 25, and so forth, where he would have the
Prophet distinguish between those precepts “which, if a person does them, he
will live in them” and others which are called “not good”; and

[1674 logical printed p. 212 | IA leaf 230 | PDF p. 230]

he would have these stand so far apart from one another that they can in no way
be joined together. Let us hear his own words.

On page 24 he thus presses the point: “Thus Ezekiel 20:11–12 distinguishes
between precepts which, if anyone does them, he will live by them—that is,
precepts which are the life (that is, the righteousness) of a person, for that
is his true spiritual life flowing from love for God; hence Stephen calls them
λόγια ζῶντα, living oracles, in Acts 7:38—and precepts that were not good, in
which a person will not live, verse 25.” Reply: 1. Isaiah and Jeremiah long ago
taught that Christ is our righteousness (Isaiah 45:24–25; Jeremiah 23:6 and,
according to some, 33:16), and the whole Gospel proclaims it (1 Corinthians
1:30). 2. Stephen indeed called those precepts λόγια ζῶντα, living oracles, not
λόγια ζωοποιοῦντα, life-giving oracles, as Piscator rightly notes. To interpret
ζῶντα as ζωοποιοῦντα, as at John 6:51, is, he says, foreign to the nature of the
law, which is the subject here—at least principally. Calvin indeed takes a
different view, because the law is a perfect rule for living piously and
holily and displays the righteousness of God. Yet what he adds must be noted:
“For,” he says, “he is not speaking of bare precepts, but embraces the whole
doctrine of Moses, in which gracious promises, and therefore Christ himself,
the sole life and salvation of human beings, are included.” Let what follows
also be read. It is accordingly evident that even things which are positive
are included among these living oracles. But perhaps he will say that by these
oracles Beza understands only the precepts which God himself had pronounced,
written with his finger, and handed down to Moses. True; but among these was
also the fourth precept concerning the Sabbath, which he wishes to be merely
positive. Consequently, this testimony does nothing for our adversary.

But how does he prove that no positive precepts can be included among those
“which, if a person does them, he will live in them”? “That,” he says, “is
inconsistent with the words and sequence of Ezekiel’s text. For in verse 11
Ezekiel speaks of the precepts which God made known to the people immediately
after he had led them into the wilderness in verse 10. Yet Jeremiah explicitly
protests that God said nothing about offering sacrifices when he led them out
of Egypt (Jeremiah 7:22). He therefore speaks not of sacrifices and dogmas but
of precepts which, if a person does them, he will live in them—that is, what we
call moral precepts.” Reply: This reasoning is weak. For although Jeremiah says
that God did not speak about offering sacrifices when he led them out of
Egypt, he does not say that God said nothing about all dogmas, or about all
those things which the adversary wishes to be merely positive. For he had
spoken of the Decalogue, whose fourth precept this man wishes to be positive.
Further, Ezekiel says that God gave them not only precepts but statutes, which,
as we showed above, denote something other than moral precepts. Third, Ezekiel
rather includes all the precepts which he had given them after leading them
into the wilderness. Moreover, if the restriction is made only to the things
which he made known to the people immediately after leading them into the
wilderness, the Sabbath precept was not excluded; indeed, it is expressly
mentioned. If, therefore, the “precepts that were not good,” from which the
precepts “which, if a person does them, he will live in them” are
distinguished, are dogmas, the Sabbath precept must not be numbered among the
dogmas. Let the adversary now choose which he prefers.

Later, on page 29, he says that he was horrified when he saw it stated that
things freely commanded by sovereign power can be numbered among the precepts
“which, if a person does them, he will live in them.” Let us see the reasons
for this horror.

For, first, he says: “What is openly contradicting Ezekiel by a direct
antithesis—or God himself speaking there—if this is not? God says in Ezekiel
20:25, ‘I had given them statutes that were not good and judgments by which
they would not live.’ We saw

[1674 logical printed p. 213 | IA leaf 231 | PDF p. 231]

above that these statutes are nothing other than decrees. Otherwise, let our
author show what they are. For since an explicit legislation is expressed
here—‘I gave them statutes and judgments,’ as he had spoken throughout the
chapter—let him show statutes ‘that were not good’ other than decrees. They are
called ‘not good’ because they are useless, ἀνωφελῆ, for righteousness and
life. God says that they would not live by them; our author openly says that
life can be obtained through things that are of God’s free decision, on page
36. What contradiction could be greater?” Reply: 1. This entire contradiction
lies within his own brain. For he imagines that this, and no other, can be the
meaning of the words which he sets forth, and thus imagines that those who
contradict him also contradict the Holy Spirit. We shall soon see whether he
has proved by any sound arguments that “statutes that were not good” must mean
dogmas. 2. Otherwise, he asks, what are they? The great Junius told him. But
this opinion displeases him because explicit legislation is expressed here—as
though a word were never used metaphorically in Holy Scripture. 3. Scripture
never says simply that the ceremonies were useless for righteousness and life
with respect to God’s intention, or insofar as they were rightly employed
according to their institution, especially while they remained in force. 4.
Will he say that only the moral precepts are useful to us for righteousness and
life? Then justification and life by works of the law will be granted, which
the Antichristians also say. 5. Let us grant that “statutes that were not good”
means dogmas, and that they are distinguished from the things which the Prophet
had named before. Will he number the Sabbath among these dogmas? Then he will
plainly be guilty of the charge he levels against us, for he himself
contradicts the text. Throughout the entire chapter the Prophet numbers the
Sabbaths, although separately, with those precepts “which, if a person does
them, he will live in them,” and never among the precepts that were not good.
Indeed, in verses 24–25 he clearly distinguishes them and sets them in
opposition to each other, saying, “because they did not perform my judgments,
but despised my statutes and profaned my Sabbaths—therefore I also had given
them statutes that were not good,” and so forth. Thus the statutes which he
says in verse 25 that he had given them are distinguished from those mentioned
in verse 24, which they had not performed but had despised and profaned. By
what right, then, can he number those Sabbaths among these statutes that were
not good?

2. He says: “On this hypothesis Paul cannot attribute τὸ ἀσθενὲς καὶ τὸ
ἀνωφελὲς, weakness and uselessness, to certain precepts (Hebrews 7:18 and
13:9), nor call them ἀσθενῆ καὶ πτωχὰ στοιχεῖα, weak and beggarly elements
(Galatians 4:9), if
righteousness and life are found in their observance.” Reply: In Hebrews 7 the
Apostle speaks of the Levitical priesthood and the ceremonies as considered
apart from Christ, without whom they certainly were weak and useless, just as
our moral duties are. This holds unless the covenant of works is to be
restored from exile as the way in which salvation is to be expected, which I
do not think the adversary will do. “This passage is especially notable
against,” says Beza on the passage, “those who give less to the sacraments,
whether old or new, than is proper, who have maintained that they were certain
ceremonies devised to distinguish the outward society of believers; and those
who attribute to these sacred signs some other power than that of the meaning
divinely established for sacred and heavenly use, transferring to the signs
what belongs to the thing signified—not only foolishly but even impiously and
with the most manifest idolatry—and, once the true sacramental effect is
destroyed (which is the general sealing of Christ alone and of all his heavenly
benefits), accommodating to the Holy Spirit, by whose power alone it is
effected in us, the very thing called *opus operatum* and another thing of its
kind.” The Belgian Annotations say that those ceremonies were useless for
sanctifying us spiritually, as the Jews sought sanctity and perfection in
their use;

[1674 logical printed p. 214 | IA leaf 232 | PDF p. 232]

yet they add that they were useful to upright Jews, in order that through them
they might be led to Christ and his sanctification, which was a great benefit.
2. In Galatians 4:9 they are called “weak and beggarly elements” with respect
to the false opinion of the Galatians, who thought that they conferred the
grace of God, as Piscator says on the passage. And the Belgian Annotations say
that they are so called with respect to our justification, in which respect
moral things are also weak. “But,” Calvin says on the passage, “he calls the
ceremonies beggarly elements because he considers them apart from Christ,
indeed as opposed to Christ. For to the fathers they were not only beneficial
exercises and aids of piety, but also effective instruments of grace; but their
entire power lay in Christ and God’s institution.” 3. Here the Apostle is
expressly speaking about typical ceremonies. And even if we granted that they
cannot be numbered among those precepts which, if a person does them, he will
live in them, nevertheless other positive precepts can be given which must be
referred here, as is evident.

3. He says: “From this all the precepts will plainly be confused with the
precepts of the covenant of works, whose characteristic proposition is, ‘The
person who does them will live in them’ (Romans 10:5; Galatians 3:13).
Therefore the covenant of works will be placed in them.” Reply: 1. When a
person expects life through these things considered in themselves, to what
covenant, I ask, must they be referred? To the covenant of grace? He will not
say so. 2. When someone had transgressed those precepts and had not repented,
by what law was he to be punished? Was it not by that which threatens
transgressors with death? 3. Since the fall, we now have no business with the
law of works in regard to justification and salvation, whether by moral or by
other works. 4. Among the precepts of the covenant of works was that positive
command to abstain from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. 5. Was
Adam not bound by the covenant of works to fulfill all the precepts of God
which would later be given? Or could God not have added new precepts day by
day?

4. He says: “Then the opinion of the Jews—that they had righteousness in the
precepts (namely, all the Mosaic ones, as we said above they believed) and
lived in them—would have been right. That opinion is utterly false and
injurious to God, and the cause of the Jews’ unbelief and rejection.” Reply: 1.
The Jews also erred in thinking that they would have righteousness in the moral
precepts, because in this way they neglected Christ, in whom alone they could
obtain righteousness. And this opinion of theirs was utterly false, injurious
to God, and the cause of their unbelief and rejection. Let him answer, then,
and he will teach us what we ought to answer.

5. “Scripture refutes this opinion,” he says, “when it says that such precepts
are only dogmas; that God does not delight in them, does not rebuke people
concerning them or demand them in the covenant, and that they are of no
benefit.” Reply: Where, I ask, does Scripture say that all positive precepts
are only dogmas? 2. God is indeed said not to delight in them comparatively,
or when they are considered in abstraction, or insofar as the Jews abused
them. But we showed above that God otherwise delights in them, rebukes people
concerning them, demands them, and that they too are beneficial when rightly
used.

6. He says: “If a law had been given which could give life, then righteousness
would truly have been from the law (Galatians 3:21). But the dogmas are
precepts which, if a person does them, he will live in them, and those who did
them lived in them. Therefore righteousness is given from the law; therefore
they were justified from the law.” Reply: Turn the tables and apply these
things to the moral law, and you will see what follows. Is righteousness not
from the moral law? The Apostle proves quite the contrary in the same Epistle
to the Galatians, in the Epistle to the Romans, and frequently elsewhere.

[1674 logical printed p. 215 | IA leaf 233 | PDF p. 233]

7. “Then,” he says, “the Savior badly directed the young man, in order that he
might enter life, only to precepts of love and not also to decrees; for Jesus
said to him, ‘Which?’ and then, ‘You shall not kill,’ Matthew 22:16 and
following.” (He should have said Matthew 19:18 and following.) Reply: This
argument will fight no less against the precepts of piety than against dogmas,
for, as the text makes clear, he did not show those to this young man either.
But does Dr. Burmann believe that our Lord showed the young man those precepts
so that through obedience to them he might be justified before God and finally
enter life? I do not think so.

8. He says: “And how would a person live by decrees—for example, by washing,
being circumcised, sacrificing, eating, and drinking—in which no love of God
is involved in itself and there is no imitation of God? Is a person like God
through these things? Is his spiritual life found in them, much less his
eternal life?” Reply: A person would live by washing and so forth, for as long
as those commands remained, in the same way that he would live by not killing,
not committing fornication, not stealing, and so forth. In these things, no
more than in those, love for God is involved in itself; through them a person
also becomes like God; and in the former, as in the latter, consists the
spiritual life of a person.

9. He says: “The vision of God, or life, is attributed to sanctity in Hebrews
12:14. But the sanctity, ἁγιασμός, that is found in observing dogmas, which
effect only εἰς καθαρότητα τῆς σαρκός, the purification of the flesh—how,
then, would life be found in obedience to them?” Reply: The sanctity in Hebrews
12:14 consists in conformity of every kind with the divine precepts, since it
is perfect obedience, whatever the precepts may be, whether natural or
positive. In observing dogmas in the manner prescribed by God there was true
inward sanctity, because they led to Christ, who has been made sanctification
for us (1 Corinthians 1:30). Yet the observation of dogmas considered in
itself and without reference to Christ effected only εἰς καθαρότητα τῆς
σαρκός, the purification of the flesh. Hence in obedience of that kind there
was no life, because it was not the obedience required and commanded.

10. He says: “Theologians distinguish between the necessity of precept and the
necessity of means. With respect to salvation, moral works possess not only
the necessity of precept but also that of means—not indeed a means which
effects salvation, but at least one through which a person passes. To positive
precepts is attributed the necessity of precept. But on this opinion all
positive precepts will also possess the necessity of means, since life is
truly found in obedience to them. Thus the Israelites were commanded to hope
for life from deeds. And all ritual things will be moral and means of
salvation. So also, on this hypothesis, someone has said that baptism saves as
a means. Yet it saves not as a means but as an ἐπερώτημα—that is, an instructive
sign and a seal on whose strength we may question God about the salvation
covenanted through Christ and given to us in faith. How dangerous these things
are is plain.” Reply: I acknowledge that theologians commonly distinguish in
this way between the necessity of precept and that of means. But I do not
remember ever reading that all precepts which flow from God’s free good
pleasure must be numbered among those having only the necessity of precept.
Nor has it been proved that only things having the necessity of means are to
be called moral, whether the word is taken broadly or strictly. For God, who
by his free good pleasure establishes the end, can by the same free good
pleasure establish the means to that end. 2. The usual sense of that
distinction is not as though there were no life in obedience to positive
precepts, since Scripture says that life consists in observing God’s precepts,
faith, and discipline (Proverbs 4:4; 7:2; Ezekiel 18:9, 17, 19, 21, 22).
Otherwise, how would they be called necessary? Rather, it means that some have
a certain very close connection or fitness with the end while others do not;
the latter,

[1674 logical printed p. 216 | IA leaf 234 | PDF p. 234]

however, because of God’s command, are not to be neglected under penalty of
divine wrath. 3. The Israelites were equally commanded to hope for life from
obedience to these and to the other precepts. Christ was their life. 4. Why
would not all ritual things prescribed by God be means of salvation? For what
other end, I ask, were they instituted by God? Nevertheless, we do not say
that all means are of the same nature, proximity, and necessity. 5. When
baptism saves as an ἐπερώτημα, as an instructive sign and seal, and so forth,
it saves as a divinely instituted means, namely, when it is rightly used
according to the prescribed manner.

These are his arguments by which he attempted to show it absurd that certain
positive precepts could be numbered among those “which, if a person does them,
he will live in them.” Since we have seen that they do not establish the
conclusion, it is evident that the passage in Ezekiel 20 does nothing against
us. This is further clear because some understand by the precepts “that were
not good” in verse 25 plagues sent by God and judgments, both bodily and
spiritual, by which God punished their iniquity and rebellion. The
circumstances of the text clearly prove this. “Because they did not perform my
judgments,” the Lord says, “but rejected my statutes and profaned my Sabbaths,
and so forth, for this reason”—as Münster translates it—“I also gave them
statutes that were not good,” and so forth. And in the following verse: “I
also polluted them in their gifts, when they made every firstborn pass through
the fire, that I might devastate them,” and so forth. Further, who does not see
how foreign to right reason it is to say that in wrath, and in order to punish
the Israelites’ disobedience, God prescribed to them ceremonies which led to
Christ, possessed an evangelical relation, and pertained to them precisely
insofar as they were the Church of God? What? Are these not to be numbered
among their privileges (Romans 11:4)? Were they not signal blessings of God?
See Nehemiah 9:13–14. Moreover, those statutes that were not good were given
after the Jews had despised God’s statutes and profaned his Sabbaths. But,
according to the adversaries, some ceremonies were instituted before the
institution of the Sabbath itself, such as circumcision and Passover, which
were no less positive precepts than others ordained afterward. And if these
precepts that were not good are restricted to those commanded after the entry
into the wilderness, Sabbath observance must not be numbered among them, for
God says that because of the violation of the Sabbath he gave them these
precepts that were not good. And if they are limited to ceremonies instituted
after their encampment in the wilderness of Sin in Exodus 16, how many
Sabbaths, I ask, did they have before the institution of certain ceremonies at
Mount Sinai in Exodus 20, 21, and so forth? Scarcely more than two, as will
appear to one who considers the thread of the history. For they came to the
wilderness of Sin on the fifteenth day of the second month, Exodus 21, and at
the beginning of the third month they came to Mount Sinai, Exodus 19:1. And
they camped for some time in the wilderness of Sin before the first Sabbath
(if indeed it was the first), as will appear to one who reads the text.
Finally, the text of Ezekiel itself increases the difficulty. For these
statutes that were not good were given not only after those whom God had led
out of Egypt despised God’s statutes and violated his Sabbaths, but also after
God had said that he would pour out his fury upon them in the wilderness
itself to consume them (verse 13), which we do not read of as having happened
before Exodus 32, before which time very many ceremonies had been instituted.
Indeed, these judgments that were not good were given after all those who came
out of Egypt had died. For the text says in verse 18, “I said to their children
in the wilderness,” and so forth, and in verse 21, “But the children themselves
rebelled against me,” and so forth.

[1674 logical printed p. 217 | IA leaf 235 | PDF p. 235]

And these statutes that were not good were given to these children, not to the
parents who had previously perished in the wilderness. But the ceremonial and
civil laws had been given to the parents. Therefore something far different
from those dogmas must here be understood by these “statutes that were not
good.” What follows also confirms this. For verse 26 explains how he had given
them statutes that were not good, namely, by “making them unclean with their
gifts,” or, “I polluted them in their gifts.” These latter words explain the
former. For otherwise, what connection would there be between these verses?
They would sound like this: because they despised my statutes, profaned my
Sabbaths, and followed the dung-gods of their fathers, I gave them dogmas and
polluted them with their gifts, בהעביר כל־פטר־רחם, “by making every firstborn
pass through,” and he adds למען אשמם, “that I might desolate them.”

Our adversary marvels that such a thought could have entered the mind of the
great Junius. But what is the reason for this astonishment? “Since,” he says
on pages 24–25, “the Prophet here joins חקים ומשפטים, expressing by them
statutes and judgments—the precepts of God to them—just as so often in the
preceding verses, when speaking of God’s precepts, he used these words (verses
11, 13, 16, 19, 21, 24), and especially verse 18, where the word חקים occurs in
the masculine, as here in verse 25, whereas in the remaining verses the
feminine word חקות occurs, lest anyone quibble about this difference of
gender; and just as in the preceding verses God always said, ‘I gave them my
statutes and my judgments,’ which denotes a precept, so he uses the same phrase
in verse 25.” Reply: Although he thinks the phrase is the same both in the
preceding verses and in verse 25, there is nevertheless this notable
difference: in the preceding verses he always says “my judgments, my statutes,”
but in verse 25 he simply says only “judgments and statutes.” And he often says
that the former were violated, but he does not say that the latter were
violated. Are we to suppose that they always observed every dogma exactly?
Then certainly the Sabbath will not be numbered among the dogmas, for its
violation is often charged against them. 2. It is as though the same words
could not be taken improperly here because they are taken properly in the
preceding verses, although the difference between the things signified is
manifest and God here uses those words by way of allusion. Do we not often see
the same word taken sometimes properly and improperly in the same verse? See 1
Corinthians 4:8; Romans 3:21, 27; John 3:16–17; 1 Corinthians 3:17; 4:8; 11:1,
not to cite more. Divine retaliation is therefore indicated here. See similar
instances in Isaiah 66:3–4; Jeremiah 34:17; and frequently elsewhere.
