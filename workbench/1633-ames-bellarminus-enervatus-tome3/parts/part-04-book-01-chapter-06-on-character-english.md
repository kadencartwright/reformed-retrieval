## Chapter VI. On the Character

The third question, which also concerns the effect of the sacraments, is about
the character.

Bellarmine 1. The character is a spiritual sign impressed upon the soul in
certain sacraments. It is not a relation but an absolute quality. It has three
functions: to render a person fit for divine worship, to configure him to
Christ, and to distinguish him from others. It inheres in the soul alone, is
indelible, and is impressed through only three sacraments: baptism,
confirmation, and ordination.

Protestant reply. 1. “The term *character*,” says Gabriel Biel in Book 4,
distinction 6, question 2, “is not much used in Scripture or among the original
teachers. For in the whole canon of the Bible”—he means the Latin
translation—“it is found only in Revelation 13 and the chapters following,
where the character of the beast is mentioned. Finally, the Master, who was a
most diligent collector of the original authorities, makes no mention of a
character except in the present disputation. There he says that those who have
been baptized by heretics, the character of Christ having been preserved, are
not to be rebaptized. There, by *character*, he understands the form of words
handed down by Christ. Its meaning is therefore sufficiently doubtful.” 2. If
the character is a spiritual sign, then, first, it is not a sacramental sign,
which must be perceived by the senses, as Bellarmine himself admits in Book 1,
chapter 14. Second, it must signify not to human beings but to God. Third, it
must also signify something less visible or less known than the spiritual
reality, but these things are exceedingly absurd. 4. Third, if it is a sign
impressed upon the soul, then it is impressed either by means of grace or—

[1632 witness | printed p. 33]

—immediately. It is not the former, because many are said to have the
character who have no grace; nor the latter, because, according to Bellarmine's
position, the sacraments effect grace proximately and immediately. 5. If it is
an absolute quality, it is either good, evil, or indifferent, and it must
either be generated or be created through the sacrament. But none of these
possibilities can stand. 6. The three functions attributed to the character
are effects of grace, as are those other functions which Ockham and Biel add
to them: signifying, recalling to remembrance, and binding. 7. If the
character is in the soul alone, then it either is not properly impressed by
the outward sacrament or is impressed without contact with the subject upon
which it is impressed. 8. If it is indelible, then it remains in those who
fully receive the character of the beast, and even in the damned. Consequently
it neither renders them fit for divine worship, configures them to Christ, nor
distinguishes them from others with any benefit, although these are said to be
the character's functions. 9. No plausible reason can be given why grace is
effected by seven sacraments but the character by only three, especially since
the character is an absolute quality and nothing absolute is peculiar to
three sacraments.

Bellarmine's arguments.

2. Bellarmine. “Who sealed us,” 2 Corinthians 1:22. By this sealing we
understand the character.

Protestant reply. 1. Your understanding proves nothing; the understanding and
testimony of Scripture do. 2. Cajetan understands the seal of apostolic grace
as a testimony to true ministers of the gospel. Dionysius the Carthusian
understands inscription in the book of life and similar things. Thomas
understands a sign of the glory to be obtained. Bellarmine alone understands
the character.

3. Bellarmine. “You were sealed with the Holy Spirit of promise,” Ephesians
1:13.

Protestant reply. Here too Cajetan understands the Spirit and grace of God as
the seal of adoption and of the eternal inheritance; Dionysius the Carthusian,
the marks of divine predestination; and Thomas, love poured into our hearts
through the Holy Spirit. Bellarmine alone understands a character distinct
from grace.

4. Bellarmine. “You were sealed unto the day of redemption,” Ephesians 4:30;
that is, on the day of regeneration through baptism.

Protestant reply. Here too Cajetan, Dionysius the Carthusian, and Thomas
understand saving grace.

5. Bellarmine. If the brand which circumcision left on the body signified
anything, it certainly signified the character of baptism.

Protestant reply. Nothing can be more aptly signified by a brand than the
branded consciences of those who twist Scripture and everything sacred to
confirm their own inventions.

[1632 witness | printed p. 34]

6. Bellarmine. It is credible that when God appoints and consecrates someone
to administer or receive sacraments, or to other ministries, he does not do so
by mere appointment, as people commonly do, but by infusing certain qualities;
and we call these qualities characters.

Protestant reply. It is credible that you yourselves do not know what you mean
by characters. For charisms, or graces freely given, are those qualities by
which God renders people fit and suitable for the duties that he lays upon
them.

7. Bellarmine. Baptism confers something sacramental even if it is given and
received without faith.

Protestant reply. It confers no inward quality at all upon adults without
faith.

8. Bellarmine. No sufficient reason why baptism cannot be repeated can be
given except the character.

Protestant reply. Durandus says that the reason is that the generation of each
individual occurs only once. Biel says that divine institution alone is the
principal cause of this unrepeatability. Whatever the character, considered
as an absolute quality, may be, this conclusion cannot be drawn from it. There
is therefore no need to dispute with Bellarmine about the proper cause.
