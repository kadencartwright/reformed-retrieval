Argument 2. When Paul disputes against justification by works in Romans 4,
he draws his argument principally from the debt and merit of works: “To the
one who works, the reward is not reckoned according to grace but according to
debt” (verse 4). Since justification in his doctrine is entirely from grace,
the argument principally bars from gratuitous justification works to which a
reward is due by merit. But these are precisely works following faith and
done from grace, which Roman Catholics loudly and shamelessly proclaim to
merit eternal life by condignity and the rigor of justice.

Works preceding faith, by contrast, have no merit before God by everyone's
agreement, as Alfonso de Castro says. A Gentile can perform them by free
choice alone, or a Jew by aid of the law without faith in Christ and even
with hatred of Christ and the Christian name. Merely because they are done
without faith they cannot please God (Hebrews 11:6), but are sins or at least
sinful (Romans 14:23). It would have been absurd for Paul to labor so hard to
prove that we are not justified by works from which curse and condemnation
are deserved for this very reason.

Argument 3. Paul excludes all works and the works of all men, including the
regenerate who have already received faith and grace. Origen's proposal is
frivolous: in “No flesh will be justified by works of the law,” he understands
“flesh” as carnal people who live according to the flesh. Why would Paul need
such effort to prove what everyone admitted, that such people are not
justified by their works? “No flesh” synecdochically means no human being, as
elsewhere in Genesis 6:12, Psalm 56:5, 12, and Isaiah 40:5–6. What Paul calls
“no flesh,” David calls “no living person” in Psalm 143:2. “Flesh” must mean
all living people, including the spiritually regenerate; it would be absurd
not to number those who live to God among the living.

More specifically, the Romans and Galatians to whom Paul writes were already
saints (Romans 1:7; Galatians 1:2; 3:2, 5; 4:9), yet he denies justification
to their works. No one denies that Paul was regenerate, yet he was dead to
the law (Galatians 2:19) and refused to be found in his own righteousness
(Philippians 3:9). David was a man after God's heart, yet placed his blessedness
and justification not in his works but in the gratuitous pardon of his sins
(Psalms 32:1–2; 130:3–4; 143:2; Romans 4:6–8).

Finally Abraham, rightly called the father of believers, worked from faith.
Bellarmine himself admits that at this time he believed and had done many good
works from faith. Yet Paul says that this very Abraham was by no means
justified by works, but that his faith was imputed for righteousness (Romans
4:2–3). Though his excellent deeds could furnish material for boasting, Paul
firmly declares that he had none before God. Chrysostom therefore says in his
eighth homily on Romans that it is no wonder if a man without works is
justified by faith, since he has nothing more; but for a man abounding in
virtues to be justified not by works but by faith was wonderful and most
highly commended faith's power. Thus, in Chrysostom's judgment, the mystery
of justification is wondrous and adorable precisely because Abraham, though
surrounded by many excellent deeds, set them all aside in this matter and was
justified by faith alone.
