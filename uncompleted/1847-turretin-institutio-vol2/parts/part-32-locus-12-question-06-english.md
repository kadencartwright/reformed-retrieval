QUESTION VI.

THE EXTENT OF THE COVENANT OF GRACE.

*Was the covenant of grace ever universal, either with respect to its offer or
with respect to its acceptance? We deny.*

[Marginal heading: The occasion of the Question.]

I. This Question concerns the *object* of the covenant of grace. It is raised
against us by the patrons of universal grace, although in different ways and
with different ends. For in order that they may διαφυλάττειν, *preserve*,
their θέσιν, *position*, concerning the universality of grace, they are bound
to defend the universality of the covenant through which grace is dispensed,
so that there is no mortal who is not in some manner subject to this covenant
and with whom it has not been contracted. They draw their reason from the
universality of Christ’s death, which they lay beneath the covenant of grace
as its foundation. For they think Christ died for all so that God might
acquire the power to contract a new covenant with the human race.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Remonstrants.]

II. In the declaration of their position concerning article 2 on the death of
Christ (p. 28), the *Remonstrants* explain their position in this way: “By the
merit of his death Christ has reconciled God the Father to the entire human
race to this extent: on account of his merit the Father, while preserving his
justice and truth, could and willed to enter into and establish a new covenant
of grace with sinners and human beings liable to condemnation.” They contend
that this new covenant was thus contracted with the entire race

[Printed p. 183 | Internet Archive PDF 211 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of humanity, so that there is no nation, indeed no person, who should be said
to be excluded from it. Corvinus writes against Tilenus in chapter 3:
“Although the nations
living under the Old Testament did not have the covenant revealed to them in
the manner in which it was revealed to the Jews, they must not therefore be
judged absolutely excluded from the covenant. They are at least called in
some manner to seek God. Indeed, the spiritual gifts of the covenant had been
set before them no less than before the Jews, provided they fulfilled the
conditions of the covenant by turning themselves to the God of Israel.” And
afterward: “Although many nations are deprived of the ordinary proclamation
of the gospel, and indeed are deprived of it by God’s will, such nations are
nevertheless not strictly excluded from the grace of the gospel itself,” and
so forth. And against Molinaeus in chapter 28: “We freely grant that no one,
considered in himself, is born absolutely alien to the covenant.”

[Marginal heading: And of the universalists.]

III. Here not only the *Lutherans* join them, but also those among us who
maintain universal grace. Testard says in thesis 112 of his *Irenicum*: “God
first contracted that covenant after the fall with Adam, and in him with the
entire human race as it was to be propagated. Then after the flood he renewed
it with Noah, and in him with the same human race as it was to be restored.
Next, while allowing the nations to walk in their own ways, he contracted it
with Abraham and his family until Christ’s kingdom, at which time, once the
distinction between Jew and Greek was removed, he restored it a third time
with the entire human race.” Hence in thesis 3 he had said that “God had
entirely concealed the grace of the new covenant from neither any nation nor
even any human being.” In what follows he adds, in thesis 113, that there are
three kinds of calling: the first is *real*, through works (Ps. 19:1; Matt.
5:45; Acts 14:17; 17:26); the second is *verbal*, through the gospel; the third
is *internal*, through the Spirit.

Amyraut speaks no differently in thesis 34 of his disputation *On the Three
Covenants*: “The degree of love or mercy by which God was induced to contract
such a covenant with sinful human beings regards them all equally. It
therefore follows that it must be judged to have been contracted with all
human beings.”

IV. This covenant can be considered in two ways: either with respect to its
external *promulgation* or offer in *objective grace*, or with respect to its
internal *bestowal* or reception in *subjective grace*. Hence some hold this
covenant to be universal in both respects, insofar as they contend that
sufficient objective and subjective grace is given to each and every person,
so that they can be saved if they will. Such are the Socinians, Remonstrants,
and others who contend for Pelagianism. Others, however, extend the
universality of the covenant only to objective grace, not subjective grace.
Such are those among us who embrace universal grace.

[Marginal heading: The common opinion of the Reformed.]

V. But the common and received opinion among the Reformed is different. They
defend the particularity of the covenant no less than that of saving grace.
Thus, although the covenant can be called general because it extends to many,
especially under the New Testament when the distinction of nations has been
removed, it was nevertheless never universal with each and every person, but
particular only with the elect who are true members of Christ.

They judge that this covenant can in turn be regarded in two ways: either
according to its *internal essence* or with respect to its *external
dispensation*. The former corresponds to the internal calling and the
invisible church of the elect constituted through it. The latter corresponds
to the external calling and the visible church of the called. In the latter
respect, the covenant is considered only with respect to the *promulgation and
offer* made through the external calling, and with respect to the external
benefits which flow from that offer in the preaching of the Word, the
administration of the sacraments, and communion in sacred things. All who
retain the same profession among the people or in the church become
participants in these benefits. Thus the covenant extends even to many
reprobates who remain in the visible church. In the former respect, however,
it extends further to

[Printed p. 184 | Internet Archive PDF 212 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the acceptance, bestowal, and reception of all covenant benefits and to
internal communion with Christ through faith. In this sense it pertains to
none but the elect, who actually become participants in the covenant according
to God’s intention. In them, namely, he fulfills the covenant’s very
conditions, and upon them he not only offers but actually bestows its
benefits. Whichever way this covenant is regarded, they deny that it was
universal—not only with respect to acceptance and bestowal, but also with
respect to promulgation or offer. Although the latter was sometimes wider and
more general, it was nevertheless never absolutely universal, nor can it be
so called.

[Marginal heading: The state of the Question.]

VI. From these things the state of the Question can be gathered without
obscurity. 1. It is not asked about the *outcome* and saving fruit of the
covenant: Are all saved by virtue of the covenant of grace? Those with whom
we contend confess that innumerable people perish and are deprived of the
covenant’s benefits. Rather, it concerns the *counsel and intention of God* in
the establishment of the covenant of grace: Did he regard each and every
person, as they maintain, or only certain people, as we hold?

2. It is not asked whether the calling to the covenant made through the Word
was universal. For οἱ ἐξ ἐναντίας, *our opponents*, cannot deny that it was
particular at least under the Old Testament. Rather, is there a real calling
through the works of nature by which all are called to the covenant of grace?
They assert this; we deny it. 3. It is not asked whether human beings ever
were or could have been saved without Christ. For they acknowledge that there
is no other means of salvation. Rather, could adults obtain salvation without
the knowledge of Christ? We deny this; they affirm it. 4. Finally, it is not
asked whether God daily bestows various benefits upon individual human beings
by which he bears witness to his goodness and grace toward miserable sinners.
This is confessed by all. Rather, are these saving benefits which flow from
the covenant of grace and the merit of Christ and are dispensed by God as
Father and Redeemer with an intention to save? They assert this; we deny it.

Thus the Question comes down to this: Is the covenant of grace which God
contracted with human beings in Christ universal, contracted with each and
every person first in Adam, then in Noah, and finally in Christ—if not with
respect to the outcome, at least with respect to God’s designation and
intention; if not with respect to the reception and use of objective and
subjective grace, at least with respect to its offer? They affirm; we deny.

[Marginal heading: The particularity of the covenant of grace is established.
1. From its designation.]

VII. It is evident from various things that the covenant of grace is
particular. 1. From its *designation*: there is no universal purpose and will
in God to have mercy upon the whole human race, but a particular counsel to
choose a certain number from the common corrupt mass and designate them for
salvation, as was fully proved in Part I, Locus IV, Question 17. Therefore, as
no such covenant was designated from eternity in God’s counsel, so neither is
it contracted in time.

[Marginal heading: 2. From its procurement.]

VIII. Second, from its *procurement*: Christ’s satisfaction, which procures
this covenant for us and upon which it rests as a foundation, was particular.
It was rendered only for those in whose place Christ, by God’s appointment,
had to substitute himself as Surety. They are the very ones given to him by
the Father to be redeemed as his Seed, People, and Body—the children of
promise—to whom he was given by God as Mediator and Head to obtain redemption
for them. By God’s appointment he merited for them not only salvation and
glory, but also the grace of the Spirit, regeneration, and faith, as will be
proved in its place.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the promises of the covenant.]

IX. Third, from the *promises of the covenant*: the covenant of grace pertains
only to those to whom the promises expressed in it pertain. But they pertain
not to each and every person, but only to the elect and faithful, upon whom
the goods comprehended in the promises

[Printed p. 185 | Internet Archive PDF 213 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

are bestowed. For these promises are absolute, not conditional; they arise
from God’s grace alone and do not depend upon any condition in man. This is
indicated not only by the notion of a testament given to this covenant, but
also by the nature of the promises, which concern not only the end but also
the very means and conditions, and therefore exclude every condition.

Although the promise of the covenant is proposed and applied conditionally to
individual persons, it does not follow that the promise itself depends upon
man’s will and is therefore not absolute. For that conditional proposal is a
consequence of the absolute promise. The condition is commanded as man’s duty
in such a way that it is produced at once in the elect as God’s gift.

X. It is vainly alleged here that the evangelical covenant must be considered
either as absolute when the condition has been performed, or as conditional,
that is, as it regards those from whom the condition, not yet performed, is
required. For it is gratuitously assumed that the covenant of grace has also
been contracted with those from whom God only wills to require the condition
annexed to it but does not will to effect it in them. This conflicts with the
nature of the covenant of grace, which differs from the legal covenant in
this: the latter promises life under a condition which it requires from man
but does not effect; the former promises salvation under the condition of
faith, which it both requires from man and effects in him. This is clearly
evident even from Jer. 31:31–33 alone, where remission of sins and regeneration,
or the inscription of the law upon hearts, are enumerated as benefits of the
covenant.

Although in a certain sense it can be called conditional, insofar as it has a
condition subordinated to it—though one to be effected absolutely by God—by
whose intervention the thing promised is given, it is nevertheless properly
absolute: both a priori, because it has no cause besides God’s most free will,
and a posteriori, because God has determined certainly and infallibly to give
the condition itself without another condition to all the covenant people.
No one could say without absurdity that this can be extended to those whom
God hates from eternity and whom he decreed to pass by, granting them neither
the Word nor faith, which are the two means of salvation.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the promulgation of the covenant.]

XI. Fourth, from the *promulgation of the covenant*: this was never universal,
as it ought to have been if the covenant had been universal. For how can a
covenant be said to have been contracted with those to whom knowledge of the
covenant never came and to whom it was never revealed? It is clearer than the
noonday light that innumerable persons and even nations lacked that knowledge
under both the Old and New Testaments, since they were deprived of the Word,
which is the sole proclamation of the covenant of grace.

As far as the Old Testament is concerned, who does not know that God
restricted this covenant to the family of Abraham alone and the Israelite
people, excluding all the other peoples of the world? They are therefore
called “Lohammi, not my people” (Hos. 1; Rom. 9) and “strangers to the
covenants” (Eph. 2:12). God did not give them his statutes as he did to Jacob
(Ps. 147:19, 20), but “allowed them to walk in their own ways” (Acts 14). Who
would say that the covenant of grace, which was the peculiar possession of
the Israelite people, pertained to such people? Or would God have abandoned
all the nations for so many ages, with the exception of one people, in the
deepest darkness in which they lay, without any proclamation of the Word—the
sole object of faith and means of salvation—if he had willed to include them
under the covenant of grace? On the contrary, does not this dispensation
proclaim that this covenant was so particular to the people of God that the
other nations were excluded from it?

As for New Testament times, although we do not deny the far greater extent of
this covenant, which extends indiscriminately to all peoples, both Gentiles
and Jews, it is nevertheless certain that it was never universal such that
all without exception were called to the covenant of grace. Even today
innumerable persons and peoples remain who lie in the darkness of paganism
and who

[Printed p. 186 | Internet Archive PDF 214 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

have never heard anything about Christ and the covenant of grace. “How will
they call upon him in whom they have not believed, and how will they believe
in him who has not been preached to them?” (Rom. 10:14).

XII. It is vainly excepted against Ps. 147:19, 20 that the words must be
understood not *absolutely*, as if the nations had been deprived of every
revelation of God, since he did not leave himself ἀμάρτυρον, *without
witness*, among them (Acts 14:17), but *comparatively*, because their
revelation was neither of the kind nor of the magnitude granted to the Jews.
Thus the comparison and antithesis would concern the *mode* rather than the
*thing*. Or it is said that the Jews were given a revelation accompanied by
the power of the Spirit which was denied to the nations.

For, 1. Revelation through nature, of whatever kind it may be, differs from
the revelation of the Word not only in degree but in kind. The former leads
to the Creator but not the Redeemer, and testifies to God’s existence, power,
wisdom, providence, and beneficence concerning τὰ φυσικά, *natural things*;
but it does not testify to saving grace and God’s good pleasure in Christ,
which is the mystery hidden from all preceding ages. 2. It is gratuitously
assumed that a revelation which had the Spirit as its companion is being
compared with one which lacked him. The subject is the external revelation of
God’s statutes made to Israel and absolutely denied to the other nations, so
that the distinction consists not in the denial or bestowal of the internal
Spirit but in the bestowal or denial of external revelation and objective
grace.

XIII. It is no more solidly said that in Eph. 2:12 Paul speaks about the
Gentiles not absolutely but comparatively with the Jews, and regards them not
so much as they were by nature—guilty of violating the one first or natural
covenant—but as they are after impenitence, guilty of contemptuously and most
unworthily rejecting the kindness offered. For it is clear that the apostle’s
purpose is not to compare them with the Jews, but only to express what they
were absolutely and considered in themselves before the proclamation of the
gospel. Otherwise what is said at the beginning of the chapter, that they
were dead in sins, would also have to be understood comparatively, which no
one would say. It is also gratuitously assumed that before hearing the gospel
they had contracted guilt for violating a covenant other than the natural
covenant. For all who are not under grace are under the law, and thus under
the covenant of works.

XIV. Nor does it help to add that if the gospel is not preached everywhere,
this happened through the negligence of human beings who failed in their
duty, or through God’s just judgment, punishing in this manner the unbelief of
parents who once rendered themselves unworthy of it when it was offered to
them. Whatever the source of the deficiency, it is certain that the gospel
was never preached to innumerable people, who therefore had no knowledge of
Christ or of the covenant of grace. But if God seriously intends their
salvation, if he willed to extend the covenant of grace to them, why did he
not will it to be revealed to them? And why did he not correct the negligence
of human beings which he doubtless foresaw?

Again, when it is said that God deprives peoples of evangelical light because
their parents rendered themselves unworthy of it by their unbelief, two
things are assumed which must be proved. First, that the gospel was once
offered to all peoples without exception, which no one would easily say about
the inhabitants of the New World. Second, that God wills to punish the
parents’ unbelief in their children in this manner, which can be admitted
even less by those who deny the imputation of sin.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the knowledge of Christ.]

XV. Fifth, from the *knowledge of Christ*: the covenant of grace cannot extend
to those to whom the knowledge of Christ the Mediator, in whom it was
contracted, never came. Such are all those who are deprived of the Word of the
gospel and therefore cannot have faith in Christ, which is conceived only
from hearing the Word (Rom. 10:17). Nor can it be replied that although no one
can be saved without Christ, it does not immediately follow that knowledge of
Christ, at least distinct knowledge, is necessary to all for salvation. For
these two things are so connected that they cannot be separated

[Printed p. 187 | Internet Archive PDF 215 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

with respect to adults. Christ saves only as known and apprehended through
faith (Isa. 53:11; John 6:29, 40; 17:3). Otherwise, what need would there be
for the gospel if salvation could come to us without knowledge of Christ?

This crude dogma is confirmed neither by the example of infants, who are
saved through Christ without knowledge of him, since it is plain that through
their age they are incapable of it, which cannot be said of adults; nor by the
faithful of the Old Testament, who were saved without at least a distinct
knowledge of Christ. Besides the fact that it is gratuitously assumed that
they lacked knowledge of Christ, the contrary of which was proved in the
preceding Question, the learned men with whom we contend do not deny that at
least under the New Testament a distinct knowledge of Christ is necessary for
salvation. Since innumerable people still lack it today, they cannot be said
to be included under the covenant of grace, which rests upon Christ.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the sealing of the covenant.]

XVI. Sixth, from the *sealing of the covenant*, which is particular, whether
the external sealing through the sacraments is regarded, which are the
peculiar possession of the church, or the internal sealing through the Spirit
(Eph. 1:13), who is given only to Christ’s members. Nor will you rightly
infer from this that the covenant therefore pertains also to the called who
are in the church and not only to the elect. For the offer of sealing is one
thing; its actual application is another. The former is common to all the
called, but the latter is special to believers. So far is the covenant from
being sealed to unbelievers for salvation that, on the contrary, their own
condemnation is sealed, because they eat judgment to themselves.

[Marginal heading: 7. From the absurdity that nature is confused with grace.]

XVII. Seventh, if through a universal calling each and every person is
invited to the covenant, nature is confused with grace, τὸ γνωστὸν, *what is
known*, with τῷ πιστῷ, *what is believed*, and the proclamation of divine
mercy revealed in Christ alone from the Scriptures must be attributed to the
book of nature and the voice of creatures. Since universal objective grace
without subjective grace is vain and illusory, it must be said either that
sufficient powers are restored to each and every person, through which, if
they will, they can obey God and be enrolled in the covenant—which is nothing
other than sacrificing to the idol of free choice and utterly abolishing
discriminating grace contrary to Paul, as if, according to Pelagius, there
were or could be something in us which is from us and not from God’s
unmerited grace (1 Cor. 4:7)—or that God intends something under an impossible
condition which man cannot have from himself and which God, who alone can
give it, does not will to bestow upon him.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XVIII. So far was the first evangelical promise in Gen. 3:15 from being
universal that it can be gathered from various things, without obscurity,
that it was particular. 1. It treats the *Seed of the woman*, not simply and
in its whole breadth, in which sense it designates human beings as such, but
comparatively and in contradistinction to the serpent’s seed. It designates
the Seed, namely, between whom and the serpent and its seed there had to be
enmity, who was to crush the serpent’s head—that is, destroy the devil’s
kingdom and works and free us from his power—and whose heel alone the serpent
injures.

But this pertains only to *Christ* and to those *who are Christ’s* (Heb. 2:14,
15): primarily to the Seed taken *individually* and antonomastically, to whom
this belongs, and secondarily to the Seed taken *collectively* for his
mystical Body, or the faithful, *for whom, in whom, and by whom*, through his
power, the serpent’s head and, after the head, its seed have either been
crushed or are to be crushed. 2. In the New Testament this is explained of
the faithful to the exclusion of others (Rom. 16:20; 1 John 2:14; 5:4, 18; 2
Cor. 2:14; Heb. 2:14, 15; Rev. 12:11). 3. The ungodly are designated by the
serpent’s seed. Therefore, they cannot be comprehended under the woman’s seed.
Hence Scripture everywhere calls them υἱοὺς πονηροῦ, *sons of the evil one*
(John 8:44; 1 John 3:8; Acts 13:10), and a “brood of vipers” (Matt. 3:7), not
indeed properly and

[Printed p. 188 | Internet Archive PDF 216 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

physically, which does not apply even to the demons themselves, who have no
seed, but figuratively and *morally*, by reason of *likeness* and *imitation*.
They do the devil’s works and obey him, and he acts powerfully and effectively
in them (Eph. 2:2), making them like himself both by instilling malice and
impiety and through an inheritance of destruction. But who would say that
those are the woman’s Seed who have perpetual enmity with him, who watch and
injure his heel, and who not only do not crush the serpent’s head, but in whom
the serpent displays both his head and venom?

XIX. Nor must it be replied here, 1. That all human beings are the woman’s
Seed. For although this could be said in a broad sense and physically, it
cannot be said morally and strictly in contradistinction to the serpent’s
seed, as the term is taken here. 2. Or that the ungodly cannot be called the
serpent’s seed or children of the devil, because serpents beget only serpents.
For this must be understood only figuratively and morally, so that conformity
of species is not to be regarded here, but conformity of malice and curse. 3.
Or that exercising enmity against human beings is alien to Christ’s command
and the duty of Christians. For this is directed not against their persons
but their venom. So far is it from conflicting with the disposition of true
Christians and Christ’s command that no one can maintain the name of believer
and conform himself to Christ’s commands without hating the evil in the
ungodly, and them reduplicatively, insofar as it is in them and insofar as
they are such.

4. Or that victory was indeed prepared by Christ for all, but human unbelief
prevents them from obtaining its fruit—that all are permitted to be joined to
Christ and enjoy his victory if they place faith in God rather than in the
enemy. For it is absurd to say that victory was prepared for those who have
heard nothing about the victor, the victory, its fruit, or the manner of
obtaining it, indeed who by reason of their age could not even have heard.
Next, what is it for victory to be prepared for them under the condition that
they believe, that is, if they already conquer, since faith is our victory (1
John 5:4)? Third, victory cannot be said to be prepared for those who do not
receive its fruit, since it aims not only that Christ crush the serpent’s head
*for them*, but also *in them*, that is, that he work faith in them to conquer
the devil. Fourth, it cannot be said that they were permitted to enjoy the
victory if they had believed, because whatever was to happen depended upon
God’s antecedent decree, according to which he had chosen some from unmerited
love and reprobated others in just hatred. Hence they did not make themselves
the serpent’s seed through unbelief; but because they already pertained to the
serpent’s seed through sin and the decree of reprobation, they showed
themselves to be such all the more through their actual sins.

XX. Although the promise made to Noah, or the covenant contracted with him in
Gen. 6:18, was universal with respect to the preservation of the world from
the flood, extending even to brute animals (Gen. 9:10, 11), it does not follow
that the covenant of grace renewed with him concerning eternal salvation had
the same breadth. It is expressly restricted to Shem (Gen. 9:26), with whom
Japheth or his posterity was finally to be associated, while Ham and Canaan
were excluded from it and devoted to the curse (v. 25). Indeed, the nations
arising from Noah’s sons and grandsons are so separated from one another that
most also depart from the church (chapter 10:11, 12). Nor, if the covenant of
grace was included and foreshadowed in that general promise, must it
immediately be universal. For nothing is more frequent than for something
particular to be foreshadowed by something universal. For example, the waters
of the flood are a figure of baptism (1 Pet. 3:21), and the first creation a
type of the second.

XXI. All human beings coming into the world are illuminated by Christ with
the light of nature, with respect to his divinity, insofar as he is the Word
and Son of God, through the κοινὰς ἐννοίας, *common notions*, and προλήψεις,
*preconceptions*, impressed upon them. But the same cannot be said of Christ
regarded *economically*, insofar as he is Mediator and

[Printed p. 189 | Internet Archive PDF 217 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

θεάνθρωπος, *God-man*, as if he illuminated all with the light of grace. For
this is the privilege of the faithful who are in the church and enjoy the
light of the Word and the Spirit. John 1:9 treats the former σχέσει,
*relation*, not the latter.

XXII. That which can be affirmed is always true, whether it is in fact
affirmed or not, but only in the sense in which it could be affirmed. But that
which could not be affirmed unless some condition were established is not
true when that condition is denied. For example, it could indeed be affirmed
that pagans are commanded and bound to believe the gospel if it were
announced to them. But it is not true that they are commanded and bound to
believe the gospel even if it is not announced to them. It could indeed be
affirmed that Christ died for them if the gospel were announced to them, and
if, after hearing the gospel, they repented and believed, because then they
would pertain to the elect and the covenant people. But it could not be
affirmed, nor is it true, that Christ died for them even if they neither
believe nor repent nor hear anything about the gospel, because then the
argument is drawn from the conditional to the absolute.

XXIII. Although God gave the pagans various testimonies of his goodness and
patience, by which they ought to have been aroused continually to glorify
their Creator and give him thanks for the many benefits bestowed upon them,
it does not follow that God’s mercy and placability were revealed to them. Or
if there could be some *generic and confused knowledge of divine placability*,
it must not immediately be gathered from this that there was a *distinct and
specific* knowledge by which the sinner’s mind could be raised to a sure and
true confidence in the remission of his sins and eternal salvation. This
cannot be had outside Christ and his satisfaction, which cannot be known by
the light of nature. Add that the covenant of grace consists not only in the
revelation of *placability*, but of an actual καταλλαγῆς, *reconciliation*,
whether already accomplished or to be accomplished. It is not sufficient for
salvation to know that God is placable unless we know that he is either
already actually appeased or certainly to be appeased.

XXIV. Although God did not leave himself among the nations without every
witness to his existence, providence, and beneficence through the temporal
benefits he bestowed upon them (Acts 14:17), it cannot legitimately be
concluded from this that knowledge of saving grace was granted to them—unless
we wish to confuse nature with grace and make rains and fruitful seasons
heralds of the gospel. On the contrary, the apostle testifies that “God
allowed them to walk in their own ways,” namely, by depriving them of the
light of the Word and saving knowledge of Christ.

It is one thing for God to testify to his beneficence and ἀνοχὴν,
*forbearance*, toward human beings through the grant of bodily goods or the
suspension and delay of due punishments. This arises from the goodness and
wisdom of God the Creator and Governor in the order of nature. It is another
thing to exhibit mercy in the remission of sins and communication of
spiritual and eternal goods, which cannot occur without Christ. The former,
not the latter, was granted to the nations.

Our Calvin teaches this excellently in this passage: “We must see,” he says,
“how these two things agree with each other. For if God testified about
himself, he did not, as far as lay in him, allow the world to wander. I answer
that the kind of testimony mentioned was such as to deprive human beings of
excuse, but not to suffice for salvation. For that statement of the apostle
is true, that by faith the ages are understood to have been framed by the
Word of God. But faith is not conceived from the bare sight of heaven and
earth, but from hearing the Word. Hence it follows that human beings cannot
be brought to a saving knowledge of God except by the direction of the Word.
Yet this does not prevent those without the Word from being rendered
inexcusable, who, although naturally deprived of light, are nevertheless
blinded by their own malice,” as Paul teaches in Rom. 1.

XXV. When Paul says in Acts 17:26, 27 that “God made the race

of humanity from one blood,” and so forth, “so that they might seek God, if
perhaps they might feel after him and find him,” and so forth, he indeed
intimates
that God so disclosed himself in nature through creation and providence, and
through the various benefits which he continually grants to human beings,
that they ought to have risen to seek—that is, to know and worship—their
Author. But it cannot be concluded that God does this with the counsel and
end that human beings might seek him savingly, that is, flee to him and await
his grace.

1. This seeking presupposes faith in the seeker (Heb. 11:6), which is not
given apart from the Word (Rom. 10:17). 2. The subject here is the kind of
seeking which can occur *as though by feeling about*, that is, in the midst of
darkness. This cannot be said of the seeking which occurs either by
invocation and prayers or by the pursuit of living in a holy and upright
manner, which occurs under the light of faith and the divine Word. 3. Paul
accommodates his discourse to those whom he addresses, namely, the Gentile
philosophers, Stoics, and Epicureans, whose questions were for the most part
about God’s existence and providence and proceeded no further. Thus he shows
that so many evidences of God’s existence and providence are extant that he
can be felt even by the blind. They must no longer adhere either to fate or
fortune, but regard God alone, the Creator and Preserver of the world.

4. In verse 30 the apostle calls that dispensation with respect to the
Gentiles a *time of ignorance*, which he opposes to the external revelation
of the Word made in the New Testament, by which God commands all people
everywhere to repent. This could not have been said if he had provided them a
sufficient revelation for salvation. Nor, if he says that *God now commands
all people everywhere to repent*, does it follow that the calling under the
New Testament is universal with respect to each and every person, any more
than it can be gathered from Paul’s statement in Col. 1:23 that “the gospel
was preached to every creature under heaven” that even then the gospel had
been preached to each and every person in Paul’s time. Thus it designates
only the generality of the calling to all peoples and persons
indiscriminately, in opposition to the Old Testament, under which it was
restricted to one nation.

XXVI. The passages of Paul in Rom. 1:19, 20 and 2:4 which are pressed here
were examined in Part I, Locus I, Question 4. See also Locus IV, on
predestination, Question 17, where various objections pertaining to this were
answered.

XXVII. The covenant of grace need not extend as widely as the covenant of
nature, although its benefits must be greater. For grace must be compared
with the fall not in *extension*, as though they were of equal breadth—in
which sense as many would be saved through Christ as perished through
Adam—but in *intensity and efficacy*. Thus it is true that where sin
abounded, grace superabounded, because it is a greater thing to save one than
to destroy a thousand.

XXVIII. If Adam represented all in the covenant of nature and in the fall, it
does not follow that he does so equally in restoration and the covenant of
grace. For the force of representation ceased in the fall, when he plunged
himself and his posterity into destruction. Hence he no longer sustains a
public person but a private one, and thus the covenant was contracted with
him and his posterity—not with all, but with those who are Christ’s and are
included under the woman’s Seed.

XXIX. The *promises* of the covenant of grace are not *absolutely and simply
universal*, because they were not promulgated to all in the Old Testament
(Deut. 7:7, 8; Ps. 147:19, 20; Acts 14:16; 17:30). Nor are they promulgated to
all in the New Testament, since it is agreed that the gospel was preached
successively, and very many nations remain to which that proclamation came
neither formerly nor today. Rather, they are universal only *relatively* and
*with limitation*, according to the twofold mode of divine dispensation: one
external, with respect to *obligation*, which extends indiscriminately to the
*classes of individuals*, though not to *every individual of the classes*;
the other internal, with respect to *application* and fruit, regarding *each
and every*

*believer*, without distinction of nation, sex, age, or condition. Hence this
universality is repeatedly restricted to believers from the Jews and
Gentiles (Rom. 3:22, 23; 10:12; Acts 10:43; 13:43; John 3:16). The nature of
the promises demands this, for they can be received only by faith (Gal. 3:14;
Rom. 4:13), but faith does not belong to all (2 Thess. 3:2), only to the elect
(Titus 1:1, 2). They are therefore the true and proper object of those
promises and are called “children of promise” (Rom. 9:6, 7, and following).

XXX. Nor can it be said that the promises are indeed universal in themselves
and by God’s intention, insofar as God seriously wills all to be saved and to
come to the knowledge of the truth; but that not all obtain them accidentally
because of the malice and unbelief of human beings, who stubbornly resist the
Holy Spirit and impede his operation. For it is falsely assumed that God
seriously intends the salvation of all. This cannot be said of those whom he
reprobated from eternity and to whom he wills to grant neither the gospel nor
faith, without which the promise can neither be known nor received. 2.
Although it is true that human beings resist the Holy Spirit and impede his
work, it is no less true that God does not provide to all that grace by which
the heart’s resistance is removed, and that this is God’s special gift (Matt.
13:11; Rom. 11:7). This therefore removes the universality of the promise.

XXXI. Although the covenant promises are not absolutely universal, the
foundation of consolation and the certainty of salvation are not therefore
overthrown on the ground that there is no firm inference from a particular.
For they always have their universality with respect to all believers. In
this respect, an argument can solidly be drawn for the consolation of
believers through a practical syllogism whose major premise is founded upon
Scripture and whose minor premise rests upon the testimony of the believing
heart, in this manner: The promises of the gospel pertain to each and every
believer; but I believe; therefore they also pertain to me.

2. So far is consolation from being established by an absolute universality
of the promises that it is instead enervated and weakened. For since on that
hypothesis the promises pertain indiscriminately to believers and unbelievers,
to those to be saved and those to be condemned, there can be no solidity of
consolation or certainty of salvation here. What foundation of consolation
can there be in something common to those who are to be saved and those who
are to be condemned?

XXXII. That Calvin’s opinion was no different from the common and received
position is gathered most clearly from his various writings. Thus, against
Pighius on p. 307: “Now let Pighius boast that God wills all to be saved,
although not even the external preaching of doctrine, which is far inferior
to the Spirit’s illumination, is common to all.” And on p. 706: “If he had
willed his will to be known to all, why did he not publish his law to the
nations? Why did he keep the light of life enclosed within narrow bounds?”
And on p. 719: “When I say that reconciliation is offered to all, I do not
take this as though that grace by which God reconciles the world to himself
had reached all,” and so forth. And a little afterward: “Why did he not will
the gospel to be preached indiscriminately to all from the beginning of the
world? Why did he allow so many nations for so many ages to wander in the
darkness of death?”

He confirms this in his response to Castalio’s first article: “How God wills
all to be saved must not be anxiously disputed, because these two things,
salvation and knowledge of the truth, cohere with each other. Now answer: if
God willed his truth to become known to all, why has its pure truth never
reached so many nations since the gospel began to be promulgated? Then why
did he not open the eyes of all equally, when the internal illumination of
the Spirit, with which he deigns to favor only a few, is necessary for faith?”
See *Institutes*, book 3, chapter 22, section 2; chapter 24, section 15; and on
1 Tim. 2.
