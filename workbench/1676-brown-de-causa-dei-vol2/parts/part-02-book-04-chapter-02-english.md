# CHAPTER II

## Whether the law of the Decalogue bound people other than the Jews

Since throughout this whole book we must argue from the Decalogue against all
the Anti-Sabbatarians, we must treat the matter in such a way that an
opportunity is given to meet them all. Some maintain that we are now in no way
bound to observe a weekly Sabbath, because the fourth commandment of the
Decalogue is merely ceremonial and consequently is now abrogated. To prove
this more easily, they imagine that the law of the Decalogue, as it was
sanctioned, set forth, and promulgated by Moses, contained the covenant of
grace itself, or the formula of the covenant of grace, and nothing besides.
There are also others who would have us freed under the Gospel from observing
any weekly Sabbath, because the whole law of the Decalogue is now abrogated
and does not bind Christians. We must therefore deal first with the Decalogue
and then with the fourth commandment of the Decalogue. In treating the
Decalogue, we shall first consider it insofar as it contained God's law, and
then insofar as it bore a relation to that covenant which Jehovah made with
the Israelites.

Therefore, in order to know whether this law of the Decalogue is still in
force, we must first see

[Printed p. 17 | IA PDF 25 | IA leaf 25 | IA decausadei02brow]

whom it formerly bound. The question, then, is whether this law ever bound
people other than the Jews.

To answer this question, it must be known that this law can and ought to be
considered in two ways: first, in itself, materially and abstractly; then
formally, insofar as it was sanctioned and promulgated on Mount Sinai, and
thus restrictively and with this limitation.

No one will deny that this law, taken in the former way, bound people other
than the Jews, if he considers that all the other nations besides the Jews
were not lawless but were bound by some law to obey God. No other law can be
devised which kept them to their duty, for this law is merely, as it were, a
new edition of the natural law. It is evident that all its commandments belong
to the natural law. As for the fourth commandment, or the weekly Sabbath whose
observance is enjoined by that command, we showed above in Book III, chapter
XXVIII, how it pertains to the natural law, or the law of our creation. No
orthodox person doubts this concerning the others; we care little what the
Roman Catholics say about the second commandment. It is also evident from the
Scriptures that people other than the Jews were both reproved and punished
for violating this law. See Exodus 23:24, 32 and 34:12–16; Genesis 6:1, 2, 5,
11, 12, 13 and 20:3, 6, 9 and 26:10, 11; Leviticus 18:3, 24 and 20:23;
Deuteronomy 6:14 and 7:4, 5, 10, 25 and 12:2, 3, 30, 31; 1 Kings 14:23, 24; 2
Chronicles 33:2, etc.; Psalm 106:35, etc.; Isaiah 10:7, 12, 13 and 13:11 and
16:6 and 47:6, etc.; Jeremiah 10:2, 3 and 48:7, 13, 29, 30, 35 and 51:13, 35,
37; Nehemiah 13:16, 21; Ezekiel 16:49 and 20:7 and 23:7, 8, 30 and 25:3, 15
and 28:2, 5, 16, 17, 18 and 32:12 and 35:5, 6, 12; Amos 1:3, 6, 9, 11;
Obadiah verses 3, 10, 11; Micah 4:5; Nahum 1:14 and 2:12 and 3:14; Habakkuk
1:11, 13, 16 and 2:5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 15, 18, 19; Zephaniah 2:8, 9, 10, 15;
Zechariah 9:3, 5; Romans 1 and 2.

As for certain supernatural duties which Adam himself in the state of
innocence was not directly and formally bound to perform, and which depend
upon divine revelation but nevertheless must be performed by the force of
this law once revelation has been made—such as faith in a mediator—it is
evident that only those to whom God has in some way revealed this are bound to
perform those duties. For *“how shall they believe in him of whom they have
not heard? And how shall they hear without a preacher? And how shall they
preach unless they are sent?”* etc., Romans 10:14–15. *“Faith is from hearing,
and hearing therefore through the word of God,”* verse 17.

The same question is raised concerning this law insofar as it was promulgated
on Mount Sinai: namely, whether it bound people other than the Jews
themselves, for whom it was then specially sanctioned.

In determining this question, the learned divide into various opinions, as
Apollonius testifies in *On the Law of God*, chapter 6, thesis 4, p. 141. Some
maintain that the law bound all nations with respect to that subject matter
which is established by the natural right implanted in the minds of all
people; but with respect to the subject matter prescribed by supernatural
divine institution, and with respect to the obligation introduced through
the ministry of Moses, it pertained only to the Jews, since it was promulgated
only to them. Others assert simply that that whole law was given only to the
Hebrew people,

[Printed p. 18 | IA PDF 26 | IA leaf 26 | IA decausadei02brow]

to whom, while present, it was promulgated, and to their successors according
to the flesh; and that that whole administration was peculiar to that people
insofar as it was a carnal people. Consequently, they say that the precepts
given by Moses did not bind the Gentiles or any others throughout the world
who worshiped the true God, because an unpromulgated law does not bind. For
there can be no obligation by the force of an external law unless the
lawgiver's will is made known through it. Finally, others judge that through
that law God intended to bind other people to render obedience to him—namely,
all those whom he would afterward bring to embrace the true religion—although
first and immediately he bound to obedience the Israelite people who were
present when it was promulgated. They explain it in this way: just as, they
say, Christ our Savior, through the law of grace, introduced the obligation of
faith and obedience for all nations to whom the Gospel is announced,
whenever that may finally occur. Apollonius himself embraces this opinion as
the truest and safest.

Indeed, the word *qua*, or *insofar as*, in the question is taken either
reduplicatively or specifically. If specifically, it is certain that the
moral law which God solemnly gave and promulgated from Mount Sinai bound
others, as has already been said, with respect to those duties which were not
supernatural and did not depend upon a new divine revelation after the fall,
but were natural and inscribed on Adam's heart. For it is evident that by this
promulgation made to the Israelites God did not exempt everyone else from
rendering obedience to that law. As Beza notes on Romans 2:14–15, although the
Gentiles did not possess the law of Moses, they nevertheless had nothing with
which to cloak their crimes, since they had something inscribed on their
hearts in place of a law, which prohibited some things as evil and commanded
others as good. This law, inscribed in some manner on the hearts of all and,
as such, binding all to obedience, was in no way erased by that renewed
promulgation of the same law to the Israelites. For notwithstanding that
promulgation and new sanction, they had conscience and thoughts mutually
accusing or even defending them, Romans 2:15. Thus, although they did not have
the law—that is, the Mosaic law—they were nevertheless not destitute of every
law, for they were a law to themselves, verse 14. Therefore, because of their
impiety and unrighteousness, because they suppressed the truth in
unrighteousness, did not glorify God, and did not give him thanks, God gave
them over to the desires of their own hearts and to shameful affections,
Romans 1:18, 21, 24, 26, 28, etc. Thus the second opinion must be rejected.

But if the question is taken reduplicatively, so that the sense is whether
that moral law, insofar as it was promulgated by God to the Israelites and
sanctioned in a singular manner, so bound everyone else that its violation
incurred greater guilt by the force of that more solemn sanction and was on
that account regarded as more wicked and dreadful, then indeed that second
opinion can be admitted. For it cannot reasonably be said that anyone's
violation of this law, except the Jews', was aggravated by those circumstances
and considerations, or that Gentiles violating the moral law were therefore
to be punished with a heavier penalty because God had promulgated that law
expressly, with a living voice, in a solemn and dreadful manner. These special
considerations concerned only the Jews, among whom the Lord was establishing
a theocracy. Accordingly, as their peculiar Lawgiver he renewed this law and,
by those special circumstances,

[Printed p. 19 | IA PDF 27 | IA leaf 27 | IA decausadei02brow]

bound that Jewish nation more strongly to obedience. For repetition of the
law, and a renewed and more solemn sanction, increases the obligation to
render obedience. The other circumstances also add weight. Hence it is clear
that the guilt of the Jews who violated this law would have been far greater
than that of others to whom all these things had never been made known. In
this respect, therefore, it can rightly be said that the moral law, insofar as
it was given and promulgated with a living voice from Mount Sinai, bound only
the Jews.

And perhaps it can also be said from this that those very Jews who beheld with
their own eyes the signs and miracles which accompanied that promulgation were
more strictly bound than their later successors. *“Only take heed to yourself”*
(Moses says in Deuteronomy 4:9, 10, 11, 12, 15, 16, 17, etc.), *“and guard
yourself diligently, lest you forget those things which your eyes saw, and
lest they depart from your mind on any day of your life—the day, namely, on
which you stood before Jehovah your God in Horeb, when Jehovah commanded me,
‘Gather this people to me, that I may set my words before them to hear’—when
you approached and stood beneath that mountain, while that mountain burned
with fire up to the height of heaven, with darkness, cloud, and thick gloom.
Then Jehovah spoke to you from the midst of that fire—therefore take heed to
yourselves,”* etc. Likewise Deuteronomy 11:1, 2–8: *“Therefore you shall love
Jehovah your God and keep his charge, his statutes, his judgments, and his
commandments—and know today, for I do not speak to your children”* (or,
*“because I do not speak to your children”*) *“who have not known or
experienced the discipline of Jehovah your God, nor his greatness, his strong
hand and his outstretched arm, and his signs and his deeds which he performed
in the midst of Egypt—and what he did in the wilderness,”* etc.

From this it can also be said that their descendants were bound more strictly
to render obedience to this law than others to whom all these things were
afterward made known, because these were privileges peculiar to that nation
and benefits bestowed upon them from special favor. Hence in the Scriptures
the Holy Spirit often aggravates the insolence and rebellion of their
descendants by consideration of those benefits which God had bestowed upon
their fathers and forefathers. See Ezekiel 16:3, 4, 5, etc.; Ezra 9 and
Nehemiah 9. It should also be observed that the descendants who were not then
present were truly bound, and Moses sometimes indicated this expressly,
Deuteronomy 29:14–15: *“Nor do I make this covenant with you alone—but also
with him who stands here with us—and with him who is not here with us”;* that
is, with their seed, as Junius notes in the margin. Hence they were severely
punished for violating that law, according to the divine denunciation in
Deuteronomy 29:22–28; Leviticus 26 throughout; Deuteronomy 28 throughout.

Although the Israelites were bound more strongly by reason of these
circumstances to render obedience to that law than others, nevertheless
others to whom that promulgation of the moral law becomes known are bound to
obey it, even insofar as its sanction was then renewed and repeated. For who
will deny that the guilt of Christians to whom this promulgation has been made
known through the Scriptures is greater when they violate this law than the
guilt of other heathen to whose ears no report of that promulgation has yet
come? Knowledge of this sanction therefore adds force to the obligation.

Indeed, unless this law bound the Gentiles also, even insofar as it was
promulgated from Mount Sinai when that fact becomes known to them, the apostle
would never have cited

[Printed p. 20 | IA PDF 28 | IA leaf 28 | IA decausadei02brow]

that law to show the Romans that the whole law is fulfilled in the single
commandment of love, as he does in Romans 13:8, 9, 10, saying: *“For he who
loves another has fulfilled the law, since this: You shall not commit
adultery; you shall not kill; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false
witness; you shall not covet; and if there is any other commandment, it is
summarily comprehended in this saying, namely, You shall love your neighbor as
yourself.”* There he recounts almost the whole second table of the Decalogue
pronounced by God from the mountain. What is more, when writing to the
Ephesians, he exhorts their children to obey their parents by an argument
drawn from the fifth commandment of the Decalogue, and even from the promise
annexed to that commandment. *“Children,”* he says in chapter 6:1, 2, 3,
*“obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. Honor your father and
mother, which is the first commandment with a promise, that it may be well
with you and you may be long-lived upon the earth.”* The apostle would never
have used this argument unless that law, also as it was given by God from the
mountain, had bound the Ephesians themselves when they now came to know it.
Hence it is abundantly clear that nations converted to the true religion, or
coming to the knowledge of this law through the Scriptures which mention this
singular promulgation, are bound to render obedience to the moral law even
insofar as it was given from Mount Sinai.

From these things it is clear how we should answer that question which is
debated not only among Roman Catholics but also among the Orthodox, namely:
*Does the moral law, insofar as it was enacted in the time of Moses, or given
from Mount Sinai, also concern and bind us Christians under the New
Testament?* For although some things in that second edition of the law are
circumstantial, and certain peculiar adjuncts properly concern the Jews, such
as earthly promises and the like, which do not enter into the very essence of
the promulgation, nevertheless the promulgation itself concerns us
Christians. The law binds us more strictly to obedience than if it had never
been promulgated in that way. We distinguish the promulgation itself from
other things to be considered in that administration which do not concern
Christians and which will be discussed later; here we speak only of the
promulgation itself, or the new legislation. We say that the law binds us
even insofar as it was then promulgated, and not merely insofar as it agrees
with the law of nature and was confirmed by Christ in the New Testament. For
nowhere do we read that this obligation of the law was removed by Christ's
coming. Christ did not come to destroy the law or dissolve its obligation, as
we shall make evident later. He would have destroyed the law to some extent
if he had utterly annulled or abolished its prior obligation. In Matthew 5,
Christ did not remove the prior obligation of the law but rather confirmed
it. He did not act as a lawgiver but as a vindicator, for he interprets the
moral law and vindicates and frees its authority and true sense from the
foul and false corruptions of the Pharisees.

The case of the moral law certainly differs from that of the other Jewish
laws—namely, the ceremonial and judicial laws—as we showed above in Book I,
chapters VIII and IX. For from their nature, end, and causes, to say nothing
of the express testimonies of the divine word, it is evident that they are
mutable and had to be abrogated by Christ's coming and the destruction of
that Jewish polity. But the moral law is of a far different nature, as we made
evident above in Book I, chapter X.

This consideration also ought to have the greatest weight in this matter: if
the moral law insofar as it was promulgated from Sinai did not now bind
Christians, the sayings, explanations, and similar statements of the prophets
would have no authority

[Printed p. 21 | IA PDF 29 | IA leaf 29 | IA decausadei02brow]

among Christians which are found in the books of the Old Testament. Thus that
part of the divine word would be useless, which cannot be admitted. Yet the
consequence is sound, for when the obligation of the law itself is removed,
the obligation of all those things instituted to explain this law and to
press and inculcate it more strongly is also removed. Dominicus de Soto, who
opposes us here, acknowledges this consequence in *On Justice and Right*,
book 2, question 5, article 4. He openly says that no testimony of the Old
Testament can constrain Christians or possess any binding force among them.
But how harsh it is so to reject the whole Old Testament that it is now of no
use for confirming moral truths or pressing moral duties! For everything in
the Old Testament tends in some way to explain those Mosaic laws, especially
the law of the Decalogue, or to rouse God's people and move them by various
arguments to render obedience to those laws. It is granted that the
obligation of the ceremonial and judicial law has been utterly removed, and
consequently all the force of the exhortations, arguments, and motives by
which God's people were pressed to obey them now vanishes. If, therefore, the
obligation of the moral law which arose from that promulgation has also been
removed, then all those things intended to corroborate, press, and explain
this obligation will likewise lose all force, no less than those which served
the judicial and ceremonial laws. Thus all the Old Testament Scripture, at
least its doctrinal part, will now be abolished and will be no more useful to
us than those parts which present to us the institution and use of the
judicial and ceremonial laws.

No one denies that the duty imposed and enjoined upon people by this moral law
is perpetual. Yet no one can fail to know how absurd it is to say that God
enjoined a moral duty by a temporary command.

When we say, however, that this law, insofar as it was delivered through
Moses, also pertains to Christians, we do not take this statement
reduplicatively, because then every law delivered through Moses would pertain
to Christians, since from *insofar as such* to *every such thing* the
inference is perfectly valid. Rather, the sense is that the law was then
promulgated not to bind the Jews alone, but also the Church in every following
age, or all who would afterward be converted to the true religion. Indeed,
although the contrary opinion does not openly favor the Antinomians, it
nevertheless grants them more than is fair. For the Antinomians at least
verbally concede that this law binds with respect to its subject matter and
insofar as it is in the hand of Christ. Those who oppose us here assert this
same thing.

But if this moral law binds only insofar as it is in the hand of Christ, then
after Christ's coming that Mosaic promulgation of the law did not bind the
Jews themselves, for *“there is neither Jew nor Greek—all are one in Christ
Jesus,”* Galatians 3:28. But James, writing to the Jews in James 2:8, 10, 11,
shows that it bound the Jews, and he calls this law the royal law. He speaks
of the law of the Decalogue, *“for,”* he says, *“he who said, Do not commit
adultery, also said, Do not kill.”* Moreover, Christ nowhere indicates this to
us, but rather the contrary, when he explains the law promulgated even by
Moses and vindicates it from the corrupt glosses of the Pharisees. By this
deed he declares that the Mosaic promulgation of the law even then retained
its force; otherwise he would not have interpreted those ancient laws but,
having repudiated them at least to that extent, would have proposed his own
anew.

[Printed p. 22 | IA PDF 30 | IA leaf 30 | IA decausadei02brow]

Furthermore, it will follow from this that no moral laws now bind us except
those which the New Testament indicates were promulgated by Christ or the
apostles. For how else shall we know which laws now bind us insofar as they
are in the hand of Christ? And what, I ask, does it mean that they bind us
insofar as they are in the hand of Christ, other than that they bind us
because Christ or the apostles set them before us for observance and expressly
ratify them? But if this is said, what is to be said about marriage within the
prohibited degrees? All those degrees expressly mentioned in the Mosaic law
are not expressed in the New Testament, to say nothing of very many other
moral duties and vices commanded or prohibited in the Mosaic law of which no
mention is made in the new law. It will also follow that after Christ was born
or died, the Church was without law until the Evangelists and apostles
committed the evangelical doctrine and law to writing, for before that time
no law of Christ was extant. This cannot be said. But perhaps they will say
that we can learn what is moral from nature, which Christ does not destroy. I
answer: the light of nature, now darkened by sin, will reveal God's will to us
quite obscurely, so that it scarcely appears safe enough to flee to that
index. Different people pass various and contrary judgments on this law, so
that some think it prohibits things which others think it approves. But we
said enough about this above in Book I, chapter VI.

Our opinion is also confirmed by the unity of the Jewish and Christian
Church. For according to the apostle in Romans 11:17, the Gentiles were
grafted in and made partakers of the same root and richness of the olive tree.
Thus the Gentiles were partakers with the Jews of the privileges and
constitutions which were not temporary and destined to last only until the
time of reformation, no less than the proselytes formerly called Proselytes of
Righteousness. Hence the Christian Church of the Gentiles would have been
bound to observe the ceremonial laws if that law had not been destined of its
own nature to perish, as shadowy and established only for a time. For in the
question which arose about the Gentiles' observance of the Mosaic law, this
alone settled the controversy: those ceremonies were beggarly elements, a
shadow of things to come whose body was Christ, Colossians 2:7; Galatians 4:9,
and the Jews themselves were no longer bound to observe them, but they were
to be buried at once. Therefore, just as Jews converted to the Christian
faith would have been bound to observe the moral law formerly promulgated by
God himself from Mount Sinai, so Christian Gentiles were bound to observe the
same law.

Those who oppose us concede that, since the rationale of that law was common
to the Jews and all humanity, and since it was the divine determination of
what is just and equitable and an exposition of the law of nature, it
consequently binds us insofar as it indicates to us what the Lord once
commanded and what we ought to take as his will and as most fitting. But if it
binds us in this way, it holds us bound not only by reason of its subject
matter but also by reason of divine authority, and it will still possess the
force of law because it is a sign of the divine will concerning the duty we
owe. It establishes this insofar as it was then sanctioned by God and
explained through Moses. For example, that law enacted by Moses against
contracting marriage within near degrees either binds us insofar as it was
given by Moses or does not bind us at all. For Christ's law does not mention
all of them, the law of nature does not explain them all clearly enough, and
it will be difficult enough for anyone to search them all out from the
innermost recesses of nature.

[Printed p. 23 | IA PDF 31 | IA leaf 31 | IA decausadei02brow]

If they say that those laws bind us by parity of reason insofar as they
contain an exposition of the law of nature, which prohibits every incest,
they will not remove the difficulty. For that exposition has the force of law
from divine authority alone, and that divine authority was ministerially
explained only by Moses. Therefore this law will bind even us insofar as it
was promulgated by Moses, and it will establish the duty we owe. And if no
part of the moral law now binds us unless it can be proved either from the law
of nature or from the law of Christ—as the same author openly says—then no
part of this exposition will bind us either unless it can be demonstrated
from the law of nature or the law of Christ.

Rivetus testifies in his *Decalogue* that this same controversy is debated
among the Roman Catholic scholastics. Some assert that we are held to all the
commandments of the Decalogue which are natural, but not held to them insofar
as they were given by Moses. Others, on the contrary, affirm that the law,
insofar as it was given by Moses, binds Christians not only according to the
natural obligation but also according to the divine obligation promulgated by
God through Moses. They prove this from Matthew 5:17; Romans 13:9; Ephesians
6:2; James 2:8, 10, 11. For it does not appear probable that Christ willed to
abrogate the same commandments insofar as they were given by God—and
therefore by himself, who is God—through Moses, and to confirm those same
commandments by his own authority. And if the promises made in the law to
those who observe them concerned only the Israelite people, there would be no
reason for Paul and James to use obsolete testimonies; nor would James have
added *“according to the Scriptures”* to show that those who exercised
themselves in the law insofar as it was delivered in Scripture did well. They
also prove the same by the authority of Augustine, who in book 10 of *Against
Faustus*, chapter 2, answers the Jew who asks, *“Why do you hold to the Old
Testament whose command you do not keep?”* Augustine replies *“that Christians
keep, even from those books, the precepts for conducting life, while the
precepts for signifying life were rightly observed then, when those things
which are now revealed were being foretold.”* Thus, when he says that the Old
Testament is retained in that Christians keep its moral precepts, he judges
that in morals the obligation of the law delivered by God through Moses still
remains in force. They also prove it by this reasoning: if those moral
precepts, insofar as they were given through Moses, have now ceased, it is
either because God revoked them, because Christ expressly annulled them, or
because, when God first enacted them, he willed them to be valid only for some
determinate period. None of these can be said. Not the first, because it would
be absurd to ascribe this to God: “I indeed will that you keep the natural
precepts which I commanded you in the law, but henceforth I do not will you to
be bound to them because of my command, but because nature so requires.” Not
the second, because it is not probable that God commanded that which by its
nature is perpetual in such a way that his command would finally become
obsolete, as though it had somehow harmed the moral law that it was once
inculcated and confirmed by an external command. No one asserts the third.

Nevertheless, Dr. Rivetus himself does not think that those who deny that we
are bound by the law insofar as it was delivered by Moses depart far from the
truth. He thinks that the entire difficulty lies in the word *insofar as*. If
it is taken reduplicatively, he thinks that those who say that to that extent
we are not bound to it do not hold a bad opinion. We also said above that this
is true. [*“But it is another matter,”* he says, *“if they understand that
‘insofar as’ relatively, so that it sometimes stands for ‘which was delivered
by Moses.’ For then they would err if they denied that we are held by that law
which Moses delivered in the Decalogue. But those who confess that we are
held to all those commandments insofar as they are natural do not seem to

[Printed p. 24 | IA PDF 32 | IA leaf 32 | IA decausadei02brow]

have intended this. For they did not intend to deny that the natural law had
been greatly obscured in humanity and that there was therefore need of a
singular revelation by which those obscure notions might become clearer to
us; and it is most evident that this revelation was set before us through the
ministry of Moses. They seem to have intended only this: Moses set the law
before Israel for a different end from that for which it is now set before
us.”*] Hence he afterward says that he sees no difference in the thing itself,
but only in the manner of speaking: [*“For,”* he says, *“since all agree that
all the moral things contained in the law and the prophets are to be
perpetually observed among Christians, and that they bind insofar as they are
natural precepts implanted by God, the author of nature, in the minds of all;
and since Zanchius and Musculus, as well as Soto, concede that all those
things are contained in the prophets and the law in such a way as to be a
great, indeed necessary, aid to our weakness and ignorance by way of
instruction, as they say.”*]

But since he said above that those who deny that this law binds us insofar as
it was delivered by Moses intended that Moses set the law before Israel for a
different end from that for which it is now set before us, it is clear from
this that this is not a mere λογομαχία—*war of words*. But what were those
peculiar ends? He enumerates three: [*“Therefore we say,”* he states, *“that
the moral law, insofar as it was delivered by Moses, was abrogated in these
respects: insofar as it was the law of the Old Testament and of Moses, or, so
to speak, with respect to its ministry and not its subject matter. For the law
remains, but is administered differently, because we no longer have anything
in common with Moses but with Christ, who in the Gospel set before us the
remission of sins from which we could not be justified through the law of
Moses, and commended and confirmed a rule for ordering life after our
renewal—a rule indeed taken from Moses. 2. He abrogated it insofar as it held
under a curse all who did not observe it, etc. 3. Hence it follows that the
law is now not burdensome to true Christians, because its severity has been
removed through Christ, etc.”*] But who does not see that it no more follows
from this that the law, insofar as it was enacted by God through Moses, does
not now bind us than it follows from it that that law, insofar as it was given
by God through Moses, did not bind believers under the Old Testament? For it
is certain that the law was not then given so that the Israelites would be
justified through obedience to it. It is also certain that Christ freed
believers from the curse of the law and that, in this respect, the law was no
less to them than to us like a serpent whose venom has been removed.
Moreover, this moral law was not burdensome to true believers either. In what,
then, does this difference consist?

Afterward he seems to place the difference in the promulgation, for he states
his opinion in this way: [*“It is certain that that whole law was given only
to the Hebrew people to whom it was promulgated while they were present, and
to their successors according to the flesh; and that whole administration
was peculiar to that people insofar as it was a carnal people, and to that
extent does not bind Christians.”*] But 1. were not some Gentiles also
present? 2. Is not this law now in some manner promulgated to us Christians?
Why, then, ought it not to bind us also?

preceptive; that is, the force of a new command was added in it, in which
there was also mingled a certain purely constitutive command which was not

necessary, but merely from the will of the lawgiver. If the law, insofar as it

that external command, there would be no difficulty, because a purely

In this sense the moral law declared by Moses and the prophets, as such—that
