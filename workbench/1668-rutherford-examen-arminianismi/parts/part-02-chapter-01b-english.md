As regards the first question: Consequences are of two kinds. Some are clear,
plain, and evident, as: *All idolaters are abominable to God; therefore, so too
are those who bend the knee to Baal.* Others are more obscure and less evident,
as: *Under the law Jewish males were circumcised on the eighth day; therefore,
the infants of Christians ought to be baptized.*

First assertion. There are three kinds of light and therefore three kinds of
certainty. 1. Light is theological and human when I know something to be true
from natural reason or from an acquired habit of theology, as Judas knew that
Christ was the Savior. 2. Light is Scriptur-*

[Printed p. 21 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 74 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*al when, from a godly inclination of the will, I know something because it is
revealed in Scripture as God's Word. 3. There is divine experiential light,
which is nevertheless infallible, as when I know that I know God because I
keep his commandments, 1 John 2:3. By Scriptural light and the certainty of
faith I know the major premise of the following syllogism. By divine,
experiential, and infallible light—though not by Scriptural light—I know that
the assumption is true; and by mixed and infallible light I know the conclusion
to be true when I argue as follows:

*Whoever believes in Christ will not perish, but has eternal life.*

*But I believe in Christ.*

*Therefore, I will not perish, but have eternal life.*

The major premise, by the certainty of faith, is the express Word of God, John
3:16. The assumption is not known formally by the light of the Word, for it is
not written in God's Word that I believe.

You will say: It is written in God's Word through signs and τεκμήρια—proofs—as
follows:

*The one who overcomes the world and keeps God's commandments believes, as in
1 John 5:4–5 and James 2:18.*

*But I have overcome the world and kept God's commandments.*

*Therefore, I believe.*

I answer: The minor premise is still not known by Scriptural light and the
certainty of faith, but by experiential knowledge and a reflex act of the Holy
Spirit testifying that I believe, overcome the-*

[Printed p. 22 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 75 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*world, and keep God's commandments. Thus reflex acts are always resolved into
the testimony of the Holy Spirit, or into sense and experiential knowledge.

Nevertheless, these things may rightly be said to be known by the certainty of
faith if the word *faith* is taken more broadly for what God reveals as true,
whether expressly revealed in the Word or revealed through gracious acts which
he produces in me through the Holy Spirit. For God speaks to us not only
through the Word but also through gracious effects, and both are equally
infallible.

This latter is called the testimony of the Spirit. It is either effective,
when the Spirit effectively inspires, works, and produces certain and
infallible acts of faith in our intellect, from which come our πεποίθησις—
confidence—and πληροφορία—full assurance. See Romans 4:21 and 14:5. Or it is
objective, and this is twofold. The Holy Spirit testifies either concerning the
premises—that is, concerning the antecedent—or concerning the conclusion or
consequence. He testifies concerning the premises when he objectively reveals
and manifests to us the acts of faith, love, and repentance produced by him,
so that in our hearts we perceive by sense and experience that we believe,
love, and repent. 2. From these acts, as from infallible and most certain
premises, he gathers this conclusion: that we are children of God, according
to Romans 8:16: *“The Spirit himself bears joint witness with our spirit that
we are children of God.”* Paul does not, however, make two numerically distin-*

[Printed p. 23 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 76 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*ct testimonies—one belonging to our natural spirit, the other to the Holy
Spirit speaking to us. Rather, when the Holy Spirit causes us to know and
perceive by a reflex act that we believe, repent, and hope, he causes us to
conclude from it that we are children of God.

That this testimony and experiential knowledge is not fallible and conjectural,
as Bellarmine says with the Roman Catholics in *On Justification*, book 3,
chapter 8, section 2; nor theological and human, and less than the certainty of
faith or Scripture, as Forbes, the pseudo-bishop of Edinburgh, taught; nor
uncertain and conditional, as the Arminians teach; but as certain and
infallible as the testimony of God speaking in Scripture, is evident from
this. 1. Perseverance and eternal salvation are objects of faith for every
believer, according to Romans 8:38; 2 Timothy 1:12; and 2 Timothy 4:8, 18.
Hence perseverance and eternal life, as applied by name and particularly to a
believer, are fundamental articles, although the saints' faith concerning them
often wavers and fluctuates under the weight of temptations. 2. Otherwise no
one could be certain of his salvation, contrary to Scripture: 1 John 2:3;
3:14; 5:13; Romans 8:38; 2 Timothy 1:12; and 2 Timothy 4:8, 18.

Hence those syllogistic consequences in which the major premise is Scripture's
testimony and the minor is experiential knowledge are objects of faith; they
can be fundamental, and in many persons they are.

[Printed p. 24 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 77 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

Fasolus, on the first part of Thomas, volume 1, question 1, article 2, doubt 9,
section 51: *“The doubts and wavering which believers experience do not arise
from an intrinsic cause and the formal motive of the act itself, or from the
act's weak adherence with respect to the object. They indicate only its
intrinsic obscurity and lack of evidence, and its dependence upon the free
command of the will in producing and preserving the act.”*

Hooker, in his response to Travers's fifth supplication, and William
Chillingworth, *The Religion of Protestants*, part 1, chapter 6, p. 311, assert
that articles of faith in their being and entitatively are as certain as the
principles of the sciences. But they utterly deny that our certainty of
adherence to the Gospel promises is equal to the certainty of the senses or
the sciences. 1. Because faith can increase, whereas such certainty is
indivisible, most perfect, and cannot coexist with doubt. 2. Because, as Hooker
says, *“God would not prove to us that his promises are true by arguments drawn
from sensible experience: we ought to be more certain of the proof than of the
thing proved. If ten people look at the moon, all would know equally that the
moon is the moon; but when ten people believe one and the same promise, they do
not all believe with equal certainty. Therefore the certainty of faith,
essentially and as required from us under penalty of damnation, is not equal
to the certainty of the senses.”*

I answer: Our certainty is equal to the certainty of the sciences and senses,
and it is not equal.

[Printed p. 25 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 78 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

It is equal 1. negatively, insofar as it excludes the doubt of unbelief in
that act. 2. It is equal insofar as it equally settles the intellect concerning the certainty
of the object and equally produces spiritual joy concerning the known object.
3. It is equally certain because a Christian would rather face death than deny
the promises, just as one who sees and is certain with scientific certainty
cannot deny that he sees and knows.

But it is not equal in the mode of knowing or with respect to the means which
exclude every doubt of blind reason. 2. Nor is it equal in the manner in which
such acts endure: sense is never, or rarely, deceived, and the intellect never
doubts the truth of propositions properly perceived, because the object is
proportioned to the intellect. But acts of faith are not of themselves equally
enduring in certainty through every difference of time, because more
temptations assail the certainty of faith—as something supernatural—than
assail the knowledge of the senses and natural intellect.

But Chillingworth, at the same place, p. 312, denies absolute and infallible
certainty with the Arminians, because probable motives sufficiently move
people to undergo mortal dangers across sea and land where the hope of a
little gain is only probable, not infallible and as certain as the certainty
of sense and the sciences.

But Ames rightly says in *Conscience*, book 3, chapter 2,

[Printed p. 26 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 79 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

question 15: *“Faith not only on the side of the object but also on the side
of the subject (question 8, theses 14–15), by its nature produces in us a firmer assent
than natural knowledge, because it rests upon a more certain cause. Yet because
the human intellect has a greater proportion to things known naturally and
more fully attains them, it therefore does not always employ the light of faith
with the certainty with which it employs natural knowledge.”*

So too, among the Roman Catholics, Andreas de Vega says in *On the Council of
Trent*, book 6, chapter 39, that *“every proposition inferred from propositions
of faith is of faith, as it were indirectly and mediately.”* Certainly, one who
denies a valid consequence denies the premise of faith.

Second assertion. Those consequences in which the major premise is from
Scripture, and the minor is the dictate of natural conscience applying God's
law to one's own deeds, are also of faith, as follows:

*Whoever pollutes the marriage bed with incestuous adultery is liable to God's
wrath.*

*But I, Herod, pollute the marriage bed with incestuous adultery.*

*Therefore I, Herod, am liable to God's wrath.*

Proof. 1. Wicked people could never be convicted and condemned by God's Word
in their consciences—contrary to Romans 2:15; 1:20; Titus 1:11; John 8:9; and
Titus 3:11—unless it were certainly established to them by infallible light
that they were guilty of such grave crimes. 2. Those who sin against the Holy
Spirit, blaspheme God-*

[Printed p. 27 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 80 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*maliciously, and despair of salvation would not know for certain that they had
committed such things, which is contrary to experience.

Third assertion. Everyone ought, by reflex faith, to be persuaded in his
conscience that his moral acts agree with Scripture and please God. But those
acts are not known directly by the light of Scripture—for Scripture directly
judges nothing concerning this or that particular act; rather, acts are known
to be wicked when conscience applies the light of Scripture to this or that
act—but by the light of conscience. Therefore, what we know by the
experiential sense of conscience, not by the light of Scripture, we
nevertheless know by the light of faith and internal persuasion. The major is
Romans 14:23: *“The one who doubts is condemned if he eats, because he does not
eat from faith; for whatever is not from faith is sin”;* and verse 5: *“Let
each be fully persuaded in his own mind.”* 2. Everyone is bound to know from
the testimony of a good conscience that his deeds please God, 2 Corinthians
1:12; Hebrews 13:18; 1 John 3:20. 3. The testimony of conscience rightly and
justly accusing and condemning, or excusing and absolving, agrees with God's
testimony, as is evident from 1 John 3:20; John 8:9; 1 Samuel 28:15; Psalms
17:3; 7:3–4; and Hebrews 13:18.

Fourth assertion. Those consequences in which the major premise is from
Scripture, while the minor is known neither by the light of Scripture nor by
the experiential light of conscience,

[Printed p. 28 (printed as 58) | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 81 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

are not of faith. Such are these consequences:

*Whoever believes will be saved.*

*But these particular Christians believe—say, John and Anna, with whom I am
acquainted.*

*Therefore, they will be saved.*

Likewise:

*One who loves the brothers has been translated from death to life.*

*But Judas, in the apostles' judgment, loves the brothers.*

*Therefore, and so forth.*

Two objections can be made here. 1. Natural reason in a Turk, a Jew, or
Aristotle sees this consequence: *If the Son of God was made like us in every
respect except sin, then he is a mortal and tearful human being.* Therefore,
such a consequence is not of faith, for what is perceived by natural light is
not of faith.

I answer: I deny the consequence. For by reason and sense we perceive the sun
as a bridegroom proceeding in the morning from his eastern chamber, and we
know this by faith, Psalm 19:6. Here reason is nothing but the instrumental
cause, while the light of faith is the principal agent.

Objection 2. Whatever is something different from God's Word is not God's Word
and thus is not of faith. But a conclusion deduced from God's Word *is
something different from the premises,* as Aristotle rightly says. Therefore,
it is not God's Word. Likewise, what is of faith is resolved into the first
believable thing: *Thus says Jehovah.* But this conclusion—*Therefore I
believe*—is not resol-*

[Printed p. 29 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 82 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*ved in this way, for God nowhere says in Scripture that I believe, or that
Lawrence or Francis believes. Therefore, it is not of faith.

I answer: 1. “Whatever is different from God's Word in itself and by an
external, not an internal, rationale”—as Cameron says in *On the Church*, p.
359—or is different in our manner of knowing but not in itself, “is not God's
Word”: this is false. I deny that what differs from the Word not in itself but
only by the arrangement of terms and as a species differs from its genus is
not God's Word. But clear consequences are something different from God's Word
as that which is contained under “every” and “none.” For if God says that a
human being is an animal, he says that Peter is an animal.

[Editorial erratum, PDF 52: at p. 29, line 5 the source has a corrupt reading
after `Quod est`; the errata directs `read non in se`, which is adopted here.]

2. All conclusions known partly by Scripture and partly by the testimony of
the Holy Spirit or the light of natural conscience are resolved into this:
*Thus says Jehovah; thus testifies the Holy Spirit; thus testifies Jehovah
through natural conscience.* Therefore, what God speaks through operations of
conscience that accord with the τεκμήρια—proofs—and signs in the Word, God
speaks no less than what is expressly in the Word. Consequently, the
subjective certainty of salvation, by which I am certain that I believe and
will be saved, is no less an object of faith—whatever the Roman Catholics and
Arminians assert to the contrary—than objective certainty.

To the second question, namely, *What evidence of consequence is required for
a consequence to be an article of faith?* I say:

[Printed p. 30 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 83 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

It is one thing to hold a fundamental conclusion firmly and another to attack
it by a less clear consequence. Peter truly and with saving faith believed
that Christ was the Savior and promised Messiah, Matthew 16:17. Nevertheless,
he did not see this consequence: *Christ is truly that promised Messiah;
therefore, he will be delivered into sinners' hands, scourged, and crucified.*
Yet it was a fundamental consequence, and because Peter did not see it he
tried to dissuade Christ from suffering death. Likewise, the Galatians
believed that Christ profited them, yet they believed circumcision necessary,
because they thought these two could stand together. Likewise, among the Roman
Catholics are simple people who believe Christ to be a true human being, yet
believe transubstantiation and think that, through God's omnipotence,
transubstantiation and Christ's true humanity stand together. Through a defect
of judgment, a corrupt education, and an entrenched opinion, such people err
with materially, not formally, fundamental errors. Their worship is impious
in itself, though not relatively, as Cameron says in *On the Power of the
Church*, pp. 214–15. But after the truth is clearly presented, such errors
become formally fundamental errors.

2. Next, concerning these fundamental consequences: 1. One kind of conscience
is doubting, assenting to them with fear; this can occur in saints who believe
all fundamentals habitually.-*

[Printed p. 31 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 84 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*2. Another is scrupulous, pressed by slight doubts arising from a slight
foundation; these people can also believe. 3. Another is an opining and
conjecturing conscience, found in atheists who are touched by no religious
conscience. In them the error is formally fundamental. 4. Finally, another is
a conscience maliciously denying fundamental consequences. These sin most of
all and are heretics, even if they grant all the cardinal principles of faith.
For conscience is violated whenever evident consequences are denied.

[Editorial erratum, PDF 52: at p. 31, line 7 the source prints `malitosè`; the
errata directs `read malitiosè`, which is adopted here.]

3. Those who have never obtained trustworthy teachers—such as less educated
Roman Catholics—and from infancy believe that the Church cannot err, so that
no suspicion of error or of a need to examine the Church's doctrine with
judgment has ever entered them, just as no such suspicion ever enters us
concerning the need to examine the clear doctrine of the Gospel which we
believe to be truly divine, are further removed from formally fundamental
errors than are more discerning people. The latter, through reports of
Protestants attacking popery, raise questions about the authority of their
teachers and the truth of popery's principles. Those who believe both
transubstantiation and Christ's humanity, whose doubt is resolved into the
philosophical error by which they think it probable that the extension of
parts is not of the essence of body, do not deviate as much as-*

[Printed p. 32 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 85 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*those who can gather from Scripture that Christ as a human being can only be
in one place at one time, as in Matthew 28:6: *“He has risen; therefore he is
not here.”* When dullness of mind is added to corrupt education, simple
credulity, and faith concerning Christ as like us in every respect except sin,
such people overthrow the foundation less directly.

Ames asks in *Conscience*, book 3, chapter 2, question 4: *“Can someone be saved
who believes something that conflicts with what must necessarily be believed
for salvation?”* He answers: *“If he understands that they conflict, he cannot
believe both; but if, through error and defective instruction, he thinks they
can stand together, that error does not exclude him from salvation.”*

The third question, *concerning the determinate number of fundamentals,* is
more difficult. I lay down these statements about it.

1. It is rightly said with Beza, *Minor Works*, volume 2, *On the Marks of the
Church*, p. 141, and with our theologians, that *all fundamentals to be
believed are either expressly (ῥητῶς) or consequentially in the Apostles'
Creed,* which, as Calvin says in *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 16, section 18,
*“has prevailed from the very age of the apostles as a public confession
received by everyone's vote; everything to be done is in the Decalogue, and
everything to be sought is in the Lord's Prayer.”*

2. Articles in the Creed are of two kinds. Some are primary and must be
believed explicitly by everyone who wishes to be saved, as the Athanasian
Creed and the martyr Vigilius say in book 2, chapter 4, as Dr. Pareus notes in
prolegomenon 3 on Hosea, question 4: for example, that Christ is a human being,-*

[Printed p. 33 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 86 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*died for sinners, rose again, and was exalted, and so forth. Others explain
the manner of a cardinal article rather than being themselves fundamental.
Thus, the article that says he *suffered under Pontius Pilate* explains the
manner of his suffering; his *birth from the Virgin Mary and conception by the
Holy Spirit* explains the manner of his humanity; and the article concerning
the time of the resurrection—namely, *that he rose on the third day*—and his
ascension into heaven explain the manner of his exaltation. Perhaps many ended
their days in faith in Christ while ignorant of these modes. I would not,
therefore, assert that all the articles expressed in the Creed are
fundamental. And there are some things not expressly (ῥητῶς) in it—namely,
concerning the sacraments, which the author of Hebrews 6 establishes as
fundamental; likewise, that *Scripture is the canon of faith* and *that we are
born in sins,* and so forth.

3. I would not dare assign how many fundamentals, openly denied, either
destroy the essence of the Church or render the denier incapable of salvation.

4. The denial of one primarily fundamental article, when malice is added,
makes an assembly which is materially a Church formally not a Church. The
Roman Church is an example, miserably erring in many fundamentals when it
overthrows Christ's person, natures, and offices and worships creatures in
place of the Creator. Thus it does not hold the founda-*

[Printed p. 34 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 87 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*tion, but to that extent ceases to be a Church, or at least the Church's
essence is harmed in it. More on these matters later.

5. A determinate number of fundamentals is not a fundamental article, because
many truly believe and thus hold the foundation who cannot define the number
of fundamentals, as experience cries out. The only true catholic Church that
cannot fail is the one which believes all fundamentals. But the catholic
assembly which cannot fail to believe all fundamentals and cannot err in
fundamentals is the true catholic Church. Hence it follows: 1. Although the
catholic Church cannot fail in fundamentals—otherwise it would not be the
catholic Church—it does not follow that it can infallibly determine a fixed
number of fundamental articles. 2. We cannot consult any particular assembly
about the number of fundamentals. 3. An assembly or particular person can
believe all fundamentals yet be unable to determine which things are
fundamental and which are not. 4. Whoever materially believes the fundamentals
is saved, even if he does not believe them all formally and under the
designation of fundamentals.

To the fourth question I say that knowledge and faith are twofold. One is
express, explicit, distinct, and unfolded, when someone is evidently and
particularly persuaded that God exists and that the Son of God was incar-*

[Printed p. 35 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 88 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*nate. The other is enveloped and implicit faith, which is such in two ways:
1. with respect to the degree of faith, and 2. with respect to the intellect
or will of the believer. Concerning the degree of faith, following Calvin,
*Institutes*, book 3, chapter 2, section 45, we can concede implicit faith in
three ways: 1. insofar as many things are hidden from us in this life; 2.
insofar as we see known things imperfectly as in a mirror, so that faith is
essentially implicit while sight is explicit and unfolded; 3. the faith which
prepares for true faith—by which many seemed to be carried into wonder at
Christ's miracles, John 4:48—is implicit.

2. Implicit faith is such either with respect to the intellect, as when one
who believes a universal proposition virtually and implicitly believes a
particular proposition contained under it. A countryman believes that it is
wicked to worship a creature; his implicit faith suffices for believing that
Vásquez raves when he asserts that it is lawful to worship straw and the lips
of Judas the traitor because they are beings that represent the first being.

Or it is implicit with respect to the will, and this is twofold. First, when
someone is ready in his will to believe more things, should God reveal them,
than he currently believes.

Knowledge and faith concerning the principal fundamentals is necessary for
everyone, even laypeople who wish to be saved, so that repentance for
ignorance of such fundamentals does not suffice unless they are imbued with
the contrary explicit faith. Acts 4:12: *“Salvation is found in no one else,
for there is no other name-*

[Printed p. 36 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 89 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*under heaven given among people by which we must be saved.”* By Christ's name
Christ himself as revealed in the Word is understood, so that inspirations of
good angels are excluded: Galatians 1:8–9; 2:6; 3:10; John 14:6; Mark 16:15–16:
*“Preach the Gospel to every creature. Whoever believes and is baptized will
be saved; whoever does not believe”*—namely, the Gospel—*“will be condemned.”*
John 3:36: *“Whoever does not believe the Son will not see life, but God's
wrath remains upon him”;* and verse 18: *“Whoever does not believe is condemned
already.”* Romans 3:20: *“No flesh will be justified by works of the law.”* The
preceding verse indicates that this is said of every mortal: *“So that every
mouth may be stopped and the whole world may become liable to God's
condemnation.”*

Becan therefore errs in part 2 of *The Theological Virtues*, chapter 2,
question 3, saying that *one who expressly believes only the two primary
articles*—namely, 1. *that God exists,* and 2. *that God exercises providence
toward human beings*—*precisely because of God's revelation is judged
implicitly to will to believe the remaining articles because of that same
revelation.* For saving faith in only two fundamental articles, precisely
because of God's revelation, cannot coexist with faith in more. But more
fundamentals must be expressly believed.

Articulorum`; the errata directs `read duorum tantum`, “only two,” which is
adopted here.]

There are three things in these fundamentals as known: 1. Knowledge according
to the substance of their meaning. 2. A fuller explanation according to what
is proposed in Scripture for edifica-*

[Printed p. 37 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 90 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

*tion. 3. The resolution of difficulties debated concerning them. Knowledge
of fundamentals in the first manner is necessary by necessity of means for
the less educated, and sufficient, as Ames observes in *Conscience*, book 4,
chapter 2, question 3. *“It is not necessary,”* says Beza in volume 2, *On the
Marks of the Church,* *“for someone to be a true member of Christ that he
understand the reason why (τὸ διότι) with the greatest exactness.”* Nor are
things required from the less educated which are required from pastors, who
ought to be apt to teach and to refute opponents. The same negative certainty
—that is, certainty excluding unbelief and error concerning the faith—is
required without exception from everyone, pastors and flock alike: Hebrews
5:11–12; Romans 14:5; Colossians 1:9; Ephesians 1:8. (Thus Suárez, *The Three
Theological Virtues*, disputation 13, section 6, section 1.) Nor is it required
by the duty of a special calling *“that the whole body should be an eye, for
where then would hearing be?”* 1 Corinthians 12:27. But the same positive
certainty is by no means required from pastor and flock, infant and father.
Hence one obligation is pastoral, required in its kind by the greatest
necessity of command; another is Christian, also required in its kind by the
greatest necessity. Therefore all Christians must *“abound in all wisdom and
knowledge,”* Colossians 2:2 and 3:16, by necessity of command. But by necessity
of means it suffices for the less educated implicitly and with a godly
inclination of mind to believe fundamental consequences and explanations of
the fundamentals.

[Printed p. 38 | Google/KBNL-UvA PDF 91 | GB 9IhiAAAAcAAJ]

Again, faith is implicit with respect to the will when someone is ready to
believe what the Church believes, as Estius says in *Sentences*, book 3,
distinction 25, section 2. Thomas explains the Romanists' implicit faith more
soundly in *Summa Theologiæ* II-II, question 2, article 5, response: *“when
someone is ready to believe whatever divine Scripture contains.”* The crasser
Suárez, in *The Three Theological Virtues*, disputation 13, section 6,
slanderously says that Calvin makes every person the rule of his own faith
because we reject their implicit faith, *“which,”* says Suárez in the same
place, section 4, *“although it includes some ignorance, preserves from the
dangers of errors because it subjects the mind to the proximate rule of faith,
which is the Church.”* Hence they call the errors of laypeople who err with the
pastors whom they implicitly believe praiseworthy acts of obedience.
