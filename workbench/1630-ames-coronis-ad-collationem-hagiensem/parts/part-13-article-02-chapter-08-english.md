### Chapter VIII

#### Seventh argument

Page 196.

For whomever Christ died and procured remission of sins and reconciliation
with God, for them he also procured by his death deliverance from the bondage
of sin and the Spirit of regeneration unto new life (Rom. 6:5–6; 2 Cor. 5:15;
Heb. 2:14–15; Jer. 31:33–34; Acts 5:31). But this deliverance from the bondage
of sins and regeneration through the Spirit has not been procured for every
individual human being, as experience teaches. Therefore neither
reconciliation with God nor remission of sins has been procured.

1. Here the Remonstrants answer briefly by denying the minor premise, because
deliverance from the bondage of sin—or its right and means—has been
procured for all and each, although it is not applied to all unto actual

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 152 | printed p. 142]

deliverance. But 1. they do not rightly explain the procurement of deliverance
by the procurement of its right and means. For it is one thing to bring it
about that deliverance is certainly granted to all, which our men understand
by this procurement; it is another that an indefinite and undetermined power
for it should be prepared, which is what they mean by the right and means. 2.
Since by the right of deliverance they understand that power which Christ
acquired for himself, namely that he might be able to save believers, this
procurement appears to be made for Christ rather than for us. 3. According to
the Remonstrants’ position, Christ also acquired for himself by his death the
right to destroy unbelievers. Nicolaus affirms on page 22 that this cannot be
denied. Therefore, if the acquisition of a right of this kind is to be called
a procurement with respect to those to whom it extends, then Christ must be
said to have procured perdition and damnation for human beings no less than
reconciliation unto deliverance from sin and death.

2. They run lightly through the passages cited. Romans 6:5–6—“we ourselves
have coalesced through assimilation to death,” etc.—speaks of the mortification
of the faithful, which pertains not to procurement but to application. Thus they
separate those things which the Holy Spirit is always accustomed to join. For
by God’s imputation we have had communion with Christ in his death, so that our
old man is said to have been crucified together with him; and by the power of
this communion the actual abolition of the same corruption also follows in
its own time, which is customarily called mortification. Concerning Hebrews
2:14–15, they observe that the ends of Christ’s death extend to all, meanwhile
failing to notice that by “all” there are understood the sons to be brought to
glory in verse 10, those to be sanctified and Christ’s brothers in verse 11,
the church in verse 12, and the children whom God gave to Christ in verse 13.
Nor can what is said in verse 14—that the devil and his death-dealing power
have been abolished with respect to them—be extended in any way to all and
each of mortal men, or what is said in verse 15, that they are rendered free.
Concerning

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 153 | printed p. 143]

Jeremiah 31:33 and Acts 5:31, they pretend not to know to what end they are
cited. But they have this stated plainly enough in the argument itself, on
page 134: namely, to prove that deliverance is joined with reconciliation in
the covenant of grace, and therefore that they ought not to be disjoined in
Christ, the author and surety of the same covenant. They say that not even
this comes into controversy. Yet this is no small part of the controversy:
whether Christ appeased divine wrath on behalf of all, yet did not obtain
that the divine law should be inscribed upon the hearts of all—two things
which are joined together in the new covenant (Jer. 31:33–34).
