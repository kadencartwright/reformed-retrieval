ON MIDDLE KNOWLEDGE

BOOK ONE — PART FIFTY-EIGHT

PENOTTUS.

I consider the opposite opinion most true and altogether to be followed: namely, that future morally evil
acts, which are moral sins, cannot be known certainly and infallibly in the divine decree alone, whether it
is a positive decree or only a permissive one. For this is the express mind of Saint Augustine, the holy
Fathers, and Thomas, whose testimonies cannot be expounded otherwise and in another sense without manifest
violence done to the truth. This assertion is proved from the holy Fathers, for among them it is a most
frequent and common manner of speaking that God foreknows all things but does not predestine, or predefine,
all things; and in particular that sins are indeed foreknown by God but are not predestined. Thus the
Council of Valence under Lothair, in chapter 10, says that by predestination God established only those
things which he himself was to do either by gratuitous mercy or just judgment, according to the Scripture
which says, “He made the things which are to be”; but in evil things he foreknew their wickedness because it
is from them, and did not predestine it because it is not from him.

Augustine says in book 2 of *Predestination and Grace*, chapters 5, 6, 13, and 14, in *The Predestination of
the Saints*, chapter 10, in book 6 of the *Hypognosticon* at the end, in *The Articles Falsely Imposed upon
Him*, and very frequently elsewhere that God predestines nothing which he does not foreknow but foreknows
many things which he does not predestine. In particular, in that book *The Articles Falsely Imposed upon
Him*, he says of sins in reply to the eleventh: “Therefore God did not predestine that any such affairs
should come to pass.” The same is found in Fulgentius, book 1 *To Monimus*, chapter 30; Damascene, book 2 *On
the Faith*, final chapter; Bede; and several others to be cited in chapter 16 of the following book. But if
God in no way predestined evil acts, without doubt they neither were nor could have been known in the divine
decree.

The argument is confirmed. Whatever may be the case with that distinction between knowing sins in a
permissive or a positive decree, and in a decree concerning the sin’s natural entity or concerning its
wickedness, it is enough that the holy Fathers say that God does not know sins in his decree and that the
assertion which says that sins are known in the divine decree is contrary to Augustine’s and the holy
Fathers’ usage and manner of speaking.

RESPONSE.

Penottus’s opinion, therefore, is that God does not know sins in his decree alone. Penottus does not deny
that sins can be known in the divine decree, but he denies that they are known in the decree alone. This is
how he began his explanation of his opinion. Yet observe the conclusion of his discussion, for he concludes
thus: “Without doubt sins neither were nor could have been known in the divine decree,” and again: “The
assertion which says that sins are known in the divine decree is contrary to Augustine’s and the holy
Fathers’ usage and manner of speaking.” Therefore, I say, Penottus’s own opinion, which professes that sins
may indeed be known in the divine decree although not in the decree alone, is just as contrary to the holy
Fathers’ usage and manner of speaking as the opinion which he attacks.

And since the present controversy between Penottus and his adversaries turns upon this hinge—namely,
whether morally evil acts are predefined and predetermined by God or are only defined and determined by
the divine will—what ancient Father was ever found to distinguish in such a way as to grant that morally
evil acts are indeed defined and determined by God and his will but are not predefined or predetermined?
Augustine, in *The Good of Perseverance*, chapter 22, speaks thus of predestination: “the fixed judgment of
the will of God.” You can see that, according to Augustine, the fixed judgment of God’s will is the same as
predestination. Unless the Fathers are found to have distinguished in this way, however, they will favor
Penottus no more than his adversaries. Indeed, what if “determined” and “predetermined,” “defined” and
“predefined,” proceed in the same sense among all the Fathers, provided it is agreed that what they call
defined and determined was defined and determined from eternity?

For what else is it to be predestined to life than to be destined to life from eternity, according to the
ancients’ phraseology? They acknowledged predestination not only to eternal life but also to eternal death.
I acknowledge that the ancients so distinguished between foreknowledge and predestination that they said
all things, even evil things, are foreknown by God but are not predestined. I also acknowledge that from
this it became customary in the schools to use “the foreknown” for “the reprobate.” For since names depend
upon convention, they wished to extend predestination only to those things which God himself would one day
do, but not to those which he would permit. Penottus himself acknowledges this, and Augustine’s opinion on
the matter is set out sufficiently clearly in *The Good of Perseverance*, book 2, chapter 17: “To order his
works in his foreknowledge, which cannot be deceived or changed—this, and nothing else whatever, is to
predestine.”

Meanwhile, although through predestination God decrees only that his own works come to pass with himself
doing them, this by no means prevents him also from decreeing through his general providence that our sins
come to pass with himself permitting them. Nor do you deny that even evil things are decreed and determined
by God, and that from eternity; but to make room for those loves of yours by which you incline toward your
Helen—I mean your doctrine of middle knowledge—you only deny that they are predetermined by God. Nowhere
does Augustine deny that sins are ordered by God to come to pass with God himself permitting them. Rather,
he

asserts it when he declares: “Nothing comes to pass unless the omnipotent wills it to come to pass, either by
permitting it to come to pass or by doing it himself.” And what do you think about Pharaoh’s hardening so
that he would not release God’s people, and also about Christ’s crucifixion? Were these not predestined by
God? Acts 4:28.

PENOTTUS.

Second, it is proved by Thomas’s testimonies. In *On Truth*, question 6, article 3, reply 6, he expressly
teaches that predestination is not certain except because it regards the future as present, and so forth.
But more clearly, in question 12, article 10, he proposes this question: “Is all prophecy sufficiently
divided into prophecy of warning, of predestination, and of foreknowledge?” Against this division he
objects to himself with the argument which is third in order: “Since foreknowledge is superior to
predestination, as though placed in its definition, foreknowledge cannot be divided over against
predestination except with respect to those things in which foreknowledge exceeds predestination. But
foreknowledge exceeds predestination in evil things, because there is foreknowledge of them and not
predestination.”

In the body of the article he says among other things: “The fulfillment of the order of causes occurs in two
ways. Sometimes it occurs from the operation of divine power alone, as with the raising of Lazarus and the
conception of Christ. According to this there is prophecy of predestination, because, as Damascene says,
the things which God predestines are not in our power; and hence predestination is called a kind of
preparation by God. A person prepares what he himself is going to do, not what someone else is going to do.
Certain things, however, are fulfilled by the operation of other causes, whether natural or voluntary; and
insofar as they are completed through other causes, they are not predestined, although they are foreknown.”

He openly thinks that human acts, insofar as they proceed from a free secondary cause, are not predestined.
This is the doctrine not only of Damascene in book 2 of *On the Faith*, final chapter, but the most common
doctrine of the holy Fathers. And Thomas responds to the third objection: “It must be said that foreknowledge
is here taken as divided over against predestination with respect to those things in which foreknowledge
exceeds predestination. Yet foreknowledge does not exceed predestination only in evil things, if
predestination is taken strictly, but in all good things which do not come to pass by divine power alone,”
and so forth.

We therefore have openly from the doctrine of Thomas and Damascene that free acts, not only evil but also
good insofar as they depend upon free choice, are not predefined or foreknown in the divine decree, and that
foreknowledge of them exceeds predestination. Those who wish may therefore see how well the doctrine
conforms which, on Thomas’s way, maintains that morally evil acts are foreknown by God in his efficacious
divine decree.

RESPONSE.

The former passage has already been answered in the preceding chapter, and it has been shown how little it
supports Penottus’s cause, although he gloried in it not a little. But he glories still more—or rather grows
insolent—over the passage immediately following, *On Truth*, question 12, article 10, reply 3. I have already
said that the ancients take predestination only insofar as it signifies the divine decree concerning the
futurition of those things which God himself is going to do, but not of those things which he is only going
to permit. It is therefore less surprising if Aquinas, following the ancients, maintained that foreknowledge
is superior to predestination, since foreknowledge also concerns evil things whereas predestination does
not.

As for those words from the body of the article which Penottus advises us to note—“And insofar as they are
completed through other causes, they are not predestined”—I cannot sufficiently marvel at the man’s lack of
foresight when he does not observe that the very things upon which he chiefly relies go for the throat of
his own cause. I shall presently show this, and from Aquinas in this very article. First, therefore, Penottus
concludes from this that Thomas openly thinks that human acts, insofar as they proceed from a free secondary
cause, are not predefined. But who does not see, I ask, that Thomas affirms this no more of the effects of
free agents than of the effects of necessary agents? “Certain things,” he says, “are fulfilled by the
operation of other causes, whether natural or voluntary; and insofar as they are completed through other
causes, they are not predestined.”

Accordingly, by whatever reasoning he thought Thomas could be credited with denying that the effects of
natural causes are predefined by God, by an equal analogy of reasoning one may also credit him with denying
that the effects of free causes are predestined by God. Again, where in Thomas, at
last, does there occur a distinction between effects as either defined or predefined by God, or as either
determined or predetermined by God? Or where, in accordance with the preceding distinction, is there any
such resolution by which the effects of free causes are held to be defined and determined by God, but not
predefined or predetermined, according to Aquinas’s opinion? Unless Penottus does this, Thomas in this
passage will hinder your cause no less than the cause of your adversaries.

Nevertheless, you proceed by adding that Damascene’s opinion is also the same, as though you either did not
know or were concealing how Thomas himself thought Damascene ought to be answered. In the first part,
question 23, article 1, Thomas asks whether men are predestined by God and introduces an argument to the
contrary from Damascene in these words: “It appears that men are not predestined by God. For Damascene says
in book 2, chapter 30: It must be recognized that God indeed foreknows all things but does not predetermine
all things. For he foreknows the things which are in our power but does not predetermine them. Human merits
and demerits are in our power insofar as through free choice we are masters of our acts. Therefore, the
things which pertain to merit or demerit are not predestined by God.”

See immediately how Thomas responds: “To the first, therefore, it must be said that Damascene calls the
imposition of necessity ‘predetermination,’ as occurs in natural things which are predetermined to one
outcome. This is clear from what he adds: For God neither wills wickedness nor compels virtue.” Alvarez too
applies this answer to that passage of Damascene and draws it from this passage of Thomas, though Penottus
splendidly conceals this. No wonder: he had sensed himself far too unequal to refuting it, for he does not
refrain from railing at Alvarez whenever his strength permits and opportunity is given.

From here he proceeds to Aquinas’s response, which indeed grants that foreknowledge exceeds predestination,
and that not only in evil things but in all good things which do not come to pass by divine power alone. At
this point he carries on triumphantly, adding that this doctrine was never retracted by Thomas and
magisterially passing judgment upon all who pull Thomas into an opinion different from the one he advances
here, according to Penottus’s interpretation. “Those who wish,” he says, “may therefore see how well the
doctrine conforms which, on Thomas’s way, maintains that morally evil acts are foreknown by God in his
efficacious divine decree.” Come, then, let us put the brake upon this man of Novara. Aquinas says this of
predestination not simply, but with this qualification: “If predestination is taken strictly.” What this
means

[Book I, printed p. 138; BSB canvas 00164]

I shall show, and from Aquinas in this very article. For when he denied that the effects of other causes are
predestined by God, he added this qualification: “insofar as they are completed through other causes.” This
applied to the effects of both natural and free causes, because the effects even of naturally acting causes
can be impeded. But according to Aquinas these same effects, not only of natural but also of free causes, are
predestined insofar as they depend not upon secondary causes alone but also upon the first cause.

Thomas himself indicates this in the words immediately following, namely in the response to the fourth
argument. “To the fourth,” he says, “it must be said that our merit is both from grace and from free choice.
It is not, however, subject to predestination except insofar as it is from grace, which is from God alone.
Hence what is from our choice is said to be subject to predestination accidentally.” Therefore, although
according to Thomas our good works, which he calls merits, are not predestined insofar as they are from free
choice or are completed through free choice, nevertheless according to Thomas himself they are also
predestined insofar as they are from grace.

In the same way, although our evil deeds or sins are not subject to God’s general will or to the divine
decree insofar as they are completed through our free choice, nevertheless insofar as the substance of
their act is from God’s operation—for according to Thomas the action of sin is from God—nothing prevents them
in this respect from both being subject to God’s decree and being known in the decree according to the same
respect. Thomas himself signifies this in the same article, in the response to the twelfth, with these
words: “To the twelfth it must be said that the superior causes, which are the grounds of things in divine
foreknowledge, never fail in the fulfillment of their effects as inferior causes fail. And therefore the
outcomes of things are known absolutely in the superior causes, but in inferior causes only under a
condition.”

And Penottus has already acknowledged that the divine decree is the cause of being of all future
contingents. By that generality he embraced not only good things but evil things too in the preceding
chapter, number 4. Let these points, then, suffice to restrain Penottus’s insolence and check his luxuriant
rhetoric at this juncture.
