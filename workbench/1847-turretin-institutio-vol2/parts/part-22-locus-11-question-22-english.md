QUESTION XXII.

THE USE OF THE LAW.

What is the use of the moral law, and how many kinds does it have, according
to the various states of the human being? And can it bind to obedience and
punishment at the same time? We distinguish.

I. We have said that four things must especially be considered concerning the
moral law: its *nature, parts, use, and abrogation*. The first two have been
treated thus far. We must now speak of its *use*, without the knowledge and
practice of which all speculation about the law would be fruitless.

[Marginal heading: The use of the law is twofold, absolute and relative. The
absolute use is to be the norm of things to be done and avoided.]

II. A twofold *use of the law* may be established: *absolute and relative*.
The former regards the law in itself; the latter, in relation to the various
states of the human being. The *absolute* use, which obtains in every human
state, is that the law should be the one, full, and certain norm of the things
each person must do and avoid, both toward God and toward one’s neighbor. No
work is truly and properly good and acceptable to God unless it agrees with
the law and is prescribed by it. Whatever things are neither commanded nor
forbidden through it must by their nature be judged indifferent and permitted
to human liberty, unless perhaps this liberty has been restricted by some
positive law.

[Marginal heading: It is proved to be perfect: 1. From the equity of the law.]

III. We gather that this rule of action is most perfect from its various
attributes. First, from its *equity*, because the more equitable a law is,
the more perfect it is; for *equity* is the *mind of the law*. Its equity is
so great that it is the aim, rule, and limit of all laws, and even the nations
most hostile to the Hebrews did not refuse to draw their laws from it.

This is further evident because, unlike human laws, it requires no
correction. No human laws are so equitable that they do not require their own
restrictions or exceptions in some cases, because of the infinity of
particular cases that occur. But there is no case so singular and no
circumstance so extraordinary that some rule and determination for it, either
general or particular, is not given in the Word of God. “The judgments of
Jehovah are truth and are all found righteous together” (Ps. 19:9).

[Marginal heading: 2. From its majesty.]

IV. Second, from its *royal majesty*, which flows from its equity; for the
greater the equity of a law, the greater its authority. Therefore, because
the law of nature, which is the source of all other laws, is most equitable,
it is also most august. James calls it βασιλικὴν, *royal*, in chapter 2,
verse 8. First, because it belongs to the supreme King and Lawgiver, who is
able to save and destroy (James 4:12) and has the power to bind and loose
human consciences. Second, because it is superior to the rest: it judges all
other laws and is judged by none.

[Marginal heading: 3. From its universality.]

V. Third, from its *universality and breadth*. This is gathered first from
its *object*, because it embraces all human duties. Human laws command
external duties, but the law of God commands internal as well as external
duties, so that there is no virtue which is not commanded here and no vice
which is not forbidden.

It is gathered next from the *subject* which the law forms and directs,
because it regulates and orders the whole human being and all human faculties
and functions, addressing the soul no less than the body. It is gathered,
finally, from its *end*, because it intends nothing less than to bring it
about that the human being should adhere to God and live blessedly.

[Marginal heading: 4. From its immutability.]

VI. Fourth, from its *immutability*, because it remains forever and is never
dissolved (Ps. 119:89; Matt. 5:18). Human laws are often mutable because of
excessive severity or narrowness. Hence the philosopher said ingeniously that
the laws of princes must be fitted to the times, just as shoes are fitted to
feet, because the smallest variation in circumstances or accidents sometimes
requires a variation in the laws.

But this cannot apply to the law of God, because he foresees all the most
contingent circumstances and all the cases that can occur. He therefore
orders his laws so that they embrace every case and every circumstance.

[Marginal heading: 5. From its holiness.]

VII. Fifth, from its *holiness*, because it is a most limpid image of God.
For God has so depicted his character in his law that anyone can contemplate
in it, as in a mirror, vivid and accurate images of God’s holiness.

This is further evident both from the *end* of the law, which tends toward
this—that it should cause the internal principles and external operations of
human beings to cohere with God by the bond of holiness—and from its
*effects*. For the law of God, inspired by the Holy Spirit and inscribed on
the heart, is a most efficacious instrument of holiness. Hence in Psalm
119:140 the Word of God is called אמרתך צרופה מאד, *very thoroughly
purified*, and in Psalm 19:8 the *precept of Jehovah* is called *pure*.

[Marginal heading: The relative use. In the instituted state.]

VIII. The *relative use* is manifold according to the different states of
the human being. *In the state instituted in innocence*, the law was the
contract of the covenant of works initiated with the human being and the
means of obtaining life and felicity according to the promise added to the
law. For although express mention is not made of a promise given to
obedience, but only of a punishment, the promise is sufficiently inferred
from the punishment. It is not credible that God threatened punishment to
the first parent if he sinned but added no promise of reward if he obeyed, or
that he promised less by way of reward than he threatened by way of
punishment. Indeed, the tree of life itself sealed to him that eternal life
would be conferred, which is opposed to the eternal death denounced together
with temporal death (Rom. 6:23), as was proved in Part I, Locus VIII,
Question 5.

[Marginal heading: In the destitute state.]

IX. Second, *in the destitute state of sin*, the use of the law cannot be
justification, because the law has been made weak through the flesh.
Accordingly, it is not said to have been given for life, but *because of
transgressions* (Gal. 3:19). Rather, the use of the law is threefold.

1. Its first use is *conviction*, when it leads a human being to the knowledge
of sin and reproves and convicts him of his guilt: “through the law comes
knowledge of sin” (Rom. 3:20). Thus it has the character of a *mirror* in
which we behold our stains.

2. Its second use is *restraint*, by which it restrains and represses human
beings through its commands and threats. Those who are touched by no concern
for justice and right unless compelled are at least restrained by fear of
punishments when they hear its dire sanctions. In this sense it is like a
*bridle* that confines sinners within the bounds of external discipline so
that the world does not turn into a den of robbers. In this respect Paul says
that “the law is not laid down for the righteous, but for the wicked and
ἀτάκτοις, the disorderly, for the ungodly and sinners,” and so forth (1 Tim.
1:9).

To this belongs another use of the law by accident, namely, the *irritation*
and increase of sin. Insofar as the law restrains sin like a bridle, it not
only fails to tame sin’s rebellion and stubbornness but increases and
provokes them further because of the sinner’s malice, which strives after
what is forbidden. Hence the law is said to have been given *so that sin
might abound* (Rom. 5), and elsewhere, in Romans 7, sin is said through it to
become καθ’ ὑπερβολὴν ἁμαρτωλόν, *sinful beyond measure*.

3. Its third use is *condemnation*, when it announces the divine curse to the
convicted sinner through the dreadful sentence it thunders in his ears:
*cursed is the one who has not continued*, and so forth. Hence the Apostle
calls it *the ministry of condemnation* (2 Cor. 3:9), and in Romans 4 he says
that it *produces wrath*. It then acts like a *scourge*, tormenting and
harassing consciences guilty of sin.

[Marginal heading: In the restored state.]

X. Third, *in the restored state of grace*, the law has various uses with
respect to the elect, both before their conversion and after it. *Beforehand*,
it serves to *convict* and humble a human being so that, having recognized his
misery and powerlessness, he despairs of himself and casts away confidence in
his own righteousness and merits, in order to rest in God’s mercy alone.

Second, it serves to *lead the human being to Christ*. It does this not
primarily and of itself, because the law does not know Christ, but by an
accident of grace. It drives the human being, cast down and despairing of his
own powers, to seek the remedy of saving grace. Under this σχέσει, *relation*,
Paul calls it a *schoolmaster to Christ* (Gal. 3:24), and Christ is called the
*end of the law* (Rom. 10:4). He is not an end of *destruction and
abolition*, because the law remains even in the gospel, as will be said
later, but an end of *perfection and completion*, because Christ had to
fulfill it in himself and in us, and an end of *intention*, because he is the
goal to which it looks and for whose sake the law was given.

This last point is true not by the law’s own nature and on the side of the
thing itself, for in that respect the end of the law was the justification of
a righteous human being. It is true, rather, on the side of God giving the
law to sinful humanity so that the sinner might be prepared for Christ and,
having perceived from the law its ἀσθένειαν, *weakness*, might recognize more
clearly the need for a Mediator and seek him still more ardently.

XI. The law not only prepares the elect person for Christ beforehand, but
*afterward* also *directs* him, now regenerated through Christ, in the ways of
the Lord. It serves him as the most perfect *norm* and rule of life, to which
he knows he has been called through Christ and for which he must strive with
every effort (1 Tim. 1:5). Formerly it was an instrument of the Spirit of
bondage to cast down and crush the human being, but afterward it becomes an
instrument of the Spirit of adoption to advance sanctification.

Thus the law leads to Christ, and Christ leads us back to the law. The law
leads to Christ as Redeemer, and Christ leads to the law as the guide and
director of life. In this respect, the *integral* and righteous human being
would have been under the law’s *blessing*, the *corrupt* human being lies
under its *curse*, and the *regenerate* human being lies under its
*direction*. This use of the law will be treated more fully in Question 23.

XII. To this belongs the question about the obligation of the law: whether it
binds to obedience and punishment at the same time. The occasion for it arose
from Cargius’s opinion that only Christ’s passive righteousness is imputed to
us, but not likewise his active righteousness. His reason was that, since the
law did not bind sinners to obedience but only to punishment, Christ,
substituted in our place, owed only punishment for us and not obedience.

To establish the state of the question, note first that it is not asked in
general whether the law binds to obedience and punishment. For since it
commands obedience under a promise of reward and forbids disobedience under
a threat of punishment, it is evident that the human being is bound to both.
The question concerns *the manner*: whether it binds to them simultaneously
or successively, and in what order.

Second, it is not asked whether in every state the law binds simultaneously
to both. In the integral state, the law bound to obedience but not to
punishment, because it would be unjust to bind to punishment one who had not
sinned. In the state of grace, it binds the believer only to obedience and
not to punishment, because there is no condemnation for those who are in
Christ (Rom. 8:1) and whose iniquities have been forgiven (Ps. 32:1). The
question concerns the *state of sin*: whether in that state the sinful human
being is bound to both. This we affirm.

XIII. First, this is proved from the twofold condition of the sinful human
being, who is both a creature and a sinful creature. As a *creature*, he owes
obedience to God forever and can never be dispensed from it; as a *sinner*,
he owes punishment. He is bound to the former because of a present and future
debt, and to the latter because of past disobedience; to the former primarily
and of itself, and to the latter secondarily and by accident because of sin.

XIV. Second, if the law does not bind the sinner to obedience,

then through the human being’s sin God would forfeit his right to require
obedience from him. And by not obeying, the human being would not sin, because
where there is no law, there is no transgression.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XV. The reasoning is not the same for the integral and corrupt states. In the
former, the human being had only one σχέσις, *relation*, namely, as a rational
creature who owed obedience to the Creator. But in the latter another
relation, guilt through sin, was added to that first relation. Hence, although
in the first state the law bound only to obedience, so that by rendering
obedience the human being would be free from punishment, it does not follow
likewise that *in* the state of sin one who has become liable to punishment is
released from obedience. Because both—each a σχέσις, *relation*—have a place
in the sinner, both obligations must be joined here. Rebellious subjects,
worthy of punishment because of their rebellion, do not cease to remain bound
to perpetual obedience to the magistrate.

XVI. Although the law binds the sinful human being to punishment and
obedience simultaneously, it does not follow that he is bound to pay the same
debt twice. The law binds him to present and future obedience, while
punishment is inflicted only for sin already committed and for the act by
which obedience was once violated.

XVII. When the sin of omission is said to be expiated through suffering
punishment, this must not be understood as though the sin had not been
committed and as though the human being had done everything he was bound to
perform, which would contradict the truth. Rather, through penal suffering he
is freed from the curse resting upon him because of sin, and such a
punishment can no longer be imposed upon him to be borne. The remission given
to him therefore removes the actual guilt, but it does not thereby give a
right to life.

XVIII. The obligation to obedience is either *natural*, which the human being
owes to God as a rational creature and which is eternal and indissoluble and
remains even in heaven, or *covenantal*. Under the latter, someone is bound
not only to render obedience to the law but to fulfill it under the σχέσει,
*relation*, of the covenant in order to merit eternal life, either for
himself or for another. Under the former obligation, Christ as a human being
owed obedience to God for himself. But he was not bound under the latter
except for us, because he had no need through it to merit for himself the
eternal life which he possessed by virtue of the hypostatic union. This will
be demonstrated in its own place when Christ’s obedience is treated.
