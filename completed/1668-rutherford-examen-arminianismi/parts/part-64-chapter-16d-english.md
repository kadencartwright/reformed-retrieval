It is asked whether the distinction of presbyters into teaching presbyters and
ruling presbyters is a novel invention. The Remonstrants say that it is in
*Apologia*, chapter 21, folio 228.

We deny it. 1. Because Scripture expressly recognizes ruling presbyters.
Romans 12:7–8 distinguishes prophets into teachers and pastors, and
distinguishes ruling presbyters from both. Verse 7: “Let the one whose office
is ministry attend to his ministry; and the one who is a teacher, to his
teaching”—for this belongs to the teacher. Verse 8: “And the one who exhorts,
to exhortation”—this is the pastor—“and the one who gives, to giving; and the
one who presides, with diligence.” This last is the ruling presbyter, who is
distinguished from deacons and ministers of the treasury. 1 Corinthians 12:28:
“And God has appointed some in the church: first apostles, then prophets,
third teachers, then powers, then gifts of healings, helps, governments, kinds
of tongues.” If “governments”—that is, guardians of ecclesiastical discipline,
or elders—are distinguished from apostles, prophets, and teachers, then ruling
presbyters are of divine institution. So in Acts 15:23, at the Jerusalem synod,
the apostles, presbyters, and brethren made the determination and subscribed
it: “By their hands they wrote: The apostles, presbyters, and brethren, to the
brethren at Antioch and in Syria and Cilicia

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 690 | PDF p. 739 | split word joined: qui]

who are from the Gentiles, greetings.” Nor can one person in a large assembly
devote himself at once to the Word of God and prayers—which belongs to
pastors—and at the same time direct the care of everyone's administration as
he ought. Acts 6:2–3.

4. And if “a gift has been given to each person, as God wills, for
edification,” 1 Corinthians 12:7, then those to whom the gift of government is
given have it for the office of governing. For many excel in the gift of
government who cannot teach. And 1 Timothy 5:17 says: “Let the presbyters who
preside well be counted worthy of double honor, especially those who labor in
the Word and doctrine.” From this we conclude that there are two kinds of
presbyters. Some only preside well; others also labor in doctrine. That is,
some only rule, and these are worthy of honor; but others also teach and labor
in the Word, and these are worthy of far greater honor.

The Remonstrants say that Paul means nothing other than that pastors endowed
with the gift of governing are worthy of double honor, but that those who not
only possess that gift but also labor earnestly in teaching and preaching are
much more worthy of double honor. It does not follow from this, they say, that
there are two kinds of presbyters.

To the contrary: 1. This exposition might perhaps stand if Paul had said in
general that a presbyter is worthy of honor, and much more one who is active
and diligent in preaching. But Paul speaks specifically of those “who preside
well” as one kind,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 691 | PDF p. 740 | split word joined: indè]

and then enumerates another kind, namely, presbyters who labor in the Word.

2. The phrase “who preside well” is not a distinguishing expression but, as
the Leiden Professors note in their *Censure*, chapter 21, section 5, it is an
indication of a shared office. For they are worthy of double honor in
comparison with widows—whom Paul says are to be honored—and the rest of the
ordinary elderly, or presbyters in age. 1 Timothy 5:1, 3.

3. On this interpretation, those “not laboring in the Word” but only presiding
well as bishops—as Cajetan, with the Roman Catholics, expounds the passage—
would be worthy of double honor. Thus silent dogs would have to be honored
with double honor, which is absurd. But to preside well includes laboring in
the Word, as in Hebrews 13:17: “Obey those who preside over you.” In Acts
20:28, presiding over the flocks includes faithfully feeding the flock, as a
whole includes its part. For no one presides well over a household unless he
uses the Word of God as a key opening and shutting the kingdom of God. And on
this account Paul would say the same thing twice: “Those who labor well in the
Word are worthy of double honor; and not only they, but those who labor in the
Word are worthy of double honor.”

4. The Greek *kopian*, “to labor,” does not necessarily signify weariness in
laboring, but positively signifies labor. 1 Corinthians 3:8: “According to his
own labor.” 1 Corinthians 15:58: “Because your labor is not in vain.” 1
Thessalonians

1:3: “And the labor of love.” Certainly, Whitaker says, if I say that all
