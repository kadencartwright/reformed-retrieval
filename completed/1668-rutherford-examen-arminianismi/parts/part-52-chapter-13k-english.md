ACTS 2:21.

And it shall come to pass that whoever calls upon the name of the Lord shall
be saved.

It is asked whether the promise of salvation attaches immediately only to
final perseverance and only to total faith—that is, insofar as it endures to
the end—so that faith is not graciously judged worthy of the reward of eternal
life insofar as it is essentially living and true faith, but insofar as it
endures to the end. The Remonstrants affirm this in article 5, p. 189. We deny
it.

1. Eternal life is promised to a true believer at the very end of life, even
at the twelfth—that is, the last—hour, and to the thief who believed late,
even if he has faith for only one day or a few hours, according to the saying,
“He who believes has eternal life.”

2. Partial faith—that is, faith which lasts for a short time, as distinguished
from the Arminians' total faith which endures to the end—does not differ in
essence and species from total faith, as the adversaries teach. Therefore, if
someone were to die in that faith which endures for a short time,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 600 | PDF p. 649]

either he would be saved, according to the saying, “He who believes will be
saved,” and thus we have what was to be proved; or he would perish eternally,
and thus someone who savingly believes in Christ would perish, which is
contrary to the whole Gospel.

3. It follows from this doctrine that the promise of eternal life is not made
to faith from grace and through God's gracious good pleasure, but because of
faith's meritorious duration and rootedness in its subject. Faith that endures
for a shorter time is wholly insufficient to obtain the reward of eternal
life, and total faith alone is graciously judged worthy of the reward of
eternal life. This also conflicts with their own doctrine. In their *Apology*,
chapter 18, fol. 182, they teach that temporary believers, who are revocably
elect, “are most properly and in reality elect to share in the life promised
in the Gospel for the time during which they believe.” Therefore, by virtue
of the divine promise and Christ's merits, they have a right to eternal life
for that time.

4. According to this assertion, if sudden death overtakes a person who has
truly believed in Christ for seventy or eighty years, through a chance
contingency antecedent to God's decree, and at an hour in which he does not
believe, he must pay eternal penalties in hell for an hour's unbelief and be
deprived of the reward of the saving faith which he had throughout his life.
On the other hand, if someone who does not believe but has lived in unbelief
for seventy or eighty years nevertheless believes for the brief space of an
hour before death, his one-hour faith will be crowned with the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 601 | PDF p. 650 | split word joined: præmio]

reward of eternal life. Meanwhile, this entire dispensation occurs according
to created free will, which has not been determined by God to believe either
from eternity or in time, but which operates fortuitously and contingently and
is changeable, slippery, and more easily turned than chariot wheels and a
revolving hinge. It necessarily follows that no one will be certain of the
full remission of his sins or of eternal glory. No one will be truly
justified, no one clothed with Christ's righteousness, and no one truly elect
to glory in this life. This will certainly torment the devout souls of
believers with perpetual fluctuation and deprive them of every living hope,
certain confidence, solid peace of conscience, stable joy of the Holy Spirit,
and pure and living consolation.

Although the Remonstrants think that they must fight for the apostasy of the
saints as for their altars and hearths, they nevertheless leave the following
as a problem disputable on either side in *Scriptum Synodale*, p. 190: “Might
God sometimes, by his absolute right and in some extraordinary manner, reward
certain believers who have been exercised long and much in the pursuit of
godliness and tried by temptations as by fire with the gift of indefectible
perseverance?” We, however, believe this to be by no means disputable.

1. God graciously gives all the regenerate the grace of indefectible
perseverance.

2. Scripture acknowledges no gift which was not purchased by the merits of
Christ, who establishes all his people until the day of his glorious advent
and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 602 | PDF p. 651 | split word joined: confirmantis]

confirms them, Ephesians 1:3. Therefore, there is no absolute right of this
kind here with respect to certain particular believers.

3. The reward of those who have been exercised for a long time in the pursuit
of godliness and tried by temptations conflicts with their reward by absolute
right. For every reward of their obedience is from strict justice, but strict
justice and absolute right conflict with each other. Otherwise God could, by
his absolute right, reward newly regenerated infants with indefectible
perseverance, which the Arminians would not concede. Indeed, the apologists
acknowledge as a problem in chapter 17, fol. 161, that “God reserves to himself
the complete right to give certain people, whose work he wills to use for the
conversion of others, an extraordinary and irresistible calling.” Hence, for
the Arminians the teaching of Calvin and the Reformed churches is a disputable
problem and indeed a very probable opinion:

1. That, given indefectible and absolute perseverance and therefore absolute
election to glory, it is highly probable that the Reformed churches teach
rightly and soundly.

2. That evangelical exhortations, commands, threats, and promises may rightly
be set before prophets, apostles, and others who have been exercised long and
much in the pursuit of godliness. Thus God may rightly threaten eternal death
to those whom he has determined to preserve for eternal life by the gift of
indefectible perseverance and the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 603 | PDF p. 652]

irresistible operation of grace—although they condemn this in our doctrine.
God may also use the hope of eternal life as an argument morally to entice
those who are nevertheless confirmed by the gift of indefectible
perseverance; and God may command those to persevere who nevertheless cannot
fail to persevere because of the power of grace's irresistible operation,
which they also condemn in our teaching.

3. That it is a very probable doctrine that prophets, apostles, and others
who persevere indefectibly both sin most freely and persevere most freely, yet
persevere and obey through the irresistible and indefectible action of grace.

4. That the Christian religion remains intact even if people are said to sin
freely and yet, by the necessity of God's decree, cannot fail to sin; and to
obey freely and yet, by the necessity of the decree and of the saving grace of
regeneration operating irresistibly, cannot fail to obey.

5. That the opinion of absolute election without the foreseeing of faith and
merits, and of absolute reprobation from God's pure good pleasure, is
probable.

4. According to the doctrine of the Arminians, it follows concerning such
confirmed apostles and others endowed with indefectible perseverance: 1. They
cannot lie, steal, perjure themselves, act unjustly, or fall into sins which
devastate the conscience. 2. It follows that such people are

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 604 | PDF p. 653]

subject to no law, no command, and no promises or threats of the law or the
Gospel. 3. They have no need to fear God or guard themselves from sins and the
snares of temptations from the world, the flesh, and the devil, but may sleep
securely on both ears and neglect prayer, diligence, and care to work out
salvation with fear and trembling. 4. For such people it is useless and
ridiculous to confess sins; to lament and grieve with Paul in Romans 7 over
indwelling sin; to seek the forgiveness of sins; to hope for eternal life
provided that they persevere to the end; and to pray for Christ's grace by
which they may persevere. Yet the Remonstrants object all these consequences,
and many others, against our doctrine of the saints' absolute perseverance.

It is asked whether, in the divine judgment, the person perseveres in faith
who, first, does not sin against the clear and open dictate of either natural
reason or supernatural revelation, and, second, applies moderate diligence to
correct and amend his infirmities, to which each and every person is more or
less subject according to constitution, age, location, and other
circumstances. The Remonstrants affirm this in these words in article 5,
p. 189. We deny it.

1. They suppose that every sin against the clear light of conscience,
together with a sin of infirmity which someone does not resist, makes an
apostate. In that case those who speak idle words, lie in jest, and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 605 | PDF p. 654 | split word joined: lacessunt]

insult their brother without a pang of conscience over such sins would be
apostates in the divine judgment. Indeed, the very same person who believes
savingly would become an apostate a thousand times in a year, even in a month.

2. Neither idolatry in the Indies nor adultery among nations which hold wives
in common and believe such unions to be lawful would be sins that devastate
the conscience and shake out saving faith. For among such people the dictate
of neither natural reason nor supernatural revelation is clear and open;
rather, their mind and conscience is polluted, Titus 1:15. Those who kill the
apostles and the most faithful pastors of Jesus Christ think that in this they
serve God, John 16:1–2, and they perpetrate such sins with no protesting
dictate of conscience. Yet such sins, if they were found in the regenerate,
would truly make them apostates. Therefore, someone who does not sin against
the clear and open light of conscience can be an apostate.

3. In the regenerate they acknowledge no principle of sin except
constitution, age, location, and other circumstances, although in everyone
there is indwelling sin, the old man, the flesh, and concupiscence perpetually
rising against the Spirit of God.

4. According to the adversaries, pagans can for a time neither sin against
conscience nor against supernatural revelation; and Cicero, Seneca, Aristides,
and others did this in their

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 606 | PDF p. 655 | split word joined: moralibus]

moral virtues, as they teach. According to them, pagans could also apply
moderate diligence in amending the infirmities of constitution, age, and
location. Therefore, in the divine judgment pagans persevere in true faith for
a time.
