Verse 29. Who has woe? Who has alas? Who has contentions? Who has talkativeness? Who has wounds inflicted with impunity? Who has redness of eyes?

[1632 witness | PDF p. 527 | printed columns 1038-1039]

Verse 30. Those who linger over wine, those who go in search of mixed wine.

Verse 31. Do not look at wine when it shows itself red, when it displays its eye in the cup, when it moves continually along straight courses.

Verse 32. At its end it will bite like a serpent and sting like a haemorrhois snake.

Verse 33. Your eyes will see strange women, and your heart will speak perverse things.

Verse 34. And you will be like someone lying in the middle of the sea, and like someone lying at the top of a mast.

Verse 35. They struck me, you will say, but I was not hurt; they beat me, but I did not know it. When shall I awake? I will go on; I will seek it yet again.

In עינו (*its eye*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for its subject, the colour. In תהפכות (*perverse things*) there is a metonymy of the efficient cause. In לב (*heart*) there is a metaphor for the middle. In ידעתי (*I knew*) there is a metonymy of the effect.

The purpose is to deter us from drunkenness by enumerating the evils that arise from it.

In these verses the discourse is:
- Figurative, verses 29–30
- Plain in the remaining verses

In the figurative discourse, consisting of a question and an answer, there are set out:
- The adjuncts of drunkenness, verse 29
- Its subject, verse 30

Among the adjuncts there are considered:
- The common, or general
- The particular

In the more general ones the reward and wages of drunkards are described, both in this life and especially in the life to come: sorrow, namely, groaning, weeping, and wailing. They indeed gather together to make themselves merry. Hence it has now become thoroughly customary to cloak the foul disgrace of drunkenness with the respectable name of merriment. But woe to that merriment which drags after it so great a force and abundance of sorrow, as though tied to its tail! A curse upon that laughter which is accompanied by so great a throng of pains as is most fully set before our eyes by the repetition of these interjections expressing the torments of the soul.

If they are led to repentance for the crime committed, which in this evil is most earnestly to be desired, sorrow nevertheless awaits them for as long as they live here, from having offended their brethren and from their guilt in violating the divine law. Although its sting is broken and blunted by confidence in the forgiveness of sins, they still cannot fail to grieve that they have been so ungrateful and undutiful toward God, so kind and merciful a Father, who drew them from the dunghill of a most foul vice and admitted them into the kingdom of his only Son. But if they persist in their drunkenness, for every moment of merriment they will mourn and wail through endless beginnings of years; for every, I do not say tankard, but even the smallest drop of wine, they will drain and drink down full casks of the wine of divine wrath. Even in this life they sometimes drink beforehand a few cups of it so unpleasant that they would most gladly, if it were possible, free themselves from it by paying out all the money they possess and handing over their estates.

Next the prophet descends to the particular evils, which are also causes of the preceding calamities. The first is that drunkards are prone to disputes and brawls. Hence intoxicating drink is said to be tumultuous, Proverbs 20:1. For since pride, with which drunken men habitually swell and puff themselves up, is the parent of contentions, it is no wonder if they readily leap into

[1632 witness | PDF p. 528 | printed columns 1040-1041]

disputes and fights. Next, since they are inflamed with wine, Isaiah 5:11, that heat with which they seethe within drives them into battles at the slightest impulse. Moreover, just as their inclination toward disputes is great, so their weakness in defending themselves is no less. When this becomes known, others are more eager to enter into a fight with them; and someone who never dared to engage a sober enemy will not hesitate to come to blows with that same enemy when he is overcome by drink.

Talkativeness holds the second place among the particular evils. How great it is is abundantly clear from common perception and daily experience. But if sin will not be absent from the many words of those who are sober, Ecclesiastes 5:2, how much less from those of drunkards?

The third is that he receives wounds with impunity, that is, unavenged. Thus Zimri slew his master when he was buried in wine, 1 Kings 16:9–10. Next, besides the unjust violence to which he exposes himself, he is also liable to the most just penalties of salutary laws. These make him liable first to a beating with rods, and then, if he persists in drunkenness, to death, Deuteronomy 21:18, 19, 20, 21. Finally, he frequently incurs injury from some accident.

By the fourth, namely, redness of eyes, Genesis 49:12, he indicates by synecdoche all bodily disfigurements and diseases to which drunkards render themselves liable, such as dropsy, paralysis, and pustules breaking out on the face itself. Yet all these are not such inseparable companions of drunkenness that they cannot vary according to the varieties of wines and men. For it is commonly said that drunkenness makes some men raging mad, others like swine, and some mute and speechless. It is likely, however, that Solomon enumerated those fruits of drunkenness which Judaea was accustomed to produce according to the nature of its wine and its people.

Verse 30. So much, then, for the adjuncts of drunkenness comprised in the question. Let us now consider its subject in the answer. The subject of drunkenness, or the drunken man, is depicted by two effects. The first is that he lingers over wine. For although the Apostle teaches in 1 Timothy 4:3 that we ought to partake of food and drink, recalling us to moderation by the word “partaking,” this man sets himself no limit in drinking. And although our lifetime is confined within such narrow bounds of time that it is not without reason compared to a measure of handbreadths, to sleep, to an uttered word, and indeed to a thought, which ought to admonish us to take particular care to employ our time rightly, this man is continually drinking, as though his life were to be prolonged for several thousand years. Finally, although labour and occupation are incumbent upon us by God's appointment, and the use of wine has been granted so that we may attend to our affairs more eagerly and energetically, the drunkard reverses the course of nature and of the divine institution. He practises drinking as though it were a craft and an occupation, while undertaking labour and the management of his affairs for no other reason than that they may supply the expense of his guzzling. It is therefore characteristic of the drunkard to prolong his drinking, to consume hours, days, and nights in it, as may be confirmed from Isaiah 6:12.

The second effect is that he zealously seeks out and searches for the finest wine, compounded with various spices. In the first effect, therefore, he describes the wine which the drunkard swallows from its adjunct, quantity; in this second, from its quality and excellence. Isaiah also touches upon this matter in a single word in 5:11.

Here two things are considered:
- The exhortation against the immoderate use of wine
- The reasons by which that exhortation is supported

[1632 witness | PDF p. 529 | printed columns 1042-1043]

Here we have the following concerning the immoderate use of wine:
- Causes
- Effects

In the exhortation the causes are assigned, because in the cup it:
- Glows red
- Leaps

In the verb of looking, however, there is a synecdoche of the genus for a more intent and firmly fixed gaze, by which the appetite of the soul is kindled. For we are not altogether forbidden to look at the colour of wine or its movement in the cup, but to look at it with our eyes so fixed upon it that we are carried away by love of it. This can be gathered and concluded from similar passages of Scripture. It is not simply unlawful for a married man to look at another man's wife or a maiden, but to do so in such a way as to lust after her, Matthew 5:28; Job 31:1. We are forbidden to look upon the afflictions and calamities of the Church in such a way that on their account we hate or despise her, Song of Songs 1:5. Otherwise, not only is it lawful to look upon and examine them, but it is expedient. The same can be said of Eve casting her eyes upon the forbidden fruit, of Potiphar's wife upon Joseph, and of Achan upon the Babylonian garment. For they did not sin because they looked, but because their looking was of such a kind that a desire to possess entered their souls from it.

This, therefore, is what we are warned about: we must not supply further incentives and fuel to the appetite for wine, to which we are already more than sufficiently addicted through the depravity of our nature. For since the very sweetness of wine, which we perceive by taste, affords us abundant pleasure, to seek that pleasure from elsewhere and increase it through other aids to stimulation cannot be free from danger. From this it is apparent how far removed from piety and the fear of God are those who swallow salted meats, bacon, and other things seasoned with brine and saltiness for this very purpose: to draw drunkenness toward themselves by these, as though by ropes, and further sharpen a desire for drinking that is excessive in itself. For what else is this but to add drunkenness to thirst, which the Holy Spirit threatens to avenge terribly, Deuteronomy 29:19–20?

Verse 32. So much for the argument from the cause of the immoderate use of wine. There follow arguments from its effects, by which he endeavours to deter us from it.

These are:
- The general argument, verse 32
- The particular arguments in the remaining verses to the end of the chapter

The general argument is that wine taken immoderately is harmful and injurious, indeed lethal and deadly. That double comparison of equals confirms this, warning that this vice of intemperance is to be shunned no less than a serpent's bite and a haemorrhois snake's sting, which everyone recoils from by natural instinct. If the wine-drinkers knew for certain that an adder or a serpent, especially a live one, lay concealed at the bottom of the bowls they drain, they would by no means gather so readily and eagerly to drink down jarfuls. How greatly, then, ought they to recoil from drunkenness, which spits out its poison to the destruction not only of this life but also to eternal ruin!

This injury is described by the adjunct of time: “at its end,” that is, after they have drenched themselves with wine. For as long as they drink energetically, they are suffused with a certain empty and momentary pleasure; but when their work is done, in place of reward and wages they receive the bitterest torments and stinging pains.

Verse 33. Afterwards he passes to the particular evils. Among these the first is baseness of life and foul lust. For besides the fact that those buried in wine are readily impelled, like Lot, to commit shameful acts while unaware and thinking of nothing of the kind, from the immoderate guzzling of wine the most loathsome

[1632 witness | PDF p. 530 | printed columns 1044-1045]

lusts are generated. Hence the Sodomites burned with unspeakable lusts. For by fullness of bread in Ezekiel, excess in drinking is also indicated by synecdoche. Thus Jeremiah says that, when fed, they gaped after other men's wives like stallions. To this is added what the Apostle affirms in Ephesians 5:18, that there is dissipation in wine, and in Romans 13:13, where sexual indulgence and wantonness are joined with feasting and drunkenness. Such, therefore, is the proximity of drunkenness and adultery that, with drunkenness going before, adultery follows immediately. For although some adulterers are not drunkards, you will find few drunkards who are not also adulterers.

He describes the crime of fornication metonymically from its efficient cause, namely, the casting of the eyes upon strange women. This expression does not simply condemn looking at women, but the dishonourable pleasure which someone takes in looking at them, from which shameful speech also arises.

Verse 34. Another effect is disturbance of the brain, as happens when someone is at the top of a mast, where the agitation of the sea is felt most strongly, and the very sight of the sea and the ship's rapid motion dull the senses and even render the mind itself stunned. For just as, when the head is disturbed and the senses impaired, things appear otherwise than they are, and things which seem to spin around are in fact at rest, so through drunkenness the mind and judgement are injured to such a degree that they cannot determine what is true and what is false. Hence the use of wine has been forbidden to priests discharging their office and to kings about to sit upon the throne of judgement, Proverbs 31:4–5. Yet the agitation of the sea and the immoderate use of wine are altogether different in this respect: in people tossed by the waves, although the mind and understanding may for a time be so disturbed by dizziness that they cannot carry out their operations, nevertheless, once the superfluous humour and harmful vapour have been discharged, the mind soon not only returns to its former state but is rendered even more purified and cleared, and better fitted to perform all its operations. Drunkards, however, never entirely recover the former powers of their intellect, memory, and judgement. Hence in Hosea 4:11 wine and so forth are said to make men senseless.

Verse 35. The third effect is insensibility, of the body as well as of the soul, of external as well as internal perception. For just as, because of the disturbance of his mind, he cannot see even the evil hanging over his head, much less escape it, so also the senses of his body are so dulled that, although surrounded by evils, he is nevertheless touched by no perception of them. And thus, through lack of reason, he becomes worse and more ignoble than men, and through lack of sense, than the beasts themselves. But just as it is a mark of constancy and courage to bear pains and wounds patiently, so to feel them not at all belongs to a log, not a man. Hence Christ refused that drink compounded with myrrh, because it would either have removed the perception of the pains he was to endure for our sake, or at least have mitigated it.

The last effect, and the most loathsome of all, is that when he has slept off the wine, he returns to it, like a dog to its vomit or a sow to its wallowing-place. For he is so subjected to the tyrannical dominion of drunkenness, like a worthless slave, that although he cannot fail to feel the troubles he suffered while drunk, nevertheless through the habit of drinking and the impulse of a certain beastly pleasure he is carried toward intemperance in drinking with the same, indeed a greater, impetus than before. Consequently he can be drawn from this muddy pool of drunkenness only by the mighty and outstretched hand of divine mercy. We must therefore see to it that we withdraw our foot from this vice in good time, lest by lingering too long we contract a habit and either become unwilling to leave it or unable to do so. For drunkenness is more dangerous than most other sins, and in this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 531 | printed columns 1046-1047]

respect even than adultery itself: as age advances, the passion for drinking increases daily from the dryness of the body, whereas adulterers worn out by old age are unfit for impure intercourse, even if they greatly desire it.

### Translator's notes

- Verses 29–35 form a single grouped commentary in the witness. The unit begins with the verse-29 lemma at the foot of printed column 1037 and ends at the top of column 1046, immediately before the rule and *CAPUT XXIV.* The bracketed analytical schemes are represented as lists, retaining their divisions and order. No Chapter Twenty-Four text is included.
- The verse-31 lemma preserves the literal images of the wine's “eye” and its movement “along straight courses” (*ad rectitudines*). Cartwright identifies the “eye” with colour and later describes the wine in the cup as red and leaping. The translation does not replace these images with a single modern paraphrase. *Haemorrhois* is retained as the source's name for a venomous snake, without assigning it a modern species. The later *quadrantalia* denotes large measures of wine and is rendered “jarfuls.”
- Printed column 1039 reads *infinita annorum initia*, literally “endless beginnings of years,” not *millia*, “thousands.” The unusual printed wording is retained in the transcription and translated without silently emending it. Its context is the duration of punishment contrasted with individual moments of pleasure.
- Printed column 1041 gives *Isai. 6. 12.* The citation is preserved as Isaiah 6:12; Isaiah 56:12 appears to be the intended passage about wine-drinking continuing into the next day. Other citations are likewise retained as printed, including Ecclesiastes 5:2 and 1 Timothy 4:3.
- Historical medical explanations and moral claims, including the account of seasickness, lasting intellectual injury, bodily dryness in old age, and the relation between drunkenness and adultery, are translated as Cartwright's assertions. They are not supplied as present-day medical advice.
