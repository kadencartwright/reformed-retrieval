QUESTION III

Was Sacred Scripture Written Only Incidentally, Without a Divine
Command?—Negative, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. This question is disputed between us and the Roman Catholics. In order to
diminish the authority and perfection of Scripture, they teach not only that
it was less necessary and that the church could have lacked it, but also that
it was delivered to the church not by an express command of God, but only on
particular occasions. They teach that Christ did not command the apostles to
write, and that the apostles did not think about writing the gospel with a
primary intention, but only with a secondary one and from an occasion,
Bellarmine, *On the Word of God* 4.3–4.

II. The question is not whether the sacred writers were impelled to write by
certain occasions. For we do not deny that they often made use of occasions
presented to them in order to commit the mysteries of God to writings. Rather,
the question is whether they wrote so incidentally that they did not also
write deliberately and by divine command. For we judge that these things are
not to be opposed but combined: they could write both on an occasion
presented to them and nevertheless by divine command and divine inspiration.
Indeed, because such an occasion was presented to them only divinely, they
also had to write by the divine will. For it was neither presented to them
rashly nor taken up by them of their own accord.

III. One command is implicit and general; another is explicit and special.
Although not all the sacred writers had a special command to write—which,
nevertheless, occurs in many cases, Exodus 17; Deuteronomy 31:19; Isaiah 8:1;
Jeremiah 36:2; Habakkuk 2:2; Revelation 1:12, etc.—they all

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 58 | IA leaf 112 | PDF p. 112]

nevertheless had a general command. For the command to teach, Matthew 28:19,
also contains the command to write, since we can teach the absent and
posterity only by Scripture. Hence one proclamation is said to take place in
writing, another in deed, and another in word. Further, the immediate
inspiration and inward impulse by which they were moved by the Holy Spirit
served in place of a command for the sacred writers. Hence Paul calls
Scripture “God-breathed” (θεόπνευστον), 2 Timothy 3:16, and Peter says in his
second epistle, 1:21, that “prophecy was not brought by the will of man, but
men of God spoke as they were carried by the Holy Spirit” (ὑπὸ πνεύματος
ἁγίου φερομένους)—that is, moved by the Holy Spirit. But it is “inconsistent”
(ἀσύστατον) to say that the apostles wrote while God inspired and moved them,
and yet did not command them. Nor is a command given more effectively than by
the inspiration of the things to be written; nor does it belong to faithful
envoys to carry out anything beyond their commands.

IV. Although the apostles nowhere mention a special command of Christ, which
they nevertheless do not rarely, as John, Jude, and others do, they
sufficiently attest it: 1. when they profess themselves to be universal
teachers of all nations; 2. when they call themselves faithful servants of
Christ, and thus solely concerned with fulfilling Christ's commands; 3. when
they testify that they were moved by the Spirit, 2 Peter 1:21. Hence Gregory
says excellently in his preface to Job, “He who dictated that these things
were to be written himself wrote them; he who was the inspirer of that work
himself wrote them.”

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

V. The apostles were not each individually obliged to write, although they
were each individually bound to preach. For just as they committed themselves
to divine inspiration in the exercise of preaching, so they had to await and
follow the same inspiration in writing. Their office was equal in those
things which were essential to the apostolate, so that they were all equally
“divinely inspired” teachers (θεόπνευστοι); but their office was not equal in
the exercise of all the particular acts belonging to it. Hence it is not
surprising if, according to the freedom of the Holy Spirit, some were employed
both for writing and preaching and others for preaching alone.

VI. A common book did not have to be composed jointly by all the apostles,
both lest they all seem to have acted by agreement and lest it seem to possess
greater authority than what would be written separately by individuals. This
also seems to be the reason why Christ abstained from writing—to say nothing
here of the fact that he is the one who writes his doctrine not with ink, but
with the Spirit of the living God, not on paper, but in the heart, 2
Corinthians 3:2. It was therefore sufficient that some should write things
which the others approved. Indeed, it adds very great weight and authority to
the apostolic writings that, while writing to various people in various
places, for various reasons and occasions, in varying style and by different
methods, they nevertheless wrote things so harmonious.

VII. It was not necessary for the apostles to compose a catechism in order to
hand down their doctrine deliberately. It was sufficient for them to hand
down those things by which all symbolic books and catechisms were to be
measured. 2. If they did not write a catechesis formally, nevertheless they
handed it down materially, whether in the Gospels or in the Epistles, from
which we can clearly “be catechized” (κατηχεῖσθαι).

VIII. Just as we ought not to lay down a law for the Holy Spirit and prescribe
to him the method of revealing his will, so we ought not to doubt that the
manner of writing which they followed is the most suitable. This is so not
only because teaching by epistles was a custom then received among most
people, and because this kind of writing was the most convenient for rapidly
disseminating the gospel, which was the apostles' chief aim, but also because
that simple and popular method of writing accommodated itself to everyone,
both unlearned and learned, and handed down a theology that was not ideal and
merely theoretical, but practical and applied to the concrete case.

IX. The Apostles' Creed is so called not with respect to its efficient cause,
as though it had been handed down by the

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 59 | IA leaf 113 | PDF p. 113]

apostles, but materially, because it was composed from apostolic doctrine and
is the marrow and compendium of apostolic doctrine.

X. Those who wrote from an occasion presented to them and its necessity could
nevertheless write by command, for subordinate causes do not conflict.
Christ's command was the principal impulsive cause, and the presented occasion
was a secondary and less principal impulsive cause, which they knew how to use
for the glory of God and the edification of their neighbor, just as the
apostles preached both by command and from an occasion.

XI. Although the apostles had to write because they were bound to teach, it
does not follow that pastors today are likewise always bound to write as they
are to teach, because the rationale of the two is different. For the apostles
had to teach all nations as ecumenical teachers; ordinary pastors do not,
since they have a particular flock committed to them.
