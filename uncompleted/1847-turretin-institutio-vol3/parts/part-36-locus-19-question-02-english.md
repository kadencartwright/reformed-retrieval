QUESTION II.

ON THE NECESSITY OF THE SACRAMENTS.

Was it necessary for sacraments to be instituted in the church, and is their
use necessary? We distinguish.

[Marginalia: The necessity of the sacraments is twofold: one of institution,
which is not absolute but hypothetical.]

I. A twofold question is raised here concerning the necessity of the
sacraments. One concerns the necessity of their institution: Why did God will
to add sacraments to the Word? The other concerns the necessity of their use:
Is it necessary to use the sacraments? Each must be treated distinctly.

II. With respect to the first, we say that the necessity of the sacraments is
not absolute and simple on God’s part, but hypothetical on our part. It is not
that the Word, which is divine and infallible, needs any confirmation in
itself, but that our weakness needs to be relieved and our faith confirmed.
Just as God communicates his truth to the ears through the Word, so through
the sacraments he presents it in a manner to be seen by the eyes, so that the
Word itself in a sense becomes visible. Hence he wills to seal the certainty
of his grace in our souls not through one sense, namely hearing, but through
several—sight, touch, and smell—so that through sensible and earthly things we
may be raised to intelligible and heavenly things. For just as the senses are
the soul’s windows through which sin and death took possession of it, so it
was fitting that the remedy of saving grace be transmitted through those same
windows. What is simultaneously offered to and apprehended by several senses
is more certain to us than what is perceived by hearing alone. Thus possession
of a house is conveyed by the handing over of a key, a field is claimed by
taking hold of a clod, and a wife is betrothed by the presentation of a ring.

[Marginalia: 1. To assist our dullness.]

III. Thus, (1) God “willed to assist our dullness and slowness and to provide
for our weakness.” Chrysostom says in homily 60 to the people of Antioch: “If
you were incorporeal, he would have delivered his gifts to you naked and
incorporeal. But because your soul is joined to a body, he delivered
intelligible things in sensible things”—that is, by clothing them in a covering
of earthly and sensible things.

[Marginalia: 2. To counter unbelief.]

(2) God willed “to meet our distrust and unbelief,” so that faith might be
strengthened more and more in us. Such is the weakness of the human mind and
its inclination toward distrust that unless it is supported by every kind of
aid, it totters and at last casts away hope in future things. This is
especially so when times occur in which some occasion for doubt

arises. At such times there is a particular need for some pledge of the divine
will, with whose help we can be secure after fear has been removed. For what
doubt can remain after so many proofs of the truth that God supplies to us?

[Marginalia: 3. To communicate the efficacy of grace more clearly.]

IV. (3) God willed “the power and efficacy of grace to be communicated more
forcefully and clearly to our souls.” The Word addresses everyone
indifferently and without distinction, but the sacraments are applied
individually to each person and move hearts far more powerfully and
effectively, not only because

> What enters through the ear stirs the mind more slowly
> Than what is placed before the faithful eyes;

but also because the particular application of the sacraments shows that the
benefit of grace pertains to everyone who uses them rightly.

[Marginalia: The origin of the sacraments.]

V. If we wish to inquire further into the origin of the sacraments, it must be
traced back to the origin of the covenants of which they are the seals. In
covenants contracted among human beings, the customary practice was to secure
the covenant documents on both sides with seals, whether the covenants were
public or private. In the same way, when contracting a covenant with humanity,
God willed to add certain sacraments as so many seals for its confirmation,
not indeed on his part but on ours. Now, the covenant is either absolute and
one-sided, consisting in God’s promise alone. Such was the covenant made with
Noah concerning the world’s not being destroyed again by a flood. A sign was
added to this covenant that depends upon God alone and in whose institution or
use no human action intervened, namely the rainbow, or bow in the cloud. Or
the covenant is two-sided and conditional, consisting of God’s promise and
humanity’s reciprocal pledge. To this kind God also added sacraments, which
require the mutual action of God and humanity: God seals the grace promised in
the Word, and a person receives the offered grace by faith and pledges worship
and obedience to God in return.

[Marginalia: Their difference from the Word.]

VI. From this arises the manifold relation of the Word and the sacraments, in
which they both agree with and differ from one another. They agree in their
author, God; in their foundation, Christ; in their end, namely salvation; in
the manner of reception, faith; and in their object, the saving grace of God.
But they differ in other respects. (1) With respect to necessity, the Word is
absolutely necessary, while the sacraments are necessary only hypothetically.
(2) With respect to manner, the Word is audible, while the sacraments are
visible; the Word produces faith, while the sacraments confirm it. (3) With
respect to the object to which they are directed, the Word is extended without
distinction to everyone, both believers and unbelievers, while the sacraments
pertain only to those in covenant. The Word offers God’s promises
indiscriminately and in common, while the sacraments seal them individually to
each person who rightly receives them. (4) With respect to effect, the Word
benefits adults without the sacrament, while the sacraments do not assist
without the Word. Yet with the Word they move more powerfully because of
sensible images and the analogy they have with spiritual things.

[Marginalia: The necessity of their use.]

VII. With respect to the necessity of their use, we say that it is not a
necessity of means but of command. It does not arise from the nature of the
thing, as if salvation could not be possessed at all without them, but from
God’s command, because he willed to prescribe their use to us as useful for
the confirmation of our faith. This is confirmed by the fact that, as will be
proved below, they neither bestow nor produce grace, but only seal it by God’s
institution and command. Thus God’s grace is not bound to the signs, but can
operate both with and without them. Not a few have been saved without them,
and therefore it is not deprivation of them but contempt for them that
condemns, as will be proved more fully when the necessity of baptism is
treated.

VIII. From this it is evident that two extremes must be carefully avoided
here. (1) The extreme of the papists, who insist upon the absolute necessity
of the sacraments, as though salvation could not be obtained without them or
at least the desire for them. (2) The extreme of the Socinians, who remove
even their hypothetical necessity, as though we could safely lack them and
without prejudice to salvation when it is possible to use them. These pledges
of God’s grace must not be despised, for we are bound to receive them from
God’s hand and confirm our faith by them. Yet neither should our salvation be
bound and tied too tightly to these signs, as if the salvation of those who
are deprived of them in any manner were already lost.

IX. Although the sacraments are means to salvation instituted by God, they do
not therefore have the necessity of a means without which salvation cannot be
obtained. God has not bound his grace to the sacraments. Although he employs
various means adapted and accommodated to our capacity by which he advances
our salvation, he can nevertheless accomplish his work in the elect even
without those external means. Thus, all means do not immediately have the
necessity of a means, as though salvation could not be obtained without them,
but only those things that in themselves attain and effect salvation itself,
such as the Word and the Spirit.

X. Although God is to be worshiped in spirit and truth in the New Testament
(John 4), it does not follow that he cannot be worshiped in the use of the
sacraments. The statement is comparative to the Old Testament, in which the
greatest part of worship consisted in the external rites of ceremonies,
whereas under the New Testament evangelical worship is now entirely spiritual
and moral and consists of very few external ceremonies. In this sense the
Spirit is opposed to the letter, and truth to the figure.
