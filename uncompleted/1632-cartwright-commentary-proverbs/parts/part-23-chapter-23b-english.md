Verse 4. Do not weary yourself to become rich; desist from your own prudence.

Verse 5. Will you make your eyes fly upon that which is not? For it surely acquires wings for itself, like an eagle that flies toward heaven.

In תעיף (*you will make fly*) there is a metaphor transferred from the flight of birds to the glance of the eyes because of its extreme swiftness. In עיניך (*eyes*) there is another metaphor transferred from the bodily senses to the disposition of the mind; or it is a metonymy of the effect, since the eyes are fixed upon what the mind desires.

The purpose is to call people back to moderation in accumulating wealth.

The Apostle, 1 Timothy 6:9, traces this sin's origin further back, and as it were to its very cradle, when he condemns the wish to become rich and, in verse 10, confirms that the love of money is unlawful and full of danger. But if it is not lawful even to will or desire this, much less are we permitted to weary ourselves for this purpose. Yet this is so customary with almost everyone that very few can be found who do not do it. And how few resemble Agur, who prayed against not only poverty but also riches, Proverbs 30:8!

The reason for the prohibition seems to lie concealed in the prohibition itself, when he says, *do not weary yourself*. For thereby he plainly intimates that someone who desires to become rich miserably torments himself with relentless toil. Certainly labor, even labor accompanied by pain, must be undertaken for sustenance and the maintenance of life, Genesis 3. Nevertheless, when the lust for becoming rich invades the mind, life, somewhat bitter by God's ordinance, is thereby rendered bitter and unpleasant beyond measure. Nor is it undeserved that one who destroys himself with such cares should be considered a murderer. For by impairing either the duration or the sweetness of sleep, and disturbing either the moderate enjoyment or the timely digestion of food, he exhausts his strength and undermines his health.

To pluck out the fibres of this desire for riches more easily, he determines that its cause, or root, must be removed far away. We must reject the prudence of the flesh, or of our corrupted reason. By suggesting that this is the only way to obtain livelihood, honors, pleasures, friends, and what not, and by asserting that one who has riches lacks nothing for perfect happiness, whereas one without them is miserable and unhappy, it excites so great a zeal for becoming rich in our minds. If we wish to be free of this vice, we must renounce this, which is not so much prudence as folly and madness, and listen instead to Christ, the supreme wisdom of God,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 513 | printed columns 1010-1011]

who teaches that our life does not consist in riches, however abundant they may be, and makes this clear through the parable of the very rich man abounding in all this life's resources and delights, Luke 12. What of the fact that riches often weave snares and lay ambushes for us, through which our life is taken away, or at least some serious injury inflicted? For who, to omit other things, must fear death or servitude at the hands of enemies? The poor? Or rather the rich and honored? Let the Jews be an example, 2 Kings 25: their nobles and wealthy men were slain to a man or carried into captivity, whereas the poor not only suffered no harm but were even assigned possessions. We do indeed read that David and Solomon accumulated great quantities of riches, and very many of the saints sought and obtained wealth. But does this support the corrupt desires of those who do not seek it honestly, as they did, to advance the true worship of God and have something to distribute to the poor, but load themselves with the spoils of the Church, shameful to say, and drain the poor to fill their own insatiable desires?

To extinguish this flame, Solomon sets before our eyes the attendant vanity of riches in the strongest words, when he says that they possess no subsistence. The fact that Christ numbers riches among the least goods, Luke 16:10–11, could check our headlong pursuit of them and cool our ardor in seeking them so greedily. But Solomon goes further, denying that riches should be counted among things that exist. From this it is clear how great a difference there is between the Prophet's most holy judgment and people's empty opinion. They have bestowed the name “substance” on riches, as though they alone possessed the power of subsisting; but he, elsewhere assigning that designation to the true knowledge of God, here judges that riches should not only be excluded from the realm of substances but relegated to things that do not exist.

Illustrating the vanity and instability of riches by the swift flight of an eagle, as a comparison of like things, he intimates that we cannot either attain them, retain them when acquired, or recover them when lost. He further adorns this with personification, when he says that riches diligently acquire wings for themselves, by which they escape from our hands and fly away. These are manifold: rust, moths, cockroaches, weevils, thieves, robbers, fires, war, oppression, and countless other things of this kind. Out of these, as from feathers, immense wings are formed. Hence we perceive the ignorance of those who greedily gape after so shifting a thing. How the wings of this bird may be clipped, however, the Savior teaches in Matthew 6:25 and Luke 12:22.

### Translator's note

“Substance,” “subsistence,” and “things that do not exist” retain Cartwright's play on the common designation of property as substance and the claim that wealth lacks enduring reality. The verse's *comparando sibi comparat* is an emphatic construction, rendered “surely acquires.” The printed wording *Ut hāc flammam* is retained in Latin; the English follows the evident sense of extinguishing the desire just described.
