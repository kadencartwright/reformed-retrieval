# Anthony Tuckney, *The Moral Law Binds Believers under the Gospel — Fifth Part*

The third member regards the moral law as confirmed by Christ in the gospel. On the surface everyone agrees here. The Antinomians themselves readily acknowledge that the law, as it is in Christ's hand, binds believers. Yet we must fear that they nourish some monster beneath their verbal disguises. If Christ confirms the law, then he does not abolish it, which is their chief contention. If he holds the moral law out to us in his hand, what prevents it from being our rule of life and conduct? This they principally deny. By the law in Christ's hand they understand not God's law written on the sacred page, which they value at nothing, but the law of the Spirit and love engraved on hearts, by which alone they wish believers to be directed and bound.

I have the following objections against them.

1. On this account murders, adulteries, and similar crimes committed by believers—their believers, of course—would not be acts of lawlessness or sins, since the law is nothing. They, with characteristic shamelessness, do not fear to profess this openly, but every godly person abhors it.

If they object that such deeds, though not against the moral law, are against the law of the Spirit and are sins on that account, I answer: what will happen if the Spirit does not act, since he is free in himself and some of them wish him to work everything in us immediately? If the Spirit does not illuminate, nothing can be sinned through ignorance; if he does not move contrarily, nothing can be sinned even through malice.

2. What God has joined, let no one separate. Subordinate things must not be opposed but joined. Why should the law be separated from or opposed to the Spirit, when:

1. It is most closely joined to him. Before Christ, the godly who kept the law were not destitute of the Spirit. If the two agreed in them, why not in believers under the gospel? Especially since Isaiah 59 says, “This shall be my covenant”—an evangelical covenant promised to believers under the gospel—“My Spirit and my words shall not depart from your mouth.” In the same covenant God promised both to inscribe the law on his people's hearts, Jeremiah 31:33, and to place his Spirit within them, Ezekiel 36:27, so that they might walk in his statutes. He who inscribes the law on the heart therefore erases it from neither page nor heart. Paul wished the Word to dwell richly in believers without excluding the Spirit, and, filled with the Spirit, said in Romans 7:16, “I agree with the law,” and in verse 22, “I delight in the law.”

[Marginalia: Colossians 3:16.]

2. As the law is joined to the Spirit, so it is subordinated to him. The Spirit speaks in the law and uses it as an instrument to begin and perfect his work. Hence the law is called spiritual. The same

[1679 new-series fol. 29 | IA leaf 349]

works are works of the law by reason of object and rule, and works of the Spirit by reason of source. The Antinomians would never have opposed these two had they not violated together with God's law the laws of reason and philosophy.

Third, they madly oppose acting from love for God and acting by force of command. Yet the law commands love and everything to be done from love. Christ likewise placed love for himself in keeping his precepts.

[Marginalia: John 14:15.]

Nor, when God promised mercy in the second precept to those loving him and keeping his commandments, should he be thought to say incompatible things. As God displays his love toward us no less by commanding than promising, so we chiefly display ours by seeking to please him in everything. To act apart from a command in religious worship is to thrust will-worship upon God, which especially displeases him.

Did not Adam and Christ act from love? Yet Christ acted by force of command too, as John 14:31 testifies. We are servants no less than sons and owe God a servant's obedience, though not servile obedience. Indeed, to act by command is most freeborn, especially when appetite protests, the world opposes, and Satan rages. We would desist if the matter were optional; cheerfully to undertake it when God commands is the mark of a freeborn disposition and filial love.

Finally, they boast so loudly of the law engraved on the heart that they value at nothing the law written on the sacred page—and why not also the written gospel?

1. Of all mortals, those who value Scripture's inestimable gift so cheaply are most ungrateful.

2. They are wiser than Christ. The law was laid up within him, yet he appealed to the scroll of Scripture, Psalm 40:8, as the apostle did in Romans 12:19.

[Marginalia: Psalm 40:7–8.]

He repelled the devil's fiery darts with Scripture, Matthew 4; pressed hearers to duty by the law and prophets, Matthew 7:12; and, risen and thus more spiritual, still expounded Moses and the prophets, Luke 24:27.

3. No wonder they trample Paul underfoot, calling him a broken vessel. He wished Timothy to attend to reading, 1 Timothy 4:13, and maintained that “every Scripture is God-breathed” and useful for everything, that the man of God might be perfectly equipped for every good work, 2 Timothy 3:16–17.

4. They charge the other apostles with foolishness because, after the Spirit descended extraordinarily, they did not cease preaching, writing, and forming the canon. John, called the Eagle and loftier than these who are so deeply wise, undertook not so much to complete as to confirm Scripture's

[1679 new-series fol. 30 | IA leaf 350]

canon. He wrote the Gospel, epistles, and Apocalypse, bearing witness to it through all. “These things are written so that you may believe and, believing, may have life,” John 20:31. “I have written these things so that you may know that you have eternal life and may believe in the name of the Son of God,” 1 John 5:13. In the Apocalypse he was commanded at least twelve times to write what he saw.
