It is asked whether, because God does not give grace to a human being on
account of prior dispositions or from any debt and obligation, the adversaries
rightly infer that, according to our doctrine, it is not necessary—indeed, it
is harmful and useless—for the unconverted to hear the word, read, meditate,
acknowledge their misery, and use the external means. The adversaries affirm
it. We deny it.

1. The unconverted are obligated to use the means, and they sin by not using
the means, even though grace is not given on account of the use of the means.
For this command extends more broadly than the promise. God can rightly command
that for obedience to which he is neither bound nor promises to reward.

2. External preparation is prerequisite in those who are to be converted; and
yet conversion is given not on account of that preparation, but from sheer
grace.

These preparations can nevertheless be accidentally useless and harmful,
insofar as Pharisaical people glory in those preparations and rest content in
them as though they were fruits of true conversion.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 344 | PDF p. 395]

It is asked whether legal contrition can be called a preparation for
conversion, just as temporary faith is a preparation and step toward saving
faith.

Answer. This contrition and faith is rightly called a step toward the thing,
but not a step within the thing—that is, a material beginning, but not a formal
one. Thus yellowing is a step toward the thing, preparing for whiteness, but it
is not a step within the thing, because it differs in species from whiteness.
Yet in the same way even blackness can be a step toward the thing and a
preparation for whiteness. In the same manner, all moral affections—such as
grieving over sins, desiring conversion, trusting in the Creator, rejoicing for
a time in the good of the gospel, and the like—which exist in the unregenerate,
differ in species from the same spiritual affections that follow conversion,
because these affections differ in their formal object. For the unregenerate
person grieves over sin, but not with grief according to God; rather, he grieves
only insofar as sin procures punishment, not insofar as it offends the goodness
of the most merciful Father. And so it is with the rest.

It is asked whether those not yet reborn can perform a truly good work before
they are regenerated. The adversaries say that their works can please God—not,
indeed, as works meriting eternal life, but can nevertheless please God insofar
as Christ will cherish the smoking flax and bring it to maturity. So Corvinus,
*Against Molina*, chapter 39, sections 2 and 16.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 345 | PDF p. 396]

We say that the moral works of the unregenerate can be truly good according to
the substance of the work and are not merely hypocritical—just as brass is a
true metal—and yet are not truly good when considered evangelically—just as
brass is not true gold. Or they are truly good κατ’ οἰκονομίαν, according to
the universal administration, but are not truly good according to evangelical
approval.

1. “The sacrifices of the wicked are an abomination to Jehovah,” Proverbs
15:8. Matthew 12:23 and Matthew 7:18: “A bad tree cannot produce good fruits.”
John 15:5: “Without me you can do nothing”—that is, unless you remain in me as
branches grafted into the vine and incorporated through faith, as verse 4 has
it.

2. The unregenerate person is dead in sins, incapable of the things of God,
regarding the wisdom of God as foolishness; and his fleshly wisdom is hostility
against God and is neither subject to God's law nor able to be subject to it.
Nor can he think anything good from himself or say that Jesus is Lord.

3. “Without faith it is impossible to please God,” Hebrews 11:6. But the
unregenerate person is without faith.

4. All good actions must be referred to God's glory, 1 Corinthians 10:31; John
15:8; Matthew 5:16. But the unregenerate person cannot refer his actions to
God's glory, because he does not know God revealed in Christ the Mediator under
the aspect of the ultimate end. Therefore, his end is the creature, or he is
his own end; and that intention is idolatry,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 346 | PDF p. 397]

which places the creature in the position of the ultimate end.

5. Because the unregenerate have not been washed by Christ's blood, their mind
and conscience are polluted. Therefore, the streams of actions flowing from
them are polluted.

6. In them is the bitter root of concupiscence, not yet forgiven in Christ or
subdued by the grace of regeneration. It always exerts a privative and
defective influence in all good acts, just as a dislocated leg influences the
motion of a person who limps.

7. The unregenerate cannot love God with all their heart, all their soul, and
all their strength, since they are habitually turned away from God and inclined
to hatred of God and their neighbor. For the flesh, the old human being, the
body of death, dwells in them and wholly predominates.

8. Romans 3:10–11: “As it is written, there is no one righteous, not even one.
There is no one who understands; there is no one who seeks God.”

9. The works of a person whose person is not pleasing and acceptable to God in
Christ are not pleasing to God, Genesis 4:5; Hebrews 11:4.

It is asked whether the virtues of the Gentiles are true virtues. The
adversaries say that they are true and are prior dispositions which God would
have rewarded with further grace if they themselves had not prevented it. We
deny it.

1. No true virtues can exist in those

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 347 | PDF p. 398]

who are outside Christ, John 15:5.

2. Those virtues were performed only for empty glory, not sincerely for God,
so that Augustine excellently called them “splendid sins.”

3. As has been proved, the unregenerate cannot perform works that are truly
pleasing to God and perfect in all their causes with the perfection of their
parts.

4. The Fathers teach the same: Augustine, *Against Julian*, book 4, chapter 3;
Theophylact on John 5; Arnobius on Psalm 29; Gregory, *Morals*, book 2, chapter
22. They maintain that actions must be characterized by the adverbs “well” or
“badly,” not by nouns.

Allied to this is the little question whether a work is therefore not stained
by sin because it is not done with the intention of that sin by which it is
stained. The Remonstrants affirm it. We deny it.

1. There are principles other than a bad intention which stain a work—namely,
concupiscence and the lack of faith and of a good intention.

2. Otherwise, a greedy person who steals his neighbor's goods with the
intention of giving alms would not sin in that act.

It is asked whether an unregenerate person can avoid any particular sin and is
constrained by no necessity of sinning. The Remonstrants say that sin is not
sin if it is unavoidable and necessary. We say the contrary.

1. The wisdom of the flesh cannot be subject to God's law,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 348 | PDF p. 399]

Romans 8:7. The natural human being cannot believe or come to Christ, John
6:44.

2. He cannot pray or thirst for God's righteousness.

3. Concupiscence cannot fail to pollute all the moral actions of those whose
mind and conscience are polluted, Titus 1:15.

4. The unregenerate are dead in sins, Ephesians 2:1.

It is asked whether sin is not sin unless the sinner can avoid it. The
Remonstrants affirm this because, according to them, the lack of original
righteousness is not sin but punishment. They deny that it is equitable for
God to demand obedience from those who have been punished with a judicial
inability to obey, just as it would not be equitable if a tyrant commanded,
under pain of death, that a person whose eyes had already been gouged out
because of an offense should read a book and distinguish colors.

We deny it. 1. God gave all people in Adam the ability to obey, and he is not
bound to restore the ability that was lost. Yet all remain sinners,
transgressors of the law, and unequal to keeping it, just as an insolvent
debtor who has declared bankruptcy remains a true debtor even though he is
unable to pay.

2. Otherwise, original sin would not be sin—which is false—because it is
unavoidable in the persons of Adam's descendants.

3. Failure to render obedience to the law which is perfect in every respect
subjects

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 349 | PDF p. 400]

all mortals to death and the curse, Deuteronomy 27:26; Galatians 3:10–12. And
yet the adversaries do not deny that it is impossible for a human being to
render perfect obedience to the law.

4. Those who have sinned against the Holy Spirit and have been punished with a
judicial inability to obey; those who have become callous and extinguished
every sense of conscience, Ephesians 4:19; those who have a heart that does not
know how to repent, Romans 2:5; those whom it is impossible to renew, Hebrews
6:4—they truly sin, and yet they cannot not sin.

Hence, we exclude from the nature of sin: 1. a necessity which impels by
physical necessity; 2. a necessity which drives by fatal force; and 3. a
necessity which compels and violates liberty. But we do not exclude every
necessity in general.

It is asked whether the afflictions of the faithful are true punishments, or
only fatherly chastisements. Arminius, response to article 9, says that they
have the nature of punishment.

For the question to be answered, the following must be noted beforehand. 1.
Punishment taken generically is the genus of satisfactory punishment and
chastening punishment as its species.

2. It must be noted that when our theologians dispute against Purgatory and
reject the Roman Catholic claim that after guilt has been remitted punishment,
or an obligation to punishment, remains, they must be understood to mean
properly satisfactory punishment, which those who are scorched for some years
in Purgatory pay. They do not, however,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 350 | PDF p. 401]

deny that chastening punishment remains after the remission of sin. This can
be seen in David's example: the sword never departed from his house even after
God had forgiven him adultery and murder. Arminius, however, understands the
afflictions of the faithful to have the nature of punishment which is even in
some manner satisfactory—namely, which is inflicted by God as a severe judge.
We maintain the contrary.

1. If the chastisements of the saints were simply satisfactory punishments,
then Christ's death would not erase all punishments, both temporal and eternal,
and a door to Purgatory would lie open for saints who suffer little or no
punishment in this life. Otherwise, God's justice would be endangered.

2. If the faithful can pay a punishment of, say, eight for their sins, then by
the growth and αὔξησιν, “increase,” of that punishment they could also pay a
punishment of a hundred and a thousand, and successively even an eternal one,
notwithstanding the λύτρῳ, “ransom,” paid by Christ. Hence, the faithful would
pay two punishments that are essentially satisfactory, one in their own person
and the other in Christ their surety. Thus they themselves would die for their
own sins, which Christ nevertheless expressly denies, John 11:25–26.

3. Although a proportion is maintained between David's chastisement and his
sin, 2 Samuel 16:22, the pollution of David's marriage bed nevertheless does
not have the nature of satisfactory punishment, because then Christ's blood
would not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 351 | PDF p. 402 | ends before CAPUT OCTAVUM]

cleanse from every sin, 1 John 1:7. Nor was there a proportion of strict
justice, but of fatherly mercy, so that the punishment would be a useful
admonition to David to think seriously about the magnitude of his guilt, and
to him and others to avoid such voluntary sins thereafter.

4. The afflictions of the saints also test sincerity and constancy, as Job's
did; they confirm God's word and his cause and illustrate his glory, as did
those of the apostles, confessors, and martyrs, who count them a great honor to
themselves and therefore rejoice, praise God, and glorify him. But if these
afflictions were punishments properly so called, the saints would not praise
God because “they were counted worthy to suffer dishonor for the name of
Christ,” Acts 5:41. For we do not praise God simply on account of punishment.
