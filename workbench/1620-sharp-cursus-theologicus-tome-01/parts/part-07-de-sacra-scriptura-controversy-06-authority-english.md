Sixth Controversy: The Authority of Holy Scripture

Even the papists themselves do not agree about the authority of Scripture.
Eck says in his *Enchiridion*, under the authority of the church, response 3
to the objection of the heretics, that “the church is older than the
Scriptures, and Scripture is not authentic except by the authority of the
church.” He places this proposition among heretical assertions: “The authority
of Scripture is greater than that of the church,” and asserts that the
contrary proposition is Catholic. It is often said in canon law that the
church is above Scripture. Pighius says in book 1, chapter 2 of *The Hierarchy
of the Church*: “All the authority Scripture now has among us necessarily
depends upon the authority of the church.” A certain Hermann says that the
Scriptures have only as much force as Aesop’s fables unless the testimony of
the church is added.

Bellarmine, however, and other more recent writers speak a little more
cautiously. For in book 2, chapter 12 of *The Authority of Councils*, he says:
“If Catholics sometimes say that Scripture depends upon the church, or upon a
council, they do not mean with respect to its authority and in itself, but
with respect to its explanation and to us.”

The position of the present-day papists, therefore, is this: the church does
not make the Scriptures true in themselves, but our accepting and approving
them as true comes about in no other way than through the approval and
authority of the church. By the name “church” they mean not only the apostolic
church, but its successor—indeed, the present Roman church. Sometimes they
mean councils, sometimes all the pastors, and sometimes the pope alone.

We, however, concede that the church has four principal duties concerning
Scripture. 1. The church is a witness and custodian

[1620 Tome I | col. 55 | BSB frame 00042]

of Scripture and is like a notary. Yet documents are not believed solely on
account of the notary. Likewise, we do not give credence to Scripture solely
or chiefly on account of the church’s custody and testimony.

2. The church is Scripture’s vindicator, because its duty is to distinguish
the true, authentic, and genuine Scriptures from the false and spurious. For
this purpose the true church has the Spirit of Christ, through whom she knows
the bridegroom’s voice, just as a goldsmith can distinguish gold from copper
with a Lydian touchstone. Yet he does not thereby make it gold, even for us;
rather, he indicates that what is gold in itself is gold, so that we may
believe more readily—not because we rely upon his testimony, but because we
ourselves discover that it is gold.

3. The church is the herald of Scripture, because she ought to publish and
proclaim it without omitting or changing anything. Yet we believe royal edicts
on their own account, not chiefly because of the herald’s voice. 4. The church
is Scripture’s interpreter, because she ought to expound and interpret it,
mixing in nothing of her own but explaining Scriptures from Scriptures.

Although these duties of the church are excellent, we nevertheless do not
assent to and embrace the Scriptures solely or chiefly because of the
church’s authority. Rather, we say that there is another, more certain and
illustrious testimony by which we are persuaded that these books are sacred:
namely, the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit, without which the church’s
commendation would be of little weight. Or, to speak more plainly, Scripture
is self-authenticating in itself, while it is received by us not solely or
chiefly because the church decreed or commanded it, but because it proceeded
from God. We know with certainty that it proceeded from God chiefly through
the testimony of the Holy Spirit, but not with the same certainty through the
testimony of the church.

It should be noted, moreover, that there are four kinds

[1620 Tome I | col. 56 | BSB frame 00042]

of means by which God causes the elect truly and certainly to acknowledge
that canonical Scripture is God-breathed. The first is the testimony of
Scripture itself—that is, of God speaking in the Scriptures—by which the
initial impulses are stirred in their hearts. The second is the inward
testification of the Holy Spirit and his efficacious persuasion in the hearts
of the elect. This fully confirms that God speaks to us in the Scriptures and
at the same time reveals his truth, majesty, and authority (1 Cor. 2:12, 15).
The third means consists of many other demonstrations, some inherent in
Scripture and some deduced from things that follow from Scripture. The
demonstrations inherent in Scripture are drawn first from the substance of
the doctrine contained in the Scriptures, which surpasses every earthly
capacity (1 Cor. 2:14); second, from the quality of that doctrine, namely its
purity, because it savors of nothing corrupt, earthly, or fleshly but, on the
contrary, is endowed with extraordinary majesty; third, from the form of its
teaching, which, although simple, nevertheless draws everyone into admiration
of it; fourth, from the harmony of the Scriptures; and fifth, from their most
efficacious power and capacity to regenerate a person. The following
demonstrations are drawn from things consequent upon Scripture: first, from
the outcomes of the prophecies; 2. from the miracles by which the Scriptures
were confirmed; third, from the fury of Satan and other enemies against this
doctrine; fourth, from its preservation against the power of its enemies; and
fifth, from the punishments endured by the persecutors of this heavenly
truth.

The fourth means is the church’s constant and perpetual testimony. In some
people it is indeed earlier than the other means in the order of time, but it
must not be judged always and in everyone to be either the principal or the
most efficacious of all.

[1620 Tome I | col. 57 | BSB frame 00043]

The church’s testimony is considered in two ways, corresponding to two kinds
of people: they are either true members of the church or outside the church.
The latter do not see the dignity of Scripture or acknowledge the preceding
testimonies, because they do not possess true faith and the Spirit of Christ.
Therefore, so long as they remain such, they acknowledge Scripture to be the
word of God only because of the church’s testimony. Consequently, since this
testimony is not sufficient for true knowledge, Scripture in itself has
little authority among them. The faithful, however, acknowledge the authority
of Scripture more certainly from the preceding testimonies.

Furthermore, “authority” has two meanings. First, it is the same as esteem,
reputation, or worthiness, by which someone is judged worthy either of belief
in his words because he has always been found truthful, or of honor because
he is regarded as an upright and virtuous man. Hence someone is said to have
acquired authority among others by his virtue, and the testimonies of people
worthy of belief are called authorities. Second, it is power, dominion, and a
right over someone, as well as the right to do something; in this sense a
magistrate is said to have authority. In both senses the church has her
authority from Scripture, not Scripture from the church. For Scripture has
from God the fact that it is true and worthy of belief. The fact that it is
acknowledged as such by the faithful comes chiefly from God speaking in
Scripture and from the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit. The church,
however, has no authority except what she borrows from Scripture and only
insofar as she uses it rightly.

The state of the controversy, therefore, is whether it ought to be believed
that Scripture is sacred and canonical solely because of the church’s
testimony, or rather because of the internal persua—

[1620 Tome I | col. 58 | BSB frame 00043]

—sion of the Holy Spirit, who both makes Scripture canonical and authentic in
itself and also causes it to appear such to us, so that without his testimony
the testimony of the church is ineffectual. The papists deny this; we affirm
it for the following arguments.

1. Argument. In every discipline, principles and axioms ought to be true,
primary, and immediate. They neither ought to be nor can be proved from other
things, but all other things are to be confirmed from them. But the whole of
Scripture and all its propositions are the principles of theological
discipline. Therefore, they neither ought to be nor can be proved by the
testimony of the church as though it were more certain.

2. Argument. If we believe the Scriptures because of the church’s authority,
then this faith is either acquired or infused. But neither can be said. They
will not say it is infused, because this is precisely what we maintain: that
infused faith by which we believe Scripture is nothing other than the
testimony and persuasion of the Holy Spirit on account of which we believe
the Scriptures, so that it does not come from the church. Nor will they say it
is acquired—that is, obtained by our study and human persuasions—because this
by itself is insufficient to persuade us with certainty. For without infused
faith we truly believe nothing, even if it were most evident—indeed, not even
miracles—as in Deuteronomy 29:4; Matthew 16:17; and 1 Corinthians 12:3, 9.
Therefore, and so forth.

3. Argument. Divine Scripture had authority among the faithful before any
judgment of the church. Therefore, its authority among them does not derive
solely from the testimony of the church. The antecedent is proved because the
judgment of the fathers and councils is recent if we consider the antiquity
of Scripture. Therefore, if Scripture’s authority depends upon the church’s
judgment, then for many centuries there was no certain canon; alternatively,
the books which the fathers and councils list as canonical were and were
regarded as authentic and canon—

[1620 Tome I | col. 59 | BSB frame 00044]

—ical before their judgment, as our position maintains. Furthermore, this
judgment of the fathers exists in their books and in the decrees of councils.
But from where will the authority of these be established more certainly than
that of Scripture? From where, for example, is it more certainly established
that those books are Augustine’s? If they say this is understood through the
living voice of the church, I reply that the authority of the fathers and
councils is insufficient for this. And how is it established that the
church’s voice is the voice of the true church? Certainly in no other way than
from Scripture. Therefore, with respect to us, Scripture’s authority is more
certain than that of the church. Finally, if those leaders of the church were
assembled by some authority in order to designate the canon of Scripture and
thereby declare its authority, they would do this either by the prompting of
the Holy Spirit, by some particular revelation, or by the authority of
Scripture. But they will affirm none of these. Not the first, because this is
what we insist upon. Not the second, because they join us in condemning such
particular revelations in the Anabaptists as heretical. Not the third, because
then they acknowledge the canonical authority of Scripture before the
church’s judgment. And if they concede that only that part of Scripture in
which the command to assemble exists was acknowledged as canonical without
the church’s judgment, I ask why this part rather than the rest of Scripture
was acknowledged as canonical without the church’s judgment. Therefore.

4. Argument. The true and proper cause of Scripture’s authority among us is
that which produces this effect perpetually and necessarily. But only the
testimony of the Holy Spirit, not that of the church, is of this kind.
Therefore. The assumption is proved: if the church’s judgment produced this
effect perpetually and necessarily, then all who heard this from the church
would believe. But the Jews, the Turks, and even some within the church do not
believe or receive the canon, although the church bears testimony concerning
the canon of Scripture. Therefore.

[1620 Tome I | col. 60 | BSB frame 00044]

5. Argument. If the church’s authority with respect to us depends upon the
authority of Scripture, then the authority of Scripture with respect to us
does not depend upon the authority of the church. But the former is true.
Therefore, so is the latter. The assumption is proved: I ask, from where does
it come that the church does not err in establishing the canon? Response:
because she is governed by the Holy Spirit. But from where do we know that
she is governed by the Holy Spirit? Response: because Christ promised this.
But where do these promises exist? Response: in Scripture (Matt. 28:20; 18:20;
John 15:26; 16:13). But if we believe the church because of these testimonies
of Scripture, then Scripture, on account of which we believe the church, has
greater authority among us and is authentic among us in itself.

6. Argument. If the power of Scripture in itself is so great that by itself it
draws our minds to itself and thoroughly persuades us of what it says, thus
establishing its own credibility by itself, then it is by itself
self-authenticating, canonical, and authentic for us. But the former is true
(Luke 8:11, and so forth). Therefore.

7. Argument. Christ is known by himself and does not rest upon the testimony
or authority of any person. Therefore, neither does Scripture. The antecedent
is proved from John 5:34. For just as John’s testimony did not bestow authority
upon Christ, not even with respect to us, so although the church as a witness
declares Scripture authentic, she nevertheless does not give authority to
Scripture, not even with respect to us.

8. Argument. If Scripture’s testimony concerning Christ is more certain than
John’s testimony, then Scripture’s testimony is more certain than the
church’s. For the adversaries do not dare say that the church’s testimony
concerning Scripture is more certain than John’s testimony concerning Christ.
But the former is clear from John 5:36 and the following verses, where Christ
lists three testimonies greater and more certain than John’s testimony:
namely, those of God the Father, Christ’s works, and Scripture. Therefore.

9. Argument. If the testimony of Scripture is firm—

[1620 Tome I | col. 61 | BSB frame 00045]

—er and more certain than divine revelations from heaven themselves, then it
is much more so than the testimony of the church. But the former is taught in
2 Peter 1:18–19. Therefore.

10. Argument. If the Thessalonians, upon hearing the word, acknowledged the
doctrine of Scripture as divine, then Scripture has divine authority among
people without a judgment of the church. But the former is found in 1
Thessalonians 2:13. At that time they had heard of no judgment of the church;
rather, persuaded solely by the character of the doctrine itself and by the
testimony of the Holy Spirit, they acknowledged the gospel. Likewise,
Galatians 4:14 says that the gospel doctrine was received by the Galatians
without any judgment of the church, as it also was by the Bereans in Acts
17:11. Therefore.

11. Argument. No testimony can be greater or more certain for us than divine
testimony. But the testimony of the church is the judgment of human beings,
whereas the testimony of Scripture is God’s. If they say the church’s
testimony is divine, then I say it is made such because the church has the
Holy Spirit, and thus they affirm the same thing we do.

12. Argument. The Spirit testifies that the spirit is truth (1 John 5:9),
where in the latter occurrence of “Spirit” he understands by metonymy the
heavenly doctrine whose inspirer is the Spirit. Therefore.

13. Argument. Those who do not believe the Scriptures will not believe
Christ’s testimony either (John 5:47), much less, therefore, will they believe
the church’s. Consequently, Scripture has greater authority among us than the
church.

14. Argument. If the canon of Scripture depends upon the church’s
determination, then the authority and truth of the promises concerning
salvation will depend upon human judgment, for we believe the promises because
of the canonical authority of the Scriptures in which they are found.

But the latter is absurd, because if the authority and truth of the promises
depended upon human judgment, there would be no security of conscience or
confidence. If they say it must be believed because the church’s judgment is
divine,

[1620 Tome I | col. 62 | BSB frame 00045]

why, I ask, do we not also believe Scripture by itself, since it is equally,
if not more, divine? Therefore.

15. Argument. Whoever does not believe God makes him a liar (1 John 5:10).
But whoever believes God speaking in Scripture only because of the authority
of another—namely, the church—believes that other more than God and thus does
not believe God without qualification. Therefore.

16. Argument. If the church’s judgment makes Scripture canonical for us, then
it makes the things in Scripture be judged and believed true, and thus our
faith is ultimately resolved into the church’s voice. But even the papists
themselves testify that this is absurd. Therefore, so is the former.

17. Argument. The church is subject to Scripture (2 Tim. 3:16). Therefore,
she ought by no means to render an authoritative judgment concerning
Scripture, which is the matter in question.

Testimonies

Augustine says in book 11, chapter 5 of *Against Faustus the Manichaean*: “The
excellence of the canonical authority of the Old and New Testaments is
distinguished from that of later books, because, having been confirmed in the
apostles’ times through successions of bishops and propagations of churches,
it is established as though on an elevated seat. Every believing and pious
understanding should serve it.” The same author says in chapter 31 of *On True
Religion*: “It is right for pure minds to know the eternal law of God, but it
is not right to judge it.” The same author writes in book 11, chapter 3 of the
*Confessions*. The same author says in book 14 of *Against the Fundamental
Epistle*: “For us to be worthy to understand what we believe, our minds must
be inwardly strengthened and illuminated by God himself.”

Ambrose says in book 1, chapter 5 of *On the Faith*: “Do not assess divine
things, but believe divine things, where you find nothing human.”

Bishop Salvian says in book 3 of *On Providence*: “All human sayings

[1620 Tome I | col. 63 | BSB frame 00046]

need arguments and witnesses, but God’s word is its own witness, because
whatever incorrupt truth speaks must be an incorrupt testimony of truth.”

Papist Testimonies

Gabriel Biel says in book 3, distinction 25 of the *Sentences*: “Catholic
truths are, from the nature of the thing and apart from every approval of the
church, immutable and immutably true, and thus they are to be regarded as
immutably Catholic.”

The Jesuit Canisius says in his *Catechism*, chapter on the precepts of the
church, section 16, “that we believe Scripture because of the testimony of the
divine Spirit speaking in it, adhere to it, and attribute the greatest
authority to it.”

Objections

Objection 1. The church is older than Scripture. Therefore, it has greater
authority. The antecedent is proved: Scripture did not exist before Moses,
but the church did. Therefore, the church is older than Scripture.

Response.

The inference of the first enthymeme is denied, because it depends upon this
general proposition: what is older has greater authority. But this proposition
is neither universally nor always true, for the law is older than the gospel,
and Moses, the prophets, and John the Baptist are older than Christ, but they
do not have greater authority.

2. In the prosyllogism there is an equivocation in the name “Scripture.” The
name “Scripture” is sometimes taken for Scripture’s substance and the thing
written—that is, the word of God—and then it is nothing other than
God-breathed doctrine. At other times it is taken for an accident of this
doctrine, namely its writing and inscription in books. In the first sense,
Scripture is older than the church, and thus the proposition is false, for
Scripture, or the word of God, is the church’s found—

[1620 Tome I | col. 64 | BSB frame 00046]

—ation (Eph. 2:20). But the foundation is older than the building. Likewise,
Scripture is the seed of the church (Matt. 13:1; 1 Pet. 1:23). But seed is
older than the offspring of which it is the seed. Finally, this same word that
is now written was then unwritten.

Objection 2.

To have a certain canon of Scripture is supremely necessary for religion and
faith. But a certain canon of Scripture cannot be had without the church’s
authority, because it cannot be established which book of the canon is true
and which is spurious unless the church teaches it. Therefore.

Response 1.

The proposition is true if they mean books that are properly canonical, which
the church ought always to acknowledge, although knowing the number of
canonical books is not absolutely necessary for faith and salvation. For many
can have faith and obtain salvation who do not know the number of canonical
books. The assumption, however, is false.

Instance 1. No authority is more certain than the church’s. But the most
certain authority is needed in order that the Scriptures may have their due
credibility and that every doubt about the canon of the Scriptures may be
removed. Therefore.

Response 1.

The proposition is denied, because the authority of God himself, Scripture,
and the Holy Spirit is greater than the church’s authority.

2. If they say that God teaches us only through the church, these authorities
will nevertheless be greater. For the authority of the one who teaches is
greater than that of the one through whom someone is taught. But God teaches
us through the church. Therefore, God’s authority is greater than the
church’s, for the church is only God’s minister, just as a prince promulgates
a law through a herald and expounds it through a legal scholar. Yet the
prince’s authority is greater than that of the herald or legal scholar.

[1620 Tome I | col. 65 | BSB frame 00047]

Instance 2. All the other means are worthless unless we have recourse to the
church’s judgment. For the church knows extremely well the style, phrasing,
and other means by which Scripture can be recognized and can judge by them.
Therefore, her authority ought to prevail.

Response. The inference is denied. For although the church knows these
things, there is nevertheless another authority that is still more certain
and greater. Furthermore, if the church’s authority is most certain because
she knows the style of Scripture and judges Scripture by its style, then the
authority of Scripture itself is much more certain, because it identifies
itself to the church by its own style. And although we concede this—which is
nevertheless false—namely, that we do not know this style to be Scripture’s
unless the church teaches us, it still does not follow that the authority of
the church is greater than Scripture’s. For many would not have known Christ
unless John had pointed Christ out, yet John’s authority was not more certain
or greater than Christ’s. John sent many to Christ who afterward believed more
because of Christ himself than because of John. Likewise, those Samaritans no
longer believed in Christ because of the little woman’s testimony after they
had heard Christ himself speaking (John 4:39, 42).

Instance 3. Scripture cannot be proved by Scripture. Therefore, it must be
proved by the church, and consequently the church’s authority is greater than
Scripture’s. The antecedent is proved: if someone denied Paul’s epistles, one
could not prove from either the Old or New Testament that they are canonical.
Furthermore, every proof proceeds from things better known. Therefore, to
someone denying even only a part of Scripture, it is not Scripture but the
church that will prove it to be Scripture.

Response 1.

The inference is denied, because its authority shines forth from the true
author himself, from whom it proceeded (2 Pet. 1:12, 19; 2 Tim. 3:16).
Finally, this very authority becomes known to us far more cer—

[1620 Tome I | col. 66 | BSB frame 00047]

—tainly from the internal testimony of the Holy Spirit.

2. The antecedent is denied, because the highest clarity and light are
inherent in Scripture (Ps. 119:105; 2 Pet. 1:19). Therefore, Scripture can be
understood from Scripture, provided that someone has eyes to see. Moreover,
if we recognize human beings speaking, why not also God speaking in the
Scriptures? 3. Just as the Old Testament is proved from itself and from the
New Testament, so in turn the New Testament is proved from itself and from
the Old Testament. The Old Testament is proved from the New in Luke 24:44;
16:29, 31; John 8:39. In Matthew 5 Christ interprets the law; see also 19:7;
22:24; 12:42; Luke 4:26; Acts 2:25, 30, 34; 13:17 and the following verses;
7:42; and Hebrews 11. Indeed, testimonies from almost all the books of the Old
Testament are cited in the New Testament to confirm the doctrines of faith.

The New Testament is proved from the Old because everything foretold in the
Old Testament is fulfilled in the New. All the ceremonies of the Old
Testament have their reality in the New. Things spoken obscurely in the Old
Testament are spoken clearly in the New. The New Testament is also proved
from itself because Peter confirms the Pauline epistles in 2 Peter 3:16. But
if someone denies the whole of Scripture, he is not a member of the church.

Objection 3.

If we recognize the sacred books from the testimony of the Holy Spirit, how
does it happen that churches which have the Spirit of Christ do not agree
among themselves?

Response 1.

Not all who claim to have the Spirit of Christ actually have him.
Furthermore, not every error or disagreement, even in matters of faith,
abolishes the church or the operations of the Holy Spirit. For not everyone
has the same measure of the Spirit, nor does the Holy Spirit work in the same
way in everyone (Rom. 12:3).

[1620 Tome I | col. 67 | BSB frame 00048]

Objection 4.

Some prophetic and apostolic writings do not have canonical authority, while
some things that are not by prophets and apostles are received into the
canon. Therefore, the canon is defined by the church’s judgment, and
consequently Scripture has its authority from the church.

Response 1.

Things that the prophets or apostles wrote as private persons—such as David’s
letter to Joab—are not canonical Scripture and therefore do not have canonical
authority. But all the things they wrote as prophets inspired by God have
been received.

2. It is false that any prophetic or apostolic writing of this kind exists
which has not been received into the canon, as will be discussed later
concerning the perfection of Scripture.

3. It is likewise false that anything has been received into the canon which
was not written by a prophet, an apostle, or some apostolic man.

Objection 5.

It came about by the church’s authority that apocryphal books of the first
kind—Judith, Tobit, and so forth—which formerly were not certainly canonical,
were nevertheless received into the canon. Therefore.

Response.

The true church has never received, and does not receive, those books, nor
ought she to receive them, as was proved above.

Objection 6.

Apocryphal and spurious books of the second kind were not regarded as divine
because the church was unwilling to approve them. Therefore, the canon of
Scripture depends upon the church’s authority.

Response.

The inference is denied. For although the church ought to distinguish true
books from false ones, even if she wholly failed to do this, the false could
nevertheless be distinguished from the true and canonical by the books
themselves, in which their falsity could be perceived.

[1620 Tome I | col. 68 | BSB frame 00048]

Objection 7.

Heretics who reject or doubt some part of canonical Scripture are refuted by
the church’s authority. Therefore.

Response.

The inference is denied. For although heretics are refuted by the church’s
authority, in which the church performs her duty, there are nevertheless
stronger arguments for the confirmation of any part of Scripture than the
testimony of the church, as was said above. Nor does the following inference
hold: atheists who deny God are refuted by the church’s authority; therefore,
there is no more certain argument by which they may be refuted. For all
creatures cry out that God exists, and the Holy Spirit testifies this most
certainly to the faithful. Finally, the Scriptures themselves teach it most
clearly.

Objection 8.

The rule of faith that is in the church’s possession and is handed down by the
church is the means by which pastors distinguish the true Scriptures from the
false. Therefore, the church alone ought to determine the canonical books,
and consequently she has greater authority than Scripture.

Response 1.

The antecedent must be distinguished. If by “rule of faith” they mean the
articles of faith, the inference is invalid, because it does not follow that
this or that book teaches things agreeing with the articles of faith;
therefore, it is canonical. For then many books of learned men would be
canonical, which is absurd. But if by the rule of faith they mean Scripture,
as they necessarily must, they say what we maintain. For if the canon of
Scripture is confirmed and authenticated by the rule of faith—that is, by
Scripture—then Scripture is confirmed by Scripture. In this sense, however,
the antecedent is false, because Scripture is handed down to the church by
God.

2. This argument works against the papists, because if Scripture is judged
chiefly by the rule of faith, then it is not judged chiefly by the church.
Finally, this rule of faith is either written

[1620 Tome I | col. 69 | BSB frame 00049]

or unwritten. If it is written, it is sacred Scripture itself. If unwritten,
it must not be admitted, even though the papists maintain that it is unwritten
and say that the church’s faith is antecedent, presupposed, and preexistent,
prior to Scripture and not Scripture itself.

Objection 9.

Augustine says in chapter 5 of *Against the Fundamental Epistle*: “I would not
believe the gospel unless the authority of the catholic church moved me.”
Therefore.

Response. Augustine speaks of himself as a Manichaean situated outside the
faith and the church. Thus the sense is: “While I was still a Manichaean, I
would not have believed the gospel unless the authority of the church had
moved me.” But we freely concede that the church has the greatest authority
among unbelievers and the unconverted, because they do not yet know Scripture
and possess neither faith nor the Holy Spirit. The question, however,
concerns the authority of Scripture among the faithful and members of the
church: whether among them only or chiefly the church has authority, or only
Scripture. In chapter 14 of the same book, Augustine teaches that members of
the church do not believe chiefly because of the church’s testimony when he
says: “When we believe with faith and have now been made stronger in faith,
we are worthy to understand what we believe, not so much by human beings as
by God himself inwardly strengthening and illuminating our mind.” Here he
teaches that the church’s testimony is indeed an introduction by which we are
led to believe, but once we have believed, we believe chiefly because of the
internal testimony of God himself speaking in Scripture and of the Holy
Spirit. See Augustine, book 2, chapter 9 of *On Order*, and his book *On the
Usefulness of Believing*.
