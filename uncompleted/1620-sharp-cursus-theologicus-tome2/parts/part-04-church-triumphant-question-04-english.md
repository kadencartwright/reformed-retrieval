### QUESTION IV

#### On the intercession of the saints for us

Bellarmine says in book 1, chapter 17, *On the Blessedness of the Saints*, that
the saints are not the authors of benefits and that it is nevertheless lawful
to say, *Saint Peter, and so forth, grant me this or that by your prayers and
merits; Saint Peter, save me, and so forth.* He says that they are not
immediate intercessors before God, but that they obtain all things through
Christ. In chapter 18 he says that the saints pray for us at least in general,
and afterward that they pray for us not only in general but also in
particular.

We, however, say that it is not evident from sacred Scripture whether the
saints pray for us, because it says only that Christ is our mediator and
advocate. Furthermore, although we concede that by reason of the perfection of
their love the departed saints wish with most ardent desires for the salvation
of the elect, we prove by the following arguments that they are not particular
mediators of intercession for us.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 17 | printed col. 34 | bsb10175568]

Argument 1. There is one mediator of God and men, the man Christ, without whom
no one can come to the Father, 1 Timothy 2:5; John 14:6. But intercession is a
part of the mediator’s office. Therefore.

Argument 2. He through whose name alone we obtain salvation is the sole
mediator between God and men, and therefore the sole intercessor. But Christ is
such a one, Acts 4:10–12.

Argument 3. He through whom alone we have remission of sins is the sole
mediator, and therefore the sole intercessor between God and men. But we obtain
remission of sins through Christ alone, Acts 10:44 and 17:31.

Argument 4. One who needs a mediator before God cannot be another’s mediator
and intercessor. But all saints—angels and men—need Christ’s intercession: the
angels so that they may persist in their goodness, and the saints so that they
may remain pleasing to God in Christ, the one mediator.

Argument 5. He who is the one perfect mediator does not perform merely a part
of mediation, nor does he perform it with others, but performs the whole of it
alone and perfectly. But Christ is such a mediator, 1 Corinthians 1:13, 30;
Hebrews 12:2. Therefore.

Argument 6. He who alone redeemed all is the only one who intercedes for all
in particular, because the power and efficacy of intercession depend upon
redemption. But Christ alone redeemed us, not angels or saints. Therefore.

Argument 7. The saints do not know our condition in particular, because they
are not everywhere, as Augustine proves at greater length in *On the Care to
Be Taken for the Dead*, chapter 15. Therefore they cannot pray for us in
particular. Augustine therefore rightly says in *Confessions*, book 10,
chapter 42: *Whom could I find to reconcile me to you? Was I to go to the
angels?* And so forth.

Argument 8. Scripture everywhere teaches that Jesus Christ is our advocate,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 18 | printed col. 35 | bsb10175568]

as in 1 John 2; Hebrews 2:17 and 4:15. But it nowhere teaches this about the
saints. Therefore, to suppose that the saints intercede for us would be
without faith and consequently is sin, Romans 14:23.

Argument 9. Ambrose says in *On Isaac*, chapter 8: *Christ himself is our mouth
through which we speak to the Father, the eye through which we see the Father,
and our right hand through which we offer to the Father.*

Augustine says on Psalm 118: *Prayer which is not made through Christ not only
cannot destroy sin, but is itself also sin.*

## OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. From Psalms 84:12 and 121:1, *Those mountains are the saints*, as
Augustine testifies. Therefore we are commanded to lift our eyes to the saints
and expect help from them.

Response. By the mountains he means human reinforcements which flowed down
from the mountains in time of war. Furthermore, by the mountains Augustine
allegorically means not dead saints, but great and illustrious men who preach
the Gospel.

Objection 2. From Jeremiah 15:1: *If Moses and Samuel stood before me, my soul
would not be toward this people.* Therefore, though now dead, they were able
and accustomed to pray for the people.

Response. The inference is denied, because it is not said that Moses prayed
for the people after his death. Rather, the statement is hypothetical: if
Moses and Samuel, who formerly prayed for the people, were now alive and
prayed, I would not spare them. The Fathers interpret it this way: God speaks
of them according to their state in this life, not the state in which they
then were.

2. Turn the argument around. If Moses and Samuel had been accustomed to pray,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 18 | printed col. 36 | bsb10175568]

then he would not have said, *if they stood*, and so forth, for they would have
prayed when the people were in the greatest danger. It is like this: if God
had found fifty righteous people in Sodom, it would not have perished;
therefore he found them, or it did not perish. This is a fallacy arising from
the form of expression. Finally, the Papists themselves maintain that the
Fathers were in Limbo until Christ’s ascension. Therefore they were not
praying before God.

Objection 3. From the last chapter of 2 Maccabees: Judas saw Onias and Jeremiah
praying for the people. Therefore.

Response 1. An article of faith cannot be confirmed from an apocryphal book.

2. A vision, not an event, is narrated in that passage. If it could thereby be
proved that the saints pray for us, it could also be proved that they carry
swords in heaven, because Jeremiah is said to have given Judas a sword.
Finally, we do not read that Judas invoked Onias or Jeremiah on account of
that prayer.

Objection 4. From Revelation 5:8: The elders had bowls, and so forth, which are
the prayers of the saints, that is, intercessions for the steadfastness of the
weak. Therefore.

Response 1. We deny the interpretation of the passage, because those saints
offered their own prayers, for it is stated indefinitely. Therefore, that the
dead offered the prayers of the living is an inference we deny.

2. It is a vision, not an event, in which he speaks of the Church’s prayers.
Therefore he means the dead—this inference is denied, for he represents those
living in the Church on earth. Furthermore, by heaven he means the kingdom of
heaven on earth, which has its origin and manner of life in heaven, as in
Revelation 12:7. Finally, this does not follow: “The Church triumphant prays
for the Church militant; therefore it prays for it in particular.”

Objection 5. From 2 Peter 1:15: *I shall take care to keep you—namely,
commended or in mind—after my*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 19 | printed col. 37 | bsb10175568]

*death.* Therefore the saints care for the living after death.

Response 1. It does not follow that they therefore intercede for us in
particular.

2. Bellarmine himself concedes in chapter 8 that the argument is not evident,
but probable. For the interpretation of the passage is false. The words are:
*I shall endeavor that after my death you may be able to make mention of
them.* Here Peter speaks of his faithful diligence in revealing the mysteries
of the Gospel before his death, which was approaching.

Objection 6. From Luke 16:17: The rich man prays for his brothers. Therefore,
much more do the righteous pray for the whole Church.

Response. It is a parable, and therefore nothing beyond the author’s purpose
can be proved from it.

Furthermore, it will follow that the intercession of the saints is proved by
the actions of the damned.

3. This rich banqueter interceded for his own before Abraham, not before God.
But the question concerns the intercession of the saints for us before God.

4. If conclusions may be drawn from all the circumstances, it follows that a
soul has a tongue and speaks; that the ungodly in hell love their friends on
earth; and that they have zeal for God’s glory and for the extension of
Christ’s kingdom through the preaching of pastors, and so forth. These things
are absurd.

Instance. Abraham knew that the rich man had received good things and Lazarus
evil things in this life. Therefore the saints know our condition in
particular.

Response. This begs the question, because Bellarmine himself above, in chapter
3, and Chrysostom concede that parables are not to be pressed beyond the
purpose of the one proposing them.

Furthermore, this does not follow: “Abraham knew by a particular revelation
or by some other means the condition of these two; therefore all the saints in
heaven, in every place and at every time, know the condition of all the saints
on earth and intercede for all.”

Objection 7. From Revelation 6:9–10:

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 19 | printed col. 38 | bsb10175568]

The souls of the martyrs ask with a great cry for vengeance upon their
killers. Therefore, much more do they ask for mercy for their brothers.

Response. An article of faith is not proved from a vision and an allegory;
rather, sound arguments are drawn from the literal sense alone, as Bellarmine
concedes in book 3, chapter 3, *On the Word of God*. This passage signifies
that the martyrs’ blood cries to God for vengeance, as in Genesis 4:10, and
that although they rest under the altar—that is, with Christ—God nevertheless
takes vengeance in his own time. Furthermore, although we concede that the
souls of the martyrs really prayed, it does not follow that they prayed for
the living on earth, but for themselves and for that universal judgment, as is
clear from the text.

Objection 8. There is a great communion between the Church triumphant and the
Church militant under Christ the head. But that communion necessarily requires
the members to be concerned for one another and help one another, Romans
2:5–6; 1 Corinthians 12:11, because we are one commonwealth, one people, one
body, and so forth. Therefore.

Response. The whole argument is conceded, for we concede that the Church
triumphant is concerned for the Church militant in the manner stated above.
Therefore it knows our needs in particular and intercedes for us—this
inference is denied.

Objection 9. According to Zechariah 1:12; Revelation 8:3; Daniel 10:13; Psalm
91:11; and Matthew 18:10, angels intercede for the living and care for them in
particular. Therefore, much more ought the spirits of men to do so.

Response. The cited passages speak of Christ interceding for the Church, to
whom the other angels render an account, Zechariah 1 and Malachi 3:1.
Furthermore, the inference is denied, because the care of the faithful on
earth has been entrusted to angels, Hebrews 1:14, as also Psalm 91:11 and
Matthew 18:10. But we read nothing of this kind concerning departed saints.

3. Revelation 8 narrates a vision in which he means saints

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 20 | printed col. 39 | bsb10175568]

on earth, not in heaven, by “saints,” and Christ by “the angel,” as in Daniel
10:13. Finally, Matthew 18 says nothing about intercession in particular.

Objection 10. According to Luke 20:36, the saints lack none of the things the
angels have, as far as this office is concerned. Indeed, they have something
beyond the angels, because they are members more closely joined to the Church
than the angels and have experienced our miseries, whereas the angels have
not. But angels know the present state of affairs and intercede. Therefore the
saints do too.

Response. Christ institutes a comparison between angels and saints not with
respect to offices, but with respect to glorification. This is therefore the
fallacy of taking a qualified statement absolutely. Furthermore, angels are
ministering spirits and therefore know by virtue of their office, but the
saints are not called such. Finally, union does not establish intercession in
particular, and Luke 20 speaks of the state of the saints at the last
judgment; compare Matthew 22:30.

Objection 11. From Revelation 2:26 and chapter 3:2, 21: The living faithful are
governed by the spirits of blessed men; hence they are said to rule the
nations, to be a pillar in God’s temple—that is, the Church—and to sit upon a
throne. Therefore.

Response. The premise is denied, nor can it be proved by the cited passages.
In Revelation 2, rule over the nations does not signify the governance of the
Church, but dominion over the enemies whom the faithful will judge at the end
of the age, as they will also judge the devil, 1 Timothy 6:3.

2. Revelation 3:12 means the Church triumphant, in which the faithful are
pillars not for supporting but for adorning. By the word “pillar” it signifies
their stable condition in the future life. Finally, verse 21 signifies the
glory and triumph of the elect after this life.

Objection 12. From Matthew 24:47: *He will set the servant who has done his
will over all his goods.* But God’s goods

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 20 | printed col. 40 | bsb10175568]

belong to the Church. Therefore the saints govern the living faithful and
consequently know their condition; thus they intercede for them in particular.

Response. The cited passage is an allegory, and therefore nothing beyond the
author’s purpose can certainly be concluded from it.

2. The assumption is denied, because by “all his goods” he means eternal life.

Objection 13. Genesis 32:9; Exodus 32:13; Deuteronomy 9:27; Psalm 132:1, 10:
Those whose intercession the godly plead before God in their prayers intercede
for us. But they plead the intercession of the saints, such as Abraham and
others. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because in the cited passages they plead
not intercession, but the covenant and the promise made to Abraham and others,
as in Exodus 6:3–4 and 32:13; Leviticus 26:40, 45.

2. The Papists themselves maintain that the Fathers were not in heaven before
Christ’s ascension. Therefore they contradict themselves when they prove
intercession from these passages.

3. While David was alive and about to bring the ark to Jerusalem, he composed
Psalm 132 and in it looks to the covenant, verse 11, as also elsewhere, Psalm
89:29.

Objection 14. From Job 33:23: *If there is an angel speaking for him, to
declare the man’s uprightness, he will have mercy upon him*, and so forth.
Therefore angels report our uprightness before God and intercede for us.

Response. The cited passage does not speak of an angel. Rather, the words are,
*if there is a messenger*, that is, a prophet announcing the will of God. The
word מלאך is taken in this way in Judges 2:1; Malachi 3:1; and often elsewhere.
Thus in Mark 1:2 John the Baptist is called a messenger. Elihu therefore
speaks of the office of a faithful minister of God who consoles an afflicted
person on earth.

Objection 15. Ezekiel 22:30: *I sought a man who would stand for the land, lest
I destroy it.*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 21 | printed col. 41 | bsb10175568]

Therefore the dead intercede for the living.

Response. The inference is denied, because the cited passage speaks of living
priests and princes, as Moses, Abraham, and Samuel formerly were, who
interceded for the people. Furthermore, God adds that he found no one. But, as
the Papists maintain, the saints always intercede in particular.

Objection 16. John 5:45: *There is one who accuses us: Moses.* Therefore the
saints in heaven are our patrons, who either accuse or defend us according to
our merits.

Response. This is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “Moses,” because there
it means not the person, but his doctrine, as in Luke 16:29. Furthermore, the
contrary follows: “He accuses us; therefore he is not our patron.”

Objection 17. If the saints in heaven know human affairs in the same ways as
the living do, then they intercede according to that knowledge. But the former
is true, because, first, they know by the report of others, namely the angels,
Genesis 28:12.

2. In the mirror of God’s essence, Exodus 33:19.

3. By sense and God’s permission. For if he permits those on earth to see the
things that are in heaven, why not conversely? Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, and the proofs are futile. First, Genesis
28 says nothing about a report made by angels to the saints. Rather, the
angels’ ascent and descent signifies God’s providence and the angels’ continual
guardianship.

2. Both Genesis 28 and Exodus 33 are a vision. Furthermore, they concern a
vision given both to Abraham and to Moses while alive here, not the fiction of
a vision of all things in God’s essence, which no one can see, as in John 1:18.

3. From the extraordinary vision of some who saw heavenly things while on
earth, such as Paul, Stephen, and others, there does not follow an ordinary
vision of all things in or outside God’s essence.
