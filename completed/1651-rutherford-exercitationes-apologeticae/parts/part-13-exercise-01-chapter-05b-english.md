12. Objection 5. “Just as, in absolute matters,” Suárez says, “when a cause
exists or is present to God's gaze as existing, it is truly affirmed in God's
mind that such an effect will flow freely from it—not because God sees this in
the power of the cause, but because by his gaze he sees that cause, although
free, as influencing that effect—so in these conditional matters, concerning
a cause conceived hypothetically as present under such a possible existence,
it can truly be affirmed and known in God's mind that this determinate effect
will flow from that free cause upon the same hypothesis; not because such an
effect is determinately known in the power of the cause, but because in itself
and in second act it would truly be determined to that act.” Reply. You hear,
reader, royal dictation: thus I will, thus I command; but he proves nothing.
By this he relieves himself of the burden. 1. For it is a fiction that an
effect in second act is to be determined in itself and determined by no cause.
What? Will it be God, having existence from no cause? If an indifferent cause
determines itself, it gives determinate being to itself. 2. If a contingent
effect has determinate being from God's will, which cannot be denied, does God
see that determinate being before his will has given it determinate being? He
then sees a nonbeing, which is invisible. For nothing possesses being and is
knowable before it has received being and become knowable; being is the
foundation of knowability. 3. Free determination as future in itself, he says,
is present hypothetically to God's eternal gaze. But prove that it is present
to the divine gaze antecedently to God's decree. Indeed, by no machinery of
ingenuity will you evade this. 4. God, he says, sees the effect

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Divine Grace*, part 1, question 2, chapter 2.]

[1651 logical printed p. 206 | IA leaf 224 | PDF p. 224]

as to be determined, not in the power of the cause, namely the contingent
cause, but in itself and in second act. But I then say that God does not see
the contingent effect as contingent but as necessary, which the adversaries
charge against us. Thus God does not know contingents as contingents. 5. I ask
whether a contingent effect is knowable in its cause. They will say no,
because the inclination of a contingent cause is indifferent toward either
side. But what then? It is indifferent disjunctively, I say, not copulatively.
Yet these men prefer to call God's knowledge itself into court rather than
suffer even the smallest thing to be taken away from their contingency. But
hear further the Jesuit making fortune an acting cause. For he denies that the
ground of the futurition of a contingent effect is to be taken from its cause.
“The form,” he says, “from which possible being in the cause is denominated
future is not the cause itself or its determination, as we followers of Calvin
(as he calls us) contend. For this,” he says, “is to remove the contingency of
effects and freedom of second causes. It is therefore a certain relation which
the thing as it is in the cause has to itself as it will be present in its own
time. This order in God's mind is the objective presence which that being has
in eternity, by reason of which that truth is known in the manner of a present
thing, though in order toward us it is denominated future.” Reply. I grant
that these things are said somewhat more subtly, but they contain no truth. 1.
That a possible is denominated future from a cause is ambiguous. It is not
denominated future simply from the second cause, but from the first cause; for
futurition is the tendency of a being from the state of possibility to the
state of actualization through the first cause. For from eternity there was no
cause besides God's will alone which could cause this tendency.

[Marginalia: Suárez, in the same place, folio 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 207 | IA leaf 225 | PDF p. 225]

2. If freedom is removed because a contingent effect has futurition from its
cause, does it have futurition antecedently to its cause? Then no cause will
be needed to give futurition if things possess futurition itself before actual
causation approaches. 3. If the form from which a possible is denominated
future is a relation or order which the thing as it exists in the cause has
to itself as it exists in time, that order terminates either necessarily or
contingently in the thing existing in time. If necessarily, the thing is both
necessarily future and contingent, which the Jesuit regards as a grave
absurdity. If it terminates contingently in the thing as it exists in time,
then the contingent future is not future, which is absurd. For every future,
whether contingent or necessary—indeed, even a possible future—is necessarily
future and cannot fail to be future; “future” is not a diminishing term. 4. I
ask which cause he understands when he says that the future is the relation of
a thing in its cause to the thing actually existing. He understands either
God's will, the first cause, or a contingent cause. If the latter, he
understands the contingent cause either as such or as determined. If the
former, futurition is not a relation of the thing in the cause

[1651 logical printed p. 208 | IA leaf 226 | PDF p. 226]

insofar as it is contingent. For a contingent cause as such is the cause of a
merely possible, not a future, thing. But if he understands the contingent
cause as determined, he understands it either as determined by God's will—and
thus we have the point at issue—or by the second contingent cause itself,
insofar as it is determined by itself. But the ground of eternal futurition
cannot be taken from a temporal determination made by a contingent cause. If
he instead wishes futurition to be the relation of a thing as it exists in
the first cause, let him teach this. For this first cause is God insofar as he
acts freely, and thus I still have the point at issue. 5. If futurition is the
relation of a thing as it is in its cause to itself, that order is something
determinate, for the future adds something determinate both to the possible
and to the contingent. But if it is determinate, the question remains: whence
is that determination? It is from nothing other than fortune, a constellation
(which must not be said), or God's will, which must necessarily be admitted.
6. The Jesuit always begs the question. For whence is it that such a relation
is an objective presence to the divine gaze and subject to knowledge? This
presence is either from the contingent effect itself, which is absurd; or
from God's will, in which God sees this present relation; or in some other
cause which they cannot supply.

13. Objection 6. If God knows future contingents only in the predetermining
decree, then he does not know future contingents as con-

[1651 logical printed p. 209 | IA leaf 227 | PDF p. 227]

tingents, for in that decree they are necessary, not contingent. Thus Suárez
in the same place, folio 4. “Nor can these be known otherwise than God knows
possibilities,” say the Remonstrants, “namely, by indeterminate knowledge or
the knowledge of simple intelligence, under the aspect of possible
existence.” Reply. This, of course, is a grave inconvenience—which we grant
without difficulty. See what Thomas Aquinas, the prince of Scholastics, says
for us here: “A contingent not yet determined to one side does not fall with
certainty under any knowledge.” He means that a contingent as contingent
cannot be known determinately as future. Indeed, a contingent as antecedent to
the decree, or as mentally considered in distinction from the decree, is
merely possible. God knows contingents most exactly and knows this to be their
nature: that they can exist or not exist—namely, by natural knowledge and
under possible being. But being future adds something to a contingent. For
one contingent is possible and another future; and we hold it impossible for
God to know future contingents as future except in his decree. They do not
cease to be contingent when through the decree they begin to be future, any
more than necessary things cease to be necessary because they are freely
willed by God. And things necessary in relation to God's will are contingent
because they could not exist if it had so seemed good to God. Thus
contingents in relation to God's determining decree are necessary in a
qualified respect and do not cease to be contingent. 2. Nothing is more
desired by us than for these doctors

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 6, article 4, folio 87.]

[Marginalia: Aquinas, First Part, question 14, article 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 210 | IA leaf 228 | PDF p. 228]

to show us that contingents, insofar as they are contingent and possess no
necessity at all, are the foundation of infallible knowledge. They shout to us
here that God sees truth, not necessity. But good God, how greatly we desire
them to demonstrate this to us with all the rhetorical apparatus they can
muster! Yet we hear mere assertions.

12. Although Peter Fonseca, a Jesuit especially renowned for more exact
philosophy, defends this middle knowledge as for altars and hearths and boasts
that he is its parent, having taught it orally in 1569 (although Luis de Molina
committed that wretched thing to writing in 1588), he nevertheless contends by
arguments not to be despised that contingents which will truly be future at
some time, if taken in an absolute state, are known only in an approving or
permissive act of the divine will. This Jesuitical mystery appears to me
greater than every wonder. For he wishes propositions about future
contingents to be determinately true or false before every act of the divine
will. Yet because God's free will is the first root and cause of every
contingency and freedom, he wishes absolutely future contingents to be known
only in God's approving or permissive will. Why this, Jesuit? “Because,” you
say, “as the thing exists or does not exist, the proposition is true or false.
Therefore, for God certainly to know what will occur contingently, he must
understand the act of his own will upon whose concurrence it depends.” Reply.
I then say that your middle knowledge is empty,

[1651 logical printed p. 211 | IA leaf 229 | PDF p. 229]

by which God knows future contingents antecedently to his own will, because
for the certainty of the knowledge he must first understand the act of his
will. The Jesuit wishes to soften this as follows: “Therefore,” he says, “it
must be carefully noted that future contingents are not denied to be certainly
foreknown by God in an instant of reason prior to that in which the divine will
determines itself to effect these or those things. We do not say this; rather,
we assert that God is not considered as knowing future contingents insofar as
they are future prior to being considered as knowing that his will concerning
their approval will freely determine itself to one side of the opposition. If
that will were not to be freely determined in the later instant of
consideration in which it determines itself, those contingents would not be
certainly known as future by the divine intellect in the earlier instant.”
Hence it is certain that the Jesuit grants what we ask, and consequently that
by middle knowledge God knows future contingents only as possible antecedently
to his free determination, not as future. But this is the knowledge of simple
intelligence, which we grant without difficulty, not middle knowledge. 2.
Since the Jesuit expressly says in the same place that “future contingents and
certain knowledge of them depend upon free acts of the divine will,” it is
evident that middle knowledge is thus overturned from its foundations. He
wishes it to be infallible antecedently to the acts of the divine will upon
which the futurition of free acts depends—a point Vasquez himself also grants:
“The creature,” he says, “is not yet future

[Marginalia: Vasquez, volume 1, disputation 65, chapter 2, folio 4.]

[1651 logical printed p. 212 | IA leaf 230 | PDF p. 230]

until the decree of God's will, from which it is future as from a cause, is
understood to exist.” Durandus says the same thing. 3. It seems to me wholly
inconsistent how a thing is not future except through the divine will as the
cause of futurition, and yet occurs freely on the Jesuit's principles; likewise
how a thing is freely future prior to being predefined by God, when
predefinition is the cause of the freely future thing. 4. Wondrous is the
mystery of the same Jesuit who denies that future contingents are certainly
known by God except in an approving act of his will—not indeed an act elicited
in that instant of reason in which contingents are future, but one to be
elicited in another instant of reason subsequent to their futurition. For in
that instant in which contingents are future, God's free act concerning their
futurition has not yet been elicited but is to be elicited. But either it must
be elicited by God necessarily, and thus is not a free act, or freely. If
freely, on their principles such an act can fail to be elicited, and thus the
futurition of a contingent thing is given without any act of the divine will,
which is impossible. Fonseca also admits in the same place, argument 2, that
God's free will is the first root and cause of every contingency, and that
contingent effects depend upon God's free act. But if the futurition of
contingents exists before God's free act is elicited, the free act is not the
cause of that futurition, and a particular contingent is prior to its root and
cause. 5. The Jesuit also says in the same place: “If every certainty of
future contingent acts depends upon the divine decrees alone, how can those
decrees fail to necessitate

[Marginalia: Durandus, book 1, distinction 39, question 1.]

[1651 logical printed p. 213 (partial) | IA leaf 231 | PDF p. 231]

the wills to all their acts?” But I ask whether there is certainty of future
free acts antecedently to God's decrees. If there is, fundamental and
entitative necessity is also in them antecedently to God's decrees. Therefore
God does not have it within his power to decree free acts otherwise than they
exist antecedently to God's decrees. Let the Jesuit now choose whether he
wishes God or human beings to be necessitated to free acts. 2. It seems to
follow from this that free acts have two necessities, one antecedent and one
subsequent to God's decrees. But why must God foreknow future free acts in an
approving and permissive act if those acts are already most certain to God
before every approving and permissive act of God's will? But let us leave this
shameful mystery to Ammonius, among the first authors of this knowledge as
Diego Álvarez observes, and to these innovators.
