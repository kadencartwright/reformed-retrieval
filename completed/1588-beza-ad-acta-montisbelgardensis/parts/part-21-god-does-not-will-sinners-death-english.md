## Part XXI: In What Sense God Does Not Will the Sinner’s Death

In what sense God does not will the sinner’s death

Andreae dares affirm that Scripture teaches the contrary, namely that God does not will any person’s death. This is as false if referred to God’s judgment—since he no more judges unwillingly than he shows mercy, both agreeing with his nature, as the second commandment declares and common sense dictates—as it is true if *not willing* is referred to what he approves not simply but only in a certain respect. As Augustine excellently teaches in *To Simplicianus* 1.2, one must distinguish man insofar as he is man from man insofar as he is sinner. God does not delight in a person’s condemnation insofar as he is human, but insofar as he is a sinner. Moreover, he does not simply will even a sinner’s condemnation, but wills it insofar as his glory is displayed by hatred and punishment of sin, according to Solomon: “God made everything for himself, even the ungodly for the evil day” (Proverbs 16:4).

To 1

God willingly decrees not only the end but also the intermediate causes of reprobation.

What can you do with a man who goes astray so often in so few words? First, as we have repeatedly said, contempt for the Word and for offered grace in Christ pertains not to everyone—indeed, to few compared with the multitude of the lost—since infinitely more people are never offered Christ or the gospel than reject him when offered. Thus even if this answer were not false, it would still be incomplete and mutilated. Next, in his usual manner he confuses the cause of abandonment with the decree to abandon through that cause, although the former is necessarily subordinated to the latter. Third, unless Andreae acknowledges that God’s decree determines not only the final end but also intermediate causes and therefore precedes abandonment itself, those whom God abandons to perish are abandoned rashly and without his knowledge—which is nothing other than denying God’s particular providence. Once this is conceded, subordinate causes do not remove the first cause. Andreae’s conclusion is no less inept than if, because natural or various accidents end this life, he sought to exclude God’s decree concerning its beginning and end, which is so fixed that neither can be hastened nor prolonged by even a moment. His imagined “absolute decree of condemnation,” as though we taught that by God’s mere pleasure some are condemned without causes of condemnation inhering in those condemned, is an empty charge.

An explanation of Matthew 23:37 and reconciliation of the apparent conflict between external and internal calling

Concerning Matthew’s passage, Christ must be considered there as discharging the ministerial office, both through the prophets’ mouths in prior ages and personally when he spoke these words. He discusses outward preaching of the Word to the whole church, where reprobate and elect are mixed, not the inward calling which is wholly of divine power and flows from God’s good pleasure to the elect alone. Through it God distinguishes those whom he decreed to choose from those destined to just abandonment and subsequent reprobation and draws them to himself in their time. God did not disclose this eternal secret counsel about who among their hearers would or would not believe to the prophets or apostles, except occasionally by particular revelation—as to Moses concerning Pharaoh and the Egyptians generally, and Isaiah concerning those to whom he was sent. Hence Jonah’s indignation, deservedly chastised by God. Hence Paul, although with the best will desiring to announce the Lord’s Word in Asia and Bithynia, was forbidden by God’s Spirit.

Likewise, Paul was restrained by God when repeatedly attempting with excellent purpose to come to Rome. Thus his Jewish brothers sought, against both God’s and Paul’s will and with prophetic testimonies added, to prevent his final journey to Jerusalem. Whence this disagreement? Not from the opposition by which people sin in resisting God’s commands, but from ignorance of God’s secret counsel, while justly and probably presuming what they thought God willed. Their will, although in some measure contrary to God’s secret will—for they willed what God did not—was nevertheless by no means displeasing to God. How so? If we consider the impulsive cause of those two conflicting wills, there was excellent agreement. What they willed and even desired with a good will relative to their goal, God did not will with a wiser and better will, to which they submitted once it was revealed. Good, insofar as it is good, can differ from another good but cannot oppose it.

Hence the holy disagreement between God and his ministers, when, inflamed with zeal for God, they desire indiscriminately that everyone be saved, even those whom they do not know are outside God’s flock. They offer ardent prayers even for persons whom they might appear to know do not belong to the elect, and their prayers are therefore ineffectual. Samuel prays almost obstinately for Saul, and Jeremiah for preservation of a people destined for destruction, until they understand God had decreed otherwise. Hence the many rebukes and laments by which many saints eventually learned they had vainly hoped to move some people.

Someone will object that no prophet, apostle, or ordinary person speaks here, but the eternal Son of God, from whom even as man nothing could be hidden. What if I answer with Luther, whose clear testimony I cited elsewhere, that Christ according to the flesh knew only as much as the Father and he himself as God disclosed to himself, since he confesses that the Son—that is, himself insofar as man—did not know the last day (Mark 13:32)? Although many ancients, with an ill-directed zeal for God’s glory, twisted that passage, Nazianzen rightly attributed it to Christ’s humanity distinctly considered. As Christ has two natures, one infinite and the other finite, we must establish in him two kinds of knowledge and will: one infinite, essential deity itself; the other a created, finite quality whose limits—

—the Word himself with Father and Holy Spirit circumscribed for himself in the days of his flesh and enlarged as he pleased after his ineffable exaltation, incomprehensible even to angels. Attributing human things to humanity and divine things to deity does not divide the person with the Nestorians, but distinguishes the natures against the ancient Eutychians and Monothelites and the monster of ubiquitous omnipresence compounded from both. Nor does it attribute any fault to Christ’s humanity. Far from it. Vicious ignorance, which does not know what was revealed and ought to be known, is entirely different from that measure of knowledge by which every created thing necessarily stands below divine infinity.

I do not wish to pursue this question further. I acknowledge that Christ according to his humanity knew the prophecies of that people’s rejection which, as God, he had suggested to prophets, and by experience knew and foretold his enemies’ incurable stubbornness and knew his betrayer by name beforehand. It does not follow that according to his humanity and as the Father’s minister to the circumcised he knew every individual who would not believe. He knew them only afterward, as the schools say, and insofar as he as God disclosed them to himself as man. John 6:64 does not add “from the beginning” rashly; this phrase designates what should be attributed to Christ according to deity, not humanity. Cyril writes: “He knew all, faithful and unbelieving alike, and the very minister of that impious deed. No wonder: he knew everything even before it was produced.”

Thus Christ as minister of his gospel indiscriminately willed and strove to gather all his hearers, as faithful ministers do while leaving God’s secret counsel to him. Yet because “from the beginning” can refer to the beginnings of various things, I grant, to avoid appearing contentious, that this is to be understood personally of Christ according to both natures. Still, Christ’s will as man—and further, as chosen minister to preach his true gospel to the Jews—by which he willed and, if possible, sought the salvation even of those most desperate adversaries of his Father and himself, must be distinguished from the eternal immutable will by which he knew their destruction, like all things without exception, had been most wisely decreed and justly ordained. No opposition but only diversity must be established between these wills, upright and faultless because it proceeds from the best source, supreme love of humanity, and tends to the best end, God’s glory. As regards the intermediate course of dispensation—

—it depends upon the condition “if it pleased God,” although not expressed in those words. Moses understood he could not be erased from the book of life, yet sincerely wished either to be erased or the people’s sin forgiven; he obtained the latter from the Lord, though not without condition. Paul was prepared to redeem his people’s rejection by his own separation from Christ, though he knew it could not occur and would not have desired it in another respect. Kind fathers sincerely warn and chastise children of whom they expect nothing good and whose unhappy end they see as certainly as if present, eventually handing them to the magistrate not from hatred but necessity. We read such examples even in profane histories: Brutus preferring his country’s safety, and Manlius public authority, to paternal love, their minds undoubtedly struggling between two very different and, simply considered, contrary wills.

What prevents our most gracious Savior, faultlessly and with testimony of immense love for humanity, from willing their salvation and, if possible, seeking it as minister of the circumcision, while as God and author with Father and Spirit of the decree he knew and approved their inevitable destination by just judgment? Who could think he disapproved what the Father approved, since the sacred Trinity has one nature and will? If Andreae calls these GLOSSES, let him believe Christ, who in Matthew 11:25 not only acknowledges this decree but thanks the Father: “You hid these things from the wise and intelligent and disclosed them to infants, because this was your good pleasure.” These were the matters by which he had vainly sought to gather those over whom he later lamented. He does not cast the cause of the decree upon their stubbornness, as those do who confuse the causes of the decree to reprobate and of the reprobates’ condemnation, but rises to that good pleasure equally regarding both members: infants saved by mercy and the others justly destroyed. Cyril distinguishes these and asks why unbelievers’ sins were not mercifully forgiven: “God is not therefore unjust in reserving wrath for them; as just by nature, he wills and determines everything consistently with his nature, although we do not know the way of his dispensation.”

Even if I grant Andreae that Christ speaks according to deity, I say that in a certain respect God wills them—not as saved persons—

—to be gathered into his church whom in another respect he determined would not be gathered, namely by his immutable eternal decree. This is not because he can simply refuse what he wills or will what he refuses, but in view of the means he proposes even to some reprobates: that patience which Paul says in Romans 9:22 he exercises toward vessels fitted for wrath. He endures until they, not because God failed to heal their unbelief nor from weakness of judgment, but solely by deliberate and desperate wickedness against conscience, either suffocate natural knowledge within themselves, as in Acts 14:17 and Romans 1:18; or, much more, knowingly reject gospel truth of which they stand convicted, like those over whom Christ so often complains; or voluntarily vomit out what they had tasted, concerning whom the Apostle announces that dreadful judgment in Hebrews 6:4 onward.
