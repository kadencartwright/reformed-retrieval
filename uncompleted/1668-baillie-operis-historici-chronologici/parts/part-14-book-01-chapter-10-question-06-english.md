### QUESTION VI

#### Concerning the fast of Esther and the Jews in chapter 4, verse 16: whether they abstained from all drink and all food for three full days and three nights?

Answer. The words of the text expressly imply this. The English annotators
therefore rightly reject what Diodati advances here after the ordinary gloss,
and Lyra’s interpretation that they abstained only from the bread which
Daniel calls desirable and from delicate food. It is indeed true that in
Daniel 10:3 Daniel interprets his fast of three weeks as abstinence from wine
and delicate bread, and the seven-day fast of the men of Jabesh-gilead in 1
Samuel 31:13 must necessarily be interpreted in this way. For according to
the teaching of Hippocrates and the physicians, healthy people do not live
after seven days without food. Some sick persons, in whom phlegm abounds and
innate heat is severely deficient, have persisted longer in fasting; and
through divine or diabolical power Hakewill, in his excellent *Apology for
the Providence of God*, gives examples of many who without any fraud
abstained from absolutely all food and drink for two, three, nine, and
fourteen full years. The fasts of Moses on Mount Sinai and of Christ in the
temptation were of this kind. But these extraordinary miracles stand above
all rules; we are speaking about ordinary and regulated cases.

> Marginalia, printed p. 156: *Lyra’s bad explanation*; *A three-day fast is
> not supernatural*.

When Paul asserts in Acts 27:33 that those sailing with him had fasted for
fourteen full days and tasted nothing, we must interpret it, with Beza,
Cornelius à Lapide, and the general run of Protestant and Roman Catholic
commentators, as a hyperbole sufficiently common in ordinary speech: not that
they had tasted absolutely nothing, but that in such danger of death and
agony of spirit they had tasted so little that it was virtually nothing, and
had neglected every ordinary refreshment at lunches and dinners.

> Marginalia, printed p. 156: *Paul’s hyperbole concerning a fourteen-day
> fast*.

But no necessity compels us to apply a distinction of this kind to the
present passage in Esther, for an absolute fast of three days and three nights
is compatible with human life and even with health. The Egyptian young man in
1 Samuel 30:12 was discovered by David to have abstained from all food and
drink for three days and three nights; and the infirmity from which he
suffered is judged to have arisen not so much from lack of food as from his
earlier illness.

We need not resort here to the astonishing endurance of hunger which southern
peoples possess beyond northern peoples, discussed by Bodin in his *Method*
as a product of their melancholic temperament caused by the sun’s external
scorching. For even among the northerners examples are not lacking of fasts
beyond three days. David Joris, father of the Familists, and some of his
followers even today are said to have persisted for four full days and four
nights and beyond in their diabolical fasts. Why, then, should we not grant
Esther an absolute and wholly unqualified three-day fast in the very
extraordinary circumstance of ruin threatening the entire Jewish nation? In
this case, indeed, we are unwilling to call it indiscriminate abstinence with
Lyra. We admit that the ordinary Jewish fasts, even the most solemn, in
Leviticus 23:32 did not last beyond one day and one night, from evening to
evening; but Esther’s situation far surpassed all those annual solemnities.

There still remains some doubt here. The fast in chapter 4 is said to last
three days and nights, yet in chapter 5, verse 1 Esther invited the king to
her banquet on the third day; therefore she did not fast on that third day.
Lyra’s solution is unsatisfactory, and Junius’s even less so. Lyra would have
the passage understood synecdochically for parts of three days. We grant that
this distinction is sound and must be applied to certain passages of
Scripture, such as the one about Christ’s three days in the tomb; but the
passage before us rejects it, for it explicitly speaks of three days and
three nights in which the Jews were to taste nothing.

> Marginalia, printed p. 156: *Whether Esther fasted on the third day*.

Junius’s solution seems worse, for he adds to the text at chapter 5, verse 1,
“on the third [completed] day.” The Hebrew has nothing corresponding to
“completed.” We admit that all interpreters sometimes rightly add certain
expository words to the original text, but in another typeface—so that they
are distinguished from the text—and only such words as the text requires, or
at least admits. But Junius’s addition is written in the letters used for the
text, and the text neither requires nor even admits it. For what happened
after the third day was completed happened either on the fourth day or only
during the night of the third day. But Esther’s first request was presented
to Ahasuerus neither on the fourth day—since it was on the third—nor during
the night of the third day. For she entered the royal court in broad daylight
while the king sat on his throne judging the people, not at night, when public
judgments cease.

> Marginalia, printed p. 156: *Junius’s bad solution*.

We must therefore answer differently, in this or a similar manner: namely,
that the Jews continued the fast through all three days and three nights,
especially on the third day and the third night, when prayers to God had most
of all to be poured out for Esther while she was engaged in the very
undertaking appointed. As for Esther herself, either she too continued her
fast

on the third day—for although the king and Haman feasted with her on the
third day, it is not stated that she feasted with them—or, if that appears
too subtle to anyone, we may say that no vow bound her to fast throughout the
whole third day. For the words in chapter 4, verse 16, “I and my maidservants
will fast likewise,” can refer to the substance, that is, their actual fast,
but not to every nonessential circumstance of it, and especially not to the
third day. Indeed, the obligation and vow itself almost explicitly indicates
that exception when she signifies that she will go in to the king while they
are fasting, so that her supplications to the king might be offered together
with their prayers to God.

> Marginalia, printed pp. 156–157: *The Jews, but not Esther herself,
> continued the fast throughout the whole third day*.
