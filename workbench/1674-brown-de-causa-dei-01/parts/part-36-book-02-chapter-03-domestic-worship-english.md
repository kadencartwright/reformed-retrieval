CHAPTER III

On Social Worship, Especially Domestic Worship

No Christian will deny that God must be worshiped in the church instituted by
God, since the visible church was instituted principally for this end. And the
law of nature itself teaches that every human society is bound to proclaim
God’s praises, acknowledge his supreme and independent dominion, and attest
its dependence upon him, as is clear from what was said in the preceding
chapter. This is also confirmed by the practice of all nations, at least the
more civilized ones.

When the number of households increased (within whose bounds the church had
previously subsisted), people began to call upon the name of God (Genesis
4:26), that is, publicly in public assemblies consisting of various
households. For it is not probable that אז הוחל לקרא בשם יהוה denotes that
people then began to profane the name of God. See the celebrated Owen, *On
Theology*, book 2, chapter 3, pages 144–145, and Dr. Hoornbeeck, *Practical
Theology*, book 9, chapter 1, page 360.

But because the adversaries hardly contradict us on this point—for they freely
admit that God is to be worshiped publicly in the church, and they maintain
that this is specially required in the fourth precept of the Decalogue, along
with (only by consequence) the time required for performing this solemn
worship of God as a necessarily attached adjunct—it is not necessary to dwell
longer upon explaining or confirming it. We will say something about it in
book 6, where we will treat the sanctification of the Lord’s Day.

But in order to say something about domestic worship—which the adversaries do
not openly deny, although they urge it little, or at least consider it little,
when they speak about the sanctification of the Sabbath—it must be noted first
that the word *household* sometimes embraces all who are of the same blood and
kinship, or who have sprung from the same stock (2 Samuel 14:7). It thus
denotes a whole tribe (Judges 18:2, 11; Leviticus 20:5; Jeremiah 8:3; Micah
2:3), and sometimes a whole nation (Zechariah 14:18; Exodus 19:3; Leviticus
10:6; 2 Samuel 1:

[1674 logical printed p. 247 | IA leaf 265 | PDF p. 265]

12; Jeremiah 2:4; 31:1). Here, however, by a *household* we understand only those
who are in the same house under the authority of one and the same master and
father of the household.

That God ought to be worshiped in this domestic society is confirmed by the
following considerations.

1. All the reasons which prove that God must be worshiped in any human society
prove it. For he is the Lord and author of all societies, and no less the
author and Lord of domestic society than of others. Every society,
acknowledging this supreme dominion and power of God, must therefore pay him
this tribute.

2. God’s glory and honor especially demand this. For he makes all things for
himself and his own glory, and therefore institutes households and domestic
societies for himself, namely, that he may be glorified through them, or that
they may celebrate his praises. It is therefore moral that a domestic society
also solemnly worship God. And nature, as it appears, taught this to the
nations, for they had their household gods, whom they worshiped.

3. Nature also teaches that everyone ought to celebrate God according to the
calling, function, and relation which he has by God’s gift. Therefore every
father of a household is strictly bound to devote himself and his household to
God and, in that relation, to advance God’s glory as far as he can. He must
also labor so that his household may be a little church of Christ and a
tabernacle of God, in which God may dwell, manifest himself, and declare his
name.

4. That something particular is required from parents and fathers of
households with respect to their children and the rest of the household is
clear from Deuteronomy 6:6–7: “These words which I command you today shall be
in your heart, and you shall diligently impress them upon your children and
speak of them when you sit in your house, when you walk along the way, when
you lie down, and when you rise.” The Lord says in praise of Abraham in Genesis
18:17, 19: “Shall I hide from Abraham what I am about to do? … For I have known
him, so that when he commands his children and his household after him, they
may keep the way of Jehovah by practicing righteousness and justice.” Joshua
pledges for himself and his household in chapter 24, verse 15. See also
Ephesians 6:4.

5. General duties, such as that we *love God with our whole heart*, and the
like, prove the same thing. For one cannot conceive how a parent or father of a
household can be said to love God with his whole heart if he does not desire,
wish, and make every effort to bring with him to God all those over whom he
has and can exercise any authority, and to join them with himself in
celebrating God’s praises. The godly burn with such zeal for God that they
often call, by apostrophe, upon all animate and inanimate creatures to praise
God with them, as may be seen in Psalms 69:34 and 148:2–4. David often invites
and exhorts others to praise God with him. See Psalms 106:1, 48; 111:1; 112:1;
113:1, 9; 115:18; 116:19; 117:2; 135:1; 146:1, 10; and 147:20. He often urges
the same thing elsewhere. How much more, then, will parents and masters who
love God with their whole heart do this—that is, invite and exhort their
children and servants to worship God with them?

6. Does God not grant many things by his pure good pleasure and freely confer
benefits upon households? What experienced father of a household will not
testify to this? Are not, therefore,

[1674 logical printed p. 248 | IA leaf 266 | PDF p. 266]

all who share these benefits bound to acknowledge God as the sole author of
all these good things? What sober person will deny this?

7. Christian communion requires the same thing. For if those who live in the
same neighborhood ought to cultivate ecclesiastical communion, why should
those who are in the same household not be bound to have household communion?
Do we not read of churches in households? Surely every household ought to be a
church. And because, by reason of this communion, they share in common the
same blows which the just God inflicts for the sins of members of the
household, especially the father of the household—as we see in Genesis 20:7,
17–18; 12:17; Joshua 7:24–25; 1 Kings 13:10–11; 1 Chronicles 21:14; and 1
Samuel 2:31–33—every consideration requires that they join together to turn
away God’s wrath by prayers and to humble themselves before God, and so forth.

8. The good of the whole household and of all its members requires the same
thing, because in this way God’s wrath will be turned away, which he would
otherwise pour out even upon households which do not call upon his name,
according to Jeremiah 10:25. Conversely, they will share in God’s blessing,
for the Lord is accustomed to bless those households which seek and worship
him, as is clear from Acts 10:4. Cornelius often prayed to God with his
household, and God blessed the whole household and illumined it with the light
of the gospel. God distinguishes between those who seek him and those who do
not seek him (Malachi 3:16, 18). That statement of Peter in his first epistle,
chapter 3, verse 7, also sufficiently indicates this domestic duty.

9. Hence Scripture prescribes all the Christian duties of every member of a
household as such: what parents are to do toward children and children toward
parents; what is likewise required from the father and mother of the household
toward one another; and, finally, what the master and mistress owe to their
servants and what servants in turn owe to their masters. Sometimes it
prescribes duties common to them all (Zechariah 12 and elsewhere).

10. Moreover, that this is a moral duty is clear from the fact that those who
performed this duty are praised precisely for it, as Abraham is in Genesis 18;
that godly fathers of households attended to it, such as Joshua (24:15) and
David (Psalm 101; Psalm 30; 2 Samuel 6); that it is promised as a fruit of the
outpouring of the Spirit (Zechariah 12:12); that it is commanded (Deuteronomy
6:7–8; 12:18–19); and that those who neglected this duty were to be removed
from every ecclesiastical office (1 Timothy 4:4–5, 8; Titus 1:6). Who,
therefore, will deny that a duty is moral when it is urged in so many and such
various ways—by precepts, examples, promises, blessings, threatenings, and so
forth?

But perhaps the adversaries will say that this domestic worship should not be
called solemn worship. Yet why, I ask? Is it because it is not performed by an
entire church? But what if that household is a church, which is not so
impossible? And what if that household lives among the nations, as Abraham’s
household lived for a time in Gerar (Genesis 20:1) and in Egypt (Genesis 12),
where it can have no others with whom to exercise ecclesiastical worship? Will
it therefore exercise no solemn worship at all? When the Israelites ate the
Passover house by house, did they not worship God solemnly? And when Esther
with her maidens, and all the Jews who were present at Susa, fasted and prayed
for three whole days (Esther 4:16), was that not solemn worship?

When the adversaries make public worship a moral element of the fourth
precept, they should not exclude this domestic worship, which is likewise
public, not solitary, and solemn, not ejaculatory. This is especially so since
the commandment itself expressly speaks about these domestic relations and
prescribes to the father of the household, as such, something which is in some
way common to him and the others and which all within the gates can and ought
to perform. But we will treat this domestic worship more fully in book 6,
where the sanctification of the Lord’s Day will be discussed. We will show
that the fourth precept itself specifically enjoins this worship of God on the
Sabbath day.

Yet this further point must not be omitted here: for some time the public and
solemn worship of God was celebrated only in households, namely, before people
began to call upon the name of God in public assemblies of the church (Genesis
4:26). But no one will say that at that time—from the beginning, that is, until
the birth of Enosh—this was not a moral duty. And if it was a moral duty at
that time, why will it not also be a moral duty now? Solemn and public worship
in the church’s public assemblies no more abolishes or destroys domestic
worship than domestic worship abolishes or destroys solitary worship.

Indeed, the nations themselves had some domestic worship in addition to the
public worship which they rendered to their gods in temples. For besides the
gods whom they worshiped publicly, they also had *Penates* or *Lares*, that is,
household gods, whom they honored in a special way because they expected from
them the protection and safety of the household.

Therefore, since this duty was always performed by the godly—before the flood,
after the flood, before the law by Job and Abraham, by others under the law,
and under the New Testament—and is not ceremonial, it must be moral and must
oblige us now.

Furthermore, that duty must be regarded as necessary which is urged in
Scripture in many ways: 1. by command (Deuteronomy 6:7–8; 11:18–19, and also
in the fourth precept of the Decalogue); 2. by the examples of godly people,
Abraham, David, Joshua, and others; 3. by God’s commendation, by which he
praises such practice, for Abraham is commended on this account; 4. by the
threatening of evils against those who neglect this duty (Jeremiah 10:25); and,
consequently, 5. by the promise of good things to those who perform it. 6. It
is promised as a special benefit (Zechariah 12:11–14), and indeed as a special
fruit of the Spirit and companion of repentance in the same passage. 8. One
who neglects this duty is declared unfit to discharge any ecclesiastical
office and, as a scandalous person, is therefore to be rejected (1 Timothy
3:5). Conversely, 9. one who faithfully performs this duty is to that extent
regarded as fit, for the performance of this duty is numbered among the
required qualifications.

From all these things it is abundantly manifest that domestic worship of God
is a necessary duty required proportionately from every father and member of
a household; that is, households are bound to institute domestic worship for
God’s praise. This seems to require all the more urging because God threatens
through Jeremiah 10:25 that he will pour out his indignation upon the
households which do not call upon his name. Nor is it sufficient to say that
*households* here means *peoples and nations*. For peoples are not otherwise
named in the text, and there is no reason pertinent to the point for excluding
households and understanding only heathen nations here. For if that nation

[1674 logical printed p. 250 | IA leaf 268 | PDF p. 268]

which does not call upon God deserves to be called heathen, so also must that
household which does not call upon God be called heathen and therefore be
included here. Moreover, it must be noted that this judgment of God is
threatened against nations not insofar as they are nations, but insofar as they
do not call upon God. This threatening is therefore directed against everyone,
whether entire nations, entire households, or individual persons. And if God
does not let individual persons or entire nations go unpunished for this
offense, should households, or smaller societies guilty of the same crime, be
thought to escape? Does not an entire kingdom consist of private persons and
households, and are kingdoms not such as their households and private persons
are? Where the households which constitute the parts of a kingdom are heathen
and do not call upon God, there the whole kingdom is heathen. Conversely,
where households call upon God, the whole kingdom should not be called
heathen.
