CHAPTER XVIII.

V. 1. He who separates himself seeks according to his own pleasure; he meddles in everything that exists.

The sum is that a man delighted with his own pursuits, and despising others in comparison with himself, while he follows those things which please his own mind, rashly thrusts himself upon everyone, like a busybody, πολυπράγμονα.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 370 | printed columns 714-715]

Men were born to maintain and preserve society and fellowship among themselves. This indeed is so true that there have been very wise men and the greatest philosophers who were going to say that one who separates himself from the society of others is either a god or a wild beast, Aristotle, *Politics* 1—though they would rather have said either a god or a demon, if indeed they had known anything of the condemned demons. Yet this proverb seems to be understood not only of the man who separates himself from the fellowship of other men, but also of those who follow certain pursuits of their own in which they try to separate themselves from others. Of both kinds it is said that they inquire into and thoroughly investigate matters according to their own pleasure, and then meddle in every business. Here the worst effects follow from an evil cause; and the cause is readily inferred: confidence in oneself and in one’s own wisdom, which Solomon greatly condemns, Prov. 3. 5. 6.

Then the first effect is that such a man inquires into and investigates matters according to his own pleasure. For those who are proud in mind and conceive in their minds some greater opinion of themselves, being puffed up by this elevation of spirit, reckon that they were born for themselves and ought to live and die for themselves. Indeed, they consider that others also ought to be the ministers and providers of their desire. If anyone asks them the reason for their action, nothing is more customary for them than immediately to answer that they do this or that because it has so pleased them. See an example of this in 2 Sam. 13. 28, where he gives no other reason for the command to kill Amnon than that he himself ordered it to be done. But if a man is under the authority of parents or of a master, he ought to direct himself and his pursuits at their command; whereas if he is his own master, he still must not immediately undertake whatever pleases him, but must examine the powers and capacities with which he is endowed, and must thoroughly learn which gifts of God have been conferred upon us, so that we may devote ourselves especially to those things for handling and accomplishing which we are chiefly equipped, and may be of the greatest possible benefit to ourselves and others.

A further effect is that he thrusts himself upon everyone and meddles both in his own affairs and in those of others. For he has altogether resolved to serve himself and to please his own mind in everything; hence, if weariness with his own affairs ever seizes him, he flies across to the affairs of others. For this reason the Apostle in Rom. 12, in order to recall every citizen of the Church to the handling of his own affairs, exhorts them to a modest and moderate opinion of themselves. And David, to prove himself free from ambition and not a seeker of things too high for him, pleads the humility of his spirit and affirms that his spirit was as humble as a newly weaned infant, Psal. 131. By contrast, in Monks who withdrew themselves from the fellowship and society of men we have seen a zeal for pleasing themselves, their own mind, and their own inclination; and then, amid the greatest leisure, a restless zeal to meddle in matters to which they were plainly unequal. For they thrust themselves into nearly all the writings of the ancients, which they corrupted either treacherously or through ignorance. Through an excessive zeal for pleasing themselves, some also betook themselves to Magic and to the Ephesian arts, as they are called.

V. 2. A fool is not delighted with understanding, but with continually disclosing his heart.

The sum is that an ungodly man, bearing a mind estranged from every good, is inwardly delighted in admitting evils.

In the first part it is taught that the Proverb applies universally to all the ungodly: they are led by no delight in piety and virtue. It is not denied that they can learn the things which belong to piety, and can even possess them when perceived and comprehended; nor indeed is it denied them that outwardly they can occupy themselves in the

[1632 witness | PDF p. 371 | printed columns 716-717]

exercises of piety; but it is altogether unfitting for them to take delight in doing good. For this is the proper and peculiar mark of God’s children: as in the law of God, Psal. 1, so also in other fruits of piety and uprightness, to be engaged with delight and cheerfulness of spirit, John 4. 34. For the carrying out of their heavenly Father’s commands is food and drink to them, indeed honey and honeycomb. But the ungodly are occupied in God’s worship like beasts driven by goads or slaves driven by whips.

This may seem to be contradicted by what appears in Matt. 13 concerning the ungodly, who hear God’s word with joy, and by what is written of Herod in Mark 6, that he heard John gladly and did many things commanded by him. Nevertheless, the truth of this Proverb of Solomon stands most firmly established. For that delight was not in God’s word itself insofar as it was God’s word, but in the advantage, pleasure, or dignity which it brought, or in the hope of obtaining these things. Nor did they set before their eyes the approval of their pursuits by God and the exact measuring of the course of their life by the rule of the divine word; or, if they did this, they did not do it under the impulse of love for God, but only so that they might escape death or obtain eternal life, without any zeal for obedience to God drawing them.

In the latter part he teaches that the ungodly man is drenched with the greatest pleasure and delight when he betrays the wickedness of his heart by words or deeds. In the exercises of the godly he is occupied unwillingly, like a stranger, or rather an exile; among ungodly things, as on his own soil and in his own home, he willingly spends himself, and is borne and rushes toward them with his whole force. He approaches the exercises of piety like a bear that must be tied to a stake; he flies to wickedness like an eagle to its prey. In this one may wonder that men whom nature has taught to conceal the shame of the body and remove it from the perception of the eyes should nevertheless be so foolish as to expose their heart—the foulest of all things, and the seed and cause of all foulness—to the eyes and knowledge of everyone; and should not consider that the nakedness and foulness of the heart is more hateful to God than to men are those parts of the body which have an unseemly appearance. But if we consider our depravity, which is natural, we shall lay aside all that wonder. For either, like infants, we do not perceive our nakedness, or, like madmen and lunatics, we boast of our nakedness, of which we ought to be ashamed. Thus there happens to us what Jude records of certain men: we foam out our own disgrace just as raging seas cast out foam, v. 13.

Let us therefore examine ourselves here: with what spirit we come to sacred assemblies and the other exercises of piety, with what delight, and with what spirit we remain in them. For if we gladly spend the whole day betraying the ignorance of our heart, and do so without any satiety, yet cannot endure calmly and with equal composure to spend one hour or another in divine worship without the utmost distaste and nausea, let us beware lest we gather against ourselves a testimony of wickedness and impiety, our own conscience bark at us, and strike us with a testimony of reprobation. Yet, lest a noose for strangling himself be rashly tied for anyone, it must be known that the godly do not listen to God as he unfolds his hidden wisdom with the same readiness and cheerfulness with which the ungodly belch out their impiety. The reason is that, while the ungodly hasten or fly toward evil with their whole force, the godly, renewed and cleansed in one part of themselves but in the other still clinging in the mire of sinful desire, are not as wholly girded for good as the ungodly are for evil. With this stain of our nature still clinging in us, we are as it were lame in one foot and like birds with clipped wings, and cannot hasten toward good as swiftly as they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 372 | printed columns 718-719]

can run or fly toward evil. Against this corruption of nature we must struggle with hands and feet.

V. 3. When the ungodly man comes, contempt comes also, and with the man of dishonour comes reproach.

The sum is that contempt is the inseparable companion of wickedness, and that contempt increases in proportion to increased wickedness.

By nature’s leading all men seek honour and flee contempt. Yet, though they are led by zeal for honour and possess a spirit that abhors contempt, they do not enter the way by which they may either obtain honour or avoid contempt. For since both honour and contempt are in God’s hand—the former with which he crowns the upright, 1 Sam. 2. 30, and the latter with which he surrounds and encompasses the ungodly—how many will be found who either pursue uprightness so that they may be honoured, or withdraw from wickedness so that they may remove contempt and dishonour from themselves? What is graver and fouler: how many will be found who do not think that the shortest road to obtaining honour and glory is to turn aside from God’s commands, and that they are furthest from contempt when they have withdrawn far from obedience to God? What is gravest and foulest of all: there are those who regard wickedness as the only road to honours and suppose that only by contempt of God can contempt be driven and thrust away from us.

In order that the opinion of all these men might be diminished, or rather entirely uprooted, Solomon has acted by this Proverb. One cannot sufficiently wonder at men’s ignorance, or rather madness. They cannot deny that exaltation comes neither from the east nor from the west, and so forth, but that God is the judge who casts this man down and lifts that one up, Psal. 75. 6. 7. Yet they establish so perverse a road to honours that, in order to obtain honour from men, they accommodate themselves to men’s will; in order to avoid the contempt which they fear men will inflict, they guard against even the least offence to them. And do they think they can so mock God, the most just and wise judge, as to suppose that by their rebellion against him the fountain of his goodness and generosity will be opened and unsealed for them? What else is this than by the wicked treachery of the heart to take away the distribution of honours and the pouring out of contempt which with their mouth they ascribe to God?

Therefore Solomon, in order to break this wicked opinion, employs figurative speech coloured by that figure which may move and strike men’s minds: namely prosopopoeia, concerning which manner of speaking see above the annotations on 11. 2. Thus neither a hare-hound nor a keen or keen-scented dog pursues the hare with greater zeal, striving, or swiftness than contempt and dishonour pursue the ungodly man; or rather, keeping equal step, it encircles his side, ready in its own time to lay its hands upon him.

Nor is there such cunning by which anyone to whom disgrace is due can free himself, nor has anyone such power or strength as to drive away dishonour when it rushes upon him. For contempt sent from heaven by God can no more be repelled than rain, lightning, or anything of that kind, so that it should fail to accomplish the purpose for which it was sent. Hence it is said that “God pours contempt upon princes,” Job 12. 21. Witnesses are the King of Babylon and many of the Kings of Israel and Judah.

The truth of this is confirmed by the fact that God does not so spare even the godly and elect as to refrain from visiting them with dishonour and exposing them to contempt because of the evil deeds they commit. For David’s adultery so stripped him of the dignity

[1632 witness | PDF p. 373 | printed columns 720-721]

and honour in which he had excelled that first his own son and then his servant conspired against him, and Shimei hurled insults at him. On account of his numbering the people, the punishment of a dishonourable flight before the enemy was also set before him. Yet this would not have happened unless he had deserved it. But if upright men who are only lightly stained with wickedness do not escape dishonour, what will happen to the ungodly, who are covered with crimes?

But it is objected here that the godly are often mocked because of their zeal for piety, as David was by his wife Michal, 2 Sam. 6. 2, the Jews returning home by Sanballat, Tobiah, and others, Neh. 2. 19; and the Prophet bitterly complains that the Church was, as it were, fed to satiety by careless and proud men, Psal. 123. 4. I answer that the greatest difference exists between this contempt and that which, being due to the ungodly, comes upon them. For the contempt which the ungodly cast upon the godly is easily shaken off. Like a garment without a nap, it cannot cling long to the godly. Nor can those black colours, which are laid on without any oil of truth, endure long, since they are washed off in a moment as by water. For God will cause the innocence of his own people to shine like the sun at midday, Psa. 37. 6. By contrast, the contempt of the ungodly, begun here, is never shaken off; dyed as if in the grain, it can never be washed away. Like oil, it penetrates to their bones and marrow. Then, when we are afflicted with reproach and dishonour because of righteousness, we should rejoice—indeed exult and leap for joy—because “the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon us,” Matt. 5. 11; 1 Pet. 4. 14. This also answers those who object that the ungodly are rather often loaded, as it were, with honour: it swiftly vanishes like the down of groundsel.

This sentence extends more widely. For it can be aptly referred not only to the contempt and reproaches by which the ungodly are afflicted, but also to those with which they afflict others, especially the godly. Nothing prevents the words from embracing both senses, since they bear both. This also belongs in a special way to Proverbs, whose highest praise is to comprehend many things succinctly in a few words.

V. 4. The words in the mouth of an excellent man are deep waters; the fountainhead of wisdom is a gushing torrent.

In נחל (“torrent”) there is a metaphor. For just as a fountainhead is the cause and origin of flowing water, so the mind of a man is the source and cause of speech.

The sum is that speeches flow from a mind abundantly furnished with wisdom, embracing more wisdom than appears at first sight, and that they cannot be exhausted. Those who instruct by wholesome speech can lose nothing, nor can the fountain of generosity ever be exhausted; rather, it is increased, so that the more freely it pours out upon others, the more it is supplied to sustain itself and relieve the want of others. In this it surpasses fountains of water, which by flowing copiously are either dried up or at least diminished. Hence in Ahab’s time water was not obtainable.

The words of a wise and excellent man are adorned with the likeness of deep water, because just as in deep water you cannot easily penetrate or reach the bottom and base, so you cannot easily perceive or search out the force and weight of a wise man’s words. It was said earlier that the wise man’s speech is sparing and that he uses much silence. It may therefore seem incongruous that so much wisdom as is here proclaimed should be present in few words and much silence. But it must be kept in mind that the calmest waters, and those same waters,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 374 | printed columns 722-723]

to be deep when enclosed within narrow spaces. For this reason they report that the Mediterranean Sea, confined within narrower channels and making less noise, is deeper than the ocean, which is more expansive and more tumultuous.

An illustration of this matter is found in Samson, who proposed a riddle which none of his thirty companions could explain, Judges 14:11. There is also the case of Solomon: the Queen of Sheba was so amazed by his exceedingly acute and ingenious answers that, as she weighed his words, her spirit was utterly exhausted, 2 Chronicles 9. Hence it is that, long after receiving the discourse of an excellent man, we recall it to memory because of some more notable event—a discourse which we had previously received from him as though it had been less fully grasped by us when it was first delivered.

This is most truly applicable, however, to the discourses of those whom God appointed as scribes for himself, to commit them to the monuments of Scriptural writings. For their discourses can never be fully grasped by anyone’s understanding, nor drained by any bucket of human wit. Hence even eminent men have professed that they perceived only a very little of the revealed will of God, Job 4:12. Then, in the latter part of the proverb, he seems to indicate the cause of such deeply hidden knowledge, disclosed and set forth in the words of the wise: namely, a mind richly furnished with knowledge, from which their discourses flow. At the same time he notes that their mind is filled with such a wealth of wisdom that its discourses belch and bubble forth, as it were, from within. In this matter he seems to run counter to the proverb explained near the end of the preceding chapter, where it is ascribed to the wise man that he restrains and compresses his words. But that restraint and compression is nothing other than what accords well with the glory of God and the edification of one’s neighbors. For when those things are brought into danger, those who are truly wise can no more suppress good words and saving discourses than they can suppress burning coals or new wine whose vessel bursts unless it has a vent, Jeremiah 20:7; Acts 17:16; Job 4:2 and 32:19.

From this we learn, first, with how much attention the words of the wise must be received, how deeply they must be lodged in memory, and how often they must be thoroughly considered—especially those which are presented to us in the ministry in the name of God.

Then we learn how those who are dry and barren in speech may acquire for themselves a river of saving discourse: namely, if they are diligent in the word of God. By this alone David declares that he acquired so great a treasure of wisdom that, though a subject, he surpassed princes; though a pupil, teachers; and though a young man, elders, Psalm 119.

V. 5. To accept the countenance of the wicked is not good, to pervert the just in judgment.

In שאת (*to accept*) there is a metaphor: lifting up signifies the exhibition of honor, because by raising and elevating something we accord it honor. In פני (*countenance*) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole matter, which is not joined with the cause. There is also an ellipsis in the latter part. For את לא טוב (*not good*) must be repeated. There is, moreover, a μείωσις in לא טוב. For something more and more serious is intended than the amount actually expressed. We had an example of this kind at 17:26.

The substance is: it is a thing supremely detestable to God that the wicked man should prevail in his case and the just man should fall in a good case.

The proverb which preceded at 17:15 has almost the same sense as this one now before us. The difference, however, is that the former is presented ἁπλῶς, but this one ἐμφατικῶς. For there he inveighed against every declaration of the just man as guilty and every justification of the wicked; but in this place he speaks of that corruption in

[1632 witness | PDF p. 375 | printed columns 724-725]

judgment which arises from προσωποληψίᾳ. This acceptance of persons is more hateful to God than when sin is committed in judgment through inexperience and ignorance of law.

To accept a person is to show someone favor beyond the merit of his cause, whether this favor has been attracted and elicited by goods of the mind or of the body. Since this must not be done even to a just man in an unjust cause, much less may favor be shown to a wicked man, for this conflicts with the office of a just judge.

When Jehoshaphat exhorts the judges of his time to purity, he warns them away from this προσωποληψίᾳ, by which judgment is violated and defiled. To bring this about, he sets before their eyes the example of God, who does not accept persons. For since he is the judge of the whole world, who does not depart so much as a nail’s breadth from what is right and equitable—and indeed cannot depart from it—it is especially fitting that men conform themselves to imitate him, insofar as it is commanded, and regulate their judgments accordingly. He accepts neither the person of a man nor of a woman; neither an old man nor a young; neither a poor man nor a rich; neither a prince nor a subject; neither a noble nor one of obscure birth; neither a learned nor an unlearned man. Here perhaps someone may object that God can appear to be a προσωπολήπτην, since in his judgment he distinguished and separated men who were by equal right children of wrath, appointing these to life and those to death. Answer: with others passed over, God chose some while looking only to himself and to his own good pleasure; for to regard a person is to behold something in the one whose person is accepted and, in consideration of it, to do more for him than equity allows.

It is further objected that [God chose the poor in the world to be rich in faith, James 2:5, and appointed to life not many nobles, not many wise men, and so forth], and that by this very fact he accepts persons. Answer: God is not moved by the riches, wisdom, or nobility of these men to reject them, nor by the poverty, foolishness, or obscurity of the others to admit them. For then he would save all the poor and consign the rich to destruction. Rather, he does this only so that he may provide for his glory in this manner, putting the powerful and noble to shame by the weak and obscure.

If someone persists here and contends that God accepts a person when he considers or regards something beyond the merit of the cause which by nature is equal and the same in all, let him understand that God looks only to the merit of the cause. For he devotes the ungodly to destruction when he regards the wickedness of their most evil works; but in the elect he considers the merit of Christ’s passion imputed to the faithful. Directing his eyes to this, he cannot fail to save them unless he were to pass a sentence contrary to the merit of their cause. Since, therefore, God delivered his only-begotten Son to death in order to avoid προσωποληψίαν, nothing ought to be so dear to us that we would not willingly go without it in order that acceptance of persons may be avoided.

This applies to those who preside in the Church and commonwealth, yet in such a way that it also has a place in the daily conduct of every member of the Church, as James teaches, chapter 1:2.

V. 6. The lips of a fool come into contention, and his mouth calls for blows.

In שפתי (*lips*) and פי (*mouth*) there is a metonymy of the cause for words. Likewise, in יבאו (*will come*) there is a metaphor.

The substance is: by an unrestrained tongue peace is violated and men come to blows.

The wise man ascribes to the proud man such hostility toward peace and concord that he takes as much trouble to destroy it as a traveler who presses on along the road which he has determined to complete. And to declare his eagerness still more inflamed and incited, he adds that the proud man, not content with this advance as it were—going out to meet contention and discord—goes on to summon and call it to himself; and he calls not only for verbal disputes and quarrels but even for beatings and blows, and not only calls for them but actually shouts for them. For what else

[1632 witness | PDF p. 376 | printed columns 726-727]

do these words mean, which are so often heard: “Add another word, if you dare; strike me, if you dare; do this or that, if you dare! You little man—indeed, as has become customary in this unhappy age, you scab—do you speak even against me? Am I to endure this, especially from you?” and six hundred insults of this sort? What else do men accomplish by them than, like drummers and pipers, sounding the call to arms? It is therefore not without reason that insults and abusive words of this kind are compared to burning coals and swords, and that swift feet for shedding blood are attributed to them, so that [destruction and misery are in their ways, as those who have not known the way of peace], Romans 3:15. If those who come when summoned to fights must be reckoned among fools, surely those who summon others to fights are extraordinary fools. And if the former will not escape God’s most just judgment, what will become of those who bring the torches near to conflict and war? If those who do not extinguish the fire of contention deserve punishment, what will befall those who kindle the fire and, as though applying bellows to it, fan it into flame, and who are sons of the devil, John 8:44, who was a murderer from the beginning?

But if we wish to be regarded as wise and counted among the wise, let us stand far away from strife and contention; let us withdraw far off when it rushes upon us; let us exhort disputants to peace and sound the retreat. Thus we shall be sons of peace, and the God of peace will be with us.

V. 7. The mouth of a fool is destruction to him, and his lips are a snare for his soul.

In מהתה (*destruction*) there is a metonymy of the effect. In מוקש (*snare*) there is a metaphor taken from fowlers or fishermen, who ensnare beasts in their nets.

The substance is: through his imprudent and contentious insults, the fool often brings evil upon himself and even procures his own death.

It is a frequent statement, especially in the Psalms, that a wicked man falls into the pit which he dug and hollowed out, and likewise that the stone which he throws, or the arrow which he shoots, falls back upon his own head. But what is often said elsewhere of the wicked man is affirmed here for a particular reason of the abusive man, or of some other person who sins through wantonness of tongue, so that it was not said without cause that life and death are in the power of the tongue.

Moreover, it seems to be restricted more particularly to that abuse of the tongue which is seen in virulent and abusive insults and provocations, so that it may be joined with the preceding proverb: through the wantonness of his tongue, by which he provokes others to blows, the fool often summons destruction upon himself. A man would consider it unjust if he were entangled as in a snare by the words of another and did not have foresight enough to avoid the traps set for him by another’s speech. But to place a snare around his own neck is most disgraceful. It is still worse not only to strangle oneself, but to do it with a snare tied and woven by oneself. And certainly, if we are willing to look about us, we shall often observe that the man who provokes another with barbed words pays the penalty for his own insults. Yet the punishment owed to an abusive tongue is not bounded by the loss of this life, since it extends to the fire of Gehenna. Let Goliath be the example, 1 Samuel 17.

Let us therefore learn to remove the barb from our tongue, to blunt and dull its edge and point, and to quench its fiery darts with meekness and gentleness of spirit.

V. 8. The words of a whisperer are like those of men continually beaten, but they descend into the inward chambers of the belly.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 377 | printed columns 728-729]

The substance is: a whisperer can cause more harm than an open reviler can cause.

Here a comparison is made between a whisperer and the man whom Solomon described just before, the open reviler, since the whisperer differs greatly from him. For the reviler openly and plainly froths out the venom of his heart; but the whisperer secretly drives in his spur and barb. Hence he is compared to a man bruised by repeated beatings and blows, who—as the Prophet says—murmurs as if from the earth and the dust, and chirps in a subdued voice, Isaiah 29:4.

Whatever he says he whispers entirely into another’s ear, and so he is always careful not to betray himself as either author or informer. In this he imitates the serpent’s hiss and obscure sound, and the disposition of dogs which bite before they have barked. It is also not without reason that men of this kind seem, like beaten men, to disseminate their slanders not only by lowering their voices but by the very substance of their speech. For when they are about to suggest a slander, they are accustomed to preface it by saying that they speak only with grief, unwillingly, and as though compelled by evil; that they are no less distressed by what they are about to relate than if they themselves had suffered some great evil; and that they wish the very best for the man about whom they begin their discourse. Then, after using this fortification in advance, they spit out their tongue’s venom.

The whisperer, however, is worse than the open reviler: 1. because he secretly and insidiously inflicts his spur and cannot be guarded against; 2. because he speaks hypocritically and, at the very height of his endeavor to do harm, acts so that he may appear benevolent toward us. Thus what the Prophet sang can truly be said of him: his mouth is buttery while war is in his heart, and his words are outwardly softer than oil while in reality they are drawn swords and reach into the innermost parts and chambers. 3. because the whisperer not only damages the reputation of the man about whom he speaks, but corrupts the hearer’s mind and draws him away from the duty of a good man—the one in whose keeping he deposits his whisperings.

Therefore, everyone must take care, 1. that this hateful evil does not cling to us. Then those especially who have authority must keep watch, lest they nourish whisperers of this kind; rather, after David’s example, Psalm 101:5, they must drive them far away. And not only this: they must not allow members of their own household to lend their ears to the affairs of others, since this breed of men pursues exquisite tables and rich morsels.

Nevertheless, this must not be understood as though all informers and all who disclose the sins of others in private are to be regarded or reckoned as whisperers. For we see that Joseph privately reported the wickedness of his brothers to their father. Those who report the crimes of others to the people whose concern it is to know of them, and indeed do so with the fitting disposition—namely, that they may recover their senses and reform—are certainly not to be placed in the number and rank of whisperers.

Yet by the whisperer’s mournful hiss, however feigned, we are admonished to show ourselves sorrowful when disclosing and setting forth the sins of our brothers. For by this fabricated and painted pretense of grief, the whisperer sufficiently teaches what the duty of a good man is. Therefore, those men are far removed from the duty of a good man who inwardly rejoice in spirit, or outwardly exult, as they recount the sins of others. As men bruised and driven only by blows, they ought to speak ill of another solely for the glory of God and the edification and benefit of their neighbor. We are also convicted and held guilty of sin when, displaying grief on a day of fasting, we nevertheless are not pierced within, in the chambers of our heart.

V. 9. Even he who conducts himself slackly, continuously in his work, is brother to the lord of destruction.

In רפה (*slackly*) there is a metaphor taken from those who have their hands hanging down or not sufficiently raised

[1632 witness | PDF p. 378 | printed columns 730-731]

for treating the matter which, by reason of their calling, they undertake and assume. Likewise, in אח (*brother*) there is a metaphor taken from those who live according to the same manner and transferred to similar things, so that inanimate things equal in structure are called sisters. Moreover, in בעל משחית (*lord of destruction*) there is likewise a metaphor transferred from the lords and possessors of some thing to those who are distinguished and notable in some respect.

The substance is: the man who gradually diminishes his estate through sloth and carelessness is in fault, and is reduced to poverty by almost the same shortcut, or at least with no less certainty, as the man who squanders it by largesse and luxury. The man who exhausts himself and his estate by lavish generosity exceeding moderation is readily perceived to be destined one day to be thrust and driven to the mattock, as the saying goes. For it is plain to everyone that largesse has no bottom. Consequently, even all the bleary-eyed readily foresee that his circumstances will come back to the pouch or, as the expression goes, be reduced to extremity.

But there is another hidden and insidious evil, perceived only by men of keener judgment and sharper sight, by which men who have an ample patrimony and sumptuous furnishings are no less surely ruined in their possessions: namely, when they are slack and dissolute in doing the work incumbent upon them. As we saw above, the hand of the industrious applied to work makes a poor man rich, Proverbs 10:4. From the contrary it follows that a hand slack in its work makes a rich man poor. And just as industrious men do not become rich suddenly or all at once, but grow wealthy and prosperous over a proper interval of time, so also slothful rich men are not stripped of their resources suddenly and all at once, but are entirely divested of them in the course of time.

Therefore the pouring away of goods and luxury may be likened to apoplexy, plague, or other violent diseases. For as those diseases destroy life, so largesse destroys an estate in a short span of time. But carelessness or sloth in one’s affairs is more like consumption, which gradually and slowly weakens the health so that a man is often near death before he is even perceived to be suffering from disease.

But if a rich man is exhausted by idleness and carelessness, what will become of the slothful and idle man who lacks all things? Will he ever escape the hand of poverty and beggary which presses him beneath its heel? Therefore, let each man carefully consider this matter, so that he may guard himself from idleness and sloth, which bring not only poverty but disgraceful poverty. Although everyone must guard against this, the servants and attendants of noblemen must do so all the more, because that condition of life is not among the most profitable ways of living and yet is particularly liable to this disease of idleness.

Here I seem to hear someone say that he cannot show himself industrious, since he has nothing upon which to exercise himself and his industry. And certainly it is the fault of both master and servant if there is no work in which you may occupy yourself, seeing that the Apostle exhorts you to turn away from theft and devote your labor to a good and useful pursuit. But some, thinking themselves born for greater things, despise small matters, as if they were not sufficiently worthy of their diligence. Yet nothing is a greater aid toward handling greater things than to show yourself diligent and painstaking in a small matter. For thus Christ promises the man who has profitably traded with even one talent that he will be entrusted with more. Conversely, it is also said that he who was not faithful in a small matter will not be faithful even in a great one.

Therefore, brothers, let us stand far away from idleness. For if, while handling small matters, we become accustomed to idleness, once that habit has been contracted

[1632 witness | PDF p. 379 | printed columns 732-733]

we shall then be unable to lay it aside when the opportunity to handle greater matters has come.

This can also be applied to heavenly riches. For the man who conducts himself drowsily and coldly in religion must fear that he is the brother and likeness of the man who, in a moment, will deny God either in hope of some good or through fear of some evil. And what difference does it make whether a ship, when a storm has arisen, is overwhelmed by the waters in a single moment, or, if it has been pierced or split, is submerged by water flowing in little by little?

This may likewise be applied to negligent magistrates and drowsy Ministers, who through their carelessness and negligence in administering the Commonwealth and the Church are no less destructive than those who are the enemies of each. For he who does not gather scatters.

Verse 10. The name of Jehovah is a fortress of strength; the righteous man will run to it and be raised into a high place.

In מגדל (*fortress*) there is a metaphor transferred from earthly strongholds to God, who is the asylum and refuge of those who betake themselves to him. Likewise, in עז (*of strength*) there is a metonymy of the effect. Further, in שם (*name*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct, namely, the divine name for God. Likewise, in ירוץ (*will run*) there is a metaphor transferred from those who quicken their pace when danger from an enemy threatens to those who in danger of any kind turn to God in prayer. Finally, in the word נשגב (*will be raised*) there is also a metaphor drawn from citadels lifted to such a height that no one can reach them, and applied to God, from whose hands neither any force nor any fraud can snatch a person.

The sum is that God keeps safe and unharmed from evil those who invoke him with true faith.

For the name of God being put for God, see Exodus 23.22, just as the name of a man is put for the man, Acts 1.15.

Nature has implanted in nearly all men a desire to defend themselves from the injuries of others who invade their borders. Hence they also build citadels, towers, and ramparts to set against marauders and into which they may withdraw when need arises. They do this even when no dispute or cause of war exists between them and anyone else, and indeed they do it at immeasurable cost and labor to themselves. We therefore deserve to be condemned for idleness if, while waging unceasing and irreconcilable war with Satan and the whole cohort of hell, we do not look out for ourselves from a citadel or tower—especially since it lies open and ready for us at no expense and by no labor of our own, and since without it all the fortifications we construct are vain and useless.

Moreover, our sloth is increased by the fact that God, our fortress and tower, is of such strength that he can never be seized by fraud or conquered by force. Let us therefore drive away this lethargy and false security, and let us not be like the citizens of Lachish, who through heedlessness, ῥᾳθυμίᾳ καὶ ἀκηδίᾳ, were suddenly surrounded and crushed, Judges 18.7.

Here someone may ask how it happens that so many perish when God, filling heaven and earth, is more than sufficient to preserve everyone. The answer is that only the righteous man betakes himself to God as to an asylum, or, when winter approaches, flees to him as to winter quarters.

Here someone may ask again why there is need to run to God when God is everywhere and in David is called a stronghold very ready, or prepared, or near at hand. For the excellence of a stronghold and asylum is that it should be close at hand, lest, if it is rather far away, either our strength fail and we become exhausted before we reach it, or the enemy pursuing us overtake us. The answer is that this course is not completed with the feet, nor is it motion from place to place, but is an invocation of the divine name proceeding from

[1632 witness | PDF p. 380 | printed columns 734-735]

faith, Acts 2.19,20,21. This is confirmed by this Proverb when it says that the man is to be lifted into a high place to which danger cannot approach or reach. Hence God is called a fortress set in a high place, Psalm 18. Genesis 11. Insane men contrived to build a tower of this kind, one which would be raised to such a height that no one could conquer it and which by its height would free its builders from every danger; but they acted foolishly, since God, dwelling above, loomed over their heads. He who runs to God with faithful invocation is free and immune from every blow.

It should further be noted here that delay is dangerous if we do not hasten to God in prayer. An outstanding example of this is Numbers 17.11.12.

Verse 11. The riches of the rich man are the city of his strength, and like a high wall in his own estimation.

The sum is that every hope placed outside God is vain.

An example of this is given in the rich man who trusts in the possessions in which he abounds; and it is joined to the preceding Proverb in order to illustrate that doctrine.

Men, conscious by natural instinct of their own weakness, seek some support by which they may be upheld when shaken and in which they may trust. But whatever they seize upon, once they have departed from the living God, is like an Egyptian reed that does not steady the man leaning upon it. Some trust in chariots, some in horses, others in friends, but very many in riches. They are all guilty of the greatest ingratitude toward God, since they abuse God's bounty in order to defect from him: with their mind withdrawn from God, they turn toward the gifts he has bestowed upon them, imitating sordid prostitutes who value the gifts bestowed by their lovers more than the very lovers by whom they were enriched.

Although there are many things in the world with which mortals fornicate, there is nothing with which men prostitute themselves in greater number or with greater impiety than riches, which are dead things and devoid of sense. Solomon assigns the reason: money and riches answer every need, so that a man abounding in money and riches is thought to lack neither horses, nor chariots, nor soldiers, nor friends. Then there is the evil that they think they can repel and drive away with their riches. In addition, the man who places his hope in riches is greedy and by that very fact an idolater. For no one fails to cling tooth and nail to that in which he places his hope. It is therefore extremely difficult for a rich man to restrain himself from placing his confidence in riches.

Hence Christ confirms that it is an ἀδύνατον—an impossibility—for the rich to enter the kingdom of heaven, Matthew 19. Hence also the Apostle exhorts Timothy to warn the rich not to be proud and not to place their confidence in uncertain riches, 1 Timothy 6.17. He assigns other excellent uses and ends of riches, and these are manifold: 1. that we ourselves and those connected with us may enjoy them; 2. that through the benefit of riches we may do the things that are good and so be rich in good works; 3. that we may be ready to give; 4. that we may be suitable and fit for entering gatherings and societies. For, supported by riches, men can introduce themselves more boldly into the acquaintance and company of others, while the poor are held back by shame. 5. that we may gather for ourselves a treasure suited to the age to come.

Whatever protection the rich place in riches, nevertheless there is no solidity beneath it for them. For whatever they fashion for themselves and devise in their mind is merely imaginary; nor does it help them any more than hunger is appeased or thirst quenched by what anyone through

[1632 witness | PDF p. 381 | printed columns 736-737]

a dream seems to himself to eat and drink. They altogether imitate madmen, who in the utmost beggary insanely suppose themselves to be Kings and Monarchs.

Although it would have been enough to draw us away from hope and confidence in riches that they do not support us, yet there is greater weight in the fact that they not only fail to sustain their master but even go on to prepare his ruin. For they are the thorns by which the seed of the divine word is choked, Matthew 13. And Ecclesiastes complains that it is a grievous evil under the sun when riches, accumulated, are kept for the destruction of the man who possesses them, Ecclesiastes 5.11.

Let us therefore remember the Apostle's saying, that we should be as though not possessing while we possess, and as though not using the world while we use it.

Verse 12. Before destruction the heart of a man will exalt itself; and abasement is before honor.

In שבר (*destruction*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things liable to breaking to signify the ruin and destruction of a man. Next there is an ἓν διὰ δύο, “one through two.” For the fall also, which is the cause of the breaking, is comprehended together with the breaking itself, as is the collision, which is the cause of the fall. Then in גבה (*will exalt*) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily objects raised to a height to the mind that is lifted beyond measure by pride.

The first part of this Proverb was set out at 16.18, and the latter at the end of 15. But what is given there separately is here joined together in a single Proverb. This is done partly because of the excellence of the doctrine, which deserves to be repeated often, just as Christ often employed that Proverb and for several uses: “he who exalts himself is humbled,” and so forth; and partly also to adapt this to the immediately preceding Proverbs and to teach that the man who abases himself in invocation of the divine name is to be exalted, while the man swollen with confidence in riches is to be brought low.

The sum is that a proud man, struck by God's mighty hand, perishes from his fall, just as a man meek and submissive in spirit is heaped with honor by that same strong and supremely powerful hand of God.

Pride is the sin by which a person grows insolent through an excessive estimation of himself. Whether that estimation be true or false, it cannot fail to be hateful to God. For just as it is foolish to boast of things that do not exist, like the man who called any ship his own, so it is sacrilegious to glory in things received from God, since they ought to have supplied an occasion for thanksgiving.

[ Elsewhere in Scripture, the man who here is said to be high in spirit is said to be broad of heart, ] that is, swollen and inflated, as though distended by his immoderate estimation of himself. Thus through pride the human mind is raised, as if by a blister, beyond its proper height and is distended as though leaven had been injected. Now just as a fleshly heart in the human body, either raised out of the position nature assigned it or distended and swollen beyond measure, brings destruction upon the whole body—especially if it suffers from both faults—so a man raised and swollen in spirit cannot be far from ruin and destruction. And whereas disease of the fleshly heart is felt because of the body's many torments, while the mind's dislocation or distension is without sensation and pain, it is so far from being in a better condition that, because of this very insensibility, ἀναλγησίᾳ, it is in yet greater danger.

When the wise man attributes breaking to him, in that breaking he includes first a collision and then, enfolded and implicated, a slipping or fall. This most aptly answers to our Proverb, “pride will have a fall.” For those who walk with their head raised and very erect, and do not lower their eyes to the ground, are liable to collide with something. Those likewise

[1632 witness | PDF p. 382 | printed columns 738-739]

who are led by a zeal for climbing and ascending ladders are, as is well known, liable and prone to fall. For by God's most just judgment it comes about that they not only lack the honor and dignity which they pursue so intensely but are also hurled down to the lowest things. We must therefore take care that we are not among those who thrust themselves forward and place a crown or little garland upon their own heads; instead, let us calmly await the hand of God. We must take care not to introduce ourselves into a more honorable seat, lest at the householder's command we be forced to take a lower one. Let us wait instead until he says to us, “Rise and take the higher place.”

Further, the proud man in his fall is not merely battered and shaken but is also broken into the smallest particles, or rather is ground after the fashion of fine flour. This too most aptly agrees with the preceding metaphor concerning height of mind. For the man who falls from a high place, or is very tall in stature, falls with a heavier and more destructive fall than the man who is short of stature or falls from a place of little height. But they fall not merely to the earth, but to the lowest bottom of hell, from which there is no restoration or hope of restoration.

Very many examples of this doctrine occur in Scripture: in Absalom, Adonijah, Nebuchadnezzar, Daniel 4.26.27, and so forth; in Haman, Esther 5.21.22 and 6.10.11, and so forth, and the final verse of 7; in Herod, Acts 12. Nor does God set an example of his severity only in individuals, but also in cities, as in Capernaum, Matthew 11, and even in the most powerful kingdoms, as may be seen in the kingdom of Moab, Jeremiah 28.29. From this it is clear that this sin is grievously punished even in those who lack knowledge of God, much more in those who are imbued with the knowledge of God.

The cause of this breaking is that God resists the proud, James 1, Isaiah 2, so that no force or strength upon which the proud man leans can contend with the hand of God.

This is confirmed by its contrary, because those who are abject and submissive in spirit are treated with honor. Here the word the Prophet uses appears to be derived from a verb that signifies affliction, because it often happens that those afflicted and smitten by God's hand are also thereby trained in submission of mind. Men generally adopt the opinion that the man who is meek and subdued in spirit will never attain anything and never reach the summit of honor; but that the man who takes courage, and, filled with confidence in himself, seeks and grasps after some office or honor, and—as they say—thrusts and inserts himself forward, will be adorned with honors and offices. Therefore the Holy Spirit, to meet this evil, teaches on the contrary that the most open road to honors is for a man to think humbly. For since advancement to dignity and offices comes neither from the east nor from the west, and so forth, but it is God who raises this man and lowers that one, who—if we are willing to judge truly—is fitter or more suitable to receive honors than the man who commends himself and his duties to God? In the distribution of offices among men, do not those who set their minds upon obtaining offices strive to commend themselves to the King? Are they so far removed from mind and reason that they hope by provoking God to render him kind and benevolent toward them and to carry away from him the rewards of dignities and honors? Or do they suppose God to be so forgetful of his worshipers that in distributing offices he casts aside care for them, if indeed it is expedient for them not to enjoy honors?

No one denies that humility and abasement of spirit is a marvelous road to the heavenly kingdom, which is the greatest of all honors. If, then, God grants to the submissive in spirit that which is of the greatest honor, will he deny the lesser things, provided they can benefit them?

[1632 witness | PDF p. 383 | printed columns 740-741]

Here, therefore, it must be carefully considered that God gives grace to the humble just as he resists the proud, 1 Peter 5.

But if the summit of honor is ascended by any path other than humility and meekness of spirit, men ascend just as those who climb the cross by a ladder, so that afterward they may be cast down headlong and perish miserably.

Verse 13. For a man to answer a word before he has heard it is folly and disgrace to him.

In אולת (*folly*) and כלמה (*disgrace*) there is a metonymy of the adjective. In שמע (*has heard*) there is an ἓν διὰ δύο, “one through two.” For the verb “to hear” also comprehends understanding of the matter together with hearing, as the verb “to hear” is used in 1 Corinthians 14.2.

The sum is that to pass judgment on matters not fully grasped is at once foolish and disgraceful.

Proverbs 21.28 has something not unlike this, where it is said that the man who hears will speak forever. Many are found who, in their pursuit of a reputation for wisdom and acuteness, cut off another man's discourse by which the account of some matter is being set forth, and take it amiss that they are dealt with at greater length. It consequently happens that they fashion an answer about a matter not fully known. But by God's just judgment, the contrary befalls those who hunt for reputation and the appearance of wisdom: because their answer is poorly suited and inelegant, they are regarded as outstanding fools. And while they fish for glory and praise from a reputation for wisdom, they are covered with disgrace and shame. Even if they have heard the whole connected account with impartial ears, nevertheless, when the matter has not yet been sufficiently grasped, they refuse to have the account repeated lest they seem dull to others; thus, when they give an answer, they incur the same folly and, from their folly, the same disgrace as the former men.

If someone should object with the proverb common in speech, “a word is enough for a wise man,” I certainly acknowledge that it belongs to a wise man to form a judgment about a whole matter from a single little word when either a supply of more words is unavailable or setting out the matter at greater length is not free of danger. But this does not apply when there is an opportunity for a fuller account and when it is not inappropriate to expound the matter more fully.

Men also often bind themselves to this folly through a disturbance of mind, especially so when they hear something about another that displeases and agitates them. At once they close, if not their ear, at least surely their mind against everything anyone can offer to make them lay aside the opinion they have formed.

Potiphar sinned in this way: he refused to hear Joseph's just defense after his wife's slander had inflamed his anger. So did David: as soon as his ears had drunk in Ziba's slander against Mephibosheth, he immediately gave an answer against Mephibosheth without hearing his case. The same must be said of other disturbances of mind. Since they all obstruct at least an impartial hearing—an accurate and diligent hearing—of the matter being set forth, they brand the person laboring under them with the mark of this kind of folly and disgrace.

Since everyone must avoid these things, those possessing authority must especially do so, because their sudden and unconsidered answers are joined with harm to the Commonwealth. They must be all the more careful because, for the sake of maintaining their honor, they do not easily unweave or retract an answer rashly given. This habit is commonly found not only in impious Kings and Governors, Herod and Pilate, but even in David, who wholly refused to revoke his most corruptly pronounced sentence. For that reason one must think and think again, and reconsider, lest anything not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 384 | printed columns 742-743]

having fully learned and perceived it, he should set it forth in the Church, and should diligently observe what Christ affirms concerning himself and John the Baptist, John 3. 11, and what John the Baptist affirms concerning Christ in verse 32. Those who claim for themselves the role of teaching before they have learned are to be called to account by this.

There is also emphasis in his saying that not even a word is to be given in answer before someone has fully perceived the matter; much less is it fitting to weave a long discourse, as happens, about things unknown.

V. 14. The spirit of a man sustains his sickness; but who can raise up a broken Spirit?

In רוח (*spirit*) there is a metonymy of the effect. For Spirit is put for the mind of a man, because the soul is the cause of breathing. In נכאה, *broken*, there is a metaphor from corporeal things liable to fracture, transferred to a dejected mind that has been ruined by dejection or consternation. In ישאנה, *raise up*, there is likewise a metaphor transferred from corporeal things lifted from a low place to a high one, to a mind rescued from a miserable and afflicted condition and restored to its former peace and tranquillity. In יכלכל (*sustains*) there is a metaphor from corporeal things, which are supported when they totter. In מחלה (*sickness*) there is a synecdoche of species for every kind of evils that comes from outside.

The sum is: it conduces to both the present and future life that a man be of a strong and cheerful mind.

It is acknowledged that a sure and tested medicine against diseases is a most desirable thing, especially if the medicine, or drug, is πάγχρηστον, useful for everything, and fit to drive away every disease. Since the spirit of a man furnishes this, it is worthwhile to acquire such a Spirit. We must therefore see what kind of Spirit, or soul, it is that has a use so great and so manifold. This is readily clear from the latter half of the verse. For when it teaches that a broken spirit can be sustained by nothing and restored to its former state, it teaches not obscurely that the spirit which ought to sustain a man in sickness must be sound, whole, and well formed. Here, then, a new question arises: who is that spirit furnished with this virtue and strength? If you inquire into this matter, there is nothing you will find by searching except God, who alone can sustain so great a mass and weight as that of the soul. Hence, when David's soul trembled and was in tumult within him, he addressed it and commanded it to place its hope and trust in God, Psalm 42. Hence comes that saying of the Prophet that he was at last sustained by God's right hand when his soul, cleaving to God, followed him, Psalm 63. 9. From this flowed that exhortation of Scripture that we cast ourselves, as it were, into God's bosom and lap; and at the same time the Prophet's prayer that God would unite his mind to himself, that it might revere his name alone. For just as the soul is the life and support of the body, so God is the life and support of the soul; and just as the body, once separated from the soul, is nothing other than a fetid corpse, so the soul, torn and wrenched away from God, exists lifeless and devoid of life. This indeed is explained by another splendid metaphor among the prophets, wholly opposed to the preceding union: there the separation of God from the people is compared to the loosening of one member from the rest of the body, Jer. 6. 8; Ezek. 23. 11.

Hence a third doubt arises: how are God united to us and to our souls, and our souls to God, especially since it is certain that God is everywhere and present in every place to all, even to the most wicked, and that the wicked have their being, live, and move in him, Acts 17? I do not deny that God is present to all; but I affirm that he is present to his own alone by a gracious and saving presence. And however God may be in the wicked, he is nevertheless not united with them as with the faithful, nor coalesces with them into one by the most holy and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 385 | printed columns 744-745]

most binding bond of his Spirit. For Christ dwells in the hearts of the elect only by faith, Eph. 3. 17. Through the intervention of this Christ we are united to God the Father by the Holy Spirit. From this faith springs a good conscience, 1 Tim. 1. 8, which, as Solomon relates, is a perpetual feast, Prov. 15. 15, by which the mind is both strengthened and gladdened.

The Proverb therefore comes to this: that by true piety—or by a soul imbued and furnished with true piety—the greatest support and protection against all misfortunes that can befall from outside is acquired. Let us now consider how important the mind is to the comfort and happiness of the present life if someone commits himself to obedience to God, Deut. 28; Lev. 26. First, it drives away diseases and external evils and banishes them far off, lest they befall him. Then, since it is necessary for all the godly to pass through afflictions into the kingdom of heaven, it mitigates and softens the bitterness and harshness of all evils in such a way that joy outweighs pains and torments. Hence the saying in James 1, that we should count it all joy when we fall into various temptations. Likewise, the peace God gives he never takes away, John 14. 27. Hence the Apostle's saying: afflicted, but not driven into straits or confined in the least; we are perplexed, but not utterly stuck fast; we suffer persecution, but are not forsaken; we are cast down, but do not perish, 2 Cor. 4. 8. 9. Hence it also happens that, although we fall seven times in a day, we nevertheless rise again; and in Rom. 8. 37, in all things ὑπερνικῶμεν, *we more than conquer*. What is greater still, even in the sixth and seventh of these calamities, nevertheless evil will not touch you, Job 5. 19. What is most excellent of all, they even work together for good, Rom. 8. 28.

Hence it happens that, when by the instinct of nature the powers of the body have been spent and exhausted by disease or age, the soul too becomes more averse and unfit for every office of piety, Eccles. 12; yet when once restored and confirmed by the power of the Holy Spirit, as was said, it is strengthened and grows vigorous all the more as the body languishes and is weakened in its powers, 2 Cor. 4. 16.

Therefore those must be judged altogether foolish who, casting aside care for the soul, turn all their thought and all their zeal to tending the body. Care is undertaken that the body be fed well and lavishly, while no care is employed to drive hunger from the soul. Much and continual labor is employed that the body be clothed well and splendidly and covered, while meanwhile the soul walks either naked or wrapped in the rags of sins. Zealous effort is made that the body be strong and vigorous, though it must necessarily wither, while scarcely anyone is concerned that the soul, which was born for immortality, should thrive and flourish. Splendid dwellings are built for the body, while meanwhile the soul dwells in a filthy and fetid body spent with crimes, as in a *lyguria*, or rather a pigsty. If the body is seized by disease, a physician is summoned at once and a drug is administered; but when the soul labors toward eternal death, there is no mention of a physician, and one who offers himself of his own accord is reluctantly admitted; if the medicine he applies is a little bitter, it is rejected. To repel hostile blows, armor complete in every part is put on, while meanwhile the unarmed soul is exposed to the assaults of enemies. This is all the more to be condemned because the host of hell—Satan, the flesh, and the world—arrays battle against the soul, 1 Pet. 2. 11. For just as those who fight as enemies always seek the head or heart in order to take away life, so spiritual enemies desire to inflict a καιρίαν, mortal, wound upon the soul.

Conversely, when the soul is fed, clothed, cared for, defended, and so forth, the body's welfare is provided for in every way. It is therefore madness in the highest degree to neglect the soul and devote oneself to care of the body, which is wholly ineffectual and vain—and not merely ineffectual, but directed toward the destruction of the body itself. Let us rather devote our labor to cultivating the soul, by which, together with the soul, the body is preserved.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 386 | printed columns 746-747]

In the latter half of the verse there is another argument by which the Holy Spirit sharpens our diligence in strengthening the soul: namely, that a soul cast down and shattered can never be restored or raised up, since, like a stone or lead plunged into the deepest bottom of hell, it is sunk to destruction. For we see men endowed with the soundest health and form who, as soon as they are tormented by sorrow and sickness of mind, at once—though gradually—waste away and languish until they have exchanged life for death. They disdain every delicacy of food; all the splendor and pomp of this life are hateful to them; their dearest friends and relatives are wearisome to them, and music is hateful. With God opposed and hostile, what is there, or who is there, that could bring him relief?

Some things are so constituted that, if they collapse, they can be restored again; hence we are not so solicitous about their soundness and preservation. But if anything is of such a kind that, once broken, it cannot be repaired and remade, we exercise great care lest it be broken by some unforeseen accident, as may be seen in vessels of glass. How great, then, must be the care employed to fortify the soul against every accident and shattering! For what price will anyone give for a soul? Or how will this loss be repaired when it burns in the fire of divine judgment, like Sodom and the rest?

What then? Must we despair when we fall into grievous torment of mind, and, all hope cast away, allow ourselves to be overwhelmed by anguish? By no means. For God alone can raise up and restore a broken and shattered mind, and that by his word; leaning upon it, we shall emerge from shipwreck and lift up our heads. But we must take care lest, if we reject God offering himself to us, he withdraw and remove himself in the time of our necessity. Rather, in the greatest peace, as in an abundance of all things, let us heap up God's consolations and store them in the chambers and, as it were, granaries of our soul, so that on the day of temptation, when famine as it were assails us, there may be no lack of things by which we may sustain our sick soul.

V. 15. The heart of the prudent possesses knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks knowledge.

In לב (*heart*) there is a synecdoche of the part: the affection of the mind, namely, for the mind. Likewise, in אזן (*ear*) it stands for the one whose ear it is.

The sum is: he is truly prudent who, with knowledge stored in the innermost chambers of the mind, further pursues it thirstily and eagerly by hearing the word.

By placing prudence in this, that a man possess it in his heart, Solomon reproves the vain and frivolous confidence of many. For there are those who think it sufficient for them merely to be hearers of prudence, so that they perceive nothing further, just as fools boast of a learned teacher from whom they nevertheless learned and retained nothing. In this class are those who receive the seed beside the beaten path, Matt. 13. Others take pride from the fact that, retaining in memory what they have either read or heard, they can recite it by heart, although, no more than parrots, do they comprehend with mind and understanding anything that they pronounce with their mouths. In this class not a few are found in our age; they may not inappropriately be referred back to the former group, since retaining nothing in the memory and not understanding what has been retained are of equal value. There is a third class of men who retain in memory what they hear and at the same time understand what is said, but meanwhile have no foundations and bases laid beneath it. They hold the chief heads of religion and, when questioned about them, will answer; but if you ask them for the reason, and upon what testimonies of Scripture

[1632 witness | PDF p. 387 | printed columns 748-749]

the doctrine they profess rests, they know how to answer nothing. These are the men who receive the seed of the word in stony ground, in whom the seed of the word takes no root. The fourth class consists of those who enclose in memory what they are taught, understand it well, have clearly perceived the reasons underlying each mystery, are able to confirm and fortify it with testimonies of Scripture, and know how to discourse accurately about these things; yet meanwhile they are led by no zeal or affection for holy doctrine. They are like soil which receives the seed deeply enough, but where it is choked, through lack of love and affection, by zeal and desire for worldly things. Therefore those are the true and genuine sons of wisdom who hear, retain in memory what they have heard, understand by means of underlying reasons what memory has retained, and pursue with zeal and love what has been well understood. These at last are the possessors and rightful masters of prudence. No one can wrest from them by any force or fraud the prudence they have acquired; they do not receive their prudence as a loan, so as afterward to give it back and restore it. Hence Solomon teaches that wisdom is a tree of life to those who lay hold of it, and that the man who firmly retains it is blessed, Prov. 3. 18. A woman does not conceive merely by receiving seed, nor is she furnished with powers for retaining it to a due and timely birth; from conception she receives nothing but sorrow.

What it is to possess wisdom Solomon himself appears to describe not obscurely by its parts: namely, not to forget it or turn aside from it, not to forsake it, and to be held by love of it, Prov. 4. 5, 6.

In the other half of the verse it is taught that he who is truly wise is a diligent hearer of wisdom, and seeks it like silver and treasure, Prov. 2. 4. Those, therefore, who are full and have learned to satiety give proof that they have perceived nothing rightly from the Gospel. Hence David, although most fully instructed, was held by a wonderful desire for hearing and learning, Ps. 42. 1. 2 and 119. 27. 33. 34. There are many who spend their whole life learning one art and do not attain a full perception of it. How much more fitting is it to grow old in this study of divine wisdom, since by studying it a man becomes wiser day by day? Prov. 1. 5.

Here those are condemned who, devoting themselves to their own studies and meditation, despise the Ministry of the word, since wisdom is acquired chiefly from hearing, Rom. 10. 17.

V. 16. A man's gift enlarges [the way] for him, and will lead him before great men.

In ירחיב (*enlarges*) there is a metaphor from those who, having been confined in narrow places, afterward come into a spacious and broad place; it is transferred to those who, when pressed by calamity, afterward enjoy favorable fortune once rescued from it.

In מתן (*gift*) there is προσωποποιΐα, personification. For he assigns to a gift, as though to a man, a hand by which it may lead the giver wherever it wishes.

The sum is: a gift has very great importance for accomplishing that which we desire.

In chapter 17, verses 8 and 23, it is taught that the corruptions caused by gifts have great power to pervert justice; in this place teaching about a gift is set forth more generally. For it is taught that a gift is suitable for accomplishing any matter, whether lawful and permitted or prohibited and forbidden. For preparing a gift is ἐκ τῶν μέσων, among the means; and accordingly, as it is applied to honorable or dishonorable causes, so it is either praised or blamed. It is counted to Jacob's praise that, to calm the fury of his brother Esau, he prepared and made ready a gift; likewise to Abigail's praise that, to bend David, she sent her gift before her. But Ziba's gift, by which

[1632 witness | PDF p. 388 | printed columns 750-751]

he induced David's mind to accept slander against Mephibosheth, is counted to his blame. For abuse of gifts does not abolish their lawful use.

Meanwhile the disposition of men is censured by the Holy Spirit, because it is generally much too inclined to receive gifts. For nothing prevents those who receive gifts from often being at fault, while those who send them are nevertheless free from blame. And even in those very gifts which are received without injustice, the desire itself to receive gifts is nevertheless not free from fault. This is what is reproved by this Proverb. For it is implied here that the way is, as it were, too narrow for anyone to enter unless it is enlarged and made spacious by a gift; that the gate is, as it were, barred unless it is opened by a gift as by a key; so that only bearers enter through it; and that great men are, as it were, in a lofty and inaccessible place which no one can ascend unless the ladder of a gift is applied. Christ wished to quench this desire for receiving by the notable saying which Paul recalls: *it is more blessed to give than to receive*, Acts 20. 35.

Provided only that this desire is absent, the giving and receiving of gifts are sometimes praiseworthy. Of this kind are presents which the rich give to and receive from the rich either to enter into friendship or to bind it more firmly. In this class are those gifts which are enjoined in Esther. Likewise, small and slight gifts are received with open countenance and cheerful spirit. For it is a kind of munificence to regard favorably a small gift from a poor man by which he wishes to testify to his gratitude.

Finally, in the receiving of gifts it is to be desired that those who receive be so disposed as God is in receiving gifts. For he expressly commanded that no one should appear before him at solemn feasts empty of a gift, Exod. 23. 15 and 34. 20. But he requires all this with no other purpose than the good of those who bring that gift, namely, so that, taking hold of this occasion, he may exercise munificence toward them and enrich them with his own good things. The same mind ought to be in us that was in Christ, who asked a woman for a cup of cold water in order to repay her with the most august and precious gift of the Holy Spirit, John 4. 7, 14.

V. 17. He who is first in his own suit appears just; but his neighbor comes and searches him out.

The sum is: unless the case has first been debated on both sides and fully perceived, nothing is to be determined in favor of either party.

Just as the desire to receive gifts is reproved by the preceding Proverb, so by this one is rashness in deciding doubtful matters. This censure, indeed, regards both the man who first states his case and especially the judge. Hence the proverb is common among us: *the first tale is good, untill the second be heard*. And indeed, as concerns the plaintiff, although no one ought to summon another to law unless some persuasion of the justice of the case he brings into judgment has imbued him, nevertheless it is not fitting that, trusting too much in himself in his own case, he should seize the judge's office for himself by determining something certain about the matter in dispute.

But there is greater danger in the judge, lest, preoccupied by prejudice from the plaintiff's narrative, he show himself unjust toward the defendant. For although judges sin in various and manifold ways with respect to both disputants equally, through respect of persons and corruptions by gifts, yet this peculiar danger lies in the Plaintiff: that the judge allow himself to be preoccupied by him. A lapse in this matter is easy for two reasons. The first is that it can come to mind as a probable conjecture that the man who presses a suit in court would not readily do so unless provoked by injury. Yet here there should come to mind,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 389 | printed columns 752-753]

what is bandied about in our proverb: that there is a kind of men who at once bite and weep; among us, “He will bite and whine.” The latter cause of falling is that the plaintiff’s speech and narrative, steeped in plausibility, easily slips into the judge’s mind. Hence it comes about that a man who brought an impartial and upright mind to the judgment inclines in this way toward the other side.

Here, therefore, the example of God, the most just judge, must be set before us for imitation. Although the cry of sin, as a grave accusation, had reached him from Sodom and Gomorrah, he nevertheless declares that he descended to learn whether the matter stood so, Gen. 18. 20. 21. 22, and 11. 5. This saying warns judges not to determine anything ἀνεξετάστως, without examination, and at random.

Next, that notable saying of the Levite must be retained: [apply your mind, enter into counsel, and pronounce judgment, Judg. 19. 30. See the example of equity in judging this matter in 1 Kings 3.

It is next added that the defendant comes and pleads his cause. By this he indicates that the defendant, being present, has received the plaintiff’s accusation with his own ears. Therefore that law can never be praised enough which also prevailed among the Romans: that neither party should be heard in judgment unless both were present and face to face. For since there is danger that the judge’s mind will incline toward the plaintiff’s cause merely because he speaks first, much greater danger threatens if, while the defendant is absent, the accuser’s speech, after enjoying an interval of time and spending the night with the judge, takes root too deeply to be removed and expelled afterward from his mind, however just the defense.

Solomon does not say that the man who speaks second wins the case, although he offers a just defense. This appears to mark the corruption of prejudgment, by which it often happens that a judge, led away and carried off by the earlier accusation, is less attentive and equitable toward the defense. That we may all avoid this—and certainly we all ought to wish to—we must take care not to utter anything rashly by which we openly disclose what mind we have in that case. For so great is the φιλαυτία, the self-love, innate in us that we desire ourselves and everything belonging to us to prevail and stand firm. Therefore care must be taken that no little word escape us by which the opinion we hold may be understood. In Proverbs 20, Solomon, prescribing a remedy against vengeance, commands a man to beware of threatening that he will do it. This can and should be transferred to the present matter with no less prudence and caution, just as it is also transferred to other crimes that are committed.

Verse 18. The lot makes contentions cease, and makes a division between the mighty.

The sum is that the lot has a singular use in composing disputes, especially when contention arises among magnates.

In the last verse of chapter 16 we learned that the lot is, as it were, the deputy of God, the judge of the world, by which God himself decides doubtful matters that no human art or ingenuity can investigate. Solomon confirms this matter by comparing the present Proverb with the one that precedes it. For in the preceding Proverb he establishes teaching about the ordinary conclusion of disputes; here he prescribes a remedy for those that cannot be decided by witnesses or the guidance of reason. Thus the very position and connection of the Proverb shines before us so that we may learn the present use of the Proverb.

Next he designates a more particular use of the lot: the settlement of disputes. It avails for investigating the most deeply hidden matters, as it has often been employed for that purpose. It further avails for fostering concord where it had previously been cultivated, as in dividing inheritances. Here, however, he designates the special use of deciding controversies that arise among magnates.

But if anyone asks whether it also

[1632 witness | PDF p. 390 | printed columns 754-755]

has strength to settle disputes among men of the lowest condition, it certainly does. It will be objected in turn why Solomon did not deliver it in the genus, since that is ἐπιστημονικώτερον, more scientific, than to deliver the doctrine in a species. I answer that it is sometimes more profitable for engendering knowledge to say something particularly which may be called γνωμικῶς, maxim-wise, especially when that species not only indicates its own genus but also, considered in relation to the remaining species, acquires the force of another argument—as here, of an argument from the greater. This is especially so because, once the truth has been known in a species, it must be perceived in the entire genus, as in this example. For if the lot settles disputes among magnates, much more does it do so among men of the lowest rank.

By this γνωμικῇ, sententious, treatment something is indicated quite clearly which would not have emerged from a universal treatment: namely, that disputes among magnates are removed painfully and with difficulty.

By this we are taught that concord and peace must be cultivated and fostered among all men, but especially among magnates and those who hold the chief place, because once it has been violated it is recovered and restored with difficulty. Relying on clients, alliances, and money, they do not yield to one another and reckon it disgraceful to give way; but what is settled by lot redounds to the disgrace of neither. For in this judgment no prejudice is done to the wisdom, power, or resources of either. Further, contention taking its beginning from them creeps and spreads far and wide to all who are under them. If a king practices enmity with another king, all the subjects on either side are drawn into the same hatred. If a dispute arises among governors of the Church, the Church is torn into factions. If in a family there is a dispute between husband and wife, the whole family is split. It is otherwise if dissension arises among subjects, citizens of the Church, or servants, for it does not disperse and disseminate itself so widely. Just as the precious ointment of unity, poured upon the head, flows down in swift course to all the members, and as rain sent down from heaven upon Mount Hermon runs down into all the valleys, so conversely contention residing in princes, as the heads of the people, and in magnates, as mountains, slides and descends to those beneath them, as to lower members and valleys.

Verse 19. A brother provoked by desertion is firmer than a fortified city, and contentions are like the bar of a citadel.

In אח (*brother*) there is a synecdoche of the part for a sister and for all who are closely joined to us by blood.

The sum is that the more closely anyone is bound to another by a bond, the more irreconcilable he proves after perceiving himself injured by his kinsman.

Men generally have this disposition: for the sake of their desire or advantage, they do not hesitate to assail with injuries those whom they know to be bound to them. For they either hope that they will not be offended at all, or persuade themselves that, if they do bear an alienated mind, they will nevertheless soon return to favor with them. This Proverb establishes that men err and are deceived in both respects, for Solomon’s saying confirms both that they receive offense and that they retain it tenaciously after receiving it.

The cause of this is readily at hand. When we have persuaded ourselves that more favor and kindness has been laid up and stored in their mind for us, and then not only receive no benefit from them but, though undeserving, are moreover burdened with injuries, it can scarcely be said with how much anger and indignation we blaze up. For it certainly happens that those who are slower to conceive anger do not easily put it aside after they have conceived it. A cord twisted from many threads is not easily cut or broken; but once severed

[1632 witness | PDF p. 391 | printed columns 756-757]

or broken, it is not so easily knotted together as that which is slender and consists of one or two threads.

A splendid example of this occurs in Esau. Provoked by a certain injury from Jacob, he received an offense which he never laid aside and which he transmitted to posterity, to such an extent that they did not grant passage through their territory to their brothers, Num. 20.

Therefore those who are joined by the inward love of blood or affinity must take pains not rashly to provoke with injuries those to whom they are joined, lest they carry away the reward of their own folly by receiving grave harm from the source from which they expected a singular safeguard of life.

This Proverb also marks the depravity of our nature, which does not allow itself to be gathered again and reconciled with those with whom it ought to live in the closest union, nor to return to favor with those with whom it has certain special aids to restoration, Gen. 13. 8 and 50. 17. For the sweet name of brother ought to move them to reconciliation. Therefore, that so great a separation should arise that it is easier to occupy a fortified city or break through the iron or steel bar of a citadel demonstrates extraordinary hardness and an aversion to charity.

Here, however, spiritual kinship comes to be considered by us. It is so firm that it cannot be dissolved or broken by any injuries; and we should reckon no enmities so great and confirmed that the blood of Christ cannot extinguish them, nor so adamantine that it cannot break them.

Verse 20. From the fruit of anyone’s mouth his belly shall be filled; from the produce of his lips he shall be filled.

In פי (*mouth*) and תבואת (*produce*) there is a metaphor transferred from gardens and fields to a man’s speech. In פיו and שפתיו (*his lips*) there is a metonymy of the effect. In בטנו (*his belly*) there is a synecdoche of the part not unlike the one we use in common speech: “he shall have his belly full.” Likewise, in ישבע (*shall be filled*) there is a continuation of the metaphor of fruit and produce.

The sum is that a man’s speech has very great power in either direction, whether for living well and blessedly or the contrary.

When he compares speech with fruits and produce, or revenue, we are taught that there is something in speech which turns either to our good or to our evil. For with these fruits and produce the case is far otherwise than with earthly fruits, which we are free either to taste or to reject; with the former, an unavoidable necessity lies upon us of receiving them and, as it were, feeding upon them. But when he adds that the belly, or each person, is filled by this fruit and produce, he teaches that very much—or rather everything—by which one goes either to misery or to a blessed condition is situated in the use of speech.

Therefore we must diligently take heed that we use speech in such a way that it is not fatal to our destruction. Indeed, what man is by nature—how virulent and purulent, how blasphemous and insolent toward heaven and earth, toward God and men—is clearly established both from the Prophets and the Apostles, Rom. 3. 13. But if that depravity which inheres in him by nature is confirmed by use, custom, and imitation of evil men, the poison and wolfsbane is made still more lethal and deadly and so brings men more quickly to maturity and satiety.

Yet here it is asked how what is affirmed in this place about fullness and satiety is true, since those who sin in speech are disposed, as in every kind of sin, to be seized with a certain hunger and thirst for cursing. Just as also those who

[1632 witness | PDF p. 392 | printed columns 758-759]

are faithful servants of God cannot retain and repress that which is holy. I answer that Solomon understands this fullness and satiety metonymically for the effect of speech, namely, its compensation and remuneration, by which God visits pure or impure, chaste or polluted speech with reward or punishment.

The tongue, then—or the lip, or the mouth—is a very narrow little field, yet wonderfully fruitful, since it yields fruit and produce by which the whole body and even the soul itself is filled. This ought therefore to stir and sharpen our diligence, so that it may be sown with pure, choice, and precious seed, since it bears most abundant produce. And this is what James 3 teaches: that moderation of the tongue causes the whole remaining body to follow. In man the first watch and the first sentries are in the heart, or the mind, as Solomon requires in 4. 23. But among the parts of the body the chief care must be placed upon cultivation of the tongue. It is a great thing in farming if anyone happens upon fertile soil which, although confined within narrow bounds and enclosures, nevertheless yields a harvest by which he feeds himself and his whole household. Such a field is the tongue, by the holy use of which a man can benefit himself, his own people, and very many others. Therefore its harmful weeds must be pulled up: first the nettle of verbal vanity, then the darnel of falsehood, and the thorns of blasphemy.

Verse 21. Death and life are in the hand of the tongue, and whoever loves to use it shall eat its fruit.

In יד (*hand*) there is a metonymy of the subject, used adjectivally for *power*. In יאכל פריה (*shall eat its fruit*) there is an allegory about those who receive the just recompense of their deeds, drawn from those who receive fruit from the trees which they themselves have planted.

The sum is that from the right or depraved use and exercise of speech, either life or death, justification or condemnation, redounds as a reward.

Just as the head of a household often warns the maidservants carefully to cover the fire with ashes laid over it, lest, if it remains, a conflagration be kindled and spread far abroad, so God repeatedly addresses men through Solomon, that they should guard the tongue.

In the first part of the verse he defines the fruit of the tongue with which he taught in the preceding verse that men are filled. Life is the fruit that a man receives from a pure use of speech, just as death comes from an impure use. Therefore, just as in the preceding Proverb diligence in bridling the tongue was sharpened by the fact that the richest fruit comes from it, so in this Proverb zeal is kindled by the fact that the fruit we receive has such weight and importance. For it is a tree of life to those who use it well, but a tree of death to those who abuse it. And the life that comes is both present, as appears in Psalm 34, and future, as is clear in Matt. 12. 36. 37, where the satiety is greatest of all. Hence it is established that the man who uses his tongue rightly is blessed and perfect, as James speaks. For eating from the fruit of the tree of life is granted only to one who reports victory over the devil, the flesh, and the world, Rev. 2. 7.

But the statement that life and death are in the power of the tongue has no force at all for asserting free will. For since the tongue by our nature contains the poison of asps beneath it, and since the throat is an open tomb, what good proceeds from it? Next, he does not speak of any capacity of the tongue in either direction, but only of the reward of the pure tongue and punishment of the impure, which necessarily—and not by choice—falls to each person according as he has used his tongue. Thus the sense is not that it is in each person’s power to choose what is good or evil, life or death, but that each

[1632 witness | PDF p. 393 | printed columns 760-761]

is put in possession of life or death according as he has abused his tongue intemperately by the instinct of nature or has used that same tongue moderately through the grace of God sent down from heaven. Possession, however, is not always voluntary or under our own choice.

In the latter part of the Proverb he teaches who they are who will receive either life from a right use of the tongue or death from a depraved use: namely, those who are governed by devotion to the one or the other, that is, to speaking rightly or perversely. Therefore not everyone from whom a depraved speech escapes will be punished with death, nor does everyone who speaks good words always attain life, but only those who are led by zeal and love for the one or the other. For Moses and David rashly blurted out certain things, yet did not die; Judas and Balaam said many excellent things, yet they do not live. This is certainly no less true of those who hear another’s speech than of the man who speaks.

Verse 22. He who finds a wife finds a good thing, according as goodwill comes from Jehovah.

The sum is that a wife, a thing good in itself, comes to the man who seeks her according as God benevolently attends the man.

That the union of a man with a woman in marriage is good is taught by God’s own authority in Genesis 2, and that while he was still uninfected by any stain of sin; how much more ought it to be good now, since all the causes of this goodness and advantage which existed at first press still more urgently because of the stain of sin than they did before sin entered. For he now needs the aid of a wife for the fellowship of life more than he did then. The loss of children is also now more bitter because of the death to which men are subject than it would have been if man had lived forever, since offspring and posterity bring some relief against death. To this increase of goodness a new cause has been added, namely, a remedy against fornication, 1 Tim. 4. 3. Therefore the Papists are justly condemned for prohibiting marriage to not a few, since every creature of God is good and nothing is to be rejected which is received with thanksgiving. To this is added what Solomon says ἠθικῶς, in a moral manner, concerning fellowship: that the condition of one living alone is disadvantageous, while the fellowship of two joined together is advantageous in many ways, Eccles. 4. 9.

Next, by saying that the man finds her, he implies that a wife must be sought. The manner of seeking is explained in the Scriptures. In Judges 14 Samson asks his parents to look out for a wife for him. But the chief manner of seeking is through prayers poured out to God. An example of this occurs in Genesis 24, where prayers are made to God in procuring a wife for Isaac, and those prayers are many: first by Abraham, next by Abraham’s servant Eleazar, then by Isaac, and finally by Rebecca’s parents. Therefore as soon as the thought of taking a wife enters the mind, let the thought of commending this marital cause to God in prayers enter the mind at the same time.

When a man has taken pains to seek a wife and has indeed obtained her from God through prayers, nevertheless he is taught that the whole good of the matter proceeds from the mere benevolence and will of God. For first, since every good giving and every perfect gift is from the Father of lights, James 1, a good wife—whom Solomon prefers to possessions and an inheritance together, Prov. 19. 14—must flow from God’s giving. And just as she flows from God, so she comes from God’s gratuitous will without anyone’s merit. For just as it comes about through God’s hatred that a man falls in with a prostitute, Prov. 22. 14, so by his gratuitous will a man obtains a wife. Therefore we should show ourselves grateful to God for

[1632 witness | PDF p. 394 | printed columns 762-763]

so great a benefit let us repay; and just as the use of food is not lawful for us except with the intervention of prayers and thanksgiving, so neither is the use of marriage, 1 Tim. 4. 4. 5.

But here it is asked whether, if anyone has fallen in with a peevish and difficult, quarrelsome and contentious wife, she too is to be numbered among benefits? I answer: certainly, if she is a wife, insofar as she is a wife she is to be reckoned among good things; and by that very fact he is bound to cherish and love her, provided she keeps herself chaste. But no one is bound to cherish and love anything unless it is good. Therefore, just as no wickedness of his own can cause a magistrate, insofar as he is a magistrate, to confer no good upon the people under his government, so neither can a wife, however troublesome she makes herself, render the beneficence of God wholly void.

But if a wife, insofar as she is a wife, is numbered among good things and benefits, how much more a good, godly, and obedient wife. Nevertheless, nothing prevents virginity from being preferred for particular persons and at particular times.

What is said of a wife may, with a slight inflection of the words, likewise be affirmed of husbands.

V. 23. The poor man utters supplications, but the rich man speaks forceful things.

In רש (*poor*) and עשיר (*rich*) there is a synecdoche for every person in distress, or for one abounding in the external comforts of this life. In עזות (*forceful things*) there is a metonymy of the subject: forceful things, namely, for harsh things. Just as anger is broken by gentle and mild words, Prov. 15. 1, so anger is stirred up by harsh words; from anger come quarrels, and from quarrels come killings. Therefore it comes about that rich men are often murderers, unless God, together with riches and power, bestows a spirit tamed and subdued by the consideration of spiritual beggary.

The sum is that there is more evil in riches than in poverty, unless a man diligently and circumspectly guards himself against falling into the sin of pride.

Christ the Saviour did not say without cause that rich men enter the kingdom of God with the greatest difficulty, Matt. 19. 23. He there assigns only one cause of this—yet plainly the chief cause and the one from which the rest spring—namely, confidence in their riches. But from this sewer flow very many other evils which bar men’s entrance into the kingdom of heaven. To quench the thirst and hunger for riches by these means, the Holy Spirit uses certain advantages of poverty, in comparison with those which befall the rich, to train mortals in patiently bearing poverty: for example, that they are better fitted to receive the Gospel, Matt. 11. 5, and that in the burning of a commonwealth and devastation by enemies they are treated more mildly than others, Jer. 39. 10.

To this pertains what is set forth in this Proverb, namely, that the poor are not so liable as the rich to the pride which produces harsh speeches.

Therefore let us examine each part of the Proverb separately, so that the truth of each may shine forth more clearly. As for the poor, there are two causes which drive them to suppliant speech. The first is their consciousness of their own weakness to withstand the force of rich and powerful men if once they provoke them with harsher words. The other is want of food and lack of the things necessary for living comfortably: lest, if they irritate with words those who possess these things, they be utterly deprived of them. To these it may be added that, tamed and subdued by the evil of poverty, they are less fierce and proud than those who live amid an abundance of every kind of thing. I speak of those whom God has not yet trained by his Spirit in submission of mind. And this is what Solomon commands the man who has become surety for another to do: namely, to prostrate himself on the ground and extol with words the man to whom he has made himself liable, Prov. 6. 3. A common saying has it that it is too much for a man to

[1632 witness | PDF p. 395 | printed columns 764-765]

pay and then entreat, or in the vernacular, “It is too much to pay & pray too.” Since, then, the poor man has nothing with which to pay, it is right that he should be suppliant.

A splendid example of this is in Jacob. Fearing the ferocity of Esau for himself and his family, he as it were cast himself and all his people down before Esau’s knees and repeatedly called him his lord, Gen. 33. 3. Thus Joseph, bound by his brothers and cast into a pit, begged them in the most suppliant words to let him go and not deal so cruelly and inhumanely with him, Gen. 42. 21. This also shines forth in Job, a prince of the region in which he lived: as a suppliant he sought help from his servants, and by the children whom he had fathered he entreated his wife, Job 19. 16, 17. But the example of Benhadad, king of Syria, confirms the truth of this most of all: he clothed himself in sackcloth and, as it appears, put a rope around his neck, and called the king of Israel his lord, or ordered that he be so called, 1 Kings 20. 32, 33.

From this Proverb we learn how suppliant it is fitting for us to be when we approach God. For if, through consciousness of our poverty and inequality with mortals, we present ourselves so submissive and humble to them, how much more ought we to do this before God! We ought, indeed, to bend a thousand knees before him, if we had them, and seek out words that testify to the greatest submission of mind. For the inequality between God and us is infinitely greater than that between the most mighty and august prince and the most beggarly, ragged vagrant who ever existed. Besides, our poverty—or rather beggary—especially in those things that pertain to eternal life, is far greater than that of any most abject little man in the things of this age. For we are blind, deaf, lame, naked, ulcerous, deprived of all good things, and filled with every evil. Indeed, we approach mortal men as suppliants supported and sustained by no promises that we shall carry anything away; but when we approach God as suppliants, we are fortified by promises that we shall attain our requests.

Those who trust in their own merits never approach as suppliants. Instead, as their right, they demand or importunately claim all the most excellent gifts, even eternal life.

In the other part of the Proverb, the disposition of rich and powerful men is offered for consideration. Lifted up by confidence and by the power in which they excel and surpass others, they speak harshly and rudely. Next, they do not fear rash boldness toward themselves from a poor and afflicted man. Lastly, they ascribe αὐτάρκειαν (*autarkeia*), or self-sufficiency, to themselves in themselves, as though they would never at all need the service of the poor. An example of this appears in Nabal, who not only gave nothing to the messengers sent by David but moreover assailed David with harsh abuse, 1 Sam. 25. Yet this inhumanity of the rich in speaking is all the more intolerable, inasmuch as the supplications and gentle speeches of the poor invite them to a corresponding affability in their own speech. But they labour under a great error. For the poor man is useful to them in many things. Further, besides the fact that they are helped or harmed by the poor man’s service, they do not consider that the Lord God is Lord of the poor and thoroughly avenges even the smallest injuries inflicted on them.

James, chapter 2. 15, accuses as devoid of Christian charity those who send the poor away and dismiss them from themselves with kind words; much more are those to be condemned who give nothing and receive them with harsh words. It is incumbent upon us, however, to join good words to good deeds, so that we may be numbered among the cheerful givers whom God loves, 2 Cor. 8.

V. 24. A man of friends ought to show himself a friend. For there is a lover who cleaves more closely than a brother.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 396 | printed columns 766-767]

איש רעים (*a man of friends*) is a Hebraism, and a metonymy of the subject for one who delights and rejoices in friendships. In דבק (*cleaves*) there is a metaphor drawn from things joined together with a kind of glue or bitumen, or with some other very tenacious bond.

In אח (*brother*) there is a synecdoche for those most closely joined by nature.

The sum is that mutual duties are required for nourishing and confirming friendship, since its use among men is so excellent.

The latter part of the Proverb must first be treated as the cause of the former. Here the worth and excellence of a friend is set forth and proved by an argument from the lesser, because he cleaves more firmly than a brother. By this form of speech he prefers the friend in efficacious love to those who touch us most closely by blood. And in another place the Holy Spirit appears to prefer a friend not only to a brother but even to a son or daughter, and, to a certain degree of firmness, even to the wife who is in his bosom, Deut. 13. 6. For he rises step by step from brother to daughter, from these to wife, and from wife to the friend whom a man holds dear as his own soul. If you ask why, then, he did not say “than a son,” “than a daughter,” “than a father,” or “than a mother,” I answer that he chose the name of brother before those names because of the parity and equality which exists among brothers and likewise prevails among friends. And certainly we see Job forsaken by his wife and all his household, yet kindly and graciously received by his friends, although, deceived by error, they increased his grief. See the example of Jonathan, who not only yielded the kingdom to David but exposed himself to mortal danger for David’s sake. See also the example of John the Evangelist, who cleaved to Christ to his last breath when Christ’s brothers had deserted him.

It follows from this that friendships must be diligently and carefully cultivated by mutual meetings and bound more tightly by mutual benefits, without which the beginnings and seeds of friendships gradually dissolve. In these matters, what Jehoshaphat rather improperly replied to Ahab ought to carry force: “I am as you are; my horses are as your horses; my servants and subjects are as your servants and subjects,” 2 Chr. 18. 3. Likewise what God promised Abraham: “I will be a friend to your friends, and an enemy to your enemies.” Nevertheless, it is fitting for us to cultivate friendship with this caution: we must not, from zeal for our friends, make ourselves enemies of truth. In friendship, therefore, we ought to stand firm, but only as far as the altars, and only so far as we do not overstep the bounds of honour, as Jehoshaphat and Judah’s friend did, Gen. 38. v. 20, 21.
