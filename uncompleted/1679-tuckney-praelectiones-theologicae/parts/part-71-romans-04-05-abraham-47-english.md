4. But Bellarmine retorts: “When two contrary forms are present, one
inherent and the other extrinsic, an absolute designation must be taken from
the inherent form, not the extrinsic one. Thus an Ethiopian must be called
black from his own blackness, not white from the borrowed whiteness of his
clothing. In the same way, since a person is ungodly and unrighteous from
inherent unrighteousness, he must truly be named ungodly and unrighteous,
not righteous from Christ's righteousness imputed through apprehension and
thought.”

But several points must be dealt with here.

1. He describes justification through imputation as clothing one who is
truly ungodly with Christ's righteousness through apprehension and thought.
He does this in the manner of one who opposes imputed righteousness to true
righteousness and regards it as merely supposed and imaginary—here, as
something effected through our apprehension and thought. But we have refuted
this several times. Illyricus and others among us do divide imputation into
essential imputation and imputation of thought, and they place this
imputation of Christ's righteousness to us in the latter class, that is, in
the class of thought. Yet they do not mean human thought, which is often
false and perverse, but divine thought, in which nothing false can exist.
Consequently, whoever is counted righteous by the omniscient and most
truthful God is truly righteous. Thus in Romans 5:19 the apostle says,
δίκαιοι κατασταθήσονται: they will not merely be counted righteous in
thought, but will truly and really be constituted righteous. For this
imputation is no fiction of human thought, but God's most effectual ordinance
and most valid gift.

2. Bellarmine ineptly treats imputed righteousness and inherent
unrighteousness as contrary forms, since contraries must bear upon the same
thing and be contained under the same proximate genus.

Imputed righteousness and imputed unrighteousness are indeed opposed, as are
inherent righteousness and inherent unrighteousness; but imputed
righteousness and inherent unrighteousness are not.

3. When he says that the designation should be taken from the internal form
rather than the extrinsic one, he accomplishes nothing. The question now is
not so much whether this designation should be taken preferably from the
intrinsic form, but whether it can correctly and rightly be derived from the
extrinsic form. We maintain that it can rightly be derived from either. For,

4. What he says about an absolute designation does not concern us. Since
“absolute” is opposed partly to “relative” and partly to “imperfect and
partial,” we do not say in either sense that a person justified in this life
is absolutely righteous. With respect to justification we do maintain that
he is perfectly, and therefore in the second sense absolutely, righteous.
Yet he is so relatively, through Christ, faith, and grace: by faith he
apprehends Christ's imputed righteousness and applies it to himself, and
thus by God's grace is relatively regarded and pronounced righteous. But
with respect to

[1679 printed p. 295 | IA leaf 303]

sanctification, although he is righteous in himself positively and
inherently rather than relatively, and thus is absolutely righteous in the
first sense, this does not happen ὁλοκλήρως, completely, in this life but
imperfectly. Therefore in the second sense of “absolute” he neither is
absolutely righteous in himself nor is said by us to be so.

5. Therefore, although he is designated absolutely righteous or unrighteous
from neither form, he can nevertheless be rightly designated relatively
from both. Bellarmine's Ethiopian clothed in white garments, though he ought
not to be designated either black or white absolutely and in every respect,
nevertheless must be called black relatively with respect to his body and
its color, but can rightly be called white with respect to his teeth by
synecdoche and with respect to his white garments by metonymy. Similarly, a
justified believer, considered with respect to the remnants of
concupiscence, the body of death, and sin still dwelling within, can humbly
and truthfully call himself unrighteous, as in particular he does. Thus the
penitent publican, though he had already been justified in Luke 18:14,
nevertheless called himself a sinner in verse 13. Yet since designation is
customarily—and properly—derived from the better part, if Bellarmine's
“rather” has any force, the believer undoubtedly ought not to be designated
unrighteous from that unrighteousness which inheres in him in some degree,
but rather righteous from righteousness both imputed and inherent. This does
not follow from the rigor of law and justice, since good comes only from an
integral cause but evil from any defect, and “whoever sins in one point is
guilty of all,” James 2:10. It follows, however, from God's gracious equity
in the gospel through Christ, which pardons sins and graciously regards and
accepts the least true good. When such a person is in the state of
righteousness, God relatively pronounces and designates him righteous. This
is so with respect to a twofold state, both justification and sanctification.

First, with respect to justification: through the imputation of Christ's
most perfect righteousness, though they can be designated righteous only
relatively and therefore not absolutely in the first sense, they nevertheless
must be called perfectly and absolutely righteous in the latter sense. For
when the matter in question is extrinsic, as it is here, why should the
designation not be derived chiefly—even solely—from the extrinsic rather
than the intrinsic form? An Ethiopian clothed in white garments, if we
consider him solely with respect to those garments, must certainly be called
white. And although a person is wholly undistinguished in himself and by
birth, if we consider him insofar as by the king's favor he has been admitted
to an illustrious family or title, he is most properly designated noble. But
in the business of justification this above all is what must be considered,
and so the designation ought chiefly to be derived from it. This very often
occurs in a human court, where one who is justified by a judge's sentence
and freed from guilt is regarded and designated upright and righteous,
though in himself he was previously exceedingly guilty and unrighteous, and
perhaps might even remain such afterward. How much more must this be granted
in the divine court: that one pronounced righteous by God's sentence should
be designated altogether righteous? This is especially so because no one
whom God in justification counts righteous remains afterward unrighteous as
he was before. For,

Second, since in our judgment and Scripture's justification always has
sanctification inseparably joined to it, a justified person may properly be
called righteous with respect to this state of sanctification. He is not
Bellarmine's Ethiopian, white only in his clothes and teeth, but is inwardly
and in his very skin imbued with the whiteness of sanctifying grace. Nor is
he ungodly, as the Jesuit says of him in blasphemy.

[1679 printed p. 296 | IA leaf 304]

God indeed, as the apostle says, “justifies the ungodly,” but this must be
understood antecedently. Consequently, after justification—to which, as I
have said, sanctification is always joined—he does not remain ungodly. In
Holy Scripture only that person is called ungodly who is devoid of all
sanctifying grace and dead in sins, indeed enslaved to the worst crimes.
Thus ἀσεβεῖς and ἁμαρτωλοί, the ungodly and sinners of the worst reputation,
are joined as synonyms in 1 Timothy 1:9 and 1 Peter 4:18. But no truly
justified person is ungodly in this sense; he is godly in the true meaning of
the word, Acts 10:2, 7 and 2 Peter 2:9. Although he sins rather often in his
acts because of sin still dwelling within him—for previously contracted
inward evil habits are not rooted out so suddenly—he is nevertheless partly
sanctified. Indeed, an entire new man has been created in him, God's seed
remains in him, and he is righteous with respect to the settled purpose and
desire of his mind and delights in righteousness. With respect to his more
frequent acts and the continuous course of his life, he also practices
righteousness. Therefore, what is customary elsewhere in ordinary usage
ought fairly to obtain here too: he is designated righteous from the better,
and indeed the stronger, part. Thus, with respect to both the state of
justification and the state of sanctification, every truly justified person
can truly and properly be designated righteous. For we join both kinds of
righteousness in the same person, although not under the same causal
relation. And thus Bellarmine's fourth argument has, I think, been answered
sufficiently and abundantly.
