QUESTION XIII.

Are the Catholic name, antiquity, continuous duration, extent, succession of
bishops, agreement in doctrine with the ancient church, union of the members
with one another and with the Head, holiness of doctrine, the efficacy of that
doctrine, holiness of life, the glory of miracles, prophetic light, the
confession of adversaries, the unhappy end of the church’s persecutors, and the
temporal prosperity of those who have defended it marks of the true church?
We deny against the papists.

I. In the preceding question we saw what the orthodox position is concerning
the marks of the church and by what arguments it is established and defended.
We must now treat the position of the papists. Just as they have conceived in
their minds an idea of the church resembling a civil monarchy, so they invent
marks suited to it and claim them for their own church. Yet they do not even
agree among themselves about their number and order, for some enumerate fewer
and others more.

[Marginalia: The papists’ varying positions concerning the marks.]

II. Some are content with one, which they wish to be either the Roman pontiff
and Peter’s chair, as Lindanus in *Panoply*, book 4, chapter 83; subjection to
the pontiff, as Canisius in the *Catechism*; or the succession of Roman pastors,
as Horantius, book 6, chapter 6. Others set down two, as the Tridentine
Catechism does in its treatment of the Creed: that the church is catholic and
apostolic. Others set down three, as Stapleton does in the proem to book 4:
multitude, visibility, and perpetuity. Others set down six, as Sanders does in
book 8 of *The Visible Monarchy*, chapter 50: 1. height and sublimity; 2.
splendor and brilliance; 3. breadth and immensity; 4. succession and
perpetuity; 5. agreement and unity; and 6. unconquered faith and constancy.
Others set down four, as Hosius does in the *Confession of Piotrków*, chapter
21: that the church is holy, catholic, one, and apostolic. Bellarmine increases
the number to fifteen in book 4, chapter 3, so that he may surpass the others
in multitude, though he admits that they can in some way be reduced to the
four commonly assigned from the Constantinopolitan Creed: one, holy, catholic,
and apostolic.

III. These marks are 1. *the Catholic name*; 2. *antiquity*; 3. *perpetual
duration*; 4. *multitude*; 5. *succession*; 6. *agreement in doctrine with the
ancient church*; 7. *union of the members with one another and with the Head*;
8. *holiness of*

[1847 vol. III printed p. 84 | IA leaf 118 | PDF p. 118]

*doctrine*; 9. *the efficacy of doctrine*; 10. *the holiness of life of its
first authors*; 11. *the glory of miracles*; 12. *prophetic light*; 13. *the
confession of adversaries*; 14. *the unhappy end of persecutors*; and 15. *the
temporal prosperity of its defenders*. We will first make some general
observations about these and then examine each one individually.

[Marginalia: Against the papists’ marks in general.]

IV. In general, we make the following observations against these marks. 1. If
these marks are taken together, they are far harder to know than the one we
propose, namely, the conformity of doctrine with the word. They therefore
cannot be marks, which must be better known. Such are antiquity, succession,
and perpetuity, which could not be known without surveying the history of all
councils and reading the annals of every age. 2. Most are drawn from merely
external, political, temporal, accidental, and contingent things. Consequently
they can constitute truly accidental and contingent marks, not necessary and
essential ones. Such are the confession of adversaries, temporal prosperity,
the unhappy end of persecutors, the holiness of life of the first authors, and
so forth. 3. They are common to other assemblies rather than proper to the
church and thus confuse rather than distinguish, such as multitude, antiquity,
perpetual duration, and so forth. 4. What is wrongly objected to our marks can
rightly be turned back against theirs: they are neither proper, better known,
nor inseparable. 5. Whatever truth they possess must all be reduced to the
primary norm of the true church and of every truth in religion which we gave
above. 6. They do not even apply to the Roman church itself.

V. If Augustine sometimes appears to adduce various marks of this kind,
especially in *Against the Fundamental Epistle*, chapter 4, first, he does
this accidentally and *ad hominem* with respect to heretics against whom the
true mark could have no place because they did not admit the Scriptures. 2.
They are not adduced by him as the principal and primary motives which ought
to persuade the mind, but as secondary and less principal ones which can
prepare the mind to recognize the church until truth itself persuades it. 3.
He admits that truth is far more powerful and must be preferred to all these
prerogatives. “To omit, therefore, that wisdom,” he says, “which you do not
believe to be in the Catholic Church, there are many other things which most
rightly keep me in her bosom. The agreement of peoples and nations keeps me;
the authority begun by miracles, nourished by hope, increased by love, and
confirmed by antiquity keeps me, and so forth. Finally, the very name Catholic
keeps me, and so forth. Therefore, so many and such precious bonds of the
Christian name rightly keep a believing person in the Catholic Church, even
if, because of the slowness of our understanding, truth does not reveal itself
most openly, and so forth. If truth is shown so manifestly that it cannot come
into doubt, it must be preferred to all those things by which I am kept in the
Catholic Church.” And in chapter 14: “Let us follow those who invite us first
to believe what we are not yet able to behold, so that, having been made
stronger by that very faith, we may deserve to understand what we believe, not
so much through human beings as through God strengthening and illuminating
our mind.” From this it is evident that Augustine adduces these things against
the Manichaeans not principally, but secondarily and as something
*supplementary*.

[Marginalia: Specifically, 1. against the Catholic name.]

VI. Specifically, against the *Catholic name* we say: 1. It cannot be an
*essential* mark, because it is often a title without the reality (Isaiah 1:10;
Romans 2:17, 28; Revelation 3:1). The angel of Sardis is said “to live,” though
he “was dead.” Christ foretold that pseudo-Christs would come who would call
themselves Christs (Matthew 24:24). The Jews boasted that they were God’s
people and Abraham’s children, though they were nothing less (John 8:33).
Pseudo-apostles call themselves ministers of righteousness (2 Corinthians
11:14–15). The faithful must be judged not by a name but by the reality of the
thing, and we must ask not who are called Catholics but who are so in reality.
Augustine, tractate 5 on the epistle

[1847 vol. III printed p. 85 | IA leaf 119 | PDF p. 119]

of John, says: “How do you boast that you are a Christian? You have the name,
but you do not have the deed.” And afterward: “If you do not show by deeds that
you are a Christian, let everyone call you a Christian; what good is the name
to you where the reality is not found?” The same must be said *about the
Catholic name*. 2. It is not *proper*, because the Novatians, Donatists,
Arians, and other heretics once claimed this name for themselves. Lactantius,
book 4, final chapter: “Each individual assembly of heretics thinks that they
above all are Christians and that their own church is the Catholic Church.”
Augustine, *Against the Fundamental Epistle*, chapter 4: “All heretics want to
be called Catholics.” 3. It is not *inseparable*, because the church existed
in the Old and New Testaments when it was called neither Catholic nor
Christian. 4. A mark for a disputed thing is wrongly taken from its name,
since the imposition of names is not only arbitrary but is also often foreign
to every rational basis. It is especially absurd to maintain from the name
someone gives himself that he is such as that word signifies. Certain people
are reproached for calling themselves Jews when they are not, but lie and are
the synagogue of Satan (Revelation 2:9). Thus an argument from a name is very
trivial and sophistical. Flatterers and sophists generally impose attractive
names upon the most shameful thing. Nor does a name always arise from
deliberate design, but often from usage and popular opinion, which bestow names
and titles on the most unworthy. Here, however, we seek real marks rather than
nominal ones—marks which signify the thing even when human beings are silent,
not words whose alteration does not alter the thing itself. 5. If being called
Catholic is a mark of the church, it arises either from God—but Scripture is
entirely silent about this—or from adversaries—but they are not our judges—or
from members of the household. But what right do they have to take as a mark
the name they bestow upon themselves, since heretics customarily affix
attractive names to themselves?

VII. (1.) The adversaries object in vain that the Catholic name belongs to no
heretics or schismatics, who are named after their authors—Arius, Nestorius,
Eutyches, Pelagius, and so forth—but only to the true church. Either the name
alone without the reality is meant, and experience in both earlier and our own
times testifies that it can belong to schismatics and heretics in this way; or
the reality together with the name is meant, and it is certain in this sense
that it does not belong to them. But then they depart from the question, which
concerns the name rather than the cause of the naming. (2.) It is falsely
supposed that heretics and schismatics are always named after their authors,
since the authors of many heresies are unknown and their adherents are named
after the thing itself, such as the Acephali, Angelici, Collyridians, Gnostics,
Alogi, Cataphrygians, Apostolici, Nazarenes, and the like.

VIII. If the fathers once distinguished the orthodox from heretics by the
Catholic name, they did not do so because they thought the mask of a name
discriminated the former from the latter, but because of the catholic and
orthodox doctrine which the orthodox constantly held. If, in view of their
places and times, they commend this name, they no more intended to establish
in it a law for marking the church than our writers do in the name Reformed.
Again, when the fathers speak in this way, they understand not the name alone
but the reality joined with it. Cyril teaches this in Catechesis 18 when he
says the church is called catholic “because it teaches universally and without
omission all the doctrines which human beings are obliged to come to know,”
that is, “because it teaches completely and perfectly all the doctrines human
beings are bound to know.”

[Marginalia: 2. Against antiquity.]

IX. The papists’ second mark is *antiquity*. But 1. it is not an *essential
mark*, because it is a common affection of all old things, whether good or
evil. 2. It is not *proper*, since the synagogue of the ancient serpent,
propagated from Cain until now, is also ancient. 3. It is not *inseparable*,
because the church when newly born in the Old and New Testaments was not
ancient, and things which are old today were once new. As the human race had a
beginning, so did the church. 4. It is not clear, but *most obscure*, because
knowledge of it depends upon an exact knowledge of every age.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 86 | IA leaf 120 | PDF p. 120]

For how many people are able to attain this knowledge and know certainly when
this or that doctrine and church began? 5. It is *deceptive*, for as the
Gibeonites deceived the Israelites by pretending to have made a long journey,
so many who arose only yesterday or the day before and whose inventions are
very recent falsely claim antiquity. 6. Pagans continually appeal to antiquity
in their disputations against Christians. Justin, *Apology* 2, attributes to
the Gentiles the statement that “it is good to preserve the ancient customs,”
and calls it the part “of fools” to place custom ahead of truth. Symmachus in
the *Epistle to Theodosius*, in Ambrose, book 3, epistle 30: “If long duration
confers authority upon religions, a faith preserved for so many ages must be
kept, and we must follow our parents, who prosperously followed theirs.”
Lactantius, *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 6: “These are the religions which
they persist most stubbornly in protecting and defending as handed down to
them by their ancestors. They do not consider what they are like, but trust
that they are approved and true because the ancients handed them down. So
great is the authority of antiquity that it is deemed a crime to inquire into
it.” Augustine, *Questions of the Old and New Testament*, question 114:
“Pagans contend that they possess the truth because of antiquity, since, they
say, what is earlier cannot be false, as if antiquity or ancient custom
prejudged the truth.” And shortly afterward: “This is the devil’s custom, that
falsehood should be commended through the channel of antiquity.” We therefore
answer the papists as the ancients answered the pagans: every religion must be
judged not from its antiquity but from its truth.

X. 7. Antiquity and “custom without truth are the age of error, and so forth,”
as Cyprian says in epistle 74; and in epistle 63: “If Christ alone must be
heard, we ought not to attend to what someone else before us thought should be
done, but to what Christ, who is before all, did first. For human custom ought
not to be followed, but God’s truth.” His master Tertullian had already taught
in the *Apology*, chapter 47: “Truth is older than all things, unless I am
mistaken, and the antiquity of divine literature laid down in advance profits
me in this.” And in *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 1: “No one can
prescribe against truth—not an extent of time, the patronage of persons, or the
privilege of regions, and so forth. Our Lord called himself truth, not custom,
and so forth. Heresies are refuted not so much by novelty as by truth; whatever
savors against the truth will be heresy, even an ancient custom.” And in
*Against Marcion*, book 1, chapter 8: “The living and genuine divinity is judged
neither by novelty nor antiquity, but by its own truth.” Arnobius, *Against the
Nations*, book 2: “Authority must be judged not by time but by divine power.”
Thus the faith is contested not by a count of years but by the weight of
arguments. Nor does antiquity cause errors to pass into truth or something
doubtful to become certain. 8. In canon law the authority of ancient custom is
subjected to truth, canon *Truth*, distinction 8, from Augustine: “When truth
has been manifested, let custom yield to truth, and so forth. Let no one prefer
custom to reason and truth, because reason and truth always exclude custom.”
And canon *If custom*, in the same distinction, from Gregory: “If perhaps you
oppose custom, it must be observed that the Lord said, I am the way, the truth,
and the life. He did not say, I am custom, but I am truth. And certainly, to use
the statement of blessed Cyprian, every custom, however ancient and however
widespread, must be entirely subordinated to truth, and so forth.”

XI. Therefore, when the church’s antiquity is discussed, it must not be
understood with respect to the *people* of whom it is composed, because they
continually change and others daily succeed those who have died; with respect
to *places* and temples, because nothing prevents a new doctrine from sounding
forth in the oldest building; or by reason of *chairs*, because those who sit
in them can change their predecessors’ doctrine. It must be understood with
respect to the *doctrine* which is

[1847 vol. III printed p. 87 | IA leaf 121 | PDF p. 121]

delivered in it, when the same doctrine has always persisted and has never
failed or been changed. In this sense the dispute will concern which doctrine
is the most ancient. Thus antiquity alone cannot be a mark of the church in the
way the papists contend, so that apart from knowledge of the doctrine one can
know certainly and infallibly from antiquity which is the true church. Rather,
the antiquity of doctrine will in this sense belong to our mark, and so we
admit that the most ancient doctrine is the truest.

XII. The antiquity of dogmas can be judged from Scripture alone, so that only
what is derived not from some centuries but from the first origin and the
institution itself may be considered true. For what can be called ancient to
us and our age, because it prevailed for a thousand years or more, can be new
in religion if it had a place only after religion’s institution. Christ recalls
us to this in speaking of divorce (Matthew 19:8), when he says, “it was not so
from the beginning,” in order to teach us to ascend to the beginning or first
origin of religion if we wish to attain its truth—from turbid and muddy streams
to the spring, from lying and inconstant human beings to Christ, who cannot lie
or change and who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. In this sense
Tertullian says that what is first is truest in *Against Marcion*, book 4: “If
it is established that what is earlier is truer, that what is from the
beginning is earlier, and that what is from the apostles is from the beginning,
then it will equally be established that what was held sacred among the
apostolic churches was handed down from the apostles. Let us see what milk the
Corinthians drank from Paul, according to what rule the Galatians were
corrected, and what the Philippians, Thessalonians, Ephesians, and so forth
read.” Here he limits the proof of antiquity to Paul’s epistles. He speaks in
this sense against the authors of sects and heresies as new and recently born
in *Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 37. After saying, “Who are you?
When and from where did you come?” he adds: “The possession is mine; I have
possessed it for a long time; I possessed it first; I have secure origins from
the very authors to whom the property belonged. I am the apostles’ heir; as
they provided in their testament, as they entrusted to faith, and as they
adjured, so I hold.” The rationale is not obscure. Scripture alone is
self-authenticating, divine, and older than all the rest. The fathers’ writings
from which the papists wish to draw their antiquity, however, are merely human,
far more recent and intricate, and many are doubtful, forged, and spurious.
Scripture alone must therefore also be the norm of this doctrinal antiquity.
We readily allow our religion and church to be weighed according to this rule,
and we do not plead against its being condemned as false if it lacks this
antiquity. But if this examination is undertaken, it will be clearly evident
that true and genuine antiquity belongs to us and that the novelty they object
to us belongs to the adversaries. For the doctrines which the Roman church
thrusts upon us and about which we dispute are nowhere read as handed down by
Christ or the apostles. It is therefore necessary that its faith has been
changed and has fallen away from its former condition.

XIII. To ward off the accusation of novelty from themselves, the adversaries
contend that their doctrines have always prevailed in the church unless it is
shown precisely by whom they were authored and at what time they arose. Thus
they maintain that no change has been made in the Roman church because, as
Bellarmine says in *The Church Militant*, book 4, chapter 5, “in every notable
change these six things can always be demonstrated: 1. its author; 2. some new
dogma; 3. the time when it began; 4. the place where it began; 5. who opposed
it; and 6. some small assembly which began gradually to grow as others joined
it.” He asserts that all these things apply very well to our churches but in no
way to his. Arnauld eagerly seized upon this ingenious invention and attempted
with great effort to confirm it in his *Treatise on the Perpetuity of Faith*,
in order to prove that no change concerning the Eucharist had been made in the
faith of the Roman church. He contends that it could not have occurred, and
proves this from the claim that so notable a change could not

[1847 vol. III printed p. 88 | IA leaf 122 | PDF p. 122]

have occurred in the church without great contention of minds and many
contradictions, of which, nevertheless, no mention is made among the ancients.

XIV. But anyone who examines more closely this subtle fabrication, in which
the adversaries so greatly please themselves, can easily recognize that it is
a mere deception by which they try to impose upon the simpler. First, when the
fact is established, it is ridiculous to hunt for its circumstances and,
though a change is evident, deny its possibility. If someone found a person
lying in a pit, would he doubt or deny that the person whom he sees there was
in the pit unless he first understood by whom and when the person was thrown
into it? If someone came upon a wounded person on the road who was dying, or
even dead, would he nevertheless not believe it unless he knew the killer, the
time, and the place? If someone found a sunken ship, would he not believe it
unless he knew how and at what time it first began to take in water? Since
these things are utterly absurd, how can the adversaries escape ridicule when
they confront us with the same argument to ward off the charge of novelty and
apostasy from themselves? We say that the Roman church, wretchedly changed,
has fallen away from the faith of its ancestors, and we are prepared to show
it. To avoid this examination, however, they flee to the impossibility of
change and refuse to believe any change has occurred unless we demonstrate to
them its authors, place, time, and other circumstances. Second, they falsely
contend that those six things must concur to demonstrate a change. The second
heading, namely the novelty of the dogma, suffices. If it can be demonstrated
by a certain argument that the dogma is new and was not handed down by
Scripture, this suffices to demonstrate the change, even though the dogma’s
author or the time and place of the change are unknown. The rule of truth God
gave us is the word, not the history of the ages. Christians are bound to know
the former for salvation as alone God-breathed, but they are not bound to know
the latter because it is a merely human writing. Add that God binds us to
reject whatever is added to the word or does not appear in it. Therefore, to
discover that a dogma is ancient and not new, it must be proved from Scripture.
Cyprian teaches this excellently in his *Epistle to Pompeius*: “From where is
this tradition,” he says, “does it descend from the Lord’s and the gospel’s
authority, or come from the apostles’ commands and epistles? For God testifies
that those things which have been written must be done, and he sets before
Joshua, saying: the book of this law shall not depart from your mouth, but you
shall meditate upon it day and night, so that you may observe to do everything
that has been written.”

XVI. [sic] Third, it is utterly absurd to infer from ignorance of the author, time,
or place of some change that no change exists. It is as though, when stolen
property was found with someone, the thief thought it sufficient for his
justification that the lawful owner could not indicate the place, time, and
manner in which the property had been stolen from him. Yet if stolen property
is discovered in the possession of a receiver, the good-faith possessor who
claims his property and proves his right wins his case and the receiver is
condemned, even if the possessor cannot establish where, by whom, and when the
theft was committed. The falsehood is known; thieves, moreover, customarily
steal at night, secretly, and without people’s knowledge. Thieves would be
treated very well indeed if they were not regarded as thieves and I had no
right to my stolen property unless I demonstrated those six things. One thing
suffices: that the property is mine. Once I establish this, a just action is
given. Likewise, as soon as an error and new dogma are established, the case
is settled, and no further proof of its author or time is necessary. Fourth,
one change is *for the better* and another *for the worse*. The former is light
which dispels darkness; it is therefore more evident and can be observed more
easily, for light flashes and does not seek secret arts. But the latter
customarily occurs not openly but secretly and craftily, so that it may not be
observed by people and may creep in more easily, like adulterers who steal in
secretly through a back entrance far from human sight, and thieves who break
into houses and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 89 | IA leaf 123 | PDF p. 123]

plunder at night without people’s knowledge. Such is the oversowing of
*tares*, which was done at night while the householder and his household were
asleep, and they consequently marvel at the deed (Matthew 13:26). “While the
people were sleeping, the enemy came, sowed tares among the wheat, and went
away.” Nor should it be said with Bellarmine that the tares were sown at night
but did not sprout then, and that they appeared as soon as they did sprout. It
is not said either that they sprouted only during the day or that they appeared
as soon as they had sprouted, but that “when the plant had grown and produced
fruit, then the tares appeared.” Thus the servants see the change but do not
know its author, time, or manner, because it was done while they were asleep.
So, while people do not attend, the hostile man scatters tares in God’s church
and changes its true condition.

XVII. Fifth, just as some diseases are *sudden* and abruptly attack a
previously healthy and vigorous person, with their time and causes capable of
being noted—such as plague, apoplexy, and so forth—while others are *slow*, with
hidden beginnings and gradual increases which are initially not felt or at
least not observed, but finally occupy the whole body—such as consumption,
dropsy, cancer, and gangrene, whose cause and time cannot be noted so
exactly—so in the church’s body some *changes* are *sudden and open*, when a
departure from the truth occurs abruptly and openly in one or more articles
while pious and orthodox teachers resist. The circumstances of such changes
can be noted. Other changes are *slow and hidden*. They have a small beginning
but afterward creep like gangrene more and more (2 Timothy 2:17) until they
finally occupy the church’s whole body. Consequently, their circumstances,
whether with respect to time or to authors and manner, can scarcely, if at all,
be noted. The reality itself cries aloud that such a change was made in the
Roman church, and the name “mystery of iniquity” sufficiently indicates it.
Here belongs Aristotle’s observation in *Politics*, book 3, that changes which
occur gradually are not perceived by the senses. They can be seen to have
happened, but it cannot be observed how and when they occur. Thus fermentation
produced by leaven is seen in a lump of dough, but neither its beginning nor
its manner can be perceived. Likewise, if some grains were removed daily from
a great heap of grain, and the same number of other grains were substituted in
their place and mixed into the heap, who could recognize the change? Who could
observe how much height a boy gains each day, or how much a person ages daily?
Who, looking upon the wrinkled and deformed face of a gray-haired old man,
could note precisely the moment, hour, month, or year in which he began to age,
change color, and suffer a loss of strength? Yet anyone who denied or failed to
recognize so evident a change in old age would be out of his mind. Countless
examples of changes can be adduced from the sciences, commonwealths, and
physical things. Because they customarily occur little by little, gradually,
and imperceptibly, neither their precise beginnings nor their progress can be
observed. Historians accordingly observe that a great change occurred in the
Roman commonwealth. As Livy relates in book 2, “As discipline gradually
declined, dissolving morals fell more and more. Then they began to plunge
headlong, until the age of Augustus was reached, when they could endure neither
their vices nor their remedies.” Yet the first authors, time, and other
circumstances of that change cannot be indicated. In precisely the same way,
by comparison with the ancient Roman church we detect in the Roman church a
notable change of doctrine and degeneration from the faith of its ancestors,
although no one can note exactly every circumstance of that change. This has
all the more place in the business of religion because the devil secretly and
fraudulently injects poison through tunnels to attain his desire, working the
mystery of iniquity covertly and deceitfully (2 Thessalonians 2:7), displaying
the horns of a lamb (Revelation 13:11), and transforming himself into an angel
of light (2 Corinthians 11:14). He did not employ one author but many, and not
all at once, but little by little and successively introduced corruptions into

[1847 vol. III printed p. 90 | IA leaf 124 | PDF p. 124]

doctrine and worship, not through direct opposition and an assault upon or
extinction of acknowledged truth, but through an indirect addition of
falsehood and a joining of error with truth. This can creep in gradually under
the appearance of explanation and be introduced beneath the cloak and terms of
truth itself.

XVIII. Sixth, this very thing can be demonstrated by various examples. No one
is unaware that the greatest and most shameful change in doctrine occurred in
the Jewish church from the time of Malachi until Christ’s advent. But anyone
would labor in vain to specify the authors, places, and times of so deadly a
corruption. Nor do we read that Christ, who had the moments of all times and
events accounted for and who, possessing the most complete knowledge of
chronology and history, could do this better than anyone, ever met the scribes,
Pharisees, and teachers of the law on this basis when he reproved and convicted
them of having altered and corrupted the ancient doctrine of the law. He used
Scripture and doctrine alone, teaching us by his example what path we ought to
follow in detecting and reforming errors—namely, the norm of the word, not the
scrupulous observation of all the circumstances which contribute nothing to
the matter when the fact itself is established. Thus it is known that the most
serious errors were introduced into the Galatian and Corinthian churches not
long after Paul’s preaching. Yet who could note either the authors certainly or
the time precisely? No one is unaware that the error of the Millenarians also
prevailed in the primitive church. Yet who could specify by whom, where, and
when it began? The same could be said concerning administering the Eucharist
to infants, the souls of the saints not entering paradise immediately after
death, and various other errors which had a place in the ancient church.

XIX. Seventh, if we were to proceed to an examination of the errors taught in
the Roman church, it could easily be shown both that they were unknown to the
apostolic and primitive church and that they were afterward introduced at
various times. They are therefore novelties and more recent things which, by
the papists’ own hypothesis, ought rightly to be rejected by us. The veneration
and worship of images was sanctioned only in the eighth century, in the Second
Council of Nicaea in the year 781 under Pope Hadrian I, but shortly afterward
was condemned by the Council of Frankfurt assembled by Charlemagne. The
supreme authority of the pontiff in spiritual as well as temporal affairs, by
which he is said to judge the whole world, be judged by no one, absolve subjects
from their oath of fidelity, depose kings and emperors, and do similar things
of this kind called the *Dictates of the Pope*, was sanctioned by Gregory VII
in 1076, as Baronius acknowledges under the year 1076. Shortly before 1050,
under Nicholas II, it was decreed in a particular council that Christ’s body is
perceptibly touched and broken by the hands of priests and ground by the teeth
of the faithful. At the beginning of the thirteenth century,
transubstantiation was sanctioned by Innocent III at the Lateran Council and
added to a new creed as an article of faith in 1218. And in the year 1764
[sic], the feast of the Sacrament was instituted by Urban IV and its procession
in 1336 by John XXII. The doctrine of purgatory was received as an article of
faith at the Council of Florence under Eugenius IV in 1439. Communion under
one species, with the cup removed from the people, had been decreed shortly
before 1414 at the Council of Constance. The same could easily be shown
concerning other errors whose times and authors are not unknown. Indeed, it
can be demonstrated not obscurely that many errors and superstitious rites
either derive their origin from paganism and Judaism or flowed from ancient
heretics, if the plan of our undertaking permitted it.

XX. The adversaries wrongly contend that the novelty of our religion is
inferred from the newness of those who professed it, such as Waldo, Wycliffe,
Hus, Luther, and others. They were not its authors but only *heralds* and
*restorers*, who taught no doctrine other than that of the prophets and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 91 | IA leaf 125 | PDF p. 125]

apostles. It is one thing to purge ancient doctrine of its corruptions and
recall people to it, but another to devise a new doctrine not previously handed
down and propose it for belief. The Reformers did the former, not the latter.
They gave nothing of their own but handed down what they had received from
Christ. Religion must therefore be traced not to them, but to Christ, who had
taught it in his word.

XXI. The passages from the fathers which the adversaries adduce for antiquity
must either be referred to the antiquity of doctrine, which we readily concede,
or they pertain only to probable signs, not to certain and essential marks.
Truth must always be preferred to them, as Augustine notes in *Against the
Fundamental Epistle*, chapter 4, when he says that if the Manichaeans could show
that they possessed the truth, he would pass over to them, setting aside all
the other things by which he was kept in the Catholic Church.

[Marginalia: 3. Against perpetual duration.]

XXII. Bellarmine’s *third mark* is *perpetual and wholly uninterrupted
duration*. For the church is called Catholic not only because it has always
existed but also because it always will exist. This mark differs from the
preceding one in that the former regards past time and this one future time.
But it is wrongly assigned. 1. It is not an essential mark, since duration is
an accident distinguished from the thing’s essence. 2. It is not proper, since
it belongs neither to every church—for example, a recent and new church—nor to
the church alone, because assemblies of pagans, Jews, Arians, Muslims, and so
forth have endured and still endure in this way. The tares must remain until
the time of harvest (Matthew 13), and the goats are not separated from the
sheep until the day of judgment. 3. It is not inseparable, as examples from
the ancient church and that of the New Testament show; more than one
interruption and variation of duration befell them. 4. Since the perpetual
duration of some assembly, considered apart from true doctrine, does not make
it a true church, this duration cannot be a mark by which someone ignorant of
the doctrine can arrive at a certain and infallible knowledge of the church.
One must always begin with an examination of doctrine. Nor can the perpetual
duration of the faith and doctrine which some assembly professes be judged
certainly from anywhere but Scripture, since God’s word alone is incorruptible
and remains forever. The adversaries, however, avoid this as a dangerous rock.

XXIII. It is one thing for the invisible church of the elect always to endure
and never fail, but another for a particular visible church, which we ought to
join, to do so. We grant and previously proved the former; we deny the latter.
It was proved above that the passages which speak of the eternity of Christ’s
kingdom and say that the gates of hell will not prevail against the church
must be understood of the catholic, invisible, and properly so-called church,
which is not bound to a particular see or succession, not of a visible and
particular church.

XXIV. Although Paul, speaking of heretics, says, “They will proceed no
further, because their folly will become manifest to all” (2 Timothy 3:9), it
does not follow that it can be said universally of assemblies of heretics that
they will not endure long, since the contrary is evident from various examples.
Indeed, the apostle adds in verse 13 that “they will advance toward worse and
will be deceivers and deceived.” Rather, the apostle’s meaning is that if pious
pastors diligently perform their duty, false teachers and heretics will not
advance as much as they wish. They will indeed deceive many, but not true and
pious believers, who cannot be seduced.

[Marginalia: 4. Against extent.]

XXV. The fourth mark is *extent*, or *the multitude of believers*. They prove
it from the claim that the truly catholic church must embrace not only all
times but also all places, all nations, and every class of human being. But 1.
it cannot be an essential mark of the church, because it is a mere accident
which belongs contingently to the church. It contributes nothing to orthodoxy
whether few or many profess

[1847 vol. III printed p. 92 | IA leaf 126 | PDF p. 126]

it; where only two or three are gathered, it does not cease to be the church.
2. It is not proper, because the church is so far from being commended for its
extent that, on the contrary, its smallness is its characteristic. Hence it is
comparatively called by Christ a “little flock” in relation to the world (Luke
12:32), and the “gate is narrow and the way constricted which leads to life,
and few are those who find it,” while “the way is broad and spacious which leads
away to destruction, and many enter through it” (Matthew 7:13–14). The false
church draws a multitude after it. Hence “the whole earth is said to follow the
beast, and all, small and great, rich and poor, free and slave, must receive its
mark on their right hand and on their foreheads” (Revelation 13:3, 16), and
“the harlot is said to sit upon many waters” (Revelation 17:1). Nor is it
obscure that this multitude belonged to Arianism, which once occupied the
entire East and West. Indeed, it belongs today also to paganism and Islam, if
Breerwood is to be believed in *On the Languages and Religions of the World*,
chapter 14: “If,” he says, “the known regions of the earth are distributed into
thirty equal parts, the portion of Christians will be five, of Muslims six,
and of pagans nineteen.” 3. It is not inseparable. There was a time when the
church was very small, as in the time of Noah, Abraham, and Christ and in those
most sorrowful ages when the woman was compelled to flee into the wilderness,
as experience demonstrates. Nor can the papists themselves deny this, since
they believe that at the advent of Antichrist the church will be reduced to the
smallest number by his cruel persecutions. Christ himself foretold that at his
advent at the end of the age faith would be very rare. 4. This mark cannot be
a mark for everyone; for how many know who and how many profess the faith
throughout the whole world? Nothing can be known about this except from the
deceptive and doubtful report of lying human beings.

XXVI. 5. The fathers confirm this more than once. Chrysostom, *To the People
of Antioch*, homily 26: “What have I to do with a multitude? I prefer one
precious stone to many small coins, and so forth.” And homily 40: “What benefit,
I ask, is there in a great deal of hay rather than a few precious stones? A
multitude consists not in the greatness of number but in probity of virtue.
Elijah was one, but the whole world was not worthy to be weighed against him.”
In the works of Athanasius, tractate 2, there is a discourse against the
Eutychians entitled *Against Those Who Measure Truth by the Multitude*. There
he teaches that when Christ sent the disciples, he did not order the disciples
to follow the crowd and the myriads, but the myriads to adhere to the twelve;
that truth always possesses its own strength, though it is found among a few;
and that a multitude without a demonstration of truth can indeed inspire
terror but not persuade. And afterward: “Do we not hear the Lord Jesus saying,
Many are called, few are chosen; the gate that leads to life is narrow, and few
are those who enter through it? Who therefore would not prefer to be among the
few who are saved rather than among the multitude who rush through the broad
gate into destruction? and so forth.” He adds many things there which are most
pertinent to the matter and very worthy of reading. Gregory Nazianzen,
discourse 25 *Against the Arians Boasting of Their Multitude*: “Where are
those,” he says, “who define the church by a multitude and despise the little
flock? They have houses; we are tenants. They have temples; we have God, in
addition to being temples of the living God. They have living victims; we have
spiritual burnt offerings. They have the populace; we have angels. They have
rashness and audacity; we have faith. They have threats; we have prayers and
supplications. They have gold and silver; we have the purified doctrine of the
faith.” Jerome, *Against the Pelagians*, book 3, speaking under the name of
Atticus to Critobulus: “A multitude of associates will by no means show that
you are a Catholic, but that you are a heretic.” See Augustine on Psalms 29 and
128.

XXVII. The passages which describe the extent of Christ’s kingdom, such as
Psalm

[1847 vol. III printed p. 93 | IA leaf 127 | PDF p. 127]

2:8 and Psalm 72:8, are oracles concerning the calling of the Gentiles. They
must not be understood absolutely, but comparatively and with qualification,
not with respect to the world and the totality of the ungodly, which has always
been greater than the totality of the faithful, but by antithesis to the state
and church of the Old Testament, which was confined to one region and nation.
It is one thing for the New Testament church to be extensive and extended
indiscriminately to all nations, but another for extent to be a mark of the
church. Although the church was to be spread among every nation, it does not
follow either that every nation would embrace the gospel at the same time and
simultaneously or that every person in those nations belongs to the church. It
suffices if some nations receive the gospel and some people in those nations
belong to the church.

XXVIII. Although Christ gave marvelous success to the apostles’ preaching,
the number of pagans in the Roman Empire was for a long time greater than the
number of Christians. When Christians prevailed over the Gentiles in the
empire, they could never be said to have prevailed with respect to the entire
world.

[Marginalia: 5. Against succession.]

XXIX. The fifth mark is *a continuous succession of bishops, brought down
without any interruption from the apostles to our times*. Here, however, a
distinction must first be made between kinds of *succession*. One is *personal
and local*, with respect to persons and places; the other is *doctrinal*, with
respect to faith. We do not deny that the latter is a mark, and in this sense
it coincides with ours. But the former, which is treated here, cannot be a mark.
1. It is merely contingent and foreign to the church’s nature unless it is
founded upon the unity of sound doctrine and true faith (Ephesians 4:4–5,
11–12). Indeed, since the church is migratory and not bound to this or that
place, its succession is also of this kind. 2. It is not proper, because it
also belongs to false teachers. Annas and Caiaphas succeeded Aaron in the
priesthood; the scribes and Pharisees succeeded the fathers and interpreters
of the law; the Arians succeeded the orthodox; and the Greek church, which the
papists regard as schismatic and heretical, boasts of an uninterrupted
succession of bishops from the apostles. Bellarmine himself admits near the
end of *The Marks of the Church*, book 4, chapter 8, that “it cannot necessarily
be inferred that the church exists where succession exists.” 3. Local and
personal succession can exist without doctrinal succession. It therefore
cannot be a mark of the church, because a church which does not have apostolic
doctrine cannot be true. For the church must not be sought in walls and temples
but in dogmas; the church is where the true faith is. Ambrose, *On Repentance*,
book 1, chapter 6: “Those who do not have Peter’s faith do not have Peter’s
inheritance.” 4. It is not inseparable, because there is no place for succession
at the beginnings of newly arising churches. The Christian church was true and
apostolic from its beginning, yet it had no succession. 5. If local succession
is a mark of the church, then many heretical and schismatic churches are true
churches, because they can have such succession when false teachers succeed to
the places and sees of true pastors. 6. We would be wretchedly treated if our
faith rested upon the faith of those who lived between us and the apostles. It
is neither for us to inquire what kind of people they were nor does it profit
at all. Add that this knowledge can only be had from histories and annals of
uncertain trustworthiness, which even the most learned often cannot attain.

XXX. The chair of Moses, upon which the scribes and Pharisees are said to sit
(Matthew 23:2), is not a continuous personal succession to Moses’ office. Since
his office was extraordinary and terminated in him, it could have no
succession; nor is any chair ever said to have been assigned to him. Rather,
Moses’ doctrine is meant. It had been entrusted to the scribes and Pharisees
and customarily was read and expounded in the synagogues every Sabbath (Acts
15). In this sense Gregory Nazianzen says excellently in his *Oration on
Athanasius*: “The one is of the same name and throne; the other is opposed in
throne and deed. The former possesses the title, but the latter the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 94 | IA leaf 128 | PDF p. 128]

truth of succession. Nor is one who holds contrary opinions a successor, but
one who holds the same faith, unless someone were to call a disease the
successor of health, darkness of light, a storm of calm, and ecstasy of sound
judgment.”

XXXI. When the fathers adduce succession in disputing against heretics, they
do not understand personal or local succession so much as doctrinal
succession, or they always join and presuppose the latter. Tertullian teaches
this openly in *Prescription Against Heretics*, chapter 37: “Therefore, let
them produce the origins of their churches; let them unfold the order of their
bishops running down through successions from the beginning, so that the first
bishop had as his author and predecessor one of the apostles, or apostolic men
who nevertheless persevered with the apostles. Let the heretics fabricate
something of this kind, but even if they fabricate it, they will advance
nothing. For their very doctrine, when compared with the apostolic doctrine,
will pronounce from its diversity and opposition that, just as the apostles
would not have taught things differing from one another, so apostolic men
would not have published things opposed to the apostles.” Likewise Irenaeus,
*Against Heresies*, book 4, chapter 43: “It is necessary to obey the presbyters
who are in the church, those who have succession from the apostles, as we have
shown, who together with the succession of the episcopate received the certain
gift of truth according to the Father’s good pleasure. But the rest, who depart
from the principal succession and assemble in any place whatever, must be
regarded with suspicion, either as heretics and people of evil knowledge, or
as schismatics, proud and self-pleasing, or again as hypocrites working here
for the sake of gain and empty glory, and so forth.” Augustine, *On the Unity
of the Church*, chapter 4: “Whoever dissents from the Holy Scriptures, even if
they are found in every place in which the church is designated, are not the
church.” And chapter 16: “We do not wish to prove our church either from the
succession of bishops, the authority of councils, or the frequency of miracles,
and so forth.”

[Marginalia: 6. Against agreement in doctrine.]

XXXII. The sixth mark is *agreement in doctrine with the ancient church and
the fathers after the apostles*. Concerning this we say that if by the ancient
church they understood the apostolic church as we possess it in the evangelical
and apostolic writings, we concede it can be called a mark, and so it coincides
with ours. But if by it they understand either the ancient church of the first
four or five centuries after the apostles or the church which preceded our
times by some centuries, we deny that it is a mark. 1. It is not *essential*,
for the church is built only upon the foundation of the prophets and apostles
(Ephesians 2:20), not upon the writings of the fathers (Ezekiel 20:18–19;
Matthew 5:21, 27; Galatians 1:8–9). 2. Nor is it *proper*, because heretics
also boast of that agreement and can possess it at least in some things, since
the purity and integrity of the ancient church was not always the same. 3. Nor
is it *inseparable*, because it cannot belong to the patriarchal and apostolic
church.

XXXIII. 4. It is deceptive and under its name the unwary are very easily
deceived, as was said concerning antiquity, especially since no witnesses or
authentic and canonical records exist which must be believed absolutely. Not
all the fathers wrote, they did not write about everything, they did not set
out to hand down to us a rule of faith, and many of their writings perished and
did not reach us. 5. It is most obscure and very difficult for the people to
know and requires infinite labor, since it demands a full and perfect knowledge
of every age and of each father’s opinion. Yet how many attain this? The most
learned labor in reading and understanding and do not always grasp the meaning
in

[1847 vol. III printed p. 95 | IA leaf 129 | PDF p. 129]

every instance. They torment themselves in reconciling passages and often
cannot extricate themselves from the difficulties they encounter. 6. The
discussion concerns either dogmas on which there is agreement or disputed
ones. If the latter, as is necessary because we seek marks for discrimination,
the greater part of our controversies were either unheard of by the fathers or
were not committed to writing. It is unjust to drag the fathers’ statements,
uttered more freely before they had observed the outcome of events, into
present-day opinions. 7. Whatever is read in one or another father must not be
regarded as the doctrine of the ancients, but only what was either regarded as
a dogma of the faith in general councils or is proposed as a dogma from the
agreement of all the fathers. 8. Even the most ancient authors who wrote were
not free of their own blemishes and errors. They often disagree with themselves
and are not all harmonious in all things. Add that their writings did not reach
us pure and intact but wretchedly corrupted and spoiled, and that many
counterfeit and spurious productions were attributed to them through
pseudepigrapha, as learned men demonstrated long ago. The fathers themselves
did not wish us to be bound to their writings or held to their opinions, as we
proved in Part I, Locus II, Question XXI. 9. The papists themselves often
depart from the fathers and do not wish to be held to their determinations when
they see that the fathers oppose them. Baronius, under the year 34, section
213: “The Catholic Church does not always follow the most holy fathers in every
interpretation of Scripture.” Bellarmine does the same throughout by carping at
and refuting the fathers’ opinions which please him less.

XXXIV. The ancient church is either the *apostolic* church or the *primitive
church of the fathers after the apostles*. The church which agrees with the
former is simply the true church (Ephesians 2:20). One which agrees with the
latter is so only with qualification, that is, insofar as it retains the
foundation of apostolic doctrine (1 Corinthians 3:11–12). Agreement with the
church of the later ages which preceded ours by a few centuries is boasted of
in vain, because no antiquity is true unless it is traced from the beginning
according to Christ’s rule in Matthew 19:8.

[Marginalia: 7. Against the union of the members with the Head.]

XXXV. The seventh mark is *the union of the members with the Head and with
one another*. But this cannot be a mark either. 1. Since union can be of two
kinds, one *internal* and the other *external*, neither can be a mark. Not the
*internal* and mystical union of believers with Christ and one another,
because it is invisible and known to God alone. Not the *external*, whether
with a visible Head, because there is none, or of the members with one another
through a profession of the same doctrine, communion in the same sacred rites,
and tokens and duties of mutual love. For this can be feigned and hypocritical,
separated from the truth of doctrine, without which there can be no true union.

XXXVI. 2. Again, it cannot be a proper and essential mark, because it also
belongs to many profane and heretical assemblies. Before the flood the earth
was of one language and all flesh had corrupted its way (Genesis 6). The ten
tribes worshiped the calves at Bethel; the Jewish synagogue united against
Christ to destroy him; the world is said to follow Antichrist with great
agreement and concourse (Revelation 17). The Arians also boasted of their
union. To this belong Hilary’s words *Against Auxentius*: “The name of peace is
indeed attractive and the idea of unity beautiful, but who doubts that the one
peace of the church and the gospels is that which belongs to Christ, and so
forth? The forerunners and ministers of the imminent Antichrist boast of their
peace, that is, the unity of their impiety, acting not as bishops of Christ but
as priests of Antichrist.”

XXXVII. 3. It is not inseparable, because disagreement can and often has
existed among Christians without loss of truth (Acts 11:2; 15:39; Philippians
3:15–16). Peace and concord continually commended by Christ and the apostles
are indeed a property of a well-constituted church, the duty of all, and the
surest means of protecting and preserving the church’s doctrine. But a church
which labors under certain disagreements and whose parts

[1847 vol. III printed p. 96 | IA leaf 130 | PDF p. 130]

are pulled apart on various points does not immediately cease to be true,
provided agreement remains on the foundation. Thus there were once various
disagreements among the apostles concerning primacy, between Paul and Peter,
and between Paul and Barnabas. Likewise among the ancients there was a very
sharp contention which endured for many ages between the Eastern and Western
churches concerning the celebration of Easter. There was more than one
disagreement among the fathers, such as between Irenaeus and Victor, Cyprian
and Stephen, Chrysostom and Epiphanius, Jerome and Rufinus, Cyril and
Theodoret, and various others. Yet they did not cause the churches of that time
to lose either their name or their truth.

XXXVIII. 4. Since there are various kinds of union, some in evil and others in
what is good and true, only the unity resting upon truth must be approved.
Unity must therefore be judged from faith, not faith from unity. Thus the truth
of doctrine upon which every true and saving union rests must first be known
before the church is established. Otherwise, as Nazianzen says well in his
*Oration on Peace*, “Dissension arising for the sake of piety is better than a
corrupt concord.” Therefore, one must attend carefully and define that in which
Christian union is to be constituted, lest it be *tyrannical* and servile
because of fear of punishments and the rigor of the inquisition; *Herodian*, as
when Herod and Pilate, otherwise hardly harmonious, united to persecute Christ;
or *erroneous*, when a union is given in the profession of the same error. This
must be called not so much agreement and true union as a deadly conspiracy,
such as those made in various robber councils which adopted and approved by
their vote the deadly errors of the Arians, Eutychians, Pelagians, and similar
heretics. Rather, it must be *evangelical* and rest upon the truth of faith.

XXXIX. The ancients never believed agreement with or obedience rendered to
the Roman pontiff to be a mark of the church. They regarded the Asian churches
as churches even if Victor had excommunicated them, as also the African
churches, though they dissented from the pontiff. Likewise, the Greek churches
retained their name no less than the Latin, although they often dissented from
the Romans and rendered no subjection to the pope. Athanasius did not cease to
be orthodox even if Liberius consented to his condemnation. Nor did Cyprian,
although Stephen had expelled him from his communion and would not admit even
to a common conversation the bishops sent to him from Africa, and had
instructed the entire brotherhood that no one should receive them into his
home, so that not only shelter but even peace would be denied to them when
they came, as Cassander notes on the seventh article.

XL. The passages of Scripture which say that the church is one body, one
bride, and one sheepfold (Romans 12; Song of Songs 6; John 10) do not properly
treat the visible church, whose marks are in question, but the invisible church
of true believers. They indeed teach that union is required for the church’s
constitution, namely in faith and truth (Ephesians 4:4–5), but it does not
follow from this that union simply and separately can be a mark of the church,
since it belongs to countless other things which possess either natural or
political unity.

[Marginalia: 8. Holiness of doctrine.]

XLI. The eighth mark is the holiness of true doctrine because, as Bellarmine
says in chapter 11, the true church “is not only catholic, apostolic, and one,
but also holy, as the Council of Constantinople has it. The church is holy
because its profession is holy, containing nothing false with respect to the
doctrine of faith and nothing unjust with respect to moral doctrine.” But this
mark clearly coincides with ours and concedes to us what the adversaries try
elsewhere to wrest from us. If holiness of doctrine is a mark of the church,
then doctrine itself is much more a mark, since holiness is only a quality of
doctrine. Again, if the church is holy because of the holiness of doctrine, it
is much more so because of doctrine itself, to which such

[1847 vol. III printed p. 97 | IA leaf 131 | PDF p. 131]

holiness belongs. Third, since holiness of doctrine is known from nothing other
than the truth of doctrine from which it is derived—“Sanctify them in your
truth; your word is truth” (John 17:17), and “You are clean because of the word
which I spoke to you” (John 15:3)—to say that holiness of doctrine is a mark of
the church is to say what we maintain: that the truth of doctrine, or conformity
with God’s word, is the mark. Nor do we refuse to acknowledge as true the
church which is called holy because it contains nothing false with respect to
the doctrine of faith and nothing unjust with respect to moral doctrine. This
can be known from nothing other than comparison with Scripture.

[Marginalia: 9. The efficacy of doctrine.]

XLII. The same must be said concerning the *efficacy of doctrine*, which is
their ninth mark, because the true church alone possesses doctrine which is
not only undefiled but also converts souls, as is said in Psalm 19:8 and
Hebrews 4:12. Since no doctrine can possess this divine efficacy for the
conversion of human beings unless it is God-breathed and recorded in the
writings of the prophets and apostles, the efficacy of doctrine cannot be a
mark of the church without the doctrine which produces such an effect being a
mark much more. Second, since error also possesses its own force and efficacy
for deceiving, according to which “the coming of the man of sin” is said “to be
with every power from the force and efficacy of Satan, and so forth,” and God
is said “to send an efficacy of error, so that people believe the lie” (2
Thessalonians 2:9–10), and Babylon is said to intoxicate all the earth’s
inhabitants with the wine of her fornications (Revelation 17 and 18), the
efficacy of Satan can be distinguished from that of the divine Spirit only by
the torch of the divine word. Not every conversion of human beings from one
religion to another immediately demonstrates the efficacy of divine grace and
the true church, but only conversion to the true faith and religion. This can
be known from nowhere but God’s word and the truth of doctrine drawn from it.
Moreover, because the adversaries wish to measure the efficacy of doctrine by
the multitude of converts, we say that in this sense it cannot be a mark of the
church. Although God’s word when preached never falls entirely in vain (Isaiah
55:11), God does not always, everywhere, or in an equal degree cooperate with
his word, but when, where, and as much as pleases him (Matthew 11:26–27;
13:11). There are times of fruitfulness when an abundant harvest is gathered
for the Lord and times of barrenness when few are converted and God’s faithful
servants lament sinners’ rebellion and unbelief, as is often done by the
prophets, Christ, and the apostles.

[Marginalia: 10. Against the holiness of the fathers’ lives.]

XLIII. The tenth mark is *the holiness of the lives of the authors, or first
fathers, of the religion*. But 1. since holiness of life is the fruit of the
efficacy of doctrine, and the efficacy of doctrine depends upon its truth,
holiness of life cannot prove the truth of the church except in relation to the
truth of the doctrine whose effect it is. 2. No one is infallibly certain about
the holiness of others’ lives because true holiness is something internal,
known to God alone as the searcher of hearts, and hypocrites often imitate it
so well that those who are most corrupt are regarded as holy, just as “Satan
transforms himself into an angel of light, and deceitful workers disguise
themselves as ministers of righteousness” (2 Corinthians 11:13–15).
Consequently, holiness of life cannot be the mark we seek. If internal holiness
is meant, it is invisible; external holiness is deceptive and doubtful. 3. Not
a few heretics boasted of peculiar holiness, such as the Donatists, Cathari,
and Manichaeans. Augustine says of them in *The Morals of the Catholic Church*
that they displayed so great an appearance of holiness that they deceived many.
Indeed, Pererius in *Daniel*, book 14, and Acosta in *On the Last Times*,
chapter 20, section 3, teach that the simulation of hypocritical holiness will
be peculiar to Antichrist. As for the fathers or authors of religion, since we
acknowledge no author of our religion besides Christ, whose interpreters

[1847 vol. III printed p. 98 | IA leaf 132 | PDF p. 132]

were only the God-breathed prophets and apostles, the holiness of his life,
however perfect, cannot be the mark we seek, because all Christians who
acknowledge Scripture claim it for themselves, and common things do not
differentiate. It certainly helps to prove the truth of the Christian religion
against unbelievers, but not to distinguish the true orthodox church from false
assemblies which boast in Christ’s name.

[Marginalia: 11. Against the glory of miracles.]

XLIV. The eleventh is *the glory of miracles*. But 1. it cannot be an
essential mark because miracles depend upon and must be judged from doctrine.
They must not be received unless the doctrine for whose confirmation they are
performed conforms to God’s word; otherwise they must be rejected as
counterfeit (Deuteronomy 13:1). 2. It is not proper, because pseudo-prophets
also boast of miracles (Matthew 7:22–23; 24:24–25), and heretics claim them,
such as the Donatists in Augustine’s *On the Unity of the Church* and tractate
13 on John, and the Nazarenes in the same author’s *On Heresies*. Above all,
Antichrist ought to be famous among them (2 Thessalonians 2:9–11; Revelation
13 and 17). 3. It is not inseparable, because the church gathered by many
prophets and by John the Baptist was distinguished by no miracles. 4. Miracles
are accidental and extraordinary gifts which were given to the church only for
a time, not forever—for the first constitution of Christianity but not its
continuation. “Miracles of this kind,” Ferus says in the preface to book 2 of
his *Commentary on Matthew*, “were necessary at the gospel’s beginning, until
the Lord’s word was placed in possession, so to speak, of the truth. But now
that it has once been received and believed by everyone as truly God’s word,
there is no need for new miracles; it suffices to produce the miracles
performed by Christ and the apostles, and so forth.” Stella on Luke 11 and
Maldonatus on Matthew 7 confirm the same.

XLV. 5. The fathers profess this more than once. Augustine, tractate 13 on
John: “My God made me cautious against those miracle-mongers, saying: in the
last days many pseudo-prophets will arise, performing signs and wonders to lead
even the elect into error, if possible. Behold, I have told you beforehand.
Therefore the Bridegroom made us cautious, because we must not be deceived even
by miracles.” And in *On the Unity of the Church*, chapter 16: “Let those
things be removed, whether they are fabrications of lying human beings or
wonders of deceitful spirits. Either the things said are not true, or if some
wonders were done by heretics, we must be all the more cautious because, when
the Lord had said that certain deceivers would come, and so forth, he added in
earnest admonition, Behold, I have told you beforehand.” And afterward: “As for
deceptive visions, let them read what has been written: that Satan himself
disguises himself as an angel of light and that their dreams have seduced many.
They also hear the things pagans relate as wonderfully done or seen concerning
their temples and gods, and yet the gods of the nations are demons, and so
forth.” Chrysostom, homily 23 on John: “Is it not also a matter of temptation
to complain even now of the scarcity of signs? There are certainly those in
our age who ask why signs do not occur now. If you are faithful as you ought
and love Christ as he ought to be loved, you do not need signs, for signs are
given to unbelievers.” Gregory the Great, homily 29 on the Gospels: “Surely, my
brothers, you do not fail to believe because you do not perform these miracles?
But these things were necessary in the church’s beginning, so that the multitude
might grow toward faith. When we plant shrubs, we likewise pour water upon them
until we see that they have taken root in the earth; once they have fixed a
root, watering will cease.”

XLVI. 6. No one is unaware that there are countless frauds and impostures in
miracles which disclose their vanity and furnish proof that things which are
nothing of the kind in reality are thrust upon the lowly populace as miracles.
Either events which never occurred are recounted; or they are wonders which
can surpass the powers of ordinary natural causes but are not true miracles
which exceed every finite power; or they are mere fabrications by which the
wretched common people are

[1847 vol. III printed p. 99 | IA leaf 133 | PDF p. 133]

deceived. Lyra observes this on Daniel 14: “The greatest deception of the
people occurs through fictitious miracles performed by priests or those who
adhere to them for temporal gain, and so forth.” In *On the Donation of
Constantine*, Valla says that itinerant performers and charlatans deceived the
people with fictitious miracles. It would not be difficult to adduce countless
examples of such fabrications and false miracles if that were our present
subject.

XLVII. Not everything necessary for a time is absolutely necessary, nor is
something necessary for a thing immediately a mark of that thing. Miracles were
necessary for Moses and Christ for the confirmation of their doctrine, but not
equally for us, because the inference does not hold from the church to be
constituted to the church constituted, or from doctrine to be confirmed to
doctrine confirmed. Bellarmine himself cannot deny this when he says that
“miracles are necessary for a new faith or an extraordinary mission.” But we
do not preach a new faith; we recall people to the ancient faith confirmed by
Christ’s and the apostles’ miracles. Nor do we boast of an extraordinary
mission outside the order instituted by God, although, as will be shown in its
proper place, not every extraordinary mission needs miracles.

[Marginalia: 12. Against prophetic light.]

XLVIII. The twelfth is *prophetic light*. But it is wrongly made a mark,
whether by it they understand the gift of predicting the future or the gift of
interpreting prophecies. 1. It is an extraordinary, not perpetual, gift which
belongs to miraculous charisms as a species to its genus. 2. God sometimes
granted this gift even to false prophets and impostors (Deuteronomy 13:2;
Numbers 23:7; 24:3; John 11:51; Matthew 7:22). Gentiles likewise displayed
their oracles and heretics their revelations. The adversaries themselves do
not deny this: Thomas, III, question 172, article 6; Pererius on Exodus 11,
disputation 11; Maldonatus on Matthew 7. 3. Many true churches lack those
illustrious gifts which were peculiarly granted to the apostolic church at the
beginning of the New Testament. The Spirit of Prophecy ceased in the ancient
church from the time of Malachi until Christ, yet the church did not cease to
exist. Nor, after doctrine has been revealed to us in the Scriptures, should
we gape after revelations or rashly believe them. 4. If it were a mark of the
church, new prophecies would have to be expected every day, which is absurd.

XLIX. Joel 2:28 indeed promises under the New Testament the most abundant
miraculous outpouring of the Holy Spirit at the beginning, but thereafter the
Spirit’s perpetual assistance and ordinary, not miraculous, gifts, according
to the Old Testament style in which gospel mysteries are expressed in legal
terms. But it does not thereby prove that this is a mark of the church, because
not every gift is a mark.

[Marginalia: 13. Against the confession of adversaries.]

L. The thirteenth is *the confession of adversaries*. But it is not an
essential mark, being an external testimony proceeding from outsiders which
has no force in a case that must be decided only by divine testimony, since the
one approved is the one the Lord commends (2 Corinthians 10:18). Nor is it
proper, because Christians in their candor freely grant to both Gentiles and
heretics whatever praise and virtue they possess. Thus Basil praises Libanius
the sophist, Ausonius praises Symmachus, and Augustine praises Tyconius the
Donatist; they do not thereby approve their faith. It is the mark of
ingenuousness to praise virtue even in an enemy. It is not inseparable, because
although they are sometimes compelled by the force of truth to grant some
praise to the church, they more often tear it apart with insults and defame it
with slanders, according to Christ’s prediction (Matthew 5:11). It is not
legitimate, because no confirmation of truth arises from it; it is not
honorable to borrow strength from enemies’ testimonies. Thus Christ closed
Satan’s mouth lest he proclaim him to be the Messiah. An adversary’s testimony
can indeed be an effective *ad hominem* argument, but it is not thereby a mark
of the church.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 100 | IA leaf 134 | PDF p. 134]

[Marginalia: 14. Against the unhappy end of enemies.]

LI. The fourteenth is *the unhappy end of enemies and persecutors*. But who
would say that something outside the church can be its mark, much less an
essential and proper mark? Add that in this life a sad end often also befalls
the church and its members, as the examples of Christ, the apostles, and the
holy martyrs show. The end of the pious is indeed blessed, but it is not
conspicuous to the world; rather, they appear most miserable. Finally, although
there are many fearful examples of divine judgment upon persecutors, not all
perished unhappily, nor were there lacking those who died a peaceful, natural
death.

[Marginalia: 15. Against temporal prosperity.]

LII. Finally, the fifteenth mark is held to be *temporal prosperity*, but the
whole of Scripture protests, speaking of the cross and calamity as Christ’s
banner, the companion of truth, and the portion of believers (Acts 14:22; 2
Timothy 3:12; 1 Corinthians 15:9; Matthew 5:10). Prosperity, however, is the
badge of Babylon which is attributed to the world (John 16:20; Revelation
18:7). Therefore, so far is the true church from being distinguishable from
the false by temporal prosperity that, on the contrary, by this criterion the
false church is distinguished from the true much more certainly. It says, “I
sit as a queen and will see no mourning.” The promises of temporal blessings
made to godliness (1 Timothy 4:8; 1 Samuel 2:30) and those which befell the
pious patriarchs and kings must be understood of true and solid prosperity,
not merely external prosperity, which is fleeting, transitory, and common also
to the ungodly. This is with the exception of chastening and the cross which
Scripture adds (Isaiah 30:20; Mark 10:30; Psalm 99:8). Moreover, it is one
thing for some promise to be given to the church, but another for it to be its
mark.

LIII. From these things it is evident that Bellarmine wrongly adduces all
these marks to prove the true church. Others do no better when they try to
construct as many marks from the Creed’s four titles, namely, that it is *one,
holy, catholic, and apostolic*. These badges of the true church belong to a
church only because of true faith and doctrine, so that the latter alone is
the church’s mark. For that church is truly one, holy, catholic, and apostolic
which religiously retains the “unity of the faith once delivered to the
saints” (Jude 3) and the *holiness* of catholic truth according to the
foundation of the *prophets* and *apostles* (Ephesians 2:20).

LIV. Even if these things which the papists thrust forward as marks were as
true as they are counterfeit, it is easy to show that Rome boasts of them
falsely and that they cannot belong to it in reality. Not *the Catholic name*,
because it is inconsistent to hold that a particular church such as the Roman
is catholic and universal, just as the world is greater than the city. Nor
*antiquity*, because the Greek church is older than the Roman, and the dogmas
which Rome today proposes for belief in disagreement with ours are novel and
recently born, or known only for some centuries. They were unheard of and
unknown to the first and true antiquity which must be traced from the times of
Christ and the apostles. We can rightly say of them with Christ, “it was not
so from the beginning” (Matthew 19:8). This could readily be demonstrated
concerning the pope’s primacy and infallibility; purgatory, human satisfactions,
and the treasury of indulgences; the bodily presence, transubstantiation, and
the sacrifice of the mass; the merit of works and powers of free choice; the
invocation of saints and worship of images; unwritten traditions; and other
errors of this kind, both theoretical and practical, which Rome has sanctioned
and thrust upon people to be believed under penalty of anathema, as our writers
have proved at length.

LV. Not *extent*, because the jurisdiction and communion of the bishops of
Alexandria and Constantinople throughout Asia and Africa is greater than the
pope’s throughout Europe. There, if you except nearly only the Spanish and
Italians, no nation adheres to him wholly, and the most powerful kingdoms and
states have

[1847 vol. III printed p. 101 | IA leaf 135 | PDF p. 135]

shaken off his yoke. Not uninterrupted *succession*. Apart from the fact that
it lacks doctrinal succession, as can be gathered from an examination of its
doctrine, “there is no true succession without true doctrine,” as Stapleton
says in *The First Principle of the Faith*, Controversy 1, *On the Church*,
question 4, article 2. Who can deny that the thread of personal succession in
that see was broken long ago? This occurred through a long vacancy of the see
or dissension among antipopes; through illegitimate occupation of the see,
whether by force, fraud, simony, or magic arts; and through various heresies
and schisms which had a place in the Roman church and through which the
adversaries themselves cannot deny that succession cannot be continued. For
example, where was uninterrupted succession when various heretical pontiffs
occupied the see and various men intruded themselves into it by evil arts?
Where was it in the ninth and tenth centuries, which Baronius calls the iron
age, when, according to Genebrard, more than fifty pontiffs fell entirely away
from the virtue of their ancestors, being apostatic and apostactical rather
than apostolic? “What then was the appearance of the Roman church,” Baronius
says under the year 912, “how most shameful, when the most powerful and equally
filthy prostitutes ruled at Rome, at whose discretion sees were changed and
bishops created and, what is dreadful and unspeakable to hear, their lovers,
pseudo-pontiffs who are written in the catalogue of Roman pontiffs only to mark
such times, were intruded into Peter’s see. For who could say that men intruded
by such prostitutes without law were legitimate Roman pontiffs? Nowhere is
there any mention of the clergy choosing or afterward consenting. All the
canons were suppressed in silence, the decrees of the pontiffs stifled, ancient
traditions proscribed, the ancient customs in electing the supreme pontiff,
sacred rites, and the former usage entirely extinguished, and so forth. Christ
was then plainly sleeping a deep sleep in the boat, and so forth. What seemed
worse, while everyone snored there were no disciples to rouse the Lord thus
sleeping with their cries. What sort of presbyters and cardinal deacons do you
suppose were chosen by these monsters? and so forth.” Let the adversaries now
go and boast of their uninterrupted succession. The same could be shown
concerning the time of those most serious and long-lasting schisms by which the
Christian commonwealth was wretchedly torn apart while antipopes raged against
one another with censures and excommunications, as historians relate at length.

LVI. Nor *agreement in doctrine with the ancient church*, whether one regards
the primitive and apostolic church, from whose faith it differs immensely, or
the church which succeeded the apostolic times in subsequent ages. A comparison
of both can abundantly teach that Rome agrees little with it. Nor would it be
difficult to show that the fathers, whatever the papists boast, by no means
favor the Roman church, provided various cautions are observed. 1. If you
consider the writers of the earlier ages in which Christianity was still purer,
for we do not deny that errors increased with wealth in the later ages. 2. If
you remove pseudepigrapha and cut away forged things. 3. If you seek the
agreement of all and do not rest in one or another. 4. If you diligently
extract and weigh not merely one or another passage, but the authors’ opinion
and mind from all their writings. 4. [sic] If you remove all the sayings which
are fabricated or were added and inserted, as can be established by comparing
many manuscripts. 6. If you read every passage whole rather than severed and
truncated. 7. If you remove fictitious statements and falsely attributed
opinions. 8. If you distinguish the things they said panegyrically rather than
didactically; when, carried away by the heat of disputation, they tended toward
extremes; to serve a cause; with a doubtful and wavering mind; and allegorically
and figuratively rather than properly. 9. If you distinguish their private
opinions or errors from the dogmas received in the church and observe other
things of this kind, which you may see explained at length in Rivet’s *Orthodox
Catholic*, Jewel against Harding and Campion,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 102 | IA leaf 136 | PDF p. 136]

Scultetus in the preface to *The Kernel of the Fathers*, Morton’s *Catholic
Apology*, Molinaeus, Cappellus, and, above all, Dallé’s *On the Use of the
Fathers*.

LVII. Rome is so far from being able to prove its agreement with the purer
ancient church—a comparison of the doctrine received in each, both in faith and
worship, can readily demonstrate this, as our writers have often done—that
rather it is easy to observe its agreement and consensus with various heretics
condemned of old. With the Angelici, Collyridians, and Staurolatrae in the
worship of angels, the blessed Virgin, and the cross; with the Montanists,
Manichaeans, Encratites, and Tatianists in the selection of foods, appointed
fasts, and the law of celibacy; with the Pelagians and Semi-Pelagians in the
doctrine of free choice, the merit of works, and the perfection of
righteousness; with the Marcionites and Pepuzians in the baptism of women; with
the Manichaeans in prohibition of the cup and communion under one species;
with the Essenes in the use of a foreign language in sacred services; with the
Euchites, Barefooted, and Apostolici in monastic vows; with the Sampsaeans in
the worship of relics; with the Basilidians, Carpocratians, and Gnosimachi in
the prohibition of Scripture; with the Eutychians, Marcosians, and Elcesaites
in transubstantiation; with the Heracleonites in extreme unction; and with all
heretics in the necessity of traditions and accusation of the Scriptures, as
Rivet in the proem to *The Orthodox Catholic*, Molinaeus, Downham, and others
have proved at length.

LVIII. Nor *the union of the members with one another and with the head*,
since the sharpest contentions and dissensions exist among the Romanists
themselves, not only concerning external rites and ceremonies but the weightiest
dogmas and principal heads of the faith: the pope’s infallibility; his authority
above a council or a council’s above the pope; predestination, grace, and free
choice; the conception of the blessed Virgin; the merits of works; the
visibility and locality of Christ’s body in the Eucharist;
transubstantiation and its consequences; and countless other things of this
kind debated every day between Thomists and Scotists, Molinists and Jansenists,
the advocates of predetermination and middle knowledge, and Franciscans and
Dominicans, which their head has not yet been able to settle.

LIX. Nor *holiness of doctrine*, since by its various dogmas it opens a broad
gate to crimes and establishes most shameful errors, impious idolatries, and
the harshest tyranny. Nor *the efficacy of doctrine*, unless you understand
the efficacy of error which the apostle attributes to the mystery of iniquity
(2 Thessalonians 2). The reality itself cries aloud how little suited it is to
the conversion of sinners. The adversaries appear sufficiently convinced of
this, for in propagating the faith they use not so much the light of persuasion
as the violence of arms, not so much arguments sought from Scripture and a
demonstration of truth to convict the conscience as the allurements of
promises and the terrors of threats and tortures. On the one hand they bait
base souls by a display of honors, pleasures, and riches; on the other they
rage with iron and flames, bloody edicts, and most dreadful persecutions. Thus
they turn the sword of the divine mouth by which error ought to have been
destroyed into the mouth of the sword by which the faithful themselves are
killed.

LX. Not *the holiness of the lives of the first authors of the religion*.
Either the prophets and apostles are meant, whose holiness can confer nothing
upon those who fall away from their faith, or the pontiffs and founders of
religious orders. The writers do not conceal how little restrained the morals
of most of the latter were, not to say how impure and ungodly. Not *miracles*,
whether you regard Christ’s and the apostles’ miracles, which can indeed serve
to confirm the apostolic doctrine we retain but contribute nothing to the
confirmation of the errors they introduced beyond and against Scripture—it
would be ridiculous to seek to prove from them purgatory,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 103 | IA leaf 137 | PDF p. 137]

transubstantiation, indulgences, and other fables of this kind whose very names
were then unknown—or regard the miracles of subsequent ages and those hawked
by papists in the most recent times. They indeed boast of them but do not
demonstrate them or perform them while we are present and watching, though it
would be fitting either to sway or punish us. Indeed, they have often professed
that the presence of some Protestant impedes the wonder-working power of their
images and relics. Although they boast everywhere that miracles occur here and
there, scarcely any of them receive the pope’s approval, so well aware are they
of frauds and lies in that class. If anyone wished to relate them, an entire
volume could be made from such impostures. See Cappellus against Ferrarius,
chapter *On Impostures*, and Molinaeus on 2 Thessalonians 2. Nor is it unknown
how many have been publicly convicted as false, as can be seen in Papon’s
*Decisions of the Court of Parliament*. Canus, book 11, chapter 6, says: “We
cannot deny that even the most serious men have sometimes gathered scattered
rumors, especially in describing the wonders of the saints, and transmitted
them to posterity in writing.” And shortly afterward: “I do not excuse here the
author of that book entitled *Mirror of Examples*, nor of the history called
the *Golden Legend*. In the former you will more often read monstrosities of
miracles than true miracles; a man with an iron face, a leaden heart, and a
mind certainly insufficiently serious and prudent wrote the latter.” Even if
such miracles were as true as they are false, those who hawk them should not
be heard when they assert doctrine contrary to the gospel, according to
Galatians 1:8, all the more because Antichrist is said to come with signs and
lying wonders.

LXI. Nor *prophetic light*, which long ago ceased in the church along with the
gift of miracles. They do not dare attribute it to pontiffs or councils, and if
any revelations are boasted of among them, they are so vain and foolish that
they cannot gain credit among their own people. Nor *the confession of
adversaries*. Although our writers do not conceal whatever good still remains
in the Roman church, no one who knows its errors can attribute to it the name
of the true and orthodox church. Nor *the unhappy end of its adversaries*,
whatever false prattle the papists utter about the unfortunate death of our
Reformers, as our writers have proved more than once by the surest arguments.
With how much greater right can we answer them with tragic examples of God’s
judgments in the deaths of various pontiffs, kings, princes, and other
persecutors of the church which historians relate? Nor *temporal prosperity*,
which the adversaries themselves cannot deny has already greatly diminished
through various blows inflicted upon the Roman church. But enough of these
things.
