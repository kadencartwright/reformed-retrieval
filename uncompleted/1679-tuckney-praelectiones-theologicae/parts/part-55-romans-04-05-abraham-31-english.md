Nevertheless, I do not deny that certain things can be objected against this
exposition of the passage.

The first is that this consummation or perfection should perhaps be
understood of the perfection they had attained in this life. The sense
would then be that under the covenant of the Gospel we have by faith
approached the fellowship of those righteous persons now deceased who,
while they lived here, reached the highest summit of perfection through
their eminent faith and excellent works. Theophylact's comment appears to
point this way: “the spirits of righteous persons made perfect, that is,
the souls of those who appeared illustrious and perfect before God, namely
through faith.” If this exposition stands, the passage seems so far from
proving our contention—that the souls of the righteous are wholly cleansed
from sin and consummated for the first time immediately after death—that

[1679 printed p. 275 | IA leaf 283]

it rather calls righteous people perfect before death, as our adversaries
wish. It does not say “spirits then made perfect for the first time,” but
“spirits of righteous persons previously consummated,” namely before death.

But if the Apostle had intended this, he would have said “to righteous
persons made perfect.” By saying “to the spirits of righteous persons made
perfect,” he plainly designates the soul's separated state and assigns this
consummation to it: the crown and prize laid up for them after the race was
completed. Thus our Hammond rightly illustrates this passage athletically
and with scholia, using the agonistic idea that occurs so frequently in
Paul. In this sense the phrase is frequent among Hebrew writers, who call
those raised to a place beneath the throne of glory “included saints” or
“perfected saints,” as they like to speak. But here we are in part, to be
perfected hereafter, 1 Corinthians 13:10; therefore we are not perfect here.

Second, Cornelius a Lapide and certain others explain this of the patriarchs,
prophets, and saints of the Old Testament. Though they were not perfect
while confined in limbo before Christ, when Christ descended to the dead
and broke the gates of that limbo, those godly souls flew out. When the gate
of heaven was opened through his ascension, they entered with him and were
at last perfectly consummated. The Apostle affirms that through Christ and
the grace of the Gospel we have fellowship with all these, just as he had
said in the final clause of the preceding chapter that “God provided
something better for us, that they should not be consummated without us.”

I answer:

1. God did indeed provide something better for us so that believers before
Christ should not be consummated without us, namely with respect to the
incarnation. They had not received the promise—that is, the promised seed,
Christ incarnate—before the fullness of time, in whom we as well as they
have been called to grace.

2. This is not the place to refute the claim that they were not consummated
throughout the whole period before Christ because, being held in limbo,
heaven had not yet been opened. Some learned and godly writers, ancient and
modern, have held and still hold that they had not yet entered heaven,
because “the way of the saints had not yet been disclosed while the first
tabernacle was still standing,” Hebrews 9:8. Some interpreters take “the
way of the saints” as the way belonging to holy people, as though the road
to heaven were impassable and pathless for them all. Thus Chrysostom and
Theophylact. But the Apostle does not speak this way. He did not say that
the way to heaven was so completely closed as to be wholly inaccessible and
impassable to everyone, even the holiest, but only that it had “not yet been
disclosed”—“not yet made known” in the Vulgate, “not manifested” in Beza,
and “not laid open” in Piscator. A door not plainly visible by night or in
darkness is not thereby altogether closed. Nor do only our theologians
explain this position in this way. Among the Roman theologians Estius
understands “the way of the saints” as “the grace of the New Testament,
through which we have access to heaven,” which in the Old Testament had not
yet been dis-

[1679 printed p. 276 | IA leaf 284]

closed or manifested—that is, openly known and preached—but lay hidden
under the shadows of the ceremonies. After Christ's advent, when his Gospel
was preached and the legal shadows removed by his passion, it began to be
manifested and openly acknowledged. If Estius's statements stand, the
scholastic cloud of limbo is empty despite this passage. The ancient fathers
under the Law entered heaven immediately after death and were therefore
perfected in their souls even before Christ. Cornelius a Lapide's
interpretation of the passage thus collapses entirely.

A third objection remains to be answered, both to explain the passage more
fully and to confirm the proof we derive from it. The Apostle, it is argued,
speaks in this clause about the things that will occur at the last day of
judgment. Then will come “the festal gathering and assembly of the
firstborn,” and then God will be “the Judge of all.” The following words
about the spirits of righteous persons consummated therefore also appear to
refer to their final and complete consummation, which occurs only then, not
immediately at death as we have maintained.

I answer:

1. I grant that all these things are said consummatively and with respect
to their highest degree about that final day. Only then will the term
“festal gathering” apply most properly, when every believer who has ever
lived anywhere will appear together before Christ, gathered into one. He
will then be manifested as the Judge of all, for it is the day he appointed
“in which he will judge the world,” Acts 17:31. Only then will all the
righteous be made completely whole and consummately perfect—not only in
their souls, which they possessed from death, but also in their bodies,
which from death until that final day remained under death's dominion. On
that final day this last enemy will be abolished, 1 Corinthians 15, and the
souls so long widowed will be restored to their bodies. From that reunion
and the glory added to it arises universal wholeness and perfection in
every respect.

2. Yet this does not prevent all the righteous from being rightly called
and truly being perfect with respect to their souls in the separated state,
since by death they are separated from sin as well as the body. They then
also approach God the Judge of all more closely, becoming fellow citizens
of the heavenly Jerusalem with all the blessed patriarchs and prophets,
who, according to Calvin's interpretation, constitute “the festal gathering
of the firstborn.” Or if, with others, that festal gathering or assembly is
to be made up of the whole Church and all believers universally, who are
elsewhere called firstfruits, then as soon as we truly believe the Gospel
we approach them all: those fighting against sin in this life and those
perfectly freed from sin immediately at death and triumphing in heaven.
