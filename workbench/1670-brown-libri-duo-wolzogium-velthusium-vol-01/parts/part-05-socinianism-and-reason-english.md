5. On page 13 Wolzogen has certain things which scarcely—indeed, hardly at
all—can be cleansed from the dregs of *Socinianism*. Whatever varnishes he
uses, he can scarcely remove this suspicion from himself. *For if*, he says,
*scoffers and profane people who do not see the truth, and all the others who
grope blindly in reading the Scriptures, see nothing not through the fault of
the Scriptures but through that of their own minds—minds covered with the
darkness of natural ignorance, under which I comprehend every impediment to
saving knowledge, so that they cannot perceive the truth described even by
the sun's rays—and although they would discover it if God willed, since he
could free them from their glaucoma, nevertheless he is not bound so to will;
for they have fallen into this blindness by their own fault, though they were
created by God with a perceptive mind burdened by no preconceived opinions.—If
some do not understand the Scriptures, this happens because they do not read
the Scriptures rightly; but they do not read rightly because they do not
apply a purified mind; and they are unable to apply this through their own
fault alone.* Anyone who weighs these things will admit with me that they are
not so purified as to have no savor of *Socinianism*. For the elder
Velthuysen, whom he regards as most tenacious of orthodox truth, acknowledges
no natural, innate, or implanted blindness or inability to know saving truths,
but only the same blindness which the

[Page 38 | ONB canvas 92 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000092]

Socinians acknowledge. For on page 61 of *On the Use of Reason, etc.*, he has
these words: *It is contrary to Scripture to posit in learning and knowing the
truth of faith any kind of inability other than that which manifests itself
in the pursuit of virtue and godliness. And Scripture teaches that the
ungodly remain willingly in ignorance and deadly error, just as willingly and
of their own accord they pursue ungodliness: 1 Peter 3:15; Matthew 11:14; 2
Thessalonians 1:10; John 5:10 and 10:13, 33; Revelation 22:17; Matthew 37;
John 20:27; Colossians 6:7; Romans 11:22; James 1:16.* But a man must himself
be willfully and maliciously blind if he denies or does not know that we are
blind by nature and unable to grasp spiritual things, that Scripture testifies
to this, and that these scriptural passages do not conflict with it. Both are
true, and subordinate truths do not conflict.

But further, on page 66, he says: *Nor does what they say have any weight:
that Scripture teaches that a person destitute of the Spirit cannot perceive
the things which are of God's Spirit, 1 Corinthians 2:14, and that it is the
common Doctrine of our theologians that without God's grace a person cannot
grasp the mysteries of God. For the passages of Scripture and the Reasons
cited above sufficiently teach us that this inability must be understood only
as a moral inability—namely, that people cannot know the mysteries of faith
because they are unwilling—not because they do not possess within themselves
the principles of that knowledge and the faculty of knowing by which the
things delivered in Scripture are grasped and understood, so that they need
new principles or a new faculty, but because the natural man is so led by the
affections of the flesh that he will never bring his faculty of knowing into
use.* Recognize here, Reader, the Socinian spirit and almost the very words of
Schlichting against Meisner: *That blindness*, he says, *of the intellect is
sometimes voluntary and affected; that blindness is treated in the first two
passages cited by you, 1 Corinthians 2:14 and Ephesians 4:18*—(*which passage
our Velthuysen also*

[Page 39 | ONB canvas 93 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000093]

*employs on page 67*)—*yet by these words it is not said so much that the
natural man utterly fails to perceive the things which are of God's Spirit as
that he does not approve them or rest in them. He does this because of his, so
to speak, animality; that is, because he looks and aspires to nothing except
this present life and the things that pertain to the earth.* Now if Wolzogen
does not hold the same position as Velthuysen, he must say that Velthuysen is
heterodox and cherishes a monster of *Socinianism* in his bosom. But since he
says that Velthuysen has a mind tenacious of orthodoxy, he cannot rightfully
condemn us for learning from the Elder what the Pastor's position is.

2. Wolzogen himself has very many things, as we shall see, which confirm us in
this suspicion and which he cannot disguise by any argument. Such are the
things he has on pages 64–66, 130, 252–254, and more often elsewhere.

3. Nothing is said here which Socinus himself or some Socinian would not say
in the same way. For, 1. he does not name the unregenerate or those not
illumined by God's Spirit, but scoffers, profane people, and the rest—of the
same flour, of course—who grope blindly in reading the Scriptures. But
Schlichting himself will admit that these scoffers and profane people grope
blindly in reading the Scriptures because they are natural—that is, because
they aspire to nothing but the things pertaining to this life, have their
minds wholly sunk in earthly things, and reject heavenly things. How easily
Wolzogen could have named the unregenerate, if he had seen fit to speak
orthodox things in an orthodox manner!

2. He says that *their minds are covered with darkness*. But one may ask what
sort of darkness this is: adventitious or innate? If merely adventitious and
acquired by their perverse affections, the Socinians too will acknowledge it,
for they admit that people corrupt themselves voluntarily and are corrupted
through a habit of acting wickedly. The Remonstrants too, in *Confession*,
chapter 1, section 14, admit that people sometimes permit themselves to be
blinded by prejudice,

[Page 40 | ONB canvas 94 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000094]

vain confidence, and other perverse affections. Moreover, Wolzogen's own words
on page 8 of *Censure of the Censure* must be noted: *For we are treating*, he
says, *the drawing out of the meaning of the Scriptures. As long as this lies
hidden from us, it leaves the mind in ignorance of divine things; once it has
been disclosed and found, it imbues the mind with saving knowledge. Now since
nothing more definitely expresses the nature of knowledge and ignorance,
whether by similarity to the thing itself or by the most common usage of
speakers and writers, sacred and profane, common and learned, than light and
darkness, I did not seem able to speak more suitably than if I said that our
minds are covered with the darkness of natural ignorance.* From these words
it is sufficiently apparent that by this darkness Wolzogen understands at
most merely the privation of that knowledge which is obtained from the meaning
of Scripture once drawn out—a privation to which even the Regenerate are
liable.

Therefore there is nothing here about that innate blindness by which people
are unfit and unable, without that most special work of the Spirit by which he
infuses and activates new light, savingly to know even the things that have
been revealed. Accordingly, from the disclosed and discovered meaning of the
Scriptures alone they cannot be imbued with saving knowledge unless the
Spirit works most effectually and savingly. Moreover, when he says that the
Spirit dispels this darkness, he implies that the Spirit does nothing other
than draw out the meaning of the Scriptures. For he says that as long as that
meaning lies hidden, the mind left in ignorance is a mind covered in darkness;
he says the Spirit dispels that darkness—that is, removes that ignorance—by
the meaning of Scripture once drawn out. How will Wolzogen varnish these
things so that they appear pure from *Socinianism* and cleansed?

3. He does indeed say *natural ignorance*. But, as we have now seen, by that
darkness of natural ignorance which covers the minds of some he understands
nothing beyond a privation of knowledge of the meaning of the Scriptures.
This knowledge,

[Page 41 | ONB canvas 95 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000095]

with respect to certain passages of Scripture, even the Regenerate can lack
and often do lack, though they have been freed, at least in part, from that
innate and original blindness understood by the Orthodox. I should next like
to ask what he understands by the word *natural*. From what has now been said,
it is plain that he cannot understand what the Orthodox understand by the
words *innate, implanted,* and *original*—because it arose from Adam's fall—and
similar terms. The Socinians themselves can concede a certain blindness which
may even be said to be born with people. Thus, if by natural blindness
Wolzogen understands that which is born with certain people or is propagated
by the power of the seed, he will nevertheless not appear cleansed from the
Socinian plague. For Socinus himself speaks as follows, cited by Hoornbeeck in
*The Refutation of Socinianism*, book 3, chapter 3, page 564: *Moreover, that
evil desire*—*why could ignorance not be added?*—*which may be said to be born
with most people does not flow from the sin of that first parent, but from the
fact that the human race, through repeated acts of sin, contracted a habit of
sinning and corrupted itself, which corruption is poured into posterity by
propagation.* Thus he speaks in *Lectures*, chapter 4, page 13, and has wholly
similar things in chapter 5, page 16. And Ostorodt in *Institutions*, chapter
34, page 281: *We freely concede that in nearly all people there is a great
lust and inclination toward sin*—*which he will not deny concerning ignorance
too*—*not indeed from the first sin, but because people corrupted themselves
through their own sins and malice and then propagate that corruption into
their children by the power of the seed.*

4. He says that under this darkness he comprehends *all impediments to saving
knowledge*. But why will the Socinians not say this too, provided that by
these impediments they understand certain external, adventitious things and
things of that kind, rather than that blindness which is innate, implan-*

[Page 42 | ONB canvas 96 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000096]

*ted, and natural to us from the fall of our first parents? We have not yet seen
Wolzogen admit this blindness.

5. He says that they *fell into this blindness by their own fault.* The
Socinians too will say and freely admit this; indeed they do say it,
understanding by this fault people's actual sins, not Adam's sin. If Wolzogen
held the orthodox position in this question, he could have explained a sound
position distinctly enough. But it was preferable to use general words, lest
he appear to depart from the Socinians.

6. He says that they do not understand the Scriptures because *they do not
read them rightly.* The Socinians too will not deny this.

7. He says that *they do not read rightly because they do not apply a
purified mind.* But not even this will remove every scruple, for one may ask
what he understands by a *purified mind*. He replies on page 12 of *Censure of
the Censure*: *I call that mind purified which is freed from every impediment
to knowledge.* But since it is not yet clear what he understands by these
impediments, the scruple will remain. For a Socinian will readily concede that
people do not read the Scriptures rightly unless they bring a mind empty of
preconceived opinions, recalled and withdrawn from earthly things, and freed
from stubbornness and obstinacy, and so forth. Hear, Wolzogen, the Racovian
Catechism, chapter 3, page 20, where they reply to the question *Whence comes
so great a disagreement concerning the understanding of Holy Scripture?* in
this way: *From the fact that people either read Holy Scripture negligently,
or do not bring a sincere heart freed from every impediment and desire, and so
forth.*

8. Finally, he says that *through their own fault alone they are unable to
apply this purified mind.* But the Socinians too will affirm this,
understanding by *fault*, of course, an *actual* or *personal* fault, which
they acknowledge properly to be their own, not *original* fault.

VI. On page 15 he has these words, which at least sound evil: *Although it is
true in its own sense that nothing was so written by God that, when all the
aids to interpretation which the nature of the passage can bear are applied,
some meaning from God's own mind cannot*

[Page 43 | ONB canvas 97 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000097]

*be drawn from it.* Who does not see him acknowledging that several distinct
and coordinate meanings of the same passage of Scripture exist or can exist?
For that expression, *some meaning*, clearly denotes that several meanings,
even from God's mind, exist or can exist, and that after all the aids to
interpretation have been applied, one of them can be drawn out. This is the
one thing the Paradoxical Essayist desires. But our theologians unanimously
oppose this when disputing against the papists. Yet it is customary for
Wolzogen to desert our theologians, as it is also for his most heterodox Elder
Velthuysen. For the latter treats this subject fully enough at the place cited
above, pages 116–118: *Every truth is also a true meaning of Scripture*, he
says, *because each and every true meaning which is legitimately elicited from
the words of Scripture is a true meaning of the author.—Others express this
doctrine in this way: The words of Scripture actually signify as much as they
can signify. That is, whatever is rightly and legitimately elicited from the
words of Scripture, with all the cautions and rules which ought to be observed
in the interpretation of Scripture having been observed, is true and at the
same time a true meaning of the author.—And by what arguments will the
adversary prove that only one meaning, and indeed the one for which he labors,
must be admitted, if he agrees that another meaning of the same words can also
exist? For if it can exist, and if the words of the text can signify yet more,
by that very same argument they actually signify many things and contain
several meanings rather than a single meaning.—And the words of Scripture
contain either both those meanings or neither, because otherwise they will
signify nothing certain.—Nor could it escape the Holy Spirit that words and
expressions placed in a certain place and manner expressed a twofold meaning.
Therefore either he intended to press that twofold meaning by those words and
expressions, or it must be said—may it never be!—that by those words and
expressions the Holy Spirit willed to lead us into unavoidable error and at
the same time did not deal can-*

[Page 44 | ONB canvas 98 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000098]

*didly with men, since he professes that by the terms of Scripture he wills to
teach people something certain, while all the while playing in ambiguity.* You
see, Wolzogen, what your Elder says. It is not necessary to refute him or you
here; it is enough to have shown that you agree with the Paradoxical Essayist
and desert the Reformed.

In this same place you say that a meaning can be drawn out if all the aids to
interpretation which the nature of the passage can bear are employed. And on
page 19, when you maintain that *there is no doubt that Scripture, considered
as a whole in this general use of its own, will at some time be understood by
someone—even a passage which appears most obscure to us, if it has not already
been understood at some time—or at least that it can be understood if it is
read rightly*, you appear to exclude the Spirit from interpretation. The
reason is that in the first passage you speak only of those things which the
nature of the passage can bear, but the work of the Holy Spirit pertains no
more to the nature of one passage than to that of another, and cannot suitably
be numbered among such aids. Next, it cannot be included in the phrase *if it
is read rightly*. For if the Spirit is included and contributes his work to
drawing out the meaning, it will be understood not merely *sometimes* but
*always*; not merely by *someone* but by *everyone*; and it will not merely
*be capable of being understood* but actually be understood, as everyone of
sound mind will readily concede. Enough now about what you had to say in the
first place concerning Scripture; let us proceed to the other things.

VII. That Wolzogen has badly defended the true and orthodox cause and has
betrayed it rather than upheld it is plain from what he treats in the second
place, on page 20 and following. For that Paradoxical Essayist whom Wolzogen
professes to refute desires this one thing to be granted to him: *that Reason
must be regarded as the norm or rule for interpreting the Scriptures.* This
is the single point he undertakes to prove. And Wolzogen concedes far too much
in this business. To that extent he gives his hand to the adversary and,
deserting the orthodox, goes over to the camp of the Socinians and other ad-*

[Page 45 | ONB canvas 99 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000099]

*versaries. This must now be demonstrated. Therefore Wolzogen's words on page 20
must be considered: *It also contributes to this*, he says, *that since truth
cannot be contrary to truth—for truth is placed in a certain simple unity—a
twofold rule for interpreting Scripture arises from this, the one sought from
reason, the other from Scripture itself, and both most necessary to know.*

The Orthodox distinguish between the *subject, instrument,* and *norm*; that
is, between Reason insofar as it is taken as the *subject*, as the
*instrument*, and finally as the *norm*. When Reason is taken as the
*subject*, it denotes the faculty of the soul which is naturally fitted to
apprehend and conceive objects of this kind. When it is taken as the
*instrument*, it denotes the act of discerning, inferring, and comparing about
and concerning the object. When it is taken as the *norm*, it judges the
object according to its own pleasure and position. Thus the most learned
Hoornbeeck, *Refutation of the Socinians*, book 1, chapter 6, page 97. Now
there is no question about the first or second sense; no one doubts that in
interpreting Scripture Reason is both the *subject* and the *instrument*. But
against the Socinians all the Orthodox deny that Reason is or can be the
*norm*. Yet Wolzogen expressly says here that *one rule for interpreting
Scripture must be sought from Reason*. I do not see how this differs from a
*norm*.

But to make this still clearer, it must be noted that he says in precisely the
same way that one rule is to be sought from Reason as he says that the other
is to be sought from Scripture itself. There is absolutely no expressed or
implied difference. The Orthodox, however, say that Scripture truly is the
norm for interpreting the Scriptures. See Polanus, *Syntagma*, book 1, chapter
45, question 5: *The norm*, he says, *for interpreting Scripture and judging
whether an interpretation of sacred Scripture is true or false is sacred
Scripture itself, which is the voice of God. For every interpretation which
agrees with Scripture is true and from God, while every interpretation which
dis-*

[Page 46 | ONB canvas 100 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000100]

*agrees with it is false, and so forth.* The reason is that *Scripture*, as
the Leiden Professors speak in *Synopsis*, disputation 5, thesis 22, *is its
own Interpreter—or rather, God in and speaking through the Scriptures is the
Interpreter. In clearer and necessary passages he openly indicates his will
to the faithful; in more obscure passages, by comparing them with clearer
ones, he confirms that same will to them more and more.*

Third, Wolzogen teaches that the rule taken from Reason must be used in
precisely the same way as the rule sought from Scripture is used, pages 21 and
23. That is, just as the meaning of one passage which is contrary to another
passage must be rejected, so no position of Scripture which is opposed to
right reason must in any way be believed. This is further confirmed from what
follows on page 21: *Since these things are so*, he says, *no position of the
Scriptures which is opposed to right Reason must in any way be believed. This
has manifold and almost infinite use, both in things considered in themselves
and in a certain consequence of things. Matters are easily brought under this
rule. For if Scripture appears to affirm something about the use of the stars,
the constitution of the earth, or natural things in general which is found to
be contrary to unwilling reason and to the attested judgments of every
person's experience, I believe it to be an act of piety so to explain God
speaking in the Scriptures that he is not contrary to himself speaking in
nature.* How well Wolzogen agrees with the Socinians in this matter is plain
from their words. Ostorodt, *Institutions*, chapter 4, page 30, cited by
Hoornbeeck at the place above, page 89: *It is impossible for the same thing
to be false and true at the same time. Therefore if Reason—that is, talent or
intellect—plainly shows the Trinity of persons in God to be false, how could
it ever enter an intelligent person's mind that it nevertheless could be true
and could be proved by God's word?* The same author speaks with Voidovius in
*Apology against the Honorable States*, page 23, cited by the same writer in
the same place: *Perhaps your Theologians will reply that this must be
believed*—they likewise speak of the Holy Tri-*

[Page 47 | ONB canvas 101 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000101]

*nity—*and must not be probed in this way by the intellect. As though the most
sacred and most holy Religion of Christ were such as contained absurdities
and things conflicting with truly sound reason! May we never think this.* And
Smalcius, cited by the same writer in the same place, against Frantzius,
disputation 4, *On Justification*, page 127: *For there is no particle of the
Christian Religion which does not agree with reason; and an opinion which does
not agree with reason can have no place in Theology.* Ostorodt also disputes
from this foundation against Christ's divine nature in *Institutions*, chapter
6, page 43, reported by the same writer on page 39: *But we say*, he says,
*that our reason bears witness to us, and does so as evidently and clearly as
the sun shines at midday, that for two natures to be found in Christ is
impossible and therefore false.* And Smalcius, disputation 7, *On the Last
Judgment*, page 407, cited by Hoornbeeck at the place above, page 114: *We
indeed hold that Philosophy ought not to be equated with Theology, but in turn
we consider that Theology false which affirms something contrary to true
Philosophy.*

Perhaps Wolzogen will say that he sufficiently explains himself on pages
23–24 of *Censure of the Censure*. But he ought to have remembered that *no
one is the best Interpreter of his own words.* Yet let us see what he says:
*The question still is*, he says, *what must be regarded as Scripture, because
the question is instituted concerning the meaning of Scripture; for nothing
must be regarded as Scripture which is not established to be the meaning of
Scripture. In order that this may be established, we employ reason and the
other aids to interpretation. If those aids produce certain and indubitable
truth for us, we prefer them not to Scripture but to that which merely appears
to be in Scripture as far as the wording goes. Therefore I pronounce nothing
affirmatively for Reason more than for Scripture, but employ certain reason so
that I may render certain to myself a doubtful meaning of Scripture, if it is
doubtful, in those matters which permit rational examination. When I am now
certain that Scripture says something, I bid reason be gone, which I use only
to discern whether it says something; once I hold that, there is no need of*

[Page 48 | ONB canvas 102 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000102]

*Reason.* Reply. That he employs Reason in a way other than as an aid is plain
from his own words cited above and will become still plainer from those cited
below. As for the rest, I say only that the Socinians would say the same
things and have already said them. For Schlichting says the following against
Meisner, *On the Most Holy Trinity*; see Hoornbeeck at the place cited above,
pages 90–91: *Either the matter concerns things recorded and expressed in the
clearest words in Scripture, so that no one of sound mind could doubt their
meaning, or it concerns obscure things about which one may deservedly doubt
whether they are read in Scripture as signified or not signified. If the
former are in question, we likewise concede this assertion, especially
because it is most certain that Scripture contains nothing repugnant to
manifest reason or involving a true contradiction. But if obscure things are
in question, anyone sees that the matter cannot be settled without reason;
yet reason must not be employed as though it could be opposed to something
which Scripture affirms or denies, but only to declare whether Scripture
contains something.* And a little later: *For we do not have recourse to
reason whenever Scripture openly testifies the contrary, but whenever the
mind of Scripture is in doubt.* Later: *The office of judging the faith does
not belong to Reason if the mind of Scripture is already established; but
when it is doubted whether Scripture has defined the matter in this way or
not, to wish to reject reason is nothing other than to extinguish the light
in darkness and, besides, tear out one's own eyes.* And on page 39: *We never
flee to the judgment of reason whenever the mind of Scripture is established.
—But only when there is doubt either about the meaning of Scripture or about
the truth of some dogma outside Scripture. Thus we are content with Scripture
alone, since it is supreme reason and truth itself, but in those matters which
are defined in Scripture. For in those matters which are not defined,*

[Page 49 | ONB canvas 103 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000103]

*or in which it is doubtful whether they have been defined, to what else shall
we flee but right and sound reason?* Who now sees Wolzogen saying anything
different from what the Socinians said?

It is true that Scripture says nothing which is repugnant to right reason by
a manifest contradiction; but I judge it rash to regard *Reason as the norm*.
Scripture says nothing which conflicts by manifest contradiction with our
sound senses; is it therefore proper also to attribute the prerogative of a
norm to the senses? And when the meaning of Scripture is sought, must we have
recourse to the senses no less than to Scripture? We wish Reason to be
employed as an instrument and means for investigating the true meaning, but
we cannot admit it as the norm or rule. Although there is no conflict or
manifest contradiction between Scripture and sound reason, and all things
proposed in the Scriptures agree with Reason, nevertheless, if they sometimes
appear to conflict, we judge it preferable for reason to be drawn before
Scripture's tribunal and submit itself to Scripture's decision than for
Scripture to conform itself to Reason. Reason must be reconciled with
Scripture; Scripture must not be examined according to Reason. In doubts we
must have recourse to Scripture, not reason, which can deceive and often does.
Nor must it ever be trusted in matters of faith, since it nowhere now appears
pure, right, and sound, but is corrupted by the fall and corrupted more and
more each day. Insofar as it is now defective, it not only cannot understand
sacred things, John 3:7, 12; 1 Corinthians 2:7, 9, 14, but even opposes and
contradicts them, Romans 8:7; Galatians 5:7.

Wolzogen, however, regards reason as the norm and wishes it to be heard as the
norm according to which a decision about the meaning of Scripture must be
made, so that Scripture must be explained according to reason as an argument,
or a pronouncement made about its meaning. The celebrated

[Page 50 | ONB canvas 104 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000104]

*Hoornbeeck, in Refutation of Socinianism*, book 1, chapter 6, page 34,
demonstrates that the Remonstrants also willed this. If it has not been sufficiently
demonstrated from what has been said that Wolzogen wills this, consider what
he has on pages 67–69, where he expresses himself clearly enough: *We do not
wish here*, he says, *to assert what they call the instrumental use of Reason,
which we presuppose.* What, then? *We seek a leader for ministering reason.*
Let us therefore hear whether he ascribes this prerogative of leadership to
Scripture, or the Holy Spirit, or Reason. He adds: *Since, however, we said
that nothing is contained in the Scriptures which is not true, while many
truths are such that they do not escape the light of reason, it follows that
Reason is both a leader and often a minister.* There is no need for me to say
anything here about the emptiness of this consequence, than which a more
futile one can scarcely be imagined. From his own words I demonstrate only
that in interpreting sacred literature he regards Reason as the Norm. For he
judges that some use other than the instrumental use must be ascribed to it
and expressly says that it is often a *leader*. Indeed, he further illustrates
this in what follows, for he adds: *And this in two ways. First, so that it
recall all things—I make absolutely no exception—all things of whatever kind
to examination.—Second, it serves in proving things, but only those which
either have a constant truth in nature or a consequence flowing from grace.
For the former are things of reason. Although the latter have their source in
revelation, whatever flows from it must likewise render its acknowledgment to
reason. Therefore here too one may use reason to determine the ambiguous
meaning of the Scriptures.* Who can now hesitate? Or who can retain any doubt
about Wolzogen's meaning and position? For in order to investigate the meaning
of Scripture, he wishes Reason to have not merely the use of an instrument,
but that of a *leader* or *judge* which recalls all things, with absolutely
nothing excepted, to examination. What could be said more clearly? Is Reason
not now his Norm, so that one must pronounce upon the meaning of Scripture
according to Reason's position? It is one thing

[Page 51 | ONB canvas 105 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000105]

to demonstrate that what is said in Scripture in no way contradicts right Reason. It
is a far different thing, when the meaning of Scripture is sought, first to
inquire what reason says and what agrees or does not agree with reason, to
summon the meaning of the statements for rational examination, and then to
pronounce upon the true meaning according to reason's position. To say this
is plainly to betray the cause before everyone and defect to the Socinians.

Moreover, although in what follows he wishes to speak a little more
cautiously, he nevertheless sufficiently indicates the spirit by which he is
driven. For on page 69 he says: *Second, one must not suppose that all things
pertain to reason's jurisdiction.* Therefore some things belong most properly
to its jurisdiction, and with respect to them one must pronounce upon the
meaning of Scripture according to reason as the norm, argument, or rather the
judge which exercises jurisdiction and pronounces sentence. But what must be
said about those things which are not subject to Reason's command—namely,
things which depend upon God's mere will or contain the deed of those who
could act in one way or another, as he speaks? *Here*, he says, *reason has
nothing to decide, except that it both can and must take care again and again
not to suppose that something is narrated as true which conflicts with Reason
by an open falsehood.* Yet as is plain, even in these matters Reason's
sentence must be heard before the true meaning of Scripture is received:
namely, whether the meaning in question conflicts with Reason or not. But what
if Reason is deceived, as it deceives the Socinians? Must that meaning be
regarded as not true? Who but a betrayer of the cause would wish to say this?
Then what does Reason accomplish in those matters which are removed from its
investigation? *Reason accomplishes nothing else here*, he says, *than to show
as probable the thing which it does not understand.* Therefore reason must be
heard first even in these matters, and unless it judges that the meaning
contains probability, the meaning must be rejected. What else do the
Socinians desire or assert than that the mys-*

[Page 52 | ONB canvas 106 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000106]

*teries of the Trinity, Incarnation, and Satisfaction must be rejected because
their Reason judges that they are not even probable? He concludes this matter
as follows on page 70: *Within these limits we employ Reason in the
interpretation of the Scriptures. It is not that the nature of interpretation,
as interpretation, bears any principal use of reason.* Yet above he had
conceded it another use than the instrumental; indeed, the Use of a Leader
which recalls all things to examination, and the jurisdiction of a *judge*
which it exercises at least in certain things; and we have now shown that,
according to him, it has in other matters more than an instrumental use. What
follows further confirms this: *But because*, he says, *before all other
writings, the divine Writers have this singular constitution, that they
contain pure truth, concerning which reason too can judge and thus often
determine whether some meaning agrees or does not agree with the words.*
Therefore Reason not only can judge divine matters, in which, according to
Scripture's testimony, it often gropes blindly, but can also determine whether
a meaning agrees with the words. Therefore one must stand, at least often, by
Reason's sentence concerning the meaning of Scripture. Therefore, in order to
find the true meaning of Scripture, we must use Reason not only as an
instrument but also as a norm or judge which determines what meaning agrees
with the words and what meaning does not. Is this not to make Reason the
Interpreter of Scripture? Is this not to betray the cause? This was what had
to be demonstrated. But more about these things later in their proper place.

Here, however, it must be seen further what Wolzogen says and how he defends
his statements. He lays as a foundation that maxim of Descartes: *Whatever I
perceive clearly and distinctly is true.* For he speaks as follows: *For if we
perceive something clearly and distinctly by Reason, I have no doubt that one
may affirm that it possesses truth, and it is more certain than certainty
itself that this truth, shining with a certain native light of its own and
inscribed upon minds, no contrary light of truth can*

[Page 53 | ONB canvas 107 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000107]

*extinguish it.* As for that philosophical maxim, I leave the matter to
Philosophers, and it is not necessary for me to say anything about it. However
Wolzogen regards it as true, I see it refuted by others with most solid arguments which I do not
judge Wolzogen equal to answering: namely, by Daniel Voetius in disputations
*On Evidence and Certitude*, and by Vogelsang in *Just Indignation*, page 138
and following, where he also scattered all Velthuysen's futile arguments by
which the latter endeavors to confirm this proposition. It will therefore
suffice to note these few things.

1. Does Wolzogen believe that we apprehend or perceive nothing, or can
perceive nothing, otherwise than it really is? Will he say that our judgment
is infallible in perceiving every matter? How will he prove this? Does daily
experience not contradict it? If he says that he speaks of clear and distinct
perception, I would again, 1., like him to be asked to say whether he thinks
that we do not often represent to ourselves things which are not really so,
or can represent clearly and distinctly fictitious things—things not involving
a contradiction. If he denies this, he contradicts experience. If he concedes
it, this axiom will be false.

3. If he says that the meaning of his maxim is this: Whatever I clearly and
distinctly perceive to be true is true, let him explain to me what it is to
perceive clearly and distinctly that something is true. Is it to perceive the
thing as it is? Then this proposition is certainly most true: Whatever I
clearly and distinctly perceive as it is, really is as it is clearly and
distinctly perceived by me. But how ridiculous! For from this clear and
distinct perception you wish to establish that what is so perceived is true.
And you have not yet proved, nor can you prove, that we perceive whatever we
perceive as it is.

4. If you say that unless a thing is perceived as it is, it is not perceived
clearly and distinctly, then I shall ask: When will I know that I clearly and
distinctly perceive a thing? And can I not suppose that I clearly and
distinctly perceive things which I do not so

[Page 54 | ONB canvas 108 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000108]

perceive? He will not deny this latter point. What rule, then, will he
prescribe for us by which we can know with certainty when our concepts agree
with the thing itself? He must propose this certain and infallible rule to us
distinctly and clearly; otherwise this axiom will be of absolutely no value.
For he thinks that he clearly and distinctly perceives this axiom to be true,
while others think that they perceive no less clearly and distinctly that
this axiom is false. Therefore either both will be true, which is absurd, or
this maxim will be false. The Socinians think that they clearly and distinctly
perceive many things which you will nevertheless affirm beyond doubt to be
false. I shall therefore ask what you would reply to refute the Socinians. For
they assert that by reason they perceive clearly and distinctly many things
which are opposed by the things we hold with certain faith. Will the things
we believe therefore be false? Or must it be said that the truth which they
say they possess from clear and distinct perception, and which they assert
shines by its own native light and is deeply inscribed upon minds, cannot be
extinguished by the contrary light of divine truth clearly and distinctly
revealed in the Scriptures? Who but a Socinian would say this?

Moreover, although it is true that Scripture affirms nothing opposed to right
reason, we nevertheless judge this foundation slippery, and no sufficiently
solid rule for interpreting the Scriptures can be sought from it, because
that right reason has not yet been established. Thus it happens that the rule
is more uncertain and obscure than the thing ruled. The reason is that
everyone thinks that he possesses right reason and perceives clearly and
distinctly. And thus what the Socinians desire is conceded. For Smalcius,
against Frantzius in his preface, says the same as Wolzogen says here: *No one
ought to doubt that whatever things are repugnant to reason are more certainly
than certain false, for a small light cannot in any way be contrary to a
great one.* Wolzogen replies on page 19 of *Censure of the Censure*: *We
reprove the Socinians, not because they admit nothing which*

[Page 55 | ONB canvas 109 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000109]

*is overturned by right reason, but because they maintain many things contrary
to reason which are by no means such.* Reply. Yet the Socinians say that they
clearly and distinctly perceive the things they perceive. And you say that
whatever we clearly and distinctly perceive is true. According to you,
therefore, it must be said that the Socinians maintain nothing contrary to
reason which is not such; or some fixed rule must be given by which we can
infallibly judge when our reason judges rightly and when it does not, or when
we do not err in perceiving.

*We*, Wolzogen continues, *divide into two classes the things which they join
in one, and we judge that one must distinguish again and again between things
which exceed our capacity and other things which we grasp to be impossible.*
Reply. Indeed, the Socinians too divide into two classes the things you think
they join in one. Let Smalcius be heard against Frantzius, disputation 3, *On
the Sacraments*, page 273: *We indeed freely admit that there are very many
things in the Christian Religion which surpass reason and nevertheless must
necessarily be believed for this very reason: that however much they surpass
reason, they nevertheless stand delivered in sacred literature and are most
agreeable to our reason, which they surpass.* And in the same place: *For
something can surpass reason and nevertheless please it. Nearly all the
things contained in the Religion, especially the Christian Religion, are of
this kind.* And in disputation 8, *On Free Choice*, page 421: *We know that
Religion is above reason, but no true religion, nor even the least particle of
it, can be contrary to Reason.* He has similar things in disputation 7, *On
the Last Judgment*, page 407. See Hoornbeeck, *Refutation of Socinianism*,
book 1, chapter 6, page 114. What will Wolzogen now say? Does he not see what
a slippery foundation he lays here and how much he gratifies the Socinians?
Does he not recall that our Reason is now corrupt? He replies on pages 20–21
of *Censure of the Censure*: *Although natural reason is so corrupt that it
cannot grasp the things which are of God's Spirit, it can nevertheless grasp
whether something conflicts by a manifest contradiction. No singular*

[Page 56 | ONB canvas 110 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000110]

*grace of the Spirit is needed for me to perceive mathematical disciplines—
indeed, in matters of faith I see sufficiently from myself whether they
contain anything contrary to reason.* Reply. 1. Do the Socinians seek anything
else than that we concede that those things are not so which their reason,
such as it is, says conflict by manifest contradiction? 2. Can our reason,
without the singular grace of the Spirit, attain the same certainty in
matters of faith which it can attain in mathematical disciplines? Do the
Socinians not say that in matters of faith they see sufficiently whether
things contain anything contrary to their reason? Resting upon this one
foundation, do they not reject the truest dogmas of Religion?

*But I deny*, Wolzogen says in the same place, *that in this question I treat
corrupt reason, for I say in express words that the Spirit of God can cause us
to use right reason.* Reply. 1. Therefore you speak about a nonexistent thing,
for all our reason which now exists is corrupt. 2. Who denies that the Spirit
can cause, and sometimes does cause, us to use reason rightly? But do you think
the Spirit causes all who also think that they perceive clearly and distinctly
to use reason rightly when they judge that certain mysteries revealed by the
Scriptures contradict reason? You have therefore not yet untied the knot.
