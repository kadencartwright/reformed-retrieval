# If One Spouse Contracts Elephantiasis

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
[`latin.txt`](latin.txt). The printed-page markers correspond to the 1871
*Calvini Opera* witness.

**[CO X/1, p. 241]**

Before your letter was delivered to us, we hope that our last letter reached
you. The matter about which you now ask is very perplexing. For illness,
whatever it may be, is not a just cause for divorce. But as long as the
marriage remains firm,

**[CO X/1, p. 242]**

each spouse appears to be bound to the other. Paul exhorts both of them to
render the affection that is due—the husband to his wife and the wife to her
husband (1 Corinthians 7:3). As for the marriage bond, we determine that it is
indissoluble, although the wife may be separated from the marriage bed.

The question then follows: if the husband is struck by elephantiasis, commonly
called leprosy, does it release the woman from the obligation of sexual
intercourse? They say that people of this class, if afflicted with itching,
are extremely lustful. He will therefore claim that he needs his wife. But if
a man with elephantiasis possessed any feeling of humanity, he would first
spare his wife and children, and then take thought for the human race, lest
the contagion spread farther. In this he is already found to perform the duty
neither of a husband nor of a father, and even in some measure to be an enemy
of the public good. Moreover, unless struck by brute insensibility, just as he
is repellent to everyone else, so by his own modesty he would voluntarily
seclude himself as though buried.

Certainly, lest we be cruel, we do not dare bind the woman by this necessity:
that she must share dwelling and bed with a man who forgets every law of
nature. Rather, we judge that she should be permitted, after judicial inquiry,
to live as a widow. Meanwhile, however, let her not cease to attend her husband
with whatever services she can, provided that he does not demand from her what
is almost monstrous.

Farewell, excellent and upright brothers. May the Lord always be present with
you, protect you, and govern you by his Spirit. Geneva, the fourth day before
the Nones of December 1561 [2 December 1561].

## Translation notes

1. Early modern *elephantiasis* and the popular label *lepra* did not map
   neatly onto modern diagnostic categories. The translation retains both
   historical terms rather than diagnosing the unnamed husband retrospectively.
2. The assertions about contagion, itching, sexual desire, and the patient's
   obligations reproduce the document's sixteenth-century medical and moral
   assumptions. They are not endorsed as modern medical judgments.
3. *A thoro separetur* means separation from the marital bed while the marriage
   bond remains intact; the response consequently permits neither divorce nor
   remarriage.
4. *Cognitione iudicum interposita* requires an intervening judicial inquiry or
   adjudication. The permission is not framed as a unilateral private decision.
5. The Nones fall on 5 December, so *4 Nonas Decembris* is 2 December by
   inclusive Roman reckoning.
