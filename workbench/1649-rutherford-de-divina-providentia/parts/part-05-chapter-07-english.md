# CHAPTER VII

## 1. Is permission the bare non-violation or non-necessitation of the will, as
the Jesuits Pennottus, Pesantius, Ruiz, and the Arminians maintain? 2. Do God's
permission and prevention of sin occur only by way of persuasion?

### I.

Seven questions concerning permission must be treated by me. 1. Is permission
the mere non-violation of the will? 2. Do permission and prevention occur in an
entirely persuasive manner? These two are treated in this chapter. 3. Does sin
follow from permission necessarily and by an inferential connection? 4. Is
permission absolute, or is it conditional, resting upon an inclination of the
will which wishes to permit before permission is completed by God in second
act? 5. Is the permission of sin an effect of reprobation intended by God? 6.
Is God's permitting will a pure non-willing of the permitted thing, and does it
deny every positive act of God's will insofar as his will terminates upon the
permitted thing? From this there will remain the principal question in this
disputation: 7. Is God's permission such an act of God's active providence
concerning sin that by it God wills in some manner that sin occur while he
permits it—that is, wills it permissively?

[Marginal summary: Seven questions concerning permission to be discussed.]

2. As the Jesuits Gabriel Pennottus, Diego Ruiz, Aegidius de Coninck, Alexander
Pesantius, and also the Arminians maintain, permission is only the abandonment
or release of the will to its own indifference and to its connatural bent and
disposition for acting, by which it is allowed to act according to its own
nature. It is therefore only the non-violation, non-constraint, and
non-predetermination of the free will, as Pennottus and Ruiz teach; or, as
Pesantius puts it,

[Marginal summary: For the Jesuits, permission is the mere exercise of free
choice.]

[Marginal references: Pennottus, *Defense of Human Liberty*, book 4, chapter 8,
figure 4; Ruiz on part 1, disputation 39, section 2, figure [number cropped in
the witness].]

[Chapter VII; printed page 74; PDF page 126]

“the non-impossibilitation of sinning. The Remonstrants maintain that true
permission requires not only that the power be free, but also that its exercise
be free and indifferent toward opposites, or that it remain immune from every
internal and external necessity.” By contrast, we teach that permission
formally includes something more than the bare non-violation of the second
cause, which I demonstrate as follows.

[Marginal references: Pesantius on part 1, question 19, article 8, disputation
2; Coninck, volume *On Supernatural Acts*, book 1, disputation 3, doubt 16,
figure 208; the Remonstrants' *Confession*, chapter 6, *On Providence*, thesis
6.]

1. An account of permission which applies to good and supernatural acts just as
much as to evil ones must not be tolerated. But non-violation and freedom from
violent constraint apply to good and supernatural acts just as much as to evil
ones. Therefore such an account of permission must not be tolerated. The major
premise is certain, because Scripture restricts permission to sinful acts. The
minor is equally certain. For God does not violate the will toward good acts,
and it is absurd for anyone to say that God permits Christ to sin and Peter to
fall into apostasy no differently from Hymenaeus and Philetus. For neither
Christ's will nor the saints' will is recalled from sins by violation; if it
were, in not sinning they would not obey God, since no violent action is
obedience. It is also absurd to say that God permits Christ to pray or preach,
or permits the apostles to preach, believe, and patiently suffer persecutions,
and that all these things occur by God's permission.

[Marginal references: Deuteronomy 18:14; Leviticus 19:17; Genesis 20:6; Psalm
105:14; 1 Corinthians 10:13; Job 31:30; Acts 13:18; Acts 14:16.]

2. That which is common to every second cause is not a permission proper to the
free will, as is evident. But this non-violation extends to every second cause,
both natural and free. For God, whose providence—as Dionysius says—“preserves
nature,” does not violate even natural causes. In concurring with the sun as it
illuminates, fire as it heats, and the wolf as it devours a sheep, he does not
inflict force but allows such causes to act in the manner most connatural to
them. Yet God permits fire to heat in a different way from that in which he
permits the Sabeans to afflict Job and Pharaoh to oppress God's people with
servitude.

[Marginal summary: Dionysius. It is proved by many arguments that permission is
not the bare non-violation of liberty.]

3. In Scripture, permission is opposed to exhortation to repentance through
the preaching of the gospel (Acts 17:30; Acts 14:16). But permission understood
as the non-violation of the free will is not opposed to exhortation to
repentance. For God does not violate fire when it burns. On this account he
would permit fire to heat by not exhorting it to burn, which is ridiculous.

4. The saints pray that God would not lead them into temptation—that is, that he
would not

[Chapter VII; printed page 75; PDF page 127]

permit them to sin. But if permission is merely the release of the will to its
own nature and connatural manner of acting, then the saints would pray that God
destroy their natural liberty and compel and violate their will. See Dr.
Binnes. Such prayers, however, would be absurd and blasphemous.

[Marginal reference: Binnes, volume 1, question 19, article 23.]

5. To release a free creature in its acts from the dominion of the Creator and
his providence is not the permission proper to providence as it rules over free
causes and their acts. But to release the will to its own indifference “so that
it acts or does not act absolutely” is to release the creature from the dominion
of the Creator and his providence, as I proved above. Therefore such a release
and dismissal of the will is not permission, and so forth.

6. Permission in Scripture is often the punishment of sin. But allowing the
will to use its indifference toward acting or not acting in a manner connatural
to its own nature is not a punishment, because it is a benefit of nature. This
non-violation therefore does not belong to every permission and so is not of the
essence of permission.

But perhaps they would object as follows: “Permission taken physically and in
first act is essentially such a release of the will; only taken morally and in
second act, whenever sin follows from such release, is it the punishment of
sin. Hence it is not correctly said that Christ, the apostles, and the saints,
whenever they pray, believe, hope in God, and love him, are permitted by God to
sin. For sin does not always follow from permission in first act, that is, from
the release of the will which is left to its own connatural manner of acting.”

I answer: 1. Yet it is a serious difficulty for our opponents that in Adam's
first fall both kinds of permission are found before any demerit of Adam.

2. On this account, in the case of devils soliciting people to sexual crimes,
poisonings, parricides, and every wicked deed, there would be the same
permission—namely, non-violation of the will, since the devils are compelled by
no violence to tempt—as there is in Christ and in those confirmed in grace in
all supernatural acts. For in both cases there is our opponents'
non-violation, and they will never escape this absurdity.

7. Permission is a positive act of the divine will, for God knowingly and
willingly permits sin. The Jesuit release of the will, however, is bare
nonaction and a merely negative act.

3. From this the reader may easily see where the permission which our opponents
assign to God's providence tends. For 1. God did nothing in the good angels by
which they stood and were confirmed in grace

[Chapter VII; printed page 76; PDF page 128]

that he did not do in the apostate angels. For he violated the free choice of
neither group, and apostasy from God was equally free to both the evil and the
good angels.

[Marginal summary: The kind of permission which our opponents fabricate for
God.]

2. On this account, human apostasy and the necessity of the incarnation would
come about by sheer chance even with respect to God. For God contributed as
much causality to man's non-fall as, indeed, to his fall and to the occasion for
displaying avenging justice and sparing mercy.

3. God did nothing of himself. Instead, he was only moved by Adam's fall—which,
on our opponents' account, he neither willed nor ever intended—both to form the
decree to send a Savior and actually to execute that method of saving him.

4. Unless God had violated the will of Adam and Eve, he could not have procured
their perseverance in obedience. Thus it was impossible and contradictory for
God to procure Adam's obedience and holy abstinence from the fall, since Adam
wished to exercise his connatural manner of acting.

5. Christ the God-man, all the elect and those confirmed in grace, and those who
most holily watch over the paths of obedience are permitted by God to fall
finally and become apostates in the same manner as the devils and our first
parents were permitted to fall away from God. On the part of divine providence,
they are equally exposed to temptation—unless we make the exception that God
grants Christ and the elect certain impediments to a final fall. Yet if the
created free choice so wills, those impediments are of themselves just as
ineffective as all the impediments placed by God before the free will of the
devils and our first parents as things foreseen to be fragile and breakable. O
slippery fortune!

4. The Jesuits' principal argument is this: “God,” Ruiz says, “left man in the
hand of his own counsel. That is, he permitted him; he did not shut him in a
prison or constrain him with bonds so that he could not move himself wherever
he willed. He did not impose a necessity of acting and remaining in one
operation, nor consign him to the impossibility of passing to another
operation. — For in the present matter, to leave is the same as to allow and
permit. — The preposition *in* generally signifies an indifference of power so
that it may act and not act. Job 6: ‘There is no help for me in me.’ Romans 1:
‘What is ready in me.’ — ‘In the hand’ signifies indifference released from
necessity and impossibility. Ecclesiasticus 33: ‘Like clay in the potter's hand,
to fashion it,’ and so forth. Psalm 30: ‘My lots are in your hands.’ — Job 1:
‘Behold, all that he has is in your hand.’”

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On Knowledge and Ideas* on part 1, questions 14–18,
disputation 39, section 2, figures 5–8, and so forth.]

[Chapter VII; printed page 77; PDF page 129]

“Satan received from God not only the will's radical power and spontaneous
propensity, but also one released from the bonds of necessity or impossibility.
The words ‘of his own counsel’ prove the same point—for an action determined by
another cannot fall under counsel, and so forth. Others argue as follows. Acts
24: Felix ordered that Paul be given liberty and that none of his associates be
prevented from ministering to him or visiting him. To release the will,
therefore, is to remove the bonds by which it is determinately tied to one thing
so that it cannot do what it wills. Thus Paul in bonds could not move himself
here or there, and when he was shut in prison his friends could not visit or
refrain from visiting him at their pleasure.”

I answer: 1. What is inferred from a false interpretation of the passage is
denied. To leave is indeed to permit and to release the first man from the bonds
of efficacious grace which predetermines him to free obedience. But 1. Adam was
not released from the dominion of providence; otherwise God would not have been
Lord over Adam's free acts of being unwilling and willing, which is blasphemous.

2. The preposition *in* does not signify indifference to acting or not acting.
Concerning the passage in Job 6, I say that if Job possessed any help, it would
have been determined to deliver the wretched man from the miseries and torments
of both mind and body—as though the wretched man, had he possessed any strength,
would have been indifferent toward freeing himself! Concerning Romans 1:
whatever grace or strength Paul possessed for evangelizing the Romans was not
indifferent toward preaching or not preaching, for grace is not indifferent
toward sinning or not sinning. Concerning the apocryphal Ecclesiasticus 33:13:
the thing “in the potter's hand” is indeed in his power, but providence always
reigns. A human being, however—for every analogy limps—is subject to God's
absolute dominion in a very different way. Concerning the first passage in Job:
it favors us, for the power granted by God to Satan was not indifferent toward
afflicting or not afflicting Job. On the Jesuits' account, the devil would thus
be moved by an incongruous moral motion to pity God's servants. On the
Arminians' account, the power of harming among demons is not subject to free
choice, for if they are to be believed, the devils no longer act freely.

[Marginal summary: The permission attributed in Scripture to man and the devil
is not an indifference of the free will released from God's predetermining
providence, by which they can sin or not sin, as the Arminians maintain.]

2. This power was determined by Satan's malice toward God's servant; by the
denial of efficacious grace through which an obstacle would be placed before
the saints against the devils who were about to harm them; and by God's wise
decree, by which he had resolved to test his servant's faith and patience and
finally to crown them.

[Chapter VII; printed page 78; PDF page 130]

Finally, the Jesuit had to confess that the principle in Satan which is
*autexousion*, self-determining, was free and indifferent toward tormenting or
not tormenting Job. Nor does the word “counsel” exclude every determination in
relation to the first cause. The soldiers truly deliberated whether they would
break Christ's legs, although it was impossible for them to be broken. I say
that predetermining providence is not like Paul's bonds and prison. Paul was
bound with chains and shut in prison against his will; his friends were
involuntarily kept from visiting him, hindered by the prison's closed doors.
But God's predetermination binds the will with most gentle and generous bonds
to that one of the opposites to which the will itself in fact binds itself. The
analogy would prove something if predetermination bound one who resisted and
struggled against it, just as the bonds would have kept Paul entangled and
bound—when he wished to be released and was unable to free himself—had he been
bound.

5. The second question concerns the manner of permission. Arminius defines it
as follows: “Permission,” he says, “is the suspension of all impediments which
God knows would prevent sin if they were applied.” But for Arminius all
permission is the mere suspension of moral persuasions. Nor, on Arminius's
account, does God prevent sins in any other way than by persuasions. This is
altogether absurd.

[Marginal reference: The Jesuits; Arminius, *Public Disputation* 10, thesis 4.]

1. Arminius maintains that one prevention of sin is sufficient and another is
efficacious. Now, when a sufficient but nonefficacious impediment is given, sin
need not occur; indeed, it is necessary that sin not occur. From this it
follows that whenever God sufficiently prevents sin, it is necessary that sin
then be efficaciously and necessarily procured; and whenever God sufficiently
persuades toward obedience, he efficaciously procures rebellion and
disobedience. The consequence is proved: if an efficacious prevention of sin
does not follow from a sufficient prevention—and certainly it does not, for
then a sufficient prevention or persuasion would be efficacious—a
nonefficacious prevention and nonefficacious persuasion must follow, since
either form or privation must be present in a capable subject. (This argument
also militates against resistible prevention.) And if a nonefficacious
prevention of sin follows, then only a sufficient prevention of sin follows;
but a merely sufficient prevention of sin necessarily entails the efficacious
existence of sin. Therefore, whenever God prevents rebellion by a sufficient
persuasion against rebellion, then

[Marginal summary: The prevention of sin which the Arminians devise is an
efficacious procurement that sin occur.]

[Chapter VII; printed page 79; PDF page 131]

he efficaciously procured Pharaoh's rebellion.

2. If God prevents sin only by persuasions, he acts in this matter with no
greater power or stronger dominion than Cicero or Demosthenes. Nor will such an
act of providence be more excellent, with respect to the species of the act,
than the persuasion of human beings or angels. Grant that God's persuasion is
more infallible and certain than human persuasion because of the infinite
knowledge of God who persuades. Nevertheless, that knowledge contributes
nothing causal to the persuasion. It follows only that God's persuasion is more
excellent than human persuasion with respect to some accident, and greater and
less do not vary a species.

[Marginal summary: The Arminians' merely persuasive prevention is routed.]

3. One who prevents sin in such a way that the futurition of sin is certain
once that prevention has been given permits sin to occur just as much as he
prevents it from occurring. In the same act God would simultaneously be both
the permitter and preventer of sin. But if God prevents sin only persuasively,
he permits sin to occur just as much as he prevents it, because no persuasion
infallibly establishes that sin will not be future, just as none establishes
that it will be. Since the permission of sin and the prevention of that same
sin are opposed to one another, however, it is absurd for God to be the
preventer and permitter of sin in the same act.

4. If God prevents sin by persuasions alone, there is no reason for anyone to
thank God for abstinence from sin rather than non-abstinence. For once all God's
prevention has been given, one who is hindered from sinning by persuasion alone
can both sin and not sin. But this is absurd. Nor can anyone pray with any
confidence in God that he be divinely delivered from committing sin, because
God prevents sin only by a persuasion which anyone can easily resist.

5. According to Arminius, Vorstius, and Corvinus, God preaches the Word to
people who are already hardened in order that their punishment may be all the
more just. But if he prevents sin only persuasively, then by the Word he says
that he wills sin to be prevented—namely, by those persuasions. And because he
uses those persuasions so that the stubborn, on hearing other things, may come
under heavier judgment, in that same act he also intends the stubbornness of
the obstinate to arise efficaciously. Thus in the same act he intends both that
sin occur and that it not occur: that it occur so that the punishment of the
rebellious may be more just, and that it not occur because he persuasively
prevents sin from occurring. In this way there will be contradictory wills and
hypocrisies in God. The Jesuits' position which posits resistible permission is
no less objectionable: a permission, that is, which the human will can frustrate
and rob of its end.

[Marginal references: Arminius, *Against Perkins*, p. 66; Vorstius, *Friendly
Conference* with Piscator, p. 36; Corvinus against Molinaeus, chapter 4,
question 4.]

[Marginal summary: Arminian permission posits contrary wills in God.]
