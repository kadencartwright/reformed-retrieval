QUESTION XI.

THE LIMBO OF THE FATHERS.

*Were the fathers of the Old Testament immediately received into heaven with
respect to their souls after death? Or were they cast down into limbo? The
former is affirmed and the latter denied against the Roman Catholics.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. This question lies between us and the Roman Catholics. In order to defend
their hypothesis concerning the imperfection of the Old Testament more
easily, they maintain that the fathers who lived under it were not immediately
admitted into heaven, but were detained in *limbo* until Christ’s ascension.
By the name *limbo* they understand the upper part of hell, as it were its
edge. The metaphor is taken from a garment, whose edge or band surrounding
its outermost part is called the *limbus*, or border, as in Virgil, *Aeneid*
4:

*She was wrapped in a Sidonian cloak with painted border.*

II. The Roman Catholics make a fourfold *hell*. The *first* they assign to
the eternal punishment of loss and of sense belonging to the ungodly, which
is called hell among them κατ᾽ ἐξοχὴν, *preeminently*. The *second* they make
the temporal punishment of loss and of sense, which they call *purgatory*.
They have imposed the common name limbo on two others. They maintain that one
is eternal, for the mere punishment of loss belonging to infants who die
before baptism, which they call the *limbo of infants*. The other is for the
temporal punishment of loss belonging to the fathers who died before
Christ’s death, which they call the *limbo of the fathers*.

Thus Altenstaig says at this term, “According to Gerson, part 2, in the
exposition of the fifth article of the faith, limbo is the upper part of hell,
in which those who were to be saved were detained until the debt of the first
parent should be paid through Christ’s death.” See Thomas on *Sentences* 4,
distinction 45, question 2, article 2, reply 2; Bellarmine, *Controversies* 2,
book 4, chapter 11.

III. The orthodox, on the contrary, assert that immediately after death the
fathers were admitted into heaven with respect to their souls, where they
enjoy happiness and are free from both kinds of punishment, that of loss and
that of sense.

[Marginal heading: Arguments against limbo; 1. From the covenant formula;]

IV. The reasons are as follows. First, the formula of the covenant of grace
under which the fathers lived does not permit them to be cast down into limbo,
but requires that they were admitted into heaven. For since God promised to
be their God not only for a time but forever, and since he is not the God of
the dead but of the living, the fathers must continue to live after death in
at least some part of themselves, namely, with respect to the soul. They must
live not just any life, but a blessed and heavenly one, which is necessarily
included in this promise, as was proved before in Question 2.

Christ expressly teaches this in Luke 20:38 when, speaking of the dead
fathers, he says πάντες αὐτῷ ζῶσι, *they all live to him*. They live not only
in God’s mind and power because he can call them back to life and will truly
do so according to his decree. Rather, they themselves live, because they are
always before him and enjoy divine life, which is both *from him* as its
principle and *unto him* as its object and end. Who would say that those live
to God who, cast down into hell, lack the vision of God and experience at
least the temporal punishment of loss?

[Marginal heading: 2. From the example of Lazarus;]

V. Second, the ancients were immediately admitted at death into Abraham’s
bosom, as is evident from the example of Lazarus (Luke 16:22, 23), and
therefore also into heavenly joy. For *Abraham’s bosom* can be referred to
nothing other than eternal life, which is everywhere foreshadowed for us
under the symbol of a sumptuous banquet,

[Printed p. 228 | Internet Archive PDF 256 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

with an allusion to the ancient custom used at banquets. They customarily
reclined so that one person rested his head in another’s bosom. This is said
of John in John 13:23, because Christ, reclining in the upper part of the
couch, had John next to him, who is therefore said to have reclined in his
bosom.

Thus nothing is more natural than to say that Lazarus was to Abraham in the
heavenly banquet what John was to the Lord, especially since Christ elsewhere
clearly explains this mystery in Matt. 8:11. He says that “many will come from
the east and from the west and recline at table with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob
in the kingdom of heaven,” that is, they will enjoy the heavenly feast with
him. “To recline with Abraham” and “to be in Abraham’s bosom” are the same,
except that being in his bosom is an indication of greater love, since not
everyone reclined in a person’s bosom, as Maldonatus rightly noted at this
passage.

Abraham is mentioned rather than Jacob or some other blessed person, first
because he was the father of believers. Those who receive the same fruit of
faith as he are gathered into his bosom, that is, into his most pleasant
embrace, just as children come into their father’s bosom when they are
tenderly received by him. He is mentioned also because of the hospitality to
which he was most devoted in life, so that the hospitality of the rich
Abraham—rich, but good—might be opposed to the inhospitality of the rich
banqueter.

VI. Hence it is evident that Bellarmine in book 4, chapter 11 vainly wishes
Abraham’s bosom to be nothing other than limbo, and the souls of Abraham and
Lazarus to have been in the same abyss as the rich banqueter’s soul. They were
indeed greatly separated from each other, he says, but nothing solid lay
between them, only a μέγα χάσμα, *great chasm*.

For first, once our interpretation concerning reclining at the heavenly
banquet is posited, this fiction collapses. Hence in Matt. 10:12, after the
mention of that reclining, it is added that others are to be cast εἰς τὸ
σκότος τὸ ἐξώτερον, “*into outer darkness*.” The similitude is taken from a
solemn supper in which the ceilings within gleam with lights, while outside
there is nothing but the gloom of night. Thus whatever lies outside the
kingdom of heaven, where eternal light shines, will be darkness.

Second, good things are received in Abraham’s bosom, and comfort is given to
Lazarus for the torments he had suffered, thus “compensating the alternating
turns of evils and goods by an ample recompense,” as Tertullian says. But this
good is not possessed in limbo, nor is any place given for comfort, because
there is the punishment of loss and exclusion from the face of God.

Third, if Abraham had been in the lower regions, how could the abyss be said
to have been so deep that passage from one to the other could not occur? How
could Lazarus be called ἀπενεχθεὶς, *carried away*, namely, to a place
different from the lower regions, where the banqueter is said to have been?

Fourth, the Roman Ritual itself favors our opinion. In the chapter on
funerals, among the things the clergy must sing when the body is first brought
into the church are these words: “May Christ who called you receive you, and
may the angels lead you into Abraham’s bosom.”

Moreover, whether this entire conversation is a history, a parable, or a
history described parabolically, it must be understood not κατὰ τὸ ῥητὸν,
*according to the letter*, and historically, but parabolically. Souls cannot
possess a tongue, speech, and fingers.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the thief;]

VII. Third, the thief was admitted into paradise before Christ’s ascension
according to his promise in Luke 23:43: “Today you will be with me in
paradise.” It must not be said here that τὸ *hodie*, *today*, should be
construed with the preceding verb *I say* rather than with the following *you
will be with me*, so that the sense would be, “Today I say to you that you
will be with me in paradise,” namely, at the last judgment. The construction
of the words does not allow this, and the addition would be pointless because
no one speaks except in the present time. It is also evident that Christ is
responding to the thief’s words, “Remember me when you are in your kingdom.”

Nor can it be said that paradise is limbo, as *Maldonatus* wishes, because
Scripture establishes that paradise is located in heaven as a place of
delight

[Printed p. 229 | Internet Archive PDF 257 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and eternal happiness (2 Cor. 12:2, 4; Rev. 2:7). Nor can it be said that the
thief asked only that Christ remember him while Christ was in paradise. It is
not absurd to say that Christ gave him more than he had asked, as a most
gracious Father customarily does above what we ask. Nor can this be called a
peculiar privilege of the thief not shared by others, since the faith and
repentance on account of which Christ gives it are common to all true
believers. Hence Bellarmine in book 1, *On the Beatitude of the Saints*,
chapter 7, rightly proves from this passage that the saints enjoy the vision
of God immediately after death.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Enoch and Elijah;]

VIII. Fourth, Enoch and Elijah were translated into heaven (Heb. 11:5; 2
Kings 2:11). Although it was peculiar to them that they were caught up into
heaven *in body*, it was not peculiar that they were admitted there *in
spirit*. Indeed, they were solemn pledges that the godly would at length
obtain heavenly glory. Although the letter said to have been written by
Elijah to Jehoram in 2 Chron. 21:12 was delivered to the king only after
Elijah’s death, it does not follow that he wrote it after his translation. It
is one thing for it to have been written after his translation, and another
for it only to have been delivered after his translation. The vanity of the
fiction concerning the translation of Enoch and Elijah into the earthly
paradise and their return to fight Antichrist was demonstrated elsewhere,
Part I, Locus VIII, Question 7, thesis 9.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the consolation of the ancients.]

IX. Fifth, the ancient believers expected the heavenly city (Heb. 11:10) and
had great consolation and a certain hope of glory in death. This could not
have occurred if they were still to be cast down into hell. How could Jacob
have testified with such zeal that he awaited the salvation of the Lord (Gen.
49:18), and David that he committed his spirit into the Lord’s hands (Ps.
31:5), if they still had to remain far from God’s face and were to be
glorified only after many ages? Nor can it be said that David in these words
commended the completion of his life to God, because in the style of
Scripture the words can possess no other sense than entrusting the soul to
God, as is evident from the example of St. Stephen (Acts 7:59).

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

X. What is said about *the grave* and *death* in Gen. 37:35 and 42:38—that
Jacob would descend with mourning לִשְׁאֹל, *into the grave*—is wrongly
applied to limbo. For שְׁאוֹל, or ᾅδης, is the grave into which human beings
descend after death.

XI. The *cistern or water* from which the ancients were to be brought out by
the blood of the covenant (Zech. 9:11) must not be applied to limbo. According
to the letter this refers partly to freedom from the Babylonian captivity and
its attendant miseries, from which the Jews had not yet been fully liberated.
According to the mystery it refers partly to the spiritual captivity under
sin, from which Christ frees us by the efficacy of his blood, in which the
covenant of grace was sanctioned, so that it became the foundation of every
liberation, including temporal ones.

XII. It was not the true Samuel who was seen by the pythoness (1 Sam. 28:12),
but only his apparition, namely, a demon pretending to be the prophet. It
came up from the earth to terrify Saul, just as Satan himself frequently
transforms himself into an angel of light. For demons possess no right or
power over the souls of the godly, whom God has received into the eternal
tabernacles so that they may rest from their labors. Otherwise their condition
after death would be worse than in life, in which, armed with faith, they are
safe from all Satan’s assaults.

Add that if it had been the true Samuel, he would not have allowed Saul to
adore him. Nor could it have been said that God did not answer Saul when he
consulted him, whether through the priests or through the prophets, because
he would then have answered through the prophets. Nor does the name Samuel,
which is given to that apparition, have any force in favor of the error. It
could be called this because of the resemblance, just as images of things are
customarily called by the names of the things whose images they are, as
Augustine proves at length to Simplicianus. Nor does the fact that it foretold
future things have any force,

[Printed p. 230 | Internet Archive PDF 258 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

because this could have occurred by divine revelation as a punishment of
human beings, as the same Augustine and, after him, Lyra observe.

XIII. John 3:13 says that no one ascended into heaven before the Son of Man
descended from heaven. It does not speak of a *local ascension* to the
beatific vision, because Enoch and Elijah had already been translated even in
body, but of a *mystical ascension*, that is, knowledge of the heavenly things
to be revealed to human beings. Thus in Rom. 10:6 and Prov. 30:4, *to ascend
into heaven* is to know the divine mysteries. In this sense John 1:18 says
that no one has ever seen God, but the Son who is in the Father’s bosom has
revealed him to us; and Matt. 11:27 says, “No one knows the Father except the
Son and the one to whom he wills to reveal him.”

XIV. Hebrews 9:8 says that the way into the sanctuary had not yet been
manifested before Christ’s ascension, not because it was hidden altogether,
but for the following reasons. 1. It had not been manifested so clearly. The
fathers therefore went into heaven through Christ, in whom they believed,
because he is the way by which one goes into heaven. But he had not yet been
clearly known because he had not yet come. 2. It had not been manifested to
all nations, as in the New Testament. 3. It had not yet been manifested by
Christ’s actual λύτρῳ, *ransom*, which had not yet been paid, but only with
respect to the efficacy of his merit. 4. The passage does not compare one
time with another, but rather the Mosaic sacrifices with Christ’s sacrifice,
to intimate that the former could not open heaven to us, something granted
to Christ’s blood alone. 5. If Christ is called the first to enter heaven,
this must be understood not with respect to time but to causality, because
Christ’s entrance was the true cause of the fathers’ entrance into heaven no
less than of ours.

XV. The Φυλακὴ, *prison*, of which Peter treats in his first epistle, chapter
3, verse 19, when he says that “Christ went in his Spirit and preached to the
spirits τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ, *in prison*,” cannot denote the limbo of the fathers.
He speaks of unbelievers ἀπειθήσασί ποτε, *who were formerly disobedient*,
when Christ in his prophetic Spirit preached repentance to them through
Noah. Rather, he indicates the infernal prison, as in Rev. 20:7, in which they
were confined and detained in Peter’s time because of the rebellion formerly
committed against Christ’s preaching through Noah. They are τοῖς ἐν φυλακῇ,
namely, οὖσι, *those who now are in prison*, not those who formerly were in
it.

XVI. It can be said that the ancients received some increase of knowledge and
consolation after Christ’s exhibition in the flesh and his ascension into
heaven, as is said of the angels in Eph. 3:10. It does not follow that they
had been completely excluded from heaven and from the vision of God’s face,
but only that their blessedness had not yet been fully consummated. Some
refer to this the apostle’s words in Heb. 11:40, “God provided something
better for us, that they should not be perfected without us.”

Calvin also seems to have had this in view by the *watch*, or act of watching,
in which he says the fathers remained from their desire for Christ, in his
comments on 1 Pet. 3. His purpose was not to exclude them from the enjoyment
of blessedness and the vision of God, but to denote their desire and
ἀποκαραδοκίαν, *eager expectation*, for Christ’s advent, toward which the
angels also are said to have looked (1 Pet. 1:12).

XVII. *The promise* sometimes denotes Christ’s first advent in the flesh,
promised in the Old Testament, and sometimes his second advent in glory,
promised in the New. The ancients died without receiving their *promise*
(Heb. 11:39), namely, that of Christ’s first advent, which was reserved for us
χωρὶς αὐτῶν, *without them*. But believers still await the ἐπαγγελίαν,
*promise*, to be received at the Lord’s later advent (Heb. 10:36, 37), which
they also will receive, yet not without us, because what we now await will be
shared by us together with them. Thus God provided κρεῖττόν τι, *something
better*, for us with respect to the first advent, in which we alone rejoice.
