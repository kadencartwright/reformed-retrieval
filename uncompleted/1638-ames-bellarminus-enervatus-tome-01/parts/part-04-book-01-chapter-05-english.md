### CHAPTER V

#### On the Judge of Controversies, Book 3, Chapter 3, and so forth

##### THESIS I

Bell.: *The question is whether the interpretation of Scripture is to be
sought from some one visible and common judge, or left to each person's own
discretion.*

Prot.: These words obscure the state of the question rather than declare it.
For: 1. No one among us has ever written, said, or thought that the
interpretation of Scripture is to be left to each person's own discretion. 2.
We distinguish judgment into public and private. 3. We further say that public
judgment is either simply and absolutely authentic and definitive, binding
people's consciences to give it credence; or limited by a ministerial calling,
declaring what it has found to be settled through due inquiry, and binding to
belief no further than it can show that what it proposes is the Word of God.
4. We say that private judgment belongs to individual people discerning and
testing for themselves what is proposed, without declaring anything to others
by virtue of some special calling. The whole controversy, however, turns on
these two questions: 1. Whether there is among human beings a visible judge of
those controversies which concern religion, in whose sentence the consciences
of all ought to acquiesce. 2. Whether individual private people ought not to
discern with judgment whether the things publicly proposed to them are from
God or not.

2. Bell.: *Some preliminary observations must be made concerning the senses of
Scripture. 1. It is peculiar to Scripture that it very often contains two
senses,*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 55 | printed p. 45 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*the literal and the spiritual, according to 1 Corinthians 10:11: All things
happened to them in a figure. 2. The spiritual sense is threefold: allegorical,
when something concerning Christ and the Church is signified in the New
Testament; tropological, when something pertaining to morals is signified; and
anagogical, when eternal life is signified. 3. It is not improbable that
several literal senses are sometimes found in the same statement. 4. It is
agreed between us and our adversaries that effective arguments ought to be
sought from the literal sense alone, because it is gathered immediately from
the words and is certainly the sense of the Holy Spirit. But mystical senses
are various, and it is not always evident whether the Holy Spirit intended
them.*

Prot.: 1. The signification of certain passages of Scripture by which types
and the things foreshadowed by types are regarded does not contain a double
sense of the words, but one sense of the words and another of the types. When
the types are adapted to signify other things, they carry with them the words
by which they are explained. The Roman Catholics contend that one and the same
worship terminates in the image and its prototype. Why, then, may not one and
the same sense of the words be referred both to the type and to the thing
foreshadowed by the type? 2. Although it is peculiar to God to order events so
as to pre-signify other things certain to occur, nevertheless, for the
signification of certain words to refer at once both to the thing representing
and to the thing represented is so commonplace that it even happens daily in
stage plays. 3. In 1 Corinthians 10:11 the apostle says nothing about typical
representations. Rather, as Bannes rightly explains on Thomas, part 1,
question 1, article 13, he says that *punishments happened to them in a figure,
that is, as an example for us.* Yet if it is conceded that the apostle speaks
there of the typical character of historical events, what does this have to do
with a typical, spiritual, and mystical sense of words, especially of words
which have another literal sense? But because, as Abulensis observes on
Matthew 2, question 57, and Matthew 13, question 38, *these mystical senses are
inventions of preachers,* fabricated proofs are therefore also suited to them.
4. It is plainly absurd to make the mystical sense threefold because three
mysteries are signified. For in that way the literal sense will be a
hundredfold sense, because a hundred different things

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 56 | printed p. 46 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

are signified by the letter. 5. If there are different literal senses of one
and the same passage of
Scripture, then equivocation is found in Scripture. Although this cannot be
excused from blasphemy, it is nevertheless affirmed by certain Roman
Catholics, including Bannes in the same place. 6. Bellarmine treats us too
familiarly when he says that we agree with them that effective arguments ought
to be sought from the literal sense alone. For although we attribute no force
to mystical senses invented by people, nevertheless, if it is certainly
evident that the Holy Spirit intended something mystical, the firmest
arguments are drawn from it. For there is no true proposition from which other
truths do not follow. Vasquez therefore speaks well in part 1, question 1,
disputation 16: *Since a mystical sense is also from the intention of the Holy
Spirit, when it is evident to us, it can without doubt serve us just as
effectively for proof as the literal sense.* But to call something which the
Holy Spirit does not think or intend the sense of Scripture is foreign to
every sense.

3. Bell.: *We say that the Holy Spirit interpreting the Scriptures is
certainly found in the Church, that is, in a council of bishops confirmed by
the supreme pastor of the whole Church, or in the Supreme Pastor together
with a council of the other pastors.*

Prot.: You therefore say absolutely nothing certain. For: 1. There is no
supreme pastor of the whole Church other than Christ. 2. You do not say what
kind of council you mean, whether general or provincial. 3. Nor do you declare
what kind of Pastors and Bishops you mean. 4. Nor do you indicate where one is
to flee if the Council and your supreme Pastor disagree. 5. Neither do you
make us more certain whether this interpretation proceeds from extraordinary
revelation or depends upon ordinary means.

4. Bell.: *The heretics teach that the Holy Spirit, the interpreter of
Scripture, is not bound to the Bishops or to any class of people, and that
therefore each person ought to be a judge, whether he himself has the gift of
interpretation or adheres to someone else whom he sees endowed with the same
gift.*

Prot.: 1. It is not so grave a crime to affirm that the Holy Spirit freely
distributes his gifts to whom he wills, 1 Corinthians 12:1, and is not bound to
the Pope with his Councils. 2. When we say that

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 57 | printed p. 47 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

each person is a judge, we mean a judgment of discernment. In this
sense, the Roman Catholic inquisitors themselves attribute just as much to
private people. Thus the *Silvestrina summa*, under the word “Scruple,” says:
*The sixth rule to be observed by the scrupulous is to use the virtue of
equity, interpreting with discernment not only human precepts but also divine
ones, especially affirmative precepts. Although this interpretation in a
contentious court belongs to the one to whom the promulgation of the law
belongs, nevertheless, according to the Archbishop, in the court of
conscience it belongs to each person for his own case.*

##### Bellarmine's Arguments

5. Bell.: *In Exodus 18 Moses answered all doubts arising concerning the Law
of the Lord.*

Prot.: 1. This passage has absolutely nothing to do with the interpretation of
Scripture, for at that time no Scriptures existed, according to Bellarmine's
testimony in chapter 2 of this book. 2. Moses then discharged an extraordinary
office, for he referred many cases directly to God, verse 19, something which
the Roman Pontiff is not accustomed to do. 3. It is evident from Jethro's
counsel that the judgment which Moses exercised could in great part have been
exercised by others; and verse 25 makes clear that it was afterward delegated
to others as well.

6. Bell.: *In Deuteronomy 17 those in doubt are referred to the Priest.*

Prot.: 1. The passage does not concern the interpretation of Scripture, but
judgment between killing and killing, lawsuit and lawsuit, or blow and blow;
that is, questions of fact or the application of law to facts, not law
considered simply. Thus Cajetan says on that passage, *doubtful personal blows
are meant,* and so forth. 2. It concerns not only the Priests, but also the
civil judge, verses 9 and 12. 3. It concerns judgment from the place of the
tabernacle, where God was to be consulted, as Cajetan rightly observes. 4.
There an express Law requires that the Priests and supreme judges in a fixed
place be consulted. But we have no law requiring consultation of the Roman
Pontiff.

7. Bell.: *Ecclesiastes 12: The words of the wise, and so forth, were given by
one Pastor; beyond these, my son, inquire no further. One must therefore
acquiesce in the sentence of the supreme Pastor.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 58 | printed p. 48 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot.: 1. This is Christ, or God, as Jerome interprets it. 2. *Inquire no
further beyond these* is not in the text. 3. It expressly treats the faithful
words of wise people, such as Solomon. But who is so senseless as to believe
that Solomon consulted the Jewish high Priest in his writings or spoke from
his mouth?

8. Bell.: *Haggai 2 and Malachi 2 show that it belongs to the Priests by virtue
of their office to explain the sentence of God.* Prot.: Who denies it?

9. Bell.: *In 2 Chronicles 19 Jehoshaphat distinguishes the office of the
Pontiff from the office of the king.* Prot.: Nor ought they to be confused.
What does this have to do with the interpretation of Scripture?

10. Bell.: *In Matthew 16 it is said to Peter, I will give you the keys of the
kingdom of heaven.*

Prot.: 1. Nothing is said here about a Council of Bishops. 2. Nothing is said
about some particular successor of Peter or an ordinary supreme Pastor. 3.
Nothing is said properly about the Scriptures, but about sins, as is explained
in Matthew 28:18–19 and John 20:21–22. 4. If *whatever you shall loose* is
understood as the interpretation of Scripture, then *whatever you shall bind*
must necessarily also be understood as the obscuring of Scripture, so that
this too would belong to Peter's office. Those who wish to listen to his
successors certainly seem to have chosen this part for themselves.

11. Bell.: *In Matthew 18 heretics are to be brought before the Church.*

Prot.: Neither is the Roman Pontiff with a council understood there by the
Church, nor is it necessary that all those before whom heretics are brought
should have an infallible power to interpret the Scriptures.

12. Bell.: *In Matthew 23 the Scribes and Pharisees sit upon Moses' chair, and
so forth.*

Prot.: 1. Will the Roman Catholics therefore say that the Scribes and
Pharisees had an infallible power to interpret the Scriptures? Let them
consult Matthew 16:12 and 23:16–19, and so forth. 2. If the Priests themselves
had been recognized as judges of controversies, the sects of the Pharisees and
Sadducees would not have endured among the Jews. 3. They erred at least in the
sentence they rendered against Christ, although there are some among the Roman
Catholics who defend the contrary blasphemy. Bellarmine's desired observation

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 59 | printed p. 49 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

that the Lord and the Apostles never rebuked Pontiffs and Priests by name, lest
they appear to rebuke the chair and the Priesthood, is not worth noting. For:
1. The Lord's Prophets in the Old Testament everywhere condemn Priests under
the name of Priests. 2. The names Scribes and Pharisees were names of the
highest perfection and were held in the greatest honor among the people in
Christ's time. 3. The Priests themselves were for the most part Pharisees,
except that they sometimes favored the more impious sect of the Sadducees,
Acts 15:17.

13. Bell.: *In the last chapter of John Christ says: Peter, feed the sheep.*

Prot.: Therefore, all whose office it is to feed the sheep are, by this
reasoning, infallible Judges of controversies—and so are their successors.

14. Bell.: *In Luke 22: I have prayed that your faith may not fail.*

Prot.: Therefore, anyone whose faith does not entirely fail in temptation is
an infallible interpreter of Scripture. Thus we shall have as many judges of
controversies as there are persevering believers.

15. Bell.: *In Acts 15 a question of faith is brought before the Council of
Jerusalem.*

Prot.: We confess that the Holy Spirit was in the Apostles, interpreting the
Scriptures without error. But with respect to certainty of direction, no
inference runs from extraordinary interpreters to ordinary ones.

16. Bell.: *In Galatians 2 Paul went up to the Apostles, lest perhaps he should
run in vain.*

Prot.: He did not do so to refer some controversy to Peter as the supreme
Pastor, nor to seek confirmation of his doctrine from Peter as judge, but to
show everyone that he maintained agreement with the other Apostles of Christ.

17. Bell.: *In 1 Corinthians 12 the interpretation of utterances is said to be
given only to certain people.*

Prot.: 1. The passage concerns the interpretation of languages, a gift which
Bellarmine

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 60 | printed p. 50 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

denied above belonged to the Roman Pontiff and the Councils. 2. How does this
follow: It is not given to all, therefore it is given to the
Roman Pontiff? 3. The text goes on: *The Spirit distributes privately to each
as he wills.* He is therefore not bound to the Roman Chair.

18. Bell.: *In 1 John 4 we are commanded to test the spirits.*

Prot.: 1. If all believers ought to test the spirits, then a judgment
discerning which doctrine is from God and which is not is not merely permitted
to them, but even commanded. 2. The rule for this testing is also delivered
into the hands of individual believers, as it were, verses 2 and 3. 3. It is
plainly indicated that this judgment is to be rendered by individuals for
themselves, and not awaited from the Pontiff with his Councils.

19. Bell.: *The same thing is proposed from the practice of the Church and the
testimonies of Pontiffs, Emperors, and Fathers in chapters 6, 7, and 8.*

Prot.: These show: 1. That public judgments about ecclesiastical matters are
to be handled in Synods by ecclesiastical people acting from a public calling.
2. That the Bishops of Rome formerly had great authority in these assemblies
because of their own dignity and that of the city over which they presided. 3.
That some of the Roman Bishops arrogated more than enough to themselves and
gradually obtained it. All these points are conceded by us. They prove nothing
else which pertains to the matter. Nor, in truth, can they prove anything
about right in Theology without Scripture.

20. Bell.: *It is proved by reason, for God was not ignorant that many
difficulties would arise concerning faith. He therefore ought to have provided
some judge for the Church, and this judge can be no other than an
Ecclesiastical prince, either alone or certainly with a Council. Scripture
admits various senses. It is a law, which in every well-ordered commonwealth is
distinguished from the judge; the judge himself cannot interpret it.*

Prot.: 1. Neither was God ignorant that many difficulties would arise
concerning the prince himself and the Ecclesiastical judge. He therefore ought
to have provided a judge of this judge. 2. This Ecclesiastical prince, the
Roman Pontiff, does not remove all the difficulties which arise concerning
faith, not even among his own subjects. For controversies now rage between the
Jesuits and the Dominicans concerning the divine decrees, predestination,
grace, and free choice; and among

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 61 | printed p. 51 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

nearly whole nations concerning the power of the Roman Pontiff, both in
temporal matters over princes and in spiritual matters over general Councils,
and therefore
concerning that very judge of controversies. 3. Through the Scriptures and
the Doctors and Pastors whom he gave to the Church, God provided as much as is
sufficient for the salvation of the faithful and godly. He was not obliged to
provide for the curiosity and perversity of others. 4. In worldly matters,
where external things regulated by coercive force are treated, there is need
of an external judge armed with such force. But faith is not compelled, and so
the rule of an external judge has no place in matters of faith. 5. Even in
human affairs Aristotle rightly observes in *Rhetoric* 1.3: *Laws ought, as
far as possible, to declare everything expressly and to leave as little as
possible to the power of judges.* And in *Politics* 3.7: *The laws ought to be
mistresses of the commonwealth, while princes or judges are only governors of
those matters for which the laws cannot provide.* And in chapter 12 of the
same book: *What a law cannot explain, neither can a human being explain. The
one who commands the law to rule commands God and the laws to rule, but the
one who commands a human being to be prince commands a human being and a beast
to be princes.* We must not therefore suppose that in the most perfect Law of
God so great a power is left to Pontiffs, or, to use Aristotle's phrase, to
Roman beasts. As much as is detracted from Scripture, which is God's law, is
detracted from God the lawgiver himself.

##### Our Arguments Recounted by Bellarmine

21. Prot.: Isaiah 54: I will make all your sons taught by God.

Bell.: *The sense is that Christians will be such that they not only hear a
teacher outwardly explaining the Word of God, but also hear God inwardly
persuading them by the grace of the Holy Spirit to do what they hear.*

Prot.: The question concerns understanding the truth. Instruction in doctrine
belongs properly and immediately to the understanding, and this passage
concerns instruction in doctrine. This understanding therefore depends upon
the grace of the Holy Spirit just as much as practice does.

22. Prot.: Jeremiah 31: I will give my law in their hearts, and so forth.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 62 | printed p. 52 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Bell. 1: *That is, so that we may not only know, but also fulfill it.* Prot.:
God will therefore grant that we know. We ask nothing more from this passage.

Bell. 2: *By these words, A man will no longer teach his neighbor, the future
life is signified.* Prot.: In Hebrews 8 the apostle expressly explains it of
the correction of the Old Testament which is possessed in this life. Bell. 3:
*It is understood of the knowledge of the one God which the Jews and Turks
possess.* Prot.: The Jews and Turks therefore know that God forgives their
iniquity and remembers their sins no more, for these are the words of the
text.

23. Prot.: Matthew 23: But do not be called Rabbi.

Bell.: *Only the pursuit of this honor is prohibited.*

Prot.: This honor cannot be pursued in any way more than it is by the Roman
Pontiffs, when they contend that they alone are the judges of all
controversies.

24. Prot.: John 5: I do not receive testimony from a human being. Therefore,
the Word of God does not need human testimony.

Bell.: *Scripture does not need human testimony for its own sake, but for
ours.* Prot.: If Scripture can have no efficacy toward us except through human
testimony, then Scripture itself, for its own sake—that is, for its own
end—needs human testimony. But the former is the Roman Catholics' position;
therefore, so is the latter.

25. Prot.: John 7: If anyone wills to do the will of him who sent me, he will
recognize my doctrine, and so forth. Bell.: *That is, they will be free of
certain impediments on account of which others cannot understand the truth of
the faith.* Prot.: 1. If this is the whole content of those words, then they
contain no promise, but only a bare assertion of a future event, which is
plainly false. 2. These impediments are not removed from many Pontiffs, who are
known to have been wicked men, but they are removed from many private people.
Therefore, many private people can understand the Scriptures more easily than
the Pontiffs themselves.

26. Prot.: John 10: My sheep hear my voice. Bell. 1: *The Lord speaks of the
predestined.* Prot.: Therefore the predestined know the Lord's voice, verse 4.
Bell. 2: *Christ speaks to his sheep through Scripture, through*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 63 | printed p. 53 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*inspiration, and most plainly through the mouth of his vicars.* Prot.: As
though the mouth of his vicars could explain the Lord's mind more plainly than
the mouth of the Lord himself! It is certain that Christ at that time spoke to
the Jews by himself without vicars, and that his words exist in the
Scriptures. Therefore, just as his sheep who were then present understood his
voice without an interpreter, so they can also understand it today from the
Scriptures.

27. Prot.: Acts 17: The Bereans searched the Scriptures to see whether the
things Paul preached were so. Bell. 1: *The Bereans were not certain that the
Apostle could not err, and therefore they rightly searched the Scriptures. But
Christians who are certain that the Church cannot err in preaching the
doctrine of faith are bound to receive it and not to doubt whether these
things are so.* Prot.: 1. Therefore the Bereans, although Laypeople, read
Scripture without some special concession. 2. They were also able to
understand by reading. Otherwise they could not have judged whether these
things were so, but would have read in vain. That is what is asked in this
passage, not the extent to which people are or are not bound to believe. 3. By
this reasoning, those who are not yet certain what the Church's prerogative of
not erring is are permitted to read the Scriptures and find it useful, and
they are even granted understanding of them, but others are not. No one of
sound mind will affirm this. Bell. 2: *While a heretic doubts the authority of
the Church, he does no wrong in searching and examining whether the passages
of Scripture approved by the Council of Trent are so, provided that he does
this with the
intention of finding the truth.* Prot.: 1. Therefore, while someone doubts the
Council of Trent, he can find the truth through reading Scripture; when he
places faith in it, he cannot. Nothing could be truer. 2. Nevertheless, there
are various doubts concerning faith even among those who believe that the
Roman Church cannot err. Would they do wrong if they searched the Scriptures?

28. Prot.: Romans 12: Whether prophecy, according to the proportion or measure
or *analogy* of faith. Bell.: *Nothing is gathered from this against us.*
Prot.: We are not accustomed to press this passage in the present case.
Nevertheless, this can be gathered from it: the gift of understanding and
interpreting the Scriptures is distributed by God to believers according to
different measures, and

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 64 | printed p. 54 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

therefore they can occupy themselves fruitfully in reading Scripture even
though they have nothing in common with the Roman Pontiff.

29. Prot.: 1 Corinthians 2: The spiritual person judges all things. Bell.:
*Nevertheless, the definitive judgment of controversies does not belong to
the spiritual and perfect. 1. Because we are not certain by faith who such
people are, as we are certain that the Pope is ruled by the Holy Spirit. 2.
Because they are ignorant of some things, 2 Kings 4:27.* Prot.: 1. The text
does not concern those who are spiritually perfect, but all who have the
Spirit, as is evident from the opposite, natural or sensual people. 2. We do
not say that definitive judgment belongs to them, but a judgment of
discernment. 3. Bellarmine contends by sheer impudence when he wants us to
be more certain that all Popes are ruled by the Holy Spirit than that Prophets
are Prophets. 4. He also plainly overturns the Pope's judgment in that he
denies that Elisha is a suitable judge of controversies because, in a matter
of fact, he says, *The Lord has hidden this from me,* 2 Kings 4:17. For it is
not fitting that the Pope should have full knowledge of all facts or events.

30. Prot.: 1 Corinthians 12: One and the same Spirit apportions to each as he
wills. This gift is therefore not bound to the Council or Pope, as experience
also teaches. Bell. 1: *The Apostle speaks of individual people, but the
principle is different for a Council and the Pontiff.* Prot.: The Pontiff is
an individual person, and Councils consist of individual people. Bell. 2: *It
does not follow that this gift is given to all because it has been given to
some.* Prot.: Nor is this our inference. Rather, because it was given to
various people who were not Pontiffs, it is not bound to the Pontiffs' Chair,
but to the will of God. Bell. 3: *New revelations were given to the Prophets,
but the explanation of received doctrine and judgment about dogmas always
belonged to the Pontiffs.* Prot.: As though the Prophets did not explain the
doctrine handed down by Moses or did not also judge dogmas! They judged the
priests themselves, who said, *We are wise, and the law of Jehovah is with
us,* Jeremiah 8:8–9. These are the very words of the Roman Pope.

31. Prot.: 1 Thessalonians 5: Test all things. 1 John 4: Test the spirits to
see if they are from God.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 65 | printed p. 55 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Bell.: *Paul and John do not intend everyone in the Church to do these things,
but those to whom it belongs.* Prot.: Yet it belongs to everyone to test the
things which concern their consciences, just as much as to *hold fast what is
good* and *abstain from every appearance of evil,* which are appended to the
prior command in the text of 1 Thessalonians 5 as the ends of the commanded
testing. It also belongs to all those who ought not to believe every spirit to
test the spirits, and they have the rule for testing the spirit delivered into
their own hands. But these things belong to all believers, 1 John 4:1–3.

Bell. 2: *Both passages concern doubtful doctrine, but the doctrine of Councils
is not doubtful.*

Prot.: 1. Something is doubtful when it is doubted, for no one doctrine is in
itself more doubtful than another. But many doubt the doctrine of the Pope and
Councils. How, then, will it be evident to us that the doctrine of the Pope and
Councils is not doubtful, except through some examination? 2. If doubtful
doctrines are to be examined by doubters according to the rule of Scripture,
then those doubters must certainly have some understanding of the Scriptures
and render some judgment about controversies through them. This is the whole
of what we infer from these passages.

32. Prot.: 1 John 2: The anointing teaches you concerning all things. Bell.:
*It speaks only of those dogmas which they had already received from the
Apostles.* Prot.: We ask nothing more. The anointing of the Holy Spirit teaches
believers to understand the things they received from the Apostles; therefore
it teaches them to understand the Scriptures in the things necessary for
salvation, for those believers had received these things from the Apostles.

23. Prot.: Ephesians 2: You have been built upon the foundation of the Apostles
and Prophets. The Pope and Councils are not the foundation of faith.

Bell.: *The Word of God administered through the Prophets and Apostles is the
primary foundation of faith. But the secondary foundation testifying to that
Word is the Church, Peter, and the Roman Pontiff.*

Prot.: 1. This secondary foundation is not built upon the foundation of the
Prophets and Apostles, because

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 66 | printed p. 56 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

they taught nothing about it. 2. Not everything which makes us know certainly
what God has revealed is a foundation of faith. The Jews themselves today
make us certain in some way about a large part of divine revelation, yet they
are not a foundation of faith. 3. The testimony of the Church is neither the
only nor the primary means by which we are made certain of God's revelation.
4. The Church, Peter, and the Pontiff cannot constitute one secondary
foundation. The Church is built upon that which is the foundation, whether
primary or secondary. Peter was an Apostle, and so his doctrine is part of the
primary foundation. The Pontiff himself is often uncertain about many things.
Certainly the Priests in Jeremiah's time did not make people certain of God's
revelation through Jeremiah, but opposed him, Jeremiah 26. Nor did people await
from Linus and Clement certainty about the doctrine which the Apostle John
preached to them.

34. Prot.: If the Pontiff judges the Scriptures, it follows that the Pontiff
is above Scripture. Bell.: *The inference would be valid if the Pontiff judged
whether what Scripture teaches is true or false, but he judges only what the
true interpretation of Scripture is.* Prot.: But: 1. If Scripture plainly says
something—for example, that the Lord's Supper is to be administered to
communicants in both kinds—and the Pontiff judges the contrary, does he not
judge the Scriptures in such a way that he subjects them to himself? 2. If the
Pontiff's interpretation is to be received absolutely because it proceeds from
him, without any other cause, then the sense of Scripture, which is Scripture
itself, at least as far as we are concerned, is subjected to the Pontiff's
authority. 3. There are some among the Roman Catholics who defend both the
inference and the consequent itself: Pighius, *Hierarchy*, book 1, chapters
2–4; Cardinal Cusanus, Letter 7 to the Bohemians; Cochlaeus, Hosius, and others
of the same flour.

35. Prot.: If our faith depends upon the judgment of the Church, it therefore
depends upon the word of human beings.

Bell.: *The word of a Council, or of the Pontiff teaching from the Chair, is
not altogether the word of a human being, but in some manner the Word of God.*
Prot.: That which agrees with the

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 67 | printed p. 57 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Word of God is in some manner the Word of God, even if proposed by some one
private person. Yet faith does not depend upon a word of that kind as such. But
if what either the Pontiff pronounces from the Chair or Councils decree is
absolutely the Word of God, then we have as many volumes of the Word of God as
there are tomes of Councils, Decrees, and Decretal Epistles, and they all have
equal authority with the Old and New Testaments.

36. Prot.: In expounding the Scriptures, the Fathers do not employ a
praetorian power of judgment. Bell.: *They discharged the office of teachers,
not of judges.* Prot.: 1. Among the ordinary offices which Scripture testifies
Christ gave to the Church, the office of teachers and pastors is supreme,
Ephesians 4:11. 2. If the Fathers and Pontiffs could err in teaching, then they
are not governed by the Holy Spirit as the Apostles were governed.

##### Question: Whether Our Faith or the Roman Catholic Faith Rests upon a More Certain Foundation

Bell., book 3, chapter 10: *It must be understood that a proposition of faith
is concluded by such a syllogism: Whatever God has revealed in Scripture is
true; God has revealed this in Scripture; therefore, this is true. Of the
premises of this syllogism, the first is certain among all. The second is also
most firm among Catholics, for it rests upon the testimony of the Church, a
Council, or the Pontiff, about whom we have clear promises in Scripture that
they cannot err. Acts 15: It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us. Luke
22: I have prayed for you, that your faith may not fail. But among the heretics
it rests only upon conjectures or the judgment of their own Spirit.*

Prot.: 1. The faith of a Roman Catholic person, according to this analysis, is
of this kind: 1. *Whatever God has revealed in Scripture is true.* Here all
unwritten traditions are excluded from the first principle of faith. 2. *God
has plainly revealed in Scripture that the Church, a Council, and the Roman
Pontiff cannot err, Acts 15 and Luke 22.* Here it is taught that the Church is
to be believed because of Scripture, not Scripture because of the Church. 3.
*The Church, a Council, and the Pontiff testify that it is revealed in
Scripture that Christ is the redeemer of the world. I therefore believe that
Christ is the redeemer of the world.*

Our faith, however, is acknowledged to be of this kind: *Whatever God has
revealed in Scripture is true. But God has plainly revealed in Scripture that
Christ is the redeemer of the world. I therefore believe that Christ is the
redeemer of the world.* Therefore, with the same certainty of faith, we
believe in Christ and all the things we believe as necessary with which the
Roman Catholics say they believe the very principle of things to be believed,
namely, that the Church, a Council, and the Pontiff cannot err. For Bellarmine
does not refer that faith back to the authority of the Church, but immediately
to Scripture. In this way we believe all things that are to be believed. Our
faith is therefore most certain by Bellarmine's own confession. For I think no
one of sound mind will deny that the testimonies of Scripture concerning
Christ are at least as clear as those concerning the Roman Pontiff. Nor, when
we believe immediately on Scripture's testimony that Christ is the redeemer,
do we follow any other conjecture or private Spirit than they must follow when
they first believe that the Church, a Council, and the Pontiff cannot err. 2.
Our faith rests upon testimony which is never false, but the Roman Catholics'
faith rests upon the testimony of a pontiff whom they themselves confess can
err and be deceived in many things. Our faith is therefore more certain. 3.
Our faith rests upon the testimony by which the first believers, who
constituted the first Christian church, were brought to believe; they did not
regard any Council, Pontiff, or Church as the reason for their faith. But the
Roman Catholics rest upon another testimony which those believers lacked.
Therefore, our faith, not theirs, rests upon a true foundation. Some light can
be brought to this consideration from this confession of Alexander Pesantius
in II-II, question 1, article 1, disputation 3: *Faith is not resolved, as into
the formal reason for assent, into the authority of the Church or of the
Supreme Pontiff, either by himself or together with a Council. For the faith
of the Apostles, which is of the same nature as ours, did not rest upon such an
authority; therefore, neither does ours.*
