TITLE FOUR

On the KNOWLEDGE OF GOD

ACTS 15:18

Known to God are all his works from the beginning of the world, or from
eternity.

It is asked whether in God there is middle, mixed, or conditional knowledge;
that is, knowledge of things outside God which, with respect to their
futurition, do not depend upon God at all, and which is neither knowledge of
vision nor knowledge of simple intelligence. The Arminians affirm it. Jacob
Arminius, disputation 4, theses 43 and 45: “Although,” he says, “it is necessary
for that middle knowledge to intervene in matters which depend upon freedom of
choice.” The Pelagianizing papists, especially the Jesuits, also think so:
Ruiz, on question 14, article 13 of the first part of Thomas, *On the Knowledge
of God*, disputation 49, section 2; Vázquez, on the first part of Thomas,
disputation 67, question 14, article 13, chapter 3; Fasolus, volume 1, question
14, article 13; Arrúbal, volume 1, question 14, article 13, disputation 46;
Arriaga, logical disputation 14, section

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 163 | PDF p. 214]

6; Lessius, *On Efficacious Grace*; Luis de Molina, on question 14, article 13
of the first part of Thomas, disputation 17, and *On the Agreement of Free
Choice with the Gifts of Grace*, disputation 52.

Knowledge is twofold—to say nothing of the reflexive and wholly natural
knowledge by which God knows himself. One is knowledge of vision, or intuitive
or determinate knowledge, by which God knows all beings outside himself which
are, were, will be, or are not. The other is knowledge of simple intelligence,
by which he knows possible things; it is called natural, indeterminate, and
abstract knowledge. But middle knowledge, by which God knows future
contingents which will occur upon a given hypothesis before any decree of God,
is a blasphemous invention.

1. For there would be some cause other than God's will why possible things
began from eternity to be future and passed from the state of possibility to
the state of futurition. That cause would be a nonbeing or fortune, if God saw
things as future before his decree.

2. There would be some temporal cause of an eternal thing—for the futuritions
of things are eternal, and the future did not begin to be future—or future
things would have no cause at all, if they were future and foreknown as future
before God's will.

3. For there are many wise connections belonging to God's wonderful wisdom
and providence, such as these: If Job is despoiled by Satan, he will humbly
submit himself to God; if Judas is tempted, he will betray Christ; if the
church is oppressed, it will believe and will—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 164 | PDF p. 215]

—pray. These connections, I say, would precede every act of God's will and
middle knowledge.

4. For God would be compelled by fate to approve future beings antecedently to
his decree, since he could neither procure nor permit their futurition.
Consequently, God would possess no dominion of providence over contingent
acts, since they have futurition before every act of the divine will, and
therefore before the act of foreknowledge and omnipotence.

5. For in those evils which befall us through the agency of contingent causes,
no comforts are to be sought from God's will, because they were fated to be
future before every decree of God.

6. For no hope and no prayer should be conceived either to procure contingent
goods or to avert contingent evils, if they occur apart from God's will and
are future when God does not will them.

7. For the determinate number of contingent acts, and of supernatural
conversions and nonconversions to Christ calling in the gospel, will rest
absolutely with created free choice, not with God and his will. For just so
many conversions, and of no more people, and just so many nonconversions, and
of no more people, will be future before every will of God. God cannot convert
more people than he converts, nor

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 165 | PDF p. 216]

leave more unconverted than are not converted. O iron Fortune, which obtains
dominion over Jehovah!

8. For through this knowledge a non-knowable thing—namely, something which is
future without the will of the first cause—is presupposed as knowable,
actually future, and actually known, although it is merely possible and
nothing more. For before the act of the divine will, things are only possible
and in no way future, but indifferent to futurition or nonfuturition. Thus they
cannot be known as future, any more than a non-visible thing can be seen or a
non-audible thing heard.

9. For the fixed and determinate number of the elect and the non-elect would
not depend upon God's will, but either upon fortune or upon created free
choice. For before every decree of God, just so many people—no more and no
fewer—will finally believe and therefore be saved; and no more and no fewer
will finally resist God when he calls and therefore be damned.

10. Future things are future from themselves, not from God's will; nonfuture
things are nonfuture from themselves, not from God's will. This overturns all
providence. For the fixed and determinate number of all contingent actions,
all afflictions and happy successes which occur through the agency of
contingent causes, and all the free—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 166 | PDF p. 217]

—acts of elect and reprobate human beings and of good and evil angels, would
not rest with God. For all these are future and foreknown as future through
middle knowledge before every act of the divine will.

It is asked whether God did not foreknow through middle knowledge that the
Tyrians would be converted if they had seen miracles; that the Keilites would
deliver David into Saul's hands if he had remained among the Keilites; and
that foreign nations would hear Ezekiel if he had been sent to them. The
Arminians and Jesuits affirm it. We deny it.

1. He sees these things as infallibly future in his decree, which connects the
conversion of the Tyrians and the sight of the miracles.

2. For he sees them in his decree by which he decreed to give them the grace by
which they would be converted.

3. For he could not see these things in the very slippery and wavering
connection between two things which occur contingently and cohere with each
other contingently, not intrinsically or by any necessity. The connecting bond
must therefore be God's most firm decree; otherwise, God would certainly know
what is not knowable.

HEBREWS 4:13

All things are naked and open to him with whom we have to do.

It is asked whether God certainly foreknows future contin—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 167 | PDF p. 218]

—gents. The Remonstrants are doubtful. Episcopius, thesis 10, section 4, *On
God*, says that it is not necessary to know whether God foreknows future
contingents in order for God to be worshiped. In the *Apology*, chapter 2,
folio 43, it is certain that the Remonstrants believe contingents, precisely
considered as future things of that kind, to be unknowable and therefore not
to be numbered among the objects of divine omniscience. Contingents, as future
by decree, are not contingent but necessary; nor could they be known by God.
Here Cicero speaks in them. He took from God foreknowledge of future things so
that the creature's freedom would not perish, and, as Augustine says, made
people sacrilegious so that they would not become slaves.

Against the devil, the Arminians, and all pagans and Socinians, we profess that
we worship the true God, who foreknows all future things.

1. For God is not God but an idol if he does not foreknow all future things.
Isaiah 41:26: “Who has declared it from the beginning, that we might know? Or
from antiquity, that we might say, He is righteous? There is no one who
declares, and no one who foretells.” Isaiah 42:8–9: “I am Jehovah; that is my
name; and my glory I will not give to another, nor my praise to carved images.
Behold, the former things have come to pass, and I declare new things; before
they spring forth, I announce them to you.” Yet things which have not yet
sprung forth are future contingents. Isaiah 41:22: “Let them bring them forth
and declare to us the things which will happen. Declare what the former things
are.” Verse 23: “Declare the things which are to come, that we may know that
you are gods.” Therefore, God is not God if he does not foreknow future
contingents. Psalm 139: “Thou understand—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 168 | PDF p. 219]

—est my thought afar off.” Verse 4: “When a word is not yet on my tongue, O
Jehovah, thou knowest it altogether.” Verse 16: “Thine eyes saw my unformed
mass, and in thy book all these things were written, from which the days were
being formed, when as yet none of them existed.”

2. All certainty of prophecies would perish, and the foundation and object of
faith concerning future contingents would be fallacious, if our God neither
knew nor could know future contingents.

3. Throughout the whole Old Testament God foretold things which he did not
know. Thus he would not know that the Son was to be crucified, that his hands
and feet were to be pierced, that lots were to be cast over his garments, and
that the gospel was to be preached.

4. He does not know future contingents before they exist. Therefore, if he
knows them when they exist, he knows in time something which he did not know
from eternity. God's knowledge increases, and he now knows more things than he
knew from eternity, which is blasphemous.

5. If God did not know future contingents from eternity, neither did he decree
from eternity that they should occur. New knowledge, new volitions, and new
decrees begin in God. Therefore, nothing is future, but all things are merely
possible.

6. The Holy Spirit's reasonings are vain: God decreed these things; therefore,
they will be. Otherwise, God decreed that things which he does not know should
occur, which would be foolish in a prudent man; or else he decreed all the
things which he decreed in such a way that they remain possi—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 169 | PDF p. 220]

—ble things which may either be or not be.

7. The decree of election from foreknown faith, and of reprobation from
foreknown unbelief, collapses. For faith and unbelief are future contingents,
and in the Arminians' opinion God today does not know who and how many will be
saved, and who and how many will be damned.

8. David, Jehoshaphat, and Ahab consulted God concerning future contingents in
vain; the priests and prophets of God did so in vain and without any faith.

9. Here the Arminians wrongly adhere to Aristotle, who said that there is no
determinate truth about future contingents. He either speaks about truth in
relation to us, since we do not know the truth of such things in nonnecessary
matter, or, if he acknowledges no determinate truth in God's decree, he erred
shamefully.

TITLE FIVE

On the WILL OF GOD

and its certain execution

It is asked whether decrees, free volitions, and actions in God are something
different from God's essence; whether this statement of our theologians is
false, “Whatever is in God is God”; and likewise, whether there are any
accidents in God. The Arminians affirm it, especially Vorstius, *Apology*, page
43.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 170 | PDF p. 221]

We deny it. 1. Although God's volitions are not extrinsic denominations but
God's vital actions, they nevertheless include two things: first, God's
essence comporting itself in a given manner; second, a relation of reason to
things willed outside God. This can be said in its own manner concerning God's
external acts. Thus God's volition is only God willing through himself and
through his essence, and not through a volitional power distinct from the act,
as it is in us. The things willed externally—indeed, things created and
preserved, and God's works produced externally—place nothing new in God. In
the same way, being on the right or on the left places nothing in a person,
but presupposes a new position of another body outside the person, at his right
or left side.

2. For whatever is added to God besides or beyond his essence is either a
substance or an accident. No substance can be added to God's infinite essence,
because then he would not be infinite. Nor can any accident come newly to God,
for there are no accidents in him, because there can be no composition in him
from an accident and a subject. No new qualification can come to God through
the arrival or departure of a new accident, because God would thereby be
changed.

ROMANS 9:19

Who has resisted his will?

It is asked whether there is in God an antecedent and consequent will. The
Arminians affirm it.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 171 | PDF p. 222]

But 1. If the antecedent will concerned an end proposed by God, and the
consequent will concerned means to the end, we would acknowledge the
distinction.

2. If the antecedent will were only delight in a thing pleasing in itself,
such that it coincided with the approving will of sign which obliges us to our
duty, and the consequent will coincided with the will of good pleasure, we
would embrace the distinction in this sense.

But for the Arminians, the antecedent will is a natural desire in God devoid of
efficacy, by which he naturally desires the obedience and salvation of all
mortals but does not effect it at all, and that in so far as they are creatures
who have yet merited nothing. The consequent will is that by which God wills
the obedience and salvation of certain people if, namely, they have shown
themselves worthy of calling; otherwise, he denies them the external means and
salvation. But this distinction must not be tolerated.

1. For if God antecedently wills that all obey and be saved, and yet few obey
and are saved, then there will be in God an ineffectual will which is not armed
with infinite power, and he will not do whatever he wills in heaven and on
earth. This is contrary to Romans 9:19 and Psalm 135:6.

2. For in that case God does not have mercy upon those whom he wills, and he
does not harden those whom he wills, according to Romans 9:18. For the
antecedent will is not free, while the consequent will is restricted by the
immutable rule of justice, by which God cannot but act according to the
principle of mer—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 172 | PDF p. 223]

—its and demerits unless he should wish to renounce his justice.

3. The natural will by which God, from an impulse of nature, wishes creatures
well is eternal. It follows from this that God even today wills the obedience
and salvation of devils and of reprobate human beings bound to eternal
torments, which is absurd.

4. For it is absurd for God antecedently to will that something be done by
those whom, foreknowing that they would finally resist grace, he peremptorily
and irrevocably reprobated, or to will that salvation befall them. For in human
beings this love of a thing which we know we will never obtain also smacks of
imperfection and unhappiness. Therefore, there is no such love in God.

5. For the antecedent will, when fulfilled, is changed into the consequent
will; or, when not fulfilled and deprived of its end, it ceases to be a will.
So miserable human beings cease to desire further things which they have often
wished for, and their wishes vanish. This makes God mutable.

6. For one who cannot obtain his wishes is not absolutely blessed. But the
antecedent will makes God such a being.

7. For the antecedent will is rash and devoid of knowledge of a preintended
end; and, as

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 173 | PDF p. 224]

the Arminians maintain, through the antecedent will God intends many things
without any foreknowledge of the end. A will devoid of knowledge, however, is
a brutish, blind, and rash appetite, inferior even to a sensitive appetite.

8. Antecedently to will salvation for those for whom salvation has not been
procured by Christ's merits, and who have not yet completed the course of
obedience, is as foreign to justice—according to Arminius's rule of justice
regulating God's will—as for God to decree a crown to someone who has not yet
fought. According to him, this sins against justice.

9. With just as solid a reason God antecedently wills the eternal destruction
of all mortals, because it is a good of justice, as he wills the salvation of
all because it is a good of the creature. Indeed, according to Arminius, the
good of the creature outweighs the good of justice.

10. For God not only ceases to will what he once willed antecedently, but also,
from justice, positively does not will that whose contrary he previously
willed, prayed for, and swore that he wished to do.

11. The antecedent will is properly called his wish. By the same reasoning,
there will be in God a vain hope for a thing which never occurs and grief over
a lost thing which he willed to occur.

12. Like an unwilling shipwrecked man casting merchandise into the sea, God is
compelled to turn the sails from the antecedent will to the consequent will,
just as a father is compelled by the incorrigible obstinacy of the son whom he
once loved uniquely to disinherit him.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 174 | PDF p. 225]

13. The consequent will concerning the impious and hypocrites, who constitute
by far the greatest number of human beings, is true repentance born from grief
over not obtaining the thing antecedently willed. They therefore teach that
God punishes unwillingly and contrary to his natural inclination.

14. The antecedent will is doubtful, uncertain, and suspended upon the outcome
of the creature's obedience.

15. God's will cannot be resisted. But human beings and devils resist the
antecedent will every day.

16. By the antecedent will—that is, by a natural affection—God willed to create
many myriads of worlds, angels, and human beings. Without any reason, these
wills remain forever frustrated in God.

ROMANS 9:18

He has mercy upon whom he wills, and whom he wills he hardens.

It is asked whether showing mercy and doing good is essential to God and as
necessary as it is for the sun to give light. The Englishman Jackson affirms
it with the ancient Anabaptists.

We deny it. 1. For God retains freedom in distributing what is his as he wills.
Matthew 20:15.

2. For if it were necessary for God to show mercy in such a way that he removed
and wholly overcame the final hardness of all, it would also certainly occur.
But experience and Scripture teach the contrary.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 175 | PDF p. 226]

3. For without any force being inflicted upon his nature, God does not will to
have mercy upon many, but, as the apostle says, wills to harden them, although
they are not worse than others in nature and demerits.

It is asked whether God punishes human beings unwillingly and contrary to his
natural inclination to show mercy. The Remonstrants affirm it.

We deny it. 1. For acts of justice are as consonant with God's holy nature as
acts of mercy, and the former are as natural to him as the latter. Exodus
34:6–7.

2. For God laughs at the destruction of the impious just as properly—if there
is no figure here—as he rejoices with gladness over the salvation of the
godly. Proverbs 1:26.

3. Although the exercise of mercy is more agreeable to God's goodness toward
us, nevertheless the attribute of justice is just as essential as mercy.
Exodus 34:6–7.

4. God does all things according to the counsel of his will. Therefore, he
does nothing unwillingly or contrary to his inclination. Ephesians 1:11.

JAMES 1:17

With whom there is no change, nor shadow of turning.

It is asked whether God is immutable. The Arminians deny that he is immutable.
Corvinus, *Against Molina* 6.5, folio 10: “It can be said that hypothetical
decrees, taken separately in their parts, change, because the condition, with
respect to a human being, is either fulfilled or not fulfilled, and—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 176 | PDF p. 227]

—thus it either exists for some time, ceases to exist even for a long time,
and begins again.” Thus Vorstius, *Friendly Reply*, chapter 1, page 6. In this
way they teach that God fails in his intention and that the primary intention
changes into a secondary intention. *Apology*, chapter 9, folio 105: “Human
beings can render their election void and frustrated”—and “render God's
counsel void.” They distinguish election into peremptory and irrevocable, and
nonperemptory and revocable.

We teach that no change befalls God. No subjective change befalls him, because
nothing can be added to or taken from the infinite, nor can anything equally
good befall him. Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 1, folio
6. Nor can terminative mutability be attributed to God, because he says of
himself, “I am who I am.” Things which change neither are nor remain what they
are. Terminative immutability is that by which God's intellect and will are
not changed by a change in the object understood and willed, or by changed
effects outside God. This is not simply because God does not change his
judgment and nothing begins to be good to God which did not previously seem
good to him, as the scholastics teach. This reasoning does not satisfy me,
because God beholds all possible things and wills them from sheer freedom, not
because of goodness which he sees in the things themselves. For the things
willed are not the formal ground of willing for God; indeed, God does not will
things outside himself because they are good, but from his own intrinsic free—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 177 | PDF p. 228]

—dom. Therefore, change from passion or moral inconstancy does not follow
because things begin to be good to God when they previously seemed not good to
him. Rather, because sheer τὸ velle (“willing”) would be changed into sheer τὸ
nolle (“not willing”) from his own intrinsic freedom, there would therefore be
a terminative change in God. Indeed, if God correctly knew two things, knew
one as a greater good and chose it, and afterward knew the other as a lesser
good and chose it after rejecting the former, there would be no change in
God's intellect and consequently no change in the will from different acts of
knowing, because he knows each object equally. Nevertheless, there would be a
change, because God's will would not comport itself in the same manner now
toward this object as it previously did toward that one. But Scripture argues
from God's immutability to the immutability of the thing willed. Therefore, if
he willed something and afterward did not will it, there would be a terminative
change in him. Malachi 3:6: “Because I, Jehovah, do not change, therefore you
sons of Jacob are not consumed.” Therefore, if things outside God were changed
and comported themselves otherwise than God had once decreed that they should,
God would be terminatively changed. Consequently, if the sons of Jacob, whom
God decreed were not to be consumed, had been consumed, Jehovah would change.
So also 1 Samuel 15:29: “The Strength of Israel will not act deceitfully, nor
will he repent.” Therefore, God's determination to reject you and place David
upon the throne stands. Therefore, if Saul had not been re—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 178 | PDF p. 229]

—jected from the throne, whom God willed to be rejected, the Strength of Israel
would have acted deceitfully and repented. Therefore, terminative change must
not be ascribed to God, nor can God will glory today for one for whom he did
not will glory yesterday.

2. For Psalm 33:10 says, “Jehovah renders the counsel of the nations void; he
breaks the thoughts of the peoples.” Verse 11: “But the counsel of Jehovah
stands forever, the thoughts of his heart to all generations.” Isaiah 46:10:
“My counsel shall stand, and I will do all my pleasure.” Romans 9:19: “For who
is there who resists his will?” Hebrews 6:17: “God, willing to show the
immutability of his counsel,” and so forth.

3. God is not in potency to anything, but from eternity his will was determined
toward everything willable and not-willable, although things outside God could
have comported themselves otherwise. For there is nothing willable and nothing
not-willable which God has not either willed or not willed from eternity. Nor
does he begin to will something anew or not to will it anew.

4. Vorstius's statement about God in his notes on disputation 6, theses 34–35,
is blasphemous: “But concerning the things which God leaves indefinite”—namely,
the things which fall under our free choice—“there is no certain will of God.”
For God's will would be in equilibrium and at a crossroads until contingent
things actually existed or did not exist, and the will would remain changeable
at the pleasure of the contingently acting cause. This is what the
Remonstrants said above when they judged that future contin—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 179 | PDF p. 230]

—gents can neither be known nor be necessarily future with respect to the
divine will.

5. It is no objection that God willed ceremonial worship from eternity and
that this worship has now ceased, because from eternity and unto eternity he
wills that worship to endure for a time. It is not thereby proved that his
volition is temporal or has now vanished, because he now wills that worship
for a time.

6. For it is blasphemous that God should will something anew or not will it
anew—as if God should begin anew to will and destine to glory a person whom he
had not destined to glory from eternity, and in turn cease to destine Peter to
glory, whom he had destined to glory from eternity. For in this way true
repentance in God, a new volition, and a new frustrated intention would succeed
many other old volitions which were previously in him. Thus our theologians
argue that God is not mutable because nothing can appear new to his knowledge
or volition, such that he judges good what he previously judged not good on
account of some new reason arising in the object. But if, on account of a new
reason arising in the object—because Peter believes and then does not
believe—God loves for eternal glory Peter whom he previously hated unto death,
or the reverse, then he is truly changed, as all our theologians argue. By the
same reasoning, there is no primary intention in God in which he sometimes
fails and which is succeeded by a secon—

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 180 | PDF p. 231]

—dary intention. 1. For Job 23:13 says, “What God's mind desires, that he
effects.”

2. For God's counsels are eternal and immutable. Psalm 33:11; Isaiah 46:10.

3. For to fail in one's end is misery and punishment in creatures. Job 22:28;
Psalm 33:10; Isaiah 8:9; Job 5:12–13.

4. God's will cannot be resisted, Daniel 4:35; Romans 9:19; Psalm 113:5, even in
things which occur through contingent causes, as those passages prove.

5. For it is foreign to infinite wisdom to will, intend, desire, pray, entreat,
and wish for something—as the adversaries teach—which he knows he will never
obtain, when he could obtain it if he willed. Such a thing is not found in a
prudent human being.

6. For Paul raises the same objection in Romans 9:6: “Has God's word failed?”
Has God's Word and the covenant entered into with Abraham concerning the
attainment of salvation failed? That is, has God's intention expressed in that
covenant been rendered void? And in verse 6 he answers that the Word of the
covenant has not failed. He denies this consequence: Israel has failed—namely,
Israel according to the flesh—therefore God's covenant and intention fail.
For God's intention was to save, not the children according to the flesh, but
the children of promise.

7. For it is the greatest imperfection and dependence of the divine will that,
when shaken from its primary end, it should intend another end *per accidens*,
as though overcome by the creature.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 181 | PDF p. 232 | stop before MATTH. 6.10.]

8. A secondary intention presupposes that something happens outside God's
intention and is therefore fortuitous with respect to God.

9. For God does not cease calling even obstinate people and those who have
fallen into the sin against the Holy Spirit. Does he therefore intend their
obedience and salvation? If he does not intend it, then, according to the
adversaries, he calls them with malicious deceit and a feigned and counterfeit
will. If he does intend it, God cannot have a primary intention here, as the
adversaries admit. Therefore, this distinction has no validity here, and by
the same reasoning neither does it have validity in the calling of others.

10. For a primary intention changed into a secondary intention entails a
change of the divine will.
