VERSE 2. [sic; verse 1] Observe my determination—the king’s determination—but
in accordance with the obligation of the oath of God.

The other part follows, concerning a certain part or kind of this wisdom,
which operates in duties toward one’s neighbor, and specifically toward the
parent—the magistrate of the fatherland. Earlier, in chapter 5,
Solomon proposed to describe that wisdom which is encompassed by the first
table and is exercised in the true worship of God. Accordingly, in this place
he appropriately opposes to vanity the wisdom and fear of God that are seen
in the obedience owed to magistrates. He therefore commands that the king’s
mouth be observed—that is, by a metonymy of the efficient cause, the king’s
command. Thus the true and lawful obedience shown to Rulers consists in

observing their commands, not in the conjecture of others about the king’s
will or in a figment of our own mind. For in this way it will often happen
that people expecting payment and reward are instead subjected to punishment.

We read that this happened in practice to the Amalekite who, supposing that
he would find favor with David because he said that he had struck Saul down,
brought the penalty of death upon himself, 2 Samuel 1. The same thing happened
to Rechab and Baanah, the sons of Rimmon. After cutting off Ish-bosheth’s head
and bringing it to David, they were put to death in place of the reward they
had almost already received in hope, 2 Samuel 4.

But if lawful obedience among human beings is defined by the command of those
who rule—even though subjects sometimes know better what ought to be done than
the rulers themselves know what they command—how much more must we attempt
nothing in obedience to God without his own command, since he alone knows
what it lies upon us to do. Thus the intentions people call good cannot fail
to displease God.

He further describes the obedience of subjects with a precaution attached:
provided that it does not conflict with the oath by which we are bound to
divine authority. For there is no king in the whole world to whom absolute
dominion without any exception belongs. That obedience is rightly owed only
to God. Therefore, if Kings command anything that conflicts with God and his
will, we must answer with the Apostles: “Whether it is right to obey you
rather than God, judge for yourselves.”

We should also set before ourselves the example of Saul’s servants, who,
though commanded to rush upon God’s Priests, refused to do it. Christ himself,
the most perfect example of rendering obedience to those entitled to it, was
asked by his mother about matters in which she had no right to command and
rebuffed her with some sharpness of speech. This becomes all the more evident
if we consider the punishments undergone by those who obeyed kings while
neglecting God, 2 Kings 1.

As for his statement that the obedience owed to a Magistrate must be measured
by the standard of the oath by which a person is bound to God, from this we
understand that we are all bound by a Sacrament to obey God. For there is a
mutual pledge between God and us. Just as God undertook to be a gracious God
to us not only by promise but also by a Sacrament, so we have bound ourselves
to obedience to God by both promise and oath. We must therefore exercise care
lest we be charged as guilty of perjury.

It is utterly disgraceful if anyone breaks faith pledged to human beings with
an oath; on that account he is judged unfit for every civil and Ecclesiastical
office. Of how great and atrocious an offense, then, will the person be held
guilty who violates faith pledged to God and confirmed by oath? Therefore,
whenever people solicit us to perpetrate something contrary to God’s will,
the oath existing between God and us should come to our mind—an oath also
sealed by the reception of the Sacraments. In just this way, we read that
God’s people of old—men, women, and finally all who understood the force of
the oath and of the covenant established by oath—sealed their covenant made
with God and confirmed by oath, Nehemiah 10:1–2, 29.

To turn people away from disobedience and rebellion, Solomon applies the ax
to the root of rebellion by telling them not to allow themselves to be swept
along by a disturbed mind. It is in this way that most people fall away from
the subjection they owe: they depart from duty through anger, indignation,
ambition, and greed for gain.

For example, one person, considering himself promoted less than his worth,
seizes upon this as a reason to shake off the yoke of subjection. Another does
so because he has not been compensated for the service he rendered; yet
another because the ruler has inflicted loss and injury upon him; and another
because he persuades himself that by flying over to someone else’s party an
open avenue to gain or honors will lie before him.

All these things are done in a disturbed manner and not without confused
agitation of mind. It is not improbable that Joab, though otherwise an
illustrious commander, underwent something of this kind. For when he reflected
that Amasa, who had stood on Absalom’s side, had been appointed by David as
commander and general in his place, he was moved by the indignity of the
matter and attached himself to Adonijah, whom David did not wish to succeed
to the kingdom.

Many of the Israelites, especially the Levites, abandoned Jeroboam’s kingdom,
fled to the allegiance of the King of Judah, and lived under his wings. But
they did not do this in a disturbed manner. Rather, they did it because the
worship of God had been utterly overthrown in Israel but flourished in Judah
under the rule of its kings.
