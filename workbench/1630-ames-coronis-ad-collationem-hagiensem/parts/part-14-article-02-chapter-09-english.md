### Chapter IX

#### Eighth argument

Page 198.

For those to whom Christ was made Reconciler, he was also made Intercessor, for
these two parts of Christ’s office ought not to be disjoined (Rom. 8:3–4; 1
John 2:2). But Christ was not made intercessor for all and each human being
(John 17:9, 19–20).

The Remonstrants forge a distinction concerning intercession, which they make
twofold: one universal, which is for the whole world (Isa. 53:12; Luke 23:34),
but the other particular, which is for the faithful (John 17; Rom. 8:34).
Therefore, they say that the proposition is not true with respect to every
kind of intercession. 1. I said that this distinction was forged by them
because I believe it is nowhere found in any suitable author, but was devised
by them for the present occasion. 2. I also call it empty because, in making a
distinction, it obscures the matter with empty words and does not explain it
by a real division. They say that intercession is universal for all and
particular for the faithful; but what it is to intercede universally,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 154 | printed p. 144]

and what it is to intercede particularly, they do not say. In this way anyone
could, through the labels of a distinction and empty phrases—“simply” and “in
a certain respect,” “broadly” and “strictly,” “generally” and
“particularly”—untie every knot whatever. 3. They can bring forward no
testimony from Holy Scripture to prove that universal intercession. Isaiah
53:12 says that he intercedes for transgressors, but not for all and each;
rather, for those many who are mentioned there and whom he is also said to
justify in verse 11: “My righteous servant will justify many,” that is, the
faithful (Rom. 3:26). Nor is that other passage, Luke 23:34—“Forgive them”—any
more to the point. For this prayer is not made for all, but for the very people
crucifying him; and not simply for all and each of them, but only for those who
sinned out of ignorance. This is expressly stated in Christ’s prayer and
confirmed in Acts 3:17. Moreover, since Christ is always heard by the Father
(John 11:42), there is no doubt that this prayer was fulfilled (Acts 2:37;
also Acts 3 and 5). As for the rest, who persisted in unbelief and impiety to
the end of life, Christ did not will to pray for them (John 17:9), nor could he
consistently with that rule of the apostle (1 John 5:16). For he himself knew
what was in man (John 2:25; John 6:64). 4. If Christ intercedes universally
for all and each, then he also rose again, ascended, and sat at the right hand
of the Father universally for the same people, for these things must be
joined. But the Remonstrants denied above, on pages 188–189, that these things,
together with intercession, were done universally for all.

They affirm that Christ intercedes and prays for all as the high priests who
were his types used to do, but that he does not pray for the same thing, nor at
the same time: for all he prays that the Father forgive their sins and that
they believe (John 17:21); for the faithful, not for all, that they be kept in
his name, be one as the Father and he are, and be kept from evil. Here there
are nearly as many errors as words. For 1. when

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 155 | printed p. 145]

they concede that Christ intercedes for all as the high priests who were his
types used to do—priests who used to offer sacrifice for the twelve tribes
and the chosen people, and likewise used to pray for all those for whom they
offered, as is manifest from the whole history of the Old Testament—this does
not allow a universal offering and an unequal intercession to be attributed
to Christ. 2. They teach that Christ procures the same thing for all and yet
does not pray for the same thing for all. This is such a paradox that it does
not have even any likeness to truth. He who did what is greater by giving
himself for all and procuring every good for them by such great merits—will he
not also ask for those same things for all in his prayers? Does he not have a
perpetual will and constant zeal to provide to all the same things which he
procured for all through his death? Does he not present himself before the
Father as one who endured his wrath for all those for whom he suffered, and
with the same disposition as aim or end? These things surpass all reason and
belief. 3. If what they relate is true, it is plain that the same thing is
requested for all as for the faithful. For they say that he prayed for all
that they might believe, in verse 21, and for the faithful that they might be
saved, be one, and be kept from evil. For human beings are saved through faith
(Eph. 2:8), are one through faith (Eph. 4:13), stand through faith (2 Cor.
1:24), and are delivered from evil (1 Pet. 5:9). But one who truly and
sincerely prays for faith also prays for the fruits and effects of faith. 4.
They openly contradict themselves when they deny that Christ prayed for the
world and for the faithful at the same time, and yet say that in the same
verse, namely verse 21, he prayed for the faithful that they might be one and
for the world that it might believe. 5. They are contrary both to themselves
and to Arminius in contending that in these very prayers in John 17 Christ
interceded for the whole world, since they themselves had said in the former
distinction that the particular intercession

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 156 | printed p. 146]

is described in John 17, which pertains to none but the disciples and those
who would believe through their word. And Arminius, in his *Oration on the
Priesthood of Christ*, testifies that this chapter, John 17, contains a
perpetual rule for the prayers and intercession which Christ offers to the
Father in heaven, and that we may conclude more certainly than certain
concerning that prayer that it will never be rejected.

They say that Musculus, Aretius, and Gualterus support them, holding that
Christ did not pray for the world because he foresaw the world’s unbelief.
Nothing is less true. For the position of Musculus is that Christ, in praying,
distinguishes between the elect and the reprobate; and not only that he prays
for the elect and does not pray for the world, but that he also expressly
declares that he prays for the former and does not pray for the world, calls
his own those given to him by the Father, and calls the reprobate and
unbelievers not given to him by the Father “the world.” Gualterus also
expressly calls those for whom Christ prays the elect and those enrolled from
eternity in God’s register. Although he describes the others by their
obstinacy, he nevertheless manifestly sets them against those elect as
reprobates. Nor does Aretius have anything to the contrary. But if I wished to
summon those Doctors who, in handing down the meaning of this passage, oppose
the Remonstrants’ position, we do not lack men to set against those three in
any court. It will be worthwhile to bring forward one very ancient author who
entirely annuls the Remonstrants’ plea: namely Cyril, book 11, chapter 19,
where he judges that in this passage the Lord wished to separate his own from
those who were not his and were not drawn by the Father, to whom he does not
grant the good of his mediation. Furthermore, if in verse 9 he refused to pray
for the world because he foresaw that it would not believe in him, then
certainly in the latter part of the same prayer he would never ask that the
same world believe, which nevertheless these men, fighting with themselves,
affirm. In the rest of their response nothing new is brought forward, nothing
that was not sufficiently refuted before.

Let it therefore suffice to have said these things concerning those arguments
by which

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 157 | printed p. 147]

our men attacked the Remonstrants’ second thesis. Certain corollaries remain,
along with what they call prescriptions; these too must be touched upon
briefly, together with the agreement of the Confession and Catechism.

The first corollary was of this kind: If, by God’s counsel, Christ really
procured reconciliation with God and remission of sins for every individual
human being without exception in such a way that they were restored to the
state of reconciliation and grace, then no one is born a child of wrath by
nature or liable to death and condemnation on account of original sin, but
all human beings are born in the state of grace.

The Remonstrants give various answers. 1. They do not deny that this inference
would be just if they themselves said that all and each had been restored to
the state of reconciliation and saving grace; but they utterly deny that they
hold this. Yet this is the same as if they said, “We deny in words but prove
in reality what is objected here.” For when I investigated the state of this
question, I proved clearly enough that this is the genuine position of at
least some of the Remonstrants. Nor is it now obscure what many of them strive
to persuade themselves and others concerning original sin. 2. They say that
this argument is not rightly drawn from grace to nature. But by “nature” here
is understood the state in which each person is born. Now if all are received
into the state of grace, grace not only accords well with this nature but must
also be most intimately joined to it. On this reasoning the Remonstrants also
teach that all and each are born free by nature and from the bondage of sin,
and partakers of grace, since they say that all possess free will from birth
for spiritual goods, yet admit that no one in whom sin exercises dominion is
constituted free in this way. 3. If this consequence holds for all human
beings from the general redemption which they posit, they say that it holds
equally

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 158 | printed p. 148]

for the elect, whom we hold to have been restored through Christ to the state
of reconciliation. But the cases are unequal. For they, or at least some of
them, teach that all and each have been restored to grace in such a way that
no liability and no personal fault remains until it has been contracted by a
new transgression. But we say that corruption and fault, together with
liability, adhere to elect infants, although God has determined with himself
to remove all these things from them in his own time and manner. We believe
that the same persons are children both of wrath and of grace in differing
respects. The Remonstrants, on the contrary, say that it is impossible for
someone to be at once a child of grace and of wrath in any differing respect
whatever. This proposition is Nicolaus’s, on page 60. One may add to it
Borreus’s assumption: “God has received all infants into the grace of
reconciliation,” that is, they are children of grace, and the conclusion will
immediately emerge: therefore no infants are children of wrath.

The second corollary is that there is no election of certain individual human
beings to salvation. The Remonstrants answer: “It is absurd to oppose the
universality of Christ’s passion to the election of certain individual human
beings, since Christ’s passion precedes but election follows.” But it was
proved above that this order is inverted. The consequence appears firm for
this reason. The Father’s will in sending Christ was that through him all and
only those whom he had given him should be saved (John 6:39). This purpose
remains constant and immutable, for all whom the Father gave the Son come to
him and are saved (John 6:37). Therefore, if all were given to Christ, and not
certain individuals in preference to others, then the purpose to save, or
election, pertains to all, not to certain individuals.

The third is that it depends chiefly upon human choice, and not upon grace
alone, that procured reconciliation is applied to someone. The Remonstrants
deny the consequence,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 159 | printed p. 149]

which nevertheless has its evident force from this: the application to these
rather than those must depend either upon God’s will or upon the will of human
beings. Those who say that in universal redemption and grace God equally wills
the salvation of all until he has foreseen in someone an act of the human will
tending to that end deny that it depends upon God’s will. Therefore it will
depend chiefly upon the human will.

Certain statements were selected from the Confession and Catechism and set
against the Remonstrants’ thesis. 1. From Confession, article 16, where it is
said that “God demonstrated himself to be just by leaving certain persons in
that destruction into which they were cast headlong by the fall of the first
man.” The Remonstrants answer that Christ’s having died for all does not
conflict with this. What that article says does not in fact conflict with the
sufficiency of Christ’s death. But for all to be left in the first destruction
and for a demonstration of justice to be sought through this abandonment
conflicts with the intention to deliver all, with reconciliation, and with the
purpose of demonstrating saving mercy toward all. 2. From article 21: “By
this offering the faithful are consecrated and perfected, and therefore he was
called Jesus, because he would save his people from their sins.” The
Remonstrants retort that they acknowledge that the faithful, and they alone,
are sanctified, but that this occurs through the application of Christ, and
that from this he was called Jesus the Savior. But they refuse to see that the
article says that the faithful are consecrated through the offering, so that
in the offering a special account was taken of those who were to be faithful,
and once the offering was accomplished, it could not happen otherwise than
that these same people should be sanctified. The Remonstrants, on the
contrary, defend that the offering was entire, complete, and perfect without
any special intention of this consecration, and that Christ was offered in
such a way that he could nevertheless have been applied to no one; and that
the name “Jesus” could have been rendered an empty designation if no one had
wished to be saved through him.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 160 | printed p. 150]

From question 48 of the Catechism it was brought forward that “God’s wrath and
eternal condemnation rest upon unbelievers while they so persevere.” From
question 86, that “we are renewed by Christ after we have been redeemed by his
blood.” From questions 1 and 34, that “those who have been redeemed by his
precious blood are Christ’s own or his peculiar possession.” The Remonstrants
say that none of these things is contrary to them, because they pertain to
application and must be explained from questions 20 and 37. What that
explanation would be they do not tell, nor do I care to divine. But if God’s
wrath so resting upon some that they were never delivered from it, all those
who were redeemed by Christ’s blood being renewed through his Spirit, and all
the redeemed being Christ’s own peculiar possession—if, I say, these things
are not contrary to a universal redemption, reconciliation, and grace which
is ineffective in many as to the outcome and, in short, makes no peculiar or
proper difference of some from others, then those things are not opposed or
contradictory of which one affirms and the other denies the same thing.

To the agreement of the Confession and Catechism was also added the testimony
of certain professors of theology at Leiden, who in 1595, in the case of
Cornelius Wiggerus, judged that “the doctrine of the universal restoration of
the whole human race in Christ and the extension of his benefit to all the
descendants of the first Adam departs from the sense of Scripture and from the
concordant position of the ancient and the new church.” The Remonstrants
answer that this allegation is both beside the point and unsuitable. Why
beside the point? “Because we do not posit a universal restoration of that
kind, nor do we pour out the benefits of the latter Adam upon all the
descendants of the first Adam.” Not of that kind? Why then do they not explain
of what kind the difference is? The most weighty Junius and his colleagues,
who marked the dogmas of Cornelius with a just censure, were not quarreling
over words and expressions. In the theses themselves they can indicate no
greater difference

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 161 | printed p. 151]

than there is between an egg and an egg. Why unsuitable? “Because if the
judgment of the whole catholic Christian church, by which the position of
their opponents was long ago condemned as Pelagian (Faustus of Riez, book 1,
chapter 16, *On Grace and Free Choice*), does not harm them, much less will the
judgment of three Leiden professors harm us, even if our position had been
condemned by them.” That they value these three professors of theology so
little would not be so surprising had there not also been a fourth present
with those three who subscribed to the same censure—not indeed an academic
professor, but nevertheless a theologian and the Remonstrants’ own leader,
namely Johannes Utenbogardus. Memory of his own judgment and profession ought
to have held him back somewhat from fostering the same error which he had
recently endeavored to extinguish, lest many hold him to be self-condemned.
But their casting disgrace upon our position as though it had Pelagius for its
author and had long ago been condemned by the whole Christian church as
Pelagian could only have been spat by Pelagian shamelessness into the face of
the church. For that heretic and his followers used in this way to object to
the orthodox the unanimous consent of the eastern church and the authority of
Ambrose and similar writers, as though the orthodox had received from them
the errors which they defended (*On Grace against Pelagius*, book 1, chapter
43; *Against the Two Letters of the Pelagians*, book 4, chapter 8; *Against
Julian*, book 1), and as though the conspiracy of a few ruined Manichaeans
which opposed them was condemned by all those authorities. For in this
reproachful style, as Augustine testifies, they used to call their opponents
Manichaeans. It is of precisely the same effrontery to call our position
Pelagian and to say that it was condemned by that name. Augustine, *Against
Julian the Pelagian*, book 3, chapter 3: “Has God redeemed the whole world?
Behold, we see human beings living in their sins. How shall we think those
redeemed whom we see remaining captive? They are redeemed, but they are not
delivered. These are

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 162 | printed p. 152]

the monstrosities of your positions, these the unexpected mysteries of new
dogmas, these the paradoxes of the Pelagian heretics.” Prosper, in the letter
which he wrote to Augustine concerning the remnants of the Pelagian heresy,
repeatedly insists in express words that this is their—the Pelagians’—
definition and profession: “The propitiation which is in the sacrament of
Christ’s blood has been offered to all human beings without exception; our
Lord Jesus Christ died for the whole human race; and absolutely no one was
excepted from redemption by his blood, even if he should pass through this
whole life with a mind utterly estranged from him.” Likewise, in the Council
of Valence, in Lothair’s time, which was also assembled against the Pelagians,
we read that the same definition—which is the Remonstrants’ position—of
universal redemption was condemned as Pelagian (*Councils*, volume 3), in
these words: “Likewise, concerning redemption by Christ’s blood, on account of
the excessive error which has arisen concerning this matter, so that certain
persons, as their writings indicate, define that it was poured out even for
those impious people who, from the beginning of the world up to the Lord’s
passion, died in their impiety and were punished with eternal condemnation,
contrary to that prophetic saying, ‘I will be your death, O death; I will be
your bite, O hell’: it pleases us that this be held simply and faithfully and
taught according to the truth of the Gospel and the apostles—that we should
hold that this price was given for those concerning whom the Lord himself
said, ‘As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of
Man be lifted up, that everyone who believes in him should not perish but have
eternal life. For God so loved the world that he gave his only-begotten Son,
that everyone who believes in him should not perish but have eternal life.’
And the apostle says, ‘Christ was offered once to exhaust the sins of many.’”
When the Remonstrants have read these things and cannot deny them, they appear
to betray their own conscience and reputation when they ascribe a plainly
contrary

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 163 | printed p. 153]

position to the Pelagians so that, by this means, they might somehow smear an
unjust slander upon their opponents. They may deservedly fear the Remmian law
unless they prove an accusation of this kind by some trustworthy testimony.
But they produce a single witness, Faustus of Riez, *On Grace and Free
Choice*, book 1, chapter 16. What sort of man? The standard-bearer of the
Semi-Pelagians, a cloaker of heresy, anathematized and refuted by many of the
orthodox. See Margarinus de la Bigne, *Library of the Fathers*, latest edition,
volume 4; Baronius, volume 6, at the year 490. But let us dismiss this infamous
witness, who bears testimony in his own cause and is by every law incompetent
to testify. Let us hear the testimony itself. Does he say in chapter 16 that
our position was condemned as Pelagian? The Remonstrants indeed say this, but
Faustus does not. In that whole chapter there is no mention of Pelagius or of
any Pelagian error. Rather, in that place, as also from chapter 3 onward, he
disputes against the enemies of the Pelagians—Augustine, Prosper, Hilary, and
the like—and proclaims, no less shamelessly than falsely, that their position
on this matter was condemned by the whole church. Nor was it unusual for the
Pelagians to cast this very position against the orthodox in hateful words, as
may be seen in the articles falsely imposed upon Augustine by them, the first
of which concerns the death of Christ. This entire ill-fated accusation has
therefore vanished, except among those most unjust judges who take no account
of either the witness or his testimony.
