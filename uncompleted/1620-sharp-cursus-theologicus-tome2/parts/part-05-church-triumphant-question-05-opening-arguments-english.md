### QUESTION V

#### On the invocation of the saints

In book 1, chapter 18, *On the Blessedness of the Saints*, Bellarmine asserts
that the saints, whether angels or men, are piously and profitably invoked by
the living.

We, however, say that God alone is to be invoked, and not dead saints or
angels, by the following arguments.

Argument 1. No religious worship is to be instituted without God’s command;
otherwise it will be will-worship and without faith, and consequently also
sin, Romans 14:23. But there is no command, nor even an example, in Scripture
for the invocation of angels and dead saints, but rather prohibitions, Matthew
4:10 and elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument 2. If Christ, when asked by his disciples to teach them to pray,
taught that God alone is to be invoked as Father in Christ, and not the
saints, then they are not to be invoked, because it is unlikely that Christ,
when asked so earnestly by his own, would have wished to conceal anything from
them on this matter. But the former is true, Luke 11:2. Bellarmine’s claim that
Christ taught only what was to be sought, not whom one was to pray to, is
false, because he teaches both.

Argument 3. God plainly forbids the religious worship of angels, Colossians
2:18. Therefore.

Argument 4. If the most holy fathers, now dead and most dear to God, do not
know us, much less do others. But the former is true, Isaiah 63:16; Psalm
27:10. Thus Augustine, in *On the Care to Be Taken for the Dead*, testifies
that the departed do not know what their living

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 22 | printed col. 43 | bsb10175568]

children do. Therefore, consequently, they are not to be invoked.

Argument 5. Those who worship creatures change the truth of God into a lie,
Romans 1:33. Therefore.

Argument 6. We cannot invoke one in whom we do not believe, Romans 10:14. But
we believe in no saint, because God pronounces a curse against those who trust
in men, Jeremiah 17:5. Bellarmine therefore says impiously that the living
believe in the saints and hope in them. Augustine, however, rightly says in
*The City of God*, book 18, chapter 58: *We who are, and are called,
Christians do not believe in Peter, but in the one in whom Peter believes.* If
not in Peter, then not in another departed person. Therefore.

Argument 7. God does not will his glory to be transferred to another, Isaiah
42:8. But invocation is a principal part of God’s glory. Therefore.

Argument 8. Those who invoke the departed acknowledge that they know human
hearts; otherwise they could not understand the prayers of hearts. But he who
knows human hearts is God, 1 Kings 8:39; 2 Chronicles 6:30. Therefore they
acknowledge the departed to be gods. But this is impious. Therefore they are
not to be invoked.

Argument 9. God alone is everywhere. But those who think the departed are to
be invoked would have them be everywhere, because they admit that, at one and
the same moment, they can be invoked in France, Spain, and so forth—indeed, in
individual houses by all Christians, even in silent thought. Therefore those
who think that the saints are to be invoked think them to be gods, which is
absurd. Therefore.

Argument 10. The saints whom they invoke either distinguish hypocritical
prayers from true ones, or hear all alike. If they distinguish them, then they
are searchers of hearts, and consequently gods, because God alone is the
knower of hearts, 1 Kings 8:39. If they hear all alike, then they are equally
beneficent toward the godly and the ungodly. But each alternative is absurd.
Therefore.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 22 | printed col. 44 | bsb10175568]

Argument 11. Scripture directs us, when about to pray, to God alone, Psalm
50:15, and promises a hearing to those who pray in Christ’s name, John 16:23.
Therefore the saints are not to be invoked, unless perhaps we should say that
they are more liberal and less difficult than God and Christ.

Argument 12. The angels refused to be invoked, Revelation 19:10 and 22:8, but
taught that God alone is to be invoked and worshiped. The saints even rejected
genuflection that bore the appearance of divine worship, Acts 10 and
elsewhere. Therefore the saints, since they are absent from us, are not to be
honored even with genuflection if it bears even the slightest indication of
divine worship, much less are they to be invoked.

Argument 13. If Christ were only a creature, he would not be invoked. Thus
Nestorius, who said that Christ was a mere man and worshiped him, was
condemned for man-worship by the Second Council of Nicaea, article 7.
Athanasius too, in Oration 5 *Against the Arians*, judged the Arians, who deny
that Christ is God and nevertheless worship him, to be idolaters. He says: *We
do not worship a creature—far be it—but the incarnate Lord of created things.*

Argument 14. The Papists invoke many who either never existed in the world or
were not saints, such as Catherine, daughter of King Costus, Christopher who
was twelve cubits tall, and so forth, about whom there is nothing in sacred or
profane history. Indeed, they imitate the pagans when they seek one thing from
one saint and another from another, invoking one against plague, another
against toothache, and so forth.

Argument 15. Jerome, *Against Vigilantius*; Epiphanius, book 3, *Against
Heresies*, on Mary; and Augustine, Letter 43: *What madman*, he says, *ever
worshiped the martyrs?* And afterward: *We do not worship angels or
archangels*, and so forth. *She was a virgin, but was*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 23 | printed col. 45 | bsb10175568]

*not given to us for adoration*, and so forth. *Know*, he says, *that among
Catholic Christians none of the dead is worshiped, but the one God himself.*
