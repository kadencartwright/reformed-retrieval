## Johannes Polyander to the students of sacred theology

Most learned young men, the whole body of doctrine received from the people of
God, by which the body of the church is governed and retained in its duty,
embraces either things to be believed or things to be done. In the former are
contained the doctrines of faith; in the latter, the precepts of life. Both
pertain either publicly to the worship of God or privately to the formation of
morals. All these things were either delivered by the Prophets and Apostles and
are called divine oracles or commands, or were illustrated by the church's
doctors and pastors with genuine interpretations and learned annotations,
which are called ecclesiastical commentaries. The former are God-breathed, or
divinely inspired, and therefore must be approved by all without exception.
The latter were gathered by the industry of very learned men, and so are
subject both to correction by their authors and to the judgment of others.
They should not be received unless they are designed to facilitate an
understanding of divine precepts and a more convenient observance of them; nor
should they be rejected unless they obstruct this use. Just as nothing is more
wholesome for humankind than the former, nothing is more useful than the
latter. For although the reading of the sacred Codex is available to everyone,
especially in our Reformed churches, its meaning is nevertheless not manifest
to everyone everywhere. By God's hidden and venerable counsel there is indeed
great variety among the passages that occur in sacred literature. Some are so
clear that no one of sound mind could call them into doubt;

<!-- 1632 witness | PDF p. 8 | Polyander preface, p. 2 -->

for example, certain historical narratives. Others are more veiled and obscure,
especially in the treatment of certain mysteries, the knowledge of which is
not equally necessary to salvation. Yet in other matters, knowledge of which
is entirely necessary to salvation, nothing is so secret and hidden that it is
not proved by clear testimonies of the sacred page. To establish these things,
skill in rightly handling sacred literature is needed: the idiom of the
language in which it was handed down must be correctly observed, and passages
pertaining to the same matter must be diligently compared. For scarcely any
passage of sacred Scripture is so clear that heretics and perverse men have
not twisted it to another meaning. What could be said more plainly than that
Immanuel, Jesus Christ, was born from the Virgin Mary? Who could conceive
anything else from these words than that our Savior Jesus Christ assumed a
human nature from the seed and substance of the Virgin Mary? Nevertheless,
many are found in these last times who utterly deny it and devise a novel kind
of birth, by which they assert that Christ took nothing from the Virgin.

Meanwhile there are throughout Scripture certain notable passages by which the
chief and necessary doctrines of the true faith are established and the
heresies that undermine them are refuted. Although some ambiguity and doubt
about their meaning may arise among those less practiced in sacred learning,
and may be implanted in their minds by corrupters of sacred literature, it has
nevertheless been divinely ordained that the true and constant meaning of
these passages should be perpetually preserved in Christ's church by the most
trustworthy expositions of highly learned interpreters. To illustrate this by
one or two examples: against heretics who once went astray in interpreting the
word *Logos*, or “Word,” used by the Evangelist John when he asserted that the
*Logos* was in the beginning, ecclesiastical writers taught with one consent
that the expression did not denote God's purpose, a divine power, or some
affection in God, but a nature that participates in reason and is marked by
its own proper characteristics—what the Greeks call a *hypostasis* and the
Latins a person—namely, the only-begotten Son of God, Jesus Christ, who was
with the Father from all eternity,

> **Margin:** John 1:1.

<!-- 1632 witness | PDF p. 9 | Polyander preface, p. 3 -->

himself true and eternal God. Likewise, against other heretics who stumbled
shamefully at the statement that the Word became flesh and supposed that
Christ's divine nature had passed into the human nature, they demonstrated by
many arguments that no other explanation of that statement could be admitted
than that Christ, the Son of God, assumed human nature while the divine nature
remained immutable and unconfused. They also demonstrated by the clearest
arguments that Paul's statement, in which he asserts that through one man sin
came to all people, must be understood not of imitation by example, as the
Pelagians wished, but of contagion by propagation. And although not a few
attacked their sound interpretations, they would not therefore abandon them,
because they were thoroughly convinced that they agreed with the analogy of
faith and with all Scripture.

Some excellent theologians of our own age have followed in their footsteps,
with the greatest labor and untiring study, and with the richest fruit and
great praise. If anyone after Calvin, Beza, Mercerus, and Junius—the leading
recent interpreters—deserves to be placed in their company, it is Thomas
Cartwright, an Englishman of Warwick, formerly Professor of Sacred Theology at
the University of Cambridge. Adorned by God with a singular gift for
interpreting sacred literature, he furnished his fellow Englishmen with
splendid monuments of that gift, written partly in English and partly in
Latin. As a testimony of my goodwill toward you, most distinguished young men,
I now offer you a notable part of these writings—namely, his learned
commentaries on the Proverbs of Solomon. In them he displays with admirable
skill a true method of rightly dividing the Word of God and aptly applying it
to the use of every kind of person. It is to be desired that every preacher,
instructed in the art of educating the flock, should use this method of
teaching in sermons to the people.

First, together with an analysis of the chapters and verses, he accurately
explains the words and the manner of speaking familiar to the Holy Spirit
wherever some obscurity or ambiguity lies hidden. Second, he sets before the
reader in each passage the substance and aim of the sacred text's author.
Third, as occasion offers, he diligently weighs difficulties that arise in
certain places concerning the matters proposed.

<!-- 1632 witness | PDF p. 10 | Polyander preface, p. 4 -->

Fourth, he sets out in the best order the doctrinal, hortatory, and consolatory
inferences that may rightly be gathered from the context. As I learned from
certain learned and trustworthy Englishmen, this Cartwright defended the
English translation of the New Testament, prepared by decree of the most
serene Queen Elizabeth and the nobles of England, against corruptions of the
Anglo-Rheims party. He also composed other writings, polemical as well as
doctrinal, some of which have been published and some of which, as I heard
from those same Englishmen, will shortly be published. Called from his native
country to serve as pastor to the English merchants doing business at Antwerp
and Middelburg, he gave both congregations abundant satisfaction in his
ministry. Staying for some time in our own academy in order to confer with our
theologians, he enjoyed familiar association with the most distinguished
Junius and the elder Trelcatius. After returning to England he discharged the
office of a most serious and vigilant churchman among the Gargefenians.
Finally, worn out by age and study, and having first obtained leave from them,
he returned to Warwick so that he might devote the remaining small portion of
his life to faithfully instructing his own townspeople. There, after completing
all the labors of a good pastor, he fell asleep in a peaceful death and found
the hoped-for rest with his mind stretched fully toward Christ.

I wished to inscribe these lucid and concise commentaries on Solomon's Proverbs
to you, most excellent young men, because you are novices in the same study of
theology, and because your age—committed no less to my care and trust than to
that of my colleagues—daily reminds me to recall you to the reading of this
and similar volumes, so that you may become not only more learned but also
better. I know very well that a large part of your order spends its best hours
unrolling the foolish subtleties of the sophists rather than the solid
writings of orthodox theologians. It neglects the commentaries of pious and
prudent interpreters, which show the method of bringing contemplative theology
into practical theology and sacred meditations into pious actions, and directs
its attention almost solely to understanding the controversies concerning the
five articles—controversies at first known among us to only a few, but now
maintained by many

<!-- 1632 witness | PDF p. 11 | Polyander preface, p. 5 -->

with the fiercest contention. This is not without great sorrow to peaceful
people, danger of a lamentable schism, and serious offense to foreign Reformed
churches. They perceive their doctrine, which agrees with ours, being torn
down through attacks upon several theologians in the biting writings of
certain men; and they foresee that enemies common to us will easily recover
through this new discord the strength that they lost through our former
concord, unless the best and greatest God at last takes pity on us and applies
some fitting remedy to so great a wound.

But to return to the point from which I departed a little while deploring the
misery of our churches before God: I consider it prudent that those especially
whom Christ has appointed as chief shepherds to feed his flock should read the
opposing writings of both parties disputing chiefly about those five articles,
lest they be regarded in this region as novel and foreign teachers. Yet I
cannot approve the plan and practice of those who devote themselves so
completely to examining these five articles that they allot scarcely any
available time to investigating the remaining doctrines of faith taught
throughout sacred Scripture, and therefore wander in ignorance of them as in
a dense fog.

To counter this growing disease as promptly as I am able, I implore you by our
one Lord and Teacher Jesus Christ: withdraw a little from the restless
contentions of certain disputants. In the future devote your age—which until
now has been engaged chiefly on that racecourse of disputants—to quieter
studies. Vigorously exercise the seed of your talent and industry in this
fertile field of Proverbs. Constantly pursue the rule of reverence pleasing to
God and the way of saving righteousness which Cartwright, after Solomon, sets
before each of you to contemplate. Learn from both teachers happily to join
the wisdom of heavenly understanding with its discipline, the fear of God
with love of neighbor, and an upright conscience with true knowledge. If I
perceive that you are disposed in this way, then besides the fact that you
will stir my pen toward a similar kind of writing,

<!-- 1632 witness | PDF p. 12 | Polyander preface, p. 6 -->

you will faithfully discharge the vow of holy obedience that you made to God
when initiated into sacred learning. From this study and pious effort of yours,
the church of Christ, the Prince of Pastors, to which you have dedicated your
labor, will someday receive the fullest fruits and leave them to posterity.

Leiden, 10 January 1617.

Yours in Immanuel,

Johannes Polyander.
