CHAPTER VII. On the comparison of the authority of the Pope and a general
council.

The seventh question is whether a general council is above the Pope, or the
Pope above a council. We, with all the theologians of Paris, nearly all those
of Constance and Basel, and many others, favor the council in this dispute;
Bellarmine, however, favors the Pope.

THESIS I.

Bellarmine. *There are three opinions of the Doctors concerning this question.
The first is that a council is above the Pope. The second is that the Pope is
indeed above a council, yet can submit himself to a council and afterward must
acquiesce in the council’s sentence. The third, almost common opinion is that
the Pope is so far above a council that he cannot even subject himself to its
coercive sentence.*

Protestants. I. The wretched Papists are compelled by this dissension among
their Doctors to be ignorant of their lord, since they necessarily do not know
whether they ought to serve the Pope or the council. II. This very principle
of Roman faith is thereby declared uncertain, since it is not yet settled
among those masters of Israel with whom supreme ecclesiastical power rests:
judgment concerning faith, or the infallible power of propounding and
explaining the truths of faith. To the Papists this is the first ground for
believing and the final thing into which Roman faith is resolved. III. That
unity of which the Pontificians are accustomed to boast so greatly is utterly
destroyed in this first principle. They are so far from agreeing about it that
they must necessarily dissent from one another about all decrees of councils
and rescripts or commands of Pontiffs, so that in all articles of faith they
cannot fail to vary in some way. IV. It is more shameful still that concerning
matters of this kind they have no common opinion, but only one that is *almost
common*. V. For this very *almost common* opinion Bellarmine could bring no
testimony from any of the ancient Scholastics, whom he admits *do not
professedly dispute about this matter*. It is the common opinion of the
parasites of the Papal Curia, not of the Church—not even of that one which is
called Roman.

2. Bellarmine. *The Roman Pontiff is the pastor and head not only of all the
particular churches, but also of the whole universal Church gathered together,
instituted immediately by Christ.*

Protestants. I. That the Roman Pontiff was instituted immediately by Christ as
head of the whole universal Church was previously demonstrated to be false in
the preceding Tome. It is also denied—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n250 | printed p. 33]

—by the most learned Pontificians: Thomas of Walden, Augustine of Ancona,
Scotus, Cordubensis, Báñez, and others, according to Azor, *Moral Institutes*,
part 2, book 5, chapter 11; and even by Bellarmine himself, *On the Supreme
Pontiff*, book 2, chapter 12, where he has these words: *That the Roman bishop,
because he is the Roman bishop, is Peter’s successor arose from Peter’s act,
not from Christ’s original institution.* II. The Parisians reply that although
the Pontiff is a ministerial head with respect to status, he is not so with
respect to the governance of the Church through infallible constitutions of
canons, or with respect to direction and coercive power over the whole
council.

3. Bellarmine. *Supreme ecclesiastical power is not in the Church or a council
when the Pope is removed, either formally or supplementarily.*

Protestants. I. Supreme ecclesiastical power, as power is properly so called,
is in Christ alone. Only ministry and stewardship are in the Church and her
governors. II. As concerns human beings, the Parisians reply, first, that the
Church is governed not by absolute power but by canon; and second, that the
supreme power of establishing those canons is in a general council, even when
the Pope is removed.

4. Bellarmine. *The Supreme Pontiff is simply and absolutely above the
universal Church and above a general council, so that he acknowledges no
judgment on earth above himself. This proposition is almost an article of
faith.*

Protestants. This is not even *almost* an article of Roman faith in France and
among others who agree with the Councils of Constance and Basel. Rather, it is
*almost* contrary to Roman faith and plainly contrary to Christian faith,
since it is the head of Antichristianism.

5. Bellarmine. *The Supreme Pontiff cannot entrust to either a council or any
human being a coercive judgment above himself, but only a discerning one.*

Protestants. He therefore cannot submit himself to the power of the keys in
the sacrament of confession so that his sins may be loosed and bound. For in
confession the confessor exercises jurisdiction—according to the
Pontificians—in the forum of conscience over the person confessing. According
to the Pontificians’ position, the Pope therefore cannot attain the remission
of sins. And yet it can scarcely happen that when he is outside the law in
this way—lawless—he is not more covered with sins than all other human beings.

Arguments which Bellarmine sets against himself.

6. Protestants. Every brother who gives offense can be judged by the Church,
Matthew 18:17. The Pope is a brother. Therefore, he can be judged by the
Church.

Bellarmine. *By “the Church” is understood either the bishop or the assembly—*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n251 | printed p. 34]

*—of the faithful with its head, the bishop. But if the bishop sins, he is to
be brought before a higher church; if the Pope sins, however, there is no
church before which he can be brought.*

Protestants. I. No part of the syllogism is denied, unless perhaps it is denied
that the bishop and the Pope are brothers. No distinction is introduced;
rather, Bellarmine denies the conclusion gratuitously. A defense devoid of
all reasoning agrees perfectly with the Pope’s primacy. It is plain, however,
that “the Church” means there the assembly of which the sinner is a member.

7. Protestants. These words were spoken to Peter, *tell the Church*; therefore
Peter and his successors must sometimes bring sinners before the Church.

Bellarmine 1. *Peter was not yet Pontiff.*

Protestants. That rule regarded not only, nor chiefly, Peter’s present state,
but the one that was to come.

Bellarmine 2. *The Pope can in some manner tell the Church—that is, himself as
president and the Church over which he himself presides—that is, publicly
excommunicate the person who has sinned.*

Protestants. I. The Pope, then, will follow Christ’s precept in this way. After
he alone rebukes the sinner at the first stage, and then does so before
witnesses, which is the second stage, afterward, in place of the third stage,
he will return to a point short of the first by telling himself, and will go
beyond the third by excommunicating the sinner before he has been admonished
by the Church. But who among sane people has ever heard it said that *the
Church* signifies one bishop or the Pope? Or that *to tell the Church* means
to excommunicate? Or that any law prescribes that something should first be
told by someone to others and then *to himself*? The Babylonian primacy of the
Pope must certainly be fortified with monstrous defenses of this kind, so
that the lid may be like the dish.

8. Protestants. The Pope is a member of the Church; therefore he is less than
the Church, because a part is less than the whole.

Bellarmine 1. *If the Church is understood without the Pope, then the Church
is not a whole but a part, greater indeed in magnitude of bulk, but lesser in
magnitude of power or authority.*

Protestants. I. In this comparison the Parisians understand the Church either
as existing with the Pope, and thus as an actual whole in relation to the
Pope, or as without the Pope, and then it is nevertheless the same whole it
was before, even with a relation to the Pope, though a potential and not an
actual relation. II. If it is understood without the Pope, it cannot be a part
of the Pope, nor can it be a part in any other sense than as a whole lacking
one member, as the Pontificians suppose—a member which, as the matter stands,
is a cancer. III. It is a monstrous postulate, gratuitously and crudely thrust
upon us here, that *one bishop considered by himself has greater power and
authority—*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n252 | printed p. 35]

*—than all the remaining bishops together with all the churches over which
they preside.*

Bellarmine 2. *If the Church is understood together with the Pope, then the
authority of the Church is greater extensively but not intensively, just as
the relation stands between a being and God, and between the Church and
Christ.*

Protestants. I. This whole response, as is impiously declared by those
comparisons, makes the Pope the essential head of the Church, not only with
respect to external governance but also with respect to inward influence.
This is such great madness that it is rejected by those who ascribe the most
to the Pope. Thomas Aquinas, part 3, question 8, article 6.

9. Protestants. A rotten member must be amputated if it infects the whole;
therefore the Pope must also be amputated if he infects the Church.

Bellarmine. *This can be done in a natural body and in temporal commonwealths,
because the members depend upon the whole. But the rationale of the Church’s
body is different, since its head received authority not from the body but
from God.*

Protestants. I. It is sophistically taken for granted here that the Pope is
the head of the universal Church, instituted immediately by God. II. If the
Pope could not be removed by the Church for that reason, then neither could
any pastor of a particular church who has authority from God. III. Although
the authority of pastors is from God, the derivation of this authority with
respect to the existence it has in this or that person nevertheless depends
upon the Church’s designation. Thus the deprivation of the same authority
also depends upon the Church, which can afterward remove from that office,
for just causes, those same men whom she formerly designated.

10. Protestants. Otherwise the Church would remain without a remedy if the
Pope could harass and destroy everyone with impunity, with no one resisting.

Bellarmine 1. *It is not surprising if the Church sometimes remains without
an effective human remedy, since her safety rests chiefly upon divine
protection.*

Protestants. I. Therefore the Church is not a perfect commonwealth, sufficient
for herself in relation to her end; she is not well instituted and is
sometimes bound to expose religion to evident danger. For these are
Bellarmine’s arguments in *On the Roman Pontiff*, book 5, chapter 7, to prove
the Church’s power to depose kings. Yet they argue much more effectively and
directly for the Church’s power to depose a ruinous Pope. II. If this reasoning
were valid, then all medical care would have to be cast aside, not only in
spiritual but also in bodily diseases. For all safety rests chiefly upon
divine protection.

Bellarmine 2. *It is also lawful to resist the Pope by force and arms if he
wishes to destroy the Church.*

Protestants. Therefore it is lawful to depose him. For Bellarmine—

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n253 | printed p. 36]

—himself explains this armed resistance in this way in *On the Roman Pontiff*,
book 5, chapter 7: *Any commonwealth can command another which is not subject
to it and compel it to change its administration; indeed, it can even depose
its prince and institute another when it cannot otherwise defend itself from
his injuries.*

11. Protestants. This was the decree of the Council of Constance approved by
Martin V: that a general council is above the Pope. Therefore either this is
certain, or a council confirmed by the Pope can err.

Bellarmine 1. *It is not defined there absolutely that councils possess power
above the Pope, but only in a case of schism.*

Protestants. The words are plain, session 4: *That the synod itself, gathered
in the Holy Spirit, constituting a legitimate general council and representing
the Catholic Church militant, has power immediately from Christ, which
everyone of whatever state or dignity, even if papal, is bound to obey in
those things which pertain to faith, the extirpation of the said schism, and
the general reformation of God’s Church in head and members, and so forth.*

Bellarmine 2. *Nor is it true that Martin V confirmed this decree.*

Protestants. His words are also sufficiently plain. I. In session 45: *Our
most holy lord, the Pope, responding to the foregoing, said that he wished to
hold and inviolably observe all and each of the things determined, concluded,
and decreed in matters of faith by the present council in conciliar fashion
(*conciliariter*), and never to act contrary to them in any way. And he
approves and ratifies the things thus done in conciliar fashion, and not
otherwise or in another manner.* II. In Martin’s bull directed to the
inquisitors, this is one of the articles: *Likewise, whether he believes that
what the sacred Council of Constance, representing the universal Church,
approved and approves in favor of the faith and for the salvation of souls is
to be approved and held by all Christ’s faithful, and so forth.* As for
Bellarmine’s quibble over the word *conciliariter*, it is of no weight. This
is sufficiently plain from the very words of the confirmation, and still
plainer from the words of the bull.
