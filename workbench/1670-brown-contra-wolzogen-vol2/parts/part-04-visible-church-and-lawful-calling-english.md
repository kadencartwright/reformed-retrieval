### Assertion III

This kingdom of Christ is the visible church. It is true that Christ is in a
special manner the king and head of the invisible church—that is, of the
assembly of the elect and those effect-

[Volume II; printed p. 58; ONB canvas 66]

ually called. For he teaches this assembly by his Spirit, leads, raises up,
comforts, justifies, sanctifies, and will finally glorify it. But speaking here
about this matter contributes nothing to the present undertaking. I therefore
speak about the visible church, with respect to which Christ is king and is
called king. I say that this church is the kingdom of Christ the mediator. The
following few points will suffice to confirm this assertion.

1. The church to which Christ grants common grace and common gifts is Christ’s
kingdom—namely, the common grace of conviction, illumination, faith, and the
like, and the gift of preaching, administering the sacraments, exercising
discipline, and so forth. But this church is the visible church. See this whole
argument confirmed by the apostle in 1 Corinthians 12:4–12.

2. The church that is called *Christ*, because of a union and communion with
Christ that is not saving but common, is Christ’s kingdom. But the visible
church enjoys this title in 1 Corinthians 12:12.

3. The church into which everyone is introduced through baptism, whether Jews
or Greeks, slaves or free, is Christ’s kingdom. But this is the visible church.
1 Corinthians 12:13.

4. The church in which the apostle exhorts that there be no schism or division
is Christ’s kingdom. But the visible church is liable to this evil. 1
Corinthians 12:25.

5. The Corinthian church was Christ’s kingdom because it was Christ’s body, 1
Corinthians 12:27. But the Corinthian church was a visible church.

6. The same can be said about the Roman church, because it too was called
Christ’s body, or *one body in Christ*, Romans 12:5.

7. The same can be said about all the churches of the New Test-

[Volume II; printed p. 59; ONB canvas 67]

ament, because the rationale of all of them is the same, and every church
mentioned in the New Testament was visible.

8. The church called the great house of God, in which there are vessels of gold
and silver and so forth, is Christ’s kingdom. But the visible church as such is
of this kind; see 1 Timothy 3:15 and 2 Timothy 2:20.

9. The visible church is expressly called the kingdom and the kingdom of
heaven. See Matthew 11:28; Mark 1:14, 15; 4:2, 3, 6, 11; 9:1; Luke 4:43; 7:28;
18:10, 27; 13:18, 20; 19:11; Matthew 3:2; 4:17; and 13:11, 24, 31, 33.

10. Christ will deliver this kingdom to the Father, 1 Corinthians 15:24; that
is, that external dispensation will come to an end. He will not, however,
deliver up the invisible church, or the kingdom of grace, because the kingdom
of grace differs from the kingdom of glory only in the degree of communion.

11. This visible church is the kingdom of which the apostle speaks in Hebrews
12:28.

12. The church in which there are ministers, whether extraordinary or
ordinary, and whether they are pastors, teachers, presbyters, or deacons, is
Christ’s kingdom. But this is the visible church.

13. The church in which ecclesiastical discipline is exercised is Christ’s
kingdom. But the visible church is such a church.

14. The church that has visible and powerful enemies who are for the most part
prevailing is Christ’s kingdom. But this is the visible church.

15. The church in which the word and sacraments are publicly administered is
Christ’s kingdom. For the gospel is called the word of the kingdom, or its
scepter and law, in Matthew 6:10; 13:11; Luke 4:43; Matthew 4:24; Mark 13:8;
Luke 8:10; and 21:10, 14. It is called the king’s sword in Revelation 1:16 and
19:15, by which he rules the church, in Isaiah 11:4; Hebrews 1:8, 9; Psalm
45:3–7; Isaiah 61:1, 2; 2 Corinthians 10:4–6; and Psalm 110:2.

16.

[Volume II; printed p. 60; ONB canvas 68]

Christ is called king with respect to certain evil people as much as good ones,
Matthew 25:26 and Luke 19:14. But these evil people are members of the visible
church. Thus that church will be Christ’s kingdom.

17. The church compared to a body, house, kingdom, vineyard, sheepfold, and
army is Christ’s kingdom. But the visible church is the church so compared.

18. Christ is king of those whose master, farmer, fisherman, head of the
household, shepherd, and bridegroom he is. But Christ assumes these relations
with respect to the visible church, in which there are good and evil servants,
tares as well as wheat, Matthew 13:25; good and evil fish, Matthew 13:27;
obedient and disobedient children; vessels for honor and dishonor, 2 Timothy
2:20; wise and foolish virgins, Matthew 25; and finally goats as well as sheep,
Matthew 25:32. I believe this assertion has now been sufficiently confirmed.
See the Leiden professors’ *Synopsis*, disputation 41, theses 14 and 15;
Voetius, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 1, chapter 1, page 11; Essenius,
*System of Theology*, title 20, thesis 8; and Wendelin, *System of Theology*,
book 1, chapter 17, thesis 6. There is no need to cite more authors on a matter
so clear and manifest. I will, however, answer one or two objections.

It can be objected, first: How can those who have no life and are instead
members of Satan be called members of Christ? I answer: 1. If none are to be
called members of Christ except those who actually possess life in themselves,
then believers’ infants will not be members of the church. 2. The members of
Satan do not live the saving life, but all members of the church profess the
true religion and therefore are holy with respect to their standing and
relatively. To this extent they are separated from the world and are not to be
numbered among the Gentiles who are without Christ. They can therefore be
members of the church and of Christ externally, but members of Satan
internally—members of Christ relatively, with respect to the external covenant
and profession, but members of Satan habitually, with respect to obedience and
service.

It can be objected, second: How can Christ be king

[Volume II; printed p. 61; ONB canvas 69]

or mystical head of those who are not members of his mystical body? I answer:
If *mystical body* is taken for the assembly of the elect, many of them—namely,
those not yet converted—lie in sins. But if it is taken for the assembly of
those effectually called, it must be said that Christ is king and head of
others besides them. And just as the visible church can be called a
metaphorical or mystical body, so Christ can be called its metaphorical head
and king, because he rules and governs it externally through the word,
sacraments, and censures. He also rules it through the influences of gifts and
common grace, through all of which he externally illuminates and convicts some,
and by the additional operation of the Spirit brings others to salvation.

### Assertion IV

This kingdom of Christ, or this church, is considered here politically; that
is, it is an assembly of people formally constituted, ruled, and governed by a
distinct polity. I do not say that the church is a part of the civil polity, or
that it possesses the same kind of polity or government as civil commonwealths
possess. For later, in its proper place, I will demonstrate how this society
differs from a civil commonwealth and how its government or polity is
distinguished from civil polity. I say only this here: that this ecclesiastical
society is an organic and political body, that is, one governed in its own
special manner. Christ is considered as king and political head. The church is
therefore a political kingdom, because here we are speaking not of the
invisible church as such, as was said above, but of the visible church. Parker
says in book 1, chapter 5, page 10 of *On Polity*: “Body and head are taken in
two ways: first, properly, for a natural head and body; second, figuratively,
for a political body and head.” He calls all those whom Christ rules externally
this political body and, with respect to them, calls Christ the political head.

Macco-

[Volume II; printed p. 62; ONB canvas 70]

vius speaks about this kingdom in chapter 27 of his *Commonplaces*, where he
treats Christ’s external government. Wendelin also speaks about it in his
*System of Theology*, book 1, chapter 17, thesis 16, when he says, “There are
two parts to this royal office insofar as it is administered in this world:
the prescription and the execution of laws,” and the latter is either external
or internal. “Externally,” he says, “Christ through the ministry of the word
prescribes to his subjects laws for believing and living, by an external
calling.” The assertion is clear from what has already been said and will
become evident from what is said below. It will suffice to say these few things
here.

1. Christ is considered as king actually ruling and governing this church.
Therefore the church is a political kingdom.

2. Christ instituted ministers to be heard in this church, gave them power to
govern the church, and furnished them with gifts fitting them to discharge this
office. Therefore this church is a political society.

3. Christ prescribed its own laws for it and instituted censures, and so
forth. It is therefore a political, or governed, society. To state the matter
in a word: whatever commonwealths possess for constituting a civil polity, this
society possesses for forming an ecclesiastical polity. It has a supreme king
who rules it externally. It has ministers appointed by the king who rule it
ministerially under the king. It has its external institutions prescribed by
Christ the king. It has laws proper to itself, enacted by Christ the sole
lawgiver. It has a special and determinate form of government, as we will prove
below. It has special censures peculiar to itself. Therefore this church is a
political society, or a society that possesses within itself governors and
government, and actually is—or ought to be—ruled by those rulers according to
Christ’s laws and institutions. Hence it is frequently called a kingdom, the
kingdom of heaven, a city in Ephesians 2:19, and an army in Song of Songs 6:10.

### Assertion V

Christ, the king and sole head of this kingdom, instituted various external
means, or “ordinances,” so to speak, by which he would carry out his work in the
church. That is, through them he would bring some to a certain illumination,
conviction, external obedience, and profession, but others to true faith and
repentance, to firm union and communion with Christ, and finally to glory.
These means are the word, the sacraments, and ecclesiastical censures or
discipline, and so forth. See Matthew 28:18; Luke 22:19; Matthew 18:15–18;
Revelation 2:2, 14, 15, 20; Luke 21:18–20; 1 Corinthians 11; Titus 3:10; 1
Corinthians 5:4; 1 Timothy 3:1–7; 4:15; Acts 13:1–3; 6:6; Titus 1:4; and 1
Timothy 5:22; 4:9. No human being may violate these institutions of Christ. For
Christ alone has power both to establish and institute new things and to
abrogate and abolish old ones. He alone abolished the ancient ceremonies, or
those legal institutions. Nor can anyone by right add new ones, because no one
has power to make those new things effectual for salvation. He alone can bless
them. Jeroboam formerly was not permitted to institute anything from his own
brain in the worship of God, nor will anyone now be permitted to add other
things to Christ’s institutions. “In vain do they worship me,” Christ says of
the Pharisees, “teaching doctrines that are human precepts.” Matthew 15:9.

### Assertion VI

Christ, the king and head of the church, instituted distinct ministers, or
particular offices and functions, for administering these instituted means
properly and in order. Three things must be considered here. First, that there
are certain ecclesias-

[Volume II; printed p. 64; ONB canvas 72]

tical offices in the Christian church, or ecclesiastical ministers distinct
from the church’s other members. Second, that these offices and functions are
of divine right, instituted by Christ. Third, that this right to institute
ecclesiastical rulers belongs to no one else.

The first point is established from the plain testimonies of Scripture, namely
Romans 12:6–8: “Having different gifts according to the grace given to us,
whether prophecy, let us prophesy according to the proportion of faith; or
ministry, let us attend to ministering; he who teaches, to teaching; he who
exhorts, to exhortation; he who distributes, with simplicity; he who presides,
with diligence; he who shows mercy, with cheerfulness.” It is clear that the
apostle speaks here about ministers distinct from the other members because he
compares the church here to an organized body. Just as he says in verse 4, “We
have many members in one body,” so he speaks about these ministers when he says
that “all the members do not have the same action.” Furthermore, these
ministers are not only designated by their own proper and distinct names, but
the work peculiar to each one is described and indeed prescribed; and that
work properly pertains to ministers. See the commentators Calvin, Beza,
Piscator, Diodati, Rollock, Pareus, Aretius, Gualter, and so forth. It is plain
in its own light that the apostle speaks about ecclesiastical ministers whom
he distinguishes by their distinct offices, because he writes to the Roman
church, not to the commonwealth, and speaks about the body that is one in
Christ, verse 5. He speaks of the ministers who by reason of their office are
bound to prophesy according to the proportion of faith and to attend to
ministry, teaching, exhortation, distribution, and government. These works are
purely ecclesiastical. The same is established from 1 Corinthians 12:27–30:
“Now you are Christ’s body and individually members. And God has indeed
appointed some in the church: first apostles, next prophets, third teachers,
then powers, then gifts of healings, helps, govern-

[Volume II; printed p. 65; ONB canvas 73]

ments, kinds of tongues, and so forth.” In this passage it is perfectly clear
that the apostle speaks about ecclesiastical ministers, both from the whole
chapter and from its particular purpose, as well as from the very words cited.
A similar testimony of Scripture appears in Ephesians 4:11, 12: “He therefore
gave some as apostles, others as prophets, others as evangelists, and others as
pastors and teachers, for the fitting together of the saints, for the work of
ministry, for the edification of Christ’s body, and so forth.” Here there is an
express enumeration of certain ministers, both extraordinary and ordinary.
Finally, these ministers are frequently mentioned elsewhere—not to linger too
long in demonstrating a clear matter. See Acts 20:17, 28; 14:23; 15:4, 6;
21:18; Philippians 1:1; 1 Thessalonians 5:12, 13; 1 Timothy 4:1, 2 and so forth;
5:17; Titus 1:5, 6; Hebrews 13:7, 17; Revelation 1:20; 2:1, 8, 12, 18; 3:1, 10,
15.

As for the second point—namely, that these ministers, offices, or functions are
of divine right—it is clear from what has already been said, because the
testimonies of Scripture cited prove clearly enough that they were divinely
instituted. See Matthew 10:1, 7; Mark 3:14; Luke 9:1, 2; and 10:1, 2, where it
is clear that Christ instituted the apostles and afterward the seventy
disciples. See also Matthew 28:18; Acts 13:2; Galatians 2:7; Acts 20:28; 2
Corinthians 5:18, 19; Colossians 4:17. It is also clear from the distinctive
titles by which the Holy Spirit marks them. For why would he have distinguished
them from the other members of the church by distinctive titles if they did
not possess distinct and proper offices? They are called pastors, Ephesians
4:11; 1 Peter 5:2; Acts 20:28; teachers, 1 Corinthians 12:28; Galatians 6:6;
stewards of God’s mysteries, 1 Corinthians 4:1; Titus 1:7; Galatians 6:10;
heralds, Romans 10:14; bishops, Acts 20:18; 1 Peter 4:15; 5:2; angels,
Revelation 2 and 3; stars, Revelation 1:20; ambassadors, 2 Corinthians 5:19,
20; leaders, Hebrews 13:17; governors, 1 Corinthians 12:28; 1 Timothy 5:17.
These distinct and distinctive names and titles designate offices.

[Volume II; printed p. 66; ONB canvas 74]

Therefore, when the Holy Spirit marks ministers by these titles, it is clear
enough that ecclesiastical ministers are of divine right. This is evident,
moreover, from the various duties they are bound by divine right to perform,
such as preaching the gospel, administering the sacraments, exercising
discipline, caring for the poor, ruling the church, keeping watch, and so
forth. See 2 Timothy 4:2; 2:25; 1 Timothy 4:13–16; 1 Corinthians 9:16, 17; 2
Corinthians 12:15; 1 Timothy 3:5; 4:14; Acts 6:2, 4; Matthew 28:19; 1
Corinthians 4:1; 10:16; Hebrews 13:17; 1 Timothy 5:22. It is also evident from
the duties that the church’s other members are bound by divine right to
perform toward them: namely, to acknowledge them, 1 Thessalonians 5:12; to
esteem them exceedingly dear, verse 13; to remember them, Hebrews 13:7; to
regard them as worthy of double honor, 1 Timothy 5:17; to obey them, Hebrews
13:7, 17; and to share every good thing with them, Galatians 6:6. Next, the
divine promises made to this ministry sufficiently indicate its divine origin,
such as the promises of Christ’s presence, Matthew 28:20; protection,
Revelation 2:1; ratification, Matthew 16:19; and sympathy, Matthew 10:40; Luke
10:16; and the like. Finally, there are other things that establish that this
office is of divine right. (1.) These ministers must be adorned with the
required qualifications. See 1 Timothy 3:2; 2 Timothy 2:2, 6; Titus 1:9. (2.)
Others are bound to examine them diligently and determine whether they are
endowed with those gifts and qualifications. 1 Timothy 3:10; 5:11, 12; Acts 6.
All these things, and similar ones that can be adduced, clearly establish that
these ecclesiastical functions are of divine right.

The third point remains to be proved: that this right to institute
ecclesiastical ministers belongs to Christ alone. It does not need a long
proof, since it follows of itself from what has been said. For if they are of
divine right, they cannot be instituted by human beings. Moreover, he alone
ascended on high and gave gifts to men, Ephesians 4:8. Therefore, if anyone now
wished to institute other ministers, he would be saying that Christ did not
institute them all and thus did not respond perfectly and faithfully to his
royal office,

[Volume II; printed p. 67; ONB canvas 75]

or consult the good and edification of the church—which is horrible to say.
Furthermore, if human beings could fashion new orders of ministers, they could
also abolish those instituted and multiply new ones without end. Finally, it
belongs to Christ alone to determine all the qualifications required in
ministers and to bestow them. But enough on these matters.

### Assertion VII

No one who has not been lawfully called according to the order established by
Christ is permitted to discharge these offices or administer these means in an
ordinary, public, and authoritative manner. I freely grant that a private
person endowed with gifts can lawfully apply the word of God read aloud in his
family and make it plain according to the measure of his knowledge, showing,
namely, which sin is reproved and which duty is enjoined, according to
Deuteronomy 6:6, 7. I also grant that a private person can, when occasion
arises, reprove a brother for sin, admonish him of his duty, teach, and exhort
him, according to Matthew 18:15; Leviticus 19:17; Hebrews 10:25; 3:13; 1
Thessalonians 4, last verse; 5:11; Romans 15:13; Colossians 3:16. He can also
instruct others privately and edify them by mutual conversation concerning
divine things, Malachi 3:16, and do similar things—particularly, as regards
announcing the gospel, when some necessity presses. It is not necessary to
relate all such things here. But we deny that anyone not lawfully called may
assume this office for himself and discharge it. This is abundantly clear from
what has already been said.

For, 1. if Christ is king of the church and has established order within it,
it is clear that no one can lawfully violate this order.

2. If Christ instituted ministers and prescribed to each his own office or
particular duties, no one will be permitted to invade the office of others.
Hear Hoornbeeck, *Confutation of Socinianism*, book 2, chapter 4, pages 214,
215: “The four-

[Volume II; printed p. 68; ONB canvas 76]

th argument. Whatever pertaining to order and decorum is necessary in the
church, so that the church cannot be well without it, must be derived as
necessary both from God—not only as the author of every order in the
church—and from Scripture, which comprehends whatever is so necessary to the
church and religion. Calling to the discharge of an office in the church is of
this kind. Therefore, and so forth. The adversaries too acknowledge that it
pertains to order. But since it is not some simple and ordinary order, such as
those things that are of merely human institution and can be omitted without
the greatest harm, much less the ruin of the whole, we must acknowledge that
it is a divine order. For it is impossible that all things in the church be
done ‘decently and according to order,’ as the apostle commands in 1
Corinthians 14:40, where it is lawful to administer sacred things without a
prior calling. Even human examples can confirm this. Suppose that anyone,
though qualified but by no means called, could undertake the government of a
commonwealth. Will we think that public tranquility and welfare could coexist
with such disorder?”

3. Indeed, if this assertion of ours were not true, Christ would have
instituted the ministry in vain. For a function established in a commonwealth
cannot be called a particular function if it is common to all. The most
distinguished Hoornbeeck abundantly proves this assertion in the place cited
above from Romans 10:15; Hebrews 5:4; the example of Christ and the apostles;
and the fact that people who taught without being called are reproved in
Scripture. He also answers all the adversaries’ exceptions. We do not need to
do what has already been done.

4. In Romans 12 the apostle vigorously dissuades the Romans from this unlawful
invasion or usurpation of offices, saying in verse 3: “I charge everyone among
you not to think of himself more highly than he ought to think, but to think
with sobriety, according as God has allotted to each the measure of faith.”
They were puffed up with pride,

[Volume II; printed p. 69; ONB canvas 77]

and some despised others and perhaps invaded the offices of others, as is
probable from what follows. For to move them to unity and humility and dissuade
them from the unlawful invasion of others’ offices, he employs these arguments
in verses 4 and 5: Just as a body has various members, and each member has its
own proper action, so the church has many functions, and each has its own
corresponding actions. Therefore, just as in the natural body no member
performs the office of another, so in the church all things ought to be done in
order. Then in verse 6 he exhorts each person to make use, according to his
calling, of those different functions that he calls graces because they are
freely given, whether they are called to prophecy or to ministry, which kinds
he subdivides again in verses 7 and 8. In sum, he seems to urge this one thing:
that each person faithfully discharge his own office and not meddle in other
affairs.

5. The apostle also appears to press the same point at greater length
throughout 1 Corinthians 12.

6. If this license to invade or usurp the offices of others is granted, all
that careful distinction of ecclesiastical functions which the apostle
proposes, urges, illustrates, and defends both in the passages cited and
elsewhere will be vain.

7. Furthermore, the apostle’s words in 1 Corinthians 7:20, “Let each one remain
in the calling in which he was called,” as well as 1 Thessalonians 4:11,
confirm our assertion. For it is clear from them that no one is permitted to
seize another’s office for himself. Rather, each person ought faithfully to
discharge the office entrusted to him.

8. Moreover, no one is permitted to assume and exercise the office of a
magistrate unless he is lawfully called. Therefore neither may anyone assume
the pastoral office. For both offices were instituted by God, both require
their own qualifications, both possess their own proper actions, and each is
assigned to its own subjects. The one may no more invade the office of the
other

[Volume II; printed p. 70; ONB canvas 78]

than the other may invade his. For we see what formerly happened to Saul,
Jeroboam, and Uzziah, though they were kings, because they wished to invade an
office God had assigned to ecclesiastical ministers, namely the priests, and
to perform work that properly and particularly belonged to the priestly
office. 1 Samuel 13:12, 13; 1 Kings 13:1, 2; 2 Chronicles 26:16, 20.

9. The confessions of the Reformed churches abundantly confirm this assertion
of ours. Consult them, for it would take too long to repeat their words here.

10. This truth is approved by the judgment of no small number of Reformed
theologians. It would be an almost endless labor to cite the words of all of
them and would undoubtedly weary the reader. Let those who wrote against the
Anabaptists and Socinians be read, and this truth will appear abundantly
confirmed. Bucanus proposes this question in commonplace 42, “On the
Ministry”: “When the ordinary calling has ceased, ought any Christian versed
in Holy Scripture to attack false doctrine and assert the true, and can he on
that account ascend the pulpit?” He answers: “By no means. For this would be to
open the door to anyone, anywhere, who considered himself wise, to hold secret
meetings under the pretext of attacking corrupt doctrine, whether truly or
falsely so called. This is what the Anabaptists and Libertines are accustomed
to do, following especially the example of those who first crept secretly into
Antioch and afterward into Galatia and elsewhere and caused such great
disturbances, concerning whom the apostles speak in Acts 15 and 14.” He is so
averse to approving this practice that he does not judge it permissible even
when the ordinary calling has ceased, though I would not wish to extend the
proposed assertion so far as to include extraordinary cases of this sort.

11. Our thesis is further confirmed because gifts are always distinguished
from calling. Hoornbeeck says in the place cited above: “The situation is the
same with schol-

[Volume II; printed p. 71; ONB canvas 79]

astic and domestic functions: aptitude alone does not thrust someone into an
office or ministry. Rather, it must be established that a suitable person was
lawfully called to these or those functions. Someone worthy of command does
not for that reason command; but it is above all right that someone who
commands should be worthy. These propositions, however, are not reciprocal.”
One thing is *dynamis*, or the bestowal of gifts; another is *exousia*, or the
conferral of power. Paul and Barnabas in Acts 13:1–3 were furnished with gifts
but had not yet been sent and set apart for the work of the gospel. Isaiah was
made fit to discharge his office by the coal applied to his mouth in chapter
6:6, 7, but was afterward called in verses 8 and 9. Thus Christ’s disciples
were first furnished with gifts and then sent. So too Christ himself, though
most fully furnished with gifts, nevertheless needed a calling to supervene,
Hebrews 5:5.

12. The titles by which they are marked also clearly prove that lawful calling
must come first. They are called stewards. But who can be such a person if he
has not been lawfully called to that office? They are ambassadors. Therefore
they need letters of good faith or credentials; they must be furnished with a
commission from the king or prince who sends them. They are called men of God,
and such men formerly were specially called, 2 Kings 5:8. They are sentinels,
watchmen, or guardians. But such people are ordinarily sent to perform this
work. They are lawful pastors. Therefore they are sent, and they are not
thieves who do not enter through the door, John 10. They are angels and
messengers. Therefore, being sent, they discharge this office. They are
leaders. But who lawfully discharges the office of leader without being
called? They are likewise governors. Therefore they are called, for no one
lawfully appoints himself as a magistrate or governor. They are bishops. But
the Athenians used to appoint such people and send them to perform that work in
some city. They are heralds and *fetiales*, all of whom are furnished with the
authority of those sending them; no one can be such a person unless he is
called and lawfully authorized. They are laborers hired into the vineyard,
Matthew 20:1; 21:33; Luke 20:4. They are servants inviting people to the
wedding, but these are specially sent to this

[Volume II; printed p. 72; ONB canvas 80]

task, Matthew 22:3; Luke 14:17. They are laborers in the Lord’s harvest, but
all these have been hired. All these and similar titles clearly indicate that
a lawful calling is necessarily required for the proper discharge of these
offices.

13. If this lawful calling were not necessary, the qualifications required in
ecclesiastical ministers would have been described to us in Holy Scripture in
vain, 1 Timothy 3:1–3 and so forth; Titus 1; and frequently elsewhere. For when
the Holy Spirit describes to us so exactly what those called to discharge this
pastoral office must be like, he indicates clearly enough that he does not will
anyone not lawfully called to assume this office. Rather, whoever desires this
office must first be examined and, when found suitable, be called and sent.

14. If calling were not necessary, the Holy Spirit would have described to us
through examples the lawful manner of calling in vain, for all those instances
would now be of no use.

15. The apostle’s command in 2 Timothy 2:2, “The things you have heard from me
among many witnesses, entrust these to faithful men who will be competent to
teach others also,” clearly teaches that Timothy ought to send and ordain
suitable men. It teaches that others, however suitable they judged themselves,
ought not and could not by right seize this function for themselves unless it
was first entrusted to them by Timothy or people like Timothy.

16. By divine right, compensation is due to all who are appointed to this
pastoral office. See 1 Timothy 5:17, 18; 1 Corinthians 9:7, 9, 11, 13, 14;
Galatians 6:6. It is therefore evident that God did not will this office to be
common to all, but particular to certain people, namely those lawfully called.

17. If lawful calling were not necessarily required, the people would not know
whom they should hear or to whom they should render that obedience, submission,
and honor which they know is due to true pastors.

18. To announce the gospel in this manner is an act of authority. For the
apostle does not will women to teach, because in this way they would usurp
authority over men, 1 Timothy 2:12. But no one can by right usurp or exercise
authority over the church or the members of the church before he is lawfully
called.

19. Those sent by Christ not only confirmed their doctrine but also
demonstrated and defended their mission and lawful calling. John the Baptist
did so in John 3:27, 28, and Paul does so throughout his epistles to the
Corinthians and Galatians.

20. No divine precept can demonstrate that the contrary practice is lawful.
How, then, can it be done from faith? A practice that does not proceed from
lawful faith is not lawful. If any precept, permission, or anything capable of
establishing divine right is given, we will see it below.

The most distinguished Apollonius adds two other arguments in *Consideration
of Certain Controversies*, pages 94, 95. I. (This is his third argument.) “The
duties imposed upon all those who publicly preach God’s word in Christ’s name
have not been imposed on all believers who possess gifts. For instance, the
pastoral care of souls rests upon them, for which they will give an account to
God, Hebrews 13:17. They ought to devote themselves entirely to reading and the
study of Scripture, 2 Timothy 4:15, 16. They must not involve themselves in the
affairs of this age, 2 Timothy 2:4. As Christ’s ambassadors, they are to plead
with people in Christ’s name to allow themselves to be reconciled to God, 2
Corinthians 5:20. At the appointed time they must distribute to everyone and
each person in God’s house his measure of food, Matthew 24:46. Authoritatively
and in Christ’s name they ought to forgive and retain sins, John 20:21. Now,
these duties have not been enjoined on all believers who have received gifts
of the Spirit.” II. (This is his fourth argument.) “Hence the privileges and
promises made to the pastors who preach God’s word in his name have not been
given to all believers endowed with gifts of the Spirit—such as that they are
worthy of double hon-
