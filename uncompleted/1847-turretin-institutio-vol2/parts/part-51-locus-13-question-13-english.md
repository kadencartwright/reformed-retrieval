QUESTION XIII.

THE KNOWLEDGE OF CHRIST’S SOUL.

*Was Christ’s soul so filled with knowledge from the moment of its creation
that it was ignorant of nothing and learned nothing? We deny against the
Roman Catholics.*

I. The scholastics distinguish Christ’s knowledge into three principal kinds:
beatific, infused, and acquired, corresponding to the threefold light of
glory, grace, and nature. They call that knowledge *beatific* by which Christ
sees God according to his essence, as the blessed behold his face in heaven.
For them *infused* knowledge is that by which he knows heavenly things through
a supernatural habit and whatever can be seen by the light of grace.
*Acquired* knowledge is that by which he understands whatever can be known by
the light of nature or reason. Yet this is not quite correct, because beatific
knowledge, which belongs to those who have attained the goal, cannot suitably
be attributed to Christ while he was still a wayfarer living on earth, as
will be said later. We therefore acknowledge a twofold knowledge in Christ’s
human nature while he lived in the world: infused and acquired, or
experiential. He received infused knowledge through the grace of the Holy
Spirit, who sanctified the human nature and filled it with his gifts, of which
Isaiah 11:1–2 treats. Acquired knowledge is actual cognition which Christ
acquired both through reasoning, by drawing conclusions from the principles
of infused knowledge, and through his own experience (Heb. 4:15; 5:8).

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The controversy here with the Roman Catholics concerns the nature and
extent of this knowledge. It does not concern the knowledge of Christ
glorified, but of Christ acting on earth. It does not ask whether omniscience
was given to him through the grace of union, which we refuted above. Nor does
it ask whether he suffered from a gross ignorance of many things, which our
adversaries calumniously impute to us. For then he could not be free from sin,
of which we acknowledge that not even a shadow was in him.

III. Rather, the question comes down to this: Was Christ’s soul imbued from the
beginning, by virtue of the hypostatic union, with such knowledge that it was
ignorant of nothing or could

[Printed p. 307 | Internet Archive PDF 335 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

learn nothing new? The Roman Catholics affirm this. Bellarmine, volume 1,
General Controversy 2, *On Christ,* 1, 2, says, “It has always been the common
opinion of Catholics that Christ’s soul was filled with knowledge and grace
from the moment of its creation, so that afterward it learned nothing which
it did not know before.” We, however, deny it. We indeed acknowledge that
Christ, insofar as he is God, is omniscient. Yet insofar as he is man, we
confess that he was endowed with knowledge which, though great beyond that of
all other creatures, was nevertheless finite and created, could have
something added to it, and in fact did have something added to it.

[Marginal heading: The orthodox position is proved. 1. From Luke 2:52;]

IV. The reasons are: 1. Christ is said to have *advanced in wisdom and
stature* (Luke 2:52). But where there is perfection, there is no advancement.
Nor can it be said that he advanced only *in the opinion of men,* as Bellarmine
wishes in chapter 5, because he disclosed his wisdom more each day. For just
as he truly advanced with the increase of age and with respect to stature, so
also he advanced with increases of gifts and with respect to wisdom, as
Toletus proves well. Hence Ambrose, *On the Incarnation,* chapter 7, says, “As
he advanced in the age of man, so he advanced in the wisdom of man,” which
Stapleton acknowledges in the *Antidote to the Gospels* at this place. This is
confirmed because he is said to have advanced not only before men but also
before God, which cannot be referred to mere opinion but imports the truth of
the matter.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Mark 13:32;]

V. 2. The Son is said to be ignorant of the day of judgment (Mark 13:32). This
is certainly not insofar as he is the Λόγος, *Word,* or Son of God, because he
cannot be ignorant of the day which he himself appointed beforehand with the
Father (Acts 17:31), and since he made time, he could not be ignorant of either
its beginning or its end. Rather, it is insofar as he is man and only with
respect to the human nature, just as the ancients had no other way to extricate
themselves from the Arians’ objection drawn from this passage against the
deity of the Son. Nor was he ignorant only because he did not know it for the
purpose of telling others, as Bellarmine, Becanus, Tirinus, and others would
have it, which would savor of Jesuitical equivocation. For who, without the
guilt of lying, can deny absolutely that he knows what he knows merely because
he ought not reveal it to others? Moreover, in this sense there would be
almost nothing which Christ could not be said to be ignorant of, because he
does not reveal his mysteries to pagans and Turks. Indeed, the Father himself
would have to be said to be ignorant of the day of judgment, since he reveals
it to no one. Nor was it only that Christ knew it theoretically but not
practically and according to experience. For in this sense the Father himself
does not know it, since it has not yet arrived. Rather, he was ignorant of it
simply because the Word had not revealed this to him. Thus Christ wills to
restrain the disciples’ curiosity, lest they dare penetrate further than they
ought or weave scrupulous questions about the time of that judgment, since the
members ought not aspire to a knowledge which was not granted even to the Head
himself according to his humanity. Nor should two Christs therefore be
posited, one of whom knew the day of judgment and the other was ignorant of
it, but only two natures in the one Christ. Just as they are conjoined in the
unity of the Person, so they retain their properties unconfused. Thus many
things are said of the whole Christ, or the Person, which do not pertain to the
entire Christ, or to both natures, but only to one or the other. Therefore,
these propositions can be true at the same time, but κατ᾽ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο,
*according to different respects:* Christ is ignorant of the day of judgment,
and Christ is not ignorant of the day of judgment. For the opposition is
between most closely conjoined natures, one of which is not included in the
concept of the other. But the account is different with propositions made
about things which differ not by a real distinction but only by a formal one,
and which are so related that one is subordinated to and included in the
other. Hence it cannot suitably be said that a man is ignorant of something
according to animal being which he knows according to rational being, because
τὸ rationale, *the rational,* is included in the animal.

[Marginal heading: 3. From Hebrews 2:17.]

VI. 3. Christ was made like us *in all things* (Heb. 2:17), *except sin* (Heb.
4:15). Nor can the ignorance

[Printed p. 308 | Internet Archive PDF 336 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

which we attribute to Christ be excepted here. For although ignorance arising
from an evil disposition, and universal ignorance of things which one must
know, is rightly numbered among vices, all of which Christ lacked, negative
ignorance of some particular thing not necessary for that state and time is
not. To this pertain the words of Athanasius, *Fourth Discourse Against the
Arians:* ὡς ἄνθρωπος οὐκ οἶδεν, ἀνθρώπων γὰρ ἴδιον τὸ ἀγνοεῖν, “as man he does
not know, for it is proper to men not to know.”

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. If Christ is said to know all things in John 21:17 and Matthew 11:27,
either this pertains to divine knowledge, or, if it is extended to human
knowledge, it must not be understood absolutely. Otherwise omniscience would
have to be attributed to his human nature, which the adversaries do not wish.
Rather, it is only in a qualified and comparative sense, with respect to human
knowledge, than which his was far more perfect.

VIII. The *treasures of knowledge and wisdom which are said to be hidden in
Christ* (Col. 2:3) do not prove that every kind of knowledge was always and
absolutely in him at every time. Rather, he possessed all the knowledge which
could pertain to the nature of the mediatorial office, which was altogether
more excellent than the knowledge of men and angels. Add that, as was seen
elsewhere, this can very well be referred either to the Person of Christ or to
the gospel, of which Paul had spoken immediately before.

IX. As the Son of God, Christ had a right to all his Father’s goods from the
incarnation itself, but by voluntary dispensation he could for a time lack
possession of some of them. He certainly had to be filled with all the gifts
necessary for the administration of the mediatorial office, but in their own
order and degree according to the economy of the divine will. The anointing
indeed took place from the very moment of conception, but the acts of that
anointing were dispensed over successive moments of time and various objects.
Thus the divine nature in Christ at first somehow restrained its majesty and,
as it were, concealed it beneath the veil of the flesh, but afterward
displayed it more clearly in the exaltation. So the Spirit of wisdom with
which he was anointed restrained his acts and did not immediately pour his
rays most fully into Christ’s understanding. Hence, although the Spirit
possessed the fullest, indeed infinite, knowledge, he did not communicate all
of it to Christ as man.

X. Something can contribute to the end of the incarnation in two ways: either
mediately and by a common account, or immediately by a singular account.
Although Christ’s negative ignorance might not be said to have been useful to
the end of the incarnation singularly and immediately, as his death and
resurrection were necessary, it is sufficient that it was useful mediately
and by a common account, so that Christ might declare himself a true
participant in our nature with its ἀδιαβλήτοις, *blameless,* infirmities. For
this ignorance is a condition annexed to our animal nature.

XI. Our position is wrongly compared with the heresy of the Agnoetae, since we
have nothing in common with those who attributed ignorance to the Word, or to
Christ insofar as he is God, as Alfonso de Castro notes in book 5, heresy 8,
of *Against Heresies,* and Isidore in *Origins* 8.5. Hence Nicephorus in 12.30
teaches that they were an offshoot of Arianism.

[Marginal heading: Was Christ simultaneously a wayfarer and one who had
attained the goal? The negative is proved.]

XII. Hence it can readily be gathered what must be determined about another
question raised here by the Roman Catholics: Was Christ simultaneously a
wayfarer and one who had attained the goal? They maintain this so that they
may more easily deny that spiritual passions had a place in Christ’s soul. We,
however, deny it. For these states are so opposed that they are ἀσύστατοι,
*incompatible.* The wayfarer is on the way; the one who has attained is at the
goal. The wayfarer labors and suffers; the one who has attained enjoys perfect
blessedness when his labors are finished. Therefore, since Christ was here on
the way, running the course set before him so that he might complete the work
of our redemption, in which he was subject to various hardships and contests,
and since he even sustained the curse and the weight of God’s wrath, during
this interval of time he could not, as one who had attained the goal,

[Printed p. 309 | Internet Archive PDF 337 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

enjoy the benefit of the fullest blessedness of the human nature. He obtained
this only at last through his exaltation, although he was always endowed with
the most perfect holiness, without any stain of sin.

XIII. The blessedness of those who have attained is a glorious and happy
state, free of pain and ignominy. Yet the whole history of Christ’s life,
passion, and death cries out that his state on earth was not thus glorious
(Phil. 2:7–8), happy (Matt. 20:28; Luke 9:58), or free of pain and ignominy
(Matt. 26:39; 27:31), but that such a state fell to him only after his death
and departure from the world (Luke 24:26; Phil. 2:8–9).

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XIV. What is most closely united to God must be wholly and in every way
perfect with respect to holiness, so that no stain of sin is found in it. But
this does not hold immediately with respect to blessedness. This can be
established from the example of Christ’s body, which was also united with the
Λόγῳ, *Word,* but could not be called perfectly blessed, since it was subject
to suffering and death. Therefore, the union of the soul with God indeed
entails possession of blessedness, but not that it must be entered into
immediately or enjoyed constantly at all times, since by God’s dispensation
suffering had to precede glory and happiness.

XV. The intrinsic and essential glory and blessedness of the deity is one
thing; extrinsic and participated glory and blessedness is another. Through
the hypostatic union, intrinsic and essential glory and blessedness were
communicated to the θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man,* together with the divine nature,
which entails communion in all the divine perfections. But only external and
participated blessedness had to be communicated to the human nature. It does
not inhere in the deity, but flows from it into the creature as an effect, and
indeed as an accident which could at one time be absent and at another be
present.

XVI. If Christ is said to have seen the Father in John 6:46, the passage does
not concern the human nature precisely, but the Person of the Λόγου, *Word,*
who, since he was always intimately united to the Father, could not but always
enjoy the beatific vision of him. Nor, if Christ’s soul is more closely united
with the deity than the souls of the blessed, does it follow that it already
enjoyed the benefit of those who have attained. For by dispensation Christ
relinquished this right so that he could fulfill the office of Surety, while
no such suretyship intervenes between the souls of the heavenly inhabitants
and their union with God.
