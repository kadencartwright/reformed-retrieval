The principal and exceedingly vexed question, however, concerns the mode of
binding. In determining it, the orthodox disagree not only with heretics but
also among themselves. Three distinctions must be employed to explain it.

First, the law may be considered either as a covenant or as a rule. Although
the word *rule* itself does not readily occur, the thing itself is found
everywhere, as in Psalm 119:1, 2, 3, 9, 11, 15, and so forth. As a covenant,
however, it may be regarded in two ways: either as the covenant of works
made with Adam, or as the covenant of grace administered in a legal manner
to the Israelite people. Sacred Scripture calls that administration a
covenant in Hebrews 8:7, 9, and it will soon be evident that the covenant of
grace lay hidden under it. I therefore say that the moral law binds believers
under the gospel neither as the covenant of works, nor

[1679 new-series fol. 23 | IA leaf 343]

as the covenant of grace administered in the ancient manner, but only as a
rule of conduct.

1. Not as the covenant of works, and this insofar as they are believers.
Unbelievers who reject the covenant of grace remain under the covenant of
works; the more they try to break these cords, the more tightly they are
bound. Believers, however, having been made participants in the covenant of
grace through Christ, are so freed from the covenant of works that they
neither expect justification from it nor fear condemnation from it, Romans
7, final verse, and 8:1. I freely acknowledge that we are justified by the
righteousness of the law itself, since it was perfectly fulfilled by our
surety. But as regards us and our own persons, whereby in ourselves we are
guilty above all, we are presented righteous before God solely by the one
covenant of grace. Believers therefore are not bound but freed from the
covenant of law, that is:

1. From the law's rigorous demand, insofar as it exacts personal, complete,
and perpetual obedience.

2. From its condemnation, which punishes its smallest violations with
eternal penalties.

3. From its barrenness, so to speak, by which it commands but does not help,
requires obedience but supplies no strength.

4. Also from its provocation to evil, by which the more it forbids
something, the more it kindles desire for it—accidentally indeed, but
nevertheless as to the result.

5. Finally, from its coercion, by which it compels the unwilling, since
believers are a law to themselves and do voluntarily what the law commands.

These and similar points are sufficiently commonplace. For us at least in
our fallen state, they concern the law considered as the covenant of works.
Everything our adversaries customarily adduce from Scripture against our
thesis can be referred to them. Believers as such are freed from them, and
therefore the moral law does not bind them insofar as it is the covenant of
works and insofar as they are believers.

2. Nor does it bind as the covenant of grace was administered in that
ancient legal manner, insofar as they are believers under the gospel.
Whatever belonged to that legal administration of grace is theirs insofar
as they are believers. But they are freed from the pedagogy which adhered to
the law as it was then administered and which suited the underage condition
of that church, insofar as they are adults under the gospel. This was the
apostle's supreme and sole purpose in his most exact and plainly divine
discourse on this subject in the Epistle to the Galatians. More will be said
about these things when we reach the third distinction.

To complete this first distinction: since this law does not bind believers
under the gospel as either the covenant of grace or the covenant of works,

3. The sole remaining way is as a rule of conduct, which binds everyone,
everywhere, and always, and believers under the gospel above all.

That the law is a covenant is an accidental and separable feature, although
Cameron—whom I favor less on this point—places the very essence of law in
this. But it is impossible for the law not to be a rule.

Moreover, God enters into a covenant with us from his own free will and
sheer love, by which he promises a reward to those who obey. This may be
rendered void by our sins, provided it is a conditional covenant such as the
covenant of works was. This law, however, was enacted by God as Creator and
supreme Governor, who by every right demands obedience from the creature.
Since this relation remains forever,

[1679 new-series fol. 24 | IA leaf 344]

the law, although rendered void through sin as a covenant of works with
respect to believers, must always bind them as a rule of conduct, and all the
more because they have been furnished with stronger motives by the help of
grace supplied to them through the gospel.

Let this be the second distinction. The obligation of the moral law must be
assessed either solely from the force of its subject matter or also from the
authority of the law itself and its legislator. We maintain that the law
binds believers in both ways. The very learned Rivet thinks it enough if the
former mode is granted and says that to contend further is a dispute over
words. On this matter—with due respect to so great a man—I suspend judgment,
or rather, I hold the contrary. Although at least some Antinomians concede
the former mode without much difficulty, they nevertheless hesitate about
the latter; indeed, some of them deny it with blasphemous audacity against
God. Thus Islebius: “The law does not actively exact; rather, the law is
passively fulfilled by the righteous person. The righteous person is active
toward the law; the law is not active toward the righteous person. That is,
the thing is done, but not by force of law.” Indeed, some of them have not
feared to assert that doing something because God commands it, or abstaining
from something because God forbids it, is the mark of a person who is, as
they say, moral, dead, or—the least severe description—an unhealthy
Christian. Alas for the blasphemy! I reply:

1. We have no reason to thank them for their concession about the force of
the subject matter, since the thing itself cries out that worshiping God,
honoring parents, loving one's neighbor, and most other things commanded by
the law are so necessarily good that those who cast off concern for them
cast off humanity at the same time.

2. And yet they have cast it off. The force of the subject matter, to which
they appear to yield, does not hold these demoniacs constrained, since they
not only struggle against the authority of the law but also rage impiously
against good works themselves. The unspeakable monstrosities of impiety with
which their writings abound everywhere testify to this. For example: “If
you have once thought the church should be so ordered that you must live
holily, soberly, and chastely, you have strayed from the gospel. We must live
so that neither Jews nor anyone else may see any good work in us. Indeed,
the very phrases *good works* and *Christian conduct* must never be heard.
Law, good works, and new obedience belong not to Christ's kingdom but to the
world. Indeed, Christians with all their good works belong to the devil.”

The Lord rebuke you, Satan.

3. By this concession they stab themselves. If the law binds by the force of
its subject matter, believers would sin on this account if they acted
against it; yet they deny this.

4. This force of the subject matter does indeed hold in moral things that
belong to natural law, but not likewise in those that belong to positive
law. The moral law nevertheless commands such things, as in the fourth
precept, whose obligation rests not on the force of its subject matter but
on the authority of the law and its legislator.

5. The apostle James not only impresses the moral law's commands upon
Christians but especially urges the legislator's authority in order to make
them more obedient: “If you fulfill the royal law according to Scripture,
You shall love your neighbor as yourself, you do well. But whoever keeps the
whole law and stumbles in one point is guilty of all, for he who said, Do not
commit adultery, also said, Do not murder,” and so forth, chapter 2:8, 10,
11. Again: “If you condemn the law, you are not an observer of the law but a
judge. There is one legislator, who is able to save and to destroy,” chapter
4:11, 12.

[1679 new-series fol. 25 | IA leaf 345]

6. Finally, I say that although the authority of the legislator can be
mentally distinguished from the force of the law's subject matter, it cannot
really be separated from obligation when God sets such matter before us to
be observed, as is the case here. An actual obligation of this kind obtains
its force not only from the thing proposed, but principally and most
formally from the will of God who proposes it. For if the theologians'
opinion is true—that something is formally good from the antecedent will of
decree, but morally good only from the will of command—then perhaps the
subject matter of the law will bind fundamentally from its own force, but it
will obtain its formal moral obligation from the will of the legislator who
proposes and commands it. This was the second distinction.
