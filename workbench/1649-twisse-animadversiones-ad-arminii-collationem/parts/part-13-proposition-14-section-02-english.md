SECTION TWO.

ARMINIUS. But that law could not be performed without supernatural grace. I prove this from the
law’s subject, from its appendage, from the instigator of the transgression, and from the manner of
the instigation. The law required obedience toward God, so that the human being would live not
according to the human being, but according to God. This life is not animal but spiritual, and its
cause in the human being is supernatural grace.

JUNIUS. We concede the minor premise concerning the common law, not because the law was natural,
but because human nature itself, and its own natural law, had been ordered to grace. What is natural
insofar as it is natural lay within the human being’s power; what was ordered and referred to grace
could not be performed without supernatural grace.

Concerning the singular law, the minor premise is false. For it is a simply natural act to eat some
fruit or not to eat it. The human being simply had the power in point of fact to eat and to abstain
from that fruit, although both were not within his power under the institution and ordinance of
that particular law pronounced by God, etc.

Four proofs are adduced to establish the minor premise. They all pertain to the mode of the common
law. Concerning the individual proofs, etc. The first pertains to the mode of the common law,
insofar as it is both natural and ordered to grace. We therefore concede the assertion concerning the
common law to be true; concerning the particular law we deny it, for by that law God required
obedience in a particular and simply natural matter. It lay within natural power to abstain from or
eat that fruit; it belonged to natural will to flee the experience of evil and death which God had
foreannounced. In a simply natural matter God tested the human being’s obedience, and in that same
matter the wretched man renounced obedience to God by his own will, not by any necessity. He
therefore had no just cause for complaint.

Perhaps you will say, “But if grace had stood by him, he would not have committed it.” What? Do you
therefore always require grace even in a natural matter, or accuse it when it is absent? God gave
nature for this reason: that in a simply natural matter Adam might employ his own natural power. He
gave what is enough; you demand more than enough. I shall conclude with the words of Tertullian,
etc.

What you say—that the cause of spiritual life in the human being is supernatural grace—we surely
believe and profess to be most true. Nevertheless, the mode of spiritual life in Adam is one thing,
and in us another. In us supernatural grace alone effects this life, whereas Adam possessed the
image of God whole and uncorrupted together with that grace, and accordingly possessed spiritual
life in both modes, natural and supernatural.

ARMINIUS. I come to the minor premise. But that law was not capable of being performed without
supernatural grace. You concede this concerning the common law but deny it concerning that law by
which eating from that tree was forbidden. I accept this, and from it I conclude my proposition. A
law which could not be performed without supernatural grace ought not to have been imposed except
upon those who had been endowed with supernatural grace by God, etc. This could suffice for my
proposition.

But I say that not even this law concerning the forbidden tree could have been performed without
supernatural grace—not, indeed, insofar as the external act of abstaining from the fruit of that
tree was prescribed, but insofar as obedience was commanded under that symbol and the human being
was enjoined to live not according to the human being, but according to God. You too confess this
when you say that

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 61 | BSB canvas 73]

it did not lie within the human being’s power both to abstain and to eat under that institution and
ordinance of the particular law pronounced by God, although both lay within his power in point of
fact. But the law had to be performed by the human being not merely in point of fact, but under the
ordinance of that particular law.

You say that the proofs which I adduced pertain to the mode of the common law. Let it be so, and
from this I conclude my proposition, as I showed and taught before: in that law which you call
particular there is something of the common law. Therefore they are also valid in this respect. And
the first is indeed valid, as appears from what has now been said. For insofar as the law required
obedience consisting not merely in the external deed but also in an inward affection of the mind,
in that respect it could not be performed without supernatural grace.

RESPONSE. As regards Arminius’s argument, we have already shown that, even if Adam had been created
in supernatural grace, it by no means follows from this that he was considered in that way by God
when God undertook the predestination of human beings. Furthermore, the present argument
does not prove that Adam was created in supernatural grace; it concludes only that he had to be
furnished with supernatural grace before that law was prescribed. Third, it seems highly
improbable that a human being could not in his pure natural endowments obey God his creator with
both inward affection and outward deed in an indifferent matter. For what? In the state of
corruption is it impossible for a son to comply with his earthly father in an indifferent matter?
Why, then, could Adam not have done this in the state of natural integrity, even with supernatural
grace excluded? Indeed, not only in an indifferent matter, but even in a matter good as to the
substance of the action, why could Adam not have rendered obedience to God, since he inclined no
more toward evil than toward good—indeed, more toward moral good than toward what is morally
evil—and that apart from supernatural grace? Indeed, it seems to me that the distinction about a
life to be led before the fall not according to the human being but according to God was introduced
without cause.

2. But let us attend to Junius. He confesses that the common law cannot be performed without
supernatural grace; but when he comes to explain the rationale for this, he delivers things which I
cannot determine whether he himself understood properly. “We concede,” he says, “that it cannot be
performed, etc., not because the law was natural, but because human nature itself and its own
natural law had been ordered to grace.” He therefore wishes that common law to be natural—that is,
as I interpret it, congruent with human nature—yet not that for this reason it cannot be performed
apart from supernatural grace. This is an altogether untimely and insufficiently sober caution.
For no one, even in his dreams, would imagine that the law could not be performed without
supernatural grace for the reason that it is natural, that is, congruent with nature. On the
contrary, we would be inclined from this to suspect that a law which is natural, that is, congruent
with human nature, can be performed even apart from supernatural grace, just as Junius himself
soon confesses that “what is natural insofar as it is natural lies within the human being’s power.”
Therefore this statement of Junius is excessively ill-formed. With its form changed, it would
proceed more suitably in this way: “We concede that this law cannot be performed without
supernatural grace. For although this common law is natural, and what is natural insofar as it is
natural lies within the human being’s power, nevertheless, insofar as this law is ordered and
referred to grace, it could not be performed without supernatural grace.” And thus it would agree
exactly with the statement of Junius immediately following. Nevertheless, the rationale indicated
by Junius for why the law cannot be performed apart from grace, and supernatural grace at that, is
so obscure that I despair entirely of comprehending any sound and solid understanding of it in my
mind. For what does it mean for God’s law to be referred or ordered to grace? And again, what does
it mean for the law, because of this relation or ordination, not to be performable without
supernatural grace? These are riddles through and through; to solve them we must consult some
Sphinx, but whoever wishes may determine where in the world one can be found to explain them to us.

I see no objection at all to one who maintains that this particular law could be performed by the
human being even apart from supernatural grace. Junius could therefore have rested in this
response, wholly omitting the discussion of that common law, which was introduced beyond the word
of God and was going to create far greater troubles for Junius, as has already been shown in part
from what precedes, while in the meantime it was by no means sufficient to weaken Arminius’s
argument directed against Junius’s opinion.

Therefore, when Junius acknowledges that the first proof proceeds concerning the common law, he
simultaneously establishes Arminius’s argument.

The anticipation of an objection which Junius makes seems no less strange to me. For what need
would there have been of supernatural grace for Adam to restrain himself in a natural and
indifferent matter? Or what use at all would supernatural grace have on this point? Then again,
does Junius deny that supernatural grace was at hand for Adam? To what purpose, then, is this
objection, and how wrongly is it introduced? It supposes, of course, that supernatural grace had in
no way been granted to Adam, although Junius supposes the contrary; and again, it attributes to
this grace the efficacy of restraining Adam from eating the forbidden fruit, when the office of
supernatural grace is not to order us rightly with respect to natural actions, but with respect to
supernatural ones. You will say, “But supernatural grace is also required for natural actions to be
ordered rightly.” But, I say, if it is required, it is surely required for this reason: because the
rectitude required in natural actions is supernatural, not merely natural. For if it were merely
natural, natural powers would suffice to provide it, and supernatural powers would be required in
vain.

Finally, however, I cannot approve his making spiritual life twofold, one natural and the other
supernatural. I think that spiritual life consists in supernatural grace alone, by which it comes
about that we rest upon God both by faith and by love above all things. And I judge that
supernatural grace is required for this alone. But now let us retrace Arminius’s steps.

3. I acknowledge that Junius accomplished nothing by this precision of his (*akribeia*) toward
warding off Arminius’s blow; and therefore at the entrance to this section I proposed to myself that
another solution to this argument must be set out.

Enough and more has already been said about how perversely this distinction of Arminius was
conceived. What else is it to live according to God than to comply with God’s admonitions and
commands? Therefore, when Adam tasted the forbidden fruit, by this very act he refused, not
symbolically but properly, to live according to God. What is more, before Adam’s first fall there
had thus far been no disobedient will in Adam according to which, while he lived, he could have been
said not to live according to God. Nor does Junius support so ill-formed an opinion when he says
that “it did not lie within the human being’s power both to abstain and to eat under that
institution and ordinance of the particular law pronounced by God.” Arminius presses this with a
bold assertion conceived without regard for modesty, although Junius’s words both concern that law
considered properly, not symbolically, and signify nothing more than that it was not entirely up to
Adam whether to eat at pleasure or to restrain himself from eating. But what sort of consequence is this?
Adam was not permitted to taste the forbidden fruit; therefore he could not restrain himself from
tasting the forbidden fruit without supernatural grace. This law certainly had to be performed by
the human being under God’s ordinance; but from this it by no means follows that it could not have
been performed by him apart from

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 62 | BSB canvas 74]

supernatural grace. I leave aside again Arminius’s ill-formed distinction when he says that the law
had to be performed by the human being not merely in point of fact, but under God’s ordinance—as
though these things were distinct. For what must be performed by the human being must undoubtedly
be performed as a duty. Nothing can be said to have to be performed by him unless the duty of
performing it rests upon him. But whence did the duty of performing anything rest upon Adam except
from God’s ordinance? Yet while Arminius wishes to be precise, see whether he does not become
delirious.

I acknowledge once more Junius’s lack of foresight, since he by no means sufficiently guarded
himself against Arminius’s attacks; and so in my initial treatment of this section I undertook to
solve those arguments differently. Arminius adds that obedience, insofar as it consisted [the
printed Latin has *cessantem*] not only in an external deed but in an inward affection of the mind,
could not have been performed by Adam without supernatural grace. But let him merely say so; it
makes no difference. It seems astonishing that a morally good human being could not to that extent
render obedience from an inward affection of the mind, when nothing else was required than that,
for the sake of the divine command, he restrain himself from a thing by no means necessary. For
what? Are not human beings, notwithstanding the state of corruption, sometimes found rendering
greater obedience than this to other human beings—servants to masters, sons to parents, subjects
to princes? And do they not do so from love for masters, parents, and princes? And will it seem
strange if Adam, newly created, could even apart from supernatural grace be borne by no less love
toward God his creator than corrupted human beings are borne toward their country, parents,
masters, and princes? I do not deny that there is a certain inward affection and love by which we
are borne toward God and which cannot be furnished apart from supernatural grace. But this is the
love of God unto contempt of oneself.

Nevertheless, there is reason for suspicion: I seem to foresee that an acute reader devoted to
investigating the truth will be somewhat astonished at this doctrine, and that scruples not to be
disregarded will arise from it in human minds. For what? Does it accord with our theology to affirm
that Adam could obey God or guard himself from sin without grace? Do not all circumscribe the
permission of sin by the denial of grace? I shall therefore apply myself to removing these scruples,
and I proceed as follows so that the reader may be left well composed on this point too. I wholly
deny that sin universally can be avoided without grace in any particular action; but I made it
clear long ago in a special digression that a certain and definite sin can be avoided apart from
grace. For animal and carnal human beings often restrain themselves from murder, adultery, theft,
and insult. Gentiles also often do the things of the law (Romans 2). But that they should holily
avoid what is prohibited by God, or holily do what is commanded by God, cannot come about without
grace. Applying this distinction to Adam, I say that it lay within Adam’s free will, apart from
grace, either to eat of the forbidden fruit or to restrain himself from eating it. But he could not
holily restrain himself without grace. You will ask what this means and how either an operation or
the omission of an action becomes holy. I answer: whatever is done or omitted is done or omitted
holily when it is done or omitted on account of God loved above all things. You will say,
“Therefore Adam could have sinned while restraining himself from the forbidden fruit.” I
acknowledge that he could have; nevertheless, God was unwilling to expel Adam from Paradise unless
he were found guilty of manifest transgression. Again, whenever God keeps even carnal human beings
from more atrocious sins, this is customarily ascribed to God’s grace—not, however, to grace
affecting them inwardly and restraining them in a holy manner, but to grace assisting externally and
restraining the demon from attacking rather than the human being from succumbing. Thus one can
easily defend the claim that no sin is avoided apart from God’s grace—not sanctifying grace, which
is supernatural, but grace exercising general providence, which is only natural.

Finally, the human being’s different states must be considered: apart from Satan’s temptation and
under Satan’s temptation. When enticements to sin are presented, flattering under the appearance
of pleasure or advantage, someone will be able to resist them because the worldly troubles are far
greater as evils than those allurements are as goods. This resistance is not holy or on account of
God, and it can occur apart from supernatural grace. But whenever someone resists on account of God
and God loved above all things, then this restraint is holy and according to God, and does not
occur apart from supernatural grace. For here the love of God unto contempt of oneself begins to
shine in the human being’s breast. See whether it was not fitting to apply these things to Adam’s
transgression.
