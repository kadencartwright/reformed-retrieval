ON VERSE FOUR, AND SO FORTH. “Just as he chose us.”

[Marginalia: Lecture V.]

There now follows an explanation or exposition of that same general
proposition, and this by an induction of its parts or species—that is, of
those particular blessings with which the Father blessed us abundantly in
Christ in the heavenly places. In this induction the Apostle observes the
natural order of the things themselves and, by a synthetic method, descends
from the highest causes to the later and lower effects themselves.

For the spiritual benefits of God in Christ, I mean those which pertain to
the heavenly life, can be considered by us in three ways. (1) They may be
considered in God’s own counsel or eternal predestination, insofar as they
were prepared for us in Christ before the foundation of the world. (2) They
may be considered insofar as they were furnished for us in Christ in time,
when he actually accomplished that wonderful work of our redemption. In him
all the treasures of blessings were deposited and laid up; he did, suffered,
and fulfilled for us everything that had to be performed and fulfilled for
our perfect redemption, reconciliation to God, justification, and adoption.
(3) They may be considered insofar as they are actually communicated and
applied to us through the preaching of the gospel and the efficacy of the
Holy Spirit, when we are given and sealed with their knowledge, faith,
apprehension, hope, joy, and an inaugurated holiness in this life, until in
the future age we obtain that hoped-for completion.

[Marginalia: Threefold consideration of God’s benefits in Christ. Application
to the present subject.]

When we consider attentively the sequence of this apostolic induction, we
observe that same order. He begins with our eternal election or
predestination, according to which God purposed from eternity to bless us in
Christ with every spiritual blessing. He then descends to the redemption
itself which Christ furnished for us in time, even before we had been given
faith or knowledge—indeed, before we had been born and brought forth into the
light of this world. Finally, he teaches in what manner we were actually made
participants in this redemption.

From this the Apostle’s general purpose in the following verses is evident:
namely, both to explain the notable proposition set down at the beginning and
to confirm it by the induction that follows. For this very full and notable
induction of heavenly benefits serves not only to illustrate that proposition
but also to confirm it in this manner: God the Father chose or predestined us
in Christ from eternity both to the end and to the means; he accomplished
those same means through Christ in the fullness of time; and at the appointed
time he also made us participants in them by effectually calling, making
alive, justifying, adopting, and sealing us by his Spirit. Therefore God the
Father blessed us in Christ with every spiritual blessing.

[Marginalia: Purpose.]

Let us now approach the first part of this induction, which concerns election
or predestination, the fountain and foundation of all the other benefits. The
Apostle therefore begins with it by the best right. Although his discourse is
so artfully connected like a skillfully fashioned chain that it may seem very
difficult to discover its several links and joints, nevertheless, relying on
divine assistance and especially employing the light of analytical logic, we
shall endeavor to investigate and propose that distinction of its several
members which the nature and condition of the matters treated will bear. We
will not drag and twist the things themselves violently into our analysis;
rather, we will adapt and apply the analysis and the other precepts of the art
to the things themselves. This first part is contained in the next three
verses, which we shall first read to you.

[Marginalia: Part 1.]

“Just as,” and so forth. It is as if he said: That God the Father blessed us,
that is, the saints and faithful, so abundantly in Christ, but did not bless
all other human beings equally, did not befall us rashly or by chance, without
any definite counsel. Nor did it befall us on account of any merits,
worthiness, or excellence in us above other human beings—far be it that any
such thought should enter our mind. Nor did it arise from any new counsel
only now undertaken by God, as blind and imprudent human beings are accustomed
to enter upon new counsels daily and repeatedly to change, establish, and
rescind their decrees. Far be it that we should think that such inconstancy,
or indeed any shadow of change, can fall upon the eternal and immutable
Deity. Rather, he blessed us in time just as he had determined within himself
from eternity.

[Marginalia: Paraphrastic exegesis.]

He chose us in Christ as our head and Mediator, outside whom in ourselves we
were not capable of such dignity, glory, and eminence. He chose us first for
this end, that we should be holy and blameless before him—that is, although
not perfect, nevertheless sincere and without pretense, so that we can approve
our minds and consciences to him as the knower of hearts. And this is “in
love,” through which that holiness and inward sincerity of mind exercises
itself in action and discloses itself in the duties of the Christian life
toward the brothers. Love alone contains in itself the duties of both tables
of the law.

[Marginalia: In love.]

I prefer to connect this phrase with the immediately preceding words in this
most simple sense and construction. I do not join it, as some do, with the
more remote verb “chose,” as though it referred to God, who chose us because
he was moved or impelled by his sheer love, which is the reading of the Greek
scholia. Nor do I combine it with the beginning of the following verse, as the
Syriac interpreter does.

He then predestined us to a yet further and more illustrious end, namely,
“unto adoption,” the adoption of sons, through that same Christ, the natural
and firstborn Son. He looked to himself, that is, to himself alone, neither
seeking nor finding any cause for his most wise counsel outside himself,
whether in us or in any creature. He acted solely according to the benevolent
affection of his will, simply according to his free benevolence. Invited by no
merit of ours and by no foreseen faith or holiness in us, he attended us most
freely and mercifully, from pure grace, according to the supreme
self-determination of his will.

[Marginalia: In himself.]

He did this “to the praise of his glorious grace,” as a still further and
supreme end: namely, so that his infinite and free beneficence toward us,
being magnificent and exceedingly glorious in itself, might be acknowledged
as such by us and proclaimed with the highest praise. I mean that grace which
he graciously bestowed upon us—or, to fashion a Latin verb according to the
form and likeness of the Greek word, the grace by which he—

[Marginalia: To the praise of glorious grace.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 53 | Caput I p. 11]

—“graced” us, that is, made us endowed with grace freely, or freely made us
accepted, in the Beloved. This is that same grace of adoption which he had
mentioned above. For when we are reconciled to God through Christ and made
his sons through the grace of adoption, we ourselves also are rendered dear
and acceptable—not in ourselves, since on account of indwelling sin and God’s
supreme purity and holiness we cannot in ourselves avoid being hateful and
abominable to him, but in the uniquely beloved Christ. In him every stain of
our sin is abolished; in him all our shame and deformity are covered; in him
we ourselves appear before God’s tribunal covered and clothed with supreme
purity, righteousness, and holiness. In a word, in him we have redemption
through his blood, namely, the forgiveness of sins, from the riches of his
grace, as the Apostle adds immediately in the following verse.

Here we see that the first part of this induction, which concerns our eternal
election and which we said is contained in these three verses, can be divided
into three members. First, the Apostle expresses our election to the first and
nearest end with respect to us, an end which is a means with respect to the
ultimate end, when he says that we were chosen so that we should be holy and
blameless, and so forth. Second, in the following verse he explains and
magnifies the same benefit more clearly by expressing a still further and
more august end with respect to us, when he says that we were appointed or
predestined by God to the adoption of sons, and so forth. Finally, he expresses
still more illustriously the final end of our election and salvation: the
praise of that glorious grace by which, although unworthy in ourselves, he
graciously accepted us as dear to himself in Christ. He did so to manifest in
us and toward us his supreme grace and mercy gloriously, so that it might be
proclaimed, acknowledged, and celebrated forever by all, as it is worthy of
every praise.

[Marginalia: Subdivision.]

Let us now approach these same members more closely and examine them a little
more precisely. Nevertheless, we will not pursue at length in this place the
teaching of this passage concerning the election and predestination of the
saints. God willing, we will have to speak of it separately when the
explanation of the first part, which extends through verse 15, has been
completed, in accordance with the method proposed by us at the beginning.

[Marginalia: Further elaboration.]

Here, then, as I remember explaining above, the Apostle rightly begins with
that eternal election as the starting point of those benefits. Before it we
cannot reach anything further or higher, since we say that the *prothesis*, or
purpose, and the *prognosis*, or foreknowledge, which he mentions in Romans 8
and 9, are rightly included and comprehended in election itself as the first
act of choosing, as will be discussed more fully in its own place. Here at
present we touch upon only so much of the matter as will suffice to explain
the purpose and words of our Apostle.

[Marginalia: Romans 8:29; 9:11.]

First, as St. James says in Acts 15, all God’s works are known to him from all
eternity. That is, in accordance with his immense wisdom God not only foresaw
and foreknew from eternity everything that would occur—for before his
all-seeing, indeed omnipresent eyes there is no distinction of time, but all
things are naked and open, or, as the Apostle expressed it with a very
emphatic word in Hebrews 4:13, laid bare without any hiding place before his
face. He also effectually decreed in his eternal counsel everything that
would occur in its own time. Although eternal, most wise, and immutable, this
counsel is nevertheless free and depends upon his sheer self-determination
and good pleasure, that is, upon the supreme freedom and good pleasure of his
will. It rests upon the supreme right, authority, and dominion which he has
from himself over all creatures and over not only everything that exists or
will exist but also everything which can exist or come to pass, that is,
everything whose existence or futurity would not be contrary to the nature of
God or of the things themselves.

[Marginalia: Acts 15:18. Hebrews 4:13. 1.]

Second, just as this counsel of God must be most wise, it is beyond dispute
that in it God proposed the best ends to himself for all things, especially
his own glory as the ultimate, supreme, and most excellent end of all. He
therefore ordained all things to their ends, not only to that ultimate and
supreme end but also to the other intermediate, nearer, or lower ends. This
especially applies to rational creatures, angels and human beings, in whom,
as the noblest of his works and those which most nearly approach his own
image, he decreed to manifest his glory, goodness, righteousness, and mercy
in an excellent and plainly divine manner.

[Marginalia: 2.]

From this, that eternal counsel of God concerning human beings in particular,
about whom our Apostle and we are speaking here, is called *predestination*
by theologians, from the end to which God in his counsel destined, that is,
appointed and ordained, both human beings themselves and all the other means
pertaining to them. But the ultimate end with respect to human beings—the end
nearest to that supreme end, which is God’s glory—is twofold: eternal life
and salvation with respect to the elect, and eternal death with respect to
the reprobate. Hence there are, as it were, two species of predestination, or
two diverse acts opposed to each other, whose immediate distinction and
difference is taken from these two diverse ends, namely, election and
reprobation. The Apostle treats only election here, which is predestination
to eternal life and salvation. He treats it insofar as it exists in God’s
eternal purpose, not insofar as God executed it in time.

[Marginalia: 3.]

Nevertheless, it does not therefore seem to me that the phrase “he chose”
should be explained with certain very learned interpreters as “he proposed or
decreed to choose.” They say that, with respect to us, we are chosen or said
to be chosen only when God executes his purpose—that is, the decree and
counsel of election—in us. They also say that election properly concerns the
human race as already created and fallen into death. Some therefore posit in
the verb “chose” a metonymy of the subject which occupies something, because,
they say, that eternal purpose of God of which the Apostle speaks here is
occupied with election as its occupying subject or object.

[Marginalia: Note in passing our judgment about this observation of Beza and
Piscator. Piscator, namely. 4.]

I say that it does not seem to me that we should speak with them in this way.
Election is in reality nothing other than that very eternal purpose or decree
of God concerning our salvation, not the execution of that decree which
occurs in time. Therefore, with due respect to such great men, to explain the
word with them as “he chose,” that is, “he proposed to choose,” seems to me
trifling, for it would mean nothing other than “he proposed to propose” or
“decreed to decree,” which is absurd and trifling. They say that with respect
to ourselves we are properly said to be chosen when God actually executes
the decree in us, that is, when in time we are called out from the crowd of
other human beings—

[Marginalia: Confirmation of the judgment.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 54 | Caput I p. 12]

—and enrolled in God’s church. Yet this is certainly to confuse election
with calling, and thus the eternal decree with its execution in time. Indeed,
it is in every way very improper for us to be said to be chosen, even with
respect to ourselves, much less with respect to God who chooses, when we are
called or already actually exist in the nature of things, since we were most
properly chosen before we existed in the nature of things.

What they say about election properly regarding human beings as already
created and fallen into sin and death is indeed true. But it does not pertain
to the present question; rather, it pertains to another question which, God
being favorable, we shall examine in its own place. For God could regard a
human being from eternity as fallen and by nature liable to death, no less
than he could in time after the man had actually been created and had fallen.
Therefore God could choose a human being from eternity, that is, propose to
save and glorify him, or set him apart and select him from the lot and number
of other human beings for life and glory.

But, to say in a word what I think, those otherwise most judicious and
clear-sighted men who distinguish election from the purpose of choosing so as
to say that the latter occurred from eternity and the former in time do not
seem to have sufficiently observed the difference between the term
*election* and other terms which designate benefits that are spiritual and
pertain to eternal salvation but are nevertheless conferred upon us in time.
Such terms are liberation, calling, redemption, gracious acceptance or our
acceptance, reconciliation, and others of this kind.

[Marginalia: Conclusion concerning the cause.]

For if it occurs anywhere in Holy Scripture, or were to occur, that we were
redeemed, saved, or reconciled to God from eternity, or that this grace was
given to us in Christ before the ages began, as the Apostle speaks in 2
Timothy 1, this must necessarily be understood of the purpose or decree which
existed from eternity, even though that grace, redemption, and so forth was
given and conferred in time. Thus, that God gave us this grace from eternity
must be explained metonymically in this way: he decreed from eternity to give
it. But the case of election is far different. Its formal character and
proper meaning, when it is referred to human beings to be saved in Christ,
is to designate God’s own decree concerning human beings to be selected and
set apart from the number, lot, and order of the rest for salvation and
eternal life.

[Marginalia: 2 Timothy 1:9.]

Election is therefore an ordination or designation to life. To ordain and
designate or destine is to decree and determine. But this occurred from
eternity. Therefore, when we are said to have been chosen from eternity,
there is no metonymy here, no trope, but proper and plain speech.

When the Apostle now says that we were chosen in Christ, he marks the
fundamental cause of our election. This statement must be understood
correctly. I do not mean the antecedent or impulsive cause on God’s part,
which is treated in the following verse, but the foundation of our election
which God himself laid and appointed. It is not the reason by which God was
moved or impelled to choose us, but that upon which he determined to build us
when he chose us from his pure good pleasure and most free will. He
determined, I say, to build us upon Christ as the foundation, so that, built
upon him, we might by his intervention be united with God himself and be able
to adhere and be joined to him.

[Marginalia: How “chosen in Christ” is to be understood.]

I will try to illustrate this with a comparison. God decreed to erect the
church for himself as a spiritual and glorious building in which he wished
to dwell and to be glorified forever in his infinite mercy and grace. First
of all, therefore, he appointed a foundation upon which he would build the
whole remaining house and through which he would communicate and distribute
his power, strength, and grace to the whole building.

[Marginalia: Comparison.]

The account of the head is the same. When we say that we were chosen in
Christ as in the head, we mean that through him the Father would communicate
and send down to us all his grace, love, benevolence, peace, joy, glory, and
eternal life itself. The only difference is that in the former comparison the
elect are likened to a house or building, while in this form of speech they
are likened to a body in need of a suitable head through whom they may be
reconciled and united to God. God freely gave them this head, namely Christ
his Son, appointed him from eternity, and in this head placed everything
necessary for eternal life: wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and
redemption.

From this it is evident in passing that, although in God’s decree and purpose
nothing is prior in time and nothing later, since all things were appointed
from eternity, nevertheless, if we regard the nature of the things themselves
according to our manner of understanding and conceiving, these degrees can be
maintained there and observed by us. In this decree of election, the election
itself which occurs in Christ was preceded by the corruption or fall of the
human race into sin, and that fall in turn by the creation of man in integrity
and righteousness, from which, however, man could fall and depart voluntarily
through his free choice, or rather through his abuse of it. The consideration
of the disease is prior to the invention or application of the remedy. Man
was fallen in God’s eternal counsel before God decreed to raise him in Christ
the Mediator and through Christ.

[Marginalia: Corollary 1.]

Accordingly, whenever phrases of this kind occur in Holy Scripture concerning
the election or predestination of man to eternal life—namely, that we were
chosen in Christ, predestined to the adoption of sons in Christ, and so
forth—they must necessarily be referred to the state of man as already
corrupt and fallen into sin. It is as if from eternity God set before himself,
as though present before his eyes, all future human beings already ungodly and
accursed, already fallen into sin and death. Considering and contemplating
them as they all would be, he chose some from that common mass and mire of
lost human beings to be adopted, reconciled, and redeemed for himself in
Christ the Mediator, leaving the rest in sin and death. He did this freely,
from the pure good pleasure of his most just, free, holy, and wise will. What
difficulty may arise here will, with God, be considered below in its own
place. For the present, let it suffice to have gathered, explained, and
observed these things from this passage.

[Marginalia: Consequence.]

It follows that we should consider the nearest end for which we were chosen
in Christ. With respect to the further ends, namely, our glorification and
God’s own glory, it is a means. Thus one may say with some that this verse
expresses election to the means. This means or end is that we should be holy
and blameless before him in love. This plainly presupposes the stain and
corruption of the human race which we have just observed. Therefore, to speak
or think of our eternal election in Christ without any mention, thought, or
consideration of antecedent human sin, misery, and curse, in that order of
causes or sequence of means which is to be observed in God’s predestination
or eternal counsel concerning our salvation, is surely to speak and think in
a disordered fashion, contrary to the natural order of the things themselves
which God the Most Excellent and Supreme established in framing and forming,
so to speak, that eternal decree of our election. Such a disordered
consideration will therefore—

[Marginalia: That we should be holy, and so forth.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 55 | Caput I p. 13]

—lead us into many destructive thoughts.

Let us now touch briefly upon this holiness and seek it from the head himself,
drawing it from the fountain itself. God in his glorious nature is supremely
pure and holy, infinitely distant from every stain or blemish of sin and from
every kind of malice, depravity, and uncleanness. He shines gloriously with
supreme goodness and righteousness; indeed, in his own nature he is holiness,
purity, goodness, and righteousness itself. Hence he is everywhere called by
the Prophets “the Holy One of Israel,” and he willed that “Holiness to
Jehovah” should once be engraved upon the plate of the high priest—I mean the
golden plate which the high priest wore fastened and sewn to the front of his
tiara.

[Marginalia: Concerning holiness.]

He displayed for us a living and graphic image of his supreme holiness,
purity, and chastity in his law delivered through Moses. This was not only in
the moral and external law, but also in the whole remaining body of laws
contained in the Pentateuch, for they savor, breathe, and exhibit nothing but
holiness and complete purity, nothing but plainly divine righteousness and
equity, as the royal Psalmist acknowledges throughout, particularly in Psalms
12, 19, and 119.

Since God the Most Excellent and Supreme is such by his own nature in that
glorious Trinity, it is no wonder that he utterly detests, abominates, and
most severely punishes every impurity, uncleanness, vice, sin, and therefore
every *anomia*, that is, everything which deviates from the most holy norm of
his law, as something most contrary to his holy nature and will. Conversely,
Christ demands holiness above all from his disciples, especially by this
argument, that God also is holy. For this is the perfection of which Matthew
5 speaks: “Be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect,” namely, true
holiness and righteousness, of which he teaches that the hypocrites of his
time possessed only the mask and shadow.

[Marginalia: Matthew 5:48.]

Hence God, as supremely good and therefore communicative of his goodness,
kindly imparted this purity and holiness of his nature to his creatures,
especially the most excellent and intelligent creatures, angel and man. For
although he created all creatures pure and holy in their kind, in their own
manner and as suited their nature, man in particular is said to have been
created in his image. In this very epistle the Apostle interprets that image
as true righteousness and holiness, chapter 4.

[Marginalia: Ephesians 4:24.]

Since man wholly lost this image of God in Adam, he recovered it in the
second Adam, that is, Jesus Christ, and did so through the Spirit of Christ.
For when we are joined to Christ through faith and the Holy Spirit—whence our
union with him and with one another is called by the Apostle in Philippians 2
“fellowship of the Spirit”—that Spirit of Christ certainly cannot be idle in
us. Rather, as Christ himself is most holy and pure, so the Spirit must also
work holiness in us, renewing our minds, wills, and affections and conforming
us to Christ’s own image. Hence he is called the Holy Spirit not only because
he himself is most holy and pure, which is common to all three persons of the
most holy Deity, but also because he is the immediate author of all holiness
in us and its nearest efficient cause in our hearts. Therefore the Apostle
also calls him the Spirit of sanctification in Romans 1:4.

[Marginalia: Philippians 2:1. Romans 1:4.]

This holiness, therefore, is nothing other than that purity and integrity of
all the faculties of our soul—understanding, will, affections, and so
forth—through which we are rendered conformable to God and to Christ our
head. It is therefore that image of Christ and that life of God begun here in
us through the operation of Christ’s Spirit, but to be perfected at last in
the kingdom of glory. Hence what the Gentile philosophers once asserted of
their philosophy, more cleverly and magnificently than truly, when they
defined it as “likeness to God so far as is possible for man,” can truly
belong only to this holiness, whose one author in the elect and regenerate is
the effectual Spirit of Christ.

[Marginalia: Plato.]

This holiness is next described first from its effect when the added word
“blameless” explains it. Those who are truly holy and free from every stain
and blemish, both in mind and in their outward life and conduct, are also
blameless and liable to no reproach, since they are associated with no fault.

[Marginalia: Blameless.]

Then two conditions are added. The former is “before him”; the latter, “in
love.” These can be taken in two ways. First, we may understand holiness as
that perfect holiness of Christ imputed to us through faith, since it alone
can bear God’s tribunal and through it alone we can appear and stand before
his face. By love, however, we may understand that imperfect holiness which
inheres in us and has been begun in us, which is bound by an inseparable bond
to the former holiness, namely, the righteousness and holiness of Christ
imputed to us, and about which we have already spoken.

[Marginalia: Before him. First exposition.]

Yet it certainly seems better to refer the former phrase, “before God,” to
sincerity and integrity of mind, which distinguishes the truly faithful from
hypocrites. Thus in 1 Timothy 1 the Apostle called faith “unfeigned.” Without
this sincerity of mind, although it is not itself perfect in every respect, no
one can be pleasing and acceptable to God; no one may dare to appear before
the searcher of hearts and stand before his eyes. Hence the Psalmist often
asks God to search, test, and examine his heart and inmost affection when he
wishes to prove and attest his innocence and sincerity of mind before his God
and vindicate it from the slanders of his enemies.

[Marginalia: Second exposition.]

Hezekiah, king of Judah, expressly explains the phrase in this way: “I beg
you, Jehovah, remember,” he says, “that I have walked before you continually,
faithfully, and with an undivided heart, and have done what seems good in your
eyes.” Here, with the Holy Spirit as interpreter, we see what it is to walk
before God: to walk faithfully and with an undivided heart.

[Marginalia: 2 Kings 20:3.]

The latter phrase, “in love,” was added by the Apostle as a necessary and
inseparable companion and effect of that sincerity before God which he had
just mentioned. Whoever conducts himself before God with a sincere and
undivided mind is also necessarily endowed with true love toward his
neighbor, and he necessarily bears outward witness to this sincere and
inward holiness and purity of his mind through outward works of love.

[Marginalia: In love.]

I do not restrict this love with some to outward works alone. Indeed, I think
the word chiefly denotes the immediate root of those works, namely, the
inward affection of the mind, that sincere love by which a person attends the
brothers on account of God and in God, from a pure heart and an undivided and
sincere mind. I mean instead that it is wholly impossible for this true love
to be idle and entirely at rest from every work. Although, therefore—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 56 | Caput I p. 14 (through Lecture V close)]

—all works of holiness can be reduced to those three heads which the Apostle
touches on in Titus 2 when he says that the saving grace of God has shone upon
all so that we may live devoutly, justly, and soberly in the present age—the
first of which pertains to God, the second to our neighbor, and the third to
ourselves—nevertheless, both the Prophets everywhere and the Apostles are
accustomed chiefly to press and commend to the faithful the works and effects
of love. These have the greatest power to attest to everyone that sincerity
of mind and unfeigned faith, indeed, more power than the outward exercises of
piety themselves, which hypocrites often perform far more diligently than
works of love.

[Marginalia: Titus 2:12.]

Therefore the Apostle rightly adds love to sincerity of mind, which cannot
exist without the true love of God. For, I ask, what heart can be truly
sincere in God’s sight unless it is a heart kindled and inflamed with the
true love of God and Christ? The love of God in Christ, that filial love by
which the faithful soul is wholly and ardently borne toward God as Father and
rests wholly in him alone, is the one holy fire which consumes all the dregs
and filth of native impurity and truly purifies and refines the soul. The
Apostle therefore adds Love as the effect and inseparable companion, and as a
better-known witness and judge.
