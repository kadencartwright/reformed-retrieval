# CHAPTER XVIII

## The objections of others are refuted

There are, furthermore, other objections brought forward by others which it
is now fitting to examine.

*Objection 1.* This law was enacted by Christ himself, who was that Angel of
the Lord who appeared in the bush. He, however, could make no covenant with
them other than the covenant of grace.

I reply: 1. We concede that no covenant other than the covenant of grace was
made with them. For although the covenant of works was then set before them,
it was not set before them so that the Israelites might seek righteousness
from its works. Rather, it was to serve as a means of driving them outside
themselves and compelling them to go to Christ, the Mediator of the covenant
of grace and the seed of the woman, and to that seed of Abraham in whom all
nations were to be blessed. Therefore, properly speaking, we cannot say that
the covenant of works was made with them. We say instead that the covenant of
grace was made with them and that the covenant of works was only set before
them to advance that covenant. 2. Christ could set forth the sum of the law of
works for this purpose, so that the covenant of grace might more easily be
admitted and take effect. Thus, when the young man wished by his own works

[Printed p. 180 | IA PDF 188 | IA leaf 188 | IA decausadei02brow]

to enter life, Christ had said to him, *Keep the commandments* (Matthew 19:17 and
following; Mark 10:18 and following; Luke 18:19 and following; and 10:25 and
following). Could Christ not teach the people how great their misery was, how
necessary it was for them to flee to the Mediator, and how they must despair
of acquiring righteousness from works of the law? Did he not very often use
threats? See Matthew 5:22, 25–26, 29–30; 22:13; 23:13–16, 23, 25, 27, 29, 33;
24:50–51; 25:41 and following; Luke 12:5; and 13:3. 3. It therefore does not
follow that, in setting forth the law or Decalogue, Christ did nothing other
than expound the covenant of grace, or that whatever he did, he always did
this one thing—immediately set forth the covenant of grace.

*Objection 2.* The words in Galatians 3:10, *Cursed is the one who does not
remain in all things*, and those in verse 12, *The one who does these things
will live in them*, are not contained in the Decalogue itself, either in their
words or in their sense.

I reply: 1. They were no less contained in it, whether in their words or in
their sense, when it was first inscribed on the stones than after the tablets
were broken and the second inscription was made on the tablets (Exodus 34:1;
Deuteronomy 10:1). Yet our opponents themselves acknowledge that the
Decalogue passed into the form of the covenant of works after the people's
sin concerning the golden calf and after the tablets were broken by Moses.
It is therefore not necessary that those words be read expressly in the
Decalogue. 2. Our opponents wish the Decalogue to be the formula of the
covenant of grace, although these or similar words are not found in it,
either in their words or in their sense: *Believe in Christ Jesus and you
will be saved; and unless you believe, you will perish.* See Galatians 3:12.
3. God's very enactment of the law includes this in its sense—namely, that all
transgressors deserve death. Nature also declares to the Gentiles, who even
now have this law inscribed to some extent on their hearts, that transgressors
are to be punished and observers rewarded. Does God treat contempt for his
law as a small matter? See James 2:10–11. 4. The Apostle also understands this
law when he opposes the law to faith and the promise and says in Galatians
3:12 that *the law is not of faith, but the one who does those things will
live by them*. Likewise, when he says in verse 10 that *as many as are of
works of the law are under the curse, because it is written, Cursed is
everyone who does not remain in all the things written in the book of the law
to do them*, he teaches that the Decalogue contains both those declarations
in their sense.

*Objection 3.* Does the Decalogue not expressly mention חסד, *grace*, in
Exodus 20:6? And what grace pertains to Israel other than *the sure mercies of
David*?

I reply: We said some things above concerning this argument brought forward
by Dr. Burmann. Here I shall add only the answer of the celebrated Dr.
Hoornbeek in *On Observing the Fourth Precept of the Decalogue*, page 76:
[He says: The grace mentioned in the second precept signifies in general
God's beneficence toward those who love him, not specifically the grace of
the gospel. Or prove that God's grace toward those who observe his precepts
signifies Christ's grace everywhere. The added argument that the sure mercies
of David must be understood here commits an error by proceeding from genus to
species. Nor are those mercies promised to the thousands belonging to those
who fear God, but only to these people. Finally, God's grace commemorated in
the reason for the precept does not constitute the precept itself. And I do
not yet see that only the covenant of grace is contained either in that
precept or in the others. What is brought forward and its argument pertain to
the precept or to the promise; to works or to grace; either to avoiding images
or to embracing Christ.]

[Printed p. 181 | IA PDF 189 | IA leaf 189 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Objection 4.* Is there not mention in the fifth precept of the land to be
given? Does its gift not pertain to the Old Testament? Is its gift not a
representation of the promise made to the fathers and thus a pledge of the
remaining promises?

I reply: We also answered this argument to some extent above. Nevertheless,
to provide full satisfaction, I am pleased to append here as well Dr.
Hoornbeek's answer in the place cited above: [He says: The point that had to
be proved is not proved—therefore, that the fifth precept contains only the
covenant of grace. For if something in a precept can properly be applied to
the spiritual grace of Christ, or if a type or even a pledge is established
somewhere in that matter, it does not follow that the whole precept is or
speaks of nothing other than that thing. By the land which God promises to
give, Canaan is understood in the proper, literal, historical sense. But if
our friend understands the heavenly land and employs a mystical and spiritual
sense, let him reconsider what he just said, as he saw the matter, about the
Socinian's manner of expounding the Scriptures mystically. Although in the
gift of the land of Canaan one can recognize a representation of the promise
made to the fathers and a pledge of the remaining promises, the fifth precept
does not signify a mystical exposition concerning Christ and the covenant of
grace. Such an exposition is not signified wherever there is mention or a
promise of that land. For Christ's saving grace is not promised to filial
obedience toward parents, nor is this obedience a condition, antecedent, or
any cause of obtaining Christ's grace. The land to be given according to the
fifth precept was the land of Canaan. Yet not only the precept but also the
promise pertains to us under the New Testament, with this change: that land
is not now understood, but any land in general. Hence the Apostle interpreted
the promise in this way: *That you may live long upon the earth.* Thus some
change can have a place in a moral precept while that precept remains intact,
with respect to a circumstance of place or time, and it can pass from one
species to another while the genus, force, and import of the precept—or of
the reason or promise added to it—remain intact.]

*Objection 5.* The Decalogue was not originally pronounced and set before the
people as law. In itself it can no more be called law than the words of
Genesis 17:1: *Walk before me and be upright.*

I reply: If the Decalogue was not originally pronounced as law, how is it
called law? Does God call things laws which are not laws? Yet the Decalogue
as originally pronounced is called law in Exodus 24:12, Deuteronomy 33:2, and
elsewhere. 2. Did the Decalogue even as originally promulgated not contain ten
words, or ten precepts, which bound the Israelites to obedience? And what is
this other than law? 3. Paul calls it law; see Galatians 3:17, 19. So do
Stephen in Acts 7:51, 53 and John in John 1:17. 4. If it was not law when
originally pronounced and set forth, when did it finally become law? Was it
after the tablets were broken? But by whom was it then pronounced, and from
where will this new designation of the Decalogue be proved? Did it become law
when these words were added, *The one who does those things will live in
them*? But then it was also a covenant and had the form of the covenant of
works, as our opponents themselves acknowledge. Hence, according to this
objection, it is plain that the Decalogue was never law, although Scripture
declares the contrary a hundred times. 5. Those words in Genesis 17:1 also
belong to law. Although that moral law had not yet been written, it was
nevertheless binding; otherwise the fathers before Moses would not have been
guilty of transgressing it, for where there is no law there is no
transgression. Indeed, neither would there be virtue. Consequently, Abraham,
by not walking before

[Printed p. 182 | IA PDF 190 | IA leaf 190 | IA decausadei02brow]

Jehovah, would not have sinned, and by walking before Jehovah he would not have
rendered obedience. For where there is no law, there is no obedience rendered
to law. Law and obedience are in some manner correlatives.

*Objection 6.* That covenant was confirmed and sanctioned by the sprinkling
of blood which prefigured Christ and his blood. Therefore, it was the covenant
of grace.

I reply: We concede that the covenant sanctioned in this way was the covenant
of grace, not the covenant of works. But we deny that this covenant was the
Decalogue alone. We have said and proved that God at that time made the
covenant of grace with the Israelites. Yet it does not follow that nothing
besides the Decalogue pertained to this covenant. Although the Decalogue in
itself is the formula of the covenant of works, it was not set before them so
that they might seek righteousness in its works. It was set before them so
that, convicted of sin, inability, and misery, they might flee to Christ; and
in this way it served the covenant of grace. The more remote end for which
this formula of the covenant of works was proposed was that the stipulated
condition of the covenant of grace might be fulfilled. Its proximate end was
to convict them of sin and misery. This latter end pertains to the covenant
of works, and the former to the covenant of grace. It was not the Decalogue
itself, with respect to its proximate end, that was confirmed in this way,
but the whole covenant to which the Decalogue was ultimately referred.

*Objection 7.* If the covenant of grace is not in the Decalogue but the
covenant of works is, then when they broke the covenant in Jeremiah 31:32,
they did not transgress the covenant of grace; and they were not sinners
before they heard the Decalogue.

I reply: 1. By violating the Decalogue they did not, properly and formally,
transgress the covenant of grace, as is evident in the Gentiles who violate
the precepts of the Decalogue. Yet they also transgressed this covenant in
another manner. That is, people who violated the Decalogue in any way did not
in the same manner and by that very act fail to fulfill the condition of the
covenant of grace. Nevertheless, because the covenant of grace commands us
to observe the law, this command is violated by transgressing the law. And
although the covenant of grace itself is not in the Decalogue, the basis of
our duties toward it, as Dr. Hoornbeek says in the place cited above, is
founded in the Decalogue. The virtues themselves and their exercise are
comprehended in the Decalogue, although their objects are various and are
shown not by the Decalogue but by the gospel. Therefore, this inference proves
nothing. 2. The other inference proves absolutely nothing. The Apostle
testifies that the law was introduced because of transgressions so that they
might have knowledge of sins. Did no law exist before that pronouncement of
the Decalogue? Where, then, was the law of nature?

*Objection 8.* If I found these words in the Decalogue, *If you do those
things, you will live in them*, I would say that the law of works is in the
Decalogue and that the Decalogue is not the covenant which God made with
Israel, because this covenant would be directly contrary to the covenant God
made with the fathers. But these words do not occur in the Decalogue; and when
the Decalogue is called the covenant, it is shown that the declaration which
thunders this law of works properly so called is not Israel's covenant, but
that the Decalogue is the law of faith.

I reply: We also answered this to some extent above. Let us now hear what the
most distinguished Hoornbeek answers in the place cited above, page 78: [He
says: I would certainly like to be shown any theologian who says that the
Decalogue is the law of faith and not of works. What? Does the Decalogue not
command? Are works not correlatively set over against precepts? Why, then, is
the Decalogue not the law of works? For you admit that it is law. I prove this

[Printed p. 183 | IA PDF 191 | IA leaf 191 | IA decausadei02brow]

from the matter of the Decalogue and its precepts. The Apostle indeed opposes
the law of works and the law of faith in Romans 3:27; 11:4–6; and throughout.
II. Although those very words, *If you do those things, you will live in
them*, do not occur word for word in the Decalogue, they are nevertheless
understood to be spoken of the works prescribed in the Decalogue and must be
taken of those works. Are works to be done by man not set before him in the
Decalogue? Did God not append life to them if a man should observe them? Did
God truly say that they should seek him in vain (Isaiah 46:19; Leviticus
18:5)? *If you observe my statutes and my judgments, the man who does them
will live in them. I am Jehovah.* Must this not be taken of the Decalogue, at
whose beginning that formula, *I am Jehovah*, is prefixed with the greatest
solemnity? And was the promise of life more appropriately appended, or more
suited, to any other statutes of God than to those of the Decalogue? Rivet
says in *Synopsis*, disputation 13: *The basis of the opposition between the
law of works and the law of faith in Romans 3:27 is the same. The former is
understood as the doctrine in which salvation is promised on condition that
you do all things.* Next, Deuteronomy 6:25 says: *And it shall be righteousness
for us if we take care to do all these precepts before Jehovah our God, just
as he commanded us.* It speaks of the precepts given to Israel after its
departure from Egypt—*and Jehovah commanded us*, and so forth. These are the
very substance comprehended in the Decalogue: namely, the fear of God and the
promise of life. Will you not take Ezekiel 20—*And so I brought them out of
the land of Egypt and led them into the wilderness, where I gave them my
statutes and made my judgments known to them, which if a man did, he would
live by them*—as spoken of the Decalogue? And Romans 10:4–5 says: *For Christ
is the end of the law*—not in the law but after the law, when a man sees
himself deprived of the righteousness of the law and in need of another's,
which the gospel supplies. *For Moses writes that the righteousness which is
of the law is that the man who does those things will live in them.* And
Galatians 3:12 says, *But the law is not of faith*—that is, the law has
nothing to do with faith; faith in Christ is not taught in or from the law,
but works are opposed to faith. *But the man who does those things will live
in them. Christ, however, redeemed us from the curse of the law.* Christ and
the law are therefore opposed, as are the righteousness of the law and the
righteousness of faith in Christ. Matthew 19:16–17 says: *Good Teacher, what
good thing shall I do that I may have eternal life?*, and so forth. And do you
still doubt that the promise of life is annexed to the Decalogue, or that the
words *If you do those things, you will live in them* must be understood of
it? How is the law of works not in the Decalogue when the Apostle James said
that whoever keeps the whole law but transgresses in one point is guilty of
all? Here he refers not to faith but to works: *You shall not commit adultery;
you shall not kill*, and so forth. IV. If this is true of the precepts of the
Decalogue—because a man who has not done them is guilty of death and the
curse—the converse, that the man who does them will live, is also true and
must be understood of it. Or will this threat not also be taken of
transgression of the Decalogue: *Cursed is everyone who does not remain in all
the things written in the book of the law, to do them*? The Decalogue is
certainly in the book of the law. *As many as are of works of the law are
under the curse, for it is written*, and so forth (Galatians 3:10). Where
there are works of the law, is the law of works not there? But works of the
law are in the Decalogue, or belong to the Decalogue. Therefore, the law of
works is there. V. But if the words *The one who does those things will live
in them* are not to be understood of the precepts of the Decalogue, of which
precepts are they to be understood? The law is divided into the moral,
ceremonial, and judicial law. If this statement is not true of the moral law,
is it therefore true of the ceremonial or judicial law, and more true of
these than of the moral law? VI. Does the Decalogue itself not contain a
promise in both the second and fifth precepts—and not a promise of life? Our
friend himself says on page 56 that

[Printed p. 184 | IA PDF 192 | IA leaf 192 | IA decausadei02brow]

the Decalogue contains *blessing, good, life.* VII. It is very strange that
those who say they see the covenant of grace in
the Decalogue do not see the promise of life. Although they do not see the
very words, should they not nevertheless see their force and sense in those
precepts, especially since they must be applied here? VIII. There is no need
to say that the Decalogue is not *the covenant which God made with Israel.*
IX. And, I ask, do these words, *the covenant which God made with Israel*,
occur in the Decalogue? X. The covenant set before them in the Decalogue is
contrary to the covenant of grace which God made with the fathers, but he also
set that very covenant before the fathers, as the Apostle testifies, *because
of transgressions.* XI. Our friend says, *But these words, “If you do,” and so
forth, do not occur in the Decalogue; and when the Decalogue is called the
covenant*, and so forth. Does the word *covenant* occur in the Decalogue any
more than those words do? From this let him see the strength of his argument
and inference. XII. *When the Decalogue is called the covenant, it is shown
that the declaration which thunders this law of works properly so called is
not Israel's covenant.* I do not understand these words or perceive their
inference, for it certainly seems to me that the contrary follows from them.]

And so this objection is fully answered, and certain things stated above are
confirmed.

*Objection 9.* The ceremonial law is reducible to the Decalogue—namely, to
the second precept—and the ceremonial law is the administration of the
covenant of grace.

I reply: 1. From this it can only be concluded that the administration of the
covenant of grace can be reduced to the Decalogue, not that the Decalogue
contains nothing but the covenant of grace. The judicial law can also be
reduced to the second table of the Decalogue. Does it follow from this that
the Decalogue contains nothing besides some political covenant? 2. Although
the ceremonies can be reduced to the Decalogue, it must not be said that the
Decalogue prescribed them specifically. The second precept of the Decalogue
indeed commands us to worship God in the manner he prescribes, and thus by the
instituted ceremonies. As long as the ceremonies are not abrogated, it
commands that God be worshiped by those ceremonies. But it neither institutes
nor specifically prescribes the ceremonies themselves; that was done
elsewhere. 3. From those ceremonies, which are said to have reference to the
covenant of grace, it can properly be inferred that God made the covenant of
grace with the Israelite people. It can by no means be gathered from them,
however, that the Decalogue contains nothing besides the covenant of grace.

*Objection 10.* If the Decalogue is the covenant of works, how can Israel hope
for righteousness and life other than from its deeds?

I reply: If God had set before them the Decalogue alone, or had set it before
them for the purpose that they should rest in it as the covenant of works,
Israel could indeed have hoped for righteousness or life only from its own
deeds. But we assert that God made the covenant of grace with the Israelites
and set before them the Decalogue, which contains the matter of the covenant
of works, *because of transgressions*, as the Apostle says. That is, they were
to recognize their guilt and inability, despair of obtaining salvation from
their own works, and flee to Christ, the end of the law, and thus seek God's
righteousness in Christ and through faith.

*Objection 11.* The Decalogue was not given to cause terror and accuse of
sin, much less as the covenant of works.

I reply: Why, then, does the Apostle say in Galatians 3:19 that the law was
added because of transgressions? How, then, were the Israelites brought into
bondage in Galatians 4:3?

[Printed p. 185 | IA PDF 193 | IA leaf 193 | IA decausadei02brow]

What else is implied in Galatians 3:10, *As many as are of works of the law
are under the curse*, and in verse 22, *Scripture confined all things under
sin*? Does the law not *produce wrath* in Romans 4:15? Why, then, does the
Apostle say in 2 Corinthians 3:7, 9 that it was the ministry of death and
condemnation?

*Objection 12.* The precepts of the Decalogue are precepts of the covenant of
grace.

I reply: They are, because the moral law always binds and remains, even under
the covenant of grace, as the perpetual norm of life and conduct. The covenant
of grace commands the same things which the covenant of works commanded, but
not for the same end in every respect. It does not command them so that we may
be justified in that way, but so that we may testify to our gratitude and
glorify our God. It therefore does not follow that the law contains nothing
other than the covenant of grace, just as it does not follow that the covenant
of grace contains nothing other than the law.

*Objection 13.* But the Decalogue commands faith in Christ; otherwise, not
believing in Christ will not be a sin.

I reply: The Decalogue does indeed command faith in Christ by means of another
command of God—namely, the command in the gospel. For the law commands us to
believe God in all things and give credence to all his revealed word. Before
the revelation of the Messiah and of faith in him was made, however, the law
did not enjoin this, for it commanded no such thing to Adam in innocence. It
does not follow from this that the Decalogue contains nothing besides the
covenant of grace, because the first injunction of faith in Christ comes not
from the Decalogue, or the law, but from the gospel. The law commands obedience
to the gospel because it commands us to obey God in all things. Dr. Burmann
himself addresses this question in *Synopsis*, book 2, chapter 16, section 5:
Is this command of faith a precept of the law or of the gospel? He answers:
1. It is a command of the law insofar as the law commands man to receive all
of God's word. 2. It is nevertheless not a legal command—that is, a precept
containing the condition of the covenant. In a word, he says it is of the
gospel with respect to its origin and of the law with respect to its
obligation.

*Objection 14.* All the prophets preached faith and repentance.

I reply: This is entirely true, and the reason is that the covenant of grace
was being administered at that time. It does not follow, however, that the
Decalogue contains nothing other than the covenant of grace.

*Objection 15.* The Decalogue commands us to love God with all our heart and
all our soul, which cannot be done without faith and repentance. Therefore,
the Decalogue also commands faith and repentance and consequently contains
the covenant of grace.

I reply: Sinful people indeed cannot love God with all their heart without
faith and repentance. From this it follows that the Decalogue now enjoins on
sinful people certain things which cannot be performed without faith and
repentance. It does not follow that the Decalogue also proclaims faith and
repentance. The Decalogue enjoins many things which cannot be accomplished
without the Spirit's special aid, but it does not follow that this aid of the
Spirit is conferred through the Decalogue. As the Apostle says in Galatians
3:5, *Therefore, does the one who supplies the Spirit to you and works
miracles among you supply him from works of the law or from faith received by
hearing?* 2. Adam could love God with all his heart and all his soul without
faith and repentance. Therefore, the Decalogue in itself does not command
faith and repentance. The fact that this love of God with the whole heart
cannot now be fulfilled without faith must be imputed not to the nature of
the virtue which the Decalogue

[Printed p. 186 | IA PDF 194 | IA leaf 194 | IA decausadei02brow]

commands, but to man's fault. Man has now lost original righteousness, and the
Decalogue neither restores it nor teaches how it can be restored. The gospel,
which is distinguished from the law, does this.

*Objection 16.* But God also wishes to be loved as justifier.

I reply: He indeed wishes to be loved in this way by all who are justified,
but not by absolutely all people to whom the law of the Decalogue is set
forth. 2. He did not wish to be loved in this way by Adam in innocence.
Therefore, the Decalogue, which contains the law given to Adam in innocence,
does not in itself command this, although it commands us to love God. 3. The
Decalogue commands us to love God in all his benefits toward us. It therefore
commands the justified to acknowledge him as justifier and love him as such,
as a testimony of gratitude whose norm is the Decalogue. Yet it does not
follow from this that the Decalogue is the covenant of grace, much less that
it is nothing other than the covenant of grace.

*Objection 17.* When God calls the ten words the covenant which he made with
the Israelites, can there be a covenant between God and a sinner without the
promise of peace and without Christ?

I reply: The answer to this objection can be gathered from what has already
been said. Here I shall append only the answer of the celebrated Hoornbeek in
the place cited above, page 75: [He says: God did not first make the covenant
of works with Israel or with sinful man, but with man considered in himself
and upright. Yet among Israel, just as among us, he often proclaims and renews
it—not so that we may be justified by it, but so that we may be taught that
we cannot be justified by it and must therefore seek another way of
justification, which the gospel alone reveals. God did not make the covenant
of works with Israel with the intention or command that they should hope for
life from deeds. He did not make it with Israel as Israel. Rather, what he
made with man and founded long ago in the first creation, he also expounded
to Israel and repeated from time to time—not so that they might seek salvation
through the law and works of the law, but so that, driven away from the law,
they might begin to despair of themselves and seek elsewhere a righteousness
which was God's and not their own. Finally, it was so that they might order
their lives worthily according to its norm, not its end. *Why then the law?
It was added because of transgressions* (Galatians 3:19). *For through the law
comes knowledge of sin* (Romans 3:20).]

*Objection 18.* The Decalogue commands sanctification, but sanctification can
come only from grace. Therefore, the Decalogue contains the covenant of grace.

I reply: This objection is similar to objection 15, and a similar answer can
be employed. Dr. Hoornbeek satisfies this objection as follows in the place
cited above, page 105: [He says: Certainly, as do all the other benefits upon
God's people. But first, God's *benefit* is one thing and our *duty* another.
The Decalogue mentions only our duty. The grace through which anyone performs
those duties comes from elsewhere. Likewise, the sanctification by which we
execute any precept in a holy manner comes not from the Decalogue or the law
of works, but from Christ's grace and benefits. Next, suppose it were taught
in the Decalogue how and from where we are sanctified. This is not in fact the
case, because it requires perfect holiness and teaches perfection, just as its
sum was first set before a perfect man; it does not provide a remedy for the
imperfection and misery contracted from sin. But let us nevertheless suppose
that it is so. Casually *indicating* something is one thing; expressly
*treating* it, or having something reduced to or openly contained in it, is
another. Even then it would not follow that the covenant of grace is contained
in the Decalogue, much less

[Printed p. 187 | IA PDF 195 | IA leaf 195 | IA decausadei02brow]

that it alone is contained there. For just as the gospel, when commending
repentance and also holiness, cannot
for that reason be said to comprehend only the covenant of works, neither
ought the Decalogue, even if it comprehended or indicated something of the
gospel, for that reason be said to be only the covenant of grace.]

*Objection 19.* The whole Law and the Prophets depend upon the Decalogue
(Matthew 22:40). Yet the word of grace is also contained in the whole Law, and
the Prophets proclaim God's righteousness and his kingdom. So Dr. Cocceius in
*Summary of Theology*, chapter 54, section 10.

I reply: Because the Decalogue commands us to believe and obey God in all the
things he reveals and commands us, the word of grace and the prophets'
teaching about God's righteousness and his kingdom can be reduced to the
Decalogue or said to depend upon it. Nevertheless, the Decalogue does not
reveal to us the word of grace, God's righteousness, or his kingdom; it
commands us to obey these things once they have been revealed and declared
from elsewhere. It therefore does not follow that the Decalogue is the
formula of the covenant of grace or contains nothing besides the covenant of
grace.

*Objection 20.* When Christ wishes to commend the righteousness of the kingdom
of heaven in Matthew 5:20, he interprets the precepts of the Decalogue. So
Cocceius in the place cited above.

I reply: Christ does not interpret the precepts of the Decalogue as if they
formed part of that righteousness of the kingdom of heaven or as if the
Decalogue contained nothing besides that righteousness of the kingdom of
heaven. Rather, because the Decalogue remains the norm of life and conduct
even under the gospel, the Lord Jesus interprets it as such a norm.

*Objection 21.* Christ commends the same precepts for obtaining life in
Matthew 19:17. So Cocceius in the place cited above.

I reply: Christ describes the righteousness of works here. As Calvin says on
the passage, Christ's answer was legal, because the young man who was asking
about the righteousness of works first had to be taught that no one is
reckoned righteous before God except the one who has satisfied the law—an
impossibility—so that, convicted of his weakness, he might turn to the aid of
faith. Because Christ knew that the young man was swollen with a manifold
confidence in works, he called him back to the law so that he might learn
that he was a sinner liable to judgment. See the Dutch annotations on the
passage and on Luke 10:25 and following.

*Objection 22.* The Apostles do the same in Romans 13:8–10; James 1:25; and
2:8, 15. So Cocceius in the same place.

I reply: The Apostles indeed press everyone to obey this law because it still
remains in force as the rule of conduct and life. It by no means follows that
the Decalogue contains nothing besides the covenant of grace or is the formula
of that covenant, but only that it contains the duties of those in covenant.

*Objection 23.* Psalm 81 arraigns the Jews who did not believe in Christ, for
on that account they had been made strangers to the goods of the New
Testament (verses 15–17). I reply: The Jews are indeed accused there of not
listening to God. This can certainly be extended to their rejection of the
promise concerning the Messiah, because God had made the covenant of grace
with them. Yet it does not follow that the Decalogue itself is the formula of
the covenant of grace or contains nothing besides the covenant of grace,
because that promise of the Messiah was not presented in the Decalogue but in
the gospel as it was then revealed according to that administration.

*Objection 24.* In the Decalogue, *doctrine* and *precept* must be
distinguished (Exodus 24:12). In Psalm 19:8–9, *doctrine*, by contrast with
the terms that follow, signifies

[Printed p. 188 | IA PDF 196 | IA leaf 196 | IA decausadei02brow]

the doctrine of saving grace which shows the
sinner the way to salvation. *Command* concerns the duties which God
prescribed to mortals; thus, considered separately from the preceding terms,
it denotes the precept which it was incumbent on Christ to keep in our place.
It is plain from this that *doctrine* in the Decalogue denotes the doctrine of
the covenant of grace, while the *commands* outline in it a summary of the
covenant of works. These things are to be compared with Ezekiel 20:11–12,
where God is said to have given them statutes and judgments, and also
Sabbaths. To *statutes* and *judgments* is attached the rule, or demand, of the
covenant of works, but to the *Sabbath* a promise appropriate to the covenant
of grace. From these things it is plain that the precepts of the covenant of
works and the promise of the covenant of grace occur in the Decalogue, since
nothing other than a summary of the covenant of works can be understood by
*commands*, and by *doctrine* a summary of the covenant of grace, which shines
most brightly in the fourth precept because the Sabbath was given as a sign
of sanctification. This is the sum of Dr. de Raadt's argument in *Apology*,
pages 180–82.

I reply: 1. No one, so far as I know, renders תורה in Exodus 24:12 as
*doctrine*. Rather, all whom I have so far happened to see explain it as
*law*. Thus Jerome in the Complutensian Bible, the Zurich Bible, Münster,
Junius and Tremellius, Pagninus, and Calvin in his commentary; and the Seventy
render it *law*. They render it in this way in six hundred passages. I do not
know whether there is any passage in which they explain it as *doctrine*.
Indeed, we do not see it rendered in this way even in the passages where de
Raadt on page 134 wishes it to be taken for instruction about grace in the
Messiah—Isaiah 1:10–20; Psalm 119:72, 77, 92, 165, 173–74—or for the
proclamation of the New Testament—Isaiah 2:1–3; 42:1–4; 51:4–8; Jeremiah
31:33–34. Nor is it so rendered in Psalm 19. How, then, will it be evident
from this that the word denotes *doctrine*? 2. Let us grant that this word
signifies the doctrine of saving grace somewhere. How will it be proved that
such doctrine is meant in Exodus 24:12? Unless this is demonstrated, the sole
foundation of this opinion—that the Decalogue contains the doctrine of the
covenant of grace—vanishes. 3. The proof he adduces proves nothing. Although
*doctrine* in Psalm 19 may signify the doctrine of the gospel, it does not
follow that this word denotes the same in Exodus 24:12. He will not say that
the word תורה always and everywhere signifies the gospel, the doctrine of the
gospel, and nothing else. 4. But he will say that where this word is placed
with synonyms, as in Psalm 19 and Exodus 24:12, it necessarily signifies the
saving doctrine of the gospel. Yet this argument proves nothing, for the
claim is not established. He will not say that this word must be taken in
this manner each time it occurs in Psalm 119—that is, twenty-four times, as
some reckon. And from where will it be proved that we must interpret this word
in this way whenever it appears joined with other similar words—that is, with
מצות (*commandments*), משפטים (*judgments*), חק (*statute*), or the like? Must
it be explained in this way in 2 Chronicles 19:10, the passage which he
himself cites? I shall add others: Deuteronomy 17:11; 2 Kings 17:13, 34, 37;
2 Chronicles 14:4; Isaiah 8:16, 20. 5. In which part of the Decalogue is that
summary of the covenant of grace found? He says in the fourth precept. But why
not equally in the first or third, since he himself interprets them in such a
way that they cannot fail to express a summary of the gospel? He says on pages
155–56: *It demands nothing other from Israel*—namely, in the first
precept—*than repentance and trust in himself as Jehovah their God, and
likewise the renunciation of all trust in any creature.* And on page 161,
when presenting this sense of the third command to us: *With a sincere heart,
having renounced your own righteousness and everything in you on account of
which the flesh appears able to glory in itself, give me the glory of my name,
acknowledging

[Printed p. 189 | IA PDF 197 | IA leaf 197 | IA decausadei02brow]

by faith that I am your God—condemning sin in the Messiah*, and so forth. He
concludes that it is absolutely impossible for this precept to belong to
the covenant of works. In this he contradicts himself, as may be noted in
passing, for on page 184 he says that all the precepts of the Decalogue except
the Sabbath, insofar as they are precepts, are precepts of the covenant of
works. 6. The reason for which he wishes the fourth precept to contain the
summary of the covenant of grace—namely, because the Sabbath was given as a
sign of sanctification—will never prove that the Sabbath indicates the
covenant of grace and nothing else. We showed several times above in book 3
that this foundation is sand, and how falsely he says here that the Sabbath
was first given to the Israelites when God rained manna from heaven. See book
3, chapter 5. Therefore, the conclusion he has on page 185, where he says,
*But if all the precepts of the Decalogue except the fourth contain the
covenant of works, it is certainly evident that the fourth precept cannot be
referred to the covenant of works*, must not be admitted. For so far we have
seen no argument which compels us to say that the fourth precept belongs, or
must be referred, to a covenant other than that to which the remaining
precepts belong. And, to say this in passing, I do not sufficiently grasp how
he can say that the remaining precepts of the Decalogue contain the precepts
of the covenant of works, both because he himself has interpreted them very
differently, as we saw, and because in speaking this way he seems to imply
that God also made the covenant of works with the Israelites on Mount Sinai.
Nor does the appended reason firmly prove that the fourth precept is of a
different nature from the others. He says on page 185: *But so that Israel
might not stumble here, the prophet*—namely, Ezekiel 20—*speaks distinctly:
“And I also gave them my Sabbaths.” Unless we wish to be hostile to Scripture
rather than to our own opinions, it cannot be asserted on any foundation that
the particles וגם, “and also,” are explanatory.* But above in book 1, chapter
11, page 133, we indicated certain instances and showed that those particles
are not taken so *distinctively* but rather *explanatorily*. It would be easy
to adduce other instances. Let the reader consult and consider Ezekiel
16:28–29; 18:11; 21:22; 23:37; and 24:9. Who does not see, then, how vain it
is to infer from this particle that the Sabbath contains the summary of the
covenant of grace and must be referred to a covenant specifically distinct
from the one to which the other precepts belong, or to infer it from the fact
that the Sabbath is called a sign, and so forth, as is evident from what was
said some time ago?
