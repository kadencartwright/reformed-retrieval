# LOCUS XI—THE LAW OF GOD

## QUESTION I

Does a natural law exist, and how does it differ from the moral law?

The first is affirmed; the latter is distinguished.

[Marginal heading: The necessity of treating the law.]

I. Just as the doctrine of the law of God, which with the good help of God we
now undertake to discuss, properly belongs to the theologian, so it has many
uses in theology. 1. *For the direction of life*, as the perfect rule of God's
right over man and of man's duty toward God. 2. *For the knowledge of sin*,
because, since sin is ἀνομία, lawlessness, its true character and gravity can
be discerned with greater certainty from the law than from anywhere else
(Rom. 3:20). 3. *For preparation for grace*, so that by disclosing man's sin
and misery, the necessity of saving grace may be revealed and a desire for it
aroused in us. In this sense the law is called a *schoolmaster leading to
Christ* (Gal. 3:24).

[Marginal heading: The etymology of law.]

II. In Hebrew *law* is called תורה (*torah*), from the verb ירה (*yarah*),
which in the Hiphil means *to teach*, because through the law everyone is
instructed concerning his duty. In Greek it is νόμος (*nomos*), from ἀπὸ τοῦ
νέμειν, *from distributing*, a verb which signifies both *to rule* and *to
distribute*, because people ought to be governed according to its prescription
and each person is allotted what is his own. In Latin, it is derived either
from *legere*, *to read*, as Isidore holds (book 2, chapter 10), because, as
Cicero observes in book 1 of *The Laws*, a law is customarily read when it is
enacted so that it may become known to everyone, or is displayed for reading
on public tablets; or because *legere* is used for *deligere*, *to choose*,
since in a law a choice is made among things to be done and avoided; or,
finally, from *ligare*, *to bind*, as Thomas holds (I-II, question 90, article
1), and most of the scholastics after him, because it ties and binds people
with a certain bond. Relevant here is the fact that laws in Scripture are
often called *bonds* (Ps. 2:3; Jer. 5:5).

[Marginal heading: Its various senses.]

III. The word is used in various ways in Scripture: *broadly*, for the whole
Word of God (Ps. 1:2; 19:7–8); sometimes for all the books of the Old Testament
(John 10:34; 1 Cor. 14:21); and sometimes for the books of Moses alone, or the
Pentateuch, as distinct from the Psalms and the Prophets (Luke 24:44; Rom.
3:21). Or it is used *strictly* for the Mosaic dispensation as opposed to the
New Testament (Heb. 7:12; John 1:17); or specifically for the covenant of works
as distinguished from the covenant of grace (Rom. 6:14); or for “the rule of
things to be done and avoided which God prescribed to rational creatures under
a sanction of rewards and punishments.” It is chiefly in this last sense that
the law is considered here.

[Marginal heading: It is divided into natural and positive law.]

IV. This law of God is divided in general into *natural* and *positive* law.
For just as God's right is twofold—one *natural*, founded upon God's own most
just and holy nature, and the other *positive*, dependent upon God's will alone
and displaying his freedom—so there is a *positive* law of God, resting on his
free and positive right. With respect to this law, things become good because

[Printed p. 4 | Internet Archive PDF 32 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

God commands them. Thus God was free either not to give such a law or to
establish it differently. Examples include the *dietary and symbolic law*
given to Adam (Gen. 2:16–17) and the ceremonial laws of the Old Testament, in
which there was no intrinsic moral goodness or evil, but only what arose from
God's command. The other kind is *natural* law, which is founded upon God's
natural right. With respect to this law, things are not called just because
they have been commanded; instead, they were commanded because they were just
and good antecedently to God's command, being founded upon God's own holiness
and wisdom. Its nature is such that, once the creation of man is posited, the
law could not fail to be given to him, since it prescribes indispensable duties
which everyone must always and everywhere perform.

[Marginal heading: Natural law is understood either broadly or strictly.]

V. This *natural* law is in turn understood in two ways. *Broadly and
improperly*, it extends even to inanimate things and brute animals and denotes
nothing other than God's supremely wise governance of creatures by providence
and his most effective direction of them toward their ends. In this sense,
Psalm 119:91, which concerns the movement of heaven and the stability of the
earth, says that “all things endure according to God's ordinances, because all
things serve him.” Psalm 148:6, speaking of the works of creation, says that
God “established them forever and set a law that will never pass away.” By this
law plants grow, beasts reproduce, and every animal has its own ὁρμαί, impulses,
and spontaneous instincts. *Strictly and properly*, it means the practical rule
of moral duties by which men are bound by nature.

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

Concerning this law, the question here is whether there exists a natural law
of God that prevails among all people as the rule of what is just and unjust,
antecedently to the positive laws of men; or whether justice and uprightness
depend solely upon the human will, arise from the consent of human society,
and must be measured by each person's own advantage. The orthodox assert the
former; the Libertines, the latter.

VI. Long ago Carneades and the Cyrenaics who followed Aristippus denied all
natural justice, maintaining μηδὲν εἶναι φύσει δίκαιον ἢ αἰσχρόν, ἀλλὰ νόμῳ
καὶ ἔθει—that “nothing is just or shameful by nature, but only by law and
custom,” as Laërtius says in *Lives of the Philosophers*. This was also the
opinion of Epicurus, for whom Gassendi says much in volume 3 of his *Moral
Philosophy*, p. 1526 and following. Likewise there are various people today
who follow in their footsteps and judge that nature supplies no rule of the
just and unjust. They hold that such a rule ultimately depends upon the free
determination of men and must be measured by each person's own advantage, so
that man is by nature released from every law and has no other standard of
right and good than his own preservation and the defense of life and limb.
Calvin, among many others, testifies against the Libertines that this is their
impious error. Hobbes joins them on this point in his *On the Citizen*.

VII. The orthodox speak very differently. They affirm that there is a natural
law which arose not from a compact or voluntary law of society, but from a
divine obligation, since God originally impressed it upon man's conscience at
creation. On it rests the distinction between upright and shameful conduct,
and it contains practical principles of unchanging truth, such as: *God is to
be worshiped; parents are to be honored; one must live honorably; no one is to
be harmed; what you do not wish done to you, you must not do to another*, and
the like. Although this law has been corrupted and obscured in various ways by
sin, so many remnants and indications of it still survive in nature that no
mortal fails to feel its force to some greater or lesser degree. They wish it
to be called *natural* not because it takes its origin from bare nature, for it
depends upon God, the supreme Lawgiver, but because it becomes known
objectively from the sight of creatures

and from man's relation to God, and because knowledge of it is implanted in
the mind by nature rather than acquired through tradition or instruction.

[Marginal heading: The origin of natural law is not to be sought in the
precepts of the descendants of Noah.]

VIII. The origin and foundation of this law, therefore, must not be sought, as
the Jews wrongly do, in the seven precepts which they maintain were given to
Adam and Noah and by which all their descendants were bound: 1. not to worship
idols; 2. not to blaspheme God's name; 3. not to commit theft; 4. to avoid
incest and foul lusts; 5. to appoint judges and magistrates; 6. not to shed
blood; and 7. not to eat a limb from a living animal, or flesh with its blood,
which is its life. For besides the fact that these precepts rest solely upon
tradition, which cannot be said of natural right, they are not all simply moral
and perpetually binding. Some are ceremonial and positive, such as the precept
not to eat blood. Nor do they exhaust everything belonging to natural right,
although they can be deduced from it as conclusions from their principles.

[Marginal heading: But from the right of nature.]

IX. It must instead be sought from the *right of nature* itself. This rests,
first, upon the nature of God the Creator, who by reason of his holiness cannot
fail to prescribe to the creature the duties which rest upon that right; and,
second, upon the condition of rational creatures themselves. Because of their
necessary dependence upon God in the moral order no less than in the order of
being, they are bound to perform or avoid those things which sound reason and
the dictate of conscience tell them to perform or avoid.

[Marginal heading: The senses in which the right of nature is used.]

X. Here we do not use the *right of nature* *broadly and improperly*, as the
jurists use it for what “nature has taught all animals” (*Institutes*, book 1,
title 2). In that sense it is distinguished from the *law of nations*, which
all nations use, and from *civil law*, which each state or human society
establishes for itself. For just as reason has no place in brute animals, so
they are not properly capable of either right or wrong. Instead, we use it
*strictly and properly* for that which regards rational creatures alone, which
the jurists include under the law of nations. It is rightly described as the
common practical notions, or the light and dictate of conscience, which God
has by nature engraved upon every person for distinguishing the honorable from
the shameful and for knowing what is to be done and avoided.

XI. Of these notions, some are *primary*, which we call *principles*; others
are *secondary*, which are called *conclusions*. The principles, self-evident
and unshaken among all people and founded upon the common good, produce
conclusions from themselves with the assistance of reason, and these
conclusions are referred back to the foundation of the common good. Some are
*nearer* and *immediate*, and, as they say, arise from nature's first dictate;
they are drawn directly from the principles and are easily known. Others are
*mediate and more remote*, inferred from the principles by a more remote
consequence and with greater difficulty. In this corrupt nature the former
admit no variation, while the latter admit considerable variation. And
although after the fall this law has been corrupted in more than one way in
these conclusions—by native corruption, bad education, and vicious custom,
through which the most shameful vices and crimes are sometimes adorned with
the name of virtues and receive praise, as the grossly unjust laws and most
corrupt customs of certain nations clearly testify—this does not prevent the
law from always remaining the same among all people with respect to its first
principles and the immediate conclusions drawn from them.

[Marginal heading: The existence of natural law is proved. 1. From Scripture.]

XII. Many things prove that such a natural law exists. 1. *The testimony of
Scripture*, which asserts that “the Gentiles who do not have the law”—that is,
the written law of Moses as the Jews do—“naturally do the things of the law and
are a law to themselves, since they show

the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience bearing witness
and their thoughts accusing and excusing one another” (Rom. 2:14–15). And in
Romans 1:19–20, τὸ γνωστὸν τοῦ θεοῦ, “what may be known of God,” is said to be
“manifest among the Gentiles because God revealed it to them.” But how could
this truth be said to have been revealed among the Gentiles, so that although
they do not have the law, they nevertheless do the things of the law—not with
teaching and instruction going before, but *by nature*—and thus are a law to
themselves and bear the work of the law inscribed within them, to which
conscience adds its testimony by approval or accusation in deeds well or badly
done, if this depended upon the bare decision of men, was not present by
nature, and had not indeed been impressed and fixed there by God?

[Marginal heading: 2. From the agreement of peoples.]

XIII. Second, *the agreement of peoples*. Among them, even those most uncivilized,
a certain primordial law of nations has prevailed, from which they learned,
even without a teacher, that God is to be worshiped, parents honored, and life
lived honorably. From it, as from a fountain, flowed the many laws concerning
justice and uprightness enacted by pagan lawgivers and drawn from nature
itself. If laws opposed to these principles are found among some peoples, they
were either accepted with difficulty and observed by few, and were eventually
abrogated by contrary laws and fell into disuse.

[Marginal heading: 3. From each person's conscience.]

XIV. Third, *each person's conscience*, which tells everyone that good is to
be done and evil avoided. Just as through *synderesis* it supplies us with
practical principles as universal standards for things to be avoided and
omitted, so through συνείδησις, *conscience*, it applies and examines itself
according to that rule. From this arise pangs of conscience, which recoils
completely at the sight of sin and the awareness of divine judgment, torments
sinners as if with familiar and relentless furies, and exacts punishments from
the most wicked night and day. Thus Cicero says in *For Roscius* and book 2 of
*On Ends*: “The impious pay the penalty not so much through judgments as
through anguish of conscience and the torment of guilt.” From where, then, do
those pangs and dreadful avengers come, if not from the natural law implanted
in man, which knows “God's righteous decree that those who practice such
things are worthy of death” (Rom. 1:32)? Hence Cicero says in *For Milo*: “It
is a law not written but inborn, for which we were not educated but made, not
trained by precepts but imbued by nature.” By others it is called ἔμφυτον
δίκαιον, *inborn right*, and ἄγραφον θεοῦ νόμιμον, *God's unwritten ordinance*.

[Marginal heading: 4. From God's rule over man.]

XV. Fourth, *God's government and dominion over man*. It is impossible for a
creature, as a creature, not to depend upon the Creator and be governed by him
physically. It is likewise incoherent for a rational creature, as rational,
not to be subject to him in the moral order and not to be governed by him in a
way suited to its nature—that is, through moral means, by the imposition of a
law. It follows either that man ought to have been created independent of God,
which is contradictory, or that he has a natural law impressed upon him by
which God governs him.

[Marginal heading: 5. From absurdities.]

XVI. Fifth, the various *absurdities* that press upon the opposing view. If
nothing is just by nature, but only whatever can be devoted to human
advantage, it follows that men were born for themselves, not for God's glory
or the good of society, although an excellent desire for that good is implanted
in them by nature. The sounder pagans themselves recognized that this could
not be said. 2. Everything will be equally lawful: hating God as much as loving
him, killing parents as much as honoring them. Each person's will alone will
stand for reason and law, so that anything whatever is permitted. Anyone can
readily understand whether this is not to pave a road to atheism. 3. Once this
right and moral government of God is removed, every foundation of right and
every human law—which could have flowed from no other source—will be taken
away. Thus every civil order, honorable practice, and order

[Printed p. 7 | Internet Archive PDF 35 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

in human society will perish, and the world will be turned into sheer
confusion and robbery.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the testimony of the pagans.]

XVII. Sixth, the *testimony* of the most distinguished pagan philosophers who
vigorously opposed this impious opinion, such as Plato, Aristotle, and the
Stoics. Cicero alone sufficiently proves this in his books *On the Laws*, where
with various arguments of the greatest weight he demonstrates that “we are
born for justice, and right is established not by opinion but by nature.” He
also relates that Socrates used to curse, with good reason, the first man who
separated advantage from nature, and complained that this was the source of
every ruin, because once that position is admitted, all justice and piety are
removed from the world. “For if right has not been established by nature,” he
says in book 1 of *On the Laws*, “all the virtues will be abolished. Where,
then, is generosity? Where is love of country? Where is piety? Where can the
will either to do good to another or to return a kindness exist? For these
arise from the fact that we are inclined by nature to love men, which is the
foundation of right. Not only duties toward men, but also ceremonies and
religious observances toward the gods are abolished; I think that these must
be preserved not from fear but from that bond which man has with God.” Hence,
in book 3 of *On the Republic*, as cited by Lactantius in book 6, chapter 8 of
the *Institutes*, he says: “There is indeed a true law: right reason, congruent
with nature, spread among all, constant and everlasting, which summons to duty
by commanding and deters from wrongdoing by forbidding,” and so forth. Later
he says: “It is not lawful to abolish this law, to subtract anything from it,
or to repeal it in its entirety. Nor can either the Senate or the people
release us from this law. There will not be one law at Rome and another at
Athens, one now and another hereafter. Rather, one eternal and immutable law
will embrace every nation in every age, and there will be one God as the
common teacher and ruler of all,” and so forth. “He is this law's author,
judge, and giver. Whoever does not obey him will flee from himself and spurn
human nature,” and so forth.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

XVIII. From the cauterization of conscience which exists in the ungodly (1
Tim. 4:2), who are said to be ἀπηλγηκότες, *past feeling*, and to give
themselves over “to every impurity” (Eph. 4:19), one can indeed infer a
suppression of natural law with respect to the second act, or its *exercise*,
but not its extinction and destruction with respect to the first act, or its
*principle*. One can infer an *insensibility* of conscience with respect to
*duty*, but not with respect to *punishment*.

XIX. Various impious laws opposed to natural law may have prevailed among the
nations, such as those which sanctioned idolatry and ἀνθρωποθυσία, *human
sacrifice*, and permitted thefts, robberies, murders, incest, and so forth.
They do not prove that men were granted no light of reason by nature, as Selden
wrongly concludes in book 1, chapters 6–7 of *The Natural Law and Law of
Nations According to the Hebrews*. They prove only that ill-disposed men
wickedly abused the light granted them and, by struggling against it and
trying as far as they could to extinguish it, were given over to a debased
mind.

XX. Although various practical notions have been obscured since the fall and
even obliterated for a time, it does not follow either that they have been
entirely extinguished or that they never existed at all. For the most universal
principle—that good is to be done and evil avoided—is unshaken in everyone,
even though people frequently err in particular conclusions and in determining
what that good is, because vice deceives us by the appearance and shadow of
virtue.

XXI. What is natural must indeed be immutable and remain the same with respect
to its foundation and principle, but not always with respect to the things
derived from the principle. Reason and understanding, for example, are
natural, but they are not present in the same manner and degree in everyone,
since some are more discerning than others.

[Marginal heading: How does natural law differ from the moral law given
through Moses?]

XXII. If it is asked how this *natural law agrees with or differs from the
moral law*, the answer is easy. They agree with respect to their *substance*
and in the nature of their *principles*, but differ with respect to their
*accidents* and in the nature of their *conclusions*. For the same duties
toward God and neighbor which are prescribed by the moral law are also
contained in natural law. The difference lies in the *manner of delivery*: in
the moral law these duties are declared clearly, distinctly, and fully, but in
natural law obscurely and imperfectly. This is both because many notions were
lost and obliterated through sin and because those that remain were corrupted
in various ways by human vanity and malice (Rom. 1:20–22). Other differences
might also be traced: natural law is engraved upon human hearts, while the
moral law was inscribed on tablets of stone; the former pertains to everyone
without exception, while the latter pertains only to those called through the
Word; the former contains nothing except what is moral, while the latter also
has certain ceremonial elements mixed into it.

XXIII. From this one can readily gather why God willed to repeat this law
through Moses, solemnly proclaim it, comprehend it in the Decalogue, deliver
it to his people with his living voice, and commit it to writing. In the state
of unfallen nature there would have been no need for this proclamation. After
the fall, however, there is such blindness of mind, perversity of will, and
disorder of the affections that only remnants of this law remain in every
heart, like a faded picture which therefore needed to be renewed by God's
voice and hand as with a new brush. There were consequently the weightiest
reasons for this proclamation. 1. That natural law might be increasingly
*confirmed*, lest the notions which remained should gradually drain away
through human vanity and malice or be regarded as uncertain and doubtful human
opinions. 2. That it might be *corrected* in the respects in which it had been
corrupted through the fall. 3. That it might be *supplemented* in the respects
in which things were lacking and had been obliterated. 4. That the *necessity
of the Mediator* might be understood and desire for him kindled more strongly
each day by man's inability and the law's inability through the flesh (Rom.
3:20; 8:3; 10:4). 5. That by this law the Israelite people might be joined into
one commonwealth and separated from all other peoples (Deut. 4:6–7; Ps.
147:19–20; Rom. 9:4).
