# CHAPTER XVII

## Whether the Evangelicals rightly demonstrate from other passages of
Scripture that God wills sin to occur while he permits it. The passage about
Christ crucified by the definite counsel and foreknowledge of God, Acts 2:23
and Acts 4:27, is examined and vindicated.

In the first class of arguments from Holy Scripture we also argue as follows.
If Herod and Pilate, in crucifying the Son of God with wicked hands, did
whatever the hand and counsel of God had predestined to be done, and if Christ
was delivered up and crucified by the definite counsel and foreknowledge of
God, then God willed the unjust killing of the Savior to occur while he
permitted it. But the former is true. Therefore, so is the latter. The major
premise is-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 200; PDF page 251]

*certain. The minor premise is found in Acts 2:23: “When you had received this
one (that is, Christ), who was delivered up by that definite counsel and
foreknowledge of God, you killed him by crucifixion with wicked hands. 24. God
raised him up,” and so forth. Acts 4:27: “Herod and Pilate and the Gentiles were
gathered together in this city, 28. to do whatever your hand and your counsel
had predestined to be done.” And certainly, if Christ was crucified by the
foreknowledge and definite counsel of God, it is absurd that he was crucified
by permission alone—and indeed by such a permission as places it absolutely in
the indifferent free will of human beings whether he would be crucified or not
crucified, and thus whether the human race would be redeemed or not redeemed.
2. It would also be by a permission that is a bare and idle non-action,
including no act of the divine will.

[Marginal references: First objection. Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*,
book 2, chapter 11. Meisner, *Sacred Anthropology*, decade 1, question 4.
Suárez, work on grace and concurrence, book 2, chapter 5, no. 3. Leo, sermon on
the Passion. Pareus, in his castigation of Bellarmine's *On the Loss of Grace*,
book 2, chapter 11, lecture 51. Beza, *Against Castellio on Predestination*,
calumny 2. The Salamanca theologians, *Course of Theology*, vol. 1,
treatise 4, on the will of God, disputation 10, doubt 7, no. 141.]

The first objection here is common to this passage and the passage from
Genesis discussed above. Bellarmine, Meisner, and Suárez answer as follows.
“God,” says Suárez, “willed and predefined the works of virtue and patience
which consist in suffering. God predefined that Joseph would be sold and
Christ killed, but God did not predefine the evil actions of killing Christ
and selling Joseph. It does not say”—he continues—‘what you decreed that they
should do,’ in the active voice, but ‘what you decreed to be done,’ in the
passive voice.” He also cites this statement of Leo: “What was foreknown in the
malignity of the Jews is very different from what was arranged in the passion
of Christ. The will to kill did not proceed from the same source as the will
to die.”

I answer. Dr. Pareus rightly says, “It is true that God willed and predefined
the good sufferings in those evil works.” But the text destroys the gloss and
the gloss the text. For Peter's purpose in that passage is not to commend
Christ's patience, but to console the church against the empty license of its
enemies. Although they vainly attempted to remove Christ and the gospel from
the world, yet unknowingly and unwittingly they served God's wise counsel for
the redemption of the world. “Against the scandal of the cross,” says Dr. Beza,
“they set the secret counsel of God the Father and his incredible love toward
his own.” The Salamanca theologians are also correct here: “Those men did not
bring about Christ's passion as it stands on God's side, or as accepted by
him, for as such it is from Christ and not from them. They brought it about as
it stands on the Jews' side—namely, insofar as it is both action and passion,
at least identically caused by them, and, besides, insofar as it is from them,
grounds the same malice as it does insofar as it is action.” This-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 201; PDF page 252]

*is the same answer given by the Rheims annotators, William Estius, and Andreas
Shaafman on predestination. They want ποιῆσαι (“they were gathered together to
do”) to refer not to the crucifixion but to the event resulting from the
crucifixion—that is, they were gathered together to bring about the good event
that followed the crucifixion. This is altogether absurd. The good event,
Calvin and Marlorat say—and even the Jesuit Lorinus in this passage—is the
redemption of the human race. But God did not decree that Herod and Pilate
would redeem the human race. He decreed that, with his permission, they would
kill the Son of God. 2. It is also false that the text contains only passive
expressions. For the passage in Genesis has an active verb,
וַיִּשְׁלָחֵנִי אֱלֹהִים: “God sent me.” Isaiah 53: “God willed to crush
him.” Zechariah 13: “I will strike the Shepherd.” And how often is God said in
the New Testament to have delivered his Son to death? Peter says that the
Jews' evil action was subordinated to God's decree. They were gathered
together, verse 28, ποιῆσαι ὅσα ἡ χείρ σου καὶ ἡ βουλή σου προώρισε γενέσθαι,
“to do whatever your hand and your counsel predestined to be done.” Hence I
argue from the text as follows. The Jews were ordained by God's permissive
decree to do what they did, verse 28. But the Jews crucified Christ with wicked
hands. Therefore, they were ordained by the permissive decree to the action of
crucifying. Peter's intention is not so much to teach that Christ's death and
suffering were subordinated to God's decree as that the wicked actions of the
enemies served God's decree. It was therefore vain for the whole world to rage
and roar against Christ's gospel. 3. To kill Christ is an evil action. But to
be killed, in itself and formally, is not a moral good, just as it is not a
moral evil, but a physical evil and an injury to nature. Joseph and our Savior
bore slavery, wounds, and death with an even and patient spirit; this was not
a passion but a virtue. Yet virtue is not a passion, but a praiseworthy habit
and, rather, an action. Therefore, the virtues of Joseph and Christ and their
free acts of patience were absolutely decreed by God and were not bare
sufferings. But the Jesuits and Arminians have hissed the claim that acts of
free obedience are absolutely decreed by God out of both the schools and
theology.

[Marginal references and summary: The Rheims annotators on Acts 2 and 4.
Estius on the passage. Andreas Shaafman, on predestination. Calvin on Acts 2.
Marlorat on Acts 2. Lorinus on Acts 4. The answer of Bellarmine, Suárez, and
Meisner is rejected: they say that God decreed the good sufferings of the
crucified Christ but not the evil actions of those who crucified him.]

4. This distinction cannot apply to the temptations of the Devil and false
prophets, in which both the action of tempting and seducing and the passive
experience of being tempted and seduced are sins. Thus, for example, the false
prophets speak a lie to Ahab in Jehovah's name, and Ahab believes the lie and
is deceived. Here the passive experience can-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 202; PDF page 253]

*not be decreed by God without sin being decreed. Nevertheless, these things
no less than other sins occur by the
definite counsel of God—indeed, by God's command and precept: “Go out and do
so.” 4. Ames, Twisse, and even Álvarez with his Dominicans, who are by no means
second to the Jesuits in learning, observe that it is absurd for the
sufferings of Christ to be predefined without all their individuating
properties also being predefined, since action and passion are physically and
really the same motion. For this obscures the glory of divine providence too:
not merely that God elicited the far surpassing end, the redemption of the
human race, from the most just and excellent suffering on Christ's part, but
that he drew the far more eminent end, the salvation of the elect, from the
wicked actions of the murderers who slaughtered the Lord of glory. 5. God's
decrees are foolishly multiplied without necessity. The same actions can be
decreed by the same decree as evil actions occurring with God's permission,
since they are the means of the redemption of the human race.

[Marginal reference: 1 Kings 22:19–20.]

Second, Vorstius replies: “The sense is”—he says—“that while pursuing another
purpose, they served the most wise counsel of God. This did not happen while
God was ignorant, idly watching, or unconcerned, still less against his will,
but with his singular providence intervening. Christ was, as it were,
delivered and conceded to the power of the Jews, partly to exact vengeance for
their contempt of the gospel, to bring Christ to completion, and effectively
to procure through him the eternal salvation of the human race. Nevertheless,
no sin occurs with God willing it, but with him unwilling it and not preventing
it.”

[Marginal references and summary: Second objection. Vorstius, *Friendly
Conference Doubled against Piscator*, p. 178. Vorstius teaches that God's
conditional and commanding will intervenes concerning sin.]

I answer: 1. It is not enough to heap up words, as though the Savior's
crucifixion occurred “not while God was ignorant, idly watching, or
unconcerned.” He names no act of special providence here, only a bare delivery
into the hands of enemies in whose power it lay absolutely to kill Christ or
not to kill him. 2. What occurs “not while God is ignorant or idly watching,
nor against God's will or with him unconcerned,” occurs with God in some way
willing and decreeing it. For bare permission, insofar as it is distinguished
from God's being unwilling and unconcerned, is a mere non-volition and bare
inspection. But elsewhere Vorstius speaks as follows: “It is not proved from
this passage that God antecedently and absolutely willed the Jews to kill
Christ.” Yet because you, Vorstius, make the approving will, or will of sign,
entirely the same as God's decretive will, your distinction here will be
defective unless you mean that God wills it consequently and conditionally,
and therefore loves, approves, and commands it.-*

[Marginal reference: *Friendly Conference Doubled*, p. 37.]

[Chapter XVII; printed page 203; PDF page 254]

*(For with you the decreeing and the approving or commanding will is entirely
the same.) Thus God would will that the Jews kill Christ. And it should be
noted here that because the Jesuits and Arminians reject our distinction
between the will of sign and the will of good pleasure, they must teach that
God decrees whatever he commands by the will of sign to occur, and that from
eternity he decreed the sins he forbade never to occur. But we wish to hear
the decree of God in Scripture by which he decreed that the Jews would never
kill Christ, and the decree by which he decreed that Judas, Cajan [the witness
clearly reads *Cajan*; the intended referent is uncertain], and all the
reprobate would believe in Christ. And we ask for the decree by which God
decreed that the apostate angels would obey and be saved. They will say that
God decreed *conditionally* that all reprobate human beings and angels would
obey. But on what condition, I ask? Namely, if they wished to obey. Yet willing
to obey is the greatest part of obedience. Therefore, he decreed that they
would obey, provided that they obeyed. What trifles these are! Moreover,
because for them the decreeing will is the commanding will, if God wills sin
by some positive act of will—as Vorstius says that God wills sin to exist and
sin to occur, “with God not idly watching, nor unconcerned, nor unwilling”—then
sin occurs with God in some way bidding, commanding, and approving it. But in
that case God will be the author of sin by commanding that sin occur contrary
to his express law, and indeed by some positive act of approving will. Hence
there will be not only injustice in God, but also two contrary and
contradictory wills. 2. If Christ's ungodly crucifiers serve God's counsel and
are punished, paying the just penalties for their contempt of the gospel, God
will be the principal agent and the principal inflictor of the penalty. But
the penalty is the sin of killing the Savior, for permission is not formally
the penalty; the permitted sin is. Thus God must here will sin as the penalty
for contempt of the gospel. Vorstius yields at this point, saying: “From a
singular judgment of God, such as is described there, one cannot always judge
concerning God's ordinary precepts.” But must God not in that case be said to
have willed the Jews to kill the Savior as a judgment of God and an
extraordinary penalty? Yet this would make God the author of sin. According to
Conrad Vorstius, the matter is safe: God is the author of sin in an
extraordinary way, though not in an ordinary way! Can God therefore sin in an
extraordinary case, cause sin, or deny himself? Away with such blasphemies.

[Marginal reference: Third objection. The Salamanca theologians, treatise 4,
on the will of God, disputation 10, doubt 7, no. 140.]

Third, the Salamanca theologians reply: “Since God can-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 204; PDF page 255]

*not predefine sin as regards either its formal or its material element insofar
as it grounds the character of malice, it follows that in this testimony, Acts
2:23, where such predefinition is signified, the discussion concerns the
predefinition of its material element taken materially. It must be said that
by his absolute and intrinsically efficacious decree God predefines the
material element of sin.”

I answer. This is jejune: the material acts of human hatred and the physical
acts of killing are the evil of punishment inflicted upon Christ our surety by
the just Judge; and the entitative acts, which are good in themselves, are
those means from which God, out of the inscrutable abyss of providence,
elicited the redemption of the human race.

[Marginal references and summary: Fourth objection. Corvinus, *Against
Molina*, chapter 4, question 4, p. 50. Arminius, *Anti-Perkins*, p. 696, Bertius
edition. Arminius admits that God decrees the sins of Christ's sworn enemies.
Ledesma, *On the Help of Divine Grace*, on I-II, question 29, article 2, doubt
1, conclusion 4. Francis Sylvius of Douai, 1609 edition published by Marcus
Wyon, section 40, part 4, article 4. Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1,
chapter 32. Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*, book 1, chapter
29.]

Fourth, Corvinus and his Anti-Perkinsian Arminius reply: “It is not said here
that the very same thing which human beings wickedly commit is justly and
wisely decreed by God, or that human beings who violate God's command serve
his secret counsel. Nor,” says Arminius, “is it taught here that God willed the
Jews to slaughter Christ himself, but only that God preordained the delivering
of his Son into the hands of his enemies, so that he would suffer from them
what God had laid upon him and what the Jews would inflict out of their malice
and hatred against Christ. But what kind of people did God consider them to be
when he preordained this to happen? Surely what they were at that time when
they brought death upon Christ—namely, Christ's sworn enemies. Besides, no
action of God appears here by which they were impelled or moved to will and do
what they willed.”

I answer: 1. Nor do we teach that what human beings wickedly do is in every
respect the very same thing that God justly decrees. “In relation to God,” says
Ledesma, “the actions are good and holy; in relation to the agents, they are
most grievous sins.” “In relation to God who effects them,” says Francis
Sylvius of Douai, “they are good; in relation to the human beings who are bound
to avoid such defects, they are culpable actions.” Bradwardine speaks likewise.
So does Augustine, who holds that what is beside God's will is not against his
will. 2. If Christ was delivered to his enemies with God willing
it, this delivery on the part of Judas the betrayer and the enemies who seized
him was a most grievous sin, ἔκδοτον λαβόντες, as the expression of
action sounds. John 3:16; Romans 8:16. But I ask: to what end was he delivered
on God's part? Arminius answers: “So that he would suffer from his enemies what
God laid upon him,” that is, wounds, mockery, blows, the piercing of his hands
and feet, and finally the accursed death of the cross. God therefore willed
and intended the killing of his Son and all those things-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 205; PDF page 256]

*which he suffered through wicked hands, and indeed by the definite counsel of
God. Yet I suppose they would not say that God delivered his Son to murderers
with the end and intention that they should keep their hands from shedding the
innocent blood of God's Son. God could not intend that end together with the
redemption of the world through the shed blood of God's Son. But in delivering
his Son he did intend the redemption of the world, John 3:16. This is plain
enough from the words, “Not my will, but yours be done.” It was God's will that
Christ should drink that cup. 3. “God preordained,” says Arminius, “that death
would be inflicted by the Jews. But what kind of people were they then?
Namely, Christ's sworn enemies.” What is this to the point? You will no more
free the Holy One of Israel from complicity in crime by saying that God
preordained with an approving will that Christ's sworn enemies should inflict
a wicked death upon him than by saying that God preordained by the same will
that Christ's friends should inflict a wicked death upon him. For God is no
more the author of sins committed by devils than of sins committed by innocent
angels. 4. Arminius says in vain: “There is no action here by which they were
impelled to sin.” For God's decree, since it does not really flow into the free
act, adds no impulse toward sinning to the will. It establishes that at some
future point there will be a predetermining impulse in the will by which the
will, with its liberty preserved, is predetermined to the act—but in no way to
its malice.

[Marginal references and summary: Fifth objection, Corvinus, same place.
Meratius, vol. 1, treatise on sin, disputation 11, section 3. The numerically
same death of Christ which he endured through these numerically same evil
instruments was established by one absolute and fixed decree, not
conditionally and under a disjunction, as Corvinus and the Arminians maintain.]

Fifth, Corvinus objects that “Christ's death did not occur inevitably in
relation to the Jews. For although God had decreed absolutely that Christ
would die, he nevertheless decreed to deliver him to death through such people
as would execute God's counsel spontaneously and by their free will. He could
have brought it about in another way and in many ways which he held stored in
the treasures of his wisdom.”

I answer. The Jesuit Lodovico Meratius speaks more consistently with Jesuit
doctrine when he says that “God did not have an efficacious and absolute decree
concerning the crucifixion of his Son before he had decreed anything about the
means, whether it would be done justly or unjustly.” Indeed, all agents can
refrain from acting and from crucifying him. For God does not have stored “in
the treasures of his wisdom ways by which he can bring it about that
contradictories are simultaneously true.” Yet for free agents to be absolutely
determined to crucify Christ implies a contradiction, for a free agent-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 206; PDF page 257]

*would then not be indifferent to acting or not acting once the absolute
decree is posited. Thus, if Jesuit theology stands, it would not be a free
agent; or else the absolute decree could be annulled and retracted. All these
things are absurd. 2. It is an entirely chimerical dream that God decreed
twice, three times, or more often that Christ would die, whether absolutely or
even conditionally. For, first, he thus decreed absolutely the crucifixion—or,
rather, the death—of God's Son, although he had not yet ascertained the manner
of death: whether he would die on the cross, by stoning, or in a popular riot.
At that moment he knew nothing about the ministers and attendants, nor whether
he would die justly or unjustly, διὰ χειρῶν ἀνόμων, “through lawless hands.”
For on this account he would have first decreed death confusedly and in
general. Then, foreseeing through *middle knowledge* that these or those free
agents would not put his Son to death, and that these others would not do so
either, God on a second and more mature consideration—and perhaps on a third
and fourth, still better-digested deliberation—foresaw that he should be
fastened to the cross through Judas the betrayer, Herod, and Pilate. Then, on
a second, third, or perhaps fourth occasion—for who can count all the
revolutions and failures in causes that operate defectibly?—God at last
established a fixed permissive decree concerning this numerically same death
which Christ endured through these determinate instruments and organs of the
Devil. Thus, on the first occasion God decreed “that Christ would die, and
indeed absolutely,” says Arnold Corvinus. On the second, he decreed that Christ
would die through free agents permitted to sin; and if the Arminian faith is
not to collapse, this decree necessarily had to be conditional. On the third,
God, foreknowing that these instruments would infallibly put him to death,
fixedly willed him to undergo this numerically particular death which he
endured and no other. But Scripture says that everything Christ suffered was
established by one fixed decree of God. “He died once at the end of the world.”
Therefore, this death was decreed once by God. This numerically same death was
ordained by the definite counsel and foreknowledge of God, Acts 2:23 and Acts
4:27. Indeed, were these not ταῦτα ἔδει παθεῖν, “the things it was
necessary for him to suffer”? Does not the Holy Spirit in express words
ascribe “the piercing of his side, the fact that his legs were not broken, the
division of his garments, the enemies' mockery,” and so forth, to the
fulfillment of the Scriptures and therefore to God's decree and fixed
appointment?
So all these numerically same wounds, death, taunts, insults, thirst, and
weariness, which most holy innocence itself underwent through these
numerically same organs of the Devil, were most certainly decreed once by God.
Thus Corvinus, that foolish man, together with the Arminians, fashioned a-*

[Marginal references: Hebrews 9:26; Luke 24:26; John 19:36, 37, 24; Matthew
27:35, 42, 43.]

[Chapter XVII; printed page 207; PDF page 258]

*God who is ignorant of future things, undecided and wavering, begging from
creatures and their free acts a new rationale for his decrees, and, as though
in the arena, adopting a new plan as the occasion requires—a thoroughly
anthropomorphic God. He does so when he says: “Therefore, one must say that God
decreed by a certain and determinate will that Christ would die. But that the
particular Jews who killed Christ would kill him was established not by an
energetic decree but by a permissive one. We therefore say that Christ's death
is contingent when considered with respect to the manner and persons through
whom he was delivered to death.” But, 1. The numerically same crucifixion which
befell Christ is thus altogether fortuitous and accidental even in relation to
the Arminian permissive decree. 2. Christ's death was decreed twice. First it
was decreed by a determinate will, and so he could not fail to die, because
this will cannot be annulled and his death is necessary. Then his death was
decreed by a *permissive decree* which leaves to the will of the crucifiers a
complete indifference to crucifying or not crucifying. But for one and the
same thing, even in relation to God's decree, to be both necessary and
contingent—because the decree is partly determinate and partly permissive—cries
out an everlasting contradiction, especially in the adversaries' ears. 3.
Thus God's absolute decree could be annulled. Whatever is contingent for the
Arminians can fail ever to exist; but for Corvinus Christ's death is
contingent. 3. The world might never have been redeemed by this numerically
same redemption which is in Christ Jesus, Romans 3:24–25; 1 Peter 1:18–19;
Ephesians 1:7, if this had pleased the indifferent free will of Judas the
betrayer, Herod, and Pilate. Ah, what slippery providence! 4. It implies a
contradiction that the numerically same death of Christ was decreed by a
decree that was both absolute and at the same time permissive. For thus his
death could occur and not occur, while it could not fail to occur, in relation
to one and the same divine will. 5. Arminius says in vain that “God did not
decree that Christ would suffer before he foreknew that the Jews were Christ's
sworn enemies.” The Jews' hostile hatred and ill will are no small part of the
things which Christ “had to suffer by the definite counsel of God.” We say that
God decreed that the Jews would regard him with hostile hatred and ill will no
less than that they would kill him. 6. Grant that they were foreknown as
“Christ's sworn enemies” before God decreed “that they would inflict death upon
him.” Even in that condition they contingently slaughtered-*

[Marginal reference: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 4, question 5, p. 52.]

[Chapter XVII; printed page 208; PDF page 259]

*Christ, and there was no greater necessity of killing him from God's decree
because “they were foreknown as Christ's mortal enemies” than there would have
been if they had been foreknown as Christ's friends. This escape of Arminius is
therefore irrelevant.

[Marginal references and summary: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 4,
question 5, pp. 54–55. According to the adversaries' principles, there is in
God no certainty of Christ's crucifixion through free agents.]

But Corvinus sees here that the inevitable certainty of the world's redemption
has been transacted through such a contingent and slippery means, and so he
adds: “Since God's permissive decree rests upon God's infallible foreknowledge,
and since from him alone could proceed the impediment which would keep from
being future the act otherwise foreknown as certainly future, it is plain that
a cause can be found why Christ certainly died even if no such absolute will
is posited by which God absolutely decreed that the Jews would crucify him.
That is, as though God could not have brought about the fastening of his Son
to the tree unless he impelled certain human beings to do it by a determining
motion; and as though he could not make use of human beings who spontaneously
willed to crucify Christ to execute that counsel of his, just as he knew it to
be suitable to his glory. You confine the bounds of divine wisdom far too
narrowly when you insist that God can accomplish nothing through human beings
except what he absolutely wills and to which he precisely determines and
impels them.”

I answer: 1. The certainty of divine foreknowledge is not the certainty of the
thing and objective certainty which we are now seeking, but the certainty of
the divine mind and knowledge. We are not asking whether God foreknows that
Christ will certainly die, but whether Christ's killing—and therefore the
redemption of the world—will certainly and inevitably be future in itself
unless both are absolutely decreed by God. For, as they teach, the Jews were
absolutely able to crucify Christ or not to crucify him. Therefore, if the
Arminian permissive decree stands, there is no certainty and inevitability of
our redemption from God or from God's free decree. Once that decree is
posited, either the world's redemption or its non-redemption can occur. All
the certainty there is comes from the free choice of Judas the betrayer,
Herod, and Pilate, which can crucify Christ or refrain from crucifying him. 2.
According to Jesuit doctrine, it is false that an impediment to the
crucifixion can proceed from God alone, for it can proceed from the free choice
of the crucifiers. Indeed, an impediment to the crucifixion is not from God at
all, because the crucifixion is future prior to middle knowledge, and
therefore much more prior to acts of the divine will, as they teach. 3. Nor do
we confine God's wisdom, because we teach that God can produce nothing with
inevitable certainty through the intervention of a free will which always
acts-*

[Chapter XVII; printed page 209; PDF page 260]

*in such a way that it can refrain from acting. For it is a contradiction in
terms that God could produce inevitable effects through means which are
avoidable, liable to fail, contingent in every way—even in relation to God's
decree—and operating contingently, as is the Jews' free choice. Therefore, they
must acknowledge here either a precise determination or some other antecedent,
unavoidable bending of the created free will. This overturns from the
foundation the alphabet's first elements of Jesuit-Arminian theology.

[Marginal summary: Other Scriptures prove that sin is decreed while God
permits it.]

Finally, I can add to these Scriptures other testimonies by which it is proved
that “God wills sins to occur while he permits them.” Thus, (a) “Speak to
Pharaoh, that he may let my people go. Yet I will harden Pharaoh's heart, so
that I may multiply my signs in the land of Egypt.” (b) “It was from Jehovah
that they hardened their heart, so that he might devote them to destruction.”
The Septuagint has διὰ κυρίου ἐγένετο, “it came about through the Lord.”
Munster: “It was from the Lord, who hardened them.” Vatablus: “The Lord did it.”
Arias Montanus: “Because it was from-with the Lord.” Junius: “For it was from
the Lord.” (c) “The sons of Eli did not listen to the voice of their father,
because,” כִּי—which Corvinus most corruptly translates “for that reason.”
Junius and Arias Montanus translate: “Because Jehovah willed to put them to
death.” Vatablus does likewise. So that rebellious (d) defection of the ten
tribes from the house of David was from the Lord. Junius: “The cause was from
the Lord.” Arias Montanus: “The cause was from-with the Lord.” Pagninus:
“Because it was God's ordinance.” Vatablus: “It was with God as its author.”
Our Calvin says: “It was by God's ordaining that the sacred unity was torn
apart.” Pareus: “The cause was from God as a punishment of preceding sins.” So,
