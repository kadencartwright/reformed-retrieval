We therefore grant the following.

On the side of the object, as concerns the reason of things, which we called
objective reason:

1. First, all God's decrees and works comprehended in the articles of faith
rest upon the highest reason. They proceed from his supreme wisdom and
goodness and are directed to the best end through the most suitable means.
For since nothing is wiser or better than God, it would be a great sacrilege
to think about him something which it would almost be wicked even to think
about a wise and good person—namely, that he acts in the least degree without
reason.

[1679 new-series fol. 5 | IA leaf 325]

Concerning his works there is David's statement, “In wisdom you made them
all,” Psalm 104:24. Concerning the decrees there is the apostle's statement:
“O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How
unsearchable, how untraceable,” Romans 11:33. It is indeed a depth which
utterly swallows our understanding, yet it contains wisdom—the most profound
wisdom—within itself. It is wisdom, though it is in a mystery even to the
saints, 1 Corinthians 2:7; it is God's wisdom, though it is foolishness to
the world, 1 Corinthians 1:23–24.

2. Second, we therefore maintain that the articles of faith are often above
reason but never properly contrary to sound reason. Augustine notes that
the Donatists once said otherwise. But faith, while seeking higher things,
perfects reason rather than destroying it. Since the same God is the source
of both truth and reason, since truth is by nature the proper object of the
intellect, and since reason itself is called sometimes *truth*, Romans 1:18,
and sometimes *wisdom*, 1 Corinthians 1:21, it necessarily follows that no
truth can be alien to reason, much less contrary to it.

On this ground we reject the monstrosities of papal transubstantiation and
ubiquitarian consubstantiation, as well as the absurd dogmas built to prop
them up. For example:

1. First, that what is true in philosophy can be false in theology, although
one truth cannot contradict another. For, as the Philosopher most correctly
says in *Ethics* 1.8, “every fact agrees with the truth.”

2. Second, that contradictories can be true at the same time. Meisner was
not ashamed to call the contrary position on their incompatibility “a most
dangerous principle in divine things.” Verronius sings the same tune,
asserting that philosophical contradictions can stand in theology. To make
the deception more effective, they summon divine omnipotence to their aid.
Thus Maximus said of the Monophysites that they use what is possible for God
to construct an indecent conception. But how utterly have they lost their
minds! Good God, what absurdity is not invented and defended for the sake of
their Helen, with whom they are so desperately in love? Yet if little credit
is to be given us, I would gladly have the former listen to their own Luther
and the latter to Augustine. “Whatever is manifestly contrary to this reason
is certainly much more contrary to God,” says Luther. “If the authority of
Sacred Scripture is set against the most manifest and certain reason, the
person who does this does not understand; he sets against it not the sense
of those Scriptures, which he cannot penetrate, but rather his own sense,”
says Augustine.

3. Third, we also grant that it is the height of reason to believe whatever
God reveals. Since he is omniscient and cannot lie, whatever he reveals
cannot be false, even if it is contrary to corrupted reason or above
unfallen reason. This principle is chief among all natural principles, and
all the rest are especially to be understood and explained from it.
Whatever the case may be, in this sense our discipline is Pythagorean, to
borrow Nazianzen's expression: “He himself said it” is sufficient not only
for faith but also for right reason.

Let these concessions suffice concerning objective reason and the side of
the object.

On the side of the subject and concerning reason taken subjectively:

1. First and generally, we do not exclude reason and its use in discerning
and investigating matters of faith. We do not wish theologians to be
irrational. On the contrary, we wish Aaron's breastplate of judgment always
fastened upon his breast. Let Osiander's saying therefore be banished
forever from our theological schools: “When one comes to theology,

[1679 new-series fol. 6 | IA leaf 326]

philosophy must withdraw.” The same applies to Meisner's statement that
“reason is so completely unable to judge the articles of faith that we do
not understand them unless we have first bid reason farewell,” and to
Vázquez's statement, who wished more weight to be given to reason than to
human testimonies in the other disciplines but established the contrary in
theology. What is this but to take people's brains away, open the window to
every heresy and fanatical enthusiasm, and finally introduce
incomprehensibility and atheism?

Since Christ is the Word, far be it from him to strip the word and all
reason from us.

Since grace does not remove nature but perfects it, natural reason ought to
serve faith, just as the will's natural inclination obeys charity, as
Aquinas rightly says.

Since dialectic, which is the art of reason, is a general instrumental art,
why should it not serve theology as much as the other disciplines? Though
the Donatist Cresconius dismissed it as insufficiently agreeable to
Christian truth, Christian doctrine never feared it, if Saint Augustine is
to be believed.

Finally, since in his infinite goodness God has granted us reason's
excellent prerogative, it would be wholly unworthy of God, ourselves, and
reason itself if, as our adversaries maintain, it regarded only these lower
and worthless things and had nothing in common with heavenly things. On the
contrary, as Ursinus very correctly says, God made intelligent natures
especially for this use. Augustine likewise carefully distinguishes lower
reason, which contemplates temporal things, from higher reason, which
contemplates heavenly things.

Moreover, Sacred Scripture sometimes uses forms and terms of argumentation,
such as *reckoning, discussing, judging, comparing, distinctions, proofs,*
and *demonstration*.

It rebukes the lack of reason in Isaiah 44:18–19.

It commands us to be ready to answer everyone who asks for a reason for the
hope within us, 1 Peter 3:15, and to be not foolish but understanding,
Ephesians 5:17. The prophets reason on God's behalf, 1 Samuel 12:7 and Isaiah
1:18. The apostles argue, Acts 15:2. Finally, Paul's most excellent letters
are highly argumentative.

Someone who entirely removes reason from matters of faith is therefore
without doubt as devoid of faith as he is bereft of understanding. For our
part, we do not blame Luis Vives for saying, when he was about to demonstrate
the truth of the Christian religion, that it must be treated by arguments
through which faith becomes reason's cultivator rather than its destroyer,
as Crocius rightly says. Let this be our first general concession.

To proceed more specifically, we say:

2. Second, reason has much force in natural truth and theology, especially
in that which is established by natural evidence: both in things to be
believed, because “what may be known of God is manifest in them,” Romans
1:19, and in things to be done, because “the work of the law is written in
their hearts,” Romans 2:15. This is more so in negative than affirmative
precepts, as Luther rightly observes. Since these things belong to the law
of nature which truth wrote by the hand of our Maker upon our very

[1679 new-series fol. 7 | IA leaf 327]

hearts, as Augustine puts it, they were consequently natural to the first
human being. Whatever the papists argue to the contrary, they have not now
been wholly erased or extinguished. These faint sparks still flash, whether
left behind by the benevolent Creator or lit in everyone by the merciful
Savior, who is “the light that enlightens every person coming into the
world,” John 1:9.

3. Third, we acknowledge many uses of reason even in supernatural matters
of faith.

1. Reason is the naturally receptive subject: as the will receives grace,
so reason and the intellect receive supernatural light. No lower faculty,
but this rational faculty alone, is capable of faith. For although Christ's
command in Mark 16:15 required the gospel of faith to be preached “to every
creature,” a *rational* creature must be understood according to the Hebrew
custom of calling a human being “creation” preeminently.

2. It is also the principle or eliciting instrument of actual faith. For
though faith does not have its seat in the intellect alone, it does not
believe without the intellect. Sacred Scripture therefore uses *to know*
and *to believe* for the same thing in Isaiah 53:11 and John 17:3.

3. Dialectic, philosophy, and the other arts supply not only a method and
instruments of reasoning and other technical devices for better explaining
and understanding matters of faith, which Osiander readily grants, but also
the axioms of the arts and principles of nature, which he denies. From
these, if not the truth of theological propositions, at least the truth of
the connection between their terms becomes more clearly evident.

4. We therefore do not greatly object if, in a sound sense, reason is called
by Luis Vives an *Isagoge*, or an introduction to faith, and philosophy is
called by Clement of Alexandria a preliminary education. It provides
certain preliminary aids through which some who place less trust in
Scripture's testimony may more readily be attracted or convinced and, as
Augustine says, be compelled through truths to which they see themselves
assenting to approve other truths which they had denied. “I do not believe!
I am led to it; I believe! I am confirmed; I resist; I am confounded by
reason, not as mistress but as servant, by a syllogism not presiding but
subserving.” Thus Melchior Cano. The account of the scribe in Mark 12:34
also applies here: when Christ saw “that he answered intelligently,” he said
to him, “You are not far from the kingdom of God.” This was all Keckermann
meant when he said—perhaps somewhat harshly—that the light of the Holy Spirit
is kindled through logic and metaphysics. Goclenius meant the same when he
maintained that a pagan philosopher was more easily converted than an
uneducated person. Though events sometimes turn out otherwise, Gerhard and
Meisner have no reason to beat them so severely, since they themselves
acknowledge that philosophy is useful in respect of this kind of
preparation.

5. As in this sense reason may precede faith as a preparation, so after
divine illumination and revelation it is far more discerning and effective.
Augustine's most correct discipline “receives the weak into the citadel of
faith, so that, once they have been placed in safety, battle may be joined
for them with the strongest reason.” It is commonly said that if reason
precedes faith, it diminishes it, which may be true in some sense; but if it
follows faith, it assists it in many ways.

It then sees and approves as consonant with reason many things which
previously seemed discordant. Hence Bonaventure's statement: “Some things
seem irrational

[1679 new-series fol. 8 | IA leaf 328]

to philosophers which nevertheless seem very reasonable to Christians.”
Before his conversion Paul therefore thought within himself, Acts 26:9,
that by firm reason he ought to persecute Christ. But after his conversion
he asserts the credibility even of the resurrection itself, which was a
strange novelty to the Athenians.

Reason not only perceives revealed things better but also confirms and
illustrates more fully what it has already perceived. Once the seven seals
which closed the book of mysteries have been opened—a book which the
immaculate Lamb alone was judged worthy to open—it is no longer very
difficult to confirm and illustrate the disclosed truth by arguments and
analogies drawn from nature, though this must be done cautiously and
reverently. Justin Martyr, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Augustine,
Lactantius, Arnobius, and other Fathers of old worked successfully in this
kind of argument against pagan philosophers. More recently Mornay, Vives,
Grotius, and other learned men have attempted in this manner to defend the
truth of the Christian religion against atheists. I hope Gerhard, Meisner,
and the others who ordinarily banish reason and philosophy as far from
theology as possible will not greatly oppose this, since they themselves
fully grant that it can be done carefully and under due authority.

6. We grant much to reason in deducing consequences concerning faith from
the principles of Scripture or the confessions of adversaries. Thus in
Matthew 22 Christ establishes the resurrection of the dead from Scripture
by a rational consequence, not by one false and resting on a false
principle, as Verronius dared to blaspheme. Thus from the prophet's most holy
and delightful axiom, “The righteous shall live by his faith,” Paul develops
the whole doctrine of justification by solid argument. The deranged method
of Perron, Verronius, Gonterius, and Arnold for dealing with us is therefore
to be hissed off the stage. They demand everything from us expressly and
word for word in Scripture and absolutely forbid all Protestants, at least,
to use consequences. Their method is contrary no less to our theologians
than to their own and not merely to recent writers but to all the ancient
Fathers; in itself it is utterly futile and ridiculous.

7. Reason is no less effective, so long as it remains holy and sober, in
discerning the sense of Scripture shrewdly and interpreting it according to
the rules of dialectic, provided it also follows the analogy of faith. Thus
Christ says in Matthew 22:29 that the Sadducees erred because they did not
know the Scriptures and either could not or would not reason correctly from
them. For unless one employs the rationale of doctrine, one may indeed be
faithful but cannot be a teacher of the faithful, as Melchior Cano rightly
says. Tertullian likewise requires the discipline of reason in interpreting
Scripture: “We seek as we ought, discover what we ought, and speak with
demonstration,” along with the other things which follow concerning this
matter in Maximus.

8. We therefore also grant much to reason in examining doctrines brought to
us by anyone, insofar as the judgment of discretion is concerned. Luke
notes this as a mark of a nobler spirit in the Bereans, Acts 17:11: they
received the Word eagerly but no less prudently examined it according to
Scripture. He said, “examining the Scriptures”; according to these they
conducted their examination as their standard, while *examining* expresses
the judgment of reason by whose aid they rendered a verdict, and did so
nobly. The heretics' old command, “Receive what is said without question,

[1679 new-series fol. 9 | IA leaf 329]

and let no one examine what in it is fitting or unfitting,” was deservedly
beaten by Athanasius or Maximus as unworthy of an honest person and utterly
unworthy of a Christian.

9. Finally, we maintain that reason has its greatest use in refuting pagan
philosophers, heretics, and other heterodox people of that kind. Clement of
Alexandria therefore properly calls it the hedge and wall of the vineyard:
not Saul's armor, but Goliath's sword. If adversaries grant anything in
Scripture, as
heretics customarily do, we may argue by rational consequence from one
article of faith they concede against their denial of another. But if they
believe none of the divinely revealed things, as may be true of some pagans,
right reason is nevertheless highly effective in refuting both their
calumnies, by which they charge our faith with contradiction and absurdity,
and the false reasonings they bring against it. For since faith, as Aquinas
best says, rests on infallible truth, and since it is impossible to
demonstrate the contrary of truth, it is manifest that proofs advanced
against faith are not demonstrations but solvable arguments. Thus, to use
the prophet's words, a potsherd can contend with the other potsherds of the
earth, and the sophisms of human reason can readily be defeated by human
reason, especially when they are shown to be contrary not only to Sacred
Scripture but also to right reason and the nature of things. Thus Isaiah
reasoned against idolaters as people devoid of understanding; and thus the
apostle—since unbelievers are unreasonable—fought the Athenians from their
own principles and the Cretans by testimonies drawn from their own people.
These weapons must also pierce the monsters of transubstantiation and
consubstantiation to the very heart, although their patrons demand faith and
cry that the intellect must be led captive here. What else do they seek but
to make human beings brute animals, to whom they, like foxes preaching to
geese, may proclaim whatever they please, as Ursinus sharply says? Or, as
another once put it, they seek “to trip up the examination so that they may
escape refutation.”

I have now explained at greater length what must be granted to reason in
matters of faith. According to these concessions, the doctrine of faith and
our whole theology can be called *rational*. In this sense we peacefully
accept the title of a book very recently published by a most learned man,
namely *On the Reasonableness of the Christian Religion*. Although we
rightly laugh at Durandus's futile *Rationale of Divine Offices*, we receive
Vedelius's learned *Theological Rationale* with the honor it deserves.

But lest we seem, by granting all these many and weighty points, to have
conceded the whole case at once, we must finally set forth what we judge must
be denied to reason or the human intellect in the articles of faith. The
whole matter consists of three points. In a matter of faith I deny that it
is the discoverer, the legislator, or the judge.
