VERSE 11. When this good thing is plentiful, there are many who consume it.
What advantage, then, comes to its owner apart from the sight of his own eyes?

He now takes up the pursuit of agriculture and animal husbandry. Since he has
demonstrated its vanity, he has established as a consequence the vanity of the
other thing, namely money, since it is far inferior to agriculture. He confirms
this from the accompanying uselessness of the abundance of produce supplied by
that abundance. He confirms this uselessness from the accompanying multitude
of servants, who are maintained in proportion to the produce. Thus, although
the owner possesses much, no more is available to him than to his servants. He
does not eat or drink more than is sufficient, and the same must be supplied to
the servants.

He confirms this by another accompanying circumstance: the master and head of
the household has nothing left above what the servants have except looking at
the goods with his eyes. If he wishes, he can indeed take more pleasure than
they do in looking at those things—if any pleasure at all is received from the
sight of them. Yet as far as the just and proper use of the goods is concerned,
the servants and their master share equally.

In this sense he appears to censure, by comparison, the foolish love of these
things in those who pursue them greedily. For the Apostle appears to mark a
different expression in Romans 3:30 when he says that there is no distinction
in the matter of justification between Jews and Gentiles, except that God
justifies the former out of faith and the latter through faith.
