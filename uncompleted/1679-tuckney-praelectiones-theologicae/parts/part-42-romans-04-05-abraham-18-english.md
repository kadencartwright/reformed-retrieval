2. As for the second point, it was objected that if we are justified by
Christ's righteousness credited to us, then it can properly be said that as
we sinned in Adam, so we satisfied God's righteousness in Christ. Indeed,
Bellarmin adds that on this account we ought to be called and accounted
redeemers and saviors of the world, since, as Sanders objects, Christ's
obedience and death were his personal righteousness, which was the
propitiation and redemption of the whole world.

I answer:

1. As for Bellarmine's latter claim, it by no means follows

that the imputation of Christ's righteousness constitutes us redeemers of
the world, because “Christ's passion is credited to each as though he
himself had suffered for himself, but not for others.”

Indeed, this imputation does not even make anyone his own redeemer. I answer
the point as follows.

1. *Ad hominem*: being called our own redeemers and saviors ought not at
least to be so distasteful to Bellarmine and his followers, since in book 1,
chapter 14, *On Purgatory*, Bellarmine expressly says that “a man can
properly be called his own redeemer and savior.” This is not merely a single
statement of Bellarmine which someone might say he rashly blurted out. It
agrees completely with the common doctrine of the other Romanists about our
merits and our own satisfactions of divine righteousness. We utterly abhor
such blasphemies and do not assert that they follow from this imputation of
Christ's righteousness to us.

2. If we held that Christ's own righteousness inheres in us, or, with
Osiander, that we are justified by Christ's essential righteousness itself,
their argument would have some force in proving this. But we do not affirm
that Christ's own righteousness inheres in us, nor even that his essential
righteousness is credited to us for justification, but only his mediatorial
righteousness. Thus their argument has not even the shadow of a valid
consequence. For:

3. Crediting something belonging to one person to another does not cause
the receiving person actively but only passively to be what is credited.
Thus when Romans 2:26 says that uncircumcision is credited as circumcision
to one who keeps the law, it does not make him one who actively circumcises
but one passively circumcised. When our sins are credited to Christ, this
imputation does not make Christ an active sinner, but passive only insofar
as he bore our sins. Likewise, from the imputation of Christ's redemption
and righteousness to us, we become not justifiers but justified, not
redeemers but redeemed. Christ's righteousness is credited to us not as
efficient and acting causes, but as receiving subjects. Nor:

4. Does our passive reception through this imputation communicate
everything that belongs in any manner to the thing credited. It communicates
only what agrees with and suffices for the recipient's capacity or need and
what the person crediting intends, since this imputation is his free act.

By this imputation God never intended to transfer to us everything that is
in Christ. He graciously chose to credit Christ's righteousness to us, not
his deity, majesty, or office of Redeemer, as though we thereby became our
own redeemers.

For

[1679 printed p. 249 | IA leaf 257]

first, we are incapable of this glory. Imputation alone does not suffice for
it; it also requires the hypostatic union with the divine nature, which is
not shared with us but belongs to Christ alone.

Second, it is not necessary for our justification. For our justification it
is enough that Christ's righteousness be credited and applied to us to the
extent required for us to obtain forgiveness of sins and a right to eternal
life. It is not necessary that everything in Christ be attributed to us in
precisely the same manner, making us redeemers and justifiers of ourselves.
These are royal prerogatives of God and Christ. It is more than enough for
us to become redeemed and justified. Our Ames rightly writes: “Christ's
righteousness is credited to individual believers according to their
particular need, not according to its universal value. A jewel of great
price given to redeem several captives is applied to each not according to
its entire valuation but according to each captive's need. Christ as the
universal Redeemer paid such a sum and prepared such a universal medicine
as suffices to discharge every person's debts and cure every person's
diseases. When he credits it to us, he alone rightly deserves to be called
Redeemer. Receiving and applying it by faith, we are blessed—thanks be to
God—to be called redeemed; if we wish further to be called redeemers, we
will be blasphemous and sacrilegious.”

“Christ's righteousness is not credited to Peter as the universal price for
all, but as the price by which his particular soul is redeemed, so that he
is called redeemed, not Redeemer, despite Bellarmine's quibble.” When
Bellarmine himself treats the imputation and application of Christ's
righteousness by indulgences granted by his pope, he knows how to
distinguish the thing from the manner of the thing. He says: “It seems
certain that Christ's sufferings, which are of infinite worth, are applied
through indulgences in a finite manner.” Had he wished to be somewhat fairer
to us, he could have answered himself on our behalf: although Christ's
righteousness is infinite, it is credited to us according to our measure
and in a finite manner, so that it does not make us our own redeemers, which
would require infinity.

Second, then, to the objection that, as Levi is said to have paid tithes in
Abraham and we to have sinned in Adam, so we may be said to have satisfied
Christ's righteousness in Christ—a manner of speaking from which some of
our theologians say we should abstain. I indeed honor and revere their piety
in this matter. Confessing that we sinned in Adam savors of humility in
acknowledging our fault, whereas saying that we satisfied God's
righteousness in Christ speaks something great of ourselves and might seem
to some to breathe a measure of arrogance. We should therefore speak as
befits us and say that Christ satisfied divine righteousness for us rather
than that we satisfied it through Christ: “Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but
give glory to your name.” Forbes says that we all satisfied God's
righteousness in and with Christ through his death. But Brashaw, chapter 13,
section 6, says: “It is unnecessary for what is credited toward another's
justification to be reckoned as though it were performed by the person who
is justified. It is enough that it be reckoned and credited as equal in
worth and merit to what he ought to have performed in his own person. Thus
what Christ did and suffered may be said to be credited toward our
justification, although we

[1679 printed p. 250 | IA leaf 258]

are not reckoned to have done or suffered it. It is enough that Christ
undertook it and God accepted it in place either of the inherent
righteousness owed or of satisfaction.”

If, then, we say that by Christ's righteousness credited to us divine
righteousness has been satisfied just as though we ourselves had satisfied
it—and indeed far more—this grants nothing to our arrogance and detracts
nothing from Christ's glory or our humble gratitude. Bellarmine himself
concedes this in express words in book 2, chapter 10, *On Justification*: “In
this manner it would not be absurd if someone said that Christ's
righteousness and death are credited to us when they are given and applied
to us as though we ourselves had satisfied God.” Coster similarly says:
“Christ offered his passion, his righteous works, and his merits to God the
Father as satisfaction for our sins. The Father accepted them for us, and
they are credited and applied to us as though we ourselves had performed
them.”

Thus from the two passages most recently explained to prove our
justification by Christ's imputed righteousness, we have also explained the
pertinent twofold analogy. First, we participate in the righteousness of the
second Adam in the same manner as we participate in the sin of the first
Adam. Participation in the latter is by the imputation of his sin;
therefore participation in the former is by the imputation of Christ's
righteousness. Second, as our sins are credited to Christ, so his
righteousness is credited to us.
