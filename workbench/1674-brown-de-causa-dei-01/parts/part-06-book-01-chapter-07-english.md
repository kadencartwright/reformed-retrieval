## CHAPTER VII

### On the division of laws into moral, ceremonial, and judicial

IT is acknowledged that God prescribed various kinds of laws to the Israelite
people, and not only renewed the natural law and vindicated it from the
corruptions fastened upon it after the fall, but also gave various symbolic and
typical laws to that people, considered as a church, by which, in rightly
worshiping God, they—

[1674 logical printed p. 90 | IA leaf 108 | PDF p. 108]

—might be instructed. And to that same people, as a polity or commonwealth, he
set forth forensic and political laws for the administration of civil and
criminal cases.

All these laws acknowledge God as their one author, for he enacted and
established them all, either mediately or immediately, as the books of Moses
abundantly testify. Thomas also confirms this in part 1 of the second part,
question 98, article 2, saying: “The old law ordered human beings toward Christ
in two ways. In one way, by bearing testimony to Christ; hence he himself says
in the last chapter of Luke, ‘All things written about me in the Law and in the
Psalms and in the Prophets must be fulfilled’; and in John 5, ‘If you believed
Moses, you would perhaps also believe me, for he wrote about me.’ In another
way, by way of a certain disposition: by withdrawing human beings from
idolatrous worship, it enclosed them under the worship of the one God, by whom
the human race was to be saved through Christ. Hence the Apostle says in
Galatians 3, ‘Before faith came, we were kept under the law, enclosed for that
faith which was to be revealed.’ It is evident, moreover, that it belongs to
the same one both to dispose toward the end and to lead to the end; and I mean
the same one acting through himself or through his subjects. For the Devil
would not have enacted a law through which human beings would be led to
Christ, by whom he was to be cast out, according to Matthew 12: ‘If Satan casts
out Satan, his kingdom is divided.’ And therefore the old law was given by the
same God from whom the salvation of human beings was accomplished through the
grace of Christ.” See in the same place the refutation of the objections
brought against this.

Therefore, in that Θιοκρατία God was the one supreme and principal Legislator,
although in promulgating these laws he used both the ministry of angels and
the ministry of Moses. Moses, therefore, must not be regarded as their Author
and Legislator, for he had prescribed nothing by his own authority, not even
that law concerning the certificate of divorce, about which Christ says in
Matthew 19:7–8 that “because of the hardness of the Jews’ hearts he permitted
them to put away their wives,” as is evident from Deuteronomy 24:1–4, where
that law is found. For when Moses had said that the man putting away his wife
could not take her back again after she had been put away, he added that this
would be an abomination to God. And surely, since by this law a Jew was
permitted first to dissolve a marriage and contract a new one, it is
sufficiently evident that Moses did not grant this faculty by his own
authority. Rather, that permission proceeded from God through the ministry of
Moses, for it was within no human being’s power to establish such a permission
by any law. Moses too was faithful as a servant, Hebrews 3:5.

For this reason, the Jews possessed a peculiar privilege. That nation was
distinguished from all others and preferred to them, and God bound and
consecrated it to himself in a singular way, chosen as his peculiar possession.
Deuteronomy 4:7–8: “For what other nation is so great, to which God is near as
Jehovah our God is whenever we call upon him? And what nation is so great that
it has statutes and just judgments like this entire law which I set before you
today?” Psalm 147:19–20: “He declares his words to Jacob, his statutes and his
judgments to Israel. He did not do so to any nation, and therefore they did not
know these judgments.” Romans 3:2: “The oracles of God were entrusted to
them.” So too chapter 9:4: “Theirs were the adoption, the glory, the covenants,
the establishment of the law, the worship, and the promises.”

Hence also those hostilities between the Jewish people and the other nations,
which were finally taken away and abolished only through Christ, as is evident
from Ephesians 2:15–16. There was a partition wall between them and all the
other nations, which Christ finally removed, Colossians 2:17.

Louis Molinaeus, however, wrongly infers from this, in *Admonition*, chapter 6,
pages 103–104, that—

[1674 logical printed p. 91 | IA leaf 109 | PDF p. 109]

—“this common division of the divine law given to the ancient people into the
moral, ceremonial, and judicial law is not without its blemishes, because,
namely, God was the one author of them.” For who is ignorant that the divine
laws, both natural and positive, have God as their one author? Are those laws
therefore not to be distinguished from one another? Does Molinaeus think that
the approvers and defenders of this division believe or say that the rationale
for this division is taken from their distinct cause or distinct author? What?
Are laws which have the same author not to be distinguished at all? Is there no
distinction or difference taken from their subject matter? I wonder what came
into that trifler’s mind that he spoke this way. And even if he acknowledges
that the varied matter of the Israelite laws concerns various orders,
conditions, functions, and sexes of human beings, when he says that “this
common division is not without its blemishes because all this varied matter is
included in one class insofar as God is the one author of them,” who does not
see how childishly he trifles?

What follows is more foolish still. “Then,” he says, “the commonwealth and the
church were united into one society, so that the political society did not
differ from the religious society. Circumcision was the κριτήριον of this one
just as much as of that one, not distinguishing the church and commonwealth
from each other, but distinguishing both, flowing together into one body, from
the other nations. The same governors decided all cases, and the one who was
the high priest was also the supreme judge.” Response: all these things suffer
from two faults: first, they are all false; next, they do nothing to the point.
For it is false that the Jewish commonwealth and church were not formally
distinct; false that the political society did not differ from the religious;
false that circumcision was the κριτήριον of this one just as much as of that
one, for other nations too practiced circumcision; false that the same
governors decided all cases, as is clear from Deuteronomy 17:12 and 2
Chronicles 19:11; and likewise false that the high priest was the supreme
judge, for the king was the supreme judge in civil matters. But this is not the
place to pursue these things more fully. Then what, I ask, will Molinaeus
conclude from this? Can he infer from it that those laws are in no way
distinguished? What if we granted that the Jewish commonwealth and church had
been united into one society; that circumcision had been the κριτήριον of this
one just as much as of that one, not distinguishing the church from the
commonwealth, but distinguishing both, flowing together into one body, from
the other nations; that the same governors had decided all cases; and that the
high priest had been the supreme Judge? Would it follow from this that there
was no distinction among the laws enacted for them? It is indeed true that, on
this supposition, those laws are not distinguished with respect to the
different societies for which they are prescribed, nor distinguished with
respect to different judges. Yet no one will be able to infer that they are
not distinguished at all. The same society can be considered materially in
various ways and, according to those various respects, can be subject to
various laws. Thus, a society of craftsmen can have its own particular laws
insofar as it is distinguished from all other societies; the same society can
also be subject to laws common to the entire commonwealth; and it can also be
subject to natural laws, which are in their own way distinguished from one
another. So the Jews, insofar as they were rational human beings, were subject
to the laws of nature, or the moral law; insofar as they constituted a polity
distinct from others, they were governed by political laws; and insofar,
finally, as they constituted the church, they were governed by ceremonial
laws.

No less ridiculous is that other rationale, namely, that “when the Old
Testament speaks six hundred times—

[1674 logical printed p. 92 | IA leaf 110 | PDF p. 110]

—about those who know God’s law, observe it with their whole heart, and walk in
the laws and statutes of the Lord, it always means those whose perpetual way
of life was agreement and conformity with all the laws handed down through
Moses.” This notwithstanding, those laws can very well be distinguished from
one another. Nor need we say that when Scripture speaks about those who
transgress the law of the Lord, it means this only of the moral, ceremonial,
or judicial law, for various and distinct laws can be violated by the same
human beings.

He also chatters when he says: “Nowhere do I read that fixed classes of laws
and cases are to be referred to particular persons and particular tribunals,
or that some governors were appointed to decide cases concerning the moral
law, others concerning the ceremonial law, and a third kind concerning the
political law.” For just as a case of one and the same kind can be referred to
various tribunals and decided by various governors, so diverse cases
considered materially can be referred to one and the same tribunal under one
formal rationale. Thus nothing can be proved from this against this customary
distinction of God’s law. And does he know anyone who borrows this distinction
from distinct tribunals before which judgment was given concerning them? If
not, to what purpose are these things? Why does he rave in his dreaming this
way?

The following is on the same level: “Why,” he says, “have those who so
carefully divide the laws into classes not yet assigned its own law to each
class? And it is doubtful whether they have declared the law concerning the
Sabbath moral in its entirety, and in what class they will include the laws
concerning interest, tithes, and the eating of things strangled.” Response:
Although some can be uncertain about the class to which certain laws should be
referred, it nevertheless cannot be inferred by any good consequence that they
cannot be referred to any of these classes, or that there is some fourth kind
to which they should be reduced. And indeed, if the opponent presses this, he
is bound to assign that fourth kind to us so that he may remove the entire
difficulty more easily. But what if there are certain actions which must be
referred, under different respects, to various of these kinds of laws? Is this
commonly received division therefore to be blamed and utterly rejected?
Further, because there are various actions by which, under different respects,
different moral precepts are violated—as is clear in drunkenness, by which the
sixth commandment of the Decalogue is violated in one respect and the eighth in
another—it would follow that the distinction of the moral law into the ten
heads of its precepts must also be rejected. Furthermore, if the opponent’s
argument against this division is valid, it will be no less valid against the
division of the moral law into ten words, because there are certain moral
actions, or precepts concerning certain moral actions, about which it is or
can be doubted to which particular head of the Decalogue they must precisely
be referred.

I will readily concede that very many mixed precepts are given which, by
reason of their mixture and composition, can be referred to various kinds of
law. For one and the same law can, according to different respects, be referred
to the moral, ceremonial, and forensic law. Zepperus shows this concerning the
Mosaic laws in book 1, chapter 6, in the very fifth commandment of the
Decalogue, saying that “it is moral, considered nakedly in itself; and
judicial, insofar as it contains the promise of the land of Canaan and of a
blessing in it; moreover, something ceremonial, insofar as the land of Canaan
was a type of that heavenly fatherland; and likewise insofar as God willed the
devout to regard this promise as looking not only to the natural use and
benefit of this life and of that land, but”—which is something more, he
says—“also to fellowship and communion in the gracious covenant, the promises,
and the doctrine—

[1674 logical printed p. 93 | IA leaf 111 | PDF p. 111]

—of salvation in the church, which was then circumscribed within the borders
of the land of Canaan. Children who perversely denied the honor owed to their
parents showed themselves strangers to that type and to the fellowship of the
true church.”

He demonstrates this in the same place concerning the law of homicide, saying:
“Insofar as it simply prohibits homicide, it is moral; insofar as it inflicts
capital punishment upon willful murderers, it is judicial; insofar as it points
out an asylum in a city of refuge to the one who has committed involuntary, or
as they call it, accidental homicide, until the death of the high priest, it is
ceremonial and typical, looking to Christ our supreme Priest.” See more in the
same place. Further, he shows there by examples how one and the same Mosaic law
is both moral and forensic at once; how it is both ceremonial and forensic;
and finally, how it is both moral and ceremonial. At last he adds this on page
39: “The symmetry of this composition and mixture of the divine laws is
recognized from their common rationale as from a base and foundation. For God
has joined piety, the ecclesiastical worship of himself, and the duties of
charity, humanity, and justice toward one’s neighbor, and his people among
themselves, and consequently love of himself and one’s neighbor, with the
duties of each, by such a connection and bond, according to his wisdom, that
they harmonize with one another in a most delightful harmony and, as it were,
sing together, and entwine themselves, as it were, in mutual embraces.”

From this it is easy to answer the objections brought forward. For the Sabbath
considered in itself, or insofar as it is commanded in the fourth precept of
the Decalogue, is moral. Yet it had something political attached to it with
respect to the punishments inflicted upon its violators, as well as something
ceremonial with respect to the Mosaic worship practiced on it or the double
sacrifice offered on that day. The laws concerning interest and tithes were
moral in one respect and political in another. So that law concerning the use
of things strangled can be called partly ceremonial and partly political. This
notwithstanding, that common division can be received until a better one is
offered.

What Molinaeus adds afterward does less to the point. “Finally,” he says,
“when the Old Testament says that the lips of the priests preserve knowledge,”
Malachi 2:7, that “the law will not perish from the Priest, nor counsel from
the wise person, nor the word from the Prophet,” Ezekiel 7:26 and Jeremiah
18:18, and that “many days passed for the Israelites without the true God,
without a teaching Priest, and without law,” 2 Chronicles 15:3, “who would
believe that by the word *law* it does not mean every law, whether moral,
ceremonial, or political? Or that God’s law concerning the diagnosis and
expiation of leprosy is not just as much God’s word as the law concerning theft
and homicide?” Response: everyone sees how empty all these things are. For
although in some passages of Scripture the word *law* is taken generally for
the whole Mosaic law, embracing all the moral, ceremonial, and forensic laws,
it cannot be concluded by any consequence from this that those laws are not
divided into moral, ceremonial, and political laws. It is therefore not worth
the effort to examine the passages cited. For even if it were granted—though
this would be sufficiently difficult to prove—that *law* is taken generally in
all these passages, Molinaeus will not prove what he endeavors to prove by even
the thousandth degree of consequence. We also concede that the law concerning
the diagnosis of leprosy was God’s word, just as the law concerning theft and
homicide was. But what follows from this? Does he think that we say or dream
that the political and ceremonial laws were not divine? He is surely dreaming
if he thinks so.

“And who would believe,” he says further, “that the priests were not the
guardians of the political and moral law—

[1674 logical printed p. 94 | IA leaf 112 | PDF p. 112]

—alike? Surely, when God commands the people to act according to all the things
the Priests have taught, he excludes no law or case which the Priests ought to
teach or the people ought to observe. For in Deuteronomy 24:8 the legislator
also commands the people to take diligent care in a plague of leprosy and to
act according to all the things the Levitical Priests have taught, including
every law among the instructions issuing indiscriminately from the mouth of
the Priest, and commanding the people to observe with equal religious care
laws which equally have God as their author and legislator.” Response: now is
not the proper time to digress in order to examine the Erastian position of
Molinaeus. Let us therefore grant that the Priests were guardians of political
and moral law. Let Molinaeus prove that it follows from this that those laws
are not distinguished from one another at all or do not differ from the
ceremonial laws. But Deuteronomy 24:8 is expressly limited to the law
concerning leprosy, and nothing can be concluded from it against us. Indeed, if
someone denied that the Priests were the guardians of absolutely all the laws,
the contrary could not be proved by this testimony unless the opponent thinks
that a general conclusion can be rightly inferred from a particular one.

The same answer must be given to what follows, when he says: “Nor should it be
believed that the Prophets, in those assemblies and houses of prayer erected
town by town, concerning which Leviticus 23 speaks, explained only the
Decalogue and taught the moral law; or that in those assemblies, on Sabbath
days and new moons, the people were not permitted to consult the Prophets
concerning any matter commanded by God through Moses which fell into dispute.”
For this consequence is nothing: therefore those laws are not distinguished
into moral, ceremonial, and forensic laws, as can be hidden from no one. But it
is customary for this man to heap up words and yet say nothing, to fill pages
and prove absolutely nothing.

Let us hear what follows. “Since,” he says, “the legislator has never pronounced
that the understanding or observance of any law—say, a ceremonial but still
divine law, as are all the laws found in Exodus or Leviticus and concerning
which a Priest or Levite was equally consulted—does not equally pertain to
divine worship, which indeed, since it was instituted by God as one and not
threefold, must likewise not be thought to have had a threefold law enacted and
commanded.” Response: if *worship* is taken for any obedience rendered to God,
then indeed the observance of the moral and political law will equally be
divine worship, just as the observance of the ceremonial law was, which was a
determination of the moral law insofar as it regarded God, as Thomas speaks in
part 1 of the second part, question 101, article 1. But if *worship* is taken
properly, for an act directed toward God under the aspect either of the end
alone or also of the object, then it is evident that all obedience rendered
either to the moral law, especially insofar as it concerns the second table,
or to the political and forensic law was not an act of worship. Next, even if
we granted that the worship of God consists in the understanding and
observance of all the precepts and laws enacted by God, it nevertheless does
not follow from this that, just as worship is simple, there was likewise only
a simple or single class of laws. For the unity and simplicity of worship does
not depend upon the unity or simplicity of the command.

Yet let us hear the proof: “For since,” he says, “hundreds of times in the Old
Testament the legislator, commanding that worship be rendered to God, meant
all the worship owed to God and established no distinction between moral and
ceremonial worship”—response: therefore internal worship, which is indeed
moral, will not be distinguished from external worship, which was ceremonial.
Therefore, whenever God was worshiped ceremonially, he was also worshiped
morally or internally. But who does not see how absurd these things are?

[1674 logical printed p. 95 | IA leaf 113 | PDF p. 113]

“So too,” he says further, “when the Apostles say in Luke, Acts 13:27 and
15:21, that the laws are read and explained every Sabbath, they did not wish
their hearers to understand that they had established a discourse concerning
ceremonial or political law but only moral law. For since political law was a
part of divine worship, it was without doubt read and explained every Sabbath
with equal religious care as the other laws.” Response: no one denies that the
ceremonial and political law was also read and explained together with the
moral law—not because political law itself was a part of divine worship, but
because all those laws had been enacted by God, had been compacted as it were
into one volume, and had been closely joined to one another; and because they
had all been enacted to instruct them both insofar as they were rational human
beings, insofar as they were the church of God, and finally insofar as they
were a peculiar commonwealth specially separated and devoted to God.

The conclusion he infers from this, therefore, is of the same stuff as the
premises. “And,” he says, “I think it becomes established by these things that
laws and cases which are decided under the same judgment, before the same
tribunal, and by the same person are divided in vain, as is seen in Deuteronomy
7”—he should have said 17—“sections 8 through 15, where the priest judges not
particularly concerning sacred affairs, but concerning any dispute, whether
capital or pecuniary.” Response: it is irrelevant to say anything here about
cases, where the discourse is instituted only concerning laws and this
division of laws which has already been repeated rather often. Next, now is
not the proper time to show that different tribunals are meant in Deuteronomy
17 and how different cases pertained to those different tribunals. Yet even if
I granted him all these things as well, his conclusion would be weak, namely,
that laws which are decided under the same judgment, before the same tribunal,
and by the same person are divided in vain. For laws can be diverse and widely
distinct with respect either to their matter, though they are not
distinguished with respect to the tribunals before which they are decided, or
to the persons who pronounce judgment according to them. Nor is this division
made with respect to different judgments, tribunals, or persons judging. The
rationale for this division is far different, as was said.

Whatever Molinaeus may say, therefore, can this division be retained? For
Scripture itself appears to favor it, since Scripture often distributes the
law into commandments, statutes, and judgments, or מצות, חקים, and משפטים, as
so many kinds of law, as can be seen in Deuteronomy 4:13–14; 5:31; 6:1, 20;
7:11; 8:11; 26:17; 30:16; Leviticus 26:46; 1 Kings 6:12; 2:3; 8:58; and
Nehemiah 1:7; 9:24. I nevertheless do not deny that sometimes only two kinds
are enumerated, although under one of them the third can easily be included,
provided the third kind is also being discussed there. I also acknowledge
that twice, if I remember correctly, a fourth is added, which can easily be
reduced to one of the three.

If you ask from where the rationale for this division is taken, Junius in
*Syntagma*, part 1, disputation 32, thesis 21, and Apollonius in *On the Law of
God*, chapter 6, page 138, say it is taken from the office of law, whose task is
to establish and sanction the order according to which a human being is joined
to God and neighbor. A human being is joined to God, he says, first by a
certain internal and external likeness and συμμορφώσει of morals, namely by
love, justice, holiness, and truth, the norm of which the first part of the law
delivers. Next, the second part, which concerns ceremonies and rites,
prescribes the rationale for the external signification and attestation of
those acts of divine worship, supported by tokens and symbols.
Finally, the last part of the law, namely the forensic part, applied more
distinctly to the Israelites, explains what a human being owes to another
human being by duty.

[1674 logical printed p. 96 | IA leaf 114 | PDF p. 114 | ends before CAP. VIII]

The rationale for this division indicated above by our writers, however,
appears fuller to us: namely, that certain laws prescribed duties common to
them with all other human beings and thus regarded them as rational human
beings and bound all nations at every time. Such are those called moral laws.
Other laws regarded and bound them insofar as they constituted a distinct
commonwealth, chosen and dedicated to God; and laws of this kind were the
political or forensic laws. Finally, other laws regarded their ecclesiastical
state and bound them insofar as they were an underage church: namely, the
ceremonial laws. So much for these matters.
