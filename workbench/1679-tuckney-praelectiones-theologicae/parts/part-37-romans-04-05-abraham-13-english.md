Let us proceed to another passage. Although it does not state word for word
that Christ's righteousness is credited to us for justification, this
follows clearly and forcefully from its genuine sense. It is the celebrated
2 Corinthians 5:21: “He made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we
might become the righteousness of God in him.” This carries great weight.

What does Bellarmine say? Like Proteus, he twists himself into every form to
escape the knot which holds him. First he attempts destructively to demolish
our exposition; then constructively he proposes several others as more
suitable.

His destructive reply is this: “Christ is not called sin—that is, a sinner;
nor are our sins credited to him as though he himself had committed them and
might be regarded as unrighteous. Only the duty of making satisfaction is
credited to him, which he voluntarily undertook. On this account he deserves
to be called not a sinner but righteous, for one who satisfies for another
is most righteous. Likewise his righteousness is credited to us as to the
satisfaction he rendered for us. Yet we cannot therefore be regarded as
righteous—that is, clean and spotless—if the stains and filth of sins truly
inhere in us.”

[1679 printed p. 226 | IA leaf 234]

So speaks Bellarmine. Some of it is irrelevant; the rest, far from weakening
our position, strongly confirms it.

First, although we truly say that our sins were credited to Christ, which
of us has ever been so blasphemous as to say they were credited as though he
had committed them, could be regarded as unrighteous, or was inherently a
sinner in himself through sin's stains? Some corrupt manuscripts once read,
as Augustine testifies, “He who did not know sin committed sin for us,” as
though Christ himself sinned for us. We mark that blasphemous reading with
an obelus, as Augustine himself did. Luther, in his characteristically bold
manner of speech, sometimes did not fear to call Christ the greatest sinner
of all because all the sins of all the elect were credited to him. The
meaning was sufficiently pious, but the expression excessively bold, and we
refrain from it.

Second, which of us was ever so self-satisfied as to claim Bellarmine's kind
of inherent righteousness, considering himself clean and entirely spotless?
Our miserable experience teaches us the contrary, which we feel,
acknowledge, and assert against the Roman party. These points accomplish
nothing against us.

Third, I grant that the one who satisfies for another is not unrighteous on
that account but righteous. Christ, to whom Bellarmine admits our sins were
credited as to the duty of satisfying, deserves to be called not a sinner
but most righteous insofar as he freely undertook and performed it. To
undertake or perform satisfaction for one's own debt is justice; to do so
for another is both justice and beneficence. Yet if the debt is joined with
guilt, as ours was, the one undertaking its satisfaction bears it as the
scapegoat bore all the people's sins placed upon it. Isaiah 53:6 similarly
says of Christ our atoning victim that the Lord laid, or caused to meet, on
him the iniquity of us all. Bellarmine himself cites this through Augustine
on Psalm 21 to prove that Christ willed to make our sins his own and satisfy
for them.

Fourth, how does Bellarmine's reply oppose our argument from this passage
for Christ's righteousness credited to us? It confirms our position so
strongly that one of our writers aptly exclaimed, “Indeed, great is the
power of truth, which does not allow itself to remain unestablished even
among its adversaries.”

[1679 printed p. 227 | IA leaf 235]

Bellarmine expressly admits that Christ's righteousness is credited to us
with respect to satisfaction. Becanus and Costerus say the same. This is all
we contend and mean when we say that a person is justified through Christ's
righteousness credited to him: through Christ's satisfaction credited to
him he obtains forgiveness, is restored to God's favor, and is so regarded
as righteous.

The Roman writers balk at this final point. They deny that because we obtain
forgiveness through the crediting of Christ's satisfaction, we are therefore
regarded as righteous. Becanus states Bellarmine's meaning more plainly:
“Although it may be conceded that our sin is credited to Christ with respect
to the duty of satisfying, one must not concede that it is so credited that
he can be judged a sinner or unrighteous. Likewise, although Christ's
righteousness is credited to us with respect to satisfaction, it is false
that it is so credited that by it we are judged righteous and holy.”

Our writers rightly answer that we do not teach that our sins were credited
to Christ so that he became a sinner or inherently unrighteous. Rather, in
view of our sins God dealt with him as though he were a sinner. We therefore
say our sins were credited to him, for without such crediting it would have
been unjust to exact their penalties from him. Likewise, Christ's
righteousness credited to us does efficiently cause us to become inherently
righteous, but that is not the formal constituent of justification. Rather,
in view of the righteousness Christ rendered, God regards and deals with us
as though we were formally or inherently righteous. Thus we say most truly
that God the Father credits Christ's righteousness to us.

Bellarmine and Becanus rest upon their fundamental error that justification
consists in the infusion of inherent righteousness. We expressly deny this
and affirm from Scripture that it consists in crediting another's
righteousness, namely Christ's. We therefore answer two things. First,
inherent righteousness efficiently flows from and is caused in us by
Christ's righteousness credited to us. Though imperfect here and unable to
justify before God's tribunal, it permits us to be called righteous, as
Scripture often calls us. Second, if we attend to justification's formal
reason, it consists solely in crediting Christ's righteousness, although
inherent righteousness is also infused. Justification and sanctification
are inseparably joined, but the imperfection belongs not to justification
but to sanctification, where it has its proper seat.

[1679 printed p. 228 | IA leaf 236]

So much for the destructive part of Bellarmine's reply, where he attempts
to disprove our interpretation that we become God's righteousness in Christ
through his righteousness credited to us. We have seen how successfully.

Let us see whether he handles the constructive part better. He gives three
interpretations:

First, this righteousness of God is the divine righteousness in Christ. We
are said to be it not in ourselves but in him because he is our head, and
what belongs to the head also belongs to the members who form one body with
him. Just as Christ is said to become sin not because he was formally a
sinner but because he was the head of us sinners, so we are called God's
righteousness in him not because his righteousness makes us formally
righteous, but because we are his members.

Second, this righteousness is righteousness inhering in us. It is called
God's because God gives it to us, and said to be in Christ because it is
given through his merit.

Third, the phrase figuratively denotes inherent righteousness as the effect
and image of God's righteousness. Christ was figuratively made sin as a
sacrifice for sin and by sharing flesh in the likeness of sinful flesh. He
became both not by crediting but truly and really: he offered himself as a
real sacrifice and assumed a true likeness of sinful flesh. Likewise, when
we receive inherent righteousness, we participate not by crediting but
really in the effect and likeness of divine righteousness.

Although Bellarmine inserts some unacceptable points into his first
interpretation, I gladly acknowledge its substance with all our theologians
who treat the matter. We become God's righteousness in Christ, according to
Philippians 3:9, “that I may be found in him, not having my own
righteousness.” Insofar as we are in and engrafted into him by faith, we are
justified in him as our head, so that what belongs to the head belongs to us
as his members. Christ's righteousness inheres in him, yet through our
mystical union, incorporation, and engrafting it is credited to us, as what
a surety truly pays is credited to the debtor. Piscator therefore explains
“that we might become God's righteousness in him” as “while we are engrafted
into him through faith.”

If they object that Christ as our head communicates inherent righteousness
to us as his members, just as a head communicates true physical life and
sensation to its members, I answer that Christ our head indeed efficiently
and progressively communicates such grace to us in sanctification. But

[1679 printed p. 229 | IA leaf 237]

Paul here treats justification, not sanctification. In the three preceding
verses he continually speaks of reconciliation, which is synonymous with
justification: the ministry of reconciliation in verse 18 and its word in
verse 19. Having said in verse 20, “We implore you in Christ's name: be
reconciled to God,” he immediately adds, “For he made him who knew no sin to
be sin for us, that we might become God's righteousness in him.” Estius
himself says the causal “for” connects this sentence to the preceding one
and gives the reason for hoping and seeking reconciliation with God: God's
great kindness in making his sinless Son sin for us. Thus even by Estius's
admission Paul treats reconciliation, which is the same as justification.

Reconciliation cannot consist in the inherent righteousness asserted by
the Roman party. First, inherent righteousness is not perfect here, so
reconciliation would not be perfect if it consisted in it. Second, we are
reconciled before we can possess inherent righteousness, because it flows
from reconciliation; God does not impart it to the unreconciled. Third,
Paul defines reconciliation in verse 19 as forgiveness: God reconciled the
world to himself in Christ “not crediting their offenses to them.” This is
precisely the justification we affirm.

Thus when Paul says that we become God's righteousness in Christ, he speaks
of justification, not inherent righteousness, but Christ's righteousness
credited to us, from whose crediting the Roman writers themselves admit we
obtain the non-crediting of sins. This answers Bellarmine's first
interpretation.

It also abundantly disposes of the second and third, both of which make the
righteousness here an inherent righteousness, called God's because it is
the effect or image of God's righteousness. We have shown that inherent
righteousness cannot be meant. They are also inconsistent with Bellarmine's
first and truer interpretation. That placed the righteousness subjectively
in Christ, because we are engrafted into him as our head. The second and
third place it in us as its subject and in Christ only as its cause.

Bellarmine further claims that Christ was made sin truly and really, not by
crediting—as a sacrifice for sin and by assuming the true likeness of sinful
flesh—and that we similarly participate really, not by crediting, in divine
righteousness's effect and likeness when we receive inherent righteousness.
He thinks this turns our argument solidly against us.

[1679 printed p. 230 | IA leaf 238]

We answer easily. Christ truly and really became a sacrifice for sins from
our sins being credited to him; likewise, we truly and really become
righteous from his righteousness being credited to us. The Roman writers
obstinately err by treating only inherent righteousness as true and real
and always opposing it to credited righteousness, as if credited meant
merely supposed and imaginary. Either they maliciously deceive or are
themselves egregiously deceived.

Credited righteousness is preeminently real and supremely true. Everything
in it is true and real: God's love which credits; Christ's righteousness
which is credited; the act of crediting; the forgiveness actually granted;
and the sinner truly received into favor. From this alone arise true peace,
solid joy, and at last an eternal weight of glory.

Is this merely supposed and imaginary? Souls do not dream, opine, or imagine
these things; while most wakeful toward God, they truly feel, taste, and
solidly glory in them. When a surety truly pays a debtor's debt and credits
that payment to him, he truly releases him from obligation and perhaps from
bonds. The debtor will not dream this is imaginary when he feels himself
truly released from debt and prison, just as Peter, once fully awake, knew
he had been freed from prison, Acts 12:11.

Those who regard credited righteousness as an imaginary dream are therefore
the dreamers, not those who have happily experienced it as utterly true and
as producing the firmest consolation in terrified consciences.

Bellarmine says that if we are reckoned righteous because Christ's
righteousness is credited to us, it would be a bare imagining of
righteousness without corresponding truth. He thereby blasphemes both
Christ and God. He blasphemes Christ as though Christ's righteousness
contained nothing true when made ours by crediting. Yet an act which truly
changes a justified person's state from guilty to righteous is no bare
imagination. He blasphemes God by attributing to him an empty imagination
without corresponding truth. If God pronounced us righteous because of
inherent righteousness, while some unrighteousness always inheres in us,
that would be the imagination without corresponding truth. God pronounces
only the perfectly righteous to be righteous; and we are not such here,
whatever they argue to the contrary.

Let me gather briefly what supports credited righteousness in this passage.

First, antithetically, as the former passage said that Christ “became
righteousness for us,” so the same words and sense say here that “we become
God's righteousness in him.” As he was made righteousness for us from the
side of God who justifies, so from our side as the justified we become God's
righteousness in him. The same judgment applies to both.

Second, we are said to become not merely righteous people, but “God's
righteousness” in him—as though righteousness itself, and God's
righteousness. Chrysostom and Theophylact understand this to mean that the
person is so perfectly justified that no charge or stain of sin is found in
him.

[1679 printed p. 231 | IA leaf 239]

In the abstract he is called righteousness itself, indeed eminently and
transcendently God's righteousness: righteousness approved before God as
the just Judge, in whom not the smallest stain or shadow of unrighteousness
is found.

Such a description can fit only Christ's righteousness credited to us, not
any inherent righteousness we possess in this life. Estius himself admits
that “the perfection of righteousness which excludes every stain of sin
belongs not to this age but the future.” Yet we are truly justified in this
age. If only righteousness excluding every stain justifies us, it cannot be
inherent in us but must be the spotless Lamb Jesus Christ's righteousness
credited to us.

The evidence forces Estius to concede that he would not strongly resist
someone who says both parts signify reckoning: Christ's being made sin and
our becoming God's righteousness in him. Good—we have won; the adversary
liberally grants our case.

But he grants it with that Roman faith by which no one will be justified,
sophistically and mockingly. He explains that Christ was made sin by the
mistaken reckoning of people, who falsely regarded him as a sinner; whereas
we become God's righteousness by God's reckoning, which never errs, from
which it follows that we are truly and not merely imputatively righteous.
He boasts that this especially refutes the fiction of credited
righteousness.

We readily reply. Christ was made or reckoned sin not merely by the
impiously mistaken judgment of people who regarded him as the greatest
sinner, but by God himself as the true sacrifice for our sins. In that
sense he became a curse, Galatians 3:13. All our sins were truly credited to
him by God the Father as a debtor's debt is credited to his surety, so that
he was regarded as debtor and dealt with as a sinner in view of our sins
credited to him.

Although the human judgment that Christ was himself the greatest sinner was
false and impious, God's crediting our sins to Christ and his righteousness
to us was not imaginary but utterly true, producing utterly true effects and
fruits in him and us. Far from refuting credited righteousness, this most
firmly establishes it.

[1679 printed p. 232 | IA leaf 240]

Third, the passage says that we become God's righteousness “in him,” namely
Christ. Our writers rightly locate much of the testimony's force there.

The Greek “in him” can sometimes mean “through him.” Roman interpreters,
Grotius, and Hammond understand it here as becoming righteous through
Christ by an inherent righteousness approved by God: Christ morally merits
it and efficiently generates it in us. But we have already shown that Paul
cannot mean inherent righteousness. God graciously approves that in its
proper order, but reconciliation cannot occur through our imperfect and
sin-stained inherent righteousness. It occurs only through righteousness
perfect in every respect: Christ's alone, made ours solely by crediting.

Thus the righteousness is not subjectively in the heart, as Grotius says,
but subjectively “in him,” Christ, and only terminatively in us—or better,
outside us. The parallel extremes then correspond: Christ became sin “for
us,” that we might become God's righteousness “in him.” The sins for which
Christ was condemned did not inhere in him but in us; likewise the
righteousness by which we are justified does not inhere in us but is in
Christ.

Fourth, finally and principally, the parallel antithesis confirms credited
righteousness: as Christ was made sin for us, so we become God's
righteousness in him. Christ became sin not inherently but by crediting.
Therefore in reconciliation or justification we become God's righteousness
not inherently but by the crediting of Christ's righteousness.

Cornelius a Lapide and Estius object that the cases are unlike: because
Christ was a sinner by reckoning alone, it does not follow that we become
righteous by reckoning alone. Christ could not truly be a sinner, whereas
we can truly become righteous. Thus our justification requires that we
actually become truly—that is, inherently—righteous. As Christ bore the true
penalty of our sins, he generates true righteousness in us.

[1679 printed p. 233 | IA leaf 241]

I answer:

First, he generates true inherent righteousness in us in sanctification,
but not for justification.

Second, he also imparts true righteousness in justification's formal
relation, although it can be wholly credited without being supposed or
imaginary.

Third, the likeness and inference from sin credited to Christ to
righteousness credited to us are complete. Paul sets the antithesis in
strict parallel, using the same sense for Christ becoming sin and us
becoming righteousness. Augustine says: “He, therefore, was sin as we are
righteous. We are not our own righteousness but God's, not in ourselves but
in him; just as he was not his own sin but ours, constituted not in himself
but in us.”

Fourth, it is remarkable Jesuit subtlety to carve inherent justification
from a passage where the apostle's parallel antithesis firmly excludes it.
If the antithesis is genuine, its terms must be taken in the same sense. If
Christ became sin imputatively, we likewise become God's righteousness
imputatively. In calling our inference unjust, they accuse Paul's
antithesis of being ill-fitted and inequitable, injuring Paul more than us.

Their reason for denying the likeness is precisely our argument: Christ
could not become inherently unrighteous, whereas we can become inherently
righteous. Since Christ could not inherently become sin, yet the antithesis
says he became sin in the same way that we become God's righteousness in
justification, it follows invincibly that as he became sin not inherently
but by crediting, so we become God's righteousness not inherently but by
crediting.

Christ could not receive sin inherently, but could and did receive it by
crediting, and is therefore specifically said to have been “made sin.” One
who commits sin is not properly said to become sin; the one to whom sin is
credited is. As sin's crediting was sufficient for him to be called made
sin, righteousness's crediting is sufficient for us to be called God's
righteousness.

We can become inherently righteous, and truly do so in sanctification,
though imperfectly while here. This further clarifies the apostolic
antithesis and our teaching: as Christ remained pure and righteous in
himself although our sins were credited to him, so although Christ's
perfectly pure righteousness is credited to us and perfectly justifies us,
we can remain sprinkled with many stains of sin in ourselves—imperfectly
righteous in sanctification, yet fully justi-
