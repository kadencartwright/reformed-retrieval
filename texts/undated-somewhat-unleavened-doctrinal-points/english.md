# On Certain Somewhat Unleavened Points of Doctrine

*An undated consilium preserved in Beza's Latin translation from French; a fresh translation from CO X/1 158–160.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 158]

Dearly beloved brothers, since your letter was delivered to us in the fourth month after it was sent, it ought not to seem surprising to you that you have received our answer later than you expected.

To come to the matter itself, we are deeply grieved that it is your lot to be driven up and down in this way, and that wherever you set foot Satan devises something by which he may bring new troubles upon you. Yet the condition of the fathers was no different. Paul testifies about himself that both he and the rest of the godly wandered without settled homes (1 Corinthians 4:11). Therefore, although you must often change your soil and what concerns the body must repeatedly be altered, you must nevertheless learn the mind's true settledness and the spirit's constancy. You must put into practice the doctrine which teaches that the children of God have to live as pilgrims in the world. That doctrine is common to all the godly, although the Lord chooses to make its experience a reality for some more than for others when he leads them here and there.

Meanwhile, we ourselves both grieve with you and would sincerely wish to provide for your repose if any means were at hand. Yet this sympathy of ours for your miseries cannot bring us to release you from your duty. Even if we were able to do that, it would be nothing other than deceiving you with empty flatteries, nor would it benefit you. Indeed, you yourselves know that our condemnation before God is increased all the more when we seek to be absolved by human beings. Nothing therefore remains but to explain to you purely and simply

### [CO X/1, p. 159]

what we think about the matter you have put before us.

First, we were greatly astonished when we heard that you thought there was nothing in the confession required of you that directly conflicts with God's Word. We believe that fear has bound your eyes in this matter. For in the article on baptism, those who believe that infants are sanctified from birth are expressly condemned. As for the Supper, they say that Judas was no less a participant in Christ's body than Peter. There is also the claim that Christ in his human nature is everywhere, as seems good to him, since he is God and man—as though the unity of the person brought about a confusion of the divine and human natures.

Your argument that these things are not properly a confession of faith is—if we may say so with your good leave—nothing but an evasion for concealing or covering the evil. The Lord is no sophist who allows himself to be mocked by quibbles of this kind. Whatever other name or title you may therefore give that document, you nevertheless testify by a solemn oath that this is what your faith holds. Even supposing the matter does not reach all the way to God, by giving such a pledge before human beings you too are so double-tongued that you cannot escape the mark of bad faith. Grant that the intention of those who put these things before you is good: that is still not enough if you pursue a right end through less than lawful means.

We therefore entreat you by the Lord that, if you have been so drowsy for a time, you now wake up in earnest. To say candidly how the matter stands, a document of this kind cannot be accepted without at the same time indirectly renouncing God's truth, which by itself ought to be dearer to us not merely than any station in life, but than life itself.

The remedy is for you to substitute the following: baptism is not superfluous for infants, because they are sanctified by the promise, while the sacrament is a necessary confirmation on account of human weakness. Thus the promise draws the sacrament along with it as a companion, far from introducing contempt for the sacrament.

In the article on the Supper, make this substitution: the substance of the true body and blood of Jesus Christ, as he once received it from the Virgin's womb, is present in the Supper to believers and unbelievers alike; and although believers alone truly and effectually receive it, unbelievers are nevertheless no less held guilty of the Lord's body and blood because they have profaned the sacrament.

Likewise, in place of the words, “We reject the doctrine of those who teach that Christ is received only spiritually,” substitute: “those who teach that only Christ's spirit is received, his flesh and blood of course being excluded.” For it is written: “My flesh is truly food, and my blood is truly drink” (John 6:55). And although that reception is spiritual, it nevertheless happens no less in reality and does not consist in imagination or opinion alone.

Likewise, in place of the words, “Christ according to his human nature sits,” substitute:

### [CO X/1, p. 160]

“We confess that Christ is in heaven according to his human nature, from where we also await him, yet in such a way that by his infinite power—which extends everywhere—since he sat down at the Father's right hand, he can truly feed us with his body and blood in an incomprehensible manner, without any change of place.”

Where the Sacramentarians are mentioned, that name must be interpreted so that it is understood to mean those who either abolish the efficacy of the sacraments or corrupt their use.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's title literally calls the doctrinal points *aliquatenus infermentata*, “somewhat unleavened.” The culinary metaphor may imply that the formulations were insufficiently developed or matured; the unusual wording is retained rather than replaced with a confident interpretation.
2. *Solum saepe vobis vertendum* literally says that “the soil must often be changed by you.” It contrasts the exiles' shifting bodily situation with the settledness of mind they must learn.
3. The Greek *συμπάθεια* is retained in the Latin file as printed and translated “sympathy,” a fellow-feeling that cannot remove the addressees' duty.
4. The addressees, their location, and the required confession are not named in this witness. The document itself supplies no date or subscription.
5. *Uti ipsi videtur* is syntactically compressed. With singular *ipsi* it most naturally refers to Christ (“as seems good to him”), although the larger clause reports the confession's proponents rather than the translators' own view.
6. The proposed replacement formulas preserve the text's distinctions: infant baptism confirms a prior promise; believers alone receive Christ truly and effectually in the Supper, while unbelievers profane the sacrament; spiritual reception is real reception of Christ's body and blood without a local displacement of his human nature.
7. “Efficacy” translates *vis* in the final definition of a “Sacramentarian.” Depending on context, the word can also denote the sacraments' force or power.
