Objection 8. The Word of God is as perfect in acts of the second table as in
those of the first, nor is it

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 116 | PDF p. 167]

more capable of addition here than there. But in acts of the second table we
cannot always give a reason for our deeds from the Word, for then all human
laws and the laws of military discipline would have to be proved from God's
Word. This, however, is impossible. Therefore, in acts of the first table the
Word is not absolute in this way. For no obedience could be rendered to rulers
or to commanders in war unless all laws were syllogistically deduced from
God's Word.

Response 1. There are two elements in human laws. One is moral; the other is
not moral in itself, but only “in relation to something else” (κατ᾽ ἄλλο), in
so far as it is a means to order, justice, peace, and the avoidance of
slaughter and confusion. Carrying arms by night in a city is an example.
Subjects in these matters are mixed agents: partly moral, partly political,
or—if there were a more suitable term—positive agents. They often act
according to an art, as happens in war: for example, when a law requires the
officer in charge of the watch to whisper a particular password into the city
commander's ear, or when a law requires the person to whom a right to land is
given to take a clod and a stone in his hand. These acts are in themselves
indifferent and, as such and in themselves, are not moral or capable of being
regulated by God's law. It is enough that such acts conform negatively to
God's Word, that is, that they not conflict with God's Word. Therefore, with
respect to the morality of acts, subjects cannot obey laws unless they know
them to be just and consonant with God's

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 117 | PDF p. 168]

Word. But as for what in these matters is merely a positive human element, or
an artistic or domestic one—I suffer here from a poverty of words—subjects,
in so far as they are agents by art or custom rather than moral agents, need
not obtain reasons for their actions from God's Word in this way.

Objection 9. Thus decency in the worship of God is moral and commanded by the
express Word of God. But it is indifferent, and depends upon the church's
judgment, that this decency should be expressed by the surplice, the sign of
the cross, and feast days. Therefore there are mixed agents here in the
church, and so it is unnecessary to prove from God's Word that use of the
surplice or signing with the cross is lawful. It is enough that they conform
negatively to God's Word, that is, that they not conflict with it.

Response 1. The reasoning here is in no way parallel to the other case. We do
not fault the physical use of the surplice itself, namely, its being fitted in
this way to the arm and shoulders by a tailor. We fault its ethical,
theological, and moral use—indeed, its sacred and religious use—because it
cannot be proved from the Scriptures. For human judgment does not determine
what is decent or indecent in the worship of God.

There are two kinds of decency in the worship of God. One is natural or civil,
and this is merely negative: nothing contrary to good morals or gravity may be
done in God's worship. This decency

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 118 | PDF p. 169]

depends not upon human judgment but upon custom and nature; no human authority
can determine in this respect what is decent and what is not. In this sense
the surplice and signing with the cross are not decent. The other kind is
spiritual and religious decency in God's worship. This comes from God's will
alone. Eating and drinking in the church are decent and grave precisely
because Christ instituted such actions; and bread, rather than other foods,
is a fitting element to signify Christ's body for no reason other than that
Christ instituted it. If human beings instituted beef, mutton, milk, and eggs
as sacramental signs, they would not be signs of Christ's body and blood, much
less signs as fitting as bread and wine. Ceremonies are symbolic rites and
signifying symbols of divine things: the day of the Nativity, for example, is
a commemorative sign of Christ's nativity, and the surplice is a symbol of
pastoral holiness. But these, if they are indeed decent, possess a sacred and
religious decency. From where does it come? No created will can impart
religious decency to things. They must therefore prove to us from sacred
Scripture that ceremonies are decent both by their nature and religiously and
positively in God's worship. It is not enough that they be decent and lawful
negatively, and not conflict with God's Word. For in this way eggs, milk, and
butter conform negatively to the Lord's

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 119 | PDF p. 170]

Supper: they do not conflict with the nature of the Supper.

I add to these things Paul's statement in Romans 14:23: “Whatever is not from
faith”—that is, from firm persuasion and full assurance (πληροφορία), as Paul
explains it in verse 5, by which I know that what I do conforms to God's
Word—cannot be done without sin. But it is not established that using
ceremonies conforms to God's Word. Therefore, using them is sin.

Robert Sanderson responds first, in an English sermon: “Whatever is not from
faith is sin” is true in the substantial elements of divine worship and in the
exercise of spiritual and supernatural grace, whether in things to be believed
or things to be done under necessity of salvation. Hence we detest, against
the Papists, every tradition and addition intended to supply a defect in sacred
Scripture. But the statement does not apply to every action of human life,
even to picking up a straw.

Response. Nor do we press the text in actions performed solely by imagination
before an act of reason, such as picking up a straw or rubbing one's beard. We
press it in all moral actions and in all our steps, as Solomon says expressly
in Proverbs 2:9; 3:23; and 4:10–12. For in every moral action—eating,
drinking, speaking, walking ten miles to accompany a friend, and doing this or
that at the command of masters and parents—it can

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 120 | PDF p. 171]

happen that we totter and strike our feet, as the wise man puts it, when in
such trivial actions we do what is contrary to God's Word. Therefore, a lamp
for our feet is needed here. Second, Sanderson speaks very badly when he
asserts that the Word is perfect in regulating things to be believed and done
which are necessary for salvation, but defective in regulating the acts of
daily life. For this makes the Word both perfect and not perfect.

3. The Papists teach that most of their additions are not necessary for
salvation. Indeed, the great Suárez, *On the Threefold Theological Virtue*,
treatise 1, disputation 2, section 6, figure 3, says: “It must be said that, as
to the substance of things to be believed, faith has always been the same from
the beginning of the human race until now.” Vásquez, volume 2 on I-II,
disputation 154, chapter 3: “It is answered that the pontiff, whether outside
a general council or in one, cannot establish anything concerning faith which
is not contained in the revealed principles and articles and most certainly
deduced from them.” Báñez, volume 3 on II-II, question 1, article 10, doubt 2:
“It must be noted that neither the supreme pontiff nor the whole church can
coin a new article or dogma with respect to its substance, nor can they
institute a new sacrament.” Scotus, prologue to the *Sentences*, question 3,
article 3: “Our theology, in fact, concerns only those things contained in
Scripture and those which can be elicited from them.”

4. If we can sin in matters of daily life, which no sane person denies, then
these include things to be done under necessity of salvation.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 121 | PDF p. 172]

Sanderson responds a second time in the same place: Faith as certain as logical
faith does not concern matters of daily life and indifferent things; ethical
and conjectural faith is enough. Indeed, nothing more than negative faith is
needed in these things: we know that they are not contrary to the law of
nature or to Scripture and therefore are not forbidden. Hence they make two
classes of lawful things. Some conform positively to God's Word, since their
equity can be proved syllogistically from God's Word. Others conform
negatively to God's Word—that is, they do not conflict with it—since they are
neither commanded nor forbidden by God's Word. In their judgment, ceremonies
conform to God's Word in this second way.

But this distinction has a place in merely natural actions, such as hearing
sounds and seeing colors, and in actions of art and the sciences, such as
constructing a syllogism in Barbara; these are neither commanded nor forbidden
in God's Word. In religious ceremonies, however—such as putting on a surplice
and signing with the cross—and in those acts commanded or forbidden by the
rulers of the commonwealth and the church, it is absurd and profane.

1. What is good and lawful negatively is neither good nor evil. But ceremonies
and what rulers command are good, while what they forbid is evil. Therefore,
ceremonies and what rulers command cannot be negatively

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 122 | PDF p. 173]

good and lawful. The major premise is clear, because whatever may lawfully be
done and is commanded is good. Rulers cannot command others to do what they
themselves cannot embrace by their own will. But rulers cannot embrace by
their will what is neither good nor evil, for such a thing is the object of no
will. Nor can ceremonies be commanded unless they are good and serve for
edification.

“Whatever is not from faith is sin” cannot be understood to mean, “Whatever
does not proceed from logical and conjectural certainty is sin.” For, first,
then sinning in most acts committed from ignorance would be from faith.
Second, if what is negatively lawful were from faith, our moral acts would be
from faith in the same way that constructing a syllogism in Barbara was from
faith, which is absurd.

2. If rulers can command one thing that is negatively lawful, they can command
everything negatively lawful. But the latter is absurd; therefore, so is the
former. The reason for the major premise is that they command it either because
it is negatively lawful and under that formal description—in which case they
can command everything negatively lawful, since “what belongs in itself”
(καθ᾿ αὑτό) “belongs to every instance” (κατὰ παντός)—and thus they can command
the picking up of a straw and rubbing of a beard in God's worship. This is
absurd, for such things would be idle, ridiculous, and unfit for edification.
But rulers govern for good, Romans 13:4. If, however, they command something
negatively lawful not as such but as good, then they command the negatively
lawful thing because it is positively good and conforms to God's Word. Thus

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 123 | PDF p. 174]

ceremonies, in so far as rulers command them, must be proved syllogistically
from God's Word or from some natural reason consonant with God's Word—which is
the same thing. They therefore resort to this distinction in vain.

3. Rulers can lawfully command nothing unless the people can do it from faith,
because “whatever is not from faith is sin,” Romans 14:23, even in the most
indifferent acts, such as the observance of days and discrimination among
foods. In their use Paul requires in verse 5 something more—full assurance
(πληροφορίαν)—than Sanderson, who contends that conjectures and ethical or
probable faith are enough. But negatively lawful things are not from faith,
because they cannot be conformed to any Word of God. To do something from
faith is to do what I know is pleasing to God and service to Christ. Verse 18:
“He who serves Christ in these things is acceptable to God.” And verse 22:
“Have you faith? Have it to yourself before God.” But that faith cannot be
knowledge by which I know that something is neither commanded nor forbidden by
God. For then in faith I would rub my beard, pick up a straw, shut my eyes, and
cough while speaking, since all these things are negatively lawful. No one
would say that these things are done from faith unless he exposed all theology
to laughter and mockery. Moreover, to eat in

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 124 | PDF p. 175]

faith, which Paul discusses there, would be to eat with the same full assurance
and persuasion of conscience with which I cough while speaking or rub my
beard. I would thus serve Christ in the use of indifferent things in the same
way that I serve Christ when rubbing my beard, than which nothing more absurd
can be said. Who would believe that Paul calls this “faith before God,” and a
faith which does not condemn but makes a person blessed, as in verse 22—a
faith which is nothing but conjectural knowledge by which I know that rubbing
my beard is neither commanded nor forbidden by God in the Word?

4. If negatively lawful things which we may do and rulers may command are
truly lawful, then another ark to represent Christ incarnate, another
circumcision different from the Jewish one, other sacrifices in so far as they
represent Christ now dead, the slaughter of a lamb, a Passover lamb, and all
the Mosaic ceremonies can be introduced into the church, provided, first,
that the Jewish intention and signification are absent and, second, that they
are regarded as neither necessary nor binding the conscience. For all these
things are negatively lawful, that is, neither commanded nor forbidden—unless
they are forbidden in the way that things not commanded are forbidden, which
is our position. For we maintain that this is a sound inference: “This
religious observance is not from God; therefore, it is unlawful.”

5. What is negatively lawful—that is, what does not conflict with God's
Word—also does not conflict with natural

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 125 | PDF p. 176]

reason and the law of nature, which is part of the Word. But what merely does
not conflict with natural reason cannot on that account be commanded by
rulers. First, if rulers can command what conforms negatively to natural
reason, then the rulers' will, destitute of every practical and natural reason,
would be the rationale and law of every constitution and canon. Thus their
bare will, without reason, would be law, which is absurd. Ceremonies would then
have no more natural reason behind them than if rulers commanded everyone to
dance in a cemetery and everyone approaching the church to shout, “Twice three
is six.”

6. If rulers may lawfully command what is neither forbidden nor commanded by
the Word or by natural reason, such as signing with the cross and the surplice,
then they may lawfully command idle actions, and therefore sins, which is
blasphemous. I prove the major premise. An action supposed to be moral, which
formally proceeds from a subject of Christ's kingdom who in that action is
bound to regard his neighbor's edification and God's glory, yet which is
neither commanded nor forbidden by Scripture or by the practical light of
natural reason, is idle—like deliberately dancing every morning before some
particular tomb in a cemetery. But actions negatively conformed to God's Word
are such actions. Therefore, and so forth. I prove the major premise. Scripture
and the light of natural reason either command or forbid every moral action.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 126 | PDF p. 177]

Indeed, it implies a contradiction for an action to be moral—such as putting
on a surplice to signify pastoral innocence—and yet to be commanded neither
by Scripture nor by natural reason, which is rightly a part of Scripture just
as the law of nature is a part of the moral law. For by the same reasoning,
constructing a syllogism in Barbara would be a moral action, though it flows
from a principle that is not moral at all but from a habit of art. And signing
with the cross proceeds either from the light of natural reason, which no one
would say; from the light of Scripture, which the adversaries deny; or from a
habit of religion, and thus from God's Word, which they would not say either.

7. A moral action which lacks what formally constitutes the lawful or legal is
not morally lawful. But a moral action negatively conformed to God's Word—that
is, one neither commanded nor forbidden by God's Word—lacks what formally
constitutes the lawful or legal. Therefore, a moral action negatively conformed
to God's Word is not lawful. The major premise is certain. I prove the minor.
If an action negatively conformed to God's Word lacks God's will commanding in
the Word, it lacks what formally constitutes the lawful or legal. But an
action negatively conformed to God's Word lacks God's will commanding in the
Word. Therefore, and so forth. The minor premise belongs to the adversaries.
The major is clear, because God's commanding will is the formal ground of the
lawful, and God's forbidding will is the formal ground of the unlawful. Thus a
father's slaying of his son

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 127 | PDF p. 178]

is lawful if God commands it to Abraham, but the same slaying is unlawful for
this reason alone: God forbids it. Eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge
is indifferent in itself before God's prohibition is revealed to Adam; when
God forbids it, eating is unlawful; and had God commanded it, eating would
have been lawful.

They reply: In doctrinal or substantial matters, the will commanding or
forbidding is the formal ground of the lawful or unlawful. I answer, first,
that they define “doctrinal” as what God commanded in the Word, just as the
ritual elements in the Lord's Supper—bread, wine, eating, and drinking—are
doctrinal. If God had commanded signing with the cross, it would have been
doctrinal. Conversely, “ritual” is what human authority commands, just as
circumcision would have been ritual if human authority commanded it. Thus they
say that God's commanding will is the formal ground of the lawful in matters
where it is the formal ground of the lawful; and in matters where it is not
the formal ground of the lawful, there it is not the formal ground of the
lawful. For they cannot define “doctrinal” otherwise than as “what God teaches
us in the Word.” Second, human will is made the formal ground of the lawful
without any reason. For then some sin would be a transgression against human
will. But theologians teach that John rightly defines sin as “transgression of
the law,” namely, divine law. That definition would not be exact if some sin
against God were a transgression of human law.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 128 | PDF p. 179]

ROMANS 14:14

But to him who considers anything unclean, to him it is unclean.

Interpreters commonly teach on this passage that foods in themselves under
Christ are no longer divided into clean and unclean. But to someone who in his
conscience thinks a clean food unclean, it is unclean, so that he would sin by
eating it no less than if it were forbidden as unclean by God's law.

It is asked whether the Remonstrants rightly teach in the *Apology*, chapter
25, folios 289–290, that although God's Word has in and through itself the
power to bind, it does not actually bind anyone unless it is understood; and
thus it binds according to the sense in which, after the exercise of every
possible diligence and prudence, it is believed to be understood, because no
one is bound to follow the true sense of the divine Word against his own
conscience, even an erroneous one. The pernicious question is whether God's
Word or conscience binds one to assent. The Remonstrants maintain that
conscience actually binds, so that a person is bound to embrace as true the
sense of Scripture which he believes in his own conscience to be true, even
though that conscience is erroneous.

We maintain the contrary. 1. People are punished when they refuse to believe
God speaking in the Scriptures, even though their conscience understands the
sense of the Word either wrongly or not at all. Numbers 14:11–12; Luke 1:20;
1 Kings 13:21–22.

2. No one is bound to sin. But to follow an erroneous

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 129 | PDF p. 180]

conscience is to sin. How blasphemously, then, the Remonstrants write against
Vedelius, book 1, part 1, chapter 2: “When conscience is erroneous because of
invincible ignorance which is either unavoidable in a person or remains in him
after every possible diligence has been exercised, then a person is bound to
follow the dictate of that conscience just as if it did not err; and he would
sin if he acted against it, because an act of the will ought to conform to its
proximate rule.” From this I assume the following: A father's conscience, from
Zechariah 13:3, dictates that he ought to pierce his son for speaking lies in
God's name. Therefore, the father is bound to stain his hands with his son's
blood just as if he understood that passage of Zechariah correctly; indeed, he
would sin if he did not commit that dreadful murder of his child. Yet I assume
he has exercised every possible diligence in understanding it: first, he
prayed; second, he compared the preceding context with what follows; third, he
considered the phraseology; and fourth, he carefully weighed the purpose. In
the same way, a Jesuit would be bound by many Old Testament Scriptures to
pierce our king with a sword, because in his conscience he believes himself
bound to kill a heretical king on the ground that an act of the will ought to
conform to its proximate rule.

3. Our erroneous conscience does not release us from the binding right which
God's Word holds over our consciences. For the Word exercises primacy and
authority over us to take the intellect captive in obedience to God. One sin

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 130 | PDF p. 181]

does not give us the right to commit another. Thinking and judging wrongly
about God's Word is sin. Are we therefore bound to another sin, namely, to
follow that erroneous judgment?

4. After the fall the wisdom of the flesh, which is utterly hostile to God,
necessarily exists in us, and the gospel declares it to be foolishness. Romans
8:7; 1 Corinthians 2:14. Therefore, we are bound to distrust and reject
Christ, and would sin if we did not follow an erroneous conscience.

5. The Remonstrants indeed concede that a person is bound to lay aside an
erroneous conscience when the error concerns necessary matters evident to
everyone, for that error is vincible and culpable. But while the erroneous
conscience remains and stands in its error, dictating that Christ is not the
Savior of the world and that human affairs are whirled about by chance rather
than providence, still “no one is bound to follow the true sense of the divine
Word”—by which it is truly said that Christ is the Son of God and that the
world is governed by providence—“against conscience, even an erroneous one,
since the Word when not understood does not actually bind.” Second, as they
teach, conscience is in all things the proximate rule of actions, to which the
will must conform. Third, in necessary matters no one is bound to blind and
implicit obedience any more than in controversies, for the understanding of
which people often exercise every possible diligence.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 131 | PDF p. 182]

6. The right which God's Word possesses over people is absolute, not dependent
upon an error of the mind or upon our knowledge as a condition without which
it would not actually bind us to assent to God's Word. The judgment of
conscience is indeed a necessary condition and the due application of the
Word to the mind and will, but it is not what formally binds us. Nor is it a
condition of the obligation which the Word possesses over our consciences; it
is a condition of our due understanding. Thus theologians rightly teach that
an erroneous conscience binds but does not oblige. It binds because a person
cannot act against an erroneous conscience without sinning. Hence Bonaventure,
book 2 of the *Sentences*, distinction 39: “One who acts against a conscience
by which he believes that God has forbidden something, even though he errs,
despises God. For to despise the king's legate is to despise the king. But
conscience is a legate and judge appointed by God; therefore, to despise it is
to despise God.” Thus the pagan Menander said, “For all mortals, conscience is
a god” (βροτοῖς ἅπασιν, ἡ συνείδησις θεός). Conscience can therefore make an
otherwise good action evil. For example, to maintain that God governs chance
events by his providence is a true and pious opinion. But if someone maintains
it against the light of his conscience and believes it false, that opinion is
impious and unlawful *per accidens* for the person who maintains a true
position against the judgment of his conscience, even though erroneous. But

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 132 | PDF p. 183]

conscience does not oblige simply as conscience, but as properly informed from
God's Word. Therefore, God's Word is the formal ground of obligation, while
the light of conscience is only a necessary condition—not of the obligation,
since the obligation is absolute, but of due understanding and true practice.

The Remonstrants reply in the same place, *Against Vedelius*, book 1, part 2,
chapter 2: “This right always remains entire and inviolate. But the right which
God's Word possesses over people is not a right by which a person is bound to
assent to the sense of the divine Word without judgment, much less against the
judgment of his conscience—that is, while its judgment stands and remains
that this sense is false. For then God's right would bind a person to what is
sin in itself.”

Response. It is true that the right which God's Word possesses over people is
not a right by which a person is bound to assent to God's Word without
judgment or against judgment. But I grant a third alternative. It is a right
binding people to assent with judgment, indeed, but with a right and true
judgment consonant with God's Word. Although people do not possess such a
judgment but its contrary—a false and erroneous one—the actual right of God's
Word over them does not perish because of their erroneous conscience, which
is a grave fault in them. For God reveals his mysteries to the humble and to
those who diligently search his law. Second, if there remains an absolute
binding right to assent to the sense of God's Word in any manner, whether with
a right judgment

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 133 | PDF p. 184]

or an erroneous one, then there remains an actual right by which we are bound
to sin. This is to fall into Scylla while wishing to avoid Charybdis. For let
them tell us, I ask, whether God's Word possesses the right to bind a person
who in his erroneous conscience thinks Mary's son an impostor to assent to Old
Testament Scriptures which seem to his erroneous conscience to establish that
Christ is an impostor. The Word, then, possesses a twofold right: a potential
right, by which people are bound to assent to God's Word, and an actual right,
by which they are bound to assent with a right judgment.

7. Their claim of a right and obligation to assent to God's Word even while an
erroneous conscience stands has its foundation in that foul error by which
they maintain that the mind is not subject to God's law because it is a purely
natural power. This is absurd. A person would therefore be bound, without sin,
to believe that chance and fortune rule, because that seems to his erroneous
conscience to follow from God's Word when the good are oppressed and the
wicked flourish.

8. A conscience which we are bound to lay aside does not oblige us to
practice. But we are bound to lay aside an erroneous conscience. Therefore,
and so forth. The major premise belongs to the adversaries. The minor is clear,
because to possess a culpable conscience is sin; therefore, we are bound to
lay it aside. The Remonstrants do not deny in the same place that “when a
judgment is culpably erroneous, both the assent and the judgment are evil and
displeasing to God.” Therefore, either God binds people to sin and to what is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 134 | PDF p. 185 | stop before It can be objected]

evil and displeasing to him, or no one is bound to follow an erroneous
judgment.

But they say that an erroneous conscience is not a fault; the fault is to have
an erroneous conscience through one's own fault. Response. If having an
erroneous conscience is a fault, then following it as a guide is a fault.
Second, it is false that a person does not sin by following an erroneous
conscience. And if a person is bound to follow it, as they say, then he is
bound to sin and acts rightly in the very act of sinning. For a person acts
rightly by doing what he is bound to do.

9. The injury done to God's Word will rebound upon God himself if a person is
bound to a false and erroneous interpretation of the divine Word.

Objection. What will a person do, the Remonstrants ask in the same place, if he
believes a particular sense of Scripture to be true although it is false? He
will either receive it as true even though it is false, or, against his
conscience, reject the sense of the Word which he thinks true. Response. I
grant a third alternative. He is bound to do neither; he is bound to lay aside
his erroneous judgment and follow the true sense.
