QUESTION X

ON THE ETERNITY OF GOD

Does God's eternity exclude succession according to earlier and later?
Affirmative against Socinus.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. God's infinity with respect to duration is called eternity. Three things
are attributed to it: 1. that it is without a beginning; 2. that it is without
an end; and 3. that it lacks succession, that is, the duration of a thing
according to earlier and later, according to present, past, and future. The
question here is not properly whether God is eternal with respect to the first
two, that is, whether he is without beginning and end. Socinus and Vorstius
cannot deny this in the face of so many exceedingly clear testimonies of
Scripture. Rather, the question concerns the third: whether his eternity is
free from succession, or whether it is subject to differences of time, as
they maintain. See Crellius, book 1 of *On Religion*, chapter 18. We, however,
hold that God is free from every difference of time and no less from
succession than from beginning and end.

II. The question does not concern eternity improperly so called and eternity
in a certain respect. In this sense it is sometimes taken for the long
duration of a thing, as circumcision is called an everlasting covenant

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 183 | IA leaf 237 | PDF p. 237]

in Genesis 17—that is, a stable covenant which had to last until the
restoration (διόρθωσιν) of all things. The hills are called everlasting in
Deuteronomy 33:15 because on account of their long continuance and constant
duration they seem to approach eternity. Or the term is used of that which
has no end although it had a beginning, as angels and souls are eternal.
Rather, the question concerns absolute eternity properly so called, both
*a parte ante* and *a parte post*—with respect to what is before and what is
after.

[Marginal heading: That God is eternal is proved.]

III. Scripture teaches that such eternity belongs to God, not only when it
calls God “eternal,” Gen. 21:33; one “who inhabits eternity,” Isa. 57:15; “the
King of ages, immortal, invisible,” 1 Tim. 1:17; and one “who was before the
world was created,” Psalm 90:1–2, “from age to age.” It also does so when it
attributes to him all the properties of eternity in Psalm 102:26–28. First it
does so separately: that he is without beginning, because he existed before
the foundation of the earth, v. 26; without end, because he remains forever
and his years do not fail, vv. 25, 28; and without succession, because he is
said to remain without any change, v. 27, and always to be the same, v. 28.
For one from whom at nearly every moment something earlier departs and to whom
something later is added is not always the same. Then it does so conjointly,
when God's perpetual continuance in the same state is opposed in vv. 13–14 to
the mutability of the most constant things, which have a beginning, an end,
and succession: “The heavens will perish, but you remain, and they all will
grow old as a garment, and like clothing you will roll them up, and they will
be changed; but you are the same, and your years do not fail.” James 1:17
tends to the same conclusion when it says that with God there is no “change,
or shadow of turning” (παραλλαγὴν, ἢ τροπῆς ἀποσκίασμα). For the succession
and flow of the parts of duration, which gradually exist one after another,
present a certain appearance of motion, which cannot befall God.

IV. The same is the point of his being called “the first and the last” in
Isaiah 41:4, and “the Alpha and the Omega” in John’s Apocalypse, chapter 1. He
is a beginning without a beginning, because although he is the beginning of
all things, he himself lacks a beginning; and an end without an end, because
although he is the end to which all things are referred, he can have no end.
What is without a beginning is also without succession, because succession
depends upon a beginning and denotes an order according to earlier and later.
The name Jehovah necessarily includes this eternity because, as was said, it
designates God as the first and independent being, subject to no change. Hence
the French Version properly renders it throughout as *l'Eternel*, because
eternity *par excellence* (κατ’ ἐξοχὴν) belongs to him alone.

V. God's eternity cannot have succession because the essence, with which it
is really identified, admits none. This is so both because the essence is
supremely simple and immutable and therefore rejects the change of earlier
into later, past into present, and present into future that succession
involves; and because it is immeasurable, since it is first and independent.
But what endures through succession is in some manner measured.

VI. The fact that true eternity is customarily described by the Scholastics
as “the whole, simultaneous, and perfect possession of interminable life”
supports this. Thus it excludes succession no less than a limit and must be
conceived as a “standing now,” not a flowing one. The reason is that nothing
flows away from God's life with time, as it does from ours. Rather, at every
moment God simultaneously possesses whatever we possess separately through
the succession of time. Hence the philosophers rightly said that neither the
future nor the past, neither “will be” nor “was,” but only the present or “is”
can rightly be said of him. For God's eternal duration embraces all times—the
past, present, and future—yet nothing in him can be past or future, because
his life

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 184 | IA leaf 238 | PDF p. 238]

always remains the same and unchanged.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Three differences of time are attributed to God in Revelation 1:4, when
he is called “the one who is, who was, and who is to come” (ὁ ὢν, καὶ ὁ ἦν,
καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος). They are attributed not formally but eminently and
anthropopathically (ἀνθρωποπαθῶς), so that God's eternity may in some measure
be described in this manner. They are not predicated divisively, as though
they were predicated of him successively, but indivisively, because God's
eternity embraces all times simultaneously. Thus the past is affirmed, but
without denying the present and future, and the present is asserted, but
without denying the past and future. Augustine explains this well in tractate
99 on John: “Although that immutable and ineffable nature does not admit ‘was’
or ‘will be,’ but only ‘is,’ nevertheless, on account of the changeability of
the times in which our mortality and mutability are situated, we say without
falsehood, ‘is, was, and will be’: he was in past ages, is in the present, and
will be in the future; he was because he never was lacking, he will be because
he never will be lacking, and he is because he always is.”

VIII. Although eternity coexists with all the differences of time, it does
not follow that they coexist with one another. For eternity does not coexist
with them taken together and existing simultaneously, but coexists with them
as they exist separately and succeed one another. Thus the past, while it
existed, coexisted with eternity; the present now coexists with it; and the
future will coexist with it.

IX. Things that agree in one third thing agree with one another, but only
with respect to that same third thing. Thus all the differences of time agree
with one another in this: each of them, when it exists, coexists with the
whole of eternity. But they should not therefore agree with one another so as
to coexist simultaneously, because the whole of eternity does not coexist
with them taken together, but separately, as they mutually succeed one
another. In the same way, the sun and its motion coexist with all the days of
an age, yet it does not follow that all those days coexist with one another.
For each day in its own order coexists with the sun, which always remains the
same.

X. Although time coexists with the whole of eternity, it does not follow that
time is eternal, because this coexistence is not adequate, as though time and
eternity were of the same nature and duration. Rather, it is an inadequate
coexistence of a thing wholly heterogeneous both in nature and in duration.

XI. God's indivisible eternity embraces all divisible times, not
coextensively or formally but eminently and indivisibly, just as the immense
God embraces by his immensity all the extended and divisible parts of the
world, although his nature is indivisible, because wherever he is, he is
wholly there.

XII. A point is either mathematical, which is the beginning of a line or of
time; physical and political, namely, the shortest extension either of mass
or of time; or metaphysical, which denotes the negation of extension and
divisibility. In this third sense it is not absurd for the world and time to
be contained in the point of eternity.

XIII. When a thousand years are said to be before the Lord as one day in
Psalm 90:4, the subject is not only estimation—that God considers a thousand
years no more than one day—but also the comparison of our duration, which is
ephemeral (ἐφήμερος), with the divine duration, which is eternal. This implies
that God must not be measured by our standard, as though his promise were
delayed even if in our judgment it is fulfilled rather late, because God is
not subject to any differences of time, but a thousand years before God are
like one day.

XIV. God is called the Ancient of Days not because he is worn out with old age
and full of years, like the pagans’ Saturn, but because he is prior and more
ancient than the days themselves and the birth of time. Thus those days and
years are not attributed to him properly but anthropopathically, because we
who live in time can conceive nothing except by relation (σχέσιν) to the time
in which we exist.

XV. When God's actions are considered as either past, present, or future, this
is said not with respect to the efficient cause, but with respect to the
effects and

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 185 | IA leaf 239 | PDF p. 239]

objects. They are produced at different times, and his actions terminate in
them.

XVI. Time and eternity are related not as part and whole, but as mutually
opposed species of duration. Eternity always has been and will be. Time,
however, neither always has been nor always will be, but will cease with the
world.
