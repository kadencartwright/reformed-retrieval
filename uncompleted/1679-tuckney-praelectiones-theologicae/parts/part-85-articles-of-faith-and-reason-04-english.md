PROOF.

The first argument is drawn from God's supreme prerogative. This extends
equally to the intellect and the will. Just as we are therefore bound to
obey him even when the will holds back, so we must believe him even when
reason resists, or at least sees dimly, Romans 4:18. Among the Pythagorean
maxims there also occurs the saying that nothing wondrous concerning divine
things should be disbelieved.

Second, this is especially supported by Scripture's custom and manner of
presenting the articles of faith. Although in some heads of natural theology
it appeals to us and to the judgment of our reason, Isaiah 5:5 and 1
Corinthians 11:13, in matters

[1679 new-series fol. 12 | IA leaf 332]

of supernatural faith it disregards reason and pays no regard to our
understanding, 1 Corinthians 1:20. “Thus says the Lord” is the prophetic
style. Christ and the apostles similarly direct their hearers to God's Word
as the foundation of their doctrine and establish faith from that alone.
They do not shout for reason but demand faith. They reason indeed, but from
Scripture, Acts 17:2; they speak and testify, but according to the prophets,
Acts 26:22. They use logical instruments and philosophical principles as
architectonic aids, but as regards the matters themselves they often pour
forth oracles above reason. Indeed, even in their manner of proceeding they
employ, as Origen says, a method of proof that leads reverence for God
toward demonstrative induction.

The third argument therefore comes from the nature of the things to be
believed by divine faith, among which:

1. Some are absolutely first principles and have no prior causes known by
the light of nature from which they might be demonstrated, as Abulensis
rightly noted. On this ground Aquinas most correctly concludes that theology
is not a science, as do Ockham, Valencia, and other scholastics against
Mairon.

2. Many flow solely from God's most free will. Such are his decrees and
works, in all of which the will itself stands entirely in place of a reason;
for if there were some cause or reason for this will, it could no longer be
God's will. If we believe Luther, no necessary cause can certainly be
assigned, since the will is most free in itself; nor can any be placed
outside it. God acts “according to his good pleasure which he purposed in
himself,” Ephesians 1:9. If people who are less penetrating than audacious
boast that they find the reason for this in us and our will, let them hear
the same apostle: “Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his
counselor?” Romans 11:34.

3. Very many natures are infinite in themselves and therefore
incomprehensible and mysterious, and thus transcend reason. Concerning
*that they are*, they can be perceived through the revealing Word as far as
salvation requires. Zwingli must be understood in this sense when he denies
that God proposes incomprehensible things for us to believe. But concerning
*what* or *why* they are, learned ignorance is our portion. They do not fall
under reason's assessment, since they far exceed its grasp.

Examples include the omnipotent, eternal, incomprehensible, triune God,
concerning whom Job 36:26 says, “Behold, God is great, surpassing our
knowledge,” and who “made darkness his hiding place,” Psalm 18:11, “dwelling
in unapproachable light,” 1 Timothy 6:16.

Christ is the God-man, whose name is “Wonderful, Counselor,” Isaiah 9:9.

God's counsels and decrees are a “depth” and “unsearchable,” Romans 11:33.

God's peace “surpasses every understanding,” Philippians 4:7.

The Spirit's joy is “unspeakable,” 1 Peter 1:8.

God's glory is within the veil, where we enter not by knowledge but by hope,
Hebrews 6:19. “Eye has not seen,” 1 Corinthians 2:9. “Ear has not heard,” or
at least there are “unspeakable words,” 2 Corinthians 12:4, concerning which
it is dangerous even to speak truly.

Finally, the whole gospel is a mystery, 1 Timothy 3:16, “into which angels
desire to look,” 1 Peter 1:12, and is above all we can think, Ephesians 3:20,
as the heavens are high above the earth, Isaiah 55:9.

[1679 new-series fol. 13 | IA leaf 333]

All these things, like the rays of the most brilliant sun, so dazzle every
power of understanding that the more intently we fix our thoughts upon
contemplating them, the more deeply, blinded by perpetual light, we lament
our frailty. But if anyone intrudes more arrogantly or curiously, as the
Beth-shemites did of old, “the searcher of Majesty shall be overwhelmed by
glory.” Whoever attempts to measure these things with the ten-foot rod of
his reason falls sometimes into madness, more often into heresy, and finally
into atheism.

Fourth, as the argument is drawn from the sublimity and subtlety of the
things believed, so it is also drawn from the weakness of human reason.

For since reason, as the School defines it, is an intellectual nature under
a shadow, it may grasp matters of faith less or more according to its
different states, but it never rises to an entirely perfect knowledge of
them.

In the states of innocence and glory it is most perfect, yet even then it is
finite and therefore has no proportion to infinite things. We nevertheless
now believe very many infinite things, as we showed above, in which the
intellect of Adam or even of an angel falls immeasurably short as regards
their perfect knowledge.

How much more does our reason fall short in the state of grace, when it is
renewed only in part? Since so much native darkness still adheres to it,
such difficulty, imperfection, obscurity, and confusion remain in knowing
divine things that we must necessarily know only in part.

Most of all, in the state of sin, that sound and right reason about which
some speak so loudly is, as regards actual existence, mutilated in most
people and therefore diseased. In some it is, as even the Remonstrants
admit, completely dead and thus nonexistent.

In natural matters, where reason is most lynx-eyed, how bleary it
nevertheless is! The wisest Socrates candidly acknowledged that he knew only
this: that he knew nothing. The sharpest-sighted Philosopher spoke of the
eyes of bats. Agur, by his own admission, becomes brutish—“What is his
name?”—if Job is to be believed in chapter 26:14. The following chapters
sufficiently show how quickly he himself was reduced to an impasse in those
common matters. We lick the glass vessel; we are wholly ignorant of the
forms of things, about which reason and sense are especially occupied. To
veil our blindness more effectively, we plead occult qualities, instincts
of nature, and principles not to be disputed. What more need be said? In
God's wisdom the world did not know God, 1 Corinthians 1:21.

In moral matters, where according to Gerson synderesis is always a virgin,
and where the Philosopher wishes right reason always to reach toward the
best, wickedness is nevertheless highly prized, as he himself admits. Again,
“pleasures corrupt the principles.” Thus those who ought to be impelled to
worship God by common notions suppress God's truth in unrighteousness,
Romans 1:18. Whatever they know naturally, like brute animals, they corrupt
themselves in these things, Jude 10. To deal with the matter point by point:

In the law, especially insofar as it is spiritual, human reason is blind,
Ephesians 4:18 and Romans 7:7, 14.

In the gospel it is sheer darkness, entirely blind, Ephesians 5:8.

As concerns a supernatural matter of faith, as was said earlier, reason:

1. Does not discover it by itself; “the vulture's eye has not seen this
path,” Job 28:7.

[1679 new-series fol. 14 | IA leaf 334]

2. Does not sufficiently grasp it when revealed: “the natural person does
not receive it,” 1 Corinthians 2:14; “the light shines in the darkness, and
the darkness did not comprehend it,” John 1:5.

3. Does not approve it when perceived: “the mind of the flesh is hostile to
God,” Romans 8:7.

4. Indeed, the most sharp-sighted are often the blindest of all, Matthew
11:25 and John 9:22. These descriptions apply especially to them: “they
became futile” and “they became fools,” Romans 1:21–22.

5. Finally, in the very matters in which we wish to be most cautious, we
often go shamefully astray and stumble, which Bellarmine rightly placed
among the wounds of the soul.

Go on, then: let right Reason at last stride along with raised eyebrow and,
while blind, proceed straight into the inner sanctuaries of divine
mysteries!

The final argument can be drawn from the sufficiently broad difference
between faith and reason, which prevents reason from being the rule of
faith. Thus the apostle defined faith as “the substance of things not seen”
and not so much distinguished as opposed walking by faith and walking by
sight. Augustine therefore says that Christ ascended into heaven so that
our faith might be built up.

Hence Gregory's familiar statement: “Faith has no merit where human reason
supplies an experiment.” If reason follows faith, it helps greatly; but if it
comes before faith, it diminishes not merely the merit of faith, as Aquinas
says, but in some measure faith itself. Although we can also believe the
same things we understand, just as Thomas believed Christ whom he saw and
touched, we nevertheless believe many other things and believe them in a
different manner than we understand them. Faith knows these things as a
rule; the intellect knows them as the subject of cognition. To conclude the
whole matter at once, three things are usually considered in faith which
serve to distinguish it from reason:

1. Infallible certainty, beneath which no falsehood can lie. Human reason
does not possess this to the same degree, and certainly not always.

2. The obscurity of the thing. The greater this is, the more obscure and
weak the intellect is, but the stronger and clearer faith becomes. In that
darkness reason gropes, but faith shines. Where reasoning ends, faith comes
to the aid, as Orosius rightly says.

3. Reason and the intellect rest upon the truth of the thing itself, while
faith rests upon testimony: if human, on human testimony; if divine, on
divine testimony. Thus the apostle in 1 Thessalonians 2:13, and Augustine:
“What we know we owe to reason; what we believe we owe to authority.” The
ultimate resolution of faith is therefore not: “I understand or judge thus
according to the truth of the matter,” but: “Because the truthful God speaks
and testifies thus, I therefore both believe and ought to believe, although
I do not understand the matter itself from every side and in every detail.”
For whoever does not believe God beyond what he sees places no more faith in
the infinitely truthful God than in the most deceitful person. Whoever made
faith and reason commensurable would at times abolish faith's strongest and
most effective act, as sometimes happens in temptation, when all knowledge
of salvation is entirely swallowed up yet faith does not fail. Whoever
measures the articles of faith by the standard of human reason will
therefore be unjust to God, to faith, and to himself.

Thus anyone who attempts to adapt the heavenly secrets of religion to our
reason will judge in vain. We should rather lift our minds to adore the
throne of heavenly truth. Bacon, *On the Advancement of Learning* 3.2.
