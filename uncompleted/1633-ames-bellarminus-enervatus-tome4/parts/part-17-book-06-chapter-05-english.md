### Chapter V

#### On the Distinction between Law and Gospel

As Bellarmine is about to lay certain foundations of the Pontifical doctrine
concerning the righteousness of works, which he calls actual, he first
discourses on law, Gospel, Christian liberty, and the necessity of works no
less calumniously than insidiously. Yet since he has not rightly formulated
the state of any controversy concerning these things, we can dispute in no
other way than by appending our observations to those things which he advances
and which are of some moment.

##### Thesis I

Bellarmine. *The Gospel can be understood in two ways: in one way, for the
doctrine which Christ and the apostles preached and which is contained in the
books of the Evangelists and the other writings of the apostles. In another
way, for the grace of the New Testament, which is also customarily called the
law written in the heart, the life-giving Spirit, and the law of faith.
Although, to confess the truth, the name “Gospel” does not seem ever to be
understood in Scripture except in the former way, that is, for doctrine.*

Protestant reply. I. First of all it must be observed that the discussion here
is not about the Gospel with respect to any use or application of the name
whatever, but as it is described by Paul with reference to justification and
salvation in Romans 1:17–18: *the power of God for salvation to everyone who
believes, revealing the righteousness of God from faith to faith.* Since
Bellarmine took no account of this limitation, it is no wonder if he confuses
everything.

II. In that first sense in which alone Bellarmine says the Gospel is
understood in the Scriptures, he errs in more than one way. For first,
Scripture everywhere makes known to us the doctrine of the Gospel either by
its subject, as when it is called *the Gospel of the Son of God, of Christ, of
the kingdom, of salvation, and of grace;* or by its effects and fruits, with
respect to which it is called *the Gospel of salvation, life, reconciliation,
peace, and glory.* But in his description Bellarmine mentions only the authors
and books in which the Gospel is contained. Second, the doctrine of the Gospel
is wrongly restricted to the preaching of Christ and the apostles and to the
books of the New Testament. For the same

[Printed p. 365 | Google Books PA365 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

doctrine in its essence was preached from the beginning and is also contained
in the books of the Old Testament, as the apostles everywhere teach and
demonstrate. Paul too in Galatians 3:8 expressly testifies that the Scripture
of the Old Testament *preached the gospel* in the very first book of Genesis,
and in verse 17 he calls that Gospel *the covenant and promise,* which was four
hundred and thirty years older than the law. III. In the second sense this must
first of all be noted: with one voice all the Scholastics—*our great
masters*—are accused of error and refuted by Bellarmine. For each and every one
of them calls the Gospel *the new law, the evangelical law,* which they also
say is *principally inscribed in the heart and secondarily written in a book.
Thomas, I–II, question 106, article 1.* It must be noted secondly how absurdly
both the Scholastics and Bellarmine restrict the grace of the New Testament to
gifts of God inhering in us, when it properly and commonly signifies the
gratuitous mercy of God. IV. These two should not be separated but joined
together in explaining the true notion of the Gospel, so that it is *the
doctrine of grace,* namely of our election, calling, justification, adoption,
sanctification, and glorification in Christ Jesus, especially that grace by
which at his coming he accomplished all those things required for the
communication of this grace.

2. Bellarmine. *We contend that the Gospel contains laws properly so called,
threats, and promises with the condition of obedience.*

Protestant reply. I. There is no reason why you should contend so earnestly
that all these things are contained in the books of the New Testament, because
you have no one who disagrees. But Thomas himself, I–II, question 106, articles
1 and 2—if his sense of grace is corrected in the manner just stated—rightly
observes that *each thing seems to be that which is chief in it; but grace is
the chief and principal thing in the New Testament, while the rest is
contained in it secondarily, either as disposing toward grace or as directing
the use of this grace.* II. A law properly so called consists chiefly in
prescribing a work and exacting obedience. Although some things are prescribed
in the New Testament, they are nevertheless prescribed not so much as works
but as means of grace instituted by Christ. Other works, which are not
instituted, are explained, determined, and urged in the New Testament, but are
not first prescribed there. III. The threats and terrors which occur in the
New Testament are no more parts of the Gospel than a doctor's indication that
death will follow from neglect of the remedy is a part of medicine. IV.
Promises with the condition of obedience as the cause of the right we have to
the thing promised belong properly to the law; but promises with the condition
of obedience, where obedience is an adjunct or effect of the thing promised or
of its bestowal, have

[Printed p. 366 | Google Books PA366 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

their place in the kingdom of most bountiful grace, where there is no place
for our merits.

3. Bellarmine. *The primary difference between Law and Gospel is that between
an inchoate and a perfect doctrine, for the Gospel is contained in the law as
a tree is in a seed.*

Protestant reply. I. This is not the difference between the law of works and
the law of faith, or between law and promise or Gospel, but between the books
of the Old Testament, which contain both the faith of the promise and deeds,
and the books of the New Testament, which present the same things in a more
excellent manner. II. The doctrine in the books of the Old Testament, compared
with the doctrine delivered in the New concerning the same things, is not
imperfect except, as Alexander of Hales, part 3, question 56, observes, *in the
form of its presentation. The New Testament does not add to the Old as regards
its sense through innovation, but only through elucidation or manifestation.
That addition is as the explicit to the implicit, the revealed to the veiled,
and the determined to the indeterminate.* II. Such a difference, although not
in the same degree, can be observed between the books of Moses and the sermons
of the prophets, and between the Evangelists and the epistles of Paul.
Therefore, what Bellarmine wishes to be the chief distinction between law and
Gospel neither touches the matter nor harms our cause in any way.

4. Bellarmine. *Another distinction is that the law was given through Moses,
who was only a lawgiver, while the Gospel was given through Christ, who was
not only a lawgiver but also a Redeemer. Hence the law came in doctrine alone;
the Gospel came not only in doctrine, but also in grace for loving
righteousness and hating iniquity.*

Protestant reply. We do not deny that this is one distinction, but it is not
the chief one, as appears from Romans 10:5–6 and Galatians 3:11–12; nor is it
rightly explained by Bellarmine; nor, finally, does it do anything against our
cause. It is enough to have given this same warning concerning the remaining
differences which Bellarmine derives from these two.

5. Bellarmine. *The adversaries place Christian liberty in this: that they are
subject to no law in conscience and before God, and so forth.*

Protestant reply. So outrageous is this license to slander that its author,
in bearing this testimony, seems to have had no regard for law or for his own
conscience before God. For we acknowledge that all Christians are subject to
the rule and direction, command and obligation, of the moral law and of every
divine command which imposes some duty upon us. Nevertheless, we believe true
believers to be free from having to seek justification through the law, from
its condemnation to be expected, and from the rigor and terror of its
exaction; and this

[Printed p. 367 | Google Books PA367 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

is that liberty which the Pontificians, from presumption in their own
righteousness and intolerable contempt of grace, refuse when it is offered.
