# CHAPTER X

## The covenant made with the Israelites was not the covenant of works

Thus far we have shown positively what kind of covenant the Lord Jehovah made
with the Israelites whom he had brought out of Egypt. It now follows, in order
to explain and confirm what has gone before more fully, that we should show
what kind of covenant it was not.

Those who deny that this covenant was the *covenant of grace* do not agree in
explaining its nature, but go off into various opinions. Some maintain that it
was the *covenant of works* properly so called, in which perfect obedience to
the law is demanded and required as the sole condition for obtaining life.
Others think it better to assert that it was a *mixed covenant*: neither the
pure covenant of grace nor the pure covenant of works, but a third, blended
and tempered covenant combined from both, and so containing a certain mixture
of the righteousness of works and the righteousness of faith. Others prefer
to say that this covenant should be called a *subservient covenant*, that is,
a covenant which served the covenant of grace in Christ and prepared and
leveled the way for it. Finally, others say that it was an *external, legal,
and typical covenant*. We shall show that none of these descriptions should
be affirmed. First, we shall make it plain here that it was not the covenant
of works.

It must be noted, however, that when we say that this covenant was not the
covenant of works, we do not deny that the covenant of works was then set
forth or proposed to that people. For when we speak of the covenant made with
them, we consider that whole economy, not some part of it taken absolutely and
in abstraction. We therefore say that this economy, considered as a whole,
was a dispensation of the covenant of grace, not of the covenant of works.
Nevertheless, we grant that the covenant of works itself was proposed at that
time. For according to Paul in Romans 10:5, *Moses described the righteousness
which is from the law in these words: The person who performs these things
will live by them.* See Leviticus 18:5. The Decalogue contains the very matter
of the covenant of works, or the sum of all the things which were required of
Adam and his descendants in the covenant of works and which the first-formed
human beings were bound to perform by reason of that covenant.

Yet the covenant of works was not in every respect proposed to the Israelites
in the same way as it was established with Adam. For:

1. The law of works, or the law of nature or the moral

[Printed p. 112 | IA PDF 120 | IA leaf 120 | IA decausadei02brow]

law, which was the matter and stipulation of the covenant of works, was
engraved upon Adam's heart when the Lord made this covenant with him. It was
not engraved upon the Israelites' hearts when this covenant was proposed to
them.

2. The covenant of works first made with Adam had its own seals and tokens:
namely, the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. These
were certainly symbolic and are called sacraments by some. They were not like
those we need now, but were suited to that state of integrity. For they
confirmed both parts of the covenant: the promise of life made to obedience,
and the threat of death to be inflicted upon the disobedient. But this
covenant, when proposed to the Israelites, lacked these tokens and was not
confirmed by any other tokens or seals.

3. The covenant of works was first made with Adam as a public person
representing the whole human race. But this covenant when renewed was not
established with any public person as the head of others. Rather, it was
proposed to all the Israelites considered individually and one by one.

4. That covenant first made was a covenant of friendship. For Adam was God's
closest friend when God made the covenant with him. But it could not be called
a covenant of friendship when it was proposed to the Israelites, for the
Israelites were outside the state of friendship, since they were sinners.

5. The covenant of works made with Adam was founded upon the integrity of
nature. Adam had an uncorrupted nature, and this integrity or righteousness
was natural to him. He could therefore easily perform everything required by
that covenant. The Israelites, however, were entirely destitute of this
righteousness and could not rightly perform anything commanded in that
covenant.

6. It follows that it was not impossible for Adam to acquire and obtain life
through this covenant of works, for he could fulfill the condition of life
stipulated in it. But it was altogether impossible for the Israelites, for at
that time the law was powerless because it was deprived of strength through
the flesh, as the Apostle says in Romans 8:3.

7. God made the covenant of works with Adam immediately, employing no
mediator. But the law was proposed to the Israelites by the hand of an
intermediary or typical mediator, namely Moses (Galatians 3:19–20).

8. The covenant of works was established with Adam for this purpose: that by
fulfilling the stipulated condition—that is, by perfect obedience—he might
obtain all the promised good things comprehended under the name *life*. But
this covenant was not proposed to the Israelites for this purpose, that they
might acquire a right to life by perfect obedience. For the law given to them
could not abolish the promise or render useless the compact previously
ratified by God, according to Paul in Galatians 3:17. Rather, *the law was
added because of transgressions* (verse 19), so that they might be more
distinctly convicted of sin, humbled and cast down before God by a sense of
their sins, and thereby more strongly spurred to seek a remedy or flee to the
Messiah, the sole mediator.

[Calvin, as quoted by Marlorat on the passage, understands that the law was
given to make transgressions manifest and in this way drive people to
acknowledge their guilt. For because they naturally forgive themselves
easily, they have sleeping consciences unless they are pressed by the law.
Hence Paul's statement that before the law, although sin was in the world, it
was nevertheless not imputed (Romans 5:13). The law therefore came to awaken
those who slept. For this is the true preparation for Christ: *Through the
law*, he says, *comes the knowledge of sin* (Romans 3:20).] In the Mosaic
covenant, says Apollonius in *On the Law of God*, pp. 169–170, God demands his
right for no other

purpose than that people, convicted of their transgression and weakness,
should flee outside themselves to God's mercy and to Christ the mediator.]

Furthermore, when we say that this Mosaic economy was not an administration
of the pure covenant of works, we are not speaking of that economy as the
carnal Israelites perversely abused it. In pursuing a law of righteousness,
they did not attain to the law of righteousness because they sought it not by
faith but as though by works of the law (Romans 9:31–32). Indeed, in striving
to establish their own righteousness (Romans 10:3), they utterly perverted
that economy and fashioned a covenant of works for themselves in full form.
Rather, we speak of that economy as it was dispensed by God and established
among them.
