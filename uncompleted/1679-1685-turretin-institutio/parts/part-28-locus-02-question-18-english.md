QUESTION XVIII

ON THE READING OF SCRIPTURE

Can Scripture be read fruitfully by every believer, and ought it to be read
without permission? We affirm, against the Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: The Roman Catholic position on the reading of Scripture.]

I. The position of the Roman Catholics can be gathered from nothing better
than Rule IV of the Index of Prohibited Books, issued by the decree of Trent,
which reads as follows: “Since it is manifest from experience that, if the
sacred Bibles in the vernacular language are permitted, more harm than benefit
arises from this because of human rashness, Bibles with all their parts,
whether printed or manuscript, in whatever vernacular language they exist,
are prohibited.” Yet because this seemed too harsh, Pius IV appears to have
wished to add some moderation, since he reduces the granting of permission to
read Bibles to the judgment of the ordinary parish priest or bishop—namely,
for those whom they judge able to receive from this kind of reading not harm
but an increase of faith and piety. But the subsequent Observation of Clement
VIII shows that hope to be wholly illusory in connection with this fourth
rule, when it denies that any new faculty is granted to bishops or others to
confer such a license, since this faculty had previously been taken from them
by command of the Holy Roman Inquisition, whose practice must be adhered to in
this affair. Thus, by taking away with one hand what they seemed to give with
the other, they show that their aim was nothing other than to put this light
under a bushel and wrest Scripture from the hands of the people so that their
errors might not be exposed. It must nevertheless be acknowledged that
various Roman Catholics regard this as harsh tyranny and judge that the
reading of Scripture should be granted to believers. But they are few in
comparison with those who urge its prohibition, whose position has been
received as the common position of the whole church, inasmuch as it is founded
upon a law sanctioned by the authority of the council and the pontiff. The
council itself, pronouncing his power to be supreme throughout the universal
church, Session XIV, *On Penance*, chapter 7.3, expressly entrusted to him the
decision and publication of the matters that pertain to the censorship of
books in the continuation of Session XXV. This therefore cannot be regarded
otherwise, whatever may be alleged to the contrary, than as the universal law
of the Roman church until it is expressly revoked. We, on the other hand,
maintain that every believer not only can read Scripture freely but also ought
to do so, so that no permission from a bishop or parish priest is to be
awaited here.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question is not whether the reading of Scripture is absolutely and
simply necessary for everyone. For infants are saved without it, and among
adults there are many who cannot read (ἀναλφάβητοι) and who have never looked
through it. Rather, the question is whether it is so permitted to everyone
that no one, however uneducated and unskilled, is to be kept from it.

III. The question is not whether a certain discretion can and should be
employed in reading the books of Scripture according to each person's
capacity, in the way that younger persons were once kept from some books of
Scripture because their judgment was not yet sufficiently confirmed on
account of their age. For this is not so much a prohibition as a method of
learning and can suitably be done for greater progress and edification.
Rather, the question is whether reading should be forbidden to anyone. We
deny this.

[Marginal heading: The reading of Scripture is proved.]

IV. The arguments are: 1. God's command, which pertains to each and every
person, Deuteronomy 6:6–8 and 31:11–12; Psalm 1:2; Colossians 3:16; John 5:39;
Joshua 1:8; 2 Peter 1:19; Revelation 1:3. 2. Scripture's purpose, because it
was given for the benefit and salvation of all and supplies everyone with
weapons against our spiritual enemies, 2 Timothy 3

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 134 | IA leaf 188 | PDF p. 188]

:16; Romans 15:4; Ephesians 6:17. 3. Scripture is the testament of the heavenly
Father; who would say that a son should be kept from reading his father's
testament? 4. The constant practice of the church, both Jewish and Christian,
Deuteronomy 17:18–19; Acts 8:27 and 17:11; 2 Peter 1:19; 2 Timothy 3:15–16.
Nor are the ancient Fathers ever so unanimously earnest about anything as
they are about commending and urging the reading of Scripture upon everyone.
See Chrysostom throughout: *Homilies on Genesis*, homily 6; *Homilies on
Matthew*, homilies 1 and 3; and *On Lazarus*, where he repeats more than once
that ignorance of the Scriptures is the cause of all evils; Augustine,
*Confessions*, book 6, chapter 5, and sermon 35, *On Time*; Basil, *On Psalm
1*; Cyprian, *On Spectacles*; Origen, homily 9 on Leviticus and homily 12 on
Exodus; and Jerome, in his letter to Laeta.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

V. What brings more disadvantage and harm in itself and properly than benefit
is not to be permitted. But this does not hold for what brings harm only
incidentally through human vice. If people abuse Scripture, this does not
happen in itself but incidentally, from the perversity of those who wrongly
twist it to their own destruction. Otherwise, if use were to be taken away
because of abuse, Scripture would have to be taken away not only from the
laity but also from learned doctors, who abuse it far more. For heresies
usually arise not from common and uneducated people, but from ecclesiastics.

VI. If errors can arise from Scripture wrongly understood, this is so far
from having to keep believers from reading it that it ought rather to arouse
them to search it, so that by understanding it properly they can oppose such
errors.

VII. Freedom to read Scripture does not remove either oral instruction, the
direction of pastors, or other aids necessary for understanding. It opposes
only the tyranny of those who do not wish the darkness of their errors to be
exposed by the light of the divine Word.

VIII. When Christ forbids giving what is holy to dogs and throwing pearls
before swine, Matthew 7, he does not wish to deny believers the reading and
use of Scripture, for the children of God cannot be designated by the name of
dogs or swine. Rather, he means only that the symbols of divine grace are not
to be communicated indiscriminately to impure sinners, that the highest
mysteries of faith are not to be rashly thrust upon unbelievers or upon those
who, with desperate obstinacy, manifestly resist the truth, but that
instruction is to be adapted to those who show themselves humble and
teachable.

IX. It is not enough that the reading of Scripture is granted to certain
people among the Roman Catholics, because what is commanded to everyone as a
duty ought not to be granted to certain people as a privilege.
