VERSE 16. Moreover, looking beneath the sun, I saw wickedness there in the
place of law—wickedness, I say, in the place of justice.

VERSE 17. I said in my heart, God himself will judge the righteous and the
wicked, because there is indeed a time for every purpose, but he who presides
over that whole affair is there.

VERSE 18. I had said in my heart, according to human reason, that God himself
would make them manifest, and I saw that these men were beasts to them.

VERSE 19. The lot of the sons of man is like the lot of the beast, and the same
lot belongs to them: as the one dies, so the other dies, and the same breath
belongs to them all. Thus man has no superiority over the beasts, for all
these things are vanity.

VERSE 20. Every one of these goes to the same place; every one is from dust,
and every one returns to dust.

VERSE 21. Who observes the human spirit which ascends upward, and the spirit
of the beast which descends beneath the earth?

VERSE 22. Therefore it seemed to me that there is nothing good except for a
man to rejoice in his works, because this is his portion. For who will bring
him to see what will be after him?

The summary is that there is both a divine and a human judgment concerning the
fact that men sitting upon the tribunal and in the place of judgment oppress
the litigants.

The parts are: 1. The oppression which Solomon discovered in the place of
judgment. 2. His judgment concerning it.

In the first part one may ask where he discovered this injury and injustice:
in kingdoms and commonwealths alienated from God, or in the Israelite kingdom?
I answer, in both, but especially in the Israelite kingdom, as that in which
justice ought for a special reason to reign and flourish. It is then asked
whether he surveyed in his mind the administration of earlier kings and
judges or that of his own kingdom. I answer that he did both. There is no doubt
that he read the annals of the times of David, Saul, Samuel, and others, which
were most carefully written out by scribes and transmitted to posterity. In
all of them, even under the most incorrupt men, he observed violence done to
justice and judgment. For after he has left off reading the divine Word, it is
a pursuit most worthy of a princely man to use the error or rectitude which
preceded him in governing the commonwealth, either to guard himself against
evil or to imitate the good. There is less reason to doubt that he did this in
his own kingdom, since it concerned him more to look into his own affairs than
those of others. We are thus taught that it belongs to kings and princes

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA163 | printed pp. 163–164 | begins with complete non after catchword on p. 162; ends before complete simul from catchword at foot of p. 164]

not only to see to it that beneficial laws are enacted, but to cast their eyes
upon every part of the kingdom so that they may understand whether the laws
are being put into execution, and to correct the faults of inferior
magistrates. See how earnestly Jehoshaphat admonishes the judges to execute law
and justice. 2 Chronicles 19.

He magnifies this evil by its subject, the place of judgment and justice. For
it is a more grievous and atrocious evil to pervert judgment and justice under
an appearance of equity in the place of justice and the judgment of the
afflicted, as though in an asylum, than to do so anywhere else. We are indeed
commanded to cultivate and exercise justice in every place: privately and
publicly, at home and abroad, under a roof and beneath the open sky. But in
those places many other things are properly done and administered. The court,
however, is a place sacred to judgment and justice, devoted and consecrated to
their execution and exercise. Just as a temple dedicated to sacred affairs
and divine worship ought not to have served any other use—so much so that it
was not lawful to bring or carry any vessel into it which was not sacred—so in
the place of justice it is lawful to execute and exercise nothing but justice.
And just as a man who in former times behaved profanely in the temple, or who
now does so in the assembly of the church by playing, laughing, and handling
love affairs, sins much more atrociously than if he behaved wantonly and
indecorously anywhere else, so a man who sits upon the bench and violates law
and justice commits a more monstrous crime than if he perpetrated the same
injustice anywhere else. For robbers to maraud with impunity in the heart of a
city and not only in the fields is a thing full of disorder. See Hosea 6,
verses 5 and 9.

For it is shameful that the tribunal of justice, which ought to be an asylum
and sanctuary for the oppressed, should be turned into places notorious for
robberies, which in our vernacular speech we call Stangate-hole and Shuters
Hill. This is the point of Solomon's construction of the throne and tribunal
of justice out of ivory overlaid with the purest gold, raised aloft upon six
steps and supported on either side by figures of lions. What else did he wish
to represent by such great magnificence but the value at which God wished law
and justice to be held, and how cleansed and pure the judgments issued and
pronounced from it ought to be? It is shameful for judgments of iron or lead
to proceed from so august a place, and shameful for dross to come from a
golden platform. Thus the prophet Isaiah also describes the corruption of
public judgment in chapter 1: “Your silver is changed into dross, and your
wine mixed with water.” Explaining the allegory, he adds, “Your princes are
the companions of thieves,” since they accept gifts and presents.

So much for the first part, which concerns Solomon's discovery that injustice
was practiced in the place of justice. His judgment concerning it follows:
the judgment delivered by the Holy Spirit in verse 17 and that delivered by
corrupt flesh in the remaining verses.

Because the Spirit places his judgment before the judgment of the flesh, and
does so consistently throughout this book, ministers in particular are taught
what order they ought to follow in teaching, especially in matters which are
disputed on both sides and are doubtful in the church. They should first set
forth and confirm the truth before assaulting the camp of falsehood. The
churches, moreover, are warned not to thrust themselves too boldly or
curiously into their adversaries' writings before they are solidly and
abundantly grounded in the church's sound doctrine, lest the reward they carry
away and bring back for their rashness be abduction into errors. For whatever
is first offered to the mind's taste is grasped more eagerly, and once the
mind is steeped in it, it becomes so entangled that it does not easily free
itself. Even if the human soul were, as the philosopher said, a scraped tablet,
it would still be worthwhile to imbue it first with all the best things. But
now, since it inclines more toward false things because of the common taint
and corruption of nature, who does not see that it must be tinctured with the
best and truest things before the foundations of falsehood are removed? The
truth, then, is this: God will judge both the righteous

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA165 | printed pp. 165–166 | begins with complete simul after catchword on p. 164; ends after opprimitur on p. 166]

and the wicked. The unjust judge also did this same thing, although he did it
iniquitously and unjustly.

But why does it seem that he should have said that God will judge both justly,
rewarding the righteous according to the merits of his cause and inflicting
due punishment upon the wicked? Since he was speaking about God and his
judgment, he would have acted superfluously if he had said that God would
judge justly. For he said this plainly enough when he said that God would
judge, since God knows how to judge only justly and is the judge of the whole
world. Genesis 18.

The fact that they have God as judge ought to console all the oppressed, so
that they do not lose heart, consume themselves with grief, and become
despondent because justice is not rendered to them in court, not even after
they have appealed from one judge to another. Let it come into their minds
that God is judge of all, that he shows partiality to no one, that he hears the
groans and sighs of the oppressed, and that he carefully gathers their tears
and preserves them sealed in his bottle. When men suffer rejection in one
court, they nourish the hope of winning their cause so long as they may appeal
to another judge, provided that they have acquired some confidence in the
equity and incorruption of the superior judge, even if they have incurred
great expenses exceeding the value of the cause. Therefore, if with Solomon
they are certain of God the judge's integrity—Solomon said this not merely
with his lips, as almost everyone does, but in his heart—it will be easy to
sustain themselves against every injury inflicted by all men, especially
since in this court they spend absolutely nothing except prayers. There is
scarcely anyone who does not profess in words his steadfast belief that God
is a just judge. But men who only say this in words and do not settle it in
their hearts as Solomon did acknowledge God as just only when God executes
his judgment. Solomon, however, professes it amid the greatest confusion of
affairs. See Job 5:3.

If those who have a bad case also considered this, they would have no such
great cause for joy or exultation, but rather for fear and trembling. For by
the same conduct through which they have won a mortal man's favor, they have
kindled God's indignation against themselves; and by an earthly judge's
acquittal, they have summoned God's condemnation. Let no one suppose that he
will go unpunished simply because punishment is not inflicted immediately
after he has committed the injury.

A prolepsis follows. Its tacit objection, drawn from the effects of the
wicked, is this: We see the wicked do everything according to their pleasure
and lust, and they cannot be restrained by any fear of God or man from
persisting in the obstinacy they have undertaken of acting wickedly and
unjustly. The answer is drawn from the circumstance of time, as if Solomon
were to say: They do indeed appear to maraud according to their lust and to
have it in their power to trample and crush anyone beneath their feet. But in
fact the matter is otherwise. They have a time appointed by God which they
cannot pass, at which, once this assembly has been concluded, punishment will
be taken upon the wicked and the righteous will obtain their desires. A day
is fixed for their visitation; a day is appointed for divine wrath; the
measure of injustice which they will fill has been defined. As soon as their
water clock, which God diligently observes, has run dry, God will immediately
take vengeance and vindicate his own. Therefore, let them set before their
eyes the counsel which the prophet also gives the godly: “Come, my people,
enter your chambers and shut your doors behind you. Hide for a brief moment
until indignation has passed. For behold, Jehovah will come forth from his
place to punish the inhabitant of the earth for his iniquity against him, and
the earth will disclose her slain and no longer cover her dead.” Isaiah
26:20–21.

He gives the reason from God's accompanying presence and superintendence over
this whole affair, in which the righteous man is oppressed both by the wicked
man who judges him and by the man who disputes with him at law.

God is present to know the matter and the cause, and he presides in order to
rescind the perverse judgment and make it void. See Psalm 88. If a mortal king,
the patron and champion of justice, were always present with the judge
deciding cases, he would stir the judge to caution and indeed strengthen him
when he judged lawfully. How much more ought this to occur when the King of
kings not merely sits beside the seat of judgment, but sits upon it and stands
all around it, filling everything above, below, and on every side? Therefore,
the disorder of earthly affairs is so far from making us suppose that
everything proceeds recklessly at men's pleasure that it is the surest sign
to us that divine judgment will be executed in its own time. 2 Thessalonians
2.

The second part, concerning the judgment of the flesh, follows. The same man
sets forth this opinion who set forth the preceding one in verse 17, but there
he spoke under the Spirit's guidance, acting as God's prophet and thus
sustaining God's own person. Here, however, he acts as God's prophet and
writes in the Holy Spirit's historical manner, but in such a way that by a
certain personification he graphically depicts Epicureans and atheists. This
refutes those who either reject this book or, while acknowledging it, wish to
construct Epicureanism from it.

The argument is connected in this way: if man is a beast to man, and men
destroy one another almost as beasts do, then there is no difference between
man and beast to prevent them from being equals in death just as they are in
their condition of life. But the former is true, and therefore so is the
latter. The assumption is shown to be true both from common experience and
from the efficient cause, God, who taught Solomon this, as Solomon himself
testifies. From this comes the saying celebrated as a proverb among all men:
“Man is a wolf to man.” Let it be refuted by its contrary proverb: “Man is a
god to man.” Here it must be noted that those who crush and oppress others are
held to be nothing other than beasts in human form. Because they regard other
men inferior to themselves as cattle, Scripture calls them hunters and
fishers of men; this is said by name of Nimrod and Nebuchadnezzar. What is
more, are they not wilder and more savage than the beasts themselves? For wild
animals may be found which do not eat beasts of their own kind. Genesis 9.
Moreover, in Micah 3 they are compared first to butchers and then to cooks.

Atheists infer from this similarity of conduct seen in beasts and men that
they have the same end and the same duration. Their eager embrace of this
error follows not so much from the nature of the things themselves as from
the causes and the inclination of their minds. For like beasts, they are
helplessly carried away to satisfy the lust of their desire and appetite; and
because they wish the sense of divine judgment to be extinguished and buried
in their minds, they seize and pursue every most trifling little argument by
which they may drive from their hearts every belief in the soul's immortality.
Thus, thoroughly steeped in this foul error, they may give themselves
fearlessly to every most shameful pleasure. It follows that theirs is a
dreadful and monstrous crime: by their savagery and brutality they induce in
mortal minds the opinion that men and beasts have the same end. Especially
because of such stumbling blocks, woe to the world.

Next, it is a childish sophism to proceed from what is true in a qualified
sense to what is true absolutely, and from a certain agreement and similarity
to conclude an agreement in every respect. Furthermore, since they see that
among mortals—and indeed among those dwelling in darkness and ignorance of
the true God—there have been men in every age who sought the advantage not of
one or a few but of an entire race and nation, and who deservedly were called
fathers of their country, why, after acquiring their error from men's mutual
violence and slaughter, did they not lay it aside because of men's mutual
aid? Again, since they see that certain men's monstrous wickedness surpasses
the ferocity or brute lusts of beasts, then by the very turn of reasoning by
which they gathered a like end from likeness of conduct, they ought to have
concluded that something graver than the beasts' lot is set aside and reserved

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA169 | printed pp. 169–170 | begins before complete prodigiosam from divided pro- / digiosam; ends before complete oportebat from divided opor- at foot of p. 170]

for such men. To this may be added that men fly upon one another by will and
design, breaking through the bars and enclosures of reason with which beasts
are not endowed. A different recompense awaits them than awaits beasts, which
are carried recklessly and headlong without judgment or design. It is
therefore vain to say that man and beast have one and the same end because
they perform the same act.

Once they have attempted to confirm this opinion from the general proposition
that both have the same end, they explain it through its parts: that each has
the same death; that each has the same efficient cause of life, namely
breathing in, breathing, and breathing out; and likewise that each is made
from the material of dust and returns to it. Here they commit the same fallacy
as above, for it does not follow that because they breathe in, breathe, breathe
out, and so forth, they are therefore alike in every respect. Things which are
the same in some respect with regard to their material do not therefore have
the same duration. This is evident in the beasts themselves: although they are
all from dust, some nevertheless remain and live longer than others by the
power of their nature. Some live for a day and some for an entire age. What is
more, the very shape of their bodies establishes a great difference, man's
erected toward heaven and the beast's bent toward the earth. Again, it does
not follow that things which agree in some effects have the same origin or
destruction, since those effects arise from diverse causes. For the breathing
and other functions of beasts proceed from a faculty which adheres to their
bodies in such a way that it cannot be separated without the destruction of
its subject; whereas man's life, breathing, and other functions have their
origin and source in man's immortal soul.

There follows the atheists' prolepsis, by which they seize in advance and
anticipate what the orthodox man offers. Its tacit objection is based upon
things which differ: although man and beast breathe out their spirit at death,
the human spirit nevertheless ascends upward and the brute spirit is carried
downward into the earth. The answer is taken from the accompanying ignorance
of this fact: no one can comprehend it.

That claim is false. Even among nations and commonwealths alienated from God,
very many have been found who not only freely and steadfastly acknowledged
the immortality of the soul, but also defended it against the calumnies of
those monsters. It is indeed not perceived by the senses, but to pass over
Scripture's surest demonstrations, there are many things which indicate belief
in the soul's immortality clearly enough: care for burial; solicitude for the
propagation of children, in whom the dead may live; and statues and monuments
by which men strive to rescue themselves from oblivion. And although the wind
is not discerned by the eyes, it is madness to deny from that fact that the
wind exists, since its effects prove it to exist and possess power.

From this exceedingly foul error they connect another: that man's highest good
is to give himself to pleasures, earnestly pursue everything which delights
the mind and body together, and deny nothing to his mind, eyes, ears, and
other senses. He then confirms this with other reasons: because this is man's
portion, which alone he may obtain in this life; and then from another
circumstance, because no one ever returned alive from the dead to bring word
of what goes on there. As to the first, even if their invention that the soul
perishes with the body were true, it still would not follow that all the reins
of honorable conduct should therefore be loosened. For the Sadducees, who
utterly denied the soul's immortality, nevertheless devoted themselves to
honorable conduct for this reason alone: that they might pass this life
tranquilly and comfortably. Therefore, even if this were man's portion, he
ought nevertheless to cultivate piety and uprightness in order to enjoy it.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA171 | printed pp. 171–172 | begins before complete oportebat from divided opor- / tebat; ends on p. 171 before Vers. 23]

As to the other claim, that no one returns from the lower regions to report
what goes on there, it is frivolous and works as much against them as for
them. For if no information about this affair is brought to us, why do they
incline toward the opinion that men have perished rather than that they
survive after this life? Moreover, the ready and joyful departure of the godly
from this life, and the utterly unwilling and sorrowful departure of the
wicked, speak openly that punishment awaits the latter and reward the former
after this life. For no one willingly puts off this body unless he is to be
clothed. 2 Corinthians 5.
