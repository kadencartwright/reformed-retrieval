THIRD DIGRESSION

SECTION NINE

The arguments laid down at the beginning are answered.

The first argument was from the authority of Augustine, *City of God* 12.20, where he says of God:
“If he withdraws from things his fabricative power, so to speak, they will cease to be just as,
before they were made, they were not. But by ‘before’ I mean in eternity, not in time.” Augustine
therefore holds that before things existed in their own time, they not only did not exist in an
earlier time but did not exist in eternity either.

NAVARRETE.

Response. Augustine intended to explain what he understood by the temporal adverb “before.” Since
it of itself denotes a certain succession of time, St. Augustine appeared to intimate that those
things which formerly did not exist had not existed in time past. Therefore, to remove this
ambiguity, he added that no time preceded, but that eternity existed before.

Moreover, the clearest understanding of Augustine's words is established by the fact that he was
not speaking of eternity insofar as it encompasses all time, but insofar as, according to our
consideration, it preceded the time in which things began to exist. For although eternity, as was
said, is formally one and indivisible, it is virtually manifold. It can be considered insofar as it
corresponds to one part of time and is distinguished from that which corresponds to another.
St. Augustine therefore says that things did not exist, before the time in which they were created,
in the eternity which was understood to precede that same time.

RESPONSE.

But the first interpretation is excessively forced. Augustine openly says not only that eternity
existed before, but that those things did not exist in eternity. Without doubt he would not have
said this if he had acknowledged any real existence of those same things in eternity, and that from
eternity. Nor should anyone find it surprising that he did not speak of eternity insofar as it
encompasses all time. It is not credible that Augustine acknowledged the kind of encompassing of
time which you attribute to eternity. Nor does he speak at all of eternity as corresponding to a
part of time. First, there would have been no need for a distinction of this kind. For if the power
of God by which he produces things is withdrawn, they will immediately cease to be, just as they
formerly did not exist—namely, did not exist in time. Time preceded the production of very many
things, so that the language proceeds exactly according to precedence of time properly so called.
Second, he speaks of the creation of the whole world, and teaches that through the withdrawal of
divine power it will return to the same state in which it existed before all time, not before a part
of time. Without doubt he presupposes that no real existence of things preceded all time.

Secondly, it is argued from Scotus's reasoning. God's immensity is not from eternity present to all
the places which will exist in time, nor are places filled from eternity by God's immensity.
Therefore future things are not contained in eternity itself from eternity either.

NAVARRETE.

Some respond to the second argument by establishing a difference between eternity in relation to
time and immensity in relation to place: eternity is an inadequate measure of time and thus is
equivalent to infinite time, if such were granted, and contains it. Immensity, however, does not
possess the character of a measure of places, and therefore God does not coexist with nonexistent
places. Navarrete does not approve this manner of responding but rather refutes it. “This solution,”
he says, “does not entirely satisfy. For God's immensity can be taken negatively, so that it is the
same as having no measure; and it can be taken positively insofar as it fills and contains all
things. But in the present case,” he says, “it is taken not in the first but in the second manner.
Thus it appears that, just as time which contains successive things and measures them is contained
in eternity as in an inadequate measure, so the place which contains and measures a located thing
is contained in God's immensity as in an inadequate measure.” Navarrete therefore removes this
response and substitutes another as follows.

Nevertheless, the argument is answered from the difference between the filling by which God's
immensity fills every place and the measuring by which eternity measures all time. The former
denotes some mutation on the side of the place filled by God's immensity. Therefore it is not
conceded absolutely that God fills every place from eternity, just as, because creation denotes a
mutation agreeing with creatures in time, it is not conceded that God creates from eternity,
although the act of creating, as it stands on God's side, is eternal. But eternity, insofar as it
actually measures all time and the successive things existing in time, neither posits nor denotes
any mutation in them. Hence in the proposition “successive things exist together in eternity,”
nothing pertaining intrinsically to time as a measure is denoted, since no mutation subject to time
is denoted. In the other proposition, however, “God's immensity fills every place which will exist
in time,” there is intrinsically denoted that mutation of the place which is filled, and thus the
measure of time intervenes according to such a mutation. It is signified that God fills a place in
relation to that mutation. Thus the first proposition is conceded and the second denied.

[Book I, printed p. 77; BSB canvas 00103]

RESPONSE.

But the difference Navarrete presents is merely invented. No mutation occurs on the side of a place
when it is filled by God's immensity, just as, when an angel occupies some space, that space is not
in any respect mutated by the angel's occupation. Moreover, the simple coexistence of God's
immensity with every permanent quantity, however spacious, is abundantly sufficient for the present
matter. God's immensity can be said in precisely the same way to encompass every permanent quantity
as eternity encompasses every successive quantity; yet it does not coexist with any spatial
magnitude before that spatial magnitude exists absolutely. Nor is it right that eternity should
coexist with any span of time before that span of time exists absolutely. Indeed, if we are moved by
Durandus's discussion, this filling of place by immensity is merely fictitious. God fills all things
with his effects, not with his immensity. But let us speak of an existing spatial magnitude. Through
immensity God indivisibly coexists with every space, though not everywhere, but with each thing in
its own place. In the same way GOD coexists with everything existing in time, though not always,
but with each thing in its own time. If he always coexists with each thing in eternity, by the same
reason he will also be said to coexist everywhere with some body, namely in his immensity.

Thirdly, if things exist really in eternity, it follows that they cannot be produced in time and in
their own measure, for the same thing, remaining in its being, would be produced twice.

NAVARRETE.

Although some authors concede that Antichrist is created in eternity, it does not appear to us that
this should be conceded, because creation implies the mutation just mentioned. When successive and
mutable things are formally transferred to existence in eternity, they are not transferred under a
successive or temporal aspect, but, as has been explained, under an indivisible and permanent
aspect. It follows that, although materially speaking things are mutated and flow in eternity—and
thus it can even be conceded that Antichrist is created in eternity—formally all these things exist
in eternity according to a certain permanence, and so the thing is produced once in time.

RESPONSE.

But if the things themselves exist from eternity and the act of creating is from eternity, as you
had said in response to the preceding argument, what prevents them from being said to have been
created from eternity, especially since real existence from eternity cannot be produced through
creation in time? Nothing real can be said to exist infinitely later than its cause. For no thing
exists from eternity by its own nature; therefore it is made to exist through some action of an
extrinsic agent. This is therefore either through production in time, which is later, or through a
creation which is also eternal. Nor would any mutation follow from this. Although temporal creation
proceeds from nonbeing to being, eternal creation would not do so; it would occur by emanation
rather than transmutation, as rays proceed from the sun.

You say that things “are transferred to existence in eternity.” But by whom are they transferred?
Either by God acting or by no one. Since this transfer is supposed to be eternal, it therefore
occurs through some operation of God, either an immanent or a transient one. No transient operation
can be imagined other than creation. But creation is infinitely later than this transfer; therefore
this transfer cannot be referred to creation as its cause. How much better it would have been to
confess candidly that this occurs through an immanent operation, namely through God's will, by which
possible things are transferred to willed being before God and consequently become knowable within
the class of future things. The being which a thing possesses before God in his volition is not real
but solely intentional being. Without doubt things possess being of this kind under an indivisible
and permanent aspect. Thus all those senseless distinctions of yours would vanish into nothing: the
distinctions about things which, materially speaking, are mutated and flow in eternity; about time
itself existing in eternity according to its successive being; and even about Antichrist being
created in eternity in this manner, while formally all these exist in eternity according to a
certain permanence, and the thing is therefore produced once in time. We easily comprehend how both
time and the things in time exist in eternity when they really exist: namely, through their
successive coexistence with eternity. But that before the world was created they really existed in
eternity from eternity is a monstrous opinion. It cannot be moderated by so tasteless a remedy as
saying that they exist in eternity according to a certain permanence, and that from eternity, just
as you formerly said that things before they exist are elevated to eternity through a certain
containment, not through any passive elevation. Perhaps these statements can satisfy those who wish
to feed on vanities and empty things.

The Fourth Argument. Whenever several things exist or coexist simultaneously with some third thing,
they must also exist or coexist with one another. But not all future things coexist with one
another; therefore neither do they really coexist with eternity.

NAVARRETE.

The fourth argument, which Vásquez regards as most difficult and insoluble, saying that he does not
see what Cajetan and the Thomists might answer, has a very easy solution, one which the elementary
logicians customarily apply to similar arguments which possess the same or a similar fallacy. They
explain the maxim “Whatever things are the same as a third are the same as one another,” and say
that it must be understood with respect to that aspect in which they are the same. Thus theologians
respond to the proposed argument that things which coexist with a third thing—namely, eternity—
coexist with one another as measured by eternity. Although Adam and Antichrist do not coexist with
one another absolutely, nevertheless, insofar as they are subjected to the measure of eternity,
they possess an indivisible coexistence. Therefore, if past and future things coexisted with eternity
according to what is proper to themselves and according to their relation to their proper measures,
they would also coexist with one another. But because they coexist with eternity under a particular
aspect, it does not follow that they coexist absolutely with one another.

[Book I, printed p. 78; BSB canvas 00104]

Similarly, although several parts of a human being are as it were the same as one rational soul
under the same indivisible information, it does not follow that they are the same as one another.

RESPONSE.

We too say that all things coexist with eternity, but successively, one after another, not
simultaneously. This is no wonder, since eternity neither had a beginning nor will ever have an end.
Yet it by no means follows from this that all things also coexist with one another. But your
opinion, Navarrete, is that every thing coexists with eternity at every instant of time. You maintain
that each thing always coexists with eternity. Hence, just as, if every body coexisted everywhere
with immensity, it would follow that all bodies also coexisted everywhere with one another, so, if
all things always coexist with eternity, it follows by precisely the same reasoning that all things
always coexist with one another.

Fifthly. That which is not yet anything in itself, and possesses neither its own being nor its own
duration—which is not distinguished from its being—cannot coexist with an alien measure and
duration. For coexisting with another appears to presuppose the coexisting thing's own existence as
its first and necessary foundation. But from eternity future things were nothing in themselves;
therefore they could not coexist even with eternity itself.

NAVARRETE.

Being in relation to the measure of eternity does not presuppose being in relation to one's own
measure. On the contrary, being in relation to one's own measure is derived from that higher measure
of eternity. It is prior that all things correspond to the higher measure than to their own
immediate measure. For they do not ascend to the measure of eternity by virtue of their own measure;
rather, what eternity is and what its manner of containing is must be understood as prior in nature
to the existence of things in it.

We certainly concede that, in the class of material cause, the being of the things which eternity
measures is presupposed and therefore must exist together with the said measure. But the ground of
existing in eternity is considered not according to such being, but rather according to the
eminence of the measure. This is the principal foundation requiring the greatest attention in this
matter, and was explained above.

By this one answers the confirmation: although eternity is an extrinsic measure of created things,
it is nevertheless an infinite measure, encompasses all things created and to be created, and, on
the other hand, contains them in its own manner—namely, by an indivisible measurement which is
wholly simultaneous. Thus it should not be conceded that it measures what does not yet exist;
rather, it measures that which is present to it by a presence drawn from that infinite scope of
eternity.

RESPONSE.

Navarrete cannot bear the light of this argument and therefore changes its terms, which a candid
disputant ought not to have done. Had he changed the terms for the better it would have been
tolerable, but he changes them for the worse, removing plainer and better-known terms and
substituting more obscure and unknown ones. The argument was this: what does not exist in itself
cannot be said to coexist with another. Navarrete responds: “Being in relation to the measure of
eternity does not presuppose being in relation to one's own measure.” By these words he signifies,
though obscurely, that something can coexist with another although it does not exist. The
explanation of the terms makes it clear that this is utterly false. For “to coexist with another”
is a phrase with a complex meaning: it signifies that each of the two proposed things exists and
also that their existences are simultaneous. Durandus used this same argument long ago. Navarrete
seems to mean that something can coexist with eternity—namely, in eternity—although it does not yet
exist in time. But the argument can be framed so as to leave no room for this escape: whatever
coexists with eternity itself exists; but according to your opinion future things coexist with
eternity; therefore they exist. I do not say, therefore, that they exist in time, but simply that
they exist. Yet your party does not admit this consequence because the conclusion concerns
existence stated absolutely. You will not acknowledge that future things exist absolutely.
Therefore, by the same reasoning, you must also deny that future things coexist with eternity unless
the claim is made with the restriction “in eternity.” This is the only way of answering the proposed
argument. But it is worthwhile to weigh separately what you say.

You say that being in one's own measure is derived from that higher measure of eternity. I note
that you do not say here that being in one's own measure is derived from being in the measure of
eternity, although this is what you ought to have said. For you undertake to show that the contrary
of the proposed statement is true; therefore it was by no means proper to change the principal terms
of the comparison. Nor could this have been said without contradicting your opinion. According to
your opinion, things are in eternity because they are produced in time rather than being in time
because they are produced in eternity. But because things are indeed produced by God in time, it
might seem plausible to affirm that things, while they are produced in time, are derived from
eternity. Yet even this is stated most absurdly. Things are not made by God as he operates according
to his eternity, but are produced by God as he operates according to the counsel of his will. Why
in the production of things should you take account of God's eternity rather than his immensity,
especially since God's immensity is reckoned not according to quantity of bulk but rather according
to quantity of perfection? Quantity of perfection appears to be more suited to play a part in the
production of things than eternity. Dictating according to the doctrine you delivered above—which
we have already weighed and found to be as light as chaff—you add: “It is prior that things
correspond to the higher measure than to their own immediate measure.” This is your phraseology. In
more familiar language, you might say: it is prior that things exist in eternity than that they
exist in time; that is, it is prior that things exist in a qualified sense and not absolutely than
that they exist absolutely. I do not deny this to be true, provided it is properly explained. For
things exist before God in known being prior to existing absolutely; further, they exist before God
in willed being prior to existing in known being. But things existing in this manner are not so much
creatures as the creative essence. Lest you seem to rave without reason, you append a reason in
these words: “For they do not ascend to the measure of eternity by virtue of their own measure.”
Certainly they do not ascend, for perhaps this ascent is purely chimerical and born in the brains of
certain men raving beyond all expectation. Among these

[Book I, printed p. 79; BSB canvas 00105]

is Navarrete, who taught above with great magisterial gravity that things are elevated to the
measure of eternity, though not by any passive and intrinsically received elevation but through a
certain containment. Yet where did this containment come from, since the things themselves were by
their own nature merely possible? It must come from elsewhere, and since it existed from eternity,
it must come from God. Did it come from God creating from eternity? You do not dare affirm this, but
say instead that he produces in time. It therefore seems more fitting that being in eternity should
be derived from being in time than that being in time should be derived from being in eternity. How
much better it would have been to profess that the existence of things in eternity—insofar as they
pass from the condition of possible things into that of future things—is effected by God's decree
and will. By this they become future and so exist from eternity before God, but solely in willed and
known being, that is, according to their intentional and not their real being. Certainly we must
understand what eternity is before we speak of existence in eternity—but what is that to the point?
We need not be anxious about the manner of this containment. For to exist in eternity according to
real being is nothing other than to coexist with it. This can be sufficiently understood even if we
know nothing more about the nature of eternity than that it has neither beginning nor end, from
which it necessarily follows that whatever exists also coexists with it.

You nevertheless allow that, in the class of material cause, the being of things which is measured
by eternity is presupposed; but you do not allow the ground of existing in eternity to be considered
according to such being. Rather, you wish it to be considered according to the eminence of the
measure. You say that this is the principal foundation requiring the greatest attention here and
that it was explained above—namely, in Section Seven, in the response to the second argument—most
marvellously indeed. I do not believe that even the Devil himself, when, as the story goes, he gave
his lectures through a lattice before certain auditors at the Academy of Toledo, ever employed more
sublime phraseology. I see no reason why Navarrete himself should not deign to consult some such
Doctor to extract the hidden meanings of these expressions. Without doubt they must be exceedingly
momentous matters which scarcely one person in a thousand properly understands. We acknowledge that
eternity is an infinite measure and encompasses all created things, insofar as it existed before
them and will exist after them. But it no more contains every quantity of time and duration than
God's immensity contains every quantity of magnitude. Both are indivisible. It no more follows from
the infinite and indivisible condition of eternity that temporal things always exist in eternity
than it follows from the infinite and indivisible condition of immensity that every quantity of
magnitude is everywhere in immensity.
