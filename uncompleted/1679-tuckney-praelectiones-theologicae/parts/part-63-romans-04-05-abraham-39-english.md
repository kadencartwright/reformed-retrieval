Putting little confidence in this light weaponry, Castellio therefore
abandons it. He suspects that we will say what we have already said: the old
man, that is, sin, is indeed killed by Christ, but only in death itself. He
raises three objections against this answer.

1. If Christ accomplishes this at death, he does so either through faith or
through death itself; but Castellio thinks he has already proved that it is
through neither.

We have already examined and rejected both of these proofs, and since this
argument is one with them, I now dismiss it. The work is accomplished by
employing both: death as the way and means, faith as the operative
instrument. Nothing here prevents salvation from being ascribed to faith
in its own manner and order, which Castellio finds exceedingly difficult to
bear.

2. He objects: “If sin is abolished through death, we attach this enemy to
Christ as an ally. Though Christ came to abolish death, he either cannot or
will not abolish sin except with death's assistance.”

But why does he so slanderously introduce Christ's power, as though any of
us had ever said or thought that Christ could not abolish sin completely
before death? We certainly believe not only that he can do this, but that he
actually did it in Enoch and Elijah, who never died but were delivered from
every sin in their translation.

We are therefore not disputing Christ's power, but speaking of his will.
We say his will is such that, setting aside those examples of Enoch and
Elijah, no one is ordinarily delivered from the remnants of sin before
death, but only through death's intervention.

Nor, if we look to Christ, do we say with Castellio that this is
accomplished with death's assistance—the form of words he favors so as to
make us odious. Thus we do not attach death, which Christ came to abolish,
to him in this work as a hostile ally providing assistance. Rather, in our
position we do not proclaim that death supplies aid to Christ, but that
Christ has gained a glorious

[1679 printed p. 287 | IA leaf 295]

victory over death. He has placed this last of our enemies beneath the yoke
so completely that he has turned it from our enemy into our friend. He has
made it work together for our good by delivering us from sin, a far more
dangerous enemy. Conversely, death and sin, formerly such good friends when
sin gave birth to death, have now become the bitterest enemies, so that
death kills the sin from which it took its origin, just as borers destroy
the tree in which they were born.
