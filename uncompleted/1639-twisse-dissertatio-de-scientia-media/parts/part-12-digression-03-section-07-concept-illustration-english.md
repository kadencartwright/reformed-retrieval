“Just as,” he says, “when a verb signifying an inward act of the soul is placed in a proposition,
it calls for its proper concept—as when I say, ‘I know man,’ the thing’s proper character as it
exists in reality is not denoted by the mode of knowing, for the sense is, ‘I know man under the
common character of man,’ although that common character is not found in reality—so future things
in time, which at this instant do not exist in themselves and cannot, so far as depends on
themselves, terminate God’s intuitive knowledge, nevertheless terminate it in fact by virtue of the
measure of intuitive knowledge itself, but insofar as they are within the measure of such
knowledge.” Alas, how greatly the keenness of our mind grows dim before these illustrations of
Navarrete!
