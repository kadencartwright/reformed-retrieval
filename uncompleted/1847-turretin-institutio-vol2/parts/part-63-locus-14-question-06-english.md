QUESTION VI.

*Was Christ taken up into heaven before beginning his public ministry, so that
he might be taught there by the Father? We deny; against the Socinians.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. The opinion of the Socinians, as Smalcius explains it in *The Divinity of
Christ*, chapter 4; Ostorodt in the *Institutes*, chapter 16; and the Racovian
Catechism on Christ’s prophetic office, is that Christ, as a mere man, was at
first untrained and ignorant of the doctrine which he had to promulgate by his
ministry for human salvation. Therefore, when he was already thirty years old
and had been baptized by John, before he began his office publicly, he was
taken bodily up into heaven, was taught there most perfectly about God’s will
by God himself, and, being thus sent back to earth, began to relate the same
doctrine fully and sufficiently to human beings. Although they do not dare to
define precisely the time of that rapture, they nevertheless think it can best
be referred to the period Christ spent fasting in the wilderness for forty
days and nights. Their aim is to evade the passages of Scripture by which we
prove that Christ existed before he was born of the Virgin, because he is said
to have gone out from the Father and descended from heaven.

II. The orthodox reject this rapture of Christ into heaven as entirely
fictitious and nowhere taught in Scripture. During the days of his flesh, they
acknowledge no descent from heaven other than the metaphorical one
accomplished through his incarnation.

[Marginal heading: The orthodox position is proved.]

III. The reasons are: First, *the silence of Scripture*, which makes no mention
of this rapture. It indeed mentions one ascension into heaven and entrance
into the sanctuary after he rose, Hebrews 9:12, but neither the slightest
trace nor footprint of another occurs. For it will be shown afterward that the
passages drawn here pertain elsewhere. But what cause could there have been
for that silence if it really occurred, since this would have contributed not
a little toward commending Christ’s doctrine? Why does Scripture mention
rather Christ’s being led away by the tempter to a very high mountain, but not
his rapture into heaven by God himself? Nor should it be said here that the
evangelists did not mention this rapture because it occurred secretly rather
than openly, while they record the things which they saw or heard. For on that
reason Christ’s resurrection also should have been passed over in silence,
since it occurred with no one present except the guards; nor could Matthew
have related the transfiguration, of which he himself was not an eyewitness
(αὐτόπτης). Therefore, they had to relate not simply the things which they
themselves had seen and heard, but the things revealed to them by the Holy
Spirit concerning Christ’s deeds which contributed to the instruction of the
faithful.

IV. Second, Christ did not need this rapture for knowledge of the mysteries.
He did not need it according to the divine nature, because he was the Father’s
Word (Λόγος), John 1:1, who was in his bosom and therefore had all his secrets
clearly known. Nor did he need it according to the human nature, because
according to it he received the Spirit without measure,

John 3:34, by whom he was sufficiently instructed for his office; and the
treasures of wisdom and understanding were hidden in him, Colossians 2:3. This
is confirmed by the fact that, while still a twelve-year-old boy, he gave
wonderful proofs of his wisdom by teaching in the temple and disputing with
the Pharisees, Luke 2:47. It is also confirmed by the fact that at his baptism,
before that fictitious rapture, the Father testified from heaven that he was
well pleased in him. But how could he be well pleased in one who did not yet
have his will clearly known? Third, if Christ had ascended into heaven after
his baptism, Scripture ought to place his ascent before his descent, whereas
it puts his descent before his ascent, Ephesians 4:9.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

V. It is one thing for Christ to have ascended into heaven *properly and
locally* with respect to his humanity, and another to have ascended
*metaphorically and mystically* through sublime knowledge of the heavenly
mysteries. Christ is not said to have ascended into heaven in the former sense
in John 3:13: “No one has ascended into heaven except the one who descended
from heaven, the Son of Man who is in heaven,” because the same one who
ascends is said to have descended, and thus could not be said still to be in
heaven. Rather, it concerns a mystical ascent, as one who penetrates heaven’s
secrets is said to ascend into heaven, Proverbs 30:4, because divine counsels
are as distant from human counsels as heaven is from earth, Isaiah 55:9. What
is near us, readily available and plain, is said not to be in heaven, such that
one must ascend there, Deuteronomy 30:12 and Romans 10:6. Thus the meaning will
be that no one besides him knows heavenly things and is privy to the divine
counsel. The train of the discourse teaches that these words must be referred
to this, because in the preceding verse he had treated the revelation of
mysteries: *If I have told you earthly things and you do not believe, how will
you believe if I tell you heavenly things?* And to give the reason why he can
both teach heavenly things by his own right and no one can know them unless he
teaches them, he adds, *No one has ascended into heaven*, and so forth.
Moreover, when the Son of Man is said to be in heaven, this must be understood
of the person in the concrete, not the human nature in the abstract.

VI. When Christ is said to have descended from heaven and gone out from the
Father, John 6:38 and 16:28, this concerns his metaphorical descent through
manifestation in the flesh—not so that he might be where he was not before,
but so that he might work there. But a local descent according to his humanity
is not presupposed, nor is Christ ever said to have returned from heaven, but
only to have come. Nor, when the Son of Man is said after the resurrection to
ascend where he was before, John 6:62, should this be understood according to
the humanity, but according to the divinity, where the Word (ὁ λόγος) was
before, when he was with God (πρὸς τὸν θεὸν), John 1:1.

VII. Although Christ speaks the things which he was taught by the Father, John
8:38, because he speaks what he heard, John 3:32, and does nothing which he has
not seen the Father doing, John 5:19—in order to intimate that the Son has
thoroughly known all the Father’s works—it does not follow that he was taken
up into heaven so that he might hear or see such things. He had this knowledge
from his intimate communion with the Father, on account of which he is said to
be in the Father’s bosom, John 1:18; and because he is in his bosom, he reveals
him to us.

VIII. Although Christ was not taken up into heaven, he must not on that account
be regarded as inferior to Moses and Paul, who were taken up so that they
might be instructed by God. 1. Even if they had been taken there, Christ was
taught by God in a far more excellent manner, by virtue of the hypostatic
union. 2. The case of servants differs from that of the Lord. They needed
singular and extraordinary instruction to know the things of which they were
ignorant. But from the moment of conception Christ was anointed with the Holy
Spirit and had these things most fully known. 3. Moses is not said to have
been taken up into heaven, but onto the mountain. Paul indeed is said to have
been taken up into the third heaven, but whether he was taken in spirit or in
body cannot be said, since Paul himself admits that he did not know this.
