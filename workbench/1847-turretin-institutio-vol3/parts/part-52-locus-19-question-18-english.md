QUESTION XVIII.

ON THE TRUTH AND RITES OF ROMAN BAPTISM.

Is the true doctrine of baptism retained in the Roman Church? A distinction.

I. The truth of the doctrine concerning baptism is considered either with
respect to its essence or with respect to its accidents, that is, the rites
and ceremonies employed in it. In the former sense, we acknowledge that by
God’s singular providence the true doctrine concerning baptism remains in the
Roman Church, because the matter of true baptism—namely, water—is retained in
it, as is the formula prescribed by Christ, according to which baptism is
administered in the name of the Trinity. For this reason, baptism administered
in that church is considered valid and is not repeated. But in the latter
sense, we deny it.

[Marginalia: Roman baptism has been corrupted in various ways.]

II. The reasons are as follows. (1) It fosters grave errors that corrupt the
true doctrine of baptism by substituting the opinion of the work performed and
of physical efficiency for moral and mystical efficacy; also by extending the
power of baptism too far, as if it entirely removed sin, and restricting it
too narrowly to sins committed before baptism, although the covenant sealed
by baptism is perpetual. (2) In practice it sins in more than one way: both
through the use of a foreign language and through various rites, some
superstitious and others useless and impious, which it introduced apart from
Christ’s institution and makes part of the sacrament. By these the original
simplicity of Christ’s institution and the practice of the apostles are
disdainfully rejected, and the essential rites of baptism are obscured to an
astonishing degree. These are the matters disputed between us and the
adversaries.

[Marginalia: The opinion of the papists concerning the rites of baptism.]

III. Bellarmine recounts several rites and ceremonies, some preceding, some
accompanying, and some following baptism. Six preceding rites are enumerated:
1. The sign of the cross on the forehead and breast. 2. Exorcism. 3.
Exsufflation. 4. The touching of the nostrils and ears with saliva, accompanied
by *Ephpheta*. 5. The tasting of salt. 6. Anointing with oil on the breast and
shoulders. There is one accompanying rite, namely, the consecration of the
baptismal water. There are three following rites: 1. Anointing with chrism on
the crown of the head. 2. A lighted candle given to the neophyte to carry. 3.
The putting on of a white garment. We pass over many other things that are
also enumerated by the adversaries and presented as rites of baptism, but
which are either preparations for the baptism of adults rather than rites—
such as declaration of one’s name, scrutiny, profession of faith, renunciation
of Satan and his works, bestowal of a name, and similar things—or have now
fallen out of use, such as the first tasting of milk and honey, the kiss, the
washing of feet, and the like, because these are not disputed between us. The
question concerns only the ten mentioned above, which we judge the papists
wrongly employ as foreign to Christ’s institution.

[Marginalia: It is proved that such rites must not be admitted. 1. From the silence of Scripture.]

IV. First, from the silence of Scripture, because neither trace nor vestige of
any of these occurs in Scripture, whether in John’s baptism, Christ’s
institution, or the practice of the apostles. We indeed see water employed in
both cases, but no other substance—not salt, oil, or balsam, nor anything of
the other elements that under Moses were

[1847 vol. III printed p. 347 | IA leaf 381 | PDF p. 381]

customary in the sacred rites of that nation. Christ indeed commands people to
baptize, but neither Christ commanded nor do we ever read that the apostles
employed immersion in oil and balsam, touching the ears and nostrils with
saliva, offering salt to be tasted, or doing any of the things added beyond
this. Therefore, since all these things are outside Christ’s institution, they
cannot and must not be received, whatever veneer of antiquity may commend that
custom. For sacred things must be tested by the standard of truth, not of
custom, and attention must be paid not to what anyone before us did, but to
what Christ, who is before all, himself did first and commanded others to do.

Nor can it be said that Christ included these things in the word *baptizing*,
as it is customary among the papists to signify all those things under the
name of baptism. For the case of our own time, in which the custom of
administering baptism with such rites has long prevailed among the
adversaries, is far different from that of the time when Christ lived. At that
time no Jew, when speaking of a sacred rite, could say or understand
*baptizein* (βαπτίζειν) otherwise than of the act of dipping, immersion, or
pouring. Oil, balsam, saliva, and the other things now employed were all
unannounced and unheard of until that day. Since this is so, it is absurd to
suppose that Christ signified, or that the apostles understood, anything other
than dipping when they received from Christ the command to baptize. Nor is it
right to suspect that Christ, especially in the most weighty matter of all
Christianity, wished to signify by those words things that neither the
apostles whom he addressed nor any mortal could understand from anything he
said.

[Marginalia: 2. From the simplicity of Christianity.]

V. Second, from the simplicity of Christianity. Since the worship of the
Christian church is spiritual and, as Paul calls it, “rational worship”
(*logikē latreia*, λογικὴ λατρεία; Rom. 12:1), and since it differs from Judaism
especially in that the latter was wholly and extensively occupied with signs
and figures, everyone judges it absurd that the very entrance of the church
should be obstructed and overshadowed by the forms and representations of so
many corporeal and elemental things, so that in the Mosaic discipline one
could scarcely find a rite enveloped in more figures. To this is added the
multiplicity of things so disparate from one another, which seems not a little
foreign to the character of the gospel. The gospel cut away the superabundant
rites under Moses and reduced piety to a few things, slight indeed in bulk but
immense in reality and power. As Augustine says in epistle 118, “He bound
together the fellowship of the new people by sacraments very few in number,
very easy in observance, and most excellent in significance, such as baptism
consecrated in the name of the Trinity, and so forth.” It is scarcely possible
for that mass of ceremonies heaped upon baptism to have a place among these,
since it makes the use of baptism laborious and difficult.

[Marginalia: 3. From the silence of the ancients.]

VI. Third, from the silence of the most ancient writers, because in the true
and undisputed books of purer antiquity nothing occurs concerning those things
with which the Romans anoint their neophytes before and after baptism, or
concerning the other ceremonies of this kind that they customarily employ in
administering this sacrament. Hence there is no reason to suspect that these
things were already in use in those earliest times. Indeed, a firm argument
that nothing of the kind had yet prevailed is drawn from this, because there
can be no doubt that the fathers would have made some mention of it, just as
in following centuries they did not pass over in silence the things gradually
added to the legitimate and apostolic rite of baptism.

This is evident especially from a notable passage of Justin Martyr in his
second *Apology*, which he wrote in the year 150 on behalf of the Christian
religion. Lest it be thought that any of our mysteries were craftily
concealed, he carefully relates the whole solemn rite of baptism and the
Eucharist. “We will now explain,” he says, “the manner in which, having been
renewed through Christ, we also dedicated ourselves to God, lest, if we pass
over this, we should seem to act maliciously and disingenuously in this
discourse. All who have been persuaded and believe the things taught by us to
be true, and who have undertaken that they can live in this way, are taught to
pray and fast, and to ask God for the remission of their former

[1847 vol. III printed p. 348 | IA leaf 382 | PDF p. 382]

sins, while we pray and fast with them. Then they are brought by us to a place
where there is water, and they are regenerated in the same manner of
regeneration in which we ourselves were regenerated. For they are then washed
in water in the name of the Parent and Lord God of all things, of our Savior
Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit.” And in the rest that he adds more fully
in the same place, he mentions no other rite of baptism besides the one
washing in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit expressly
commanded by Christ. He says not a word about oil, saliva, chrism, or the other
things now employed by the Romans. Hence it is certainly concluded that none
of these things had yet prevailed in the church at that time.

[Marginalia: 4. From the testimony of the adversaries.]

VII. Fourth, because not a few of the adversaries have acknowledged the
novelty of these rites, including Erasmus in his *Letter to Jodocus Gaverus*,
Rigallius on Cyprian, and others. Hence Catherine de’ Medici, queen of France,
mother of Charles IX, and regent of the kingdom at that time, in a letter sent
to Pius IV in 1561, requested among other points of reformation “that in
baptism the exorcisms and certain forms of prayers, which are outside its
institution, be omitted, and that it suffice for water and the word to be
employed according to God’s prescription; that the injection of spittle into
an infant’s mouth is seen not only as unnecessary but also as not free from
danger.” Thus reports the illustrious Thuanus; the expurgatory indexes order
this letter to be deleted.

VIII. The celebrated Dallæus relates fully and accurately in book 1, chapters
10, 11, 12, 13, and 14 of *On the Worship of the Latins* at what time or in
what century such rites and ceremonies were gradually introduced. Consult
him.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

IX. Although Scripture sometimes mentions oil and anointing (2 Cor. 1:21 and
1 John 2:20), exsufflation (John 20:22), white garments (Rev. 3:4), and candles
(Matt. 25:1–3), where the virgins are introduced with candles, it does not
follow from this that the rites added by the papists in baptism are confirmed.
For these things are mentioned not as pertaining to baptism or its celebration
but for another reason, and generally in relation to the performance of
miracles, whose rationale is entirely foreign to the sacrament. The anointing
discussed in 2 Corinthians 1:21 and 1 John 2:20 is not external and material
but mystical and spiritual, concerning the gifts of the Holy Spirit with which
believers are adorned. If Christ used spittle to heal a blind man miraculously,
if he promises white garments to the victor, if he commands us to have salt in
ourselves—that is, to conduct ourselves prudently toward everyone—and if the
virgins are introduced with candles to designate their vigilance and faith,
the use of chrism, saliva, spittle, candles, and white garments in baptism
cannot on that account be called necessary.

X. Just as exorcism was rightly employed upon demoniacs while the gift of
miracles flourished, so it is now employed, not without insult, upon the
infants of those in the covenant, as if they were slaves of the devil, though
Christ reckons them in his covenant and pursues them with singular kindness
(Matt. 19:14). And since the name *exorcists* was given not to all who had
power over unclean spirits but to those who drove them out of possessed
bodies, even if we were to grant that the power of exorcising still remains
with the church, it would not follow that it ought to be exercised in baptism,
as though every infant to be baptized were bodily possessed by a demon. Hence
Chemnitz and, after him, Gherardus acknowledge that “perhaps it would not be
useless to abolish this indifferent ceremony for reasons not to be despised”
(*On Baptism*, p. 1172).

XI. So far are the rites added by the papists from shedding light on the
things contained in the sacrament itself and placing them before our eyes in
this way so that they may be administered with greater reverence and sanctity,
that, on the contrary, we believe the sacrament is obscured and corrupted.
This is altogether the force and rationale of signs: the simpler they are and
the freer from every foreign apparatus, the more suited they are

[1847 vol. III printed p. 349 | IA leaf 383 | PDF p. 383]

to signify; and the things added apart from the institution, however great an
appearance they may seem to possess, are less suited to signify.

XII. If we add certain things in the administration of baptism for which
neither a command nor an example is given, they are not parts of the sacrament,
nor is any mystery attached to them, or a signification of those effects that
belong to the water of baptism. Rather, they are circumstances of orderly
celebration left to ecclesiastical prudence and liberty, which may be
instituted or changed as occasion requires in order to preserve decorum. Such
are bringing an infant into the church on the Lord’s Day, or another day on
which a sermon is held; the sponsors’ question and response concerning the
instruction of the infant; the bestowal of a name; and similar things. But the
case of the papal rites is far different. They either have something impious
or superstitious mixed into them, are imposed under the claim of necessity and
worship, and have some power and efficacy outside God’s Word ascribed to them;
or else they contain idle games and do not serve edification, but transform
this holy action into a theatrical and histrionic spectacle.

XIII. The papists’ doctrine concerning baptism can be called true and false
“in one respect and in another” (*kat’ allo kai allo*, κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ ἄλλο).
It is true with respect to the essence of the institution, the visible
element, and the word of institution. It is false with respect to accidental
opinions and rites. Nor, because baptism is considered true, is the truth of
the papal religion thereby rightly established, any more than the doctrine of
the Pharisees and Sadducees was true because they legitimately administered
the external rite of circumcision. Doctrines of faith do not always correspond
to correctness in external administration. For through the fault of those
administering, it can accidentally happen that one who legitimately
administers the external sacrament perverts the sacrament itself with corrupt
opinions and faith with destructive errors.

XIV. Hence we rightly conclude that papal baptism must not be repeated. (1)
Because the essence of baptism still remains intact in it. (2) Because the
power and efficacy of baptism depend not upon an erring or heretical minister
but upon Christ (Matt. 3:11; 1 Cor. 3:5). (3) Because remnants of the church
still exist in the papacy (from Rev. 18:4), and God has not yet fully
repudiated that church; but baptism is proper to the church and is administered
in its stead, although through the most corrupt ministers.

XV. Although we do not judge that this baptism, once performed, must be
repeated, nevertheless we do not think that infant baptism can be requested or
received from papal priests without sin. (1) Because the papists’ errors
concerning the absolute necessity of baptism and its efficacy from the work
performed, as well as the superstitious rites added by men with which the
purity of this sacrament is foully defiled, seem thereby to be approved. (2)
Because those who do this profess that they wish to cultivate communion with a
heretical and idolatrous church, since the end of baptism is none other than to
engraft the baptized into the church in which they receive baptism. (3)
Because the antichristian ministry is thereby approved.

XVI. I know that Melanchthon and Bucer think that in a certain case such a
baptism can be received without sin, provided certain conditions are set:
namely, if the parents live in a place where an orthodox minister cannot be
obtained, and then if an express protest is added against papal errors and
corruptions that conflict with Christ’s institution. But because this cannot
be done in the papacy without danger to life, it is better for us to say
simply that it is not lawful and that parents must not allow such a baptism to
be administered to their children. This is all the more so when it is clear to
them that the papists wish to administer baptism to their children for no
other reason than that they may be torn from Reformed communion and enrolled
in the register of the papists. What else would this be than deliberately
consenting to the destruction of the infants by permitting them to pass into
an idolatrous communion in which salvation cannot be given? No father can
allow this without grave sin.
