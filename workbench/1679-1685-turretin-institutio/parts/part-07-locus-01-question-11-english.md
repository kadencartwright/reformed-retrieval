QUESTION XI

Is There Any Use for the Testimony of the Senses in the Mysteries of Faith,
or Is It to Be Entirely Rejected? Affirmative to the Former; Negative to the
Latter.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. This question is raised for us by the Roman Catholics. In order to weaken
the argument we draw from the testimony of the senses to attack the fiction
of transubstantiation and of Christ's carnal presence in the Eucharist, and
to confirm the reality of the substance of bread and wine—because the senses
see nothing, touch nothing, and taste nothing but bread and wine—they go so
far as to say that the testimony of the senses is not to be heard in the
mysteries of faith, because mysteries are above sense and faith consists in
our believing the things we do not see.

II. Although the orthodox do not wish the senses to be heard in all
mysteries, they nevertheless maintain that the testimony of the senses ought
also to have its place when sensible and bodily things within the sphere of
their activity are under consideration.

III. To establish the state of the question, it must be observed that just as
there are three kinds of things that can be known—things of faith,
intelligible things, and sensible things—so there are three faculties occupied
with them: sense, reason, and faith. Sense perceives sensible things, reason
intelligible things, and faith spiritual and supernatural things. Now, just
as sense does not arrogate to itself judgment concerning things that are the
object of reason, much less can reason and sense judge concerning the things
of faith. Rather, each faculty has its own object with which it is occupied;
just as they must not be confused, so neither must they be opposed to one
another.

IV. The question, therefore, is not whether the testimony of the senses must
be heard everywhere, so that we grant nothing except what the senses can
grasp. For we acknowledge that there are many mysteries to which reason, and
much less sense, cannot rise, such as the mysteries of the Trinity, the
incarnation, and so forth. Rather, the question is whether, when the senses
judge concerning their proper object and do not go outside their sphere,
their testimony must be rejected or admitted. The question is whether faith
is opposed to the well-ordered judgment of the senses and overturns it. We
deny this.

V. Some mysteries of faith are purely spiritual and placed far above our
grasp, such as the mysteries of the Trinity, the incarnation, and so forth.
Others

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 33 | IA leaf 87 | PDF p. 87]

consist in sensible and bodily things, whether God uses these things as
instruments and means for carrying out his purposes or wishes through them to
raise us to a clearer knowledge of higher mysteries. Such are Christ's
miracles, the types and figures of the Old Testament, and the sacraments of
the New. The former are properly the object of our faith and in no way fall
under the senses. But as regards the latter, in which a spiritual thing is
joined with a bodily thing, the spiritual thing always remains the object of
faith, while the bodily thing remains the object of sense. The question,
therefore, is whether faith uses the testimony of the senses in bodily
things—for example, when it judges concerning the substance of bread and wine
in the Eucharist and concerning the absence of Christ's body—or whether it
rejects that testimony.

[Marginal heading: The testimony of the senses is proved.]

VI. That the testimony of the senses is not to be rejected is evident: 1.
From Christ's example. To prove the reality of his body after the
resurrection, he appeals to the senses: “See and touch,” Luke 24:39. He
certainly would not have done this if their testimony were deceptive and
doubtful. The angel likewise uses this argument, Matthew 2:86: “He is not
here, but has risen; see the place where he lay.” And in Acts 1, the apostles
use the same proof when they promise Christ's return in the manner in which he
departed. Thus 1 John 1:1–2 and 2 Peter 1:17 base a principal argument for the
truth and divinity of the gospel upon the testimony of the senses. 2. Because
God uses the preaching of the Word and the contemplation of his works to
bring us to faith, it is certain that these means presuppose not only the use
of the senses—without whose ministry both God's Word and his works would be
unknown to us—but also the faithfulness and truth of their testimony. For if
the senses could be deceived, what certainty could be engendered in us either
from the Word or from God's works? 3. The Holy Spirit uses the operations of
the senses to describe the actions of the intellect, such as seeing, hearing,
tasting, and so forth. This could not be done unless the testimony of sense
were certain and not unstable. 4. God's faithfulness does not allow us to
believe that God wished to delude human beings by directing them to testimony
that could deceive and mislead them.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

VII. Although the senses are not absolutely infallible, it does not follow
that their testimony must be regarded as nothing, because there are various
conditions under which the senses do not err: 1. The object must be at a
proper distance. 2. The medium must be pure and free from everything that
could distort the image. 3. The organ must be properly disposed. 4. All the
senses, at least those capable of knowing a particular object, must be
consulted and render the same judgment. 5. The senses must act attentively
and not hastily; otherwise they can be deceived. 6. The imagination must be
free, with neither frenzy nor fever present in it; otherwise it often happens
that we believe we see and hear things which we nevertheless do not see or
hear. If we attend to all these conditions, it will be evident that they all
concur in the testimony concerning the reality of the substance of bread and
wine in the Eucharist in such a way that it cannot by any means be deceptive
or called into doubt.

VIII. It cannot be said that an illusion was produced in the senses in the
case of Mary Magdalene, who believed that Christ was the gardener, John 20:15,
because she was deceived by a hasty judgment. She corrected it immediately
when her ears came to the aid of her eyes and she recognized the Savior by
his voice. Nor can this be said in the case of the disciples at Emmaus, Luke
24:31, who did not recognize Christ, because this shows that their knowledge
was imperfect and obscure, but not false. They indeed believed that the one
walking with them was a true human being, but they did not think he was
Christ. Nor can it be said in the case of angels appearing in human form. For
although they were not true human beings, the bodies under which they
appeared were nevertheless real bodies and not phantoms. There was always
something in them from which people could discern that they were not mere
human beings but something supernatural, such as

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 34 | IA leaf 88 | PDF p. 88]

the splendor that surrounded them, as was seen in the angel announcing
Christ's nativity, and the white garments under which the angels who
witnessed Christ's resurrection appeared. Hence it was not difficult for
sense and reason to make a correct judgment concerning them. But if some
people were deceived, as it is credible that this happened not infrequently,
the error was not on the part of the object, which sufficiently revealed
itself, but on the part of the subject and the principle of sensory judgment.

IX. It is one thing for someone to be struck with blindness by God so that an
object is not seen rightly and clearly; another for well-constituted senses to
be deceived in the testimony they give concerning their proper and suitably
disposed object. The former is said of the Sodomites, Genesis 19:11, and the
Syrian soldiers, 2 Kings 6:18, who could not find Lot's door and recognize the
city of Samaria, but the latter is not.

X. If Christ escaped through the midst of the crowd that wished to drive him
headlong from the mountain, Luke 4:30, we should not therefore believe that
he rendered his body invisible and imperceptible to the senses. Rather, he
concealed himself in the crowd in order to withdraw from their fury, whether
he struck them with blindness or suddenly calmed or restrained their fury, as
Ambrose and Bede maintain.
