### QUESTION IV

#### Whether Belshazzar or Darius the Mede was the last king of the Chaldaeans?

Answer. There is a serious, bitter, and tangled controversy on this matter
among chronologists and interpreters of Scripture, ancient as well as modern.
There are two principal opinions. The first and common one holds that
Belshazzar, son of Evil-merodach and grandson of Nebuchadnezzar, after a reign
of seventeen years was killed by enemy hands on the night when Babylon was
taken by Darius the Mede and Cyrus. The latter opinion belongs to others,
especially Scaliger. He maintains that after Evil-merodach had reigned two
years he was killed by his brother-in-law Neriglissar; that Neriglissar,
Nebuchadnezzar’s son-in-law, governed the Babylonian kingdom for four years as
guardian and protector in the name of his son Belshazzar—otherwise
Laborosoarchod, Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson by his daughter; and that after him
his own son Belshazzar, when he had sat on the throne for only nine months,
was killed by a faction of Chaldaean nobles. Immediately after Belshazzar’s
murder, they elected as emperor Darius the Mede—otherwise Nabonidus or
Labynetus—then sixty-two years old. After he had reigned seventeen years, he
was deposed by Cyrus, though he was allowed to live peacefully in Carmania to
the end of his life.

> Marginalia, printed p. 149: *Who Darius the Mede was*.

With extremely lofty disdain and thoroughly dictatorial authority, Scaliger
commands the entire nation of chronologists to abandon the first opinion and
run bodily into the second, which is his own. He does have as followers
Capellus, Reynolds, Calvisius, and—surprisingly—the Jesuit Petavius, chief of
his opponents. No less than Scaliger, Petavius angrily resents that the tribe
of chronologists and interpreters of Scripture is not pliant enough to follow
the opinion of Scaliger, otherwise his greatest antagonist.

> Marginalia, printed p. 149: *Scaliger’s dictatorship in letters is much too
> tyrannical*.

In his notes on Daniel 5:31, Diodati does indeed report both opinions, but
without any indication which he himself embraces. The English annotators in
both their editions on Daniel 5, Junius there, Torniellus, Salianus, Henricus
Philippi, Ussher, Howes, and many others stand for the first. We like to stand
among the common people and adhere to ancient and common teachings unless a
very clear necessity drives us away from them.

Although Scaliger in *On the Emendation of Times*, book 6, page 543, seems to
treat the matter with the utmost seriousness and certainly speaks with ample
authority, he advances only a few and rather slight arguments for our side,
but many demonstrations against what he calls our obstinacy. Yet those are
far too faint-hearted who, terrified by long and imposing words, do not dare
to engage, or to compare the weight of the arguments on either side in the
even balance of judgment.

We argue first for the common opinion as follows. The Babylonian Empire
continued in Nebuchadnezzar’s line right to its end, in the hands of his son
and his grandson through that son, as Jeremiah 27:7 says explicitly. But
Scaliger’s opinion grants nothing of the kind. It makes Belshazzar not the son
of Nebuchadnezzar’s son but of his daughter, and places the Chaldaean monarchy
for a full seventeen years in the hands of Darius the Mede, who had no kinship
with the Chaldaean royal family, while allowing Nebuchadnezzar’s descendants
to hold it for scarcely seven years. The substance of Petavius’s answer is
that Belshazzar was Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson through his daughter, since his
father Neriglissar was Nebuchadnezzar’s son-in-law; and, further, that the
Hebrew word *ben* is used not only for sons but also for sons-in-law and even
for daughters themselves.

> Marginalia, printed p. 149: *Against Scaliger and Petavius, in support of
> the common opinion, argument 1*.

Reply. These points will satisfy no sensible person. Grant all this to
Petavius and to Scaliger, his leader here; it still does not touch the point
of our argument. Jeremiah means that Belshazzar—or whoever finally sat last
upon the Babylonian throne—was Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson and, as is often
read in Daniel, Nebuchadnezzar’s son, not in some loose and broad sense,

> Marginalia, printed p. 149: *Reply to Petavius’s answer*.

[Printed p. 150 | IA leaf 180 | access PDF 180 | OCR object 179]

but in the strictest sense: he was not the son of a daughter but the son of a
son, and not of just any son but of Evil-merodach, whom the subject nations
served after Nebuchadnezzar. This can in no way be true of Darius the Mede,
nor even of Belshazzar himself unless he was the son, not of a daughter, but
of a son—and of the particular son who immediately succeeded Nebuchadnezzar
in the empire. The chief force of our argument lies in the prophet’s final
words, which the answer makes no attempt at all to touch: namely, that
Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson sat on the Babylonian throne until it was overthrown
by the Persians. Jeremiah’s words concern the very end: “until the time of his
land comes, even his own.”

Second, we argue from Daniel 5:28, 30, and 31. The man from whom the empire of
the Chaldaeans was taken so that it might be handed over to the Medes and
Persians was the last Chaldaean monarch; and the Mede in whom God’s
declaration that the kingdom would be separated from the Chaldaeans and
handed to the Persians and Medes was first fulfilled was not a monarch of
Babylon, but the head of the second monarchy of the Medes and Persians. Daniel
says the former about Belshazzar and the latter about Darius the Mede. The
empire was taken from Belshazzar not to be handed to another Babylonian, or to
remain for some more years in Chaldaean hands, but to be handed at once—not
to just any Mede, but to the nation of the Medes, and not to that nation
alone, but to it together with the Persians, indeed principally to the
Persians. This is indicated both by the word *upharsin* in the oracle and by
*Peres* in its interpretation. The declaration about the Persians and Medes
coming to the second monarchy was also fulfilled in Darius immediately after
Belshazzar’s death. For after Daniel had said, “Belshazzar was killed that
very night,” he adds in the words immediately following, “Darius the Mede
received the kingdom.” This wholly excludes Scaliger’s evasion that the Medes
and Persians succeeded only indirectly to Belshazzar’s empire, after
seventeen years of Nabonidus, and not immediately after Belshazzar’s death.
He compares David, who did not sit immediately on Israel’s throne but only
some years after Samuel had pronounced that the kingdom was taken from Saul
to be given to David. But Samuel’s passage is not parallel to Daniel’s unless
Samuel had added that on that very night Saul and his son Ish-bosheth were
killed and David son of Jesse sat upon the throne. If Samuel had pronounced
this, it would have been remarkably bold to call into doubt Judah’s immediate
and sudden succession to Israel’s throne, with Saul’s family and the tribe of
Benjamin set aside.

> Marginalia, printed p. 150: *Argument 2*; *Reply to Scaliger’s answer*.

Third, we argue: whoever administered the Chaldaean kingdom according to the
laws of the Medes and Persians should be placed in the class of Median and
Persian kings, not Chaldaean kings. But Darius did this in Daniel 6:8, 12, and
15. Did the Chaldaeans, before their empire had been abolished by the Medes,
voluntarily abolish their own laws and royal statutes and freely put on the
yoke of hostile laws? Certainly at the end of Nabonidus, the last Chaldaean
king, deadly hostilities existed between the Chaldaeans and the Medes; and
before Cyrus’s reign neither the nations nor the laws of the Medes and
Persians were joined. What indeed had the Babylonians to do with Persian
statutes before Persian arms had subdued Babylon?

> Marginalia, printed p. 150: *Argument 3*.

Fourth, the man from whom the remaining Persian monarchs are numbered belongs
to the class of Persian, not Chaldaean, monarchs. But the remaining Persian
monarchs are numbered from Darius the Mede in Daniel 11:1–2: “Three kings
will yet stand in Persia”—that is, after Darius the Mede—“and the fourth will
be enriched above all,” namely Xerxes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 150: *Argument 4*.

Fifth, it is altogether improbable that the Babylonians willingly chose to
eradicate the entire line of Nebuchadnezzar the Great in favor of a foreigner
from the Median nation, with which the Chaldaeans then maintained deadly
hostilities. It is likewise improbable that in the ninth month of his reign
they chose to kill Nebuchadnezzar’s grandson by the cruel torture of beating,
before they had experienced the young man’s character for even one year,
especially when his father Neriglissar had recently fallen fighting bravely
for his country, killed by the Medes, as the authors of this history maintain.

> Marginalia, printed p. 150: *Argument 5*.

As a sixth argument, consider the authorities not only of almost all recent
writers, which the opposing side concedes, but also of many ancient writers.
They concede Xenophon, Josephus, and Jerome to us; in authority these far
outweigh all the adverse witnesses. Our opponents depreciate Xenophon’s
authority here, as though he had written his *Education of Cyrus* not with
historical fidelity but as instruction in ethics and politics. It is true
that in that work Xenophon intends the office of a teacher of morals more
than that of a historian, but not so that he wishes to lie in the matters he
positively asserts as history. Their example of Xenophon’s historical error—
the peaceful death of Cyrus, whom they say was killed by Tomyris the
Massagetan—does not undermine the author’s credibility. Indeed, later
centuries furnish not only firm belief but eyewitness evidence for Xenophon
against Herodotus here. Herodotus would have Cyrus and his vast army destroyed
by the Massagetae in so complete a slaughter that no messenger survived the
disaster. Xenophon says that Cyrus died peacefully at home in Persia and was
buried at Pasargadae; Quintus Curtius,

> Marginalia, printed p. 150: *Argument 6*; *Xenophon’s authority is defended
> here*; *Was Cyrus killed by Tomyris?*

[Printed p. 151 | IA leaf 181 | access PDF 181 | OCR object 180]

and the other later historians say that, well within two hundred years of
Cyrus’s death, Alexander the Great entered his tomb and handled the man’s
bones, ornaments, and weapons with his own hands. At that time no Greek or
Persian raised a controversy about the tomb’s authenticity. And in describing
Belshazzar’s killing by Gadatas, Gobryas, and the other Babylonian deserters
in the midst of a great banquet, Xenophon comes closer to Daniel than any
other ancient writer.

They also depreciate Josephus’s credibility here, but with what justice?
Grant that copyists corrupted Josephus’s writings in many places and that he,
being human and a Jew, erred in some matters. For what purpose would he have
departed from Berossus and Megasthenes in this history except to follow the
truth of Daniel and sacred Scripture rather than any human writing? Indeed,
Scaliger, the leader of our opponents, ably defends Josephus’s authority
against detractors in this very place.

They maintain that Jerome was deceived by Josephus, yet they must admit that
Jerome, a man of the keenest judgment, surveyed the writings of the ancients
as thoroughly as anyone; that he weighed Berossus and Megasthenes, whom he
cites, no less than Josephus himself; and that he left their accounts for no
other reason than to adhere to the sacred histories.

> Marginalia, printed p. 151: *The authority of Josephus and Jerome is
> defended*.

To the preceding authorities I add Herodotus, who carries the greatest weight
in an argument of this kind, as the opposing side itself admits, and here
agrees with Xenophon. He asserts—Scaliger cites him, though he unjustly rejects
him—that Nabonidus or Labynetus was the son of Nebuchadnezzar, or Labynetus
the Great. This should have stopped the mouths of Megasthenes and his
followers, who assert that the last Chaldaean king had no blood connection
with the royal family.

> Marginalia, printed p. 151: *Herodotus also stands for us here*.

But let us weigh the opposing arguments. Scaliger’s first and principal
reason, which he magisterially presses and Petavius repeats after him, is
this: Darius the Mede obtained the Babylonian throne by no force, but by the
people’s election. Cyaxares, however—or whoever joined Cyrus in taking up
arms against the Chaldaeans—obtained that throne by force, not by the
people’s election. Therefore Darius the Mede was not Cyaxares or any of
Cyrus’s allies, but Nabonidus, elected by the Babylonian people in
Belshazzar’s place. They prove the major premise from Daniel 5:31, “Darius the
Mede received the kingdom.” These words, they say, signify obtaining a thing
without any force, either by right or through another’s voluntary transfer.

> Marginalia, printed p. 151: *Scaliger’s arguments are refuted. 1*.

Answer. As it seems to us, this argument is frivolous, false, and irrelevant.
It is frivolous because it is merely verbal and grammatical, based upon the
meanings of words. It is false because it proceeds from a genus to one
particular species: from receiving in general it concludes a particular kind
of receiving without force, through popular election or right of succession.
If we inspect either the nature of things or the usage of words in sacred
Scripture itself, those who take by any means are said to receive, whether
someone delivers the thing or no one does. Our opponents establish no
difference in Hebrew, Greek, or Latin, in simple or compound words, between
“take” and “receive”; all the corresponding terms signify one and the same
action. This may be seen in 1 Samuel 21:9, concerning Goliath’s sword, which
David took from the priest. The Greek interpreter says, “If you will take
this for yourself, take it.” Jerome has, “If you wish to take this, take it
for yourself”; Arias Montanus, “If you take this, take it for yourself”; and
Junius, “If you are going to receive it, receive it for yourself.” Thus we
see that the Hebrew and Greek terms and the Latin *capere, accipere,* and
*tollere* are synonyms. That things seized by force, to which there is no
right and which no one voluntarily delivers, are also said to be “received”
is clear from many passages of Scripture. First Samuel 5:1 says, “And the
Philistines took the ark of God.” Did the Philistines have any right to God’s
ark? Did anyone deliver the ark to them? Second Samuel 1:10 says, “And I took
the crown that was upon his head.” Did Saul deliver his crown to the
Amalekite? Or did the Amalekite have any right to Israel’s diadem? Again, 2
Samuel 21 says, “Take his spoils for yourself.” Are things stripped by force
from an enemy voluntarily delivered by anyone?

The New Testament uses the same phrasing. Matthew 4:1 says, “Then the devil
takes him,” that is, receives or takes Christ. John 19:16 says, “But they took
Jesus and led him away.” Matthew 27:27 has, “Then the soldiers, taking Jesus
into the praetorium.” Was no force involved in these actions of the devil and
of those who crucified Christ? But to dispute no further, in 1 Samuel 2:16 we
read in explicit words of receiving by force: “You will give it now; and if
not, I will take it by force.”

Perhaps these passages convicted Scaliger of error. In his later writings he
does acknowledge that “to receive” and the Greek “to assume a kingdom” are
said of one who invades a kingdom by force. See his notes on the fragment of
Berossus, page 15. Nevertheless, even there he retreats to the Hebrew word
*qabel*, which he claims is never used except of receiving something delivered
by another. The word is indeed rather rarely used among the Hebrews and is
Chaldaean rather than Hebrew; this should have sufficed for the present
argument, since we have proved that its Hebrew, Greek, and Latin synonyms are
commonly used in sacred Scripture contrary to

> Marginalia, printed p. 151: *Not every act of receiving is without force*.

[Printed p. 152 | IA leaf 182 | access PDF 182 | OCR object 181]

Scaliger’s assertions, as he finally admits. As to this last refuge, we attack
it as follows. *Qabel* is used for receiving something voluntarily delivered
by no one, but found lying at one’s feet, or even seized by force. Thus 2
Chronicles 20:17 and 22 says, “The priests carried the filth out of the temple;
they received the blood of the bulls.” Did the temple deliver its filth, or
the bulls their own blood, into the hands of the priests and Levites? Daniel
7:18 says, “The saints will receive the kingdom.” Is the Hebrew-Chaldaean word
not rightly rendered by the Septuagint as “will receive”? And since the
former beasts are killed so that the kingdom might come to the saints, was
there no force involved in this reception?

I said that this chief argument of Scaliger’s is not only frivolous and false
but also irrelevant. Grant that Darius the Mede, or anyone else who received
the Chaldaean scepter, received it from someone who handed it over—but what
has this to do with the matter? The entire question is from whose hand he
received it. Our writers teach that Cyrus the conqueror granted the scepters
of both Media and Babylon to his uncle and father-in-law Cyaxares. But does
Daniel affirm that the Chaldaean people voluntarily handed their power to
Darius the Mede without force? This is the proof that Scaliger could never
wring from Daniel’s words by any torture.

The second argument. Although Scaliger boasts excessively of its strength, it
is no stronger than the first. Daniel 9:1 says that Darius “was made king over
the kingdom of the Chaldaeans.” By this expression, Scaliger says, whether we
like it or not, all armed force is excluded, and Darius was created emperor
of the Chaldaeans either by the people’s votes or by a faction of princes.

> Marginalia, printed p. 152: *2. How Darius was made to reign*.

Answer. The force of this argument is based on the native significance of the
Hiphil and Hophal forms in Hebrew verbs. This entire dispute is also
grammatical. If we denied that these conjugations are always transitive, many
examples could be offered. Indeed, both the Septuagint interpreters and Jerome
render the verb before us and its synonym in Hosea 8:4 intransitively: “they
reigned for themselves and not through me”; “princes arose, and I did not
know.” But let us grant Scaliger this grammatical argument and render
Daniel’s passage, “Darius did not reign but was made king.” Does the force of
the word necessarily entail that he was appointed by a popular vote or a
council of princes without any force whatsoever? How foreign this is to
sacred Scripture! How often is the most savage tyrant Abimelech said to have
been made king of Israel in Judges 9? Was no force involved in that action?
In Isaiah 7 Rezin and Remaliah wish to appoint the son of Tabeel as king of
Israel. David made Solomon king, Pharaoh made Jehoahaz king, and
Nebuchadnezzar made first Jeconiah and later Zedekiah king. The same word we
are debating appears in all these places. Did any faction of princes or vote
of the people appear there? Abner made Ish-bosheth reign; Joab and the other
soldiers made David reign; God made Saul reign by his lot, without any
election or faction of the people. The passages of Scripture are obvious.
Certainly this transitive conjugation, whether in the active or passive
voice, can indicate nothing more than that someone was appointed king,
whether God alone, the whole people, a faction of the people, an army, or a
prince—native or foreign, against the people’s will—was the author or helper
in that action. Our writers indeed maintain that Darius was placed by Cyrus
on Babylon’s throne. Although the thrones of both Media and Babylon were owed
to Cyrus, he granted both for life to his uncle and father-in-law, and by the
arms of his own army protected him, whom he had placed upon those thrones,
against every enemy.

Scaliger would have his third argument surpass every conjecture: that the
times of Darius the Mede and Nabonidus agree, since each reigned seventeen
years.

> Marginalia, printed p. 152: *3. The times of Darius the Mede and Nabonidus
> do not agree*.

Answer. Let Scaliger himself be judge and Petavius his assessor, and this
argument will be nothing. The times of Darius the Mede and Nabonidus do not
agree at all. Even if what our writers assert from Scripture and most probable
reasoning were false—that Darius the Mede governed the Babylonian state for
only one year—Scaliger’s own assertion in his *Eusebius*, page 38, and notes
on Berossus, page 15, that Nabonidus had thirty-four years, disagrees so much
that Petavius no less than Calvisius abandons the master here. They remove
almost half of Scaliger’s time from Nabonidus and assign him seventeen rather
than thirty-four years. Yet even then the times do not agree, for on this
reckoning Belshazzar is compressed into such narrow bounds that not even one
year remains for him, although Daniel mentions his third year.

Scaliger’s escape—that Neriglissar’s four years, in which he governed as
guardian in the son’s name, should be credited to that son—is asserted
gratuitously and without any witness. Indeed, it contradicts the ancients,
who say that Neriglissar was the true king of Babylon, not the son’s guardian,
and that, fighting bravely for the kingdom with royal insignia, he was killed
by Cyrus.

Moreover, if Nabonidus was Darius the Mede, then according to Daniel he
entered the kingdom in his sixty-second year. Cyrus attacked him in his
seventeenth, that is, as Scaliger himself admits,

[Printed p. 153 | IA leaf 183 | access PDF 183 | OCR object 182]

in his seventy-ninth year of age. Did such extreme old age allow Nabonidus to
perform every military duty, lead an army, contend with Cyrus, and, when
defeated in the field, fortify cities and strongholds and defend them long and
bravely against Cyrus’s entire strength? Indeed, after all these military
labors, could he prolong his life to his ninety-sixth year—and do so in a
kingdom? Which of even his most devoted ancient or modern followers can
Scaliger name as his companion in these dreams?

Scaliger’s final argument, which he claims leaves no room for obstinacy, is
this. The last Chaldaean king against whom Cyrus took up arms was a Mede; but
Cyrus took up no arms against his uncle Cyaxares. Therefore Cyaxares was not
that Mede, and the name must be assigned to Nabonidus. He proves the major
premise from Megasthenes, cited by Eusebius in *Preparation for the Gospel*,
book 10.

> Marginalia, printed p. 153: *4. Whether the last king of the Chaldaeans was
> a Mede*.

Answer. The whole argument rests upon Megasthenes’s testimony; but is
Megasthenes of as much authority as Xenophon or Herodotus? Megasthenes and
Berossus wrote after the time of Alexander the Great; Xenophon wrote much
earlier, and Herodotus not many years after the time of Cyrus now at issue.
Although Scaliger unjustly detracts from Xenophon’s credibility in historical
matters because he deliberately mixes in much ethics, he is accustomed for
many reasons to prefer Herodotus to both Berossus and Megasthenes. But
Herodotus asserts that Nabonidus or Labynetus the Younger was
Nebuchadnezzar’s son, that is, Belshazzar. Moreover, in the passage cited,
Eusebius himself declares that Megasthenes erred in various matters through
ignorance of sacred Scripture.

> Marginalia, printed p. 153: *How great Megasthenes’s authority is*.

Yet let us deal more gently with Scaliger and grant that the entire fragment
of Megasthenes which he brought from Eusebius is completely true. We suffer
no loss from it but gain much instead. The words on which Scaliger’s argument
rests are these: “the glory of the Assyrians that survives will belong to the
Medes”; it is asserted that a Mede will assist Cyrus in obtaining rule over
Babylon. This is our very assertion, for Cyaxares joined arms with Cyrus
against the Chaldaeans. But is there a single letter in Megasthenes saying
that the Mede treacherously handed his own subjects over to Cyrus, as Scaliger
maintains? Do not all the ancients assert that Nabonidus, whoever he finally
was, resisted Cyrus with all his strength and, after being defeated in battle,
fortified Babylon so ably that the Chaldaeans scorned Cyrus as he besieged it,
and that Cyrus gained the city only after much labor and by a stratagem?

> Marginalia, printed p. 153: *Megasthenes says nothing against us*.

The one thing in Megasthenes’s testimony which seems to work against us is
that a Mede is called the hope of the Assyrians, whereas Cyaxares, Cyrus’s
uncle, was an enemy of the Chaldaeans, not a friend in whom they could place
any hope or confidence. But we resolve this knot from the ancient history
acknowledged by Scaliger himself. The words Megasthenes reports are those of
the dying Nebuchadnezzar, foretelling the ruin of his empire by the Persians
and Medes. Nebuchadnezzar had entered into a close alliance with the Medes and
ratified it by his marriage: he had married Nitocris, sister of Astyages the
Mede and Cyrus’s great-aunt. He loved her uniquely until the end of his life
and, for her sake, always held the Median nation in great honor. Among the
neighboring nations, he ought to have placed no greater hope for his
descendants’ friendship and aid than in the Medes. Therefore, foreseeing by
the spirit of prophecy which Megasthenes attributes to him as he died the
ungrateful and scarcely credible conspiracy of Medes with Persians for the
destruction of his family, kingdom, and people, he laments it. Nothing here
supports Scaliger against our opinion.

> Marginalia, printed p. 153: *A Mede was the Chaldaeans’ hope*.

But if we might forge conjectures with Scaliger in this argument, why could
not the Greek word *Mēdis* here be Nitocris—Nebuchadnezzar’s Median wife and
the true glory of the Assyrians? Scaliger maintains that throughout the seven
years of Nebuchadnezzar’s infancy she administered the Assyrian Empire,
wonderfully fortified Babylon’s walls after her husband’s death, deservedly
attained the glory of Semiramis, and lived in the highest honor at Babylon
until Cyrus’s time. According to Scaliger, this Median woman saw her son
Evil-merodach cruelly killed by Neriglissar and her grandson Belshazzar killed
by Nabonidus. When Cyrus, her nephew through her brother Astyages, sat before
Babylon’s walls with the army of the Medes and Persians, why might she not,
through her influence among the Babylonians, have arranged for the city to be
handed to him, partly to avenge the killing of her family and partly to oblige
her kinsman Cyrus? But we do not wish to advance any conjectures, however
probable, too boldly and without evidence.

> Marginalia, printed p. 153: *Perhaps Nitocris, Nebuchadnezzar’s wife, was
> that Mēdis*.

At first sight, Megasthenes’s reference to a man rather than a woman militates
against this fairly probable conjecture. Yet this might be denied with at
least some appearance of reason. No author uses *Midis* as a masculine, but
*Midros*, while *Midis* is feminine. Textual criticism will allow that through
a copyist’s carelessness a slip from *o* to *i* is as easy as one to *ē*.
Besides, some words ending in *-is* in both the first and fifth declensions
are feminine. If it were objected that *Midis* here must be masculine because
it is construed with the masculine *sōtēr*,

the answer would be easy from the common Atticism by which compound
adjectives in *-ēs* are common in gender and are very frequently construed
with feminine nouns. But enough about textual criticism and conjectures.
