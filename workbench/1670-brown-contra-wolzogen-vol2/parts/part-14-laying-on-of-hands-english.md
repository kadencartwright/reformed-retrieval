ASSERTION XI.

In the proper performance of this Ordination, the imposition of hands is
required. I consider it necessary to add this Assertion because of what
Velthuysen has on page 298 and elsewhere in the preface. But so that it may be
rightly understood, the following points must be stated first. 1. We do not
say with the Roman Catholics and Hierarchists that the imposition of hands is
a sacrament or part of a sacrament. We do not say that it is an external sign
of inward and spiritual grace. We do not say that it is an effective means of
conferring the Holy Spirit, the gifts of the Spirit, or grace—far from it. We
abominate this. 2. We do not inquire whether it is an essential part of
ordination. Indeed, we profess that an Ordination in which there is no
imposition of hands is not thereby void or null. 3. We do not say that this
imposition of hands is necessary to Ordination by a necessity of means or of
the end, as if an Ordination which lacks it were no Ordination. 4. Nor do I
dispute whether it is an integral part of Ordination, as if an ordination
performed without the imposition of hands were imperfect. For whether it is so
or otherwise makes no difference to me. 5. Nor do I anxiously inquire what
kind of rite it is or what it properly signifies. Some have too freely
invented very many mystical significations, to which I do not subscribe. It is
enough if it is regarded as a simple, Evangelical rite instituted by Christ,
so that by the necessity of a command we are bound to use it in ordination.

With these points stated first, we prove the Assertion as follows.

[Volume II; printed p. 194; ONB canvas 202]

1. From the perpetual practice of the Apostles. In ordinations they always
observed this rite, Acts 6:6 and 13:3; 1 Timothy 4:14. Now the practice of the
Apostles and of the Apostolic Church in lawful matters ought to be regarded by
us as a rule to be perpetually observed when nothing peculiar to that time or
extraordinary appears in the action, but the same reason still presses now;
especially in matters which concern the order to be observed in Christ’s
Church. See what we say below about this in our final Assertion. Calvin rightly
says in *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 3, section 16: “Although no definite
command concerning the imposition of hands exists, nevertheless, because we
see that it was in perpetual use among the Apostles, their careful observance
of it ought to have the force of a command for us.”

2. Indeed, it appears that this rite is commanded in 1 Timothy 5:22, where
Paul commanded Timothy “not to lay hands hastily upon anyone.” Now when the
Apostle prescribes the manner of an action, he commands the action itself. So
Walaeus, on Pastors: “And certainly, since the Apostles always used it (that
is, the imposition of hands), and indeed the Apostle gives Timothy a command
in 1 Timothy 5:22, ‘Do not lay hands hastily upon anyone,’ we do not judge that
it should be omitted, because—note well—the affirmative is also contained in
that negative command: that he should lay hands upon worthy persons.”

3. The Apostle places “the imposition of hands” among the foundations and
first principles of Christian doctrine in Hebrews 6:1–2. Here the Roman
Catholics (and Hierarchists) understand confirmation, which they also regard
as a sacrament. But this opinion rests upon no foundation, because it will
never be proved that this confirmation is so necessary that it can and ought
to be numbered among the foundations. Nor will it ever be proved that this
Episcopal confirmation was used in the time of the Apostles, or that hands
were laid upon all baptized adults. Then others understand by this imposition
of hands the gifts of the Holy Spirit which at that time were given through

[Volume II; printed p. 195; ONB canvas 203]

the imposition of hands. But if it had once been proved that the imposition of
hands was used for this one purpose, and no other, then this opinion would be
probable. Yet it is too well known to need proof that this rite was used in
actions other than the communication of those extraordinary gifts—namely, both
in blessings and in ordinations, as is evident. Moreover, those extraordinary
gifts conferred in this way were in some measure peculiar to that time, but
these catechetical heads also concern us. Therefore it is much more probable
that by this imposition of hands the ministry and the ordination of ministers,
of which this was the rite, are understood. Ames judges so in *Bellarmine
Enervated*, volume 2, page 76. Bullinger understands the passage as the
ministry and the calling, mission, and power of Ministers. Therefore, if the
imposition of hands is taken for the whole ministry, the rite is not to be
despised. And whoever despises and rejects this rite can by the same right
reject the whole ministry.

4. The whole of Ordination is designated by this imposition of hands in Holy
Scripture, 1 Timothy 5:22 and 4:14. Therefore it must be some part of
ordination which is by no means to be despised. Let Walaeus be heard in the
place cited above: “Since it is taken by synecdoche for the whole election of a
pastor, it certainly must be regarded as a rite or an essential part;
otherwise it could not be taken for the whole, or at least it must be regarded
as a proper adjunct common to all callings.” But someone objects: “Prayers are
sometimes put for the whole of worship; they are not on that account a
principal part of worship. So also the observance of the Sabbath is put for
the whole of worship.” *Reply.* We need not inquire whether prayers and the
holy observance of the Sabbath are principal parts of divine worship. This is
enough for us: namely, if it is granted (as indeed cannot be denied) that
prayers and the observance of the Sabbath are necessary parts of divine
worship, and not indifferent parts of such a kind that we are free to reject
or retain them. Conse-

[Volume II; printed p. 196; ONB canvas 204]

quently, if it is granted that the imposition of hands bears the same relation
to ordination as prayers bear to worship, we will obtain our wish. For it will
follow that the imposition of hands is a necessary part of ordination and is
by no means to be rejected or regarded as an indifferent rite. He further
objects: “The raising of hands is put for Election in Acts 14:23, yet you do
not contend that it is a rite necessarily required in all elections.” *Reply.*
That custom is also perpetually observed among us in Scotland. Nevertheless, I
do not judge this extending or raising of hands in elections to be as
necessary as the imposition of hands is in Ordination, both because the people
can attest their consent and give their vote in other ways and because I do
not see it put for the whole election. For, as was said above, the verb
*cheirotonein* there denotes ordination, since it is applied to Paul and
Barnabas. And even if it were taken for election by votes, it would accomplish
nothing against us, because this is the force of the word itself: namely, to
elect by votes. But the case of the *imposition of hands*, or *cheirothesia*,
is different: it is applied only to this matter and figuratively denotes
ordination. Finally, it is not intended so directly or mentioned so often as
the imposition of hands. Therefore we cannot judge it equally necessary.

5. The point made by Aretius in *Problems*, locus 65, carries its own weight:
namely, that it is an ancient custom derived from the times of the Patriarchs,
observed by Christ, left to the Church by the Apostles, and used today. And
indeed it is used in almost all Churches. Gerhard testifies for the Lutherans
in *On the Ministry*, section 159, and Chemnitz in *Commonplaces*, on the
Church; Peter Martyr testifies for the Swiss. And according to Walaeus in the
place cited above, it is required in all the confessions of our Churches
except one or two. Therefore it is not rashly to be rejected. Zanchi says in
his work on the fourth commandment, page 789: “I have never approved, nor can I
approve, that in many places it

[Volume II; printed p. 197; ONB canvas 205]

has been cast out together with the other ceremonies.” And rightly so, since,
as Calvin says in *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 3, final section: “The Spirit
of God instituted nothing in vain in the Church. And since this ceremony
proceeded from him, we judge that it is not useless, provided that it is not
turned into a superstitious abuse.” Bucer has the following among his *English
Writings*, in *On the Restoration of the Lawful Ordination of the Ministers of
the Church*: “Therefore we must see what the Holy Spirit teaches in his
Scriptures to be the lawful Ordination of Ministers. It is the solemn
appointment and initiation, by the word of the Lord, the prayers of the
Church, and the imposition of hands, of those who can and ought to be
recognized as having been rightly called and approved for the Church’s
ministries, into those same ministries by a lawful Ordainer.”

6. The use and usefulness of this rite must also be considered. Calvin says in
the place cited above: “And certainly a symbol of this kind is useful both to
commend the dignity of the ministry to the people and to admonish the man who
is ordained that he is now no longer his own master, but is bound over to the
service of God and the Church.” Aretius also says in *Problems*: “It had a
useful consideration: first, it rendered them certain about their office, for
by this symbol they finally received the full power of teaching, just as
Joshua was confirmed by Moses through the imposition of hands and was rendered
certain about his office. So it was when Paul was to be sent to the Gentiles
and Timothy was chosen for the ministry. Ministers upon whom the imposition of
hands was employed in their elections must therefore remember even today that
by this symbol they received the charge to teach the truth.” And Bucanus says
in *On the Ministry*, question 49: “Nevertheless, that former practice seems
to need observance, not only for the sake of propriety, but so that by a symbol
of this kind of blessing the dignity of the ministry may be commended to the
people, while the man who is thus ordained or inaugurated and is put into
possession of his office may be made certain about his calling and admonished:
1. That he is no

[Volume II; printed p. 198; ONB canvas 206]

longer his own master, but is bound over to the service of God and the Church.
2. That a great burden has been placed upon him, and that he must therefore
diligently call upon God so that he may be equal to his office. 3. That he
must both seek and expect from God blessing, aid, defense, perseverance,
patience, counsel, comfort, a fuller understanding of sacred things, and other
gifts of the Spirit. 4. That he has been offered to God in the world as a
clean, pure, and chaste sacrifice, and therefore must devote himself to purity
of body and mind.” Chemnitz says in the place cited above: “By that public rite
we testify that this work, although mediated, is nevertheless truly divine;
and, second, by this imposition of hands the ordained man is presented to the
Church, so that it too may be admonished that through this person and his
ministry God wishes to teach, exhort, console, administer the sacraments, and
either absolve or bind sins for them.” From Chemnitz’s *Examination of the
Council of Trent*, Gerhard in the place cited above has the following: “The
Apostles regarded the rite of *cheirothesia* as fitted 1. so that this person
might be publicly designated and announced to the Church as having been
lawfully elected and called. For by this rite Moses in Numbers 27:18 and
Deuteronomy 34:9 designates and announces to the people the calling of Joshua,
his Successor. 2. So that by this rite the man who had been called might be
confirmed concerning his lawful and divine calling and at the same time be
admonished that he was destined, bound over, and as it were devoted to the
ministry and worship of God. So hands were formerly laid upon sacrifices,
Leviticus 1:4, and so forth. 3. So that it might be, as it were, the Church’s
public and solemn declaration before God that the form and rule prescribed by
the Holy Spirit had been kept in the election and calling. Therefore Paul says
in 1 Timothy 5:22, ‘Lay hands hastily upon no one; do not share in the sins of
others.’ 4. So that by that visible rite it may be signified that God approves
the calling which is thus made through the voice of the Church: just as he
chooses Ministers through the voice of the Church, so he also approves the
calling through the testimony of the

[Volume II; printed p. 199; ONB canvas 207]

Church, Acts 6:6. 5. When God’s name was invoked over a person in prayers, the
imposition of hands was customarily employed because that person was, as it
were, offered to God and presented in his sight, with supplications added that
God might deign to bestow his grace and blessing upon him. So Jacob the
Patriarch lays his hand upon the boys whom he blesses, Genesis 48:14. So Christ
blesses the little children by laying his hand upon them, Matthew 19:15 and
Mark 10:16, and the presbyters pray over the sick, James 5:14. Therefore, so
that in the ordination of Ministers—considering the usefulness and difficulty
of that office, and also setting out the hindrances of Satan, the world, and
the flesh—it might be signified how necessary particular grace and divine
blessing are, and so that the Church’s prayer might thereby be kindled and,
according to James, rendered active or effective, for this reason the external
rite of the imposition of hands was employed.”

In brief, we say that this rite must be employed: 1. Although not as an
effective and operative ceremony, nevertheless as a moral sign to signify and
declare who it is that has been separated to the pastoral office. 2. As the
rite and ceremony by which the office is applied to the called man or
transferred to him, or by which this office of the ministry is solemnly laid
upon him. 3. As the rite and ceremony by which the called man is solemnly
separated to discharge the pastoral office and by which he is offered,
dedicated, and consecrated to God. 4. As a rite indicating the person for whom
prayers are poured out to God. And 5. as a sign of authoritative and
ministerial blessing—though not as a sign representing any grace, nor as a
sign calling some past thing to remembrance, nor as a prefiguration of some
future thing, nor as a sign shadowing forth some present invisible or spiritual
thing, as Didoclavius speaks in *The Altar of Damascus*, page 530. Let us now
hear what the adversaries customarily object.

*Objection 1. The Apostles were not ordained through the imposition of

[Volume II; printed p. 200; ONB canvas 208]

hands.* *Reply.* 1. This argument would remove calling, ordination, and
election no less than the imposition of hands—indeed, every mediated calling,
for they were called without the intervention of men, whether in election or
ordination; see Galatians 1:1. But we are speaking about ordinary Ministers.
2. We also read that hands were sometimes laid upon them, Acts 13:3.

*Objection 2. When Paul commands Titus to ordain presbyters, he makes no
mention of the imposition of hands.* *Reply.* 1. Nor does he make mention of
the people’s Election. 2. Titus knew well enough that this rite had always
been used by the Apostle in ordinations, and so there was no need for him to
be reminded of it. Both Paul and Titus presupposed this. See the Belgian
Annotations on the passage.

*Objection 3. The Apostles used this rite only on account of the circumstances,
just as they abstained from what was strangled and from blood on account of the
Jews.* *Reply.* This cannot be established by any argument, even a probable
one. Nothing appears in the text which intimates any such thing. 2. Timothy,
as is probable, was ordained among the Gentiles through the imposition of
hands. 3. And the Apostles observed this rite even when they ordained among
the Gentiles. 4. Timothy is commanded to use this rite, as was said, yet he was
at Ephesus and not among the Jews.

*Objection 4. There was something singular in the Apostles’ imposition of
hands: singular and extraordinary gifts were conferred in this way. But
nothing of this kind is to be expected nowadays. Therefore there is no need
for this rite.* *Reply.* 1. It would then follow that prayers need not now be
used in ordination, because those extraordinary gifts were also given through
prayers, Acts 8:15–17 and 9:40; James 5:14–15. 2. It has not yet been proved,
nor can it be proved, that the Apostles used this rite

[Volume II; printed p. 201; ONB canvas 209]

only in conferring those gifts. 3. Indeed, we proved above that this rite was
employed in ordinations, as in Acts 6 and 13, where we do not read that any
gifts were granted in this way. 4. We do not urge the imposition of hands for
that purpose, precisely because that purpose was extraordinary. But we say
that its ordinary use in ordinations must be observed because of the example
of the Apostolic or primitive Church, which is recorded and fully described by
the Holy Spirit for our use so that we may know how we ought to conduct
ourselves in the House of God. 5. We have seen that this rite was employed by
others besides the Apostles, namely, by the presbytery in 1 Timothy 4:14.

*Objection 5. This rite was used in many other actions, both under the Old
Testament and under the New Testament. Therefore it is not a necessary rite in
ordination.* *Reply.* We do not deny that this rite was employed under the law,
both in blessings, Genesis 48:19–20, and in ordaining those who had been
admitted into the number of the Senators of the great Sanhedrin and of the
Magistrates, as appears in the case of Joshua, Numbers 27:18, 23 and
Deuteronomy 34:9. And in the New Testament it was used in blessings, Matthew
19:13, 15; in healing the sick, Matthew 9:18; Mark 5:23; 16:18; and 16:18;
Acts 28:8; and in conferring extraordinary gifts, Acts 8:15, 17. But nothing
against us follows from this, because one might argue in the same way: the
baptism of water was employed for other purposes in washing sacrifices and so
forth; therefore it is not necessary now to use water in administering
baptism. And it is not necessary now for the elements in the eucharist to be
blessed, because we customarily use blessings in other ways. This consequence
therefore amounts to nothing.

*Objection 6. They borrowed this rite only from the sacrifices upon which the
Priests laid hands, from the typological blessings of persons, or from the
Jewish custom, whether scholastic or ecclesiastical, in the promotion of
someone to the Rabbinate.* *Reply.* From where

[Volume II; printed p. 202; ONB canvas 210]

they borrowed it is not something we need anxiously inquire into. This is
clear: the Apostles always employed this rite in ordinations, and we judge it
rash to regard as indifferent something which they consistently used. In
matters concerning the government of the Church, the practice of the primitive
Church is a surer rule for us than the judgment or determination of anyone
nowadays. And we judge it safer to imitate the Apostles than to depart from
their practice when no argument compels us and no necessity presses us.

*Objection 7. By this reasoning all Churches which do not employ this rite in
the ordinations of Ministers and Deacons according to the example of Acts 6,
and consequently in the ordinations of Elders, will be condemned.* *Reply.* It
is too well known to be denied that many things in many Churches, and some
things in all Churches, need to be reformed. We strive—oh, if only we did
strive—toward perfection. I believe I have sufficiently demonstrated that
this rite is something instituted by the Apostles and that we are all always
bound to employ it. I except, as above in the case of Ordination itself,
extraordinary cases when it is not permissible to use it, either because of
insuperable physical difficulties or because of moral impediments—that is,
when it has been so turned to superstitious use that it cannot be employed or
used without sin. And if any neglect it, I do not judge that they are to be
approved in this respect. 2. As for Elders and Deacons, I confess that I have
not yet heard of a Reformed Church in which they have been ordained through
the imposition of hands. I have never heard of it being done in our Scottish
Church. But, to state freely what I think, I judge that Deacons and Presbyter
Elders ought to be ordained through the imposition of hands no less than
Pastors, because the offices of the Diaconate and Presbyterate are
ecclesiastical offices no less than the pastoral office. And the example of
the Apostles binds us no less in the Ordination

[Volume II; printed p. 203; ONB canvas 211]

of Deacons than in the Ordination of Pastors or Teachers. But the reason (as I
judge) that this custom has been received in none of the Churches was this:
these Presbyter Elders and Deacons were called to discharge this office only
for some period, say one or two years, and not for as long as they lived or
until they were found unworthy and unfit, as Pastors were called. But neither
does this agree with the practice of the primitive Church. Nowhere do we read
that anyone was called to an Ecclesiastical office only for a set time. And
hence the Erastian and Hierarchist Adversaries call our Presbyter Elders mere
laymen—that is, mere private persons—because when the year or two years have
ended, they return to their former state and condition and must no longer be
regarded as Ecclesiastical Officers. And even during that prescribed time they
do not seem to be such Officers, because Ecclesiastical work is only their
side work, as it were. There is something else which they principally do, so
that they may care and provide for their families. They are not separated to
this work as Pastors are to the pastoral office. I confess that among us too
it has been customary in some places to replace these Elders each year and to
elect new ones. But elsewhere the matter has been otherwise: once elected,
they discharged this office throughout the whole course of their lives unless
they were removed from office because of scandal or because they were found
unfit or lacking the qualifications required by the Apostle. But nowhere were
they separated and consecrated to this work in such a way that they did this
alone, gave themselves wholly to this work, and applied themselves to it
entirely, as the reason and practice of the primitive Church required. But I
know that there was—and still is—another cause of this evil: namely, that there
is no source from which all these men may live if they are separated to
discharge these offices. Yet equity and Christ’s law require that they have
remuneration. According to 1 Timothy 5:17, “they must be regarded as worthy of
double honor”—that is, the honor both

[Volume II; printed p. 204; ONB canvas 212]

of support and of reverence. But as Didoclavius says in the place cited above,
page 927: “This is the unhappiness of these times: they are not permanent
(that is, the Elders) everywhere, although they are in most of our Churches;
and where they are permanent, the Church’s goods and resources are not
collected into the Church’s public treasury, but are plundered by Courtiers,
Magistrates, and others of lower rank.” The authors of our Second Book of
Discipline say in chapter 6 that Presbyter Elders, once lawfully called and
furnished with gifts, ought not to lay down that office, and therefore they
intend them to be permanent, not temporary. And in chapter 8 they say that
Deacons ought to be called and elected in the same manner as other spiritual
Ministers. Concerning this election, they say, it was discussed above—that is,
in chapter 3, where they have the following: “Calling is common to all
Ecclesiastical Officers; it is the lawful manner by which those furnished with
gifts are advanced to spiritual office in the Church of God. Without this
lawful calling it was never permissible for anyone to take up an
Ecclesiastical function. Ordinary and external calling has two parts: Election
and Ordination. Ordination is the separation or consecration of a person to
God and the Church. The ceremonies of Ordination are fasting, prayers, and
the imposition of the hands of the presbytery.” From this we can gather that
they intended Deacons no less than pastors to be ordained through the
imposition of hands. Further, in chapter 9, where the Church’s patrimony and
its distributors are treated, they say that by the Church’s patrimony they
understand whatever has been or will be given to the Church, or is applied to
the Church’s public use and benefit by universal consent or by the custom of a
Kingdom professing Christ: whether lands, buildings, possessions, and annual
revenues have been given to the Church by donations, foundations, endowments
in mortmain, or by any other title of Kings, Princes, or persons of lower
rank; whether they are the continual offerings of the godly; and also all
those things which by laws, custom, and national usage have been applied to
the Church’s use and benefit, such as

[Volume II; printed p. 205; ONB canvas 213 (upper portion)]

tithes, manses, and glebes, as they are called. They say that these
ecclesiastical goods are to be collected and distributed by the Deacons, just
as in the Apostolic Church the Deacons were bound to collect and distribute
whatever was given by the Saints, whether alms or other movable and immovable
goods, lands, and possessions whose price had been laid at the Apostles’ feet.
And they say that this custom was always received until Antichrist corrupted
everything, as the ancient canons testify. These also indicate that these
goods were divided into four parts: one given to the Pastor for his support
and for exercising hospitality, another to the Elders and Deacons, a third to
the poor, the sick, and pilgrims or strangers, and a fourth for performing
other tasks. To these they add schools and schoolmasters, clerks of Synods,
legal agents of the Church, precentors, and so forth. Calvin has similar things
in *Institutes*, book 4, chapter 4, sections 5–7. Here we have a form sketched
for us by which everything could be established according to our desire, and
I hope that all good men join me in wishing that matters stood this way. But
when will we obtain our wish? I show here what ought to be done by right and
what should be desired; I do not know whether it should be hoped for.
Meanwhile, far be it from me to condemn those Churches which, hindered by
these insuperable obstacles, consult the good of the Church as far as they can
and admit Elders and Deacons without ordination and the imposition of hands,
even for a year or two when no others can be had than men who, necessarily
entangled in other business so that they may provide for themselves and their
families, cannot devote longer labor to these Ecclesiastical affairs.
