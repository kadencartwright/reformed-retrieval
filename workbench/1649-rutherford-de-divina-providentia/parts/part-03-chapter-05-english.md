# CHAPTER V

## The arguments of Diego Ruiz, Fasolus, Pedro de Arrúbal, Pesantius, and
## others contending for middle knowledge are brought over to our side

**1.**

Not the least place among our adversaries’ arguments is held by CHRIST'S
denunciation in Matthew 11 against Bethsaida: “Because if an abundance of such
signs as they possessed had been performed for Tyre and Sidon, they would long
ago have repented in ashes and sackcloth.” GOD therefore foreknows something
conditional that would contingently occur, antecedently to his decree.

**2.** But I answer: 1. There is more in the conclusion than in the premises,
for the text says nothing about a decree of God subsequent to the hypothetical
futurition of the Tyrians’ repentance. 2. There are serious men who maintain
that CHRIST does not speak from foreknowledge but adapts the style of a
reproachful discourse to the ordinary manner of speaking. Thus Jansenius,
Martinez, and Ribera: “If I had conferred such benefits upon mortal enemies,
they would not have shown themselves so ungrateful.” This detracts nothing
from the certainty of CHRIST'S knowledge, since he is reproaching, not
predicting. Our Calvin also says that “Christ does not predict, but reproaches
in human terms.” I therefore say that comparative speech affirms nothing
absolutely but only relatively, namely by comparison. When I say that rocks
are more easily softened by the preaching of the gospel than stony, obstinate,
and blinded Jews, I do not affirm that rocks can be softened by the preaching
of the gospel, but that they would be softened sooner or more easily than the
Jews. David Pareus, that most illustrious theologian, therefore says that
CHRIST means “that the Tyrians would have been moved more by those signs than
the Chorazites.” Bullinger says, “The idolatrous and corrupt cities of the
Gentiles could have been converted to repentance sooner than these.” For the
Jews regarded the neighboring Tyrians, says Theodore Beza, as abominable
despisers of God.

[Marginal references: Jansenius, chapter 47, *Harmony of the Gospels*;
Martinez, book 2, *Hypotheses*, rule 2; Ribera on Amos 2, figure 19; Calvin,
*Gospel Harmony*, chapter 11; David Pareus, book 1, *Grace*, chapter 13,
against Bellarmine; Bullinger on Matthew 11; Beza in the minor notes on
Matthew 11.]

[Chapter V; printed page 51; PDF page 103]

Junius likewise says that God speaks comparatively. So does Titelmannus.
Hilarion of Geneva says that it is hyperbole, as in the expression, “You will
soften a rock more easily than this man.” Alfonso Curiel and Aquinas are so far
from the Jesuits’ invention that they say, “Christ does not indicate what
would occur absolutely, but what would occur from the disposition of secondary
causes.” It is also certain that the particle “perhaps” does not indicate that
GOD is in doubt, as Augustine, Chrysostom, Ambrose, and Eusebius observe.
Indeed, Curiel says that Stapleton was deceived in attacking Calvin's
exposition. Ludovicus à Dola acknowledges that Scripture does not always use
the word “perhaps” philosophically, but often popularly. And certainly, when
CHRIST predicts only the comparative ease of the Tyrians’ repentance, no one
would say that he predicts an act infallibly future. Nor does CHRIST here
compare act with act, as many suppose, but disposition with disposition; he
says only that the Tyrians despised God with less habitual obstinacy than the
Jews, upon whom the light of the gospel and the glory of CHRIST'S miracles had
shone.

[Marginal references: D. Junius on Matthew 11; Titelmannus; Hilarion of Geneva
on Matthew 11; Alfonso Curiel, book 4, *Against Sapientius*, chapter 4, figure
35; Aquinas, *Summa*, part 1, question 39, article 3, reply 2; Augustine, Psalm
2, tractate 37; Chrysostom, homily 78 on Matthew; Ambrose, *On Repentance*,
book 2, chapter 5; Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel*, book 6, section 9;
Alfonso Curiel, book 4, *Against Sapientius*, 7, figure 77; Ludovicus à Dola,
*On God's Concurrence*, part 1, chapter 3, figure 4.]

The Salamanca College, opposing middle knowledge, says that CHRIST “does not
speak through hyperbolic exaggeration, but according to foreknowledge,”
because, as Cornejo says from Augustine and Prosper, “Christ expressly says
that they would have repented. For if he speaks only with moral certainty, it
follows that God did not possess an efficacious conditional decree concerning
them, and that God's liberty was suspended here and was not most fully
actual.” But this is easily solved. CHRIST speaks according to foreknowledge,
not with merely moral or conjectural certainty; but this is the foreknowledge
by which CHRIST neither foresaw nor predicted the act of the Tyrians’
conversion, but only the ease of the act—and that comparatively. It follows
that infallible knowledge and GOD'S liberty, not suspended but most fully
actual, were terminated upon the ease of the Tyrians’ conversion rather than
the Jews’.

[Marginal references: Salamanca College, tractate 3, *On God's Knowledge*,
doubt 2, assertion 3, disputation 9, figures 15–16; Cornejo; Augustine;
Prosper; Álvarez, disputation 7, figures 8 and 23.]

It is no objection that CHRIST'S words appear to express an act. Matthew
11:21: “Μετενόησαν—they would have repented—the Tyrians.” It is well known
that active verbs often denote a disposition: Psalms 50:17–19; 7:14; Isaiah
52:4; John 14:21. Add that Diego Álvarez, Cumel, Cabezudo, and Cabrera—as Diego
Ruiz also acknowledges—say that CHRIST foreknew that the Tyrians would not
have been converted by miracles alone, for that is plainly Pelagian, and
Fasolus says the same thing in substance. Augustine says that “their ability
to believe did not profit them, because they had not been predestined by him
whose judgments are inscrutable.”

[Marginal references: Cumel on part 1, question 23, disputation 8, no. 3,
reply to argument 5; Cabezudo, part 3, question 62, article 5, question 2,
reply to argument 13; Cabrera, part 3, question 18, article 4, distinction 5,
article 1; Ruiz on part 1, disputation 1.61, section 1, figure 3; Fasolus, tome
1, part 2, question 14, article 13, doubt 13, figure 217; Augustine, *On the
Gift of Perseverance*, chapter 14.]

[Chapter V; printed page 52; PDF page 104]

Ruiz objects in the same place that “given a predetermining decree, present
imputability would have been lighter among the Jews than among the Tyrians.
And,” says Arrúbal, “a teacher would be ridiculous if he accused one pupil of
slowness because he had not understood in a short time something another
understood only after a longer time. Likewise, if it were God's particular
will that the Tyrians repent if they saw the signs, a will which God did not
have concerning the Jews, the other conditions would not be equal and
Christ's rebuke would be ridiculous.”

[Marginal references: Ruiz, in the place cited; Arrúbal on part 1, question
14, article 3, disputation 46, chapter 5, sections 15–16.]

I answer: 1. “The Salamanca College turns this argument back. The Bethsaidites
could have replied,” they say, “‘If you had called us in that order in which
you foresaw through middle knowledge that we would be converted, just as you
were going to call the Tyrians, we would have repented no less than the
Tyrians.’” The Jesuits reply that GOD gave the Jews sufficient aids of grace
but was not bound to give congruous means. Likewise, we answer that GOD was
not bound to give the Jews predetermining grace.

[Marginal reference: Salamanca College, tractate 3, *On God's Knowledge*,
disputation 10, doubt 1, no. 4, figure 24.]

2. To the argument I say that it is indeed true that the Jews no less than the
Tyrians would have been converted, given GOD'S predetermining decree. It does
not follow, however, that the Jews were not inferior to the Tyrians in
habitual obstinacy and cultivated blindness, and more insensible to the light
of the gospel and of CHRIST'S signs. They therefore hardened their hearts far
more inexcusably than the Tyrians. Nor would the rebuke be ridiculous if a
teacher reproved the more obtuse of two pupils—if he had brought that
obtuseness upon himself by his own idleness—because from habitual dullness he
did not write so skillfully as the other of his fellow pupils, even though the
teacher caused both to write equally skillfully and guided the hand of each in
writing. The inequality between the Tyrians and the Jews therefore lies in
habitual obstinacy. The Savior's rebuke is altogether most wise in this sense:
“Although it rests with me, through my grace that predetermines stony hearts,
to bring both Tyrians and Jews to repentance, yet the Jews are more culpable
than the Tyrians in this: unlike the Tyrians, who lacked CHRIST and the gospel,
the Jews ought to have been far more teachable, since I indulged them with the
most eminent privileges of my covenant and calling through the preaching of
the gospel—with me, the Savior manifested in the flesh, preaching and
confirming the Word with so many glorious miracles. Meanwhile the Jews opposed
habitual obstinacy at a magnitude of twenty to my outward calling, whereas the
Tyrians opposed obstinacy at only eight.” 2. This is nothing other

[Chapter V; printed page 53; PDF page 105]

than what the Pelagians formerly objected against Augustine: “Why are we
corrected, accused, reproved, and charged? What do we do, since we have not
received? Why then are we corrected? We have not received obedience. Why then
are we corrected, as if we could give it to ourselves?” The same answer will
therefore satisfy the passage in Ezekiel 3:6 and many others of this kind.

[Marginal reference: Augustine, *On Correction and Grace*, chapter 6.]

**2.** Ruiz objects: “If God foreknows future conditionals not through
conditional knowledge but in an efficacious decree, it follows that a suitable
ground for persuading people is drawn not from the essences of things but from
God's efficacious will. Thus thieves hanging on a cross would possess no power
to deter robbers from crimes; nor could God see that robbers would be deterred
by this spectacle, but would foreknow that they would be deterred because he
had efficaciously decreed their deterrence. By his efficacious will God would
bring about all things merely at the presence of secondary causes. And this
statement in Deuteronomy 4, ‘You saw no bodily form on Horeb; therefore do not
make a carved image for yourselves,’ would prove its conclusion no more than
this: ‘This formal man does not move his right foot before moving his left on
that tenth day on which Jehovah spoke with you on Horeb; therefore do not make
a carved image for yourselves.’ Nor would there be any need for us to guard
against dangers through divine providence unless God foreknew conditionally
future evils before the predetermining decree.”

[Marginal references: Ruiz on part 1 of Aquinas, disputation 69, section 1 and
section 4, figures 56 and 7. Diego Ruiz argues that predetermination by decree
removes the persuasive power of motivating objects, but badly.]

I answer: 1. The argument labors under an invalid inference. Although GOD does
not foreknow future contingent conditionals through middle knowledge—that is,
before he has decreed that they occur—it does not follow that a suitable
ground for persuading people is not drawn from the essences of things. Objects
that motivate by considerations drawn from good and evil possess the power to
persuade people, because it is natural to the will to embrace good and reject
evil. But this power comes from GOD'S decree, which gives such a nature both
to the motivating objects and to the will being moved. If the argument
therefore proceeds concerning potential power, that power presupposes GOD'S
decree, and so the argument proves nothing against us. But if it proceeds
concerning actual persuasion, I say that GOD does not foreknow that a person
will be deterred from crimes by the sight of thieves hanging on a cross unless
in his decree that determines the person's will to the actual fear conceived
from that sight. 2. The sight of a bodily form possesses a danger of enticing
people to idolatry which the rightward movement of a foot does not possess,
because GOD by a predetermining decree established that this power to motivate
the will should reside in the sight of an image. Yet the whole actual efficacy
comes from GOD'S decree as the first and highest cause, and from the sight of
the bodily form as the proximate cause. 3. This argument

[Chapter V; printed page 54; PDF page 106]

overturns God's wisdom, on behalf of which it appears to contend. For if the
sight of thieves hanging on a cross possesses wisdom in itself and antecedently
to GOD'S decree, and can deter robbers from theft, then circumstances and
arguments possess a power to persuade wills apart from God's will and wisdom,
and thus apart from all God's providence. In this way, apart from GOD'S
providence and care, the power both of acting and undergoing, and sympathy and
antipathy among all things in nature, will reside in every secondary cause.
4. Nor do motivating reasons move GOD'S will, but only the created will, and
that according to GOD'S most wise will which determines every nature.

Cabrera, Navarrete, and Álvarez also teach the same. So does the acute
Bradwardine, a truly great scholastic: “If a reason moved the will of God, then
a greater reason would move it more. Since, therefore, God could make all
things better than he does, and it would be better and more rational if he did
so.” Guillaume Gibieuf says, “Nor does God take the rationale of his
disposition from the things themselves, but from his own wise will.”

[Marginal references: Cabrera, part 1, question 62, disputation 12; Navarrete,
tome 1, controversy 56; Álvarez, disputation 7, *On Aids*, folio 29;
Bradwardine, *The Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 21; Guillaume Gibieuf.]

God, therefore, could in accordance with his infinite wisdom have decreed and
foreknown another way to guard against idolatry—and every danger—than by not
displaying a bodily form. He could have implanted in fire a power to cool and
in ice a power to heat, if he had given another specific form to fire—that is,
to the thing which is fire—and to ice. But given this series and order of
things which now obtains, the bodily form possesses a power to entice people
to idolatry. This power comes from GOD'S predetermining decree as its first
cause, but from the nature of the bodily form and of the person led by the
senses as its proximate causes. The predetermining decree established that it
would operate through secondary causes. If you ask why a person is enticed to
idolatry, the answer is that in divine worship reason follows sense as its
master, while sense is led by the sight of a bodily form. If you ask in turn
for the reason of that reason and the cause of that cause, you must at last
come to the predetermining decree as the first cause of all causes.

**3.** Ruiz objects in this way: “If God foreknew not certainly but under
objective indeterminacy what each person would do under any temptations, then
God would not certainly know the obedience that would occur under temptations
which never in fact occur. God would not know whether Abraham would have
slaughtered his son on the hypothesis that God had never tempted him to
slaughter his son by an exploratory command; and thus God would acquire
experiential knowledge of something which he had previously not known. This
is absurd.”

[Marginal reference: Ruiz on part 1 of Aquinas, disputation 70, section 6,
figures 1–2.]

[Chapter V; printed page 55; PDF page 107]

I answer: 1. The same disease of invalid inference is here as in the preceding
argument. In the predetermining decree, by which from eternity GOD linked the
coexistence of condition and conditioned outcome—even if neither condition
nor conditioned outcome should ever pass from the state of possibility to the
state of actualization—he foreknows what each person would do under
temptations which never in fact occur. By an absolute decree, God decreed that
Abraham's willing mind to sacrifice his son to GOD and the command of GOD who
was testing him should coexist. Likewise, by an absolute predetermining decree
God decreed that the sight of the signs and the Tyrians’ repentance should
coexist; also Joash's striking the ground five or six times and GOD'S people's
victory over the Syrians; and the preaching of the preacher from hell and the
nonconversion of the rich man's five brothers.

[Marginal summary and reference: In the predetermining decree God linked the
conditioned outcome, even in all cases which will never occur. Exercise 16.4,
pages 126–127.]

Here I say in passing that those are deceived who, as I hear, say that I defend
GOD'S conditional decrees, which I appear to attack. For these are absolute
decrees, not conditional: absolutely and with no condition required, GOD
decreed the connection between the sight of the signs and the Tyrians’
repentance. But to the point: from this it follows that GOD does not acquire
knowledge by a new experience, but possesses it in the predetermining decree.
2. From the contrary opinion it follows that God, as if in doubt, hunts for
knowledge from known things as the primary cause of the infallibility of his
knowledge, although “all things are inscribed in his book,” Psalm 139:16. That
is, all things have been decreed and fixed by GOD'S determining will.

Fasolus replies that “God draws knowledge from things outside himself, not
from need, because there is here merely an objective presupposition. For a
created termination does not actualize anything in the act of God's knowledge,
but purely terminates it.” But I reply: the determination of a future free act
apart from every causative dominion of GOD is itself the sole first cause,
exercising absolute dominion and causing such infallible knowledge to exist in
God. God, then, in need of counselors, draws his middle knowledge from an
object which possesses futurition against God's will. Hence our adversaries
teach two things unworthy of God. 1. God cannot fail to know this object unless
he is ignorant of some thing outside himself. Sound theology teaches that God
freely knows everything future, because he could cause it never to be future;
and therefore, speaking simply, the cognition GOD possesses of things actually
future is at least mediately free. 2. A free creature which causes against
God's will, and antecedently

[Marginal references: Fasolus, tome 1, question 14, article 13, doubt 11,
figure 147. According to the adversaries who defend middle knowledge, God
draws cognition and counsel from creatures outside himself.]

[Chapter V; printed page 56; PDF page 108]

to his free will, the futurition of contingent things causatively makes the
object of this middle knowledge. Therefore the creature will be the cause why
such knowledge exists in God. Nor should one invent a merely objective
termination here: the creature brings it about against God's will, and once
the creature has done so, God necessarily knows it. A perfectly wise and
powerful emperor who administers all things according to his good pleasure,
with no one resisting, does so in a far more perfect manner than another who
hunts for knowledge of an outcome outside himself from messengers, counselors,
and the free and unstable course of events. To the one who asks, “Who was God's
counselor?”—Isaiah 40—or who was the cause that he entered into this counsel
rather than that one, or that he knew this which has conditional futurition
from a free cause rather than never knowing it, the answer according to the
Jesuits and Arminians must be: “Angels, demons, and human beings freely do many
things which possess futurition antecedently to God's decree and God's
knowledge.” Therefore God cannot fail to decree and know what creatures do
before the will and choice of the supreme potter; and therefore creatures are
causes with dominion of their own why this counsel is in God rather than that,
and why God knows this rather than never knowing it. Thus the created object
will not be purely terminative of divine knowledge, but also causative.

I illustrate the matter thus. I suppose there to be a person in the nature of
things who makes and creates all the colors of things, and suppose that people
open their eyes to look at colors by a necessity of nature. As creator of the
colors he would ultimately be the cause why all people actually saw colors,
because by making and creating colors he would be the cause why all beheld
them. Likewise, free agents which absolutely create or do not create free acts,
notwithstanding all dominion of divine providence, are the true causes why God
knows such acts and their futurition rather than never knowing them, for it is
repugnant that GOD should not open the eyes of his eternal knowledge to all
knowable things.

**4.** The fourth objection: “On the hypothesis that God had made no decree
concerning the connection between the sight of the signs and the Tyrians’
repentance—for he freely decrees all things—then if God were asked whether or
not the Tyrians would repent if they saw the signs, he would necessarily
answer, ‘I am entirely ignorant.’ This is absurd. Therefore God knows future
contingent conditionals apart from every decree.” I answer that it is no more
absurd for GOD not to know what is not knowable than for people not to hear
colors or see sounds.

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, 16.]

[Chapter V; printed page 57; PDF page 109]

For colors are visible, not audible; sounds are audible, not visible. But in
that case GOD would know the Tyrians’ repentance in the manner in which it is
knowable: he would know it to be possible, but would not know it to be
determinately future, because it would not be determinately future. 2. Although
God freely decrees all things in particular, his will nevertheless necessarily
settles the futurition or nonfuturition of all beings. Although he freely
decreed the world's creation, he nevertheless could not have failed from
eternity to decree either the world's creation or its noncreation. For God
cannot stand merely negatively with respect either to future things or to
things that will never be future; rather, he must also determine by a positive
decree the nonfuturition of many worlds.

[Marginal summary: God's will is determinate with respect to everything future
and nonfuture, while before the decree all things are possible.]

This is rightly held by the Salamanca College, Ripa, Nazarius, and the profound
Bradwardine, as also by Vázquez and Arrúbal. For just as it belongs to the
perfection of intellect to know everything intelligible, and to omnipotence to
be capable of everything feasible, so it belongs to GOD'S will, since it is
most fully actual—for everything which exists in infinite act is most fully
actual—so every willable and rejectable thing belongs to GOD'S will. 2.
Because everything
possible is actually known by the intellect as future or nonfuture, and that
necessarily. Therefore the intellect will necessarily propose every possible
thing to God's will so that he may will or reject it. 3. Providence, and
therefore the will, would not be providence if it did not extend to future
things, as is evident; but it would also be imperfect if it did not positively
extend to nonfuture things. For then I would not patiently submit myself to
God's will in not being a king, rich, learned, or strong; nor would gratitude
be terminated upon God's will that David was not killed by Saul, the Church
was not devastated by enemies, and Israel was not submerged together with the
Egyptians in the Red Sea. Indeed, says Bradwardine, “God's being supremely
actual and perfect is not free but absolutely necessary; God therefore does
not have a will suspended from eternity concerning possibilities which will
never be future.” Let the adversaries therefore see what follows from the
impossible.

[Marginal references: Salamanca College, opinion in tractate 4, *On God's
Will*, disputation 5, doubt 3, question 2; Ripa, part 1, question 14, article
13, doubt 4, *cult.*; Nazarius, part 1, question 14, article 13, controversy 1,
notable 1; Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 15; Vázquez, part 1, disputation 79,
chapter 2, section 17; Arrúbal, disputation 56, chapter 2, figure 5;
Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 15.]

**Objection 5.** “Providence would be gentler given middle knowledge, because
the reprobate are not necessitated, they need not despair, God's complaint is
satisfied, and human liberty remains intact.” Response 1. The necessitation of
the reprobate, since it is consistent with their liberty, is a gentle
providence. For the decree of reprobation produces no other necessity of
sinning in the reprobate than that produced by the free will of the reprobate.
The necessity from the hypothesis of God's decree and the necessity of
exercise, or

[Marginal reference and summary: Ruiz, disputation 69, section 10, figures
1–2. The necessity of the decree and free will coincide.]

[Chapter V; printed page 58; PDF page 110]

the necessity from the hypothesis that the reprobate themselves will it, are
the same necessities—a marvelous agreement of providence. There is therefore
no reason for the reprobate to despair in God's decree; rather, let them seek
the cause of despair in their own free will. And there is room for God's
complaints so long as the reprobate willingly bring upon themselves the
necessity of sinning, which they do every day. 2. Alas, what end or limit will
there be to their complaints, when the reprobate believe with the Jesuits that
connections involving ill-fated hypotheses are insuperable even to God—which
cannot be said without horror—and that God cannot by any dominion of
providence, of eternal liberty, of the supreme potter over his rational
creature, or of most powerful grace, convert more people than he converts, or
bend free human wills with a gentle inevitability wherever he wishes? Consult
Dr. Twisse.

[Marginal reference: Dr. Twisse, *Vindication of Grace against the Arminians*,
book 2, digression 2, chapter 14, charge 3, pages 110–111.]

**Objection 6.** “The permission of sin cannot possess certainty except
through middle knowledge, because God wills neither the act nor the malice.
2. Sins infallibly follow from permission, but not by inferential necessity.
God nevertheless foreknows them. Therefore he does so through middle
knowledge,” says Ruiz. Response 1. He knows them from infallibilizing motives,
apart from this knowledge. You yourself teach that GOD knows the futurition of
sins; you also teach that the sinner himself is subordinated to God as the
principal agent punishing the Israelites through that person's sins. But the
judge sees the act of punishing in his decree to punish. What need, then, is
there for middle knowledge? For God does not foreknow that he will punish the
Israelites before he has decreed to punish them.

[Marginal references and summary: Ruiz, 16, figure 8; Ruiz, *On Providence*,
tractate 2, disputation 11, section 2, figure 8. The permission of sin possesses
certainty apart from this fictitious middle knowledge.]

2. The certainty of sin through middle knowledge is empty. That is, first
comes the futurition of sin and the permission of future sin, and then GOD
decrees to permit sin. Thus GOD foreknows that sin will be future if it is
future, and foreknows that it will be future if it is future; but in this way
angels and human beings foreknow sin. 3. There is no hypothesis with which the
futurition of sin coheres necessarily. Rather, under each and every hypothesis
sin can follow or not follow—if you except the very futurition of sin, which
must necessarily be said. 4. It is false that GOD does not will the act of
sin, for God cannot fail to will that of which he is the author through the
true causality of the first cause, since God as first cause causes the act of
sin insofar as it is an act.

**Objection 7.** “Human beings know the very uncertainty and probable
inclination of a secondary cause toward this rather than that. For example, if
a blind drunk is permitted to be where there are many pits, he will fall into
a pit, and people know this. Therefore God's infinite knowledge much more
penetrates into every corner of the will and knows what it will will apart
from every decree.”

[Chapter V; printed page 59; PDF page 111]

Response 1. Human beings know only by conjectural knowledge that the blind man
will fall into a pit, for the angels who guard people can lead the blind drunk
elsewhere, and do lead him miraculously. Thus the inference from human
conjectural knowledge to GOD'S knowledge, by which what is not knowable is said
to be known, is unsound. 2. Potential inclination is known most exactly by God
in his decree which forms the inclinations of all causes. “Therefore free acts
are known in no such decree” is no inference at all.

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, 16, disputation 63, in the place cited.]

**Objection 8.** “It is impossible,” says Ruiz, “for God to know as future in
reality an act which precedes an act of God's will, or a decree to arouse the
will by offering a concurrence prepared through aids of grace; nevertheless,
he knows a free act which precedes the predetermining decree.” Response 1. A
future free act therefore precedes GOD'S conditional decree in conception,
not in reality. But what, I ask, does this mean? God says, “I conditionally
will that the free act be future.” Thus in reality God first wills that the
free act be future before he foreknows it will be future. But this overturns
middle knowledge, whose object precedes every act of the divine will. 2. If
GOD knows a free act antecedently to the decree by which that act is determined
to be future, then he must know that the free act will be future and has
futurition from itself, without any decree causing futurition. But this is
absurd, for he knows a nonbeing to be future. Nothing is future without GOD'S
will establishing the futurition of the thing.

[Marginal reference and summary: Ruiz on part 1, disputation 71, section 6,
figures 21–22. God knows nothing future apart from the decree causing its
futurition.]

**Objection 9.** “God snatches certain people from this life lest wickedness
should change their understanding. Therefore God foreknows the apostasy of
many if they should survive. Likewise, in Luke 16 the rich man accuses God's
providence because God had employed only suitable means, namely Moses and the
Prophets, which were not sufficient means. But there would be room for the
accusation if God had denied predetermining aid. There is no reason why God
would deny a preacher from hell because he had decreed to deny efficacious
aid, for in this way God would also have denied Moses and the Prophets to the
rich man's five brothers. Therefore God foreknew before the predetermining
decree that the sermon from hell would not profit the five brothers for
salvation; otherwise, he who provides every useful means would not have
omitted it,” says Ruiz. I answer: 1. Álvarez rightly says

and disputation 36, section 5, figures 2–3. Denial of an efficacious decree
before the means is no more an obstacle to providence than denial of a

1. The Arminians maintain that GOD exhorts or reproves people according to no

**Objection 11.** “If in his absolute will God foreknows Judas's betrayal, he

Judas's betrayal in his absolute permissive will, but this is an impious
inference: “Therefore God is necessitated by the intended end to execute the
means with pain,” as if he had made the decree imprudently. For he decrees
freely and wisely concerning both the end and the means to the end. 2. From
this reasoning God says, “I know Judas will be a betrayer if he is elected to
the apostolate, and therefore I elect him to the apostolate.” But this is in
reality the same as if God determined Judas's betrayal by an absolute will,
which overturns our adversaries’ principles. 3. Thus the apostolate and

**Objection 12.** Ruiz also argues as follows: “Whatever is conceived by the
mind to be can be asserted either to be so or not to be so; for a mental
proposition is capable of being assented to or dissented from. But the
coexistence of a free act with a hypothesis can be conceived by the mind even
before the decree. Therefore such propositions are assertoric and are known
by God.” I answer: 1. The major premise must be distinguished. Whatever is
conceived by the mind to be determinately can be asserted determinately either
to be so or not to be so. In this sense the major is true, but the minor is
false. For I deny that the coexistence of a free act with a hypothesis can be
conceived by a true conception conforming to the thing outside the mind as
something possessing determinate truth. Whatever is conceived by the mind to
be possible, if God freely decrees that it will be, can be asserted possibly
either to be so or not to be so. But this is not how the Jesuit understands
the major premise, for the conclusion is not what is denied, and there is more
in the conclusion than in the premises. 2. Assent or dissent can be given to
the assertion “the world is to be created” even before God decrees the creation
of the world. But that assent or dissent will be given to it as an assertion
of possible, not determinate, truth. Every assertion is capable of assent or
dissent according to the character of the things asserted—whether their truth
is possible or determinate.

**Objection 13.** “Before the decree this is true: ‘If Peter is placed in such
an order of things, he will sin,’ for in this prior state it has conformity
with the thing signified. The instant of time in which Peter sins merely
manifests this conformity; it does not produce it.” This is confirmed: “Since
a conditional proposition differs from an absolute one only in its condition,
the positing of the condition is sufficient to determine the truth. Thus, just
as determinate truth is on one side of the contradiction after the truth has
been determined by positing the condition, so it was determined at a time
prior to the condition's fulfillment.” The consequent is proved: “The truth
of a conditional differs from absolute truth only in its condition. Therefore,
once the condition has been posited, it is the same truth that existed before
the condition was posited. Hence dialecticians say that the truth of a
proposition about the past and future is proved through a truth *de inesse*—a
categorical truth—which, if asserted at that time, would be true. For example,
‘The Antichrist will sin’ is true because ‘The Antichrist sins here and now’ is
true.” Thus Ruiz, Vázquez, Arrúbal, Alcázar, and Pesantius.

[Marginal references: Ruiz, disputation 68, ibid.; Vázquez, on the *First
Part*, volume 1, disputation 67, question 14, article 13, chapter 3, figure 45;
Arrúbal, part 1, question 14; Alexander Pesantius, on *ST* I, question 14,
article 13, distinction 14.]

I answer. Before God's decree, “If Peter is placed in such an order of things,
he will sin” is true not determinately, but only possibly; for its sense is,
“If Peter is placed in such an order, it is possible that he will sin.” If the
decree to permit the sin has not yet been posited, Peter will not sin
determinately and certainly. It is only possible that he will sin—namely, if
God decrees to deny efficacious grace. But if God decrees to give Peter
efficacious grace, then Peter, placed in any order of things whatsoever, will
holily abstain from sin. Therefore this conformity with the thing is
conformity with a possible thing, not with a thing certainly future. 2. It is
false that “a conditional proposition differs from an absolute one only in its
condition and has the same determinate truth as the conditional.” For if the
discussion concerns an occasional rather than determining condition—as
temptation is in relation to Peter's sin—then it posits mere possibility, not
the determinate futurition of the thing. Thus “If Peter will be tempted, he
will sin” has only possible truth or falsity. For if Peter is tempted, he can
sin and can refrain from sin. But “If Peter is being tempted, he is sinning”
is a proposition determinately true or false. Therefore the argument simply
begs the question. In the same way, “It is possible for the world to be
created” is true even in the logical instant antecedent to God's decree. But
once the decree to create the world has been posited, “the world is now being
created,” and the creation exists in time determinately and necessarily—with
the necessity of the decree. 3. Although the truth of the thing is the same

fore the positing of the condition and after the positing of the condition, it
is nevertheless not the same with respect to determination if the condition
yet to be posited does not presuppose God's decree, while the condition once
posited does presuppose God's decree. Thus, if the condition is only yet to be
posited, the proposition has possible truth; if it has now been posited and
the decree is presupposed, the proposition has determinate truth. Ruiz presses
the point as follows: “Although a decree to concur with free choice—without
physical predetermination—is required beforehand in order that our free act
may be elicited, it is nevertheless unnecessary to presuppose that decree as
actually existing. It is enough that, in that logical instant, it be
presupposed as future; for the futurition of a cause suffices for its effect to

from it. Otherwise God could not have foreseen that the Philistines' crops
would be burned until Samson actually existed and actually tied fire to the

I answer: 1. For a free act truly to be elicited—that is, for the futurition
of a free act—a decree which is the cause of that futurition must be
presupposed as actual. For an effect in act, such as the true futurition of a
free act, requires beforehand a cause in act, because an effect truly
presupposes its cause as prior to itself, just as an effect in potency
presupposes a cause in potency. 2. If God sees the futurition of a free act,
while God's decree to concur with that free act does not yet actually exist,
then the creature's free act is future while God's decree can only come to be.
But that decree of God can come to be either freely from God or necessarily.
If freely, that decree of God can never come to be; for whatever God decrees
that a free agent will do, God can refrain from decreeing. But in that case the
creature's free act would be future in the complete absence of any decree of
God, whether existing or future. If God's decree is necessarily future, that
necessity would be either conditional or absolute. It is not conditional, for
whenever God decrees something necessarily, with necessity from a hypothesis,
that hypothesis depends upon God; but in this case the hypothesis would not
depend upon God. For: 1. Absolutely speaking, whatever God might decree,
according to the nature of freedom held in equilibrium between not willing
and willing, once every action of God has been posited, the futurition or
non-futurition of every free act—as it seems good to free creatures, not as it
seems good to God—precedes God's will. But God cannot will from a hypothesis
something that possesses true futurition antecedently to his will.

[Marginal summary: A determining decree of God is presupposed for absolute
futurition; for possible futurition a decree concerning the possibility of the
thing suffices.]

Yet, according to the Jesuits, all free acts are of this kind, possessing true
futurition antecedently to God's own free will. 2. Whenever God decrees
something with necessity from a hypothesis, that hypothesis depends upon God;
but the hypothesis here does not depend upon God, for a hypothesis is always
something contingent which also possesses futurition antecedently to God's
will. Nor can it be said that the creation of the free agent is the
hypothesis. Creation does indeed depend upon God, because he can refrain from
creating what he creates. For then God would have a decree like this: “I
decree to concur with Judas's will for killing himself, provided that I shall
have created Judas as a completely free agent.” But the creation of Judas's
freedom, or of Judas himself, is neither the condition nor the cause why Judas
kills himself. No reason can be imagined why the creation of a free agent
should be the condition of his willing this rather than of his not willing
this. Moreover, it is a wretched thing to affirm that God wills the futurition
of free acts with absolute necessity, for no fate more fatal than this could
be devised. 3. It is astonishing how brazenly the Jesuit fails to prove that
consequence so damaging to our cause. He infers: “If an actually existing
decree of concurrence is necessarily required beforehand for the futurition
of a free act, then God could not have foreseen that the Philistines' crops
would be burned until Samson actually existed.” He grandly pronounces, but
does not prove, that this follows. For in God's actual decree predetermining
that Samson's spirit should be stirred against God's sworn enemies, God saw
and foreknew from eternity that the Philistines' crops would be burned. We do
not require, for the futurition of that burning, God's predetermining
providence as it actually stirs Samson to burn the crops—we are not so
stupid. Rather, for the futurition of the burning we require God's decree
actually existing from eternity, which was the cause of that futurition
rather than its non-futurition. The Jesuit's argument does not militate
against this assertion of ours.

could not foreknow the conditionally future outcome.’ Genesis 43: ‘Could we
have known that he would say, Bring your brother with you?’ Therefore they
presuppose that such propositions can be asserted and are determinately true
or false. Nor is something said to be unknown unless it is in itself
affirmable and knowable. Thus no wise person would accuse someone else with
the words, ‘You do not know Peter by walking.’”

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On God's Knowledge*, disputation 68, section 5,
figures 1–2 and following.]

“For there is no meaning in this. Hence people often accuse themselves of not
having seized opportunities to act. Therefore they suppose that this good
would have conditionally happened to them if, on such an occasion, they had
done this or that. Likewise, when asked, ‘What if you had done this—what would
then have happened?’ they say, ‘We do not know; God knows.’ Therefore such
conditionals are knowable.” I answer: 1. The point in dispute, τὸ κρινόμενον, is
not established. We do not deny that conditional futures are knowable. They
are knowable, however, as possessing only possible truth; insofar as they have
determinate truth, they are knowable in God's decree connecting the condition
and the conditioned event. God knew that Joseph would say, “Bring your brother
with you,” if his brothers said that they had a brother at home, in the decree
by which from eternity he connected this condition and this conditioned event.
2. Although people often say, “If I had done this, the matter would have turned
out well,” they nevertheless do not know whether it would have turned out well
even if they had done this; for God had decreed a connection between every
excellent opportunity to undertake that matter and an outcome that was not at
all prosperous. 3. I say that God knows what would have happened if Joseph's
brothers had not sold Joseph, and also knows Joseph's uncoerced journey into
Egypt, in his decree by which he connected Joseph's non-sale and such an
outcome, even though Joseph's non-sale and that outcome would never be posited
in the world of created things. For according to our position, all wise
connections of things are absolutely decreed by God. But the Jesuits and
Arminians sacrilegiously snatch these connections away from God's decision;
they must then ascribe them to the dominion of fortune or fate.
