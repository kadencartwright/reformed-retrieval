VERSE 16. Do not be excessively righteous, nor seem to yourself to be wiser
beyond measure. Why should you bring desolation upon yourself?

VERSE 17. Do not be excessively wicked, nor be foolish. Why should you die
before your time?

VERSE 18. It is better that you grasp this; indeed, do not withdraw your hand
from this either. One who reveres God comes out from all these things.

VERSE 19. This wisdom gives strength to the wise person, more than ten rulers
who are in the same city.

A mean and a certain measure must be employed in all actions. It is maintained
whenever every particular is governed by the fear of God.

There are two parts: that a mean must be employed in affairs; and how we may
most successfully attain it.

VERSE 16. Do not be excessively righteous, nor seem to yourself to be wiser
beyond measure. Why should you bring desolation upon yourself?

Κατὰ ζῆλος (“According to zeal”). Solomon appears to have derived this
dissuasion from excessive righteousness from the preceding verse, by an
argument from the greater. For if the righteous person perishes in his own
righteousness, it is not prudent to overstep the measure in righteousness,
from which serious harm must necessarily follow. The person to be called
excessively righteous, however, is the one who, deceived by the appearance of
righteousness, admits things that bring danger upon him.

An excellent example of this is found in the man who was ordered by a Prophet,
at God's command, to wound the Prophet by striking him a blow. Under the
appearance and name of righteousness he refused to do it, and thereby brought
destruction upon himself when a lion tore him apart, 1 Kings 20. For it seemed
alien to him to wound a man who was not only innocent but also outstanding in
piety.

What Saul did was not dissimilar. Fearing that he should preserve nothing
alive when cutting down the Amalekites, he hoped that he could preserve some
of the fatter herd and flock to offer as a sacrifice to God. In this way, he
thought, he would not only avoid provoking God but would also do what would be
pleasing to him.

Timothy was likewise excessively righteous when, under the name of excessive
austerity in his manner of life, by which he might recall the luxuriant
Ephesians to moderation, he almost wasted himself away and weakened his
health. The Corinthians were likewise excessively righteous when they
continued to afflict the incestuous man after he had repented. Setting before
their eyes the intention of pleasing God, by their excessive severity they
incautiously advanced the devil's snares, 2 Corinthians 2:6–11.

In this kind of thing people formerly sinned who, under the name of subduing
the flesh, partly withdrew themselves from human society; partly abstained
from meat and fish, lay upon the ground, or slept upright against a wall.

To turn to matters that may be of use to us: if rulers were unjustly to demand
something from someone, and he refused to give it lest he share in the
injustice, though his refusal often causes him to lose everything he possesses
and even his life, he could rightly be called and considered excessively
righteous. Some think that Naboth sinned in this way, but falsely, since God's
law prohibited him from alienating his inheritance in such a way that he
would cut off from his heirs every hope of recovering it. Among us too are
found those who deny that anything should be given to robbers unless by that
gift they could redeem their life. Those who in any age introduce schisms into
the Church may rightly be counted in this number.

Solomon describes this excessive righteousness by its effect—namely, from an
excessive opinion of his own wisdom, by which, with hardened mind, he
tenaciously retains what he has once fashioned for himself and his own mind.
We must guard against that precipice. For in this way it happens that people,
moved by ambition, desire to overstep and leap beyond the customary manner

He further describes this vice from the accompanying penalty of desolation.
It is plainly evident from the foregoing examples that this penalty almost
always accompanies such a scruple of mind.

VERSE 17. Do not be excessively wicked, nor be foolish. Why should you die
before your time?

Solomon derives another caution from verse 15 by an argument from the
contrary, just as if he said: although the wicked sometimes prolong their life
by their wickedness, nevertheless take care not to derive from this the spirit
to act wickedly and to plunge yourself into every kind of wickedness, trusting
that, since one person prolonged his life by his wickedness, you will gain a
greater advantage by still greater and more monstrous wickedness. For it
usually happens that impunity for a crime drives many others into the same
crime. How much more does this happen when they not only get away with it
unpunished but are rewarded besides? An outstanding example of this occurs in
Genesis 4:23–24. God does not prolong the life of wicked people by means of
their wickedness so that others may thereby be encouraged to commit
wickedness, but so that by this patience they may be brought to repentance.

Here an admonition is needed, lest anyone seize upon the concession that it is
permitted to act wickedly provided one is not extraordinarily wicked. But in
this place the Prophet was content to refute what people were ready to infer
from verse 15. When we are forbidden to let the Sun set upon our anger, no one
is thereby given permission to be rashly angry until sunset; rather, if
someone has grown angry because he is overcome by common weakness, he must
endeavor to cast it off as soon as possible. It can further be answered that
the pious, even amid the greatest innocence, are capable of acting wickedly;
but care must only be taken that sin does not rule in their mortal body, which
is to act excessively wickedly.

Solomon also assigns the efficient cause of such abandoned wickedness:
inexperience, or gross and brutish ignorance. He gives it the name of folly,
from which everyone naturally recoils, in order to deter people from the
abandoned audacity of sinning.

He confirms this prohibition from its accompanying penalty, an untimely and
premature death. Here he very openly confronts the vain hope in which those
people delight who are inflamed to commit wickedness because they see that
others appear to have prolonged their lives by wickedness, hoping that they
too will prolong their lives in the same way. The Holy Spirit, on the contrary,
declares that the days of the wicked will be cut short and contracted. This is
exactly the point of the Law's threats against its transgressors, which are
repeated often and at length, just as the prolongation of this life is
frequently reiterated to those who keep the Law. See Proverbs 3:16.

Although it sometimes happens that wicked people grow old in their crimes,
nevertheless, ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ, “for the most part,” the life of the wicked is
short and contracted. For long life is often promised to the pious in the Law,
while it is easy to show that shortness of life is announced to the wicked.

VERSE 18. It is better that you grasp this; indeed, do not withdraw your hand
from this either. One who reveres God comes out from all these things.

Thus far Solomon has treated the first part, concerning the mean and measure
to be established in affairs. The second part follows, concerning how we may
most successfully attain it. In a word, it is the fear of God. One imbued with
it today will fall into neither direction, whether excessive righteousness or
wickedness. For through the fear of God a person both withdraws from evil and
cultivates and carries out what is good, Psalm 34:12, 15.

Solomon stimulates us to this fear of God from its effect: by its benefit
people are delivered from all the evils in which both excessive righteousness
and wickedness entangle them and keep them ensnared. David, having experienced
this in his deliverance from the hand of Achish, or Abimelech, makes a general
declaration about all who fear God, Psalm 34:10–11. For he teaches that those
who fear God will lack no good thing, so that the fear of God is a
πάγχρηστον, an all-purpose remedy for evils. And in Psalm 25:12 he teaches
the person who fears God the way in which he should walk.

From the effect of this splendid fear another effect arises: namely, that we
should grasp it with the hand, as it were, of our mind, and, once we have
grasped it, should never let it go. See Proverbs 4:13: “Grasp instruction
firmly and do not let it go, but guard it, because it is your life.”

VERSE 19. This wisdom gives strength to the wise person, more than ten rulers
who are in the same city.

Solomon illustrates this virtue by a comparison with lesser things, namely,
ten rulers equipped with great power and authority. For it confers more
strength upon the person endowed with it than ten rulers who devote all their
forces and power to the defense of only one city. If their power were divided
and apportioned, it would not have so much force to protect those to whose
defense it is applied.

Psalm 34:8 teaches how true and certain this is. There it teaches that the
Angel of God, attended by the arms of other Angels, encamps around those who
fear God in order to rescue them forcibly from evils. Indeed, one alone from
among those Angels is more than amply sufficient to destroy an infinite battle
line of armed soldiers. Song of Songs 3:7–8. For each of them is a mighty man
of God, trained for war—not to mention the wisdom with which those who fear
God are inwardly imbued by God in their minds. An outstanding example of this
exists in David, who feared God and managed everything prudently in war.

Almost everyone devotes himself to strengthening himself and his family by
making treaties, by affinity, by dependents, and by a multitude of friends, so
that if enemies ever attack them they may flee to these people's protection.
Yet how few are concerned about this citadel or tower of the Fear of God, in
which deliverance more certain, more readily available, and more immediate is
found! A surer and safer way to deliverance from all dangers is for someone to
have his guards constantly near to protect him than to be forced to summon
them from elsewhere when danger threatens. For that reason the fear of God,
which is always present and clings to us, is to be preferred to external
defenses. Therefore, the more those who hate us and contemplate our
destruction increase in number and power, the more diligently we should labor
to increase the fear of God. See the example in Isaiah 8:21–23.
