## Lambert van Velthuysen’s Libertine-Erastian position on the ministry,
government, and discipline of the church refuted

Satan is aware how necessary and effective a means ecclesiastical discipline
is for promoting the edification of the church; restraining the ungodly and
those who give scandal; curbing or eliminating errors, heresies, and schisms,
those ruinous evils of the church; and strengthening peace, concord, and
unanimity. He has therefore exerted every effort in every age either to abolish
this institution of Christ altogether or to corrupt it. Thus, while the church
was wailing in its cradle, he stirred up the pagan emperors against the church,
ecclesiastical power, and discipline through instruments he always found most
ready and most thoroughly prepared to perpetrate such a crime. Those utterly
wicked slanderers whispered that this ecclesiastical power could not coexist
with civil power; that if Christ reigned, the emperors could not securely
possess their dominion; and that once this ecclesiastical discipline and power
were established, civil government was finished. The history of the primitive
church and the apologies of the Christians—especially Origen’s *Against
Celsus*—attest this. But when that enemy, the Devil, perceived that by this
open method and armed force he could not attain his intended end, he entered
upon another

[Volume II; printed p. 2; ONB canvas 10]

way and devoted himself wholly to corrupting it. At length he gradually
prevailed by his wiles under the papacy and had attained his desire. But when
it seemed good to God Most High and Most Great to have mercy upon his church
and say to her: “Awake, awake, put on your strength, O Zion; put on your
beautiful garments. Shake yourself from the dust; free yourself from the bonds
of your neck, O captive daughter of Zion” (Isaiah 52:1–2), and, “Arise, shine,
for your light has come, and the glory of Jehovah has risen upon you” (Isaiah
60:1), and to lead her out of the Cimmerian darkness of the papacy—then that
most hostile enemy of the church also labored by every means he could either
to deprive the church of every distinct government and distinct discipline,
or to corrupt it in practice or through human additions and institutions.
Nor, until now, has Satan lacked instruments: men for the most part worldly
and corrupt, courtly parasites devoted to luxury and licentiousness, ignorant
of the Spirit and caring little for spiritual things. They have lent their
efforts to the church’s enemy in stripping her of her power and privileges.

Therefore all to whom Christ’s church is dear, and who desire to keep the
faith pledged to Christ, ought to watch with the greatest diligence and take
care that Christ’s kingdom and church suffer no harm here from malicious
enemies who daily plot her ruin. At the signal they ought to join unanimously
in checking all the savagery and assault of these combatants. No one will deny
that this is to be expected of all faithful sons of the church, and especially
of pastors, who have been appointed sentinels and watchmen. No one, therefore,
can rightly find fault with me because I have wished to contribute all my care
and such effort as I have toward averting this evil now threatening the church
and preserving the church’s right and all her privileges safe and intact.

A vernacular book by L. Velthuysen recently came into my hands,

[Volume II; printed p. 3; ONB canvas 11]

on idolatry and superstition. In its preface I saw very many assertions that
appeared to me to overthrow altogether all ecclesiastical government and all
discipline—not by mining tunnels, indeed, but openly—and that I could not read
without righteous indignation. Who would not wonder with me that Velthuysen,
a Dutchman and, as I hear, bound by oath to defend the Reformed Church and all
the church’s privileges—indeed, an elder of the French-Dutch Church—should have
wished to make such things public, and to make known to everyone what grievous
hostility this most ungrateful son, most corrupt member, and most hostile
governor or elder bears here against his mother, the church? For shame! Is
this a fit presbyter, who goes about to overthrow all ecclesiastical
government? Is this a sound and uncorrupted member of the church, who rejects,
condemns, despises as worthless, and tramples underfoot both the Belgic and the
French Confessions of faith—indeed, the confessions of all the other churches
as well? But when I examined the book itself, I was dumbfounded. For there—to
say nothing about the many other matters that do not relate to the subject now
before us—I found not only things agreeing with what had been said in the
preface and containing some appearance of proof, but also very many things
that seemed quite directly and openly to aim at the very throat of the
ministry. I wondered what the man intended in wishing to declare such open war
upon the church herself. If the ministry is removed and all ecclesiastical
discipline abolished, what, I ask, will the church be? How long will Christ
have a church on earth? Surely, if all the things that give Velthuysen so
little pleasure were once removed, Christ’s church would be finished and the
Devil would very quickly attain his desire. But Christ, the church’s
bridegroom, head, and king, lives. Sitting in heaven and hearing some say, “Let
us tear apart their bonds and cast their thick cords away from us,” the Lord
laughs and

[Volume II; printed p. 4; ONB canvas 12]

mocks them. “Then he will address them in his wrath and trouble them in the
heat of his anger, and he will break them with an iron rod; he will shatter
them like a potter’s vessel” (Psalm 2:3, 4, 5, 9).

To refute Velthuysen’s Libertine-Erastian position set out in his book, we will
follow this method. First, we will see what his position is. Next, we will
investigate whether his position agrees with that of the Reformed churches.
Finally, we will set out and confirm the orthodox position, and in the
appropriate places refute the apparent objections made against it in this
book.

As for the first task, it is unnecessary for me to repeat all his statements
here. It is enough to reduce them to a few principal headings, as it were, and
state them briefly.

He freely admits on pages 5 and 6 of his preface that, with respect to the
pastoral office, the right and nature of the church, and their appendages, he
appears to hold an opinion different from the common position of the doctors.
These doctors, as he supposes, do not penetrate to the heart of the matter,
entertain confused and tangled conceptions, and speak about the subject in
such a way that it is impossible for those relying on their foundations either
to defend themselves against the papists or to refute their opponents’
objections. But he speaks about the Reformed theologians just as those who
nurture some monstrosity within are accustomed to speak, clearing a path by
this means in certain people’s minds for the reception of their new dogmas. We
have heard Wolzogen, this Velthuysen’s pastor, pronounce upon the orthodox
theologians in precisely the same way. But we ought to be little moved when we
hear men of this kind treat as worthless the Lord’s heroes and those who have
rendered the best service to Christ’s church. He also says that he thought he
had served the cause when he set out his position on this subject. I do not
know what books he had previously published about this matter, for I have not
yet happened to see any appear publicly under his name. Mr Van der Wagen,
indeed,

[Volume II; printed p. 5; ONB canvas 13]

ascribes to him the book *Lucius Antistius Constans’ Singular Treatise on the
Right of Ecclesiastics*, printed at Alerbopolis by Caius Valerius Pennatus,
where everything is fictitious; but others doubt the attribution. And now, as
I see, he acknowledges as his own offspring the vernacular book entitled *Het
Predick-ampt* [*The Preaching Office*], and so forth. From this we may learn
that he is much bolder now than he was before: he now openly professes what he
was previously ashamed to acknowledge. But how well he has served Christ’s
cause, the church’s cause, and the truth defended by the orthodox in both
these books—if he is also the author of the earlier one, which, as I hear, he
denies—is easy to show. God willing, I shall later show how profane, false, and
absurd a position he maintains in them.

But we proceed in setting out the position he maintains here. After making
certain statements about the papists’ position and the absurd consequences
with which they strive to burden ours, he joins the papists in attacking our
men. He strives to press our cause with the same absurd consequences used by
the papists, and so rushes to the opposite extreme, as though, in this
question, there were no middle position between the papists and the Socinians,
Remonstrants, and Erastians. What? Because we do not acknowledge that the
church has dominion over consciences, since Paul himself says, “Not that we
exercise dominion over your faith, but that we are ministers of your joy” (2
Corinthians 1:24), must no ministerial power in matters concerning conscience
therefore be ascribed to her? Because we do not acknowledge an infallible
authority in the church, must it therefore be said that she has absolutely no
ministerial power or authority to determine cases, decide questions, make
decrees, establish canons, and so forth? Because we do not say with the
papists that the ordination of ministers is a sacrament, must we therefore
acknowledge with the Socinians that it is nothing, and that there are now no

[Volume II; printed p. 6; ONB canvas 14]

church officers separated by divine right for that ministerial office, or
that this distinct order of ministers was not divinely instituted? This is how
he argues with the papists against the orthodox. Because he does not see
(perhaps because he refuses to open his eyes) how our theologians defend
themselves against the papists, he embraces the position of the Socinians,
Remonstrants, and Erastians, and thus

> Desiring to avoid Charybdis, he falls into Scylla.

But let us see what assertions he lays down.

On page 22 of the preface he rightly asserts that God has given no one on earth
power or dominion over consciences. We readily grant that neither political
nor ecclesiastical power can make laws that are able by themselves, directly,
and properly to bind consciences. Yet this tells no more against political
power than it does against ecclesiastical power. Thus he draws a very bad
conclusion in the same place: “There is no power in the church to make
confessions of faith or decrees, especially new ones, that ought to serve
others as rules; and, insofar as conscience is concerned and they are
considered outside a political condition, individual Christians are free to
embrace or reject those decrees.” If he means a regulating rule and not a
merely regulated one, we readily grant him this too. For we acknowledge God’s
word to be the one supreme and infallible standard of faith and morals. Yet we
assert that the church has ministerial power to make a subordinate rule
regulated according to God’s word, and we will prove this in its place. Nor do
we say that these canons and decrees, insofar as they are made by the church,
bind consciences directly and formally, but only insofar as they agree with
God’s law. Nevertheless, it certainly does not follow, as he repeatedly
insinuates, that these canons or decrees do not concern conscience or the
inner person. He confuses these distinct things and so treats the matter
without distinction. He promises to explain later what he means by a

[Volume II; printed p. 7; ONB canvas 15]

political condition. Yet this must be noted: he supposes that in that
condition the decrees of synods bind members of the church and touch their
consciences. But whatever that condition may be, we will not grant that in it
human decrees can bind consciences directly and formally. He makes similar
statements on pages 23 and 24 of the preface, when he says: “The decrees of
synods, considered outside the political condition, are nothing other than a
mere declaration of the faith of those who make them, and possess no force or
authority of a law that touches conscience.” This is confused enough, for he
does not explain what it means to touch conscience. If he thinks it is the
same as binding conscience properly, formally, and directly, we have already
said that this can happen in no condition. For neither in the constituted
church (which he perhaps means by the political condition) nor in a church
being constituted do the canons of a synod, as such, have the power to bind
consciences directly and formally. Hence he says it follows in the same place
that “the church has no judges, no tribunal, no accused, and no plaintiffs with
respect to consciences, and thus no ecclesiastical power at all.” We do not
deny that ecclesiastical power differs greatly from political power, and that
the things he has listed, taken politically—that is, as they exist in civil
courts—have no place in the church. But we say that, considered
ecclesiastically, they are also in their own manner to be attributed to the
church. And although, as stated, the church or her governors have no dominion
over consciences, they nevertheless most truly have spiritual power, as we
will make evident. He repeats these same things on page 61 and on page 464 of
the book.

Moreover, on page 22 of the preface he affirms that “it cannot be demanded
that the obedience of faith be rendered to these decrees, confessions, and so
forth, whether by way of command, of exhortation to render due obedience, by
reason of any subjection owed, or even by reason of any promise given.”

[Volume II; printed p. 8; ONB canvas 16]

And why? Because, he says, “No one can subject his conscience to another in
such a way that he can promise to persevere in that position, insofar as
conscience is concerned.” But if the decrees of a synod conform to God’s word,
the members of the church are bound in all these ways to render obedience:
Christ commands them to obey their officers in the Lord, and they promise to
do so, at least implicitly, when they are received into the church as members.
Therefore all are bound to subject themselves to decrees of this kind and to
remain to the end in the true position they have once embraced, because God’s
law is always the same—an unvarying, immutable, and firm standard. Hence it is
evident how false are the further statements on page 23 of the preface, where
he says: “He thinks that one who, having changed his religious position,
abandons the society he previously joined is by no means breaking faith; and
that to excommunicate such a person by an excommunication having the form of a
penalty is absurd and in no way agrees with the foundation of our
Reformation.” For Christ’s law teaches, as we will show later, that people of
this kind who defect from the faith and give scandal are to be marked by
ecclesiastical censure. We will prove that the church borrowed this power to
impose ecclesiastical penalties or censures not from the pope, as he insinuates
in the same place, but from Christ. I wish he would explain these two kinds of
excommunication, for he denies that in the case just mentioned the church can
excommunicate someone by an excommunication having the form of a penalty.
Therefore she can do so by another excommunication. Therefore there is another
excommunication: let him explain both. We acknowledge no excommunication or
censure that is not distinguished from a civil penalty. But he explains his
view of excommunication more clearly on pages 35 and 36 of the preface. From
the premises “that the church exists by the voluntary consent and association
of many Christians; and that no Christian has any power, right, or

[Volume II; printed p. 9; ONB canvas 17]

authority over matters of faith, or to judge another as disobedient,
erroneous, or heretical in the manner of a sentence, insofar as spiritual
things are concerned,” he concludes “that the church has no greater right, and
cannot act as judge, possess a tribunal, or impose a penalty. Rather, whatever
is done by the church in a matter of this nature concerning a sinner or one
who errs in articles of faith is nothing other than the action of a private
society, by which it avoids such a person and simply declares why it regards
that person or church as such.” In short, this means that the church has no
power of excommunication because no private individual can excommunicate or
judge another, and the church is composed of private individuals. It is also
evident from this what he understands by the excommunication he attributes to
the church: namely, nothing other than what any private individual can do—to
avoid one living “outside” and to declare the reason.

Although this is not the appropriate place to refute these things, I cannot
fail to note that an Anabaptist could use the same means to conclude that the
magistrate has no power. For no private individual has the right or power to
judge another guilty, condemn him, or sentence him to death, while the
commonwealth is composed of private individuals—that is, it arises from the
voluntary consent and association of many private persons. Therefore neither
will the commonwealth or its governors be permitted to judge and condemn a
private person. What will Velthuysen answer to this argument in order to
preserve the magistrates’ power safe and intact? He must either retract his
position on the church’s power or aim at the throat of magistracy, or political
power.

Furthermore, on page 25 of the preface he does not deny that the primitive
church had governors and governed people, and that it was governed by certain
laws and rules. But he says that “this authority of governing and commanding
did not flow from the nature of the gospel and was not introduced by the
teaching of the gospel, but arose from that natural

[Volume II; printed p. 10; ONB canvas 18]

liberty which the worldly power had left to those assemblies, or which they
had assumed for themselves by right, so long as the political magistrates had
not undertaken the care of the church but had persecuted it.” He repeats these
same things on pages 53, 56, and 32 of the preface, and on page 462 of the book
says that the church borrowed her government from the magistrate or from the
natural liberty the magistrate left to private Christians. On pages 25, 33,
and 56 of the preface, he illustrates this by the example of a son cast out by
his parents, who is permitted to do many things that otherwise belonged to him
by no right. But an illustration is not a proof. Nevertheless, his position
rests on this single illustration or analogy. As anyone can now readily see,
it denies that any government, power, or discipline belongs to the church
insofar as she is the church, but says that it does so only accidentally when
the magistrate casts aside all care of the church and persecutes her. It also
denies that the church has any power or authority granted by Christ to conduct
her business and preserve herself pure and safe from internal evils.

Hence on page 56 of the preface he infers that “the church cannot claim or
seize this power for herself when the magistrates undertake the care of the
church.” Thus no doubt can now remain about the meaning of this
anti-presbyterian elder, nor about the circumstances and duration in which,
according to him, the church may employ this authority. See also page 463 of
the book, where he draws the same inference.

If you ask about the objects with which this ecclesiastical power is occupied,
he answers on page 26 of the preface that, at least after the death of
infallible men, it is occupied only with certain bodily things that are
nevertheless instituted and directed toward the advancement of spiritual
things. He repeats this on page 51 of the preface. From this we may note two
things in passing.

1. He calls everything bodily that does not concern conscience or faith. For
on page 51 he sets these two things against

[Volume II; printed p. 11; ONB canvas 19]

one another. Thus he recognizes nothing spiritual or touching conscience
except that which exercises dominion over conscience, for he acknowledges no
middle ground.

2. Hence he acknowledges that in the time of the apostles the consciences of
church members were subject to pastors or apostles. For he insinuates here
that before the death of infallible men ecclesiastical power handled things
other than these bodily, political, external, and worldly matters—namely,
spiritual ones. But these concerned conscience, and we heard the apostle say
above that he had no dominion over consciences. It is therefore evident that,
although we say that ecclesiastical power is still exercised about matters
concerning conscience, it by no means follows that the church or
ecclesiastical governors have dominion over people’s consciences.

On pages 26 and 27 of the preface he says: “Ecclesiastical government is
political, not spiritual, because there are now no infallible men through whom
alone such government can be exercised.” Similarly, on pages 462 and 463 of
the book he says: “Properly speaking, ecclesiastical government is not
spiritual but civil and worldly. It must therefore be governed by the same
rules, and in this respect subjection and coercion belong to it. It is
therefore supported by carnal power, and the disobedient are corrected by
bodily penalties, shame, and so forth.” We see, then, what he determines
concerning ecclesiastical government: namely, that it is not distinct from
political government but of the same nature, and is not spiritual. We will
later prove the opposite; here we only point out his position.

On page 26 of the preface he grants that “the first form of government among
the Christian churches was nearly one and the same, and was instituted after
the pattern of synagogue government.” It can readily be demonstrated that
there was one and the same ecclesiastical government in substance in all the
constituted churches. But as to its institution after the pattern of the

[Volume II; printed p. 12; ONB canvas 20]

synagogues, this is not established from Scripture. Even if we granted it, it
would do nothing for Velthuysen’s scheme, for, as we will hear, he concludes
that we are not bound to observe the same kind of government. We, however, do
not embrace or regard the form of government mentioned in the New Testament
because, or insofar as, it was instituted after such a pattern. We do so
insofar as, and because, it was instituted by Christ and the apostles for the
edification of the church and to be observed to the end. But what does he say
about this form of government? On page 26 of the preface he says that “he does
not think anyone can demonstrate by compelling arguments that those parts of
government which can be separated from the spiritual and necessary end of
Christians were instituted by divine right and bind posterity as laws.” He
does not explain his meaning clearly enough here, for he does not say what he
means by the parts of government that can be separated from this end. On page
27 he indicates his meaning more clearly, saying: “Individual churches are
free to choose whether they wish to be governed by bishops, by classes and
synods, or in another way, just as people enjoy this freedom with respect to
forms of political government.” It is therefore evident that, according to
Velthuysen, there is no particular form of church government by divine right.
We will prove the opposite with sufficiently strong arguments. He makes
similar statements on page 32 of the preface, where he says: “We have as much
freedom with respect to the form of government as every political society has
concerning its form of political government, and the form they find most
useful is the best.”

Furthermore, on page 27 of the preface he concludes “that all those parts of
government which were introduced or instituted because political magistrates
cast off the care of the church or persecuted her ought to cease.” That is,
when political magistrates undertake the care of the church, governing the
church belongs exclusively to them, just as governing the commonwealth does,
and not to ecclesiastical governors.

[Volume II; printed p. 13; ONB canvas 21]

For, as stated, he says that no power or authority belongs to the church as
such, and that all the power she ever exercises is political and is exercised
only about external temporal matters concerning the body, not the soul. Hence
he consequently refuses to regard excommunication as a spiritual censure, but
makes it the same as the expulsion of some craftsman from the society of which
he was previously a member. So he says on page 54 of the preface, and thus he
makes excommunication something purely and entirely civil, affecting only the
body, not the soul and conscience. A man who speaks this way holds thoroughly
carnal and unworthy conceptions of ecclesiastical government.

Similarly, on page 48 of the preface he insinuates “that excommunication and
suspension from the Holy Supper are not an ecclesiastical censure or penalty,
and that it is no more lawful to bar anyone from the Supper than from shared
prayers or hearing the word.” The church will then have absolutely no
discipline and no censures. About what, therefore, will that power he sometimes
appears to attribute to her be occupied?

Likewise, on page 55 of the preface he says: “The church can do absolutely
nothing without the aid of the secular power, nor can she preserve herself
pure and free from schisms, scandals, and contempt.” We will later show the
opposite: namely, that ecclesiastical discipline is in itself complete for
attaining its ends, provided that it is properly applied and employed.

On page 52 of the preface he distinguishes between “what the church does
considered simply as such, and what she does by virtue of the covenant and the
mutual pledge of members. By natural right these members have power to make
contracts about certain matters and act concerning them, but they cannot act
upon or concerning spiritual things.” So he says. On page 63 of the preface he
similarly says that “ecclesiastical power springs from natural right, rests on
it, and stands upon mutual promises and covenants.” As if these things could
not coexist with positive divine right! But we will speak in the proper place
about this covenant upon which certain men, Velthuysen among them, wish all

[Volume II; printed p. 14; ONB canvas 22]

ecclesiastical power in the apostolic church to rest.

No less absurd are his statements on pages 33 and 34 of the preface: namely,
“that the members of a church are free to publish the confession of faith on
which they agree, and to choose teachers who will regulate the members in
doctrine according to this confession and administer the sacraments to them;
and that it is repugnant to the nature and origin of this pastoral office for
one elected as pastor to teach things contrary to this confession, as if he
had received from God the power, authority, or commission”—as he puts
it—“to teach those things he judges to agree with God’s word, and to do so as
a pastor, since no one has this office except through the mediation of men,
and is limited by those same men to such a confession.” Behold the true
character of this elder. He wishes the church first to teach the pastor what
he ought to teach her. Whether that confession conforms to God’s word or not
makes no difference; the elected pastor is bound to retain the prescribed
doctrine, but not to declare the whole will of God. O how wide a door he opens
here for the eventual introduction of atheism! But here I only state his
position; later I will refute it, although merely stating it is certainly more
than enough to refute it. But what will an elected pastor do where no such
confession is prescribed? “He is indeed free from a pledge,” Velthuysen says on
page 35, “yet he has no other power, authority, or kind of commission than
others elected by the people possess.” That is, he has no power or authority
from God, but receives all of it from the electing people. This is the
orthodox Velthuysen, whose breast is so tenacious of orthodoxy.

On page 315 and following of his book, he acknowledges that under the Old
Testament ecclesiastical governors or ecclesiastical courts possessed power

[Volume II; printed p. 15; ONB canvas 23]

to judge matters concerning religion. We will make some observations about
these in the appropriate place. But on page 320 and following he says: “In
the present, contrary condition matters are different, and no such power
belongs to ecclesiastical ministers or synods.” We will later see the arguments
with which he confirms this position.

We have seen Velthuysen’s position on the ecclesiastical power of jurisdiction,
as it is commonly called. We must examine whether he holds a sounder opinion
about the power of order, as they call it. In his book he abolishes all of
this as well.

For in very many places he openly affirms that pastors possess no authority in
announcing the gospel and do not serve as ambassadors for Christ. See pages
275, 278, 279, 282, and 331. There is no reason to quote his words here; they
will be examined later.

Indeed, on page 273 and following he charges with superstition all who esteem
the preaching of pastors highly or value it more than private reading or a
private individual’s declaration of the same truth. I cannot recount here all
the words with which he scorns and tramples upon this institution of Christ;
they will be called to examination in their place.

Indeed, he goes about abolishing all public proclamation of the word and
wishes nothing peculiar in this work to be attributed to pastors. See pages
289, 302, and 307 of the book, with many others; but more about these later.
Furthermore, he denies that the proclamation of the word by pastors is God’s
moral means or instrument for converting his elect. So he says on pages 323
and 324. We will see how false this is.

Similarly, in the same place he does not wish private persons to avoid one who
has not been lawfully called.

As for the sacraments, on pages 313 and 314 he says that persons set apart or
consecrated are not necessarily required to administer them. But we will hear
his arguments

[Volume II; printed p. 16; ONB canvas 24]

in their place. He has similar statements in the preface, as we will hear
immediately.

What then? Is it not sufficiently evident from these things that he wishes to
abolish the ministry itself? But we will hear more. On pages 28, 29, and 30 of
the preface, he asserts that there is now no mission, authorization, or setting
apart of ministers to administer the word and sacraments, because it would
then follow that the power to send or set apart must belong to one church
alone, just as the power of appointing all ecclesiastical ministers formerly
belonged to the Jerusalem church. He does not clearly indicate what he means
by the Jerusalem church, but he probably means the church under the New
Testament. Yet although the apostles ordained other ministers at Christ’s
command, and these in turn ordained others, we do not read—nor will Velthuysen
prove—that this right belonged to the Jerusalem church alone. But he says that
if we assert this, we must appeal to succession. But then what? Will it follow
that pastors today are not pastors unless they can demonstrate this succession
through every step all the way from the apostles? There is no such inference.
Velthuysen undoubtedly descended from Adam, yet I can scarcely believe that he
can reckon every step of his descent. Nevertheless, he is a man. But on page
30 of the preface he proceeds and concludes that “there is now no mission,
appointment, or consecration of ministers, and everything that appears to be
such is nothing other than certain rules devised by Christian prudence.
Consequently, there is no spiritual power or authority in the church, nor any
spiritual, holy, set-apart, or divinely appointed person who, by virtue of
God’s institution, is invested with power to administer sacred things, so that
another not set apart in this way is not permitted to preach the word or

He acknowledges that ministers are indeed to be appointed, but only for the
sake of order. See page 325 of the book and page 63 of the preface. Thus he
asserts nothing other than what the Socinians say, as we will later make
evident.

And lest we forget these things, on pages 61, 62, and 63 of the preface he
repeats certain principal points and says: “The Reformed churches possess
absolutely no power to resolve controversies of faith by decrees that bind
conscience”—that is, as we saw above, decrees that concern conscience in any
way—“and with respect to this obligation, the decrees and judgments”—those of
synods, namely—“are nothing other than declarations of the position held by
those who were personally present in the assembly. With respect, however, to
temporal things and the covenant of members and governors concerning certain
bodily or worldly matters, which Christians can rightfully claim for
themselves from the remnants of their natural liberty, notwithstanding that
they live under a political magistrate, or on account of the magistrates’
persecution, these courts,” he says, “possess judicial

[Volume II; printed p. 18; ONB canvas 26]

power, the power of punishing and rewarding, in the matters they can handle,
but it does not touch spiritual things or conscience. Therefore we assert,”
he says, “that there is neither power nor right in the church, by any authority
of governors, judges, or teachers, to prescribe to others what they ought to
believe. We acknowledge,” he says, “no excommunication pronounced with
authority or as if by judges; no keys except the keys of doctrine; no authority
in teaching; no commission; no ambassadors sent by Christ who have some charge
laid upon them by reason of which we are bound to believe them rather than
others. We acknowledge order,” he says, “for avoiding confusion, so that it is
not lawful for everyone to discharge the office of teaching. We speak and
think similarly about administering the sacraments: namely, that consecrated
persons are not needed to administer them, although for the sake of order it
is necessary that some be chosen to perform this work.”

From these things Velthuysen’s true position is abundantly evident. It may be
briefly stated as follows: that the church as such has no power or authority
in spiritual matters that concern conscience; that synods cannot exercise any
power in these matters by making decrees or canons or deciding controversies
of faith; that ministers cannot exercise any spiritual ecclesiastical
discipline; that excommunication is not a spiritual censure; that pastors may
not bar anyone from the Holy Supper; that they have no keys except the keys of
doctrine; that by reason of ordination or setting apart it is not peculiar to
ministers to administer the word and sacraments; that in administering the word
they possess no authority; that it is no more necessary to hear them than
private persons; that they are not Christ’s ambassadors; that for this office
they enjoy no peculiar calling and are set apart by no

[Volume II; printed p. 19; ONB canvas 27]

special ordination through the laying on of hands, but are appointed by men
only for the sake of order; that when they are elected they must conform
themselves to prescribed formulas or confessions and propose nothing else in
public preaching, however conformable it may be to God’s word; that whatever
power belongs to the church in any way is temporal, carnal, or worldly, wholly
occupied with matters concerning the body, and is thus part of political
power, flowing from natural right and mutual covenant, when the political
magistrates renounce all care of the church or even persecute her (which was
the rationale and origin of government in the primitive church); that when
people submit to an established order and promise obedience—this, as far as I
can gather, is Velthuysen’s political condition—it is not lawful to refuse
obedience in any case, for he makes no exception; that outside this case or
condition, obedience or subjection can be required by no right, since whatever
is then decreed is only a declaration of the position of those who make such
decrees; that the power exercised by a private church by reason of covenant
ought now to cease when political magistrates wish to undertake the care of
the church; and finally, that ecclesiastical discipline cannot attain its ends
without the aid of the secular arm.

What is to be done with this Libertine-Socinian-Erastian-Remonstrant, who shows
how insolent a talent he possesses for tearing up all the foundations of the
church’s polity, discipline, and government—indeed, of the ministry and divine
institutions as well—and for overthrowing them by his bare denials and
assertions, supported by no arguments or by utterly ridiculous ones? Shall I
say that he displays this talent, or that he parades it? It would certainly be
a long task to prove more fully everything this man denies. In the space of a
single hour, one person can deny more than several learned men can confirm and
demonstrate in the space of many

hours—indeed, of days.
