3. But Castellio especially presses this point: “If Christ kills sin in
death itself, why does he not instead accomplish this in life, to the

1. But the more modest Paul said, “Who has been God's counselor?” Who
appointed Castellio as God's accountant or auditor, to command or demand
the times and moments of his operations, “which the Father has placed in
his own power,” Acts 1:7?

To come more closely to the matter, by the same right—or rather the same
audacity—let Castellio ask why the householder made “those laborers who
entered the vineyard around the eleventh hour,” or near the end of the
eleventh hour, as Lucas Brugensis explains the passage, “equal to those who
bore the burden and heat of the day.”

That murmurer received an answer, and let Castellio receive the same:
“Friend, is it not lawful for me to do what I wish with what is mine?” Let
him also ask why Christ did not call the thief to repentance before he was
about to depart from human life, “to the greater glory,” as Castellio puts
it, “of both his Father and himself, and as a more effective example to move
others to imitation.” In Castellio's judgment, the thief would have served
both ends better if he had returned to better fruit long before. Yet it
seemed good to Christ to do otherwise, and we calmly rest in his good
pleasure, since he knows best what is most suited to his own glory and the
benefit of others.

But Castellio will say that the case of that thief and that of our dying
believers are very different. Though the thief was already close to death,
his body was sound and his mind whole, and so he produced a sufficiently
clear confession of both his repentance and faith for God's glory and the
imitation of others. Our dying believers, however, with bodies half dead
and minds numb, neither perceive nor understand; they are all but dead
already. But, my good man, not everyone who dies exhausted by age or disease
is like this. We have said that many remain self-possessed to their final
breath; they breathe forth God, praise him distinctly, and at the boundary
of death or in death's very shadow display illustrious examples of faith
and godliness, just as the thief did, for God's glory and the benefit of
everyone standing by. Even if many others are in a different condition,
examples for imitation can be taken from what they did in their preceding
life. Or others are admonished by the very fact that, because of the
violence of disease, the sparks of life in the dying barely flicker as they
approach extinction. While still living and well, they should therefore
“work out their salvation before the night comes, when no one can work.”

As for those lying in such a condition, whatever God can or even wills to

work in and through them at that time, let us leave it entirely to him, as
he sees most fitting for his will and his glory.
