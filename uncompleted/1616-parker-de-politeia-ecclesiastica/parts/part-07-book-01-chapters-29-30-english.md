CHAPTER XXIX.

The Opponents’ Second Objection: That England Is Not Bound to Imitate the
Reformed Churches.

There follows a second objection against imitating the transmarine reformation
in discipline. It is said that England is bound by no tie to imitate the
reformed Churches, even if it is granted that they by no means approve the
English polity. Thus John Whitgift says: “England is not bound to the example of
either France or Scotland. Would that both, if it so pleased God, were in the
state and condition in which England is as regards religion.” Thus John Bridges:
“We live by laws, not examples. We are not bound by the example of the primitive
Church, much less by that of the reformed Churches which follow the primitive
Church.” Not to linger over each man, Thomas Brightman states the Hierarchists’
position succinctly: “We do not see why the reformed Chur—”

“—ches ought not rather to imitate our form of government than we theirs, since
we are inferior to them in no respect.” Yet Brightman has not fully encompassed
their position in these words. It must be added that they are accustomed not
only to prefer England to the other reformed Churches, but even to prefer it in
such a way that, in comparison with it, they presume almost to condemn all the
others, although they vigorously conceal this and profess the contrary.

For when George Downham makes the Presbyterian reformation guilty of the Aerian
stain, which he calls heretical and contrary to God’s Word, does he not thereby
condemn the reformed Churches as though they were heretical and rebels against
God’s Word? William Whitaker has this: “But if presbyters receive their office
from a Bishop, then the presbyters in the reformed Churches have no
jurisdiction.”

What does Downham say to this? He certainly has nothing with which he can make a
solid reply; hence, in one place, he tells the reformed Churches to mind their
own affairs. He speaks as follows: “Let the reformed Churches be excused by
necessity because their ministers are not called and ordained by a Bishop. But
if that is less pleasing, I do not wish to prejudice my case; let the reformed
Churches defend themselves.” Far be it from me either to call this boasting by
England good, or to say that this condemnation—or at least this desertion—of the
reformed Churches should not be strongly condemned.

First, in Laodicea, the type of England, this is expressly condemned: that it
thinks itself in need of no one. So it happens here, when England proclaims that
it has no need to imitate any Church.

Second, in Philadelphia, the type of the reformed Churches, nothing is rebuked;
everything is commended, including, among other things, discipline, which is
signified by the key of David, Revelation 3:7. In England, by contrast—which is
the antitype of Laodicea because of its corrupt discipline—everything is
rebuked, with no mention of praise, except that in doctrine Christ displayed
himself there as a faithful and true witness.

Third, when England is reformed at the sounding of the seventh Angel,
Revelation 11:15, no mention of the living creatures is found in it. This is
contrary to the usual practice. In other well-constituted Churches the living
creatures are introduced by their own name in chapters 4:9, 5:8, and 7:11.
Indeed, the same is true in the reformed Churches themselves of which we speak:
when their reformation is treated, the living creatures are introduced by name
as being in heaven, as in chapters 14:3 and 19:4.

I ask, therefore, why they are not named in the English reformation, while only
the twenty-four elders, who represent the holy assembly of the faithful, are
named. Is it because the living creatures bear the type of those ministers who
are approved by God, whereas our English ministers wander so far from the
divine institution that the Holy Spirit deliberately avoided bestowing the name
of living creatures upon them? Whatever the case, some defect certainly seems
to be signified here in the English Church which is not found in the other
reformed Churches.

CHAPTER XXX.

The Third Objection: That Discipline Was Introduced into the Reformed Churches
in a Perverse Manner.

The third objection argues from the manner in which discipline was introduced
first into the Genevan Church and then from it into other Churches. Those who
are themselves the most perverse of all men shout that this manner was full of
the most crafty seduction and the most perverse rivalry. Richard Bancroft rails
against Beza because he had said that the Gospel was wonderfully restored at
Geneva, and he threatens that the memory of this miracle will last longer than
the space of nine days.

He means that in this way a perpetual mark of disgrace was branded upon Geneva.
He affirms that this action displeases him, just as does the expulsion of the
other Swiss tyrants, because in it not only, as Melanchthon put it, did the
remedy exceed the measure, but the remedy also exceeded the evil. Nevertheless,
he denies that he wishes to interpose his judgment concerning civil matters. He
says only that he cannot but condemn the fact that the change in the Genevan
commonwealth, as Bodin testifies and Calvin states in his Letter to Sadoleto,
took its beginning from the preaching of Lord Farel and the other ministers,
who taught that civil jurisdiction in Bishops was forbidden by God’s Word.

I reply first that if there is any fault in the change of the commonwealth at
Geneva, all of it must be hammered upon doctrine, not discipline—especially
since Genevan discipline was not enacted until after the Bishop’s expulsion and
the change of the civil state, as both the matter itself cries out and Bancroft
confesses. Nor is it true that an occasion for changing the commonwealth was
offered by Farel’s preaching about discipline against the Bishops’ civil
jurisdiction. It was rather offered by his Evangelical preaching directly
against Papism. These are John Bodin’s express words: “From zeal for changing
religion, they seized the opportunity offered for changing the commonwealth.”

John Calvin teaches the same thing—even as Bancroft cites him—in his Letter to
Sadoleto, where he says that the Genevans were prepared to obey the Bishop’s
civil authority perpetually, if only he had been willing to allow them the
liberty of the Gospel. Why use many words? Peleus himself, who inflicted this
wound, heals it. For Bancroft writes: “The contention between the Bishop and the
people arose from this: as a result of Farel’s preaching, they began to grow
weary of Papism and inclined to the reformation of religion, after the example
of the neighboring cities with which they were joined in alliance.”

Second, Bancroft himself did not dare to say that the Bishop of Geneva ever had
any established civil authority over that city. Hence, after bringing Zanchi’s
judgment about the obedience owed to the Bishop in civil matters into the battle
line, he nevertheless dismissed it be—

[Marginal references: Bancroft, chapter 2, page 16; page 91; Bodin, *On the
Commonwealth*, page 353; pages 122, 115.]

[1638 control | printed p. 90 | BSB canvas 100 | bsb10507786_00100]

—fore the battle. For he remembered what he had previously alleged on this side:
namely, that Geneva was among those cities concerning whose permission he had
said that he was unwilling to dispute against the Emperor.

Francis Bonivard, as is clear from Münster, says that in the year 1224 the
Bishop of Geneva obtained from Emperor Frederick I the right to hold sole
sovereignty at Geneva. Haymo, who was then contending with him over the rule of
the city, was thereby cast down. The Dukes of Savoy, however, did not acquiesce,
even down to our times. Indeed, not even the Genevan people themselves
acquiesced in this government by their Bishop. Josias Simler says that, although
the bishopric of Geneva possessed extensive privileges, the people always
zealously preserved both their perpetual agreements with the Bishop and the
liberties they had received from their fathers.

“For their preservation,” says Bodin, with whom Bancroft himself agrees, “they
entered into an alliance with the people of Bern and Fribourg, and finally came
to the point that they declared that they were unwilling to be bound by the
laws either of the Duke of Savoy or of their own Bishop.” All these things took
place under Papism, so that the man who wishes to condemn the Evangelical
reformation at Geneva—whether as regards doctrine or as regards discipline—as
treason must be utterly shameless.

Third, it should be noted that Bancroft’s calumnies mentioned above displease
even his own men. John Bridges says that God should be praised because the
Bishop, who was addicted to Papism and obstructed reformation, was expelled
from Geneva; he declares that the Bishop’s tyranny deserved this. Bridges
further adds: “Although the Genevans ought to have retained the Bishops’
government of the Churches, nevertheless, if they have now recognized that, as
regards discipline, they can rest in some Apostolic order and custom, let that
be well,” he says, “and let God be blessed on this account. We do not begrudge
it to them or disturb their repose in this Apostolic custom. Nor, conversely,
can they prescribe to us, or indeed do they prescribe to us.”

Hadrian Saravia speaks similarly: “The Bishop of Geneva and his clergy, struck
with empty fear, deserted their station. A bad conscience often causes the
wicked man to flee when no one pursues him. Hence it happened that faithful
servants of Christ succeeded to their vacant places. If the Bishop of Geneva
had been willing to receive the Gospel of the Son of God, he would never have
been cast out of his see,” and so forth. Thus discipline incurred no blame in
the alteration of Geneva’s civil state.
