17. Since the Jesuits and Arminians all but triumph by this single argument,
let us see how strong it is. Reply. This argument tends toward saying that
people have God's decrees within their power, so that people make themselves
the craftsmen of their own election and reprobation and rob God of dominion
over creatures and of freedom in electing and reprobating. On this account God
will yield in dignity to an earthly potter and to earthly judges, who very
reluctantly submit their decrees and statutes to the choice of their subjects.
Thus let them see whether absolute freedom belongs to God or to human beings.
But if people cause God to form this or that decree, it follows that the human
will is the occasion or cause of the divine volition, which Arminius expressly
affirms. Consequently, a cause of God can be given, or else God's will is not
God's essence—which is for God not to be God. 2. Because it lies in everyone's
choice to believe to the end, people have the universal decree of election
within their choice; and because it lies in everyone's choice never to
believe, they have

[Marginalia: See Suárez, *On the Aids*, bk. 1.]

[Marginalia: Arminius, disputation 4, thesis 53.]

[1651 logical printed p. 140 | IA leaf 158 | PDF p. 158]

universal reprobation within their choice. 3. Nor will God have mercy on whom
he wills, but his will to show mercy will be bent by obligation. Yet the
Arminians will not subject their efficacious grace, administered by a decree
of predestination distinct from the decree of providence, as Arminius
fabulously maintains, nor will the Jesuits subject their congruous motion to
human choice. How, then, do people have within their choice all antecedents
required for a free act? 2. The assertion, “People do not have within their
power that whose necessary antecedent requisite they do not have within their
power,” is openly false in many respects. It lay in Adam's choice to fulfill
the law and not eat the forbidden fruit; but it lay not in Adam's choice, but
in God's alone, to forbid eating from that tree, although this was an
antecedent requisite for Adam's obedience. By God's grace the elect have it
within their power to believe God's promises; but proposing the promises,
which is antecedently required for faith, belongs to God alone. Subjects and
servants have it within their power to obey legislators and masters who issue
just commands; but they do not have it within their power that legislators
and masters command these things or refrain. Those congruously called have it
within their power, according to Jesuit principles, to obey God when he calls
congruously; but they do not have it within their power whether he calls
congruously or not. 3. Álvarez replies: “If the supposition so precedes the
will that it stands on the side of a created cause distinct from the human
will,

[Marginalia: Álvarez, disputation 21, at figure 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 141 | IA leaf 159 | PDF p. 159]

then the proposition is true; but it is not true if it stands on the side of
the first and most universal cause. The reason is that the first cause alone
can efficaciously change the human will while the will preserves its natural
order of operation.” Suárez attempts to mock this answer in section 16 at
figure 12, but in reality begs the question. 4. The assertion can be
distinguished very well. If that antecedently required thing twisted choice by
force, contrary to its nature and by fatal necessity, the argument would be
valid. But we teach nothing of the kind. If, however, that antecedent
requisite gently determines the potency toward the numerically same object,
and no other, which the will in fact freely chooses, then the free act is in
the will's power, although that antecedent requisite is not in our power,
because the decree and freedom agree upon the same willed object. 5. The minor
can also be distinguished: “But God's absolute decree is not in a person's
power.” God's decree is in the power of choice in two ways, either as in a
cause or as in an effect. It would be in it as in a cause if the decree itself
were in our choice. This need not be said, nor is it possible for an eternal,
immanent act in God to be in the power of our choice, which operates in time.
But God's decree is in the power of choice in the manner that is possible and
sufficient to preserve freedom: it is in our choice according to its effect,
since what is determined by God

[1651 logical printed p. 142 | IA leaf 160 | PDF p. 160]

in the absolute decree is in our power. A person who freely does not believe
in effect and interpretatively affixes his seal to the decree of reprobation,
as Proverbs 8:36 says, “Those who hate me love death”—interpretatively.
Accordingly, God's decrees are in this sense also in our power, because we
would not have willed such things unless God had decreed that we should will
them. The decree is therefore in our power in this sense: its effect, object,
and terminus—namely, the free act absolutely decreed by God—is in our power,
and this suffices to preserve freedom. This, then, is the sound and true sense
of that assertion: a thing is not in our power which is in our power neither
in its cause or in itself, nor in its effect, interpretatively, or in the
willed object. In this sense the major is true and the minor false. The reason
is plain: the formal ground of obedience or disobedience, merit or demerit,
does not consist in us little humans having within the command and power of
our choice the decree antecedent to our acts. For no law commands me either
to have or not have God's absolute decree in my power. Rather, the ground of
obedience or disobedience consists in my choosing or not choosing that which
is not only decreed by the absolute decree but also revealed by God's law.

Objection 12. 3. Thus Suárez: *A human act is free not by its relation to
God's will, but by its relation*

[Marginalia: Proverbs 8:36.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, ibid., fol. 17.]

[1651 logical printed p. 143 | IA leaf 161 | PDF p. 161]

*to the human will, just as it is a vital act not because it proceeds from God
but because it proceeds from the intrinsic principle of human life. Therefore,
for an act to be free to a person, it must not be necessary to that person
through a necessity antecedent to his free influx.* Reply. 1. It is not
formally free in relation to God's will, yet it is causally free from him;
otherwise the Jesuit does not want us to thank God for the supernatural acts
of faith, love, hope, and repentance. 2. It does not follow, because when
God's determination falls upon the same object upon which created freedom
falls, as it does here, the same act is free in God independently and
principally, and in the person dependently and subordinately.

14. Objection 12. 4. He argues as follows: *Antecedent necessity is not
necessity from a supposition, because it is not founded upon the principle,
“it is impossible for the same thing to be and not be,” but upon this:
“it is impossible for such a cause, so disposed, to exist and for the effect
not to follow from it.” It is therefore causal and physical necessity, and so
destroys freedom.* Reply. 1. It is founded upon this principle: it is possible
for God to move freedom and for it not to be moved. 2. If we speak properly,
the free act does not flow causally from the decree, but properly flows from
the positing of the decree through the mediation of power. For the decree
posits that the will will incline itself freely and also, in a qualified
sense, necessarily. 3. Although causal necessity conflicts with freedom,
necessity from an infinite cause such as God does not, because by his infinite
skill he can move

[Marginalia: Ibid., fol. 26.]

[1651 logical printed p. 144 | IA leaf 162 | PDF p. 162]

freedom by stirring up, as it were, that part of the potency which regards the
object absolutely determined by him, so that it is nevertheless moved both
necessarily and freely.

15. Fifth objection. He argues as follows: *If the supposition of the decree
precedes freedom's influx, then, just as freedom's exercise does not intervene
in positing it, neither does freedom intervene in the effect's emanation from
such a supposition, because the supposition is not subject to freedom's
dominion, and therefore freedom cannot impede that emanation.* Reply. 1. It
was proved above that the consequent is within the will's choice although the
antecedent is not. 2. It cannot impede it because it freely cannot and
therefore does not will to do so. Yet it follows that absolute dominion over
its own act does not belong to created freedom, which we readily grant, for it
belongs to God alone.

To this belongs what Suárez says concerning a conditional decree to concur at
this hour and place with the human will, not determinately toward acting but
toward coacting, if created choice should will to coact. Reply. They imagine
that our freedom cannot be preserved unless by such a decree God sets himself
at a crossroads, ready to concur or not concur according as the person wills
or does not. But 1. Suárez himself permits no confusion in God's decrees,
because he says that this smacks of imperfection. Yet a will to concur with
Peter if he wills, or not to concur if he does not, is a confused decree.
What does Suárez say here, ibid., figure 16? *This decree*, he says, *is called
general but not confused, because through*

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Grace*, part 1, prologue 8, ch. 8.]

[Marginalia: Objection 16.]

[1651 logical printed p. 145 | IA leaf 163 | PDF p. 163]

*it concurrence is offered not for one act only, but for several or even all
the acts which free choice can exercise here and now concerning the object so
proposed—all*, I say, *that are possible not individually but specifically;
yet not confusedly but particularly, for a determinate, singular act under
each species, such as an act of love, hatred, assent, or dissent.* Reply. 1.
Suárez himself, ibid., figure 10, acknowledges that decrees concerning
possible futures are idle and foreign to God's wisdom. But this is a decree
to concur with Peter if Peter should will to coact. Namely, if Judas wills
finally to believe, God decreed to concur with him; yet Judas will never
finally believe. What shadow of wisdom is here, even by Suárez's judgment? 2.
This conditional decree either rests upon knowledge or does not. If not, it is
a blind and irrational decree. If it does, God absurdly decreed to concur with
an act which he foresees will never occur. In this manner one could say that
God decreed the repentance of demons and the final faith of all the
reprobate. 3. How this decree can be general, extended to all possible acts,
and yet not confused is incomprehensible. 4. By this decree God wishes
concurrence to be offered to acts possible not individually but specifically,
and nevertheless to all which free choice can exercise here and now. This is a
contradiction: concurrence is offered to all individually and not to all here
and now—that is, not

[1651 logical printed p. 146 | IA leaf 164 | PDF p. 164]

to all individually. 5. If God decrees to send Jeremiah to prophesy by a
decree through which he offers his concurrence for prophesying or not
prophesying, God reasons badly from the decree to the necessity of
prophesying. For by such a decree Jeremiah is as much going not to prophesy as
to prophesy. And who would think that when God declares the decree concerning
the devastation of the Assyrians, Tyrians, and Chaldeans through free agents
and confirms it with the assertion, “I Jehovah have spoken and will do it,”
God wills the enemies as much not to devastate as to devastate? For by a
plainly affirmative decree he affirms that he will devastate them. Or what
sane person, unless carried away by a Jesuit spirit, would expound an
affirmation in a disjunctive sense through affirmation or negation, and thus
through yes or no? 6. Vázquez perhaps also overlooked this. *Because*, he
says, *God does not in time concur with free choice generally and confusedly,
neither did he determine from eternity to concur generally and confusedly.*
For he asserts in the same place that the Pelagians taught this. And in fact,
if God is prepared in that indeterminate manner to concur both with Peter's
refusing and his willing, who does not see here a confused decree to concur in
such a manner from eternity? No one among human beings does anything under
the disjunction, “if not this, nevertheless that or this; or if not, that,” in
which there is not both ignorance and a confused suspension of mind.

Objection 17. Seventh, Suárez argues: *That predetermination is so necessary
that it is essentially included*

[Marginalia: Vázquez, tome 1, q. 4, art. 13, disputation 65, ch. 2.]

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On the Divine Aids*.]

[1651 logical printed p. 147 | IA leaf 165 | PDF p. 165]

*in the concurrence of the first cause. Therefore, if God by his absolute will
determines the human will to one act and not to others, he supplies his
concurrence for that act alone and leaves the will without concurrence for
the others. But without the first cause's concurrence the will cannot operate.
Therefore, other acts do not remain within the will's true, proximate, and
moral power.* Reply. Whoever does not operate lacks actual concurrence, but
it does not follow from this that one who thus does not operate cannot
operate. Suárez replies in the same place: *It is one thing not to have
concurrence in second act, another not to have it within one's power or in
first act, but to have it altogether denied by God. The former does not imply
a lack of proximate and ready power for operating, although it removes the
intrinsic faculty for operating.* Reply. 1. Even if the human will had God's
concurrence within its power in first act, it would not be more powerful for
acting or not acting than when it has that concurrence only in second act. For
God's concurrence, by which he is nearer, adds no new potency to freedom, just
as the presence of light adds to the eye no new real visual potency which it
did not possess while placed in darkness. Therefore, it is a dream that the
will is not in proximate potency for acting unless it has God's concurrence
within its power. 2. When that concurrence of God which we say the will has,
not within its power but in second act although not in first act, falls upon
the same object upon which freedom electively flowing falls, the proximate

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On Predetermining Concurrence*, bk. 1, ch. 12, fol. 8,
p. 38.]

[1651 logical printed p. 148 | IA leaf 166 | PDF p. 166]

potency of freedom is preserved. 3. We deny that freedom has such a nature
that, when all things required for acting are posited, it can indifferently act
or not act. We think it absurd that the created will should have within its
power the ability to draw God's concurrence indifferently toward this or that
specific act according to its own absolute choice, because this would remove
God's freedom and providence. It is also absurd because there would have had
to be from eternity both the futurition and nonfuturition of this same free
act, which is a palpable contradiction. For when all requisites are posited,
the will could will this, and the eternal futurition of this volition must
necessarily have preceded; and it could also refuse this, and thus the eternal
futurition of the nolition necessarily preceded, which is absurd. Therefore,
it must necessarily be said that only one volition was future in that potency
which Suárez calls proximate.

18. Objection 8. Suárez argues: *Let us posit a person to whom this object to
be loved is here and now proposed—for example, entering a religious order—so
that, on the intellect's side, he has every consideration and judgment
necessary, and in the will has no positive, contrary impediment. He lacks only
the efficacious divine will by which God wills that he love that object. What,
I ask, will that person do to love the object? He is neither moved nor
determined by God. Nothing can be imagined within his power by which he would
be determined by God, because that efficacious premotion would necessarily be
required for that thought. Therefore, it cannot be morally imputed to such a*

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On the Aids*, bk. 1, ch. 12, fol. 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 149 | IA leaf 167 | PDF p. 167]

*person that he does not love such an object.* Reply. 1. The person to whom
this object to be loved is proposed either loves it or desires to love it.
Both the loving and the desire to love were preceded by God's eternal and
efficacious volition. Therefore, in second act he had God's predetermination
within his power, and nothing else is required of him except that he love the
object. 2. If he lacks God's predetermination, he lacks it freely, at least
interpretatively, because he freely refuses to love the object, and the free
act of refusing tends objectively toward non-love, toward which God's eternal
efficacious will and decree tend. Accordingly, even if he lacks God's
predetermination, the non-love is imputed as sin, because he freely does not
love and thus consequently subscribes to the absence of predetermination. 3.
If the intellect has every consideration and judgment and the will has no
impediment, an efficacious volition cannot fail to have preceded. Thus the
instance is empty however the matter stands. We deny that non-love of the
object is given from the mere absence of God's efficacious volition, because
it is impossible to combine simultaneously the absence of God's efficacious
volition and a sufficient exertion on the will's part by which it inclines
toward love of an object which it cannot actually love solely because that
predetermination is absent. Thus the instance is impossible in the strict
sense in which it is proposed, because denial of God's efficacious volition
and free non-love of the object always fall upon the same object.

[1651 logical printed p. 150 | IA leaf 168 | PDF p. 168]

14. But before we solve the other objections, it is asked whether a
conditional decree can be admitted in no sense. Reply. A conditional decree is
twofold. Either it is so conditional that the condition is referred to God's
will, as though one said that God decrees and wills this if a person does
something. In this manner we utterly deny every conditional decree in God,
because God's will is suspended upon no condition, cause, or occasion by which
his will is moved to decree or will. But a decree is also called conditional
from the side not of God willing but of the willed object. Conditional decrees
can be posited in God, especially in conditioned contingent effects—that is,
effects future from the positing of the decree. Thus in a conditional
predetermining decree God saw the Tyrians' repentance if miracles and signs
had been performed there; the Keilites' delivering David into Saul's hands if
David remained with them; and foreign nations' hearing Ezekiel if God's
prophet had prophesied among them.

I see three objections raised here by Suárez. 1. Such things cannot be known
in a conditional predetermining decree. *For*, he says, *if God had such a
conditional decree to predetermine the created will toward such an effect
when such a condition obtained, by force of such a decree he would have had
to be prepared to bestow that predetermination; otherwise the decree would
have been empty. But in that case God*

[Marginalia: Suárez, *On the Necessity of Grace*, prologue 2, on conditional
knowledge, ch. 8, fol. 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 151 | IA leaf 169 | PDF p. 169]

*is not prepared to give such aid, because if he were prepared, he would give
it, since it does not depend on a person*—namely, that signs be performed
among the Tyrians—*but on God alone. Therefore, he had no such decree.* Reply.
1. I deny the connection of the major, because this conditional decree is only
God's volition by which he willed that the Tyrians, seeing the signs, should
repent. He freely withheld the signs from them and freely withheld repentance
from them. For God can decree the coexistence of two connected extremes upon
a hypothesis without decreeing the extremes themselves. Thus he wills and
decrees that the faith of reprobates and eternal life cohere, while decreeing
neither Judas's faith nor eternal life. For this is the object of God's
decree: every believer will have eternal life. 2. Such a decree is not empty;
it has weighty causes in God. God can will things connected upon a hypothesis
while not willing the extremes, and such a decree is free. For Scripture
asserts that God willed to give them repentance if they saw the signs. 3. Nor
does it follow that God was not prepared to give them such aid, since he
freely gives and would have given it if they had seen the signs. But he freely
withheld the signs. Nevertheless, I do not deny that this is not properly a
conditional decree, but rather an absolute volition by which he wills the
coexistence of two things cohering conditionally.

2. Suárez objects: *That condition*, he says, *as it is supposed, depends upon
God's will alone; otherwise God decreed of himself not to posit such a
condition, because*

[1651 logical printed p. 152 | IA leaf 170 | PDF p. 170]

*it is supposed never to be future. Therefore, he cannot be prepared to give
the aid which necessarily requires that condition to be fulfilled.* Reply. I
deny the consequence. For he decrees that believers be saved, but never
decrees that Judas should believe. Therefore, it is not necessary that a real
change in creatures follow from a conditional decree on the object's side.
For he expressly says that foreign nations would have heard Ezekiel if he had
been sent to them, and yet God was unwilling to send Ezekiel to them.

3. Objection. *The condition under which the Tyrians' repentance was predicted
was, “if such mighty works had been done in you,” while by an absolute decree
it was predetermined that such mighty works would never be done in you.
Therefore, God had no such conditional decree. The consequence is proved:
such a decree would have concerned a thing depending on God's will alone,
under a condition likewise depending on that same will alone and never to be
fulfilled by the absolute decree of that same will. But such a conditional
decree is altogether idle and fruitless, because through it God in reality
bestowed nothing upon the Tyrians, was not prepared to bestow anything,
contained no promise, fell under no generality of divine providence, and
contributed nothing toward the matter.* Reply. 1. The consequent does not
follow when that antecedent is posited. The decree is not fruitless. For, just
as God by a free decree willed actual beings to cohere—the elect's faith and
salvation, his people's passage through the Red Sea and the Egyptians'
submersion, and many other things contingently connected in themselves—so by
the same free decree it accords with

[1651 logical printed p. 153 | IA leaf 171 | PDF p. 171]

the perfection of infinite and most exalted providence that conditioned
futures also be connected. Why should this not serve to establish God's
freedom? We thereby see in God's absolute decree the connections of merely
possible things even though those things themselves will not be future. This
teaches us that not even possibles escape providence, and teaches us most
humbly to subject ourselves to God and adore the abyss of his judgments. He
willed to send Ezekiel to a people that would not hear and was unwilling to
send him to one that would hear, and did this solely by his good pleasure, so
that we may see that God does not call these from need, since he could call
others more compliant; that he calls from sheer grace; and that by absolute
choice he determines combinations even of possible things. 2. This decree is
not only not idle but must exist in God, because God's will cannot be suspended
and indeterminate concerning beings in actuality or even beings in potency.
Since the sight of miracles and the Tyrians' faith are possible as regards
coexistence—or the coexistence of these two is possible in itself and also
future upon a hypothesis—God either decrees and wills that coexistence or does
not decree and will it. We cannot safely affirm that God did not will that
coexistence upon the hypothesis, because Scripture says that the Tyrians would
have been converted upon seeing the miracles. But if that conversion had
preceded God's decree, people would be converted by no providence, no previous
act of God's will, and consequently no preceding grace

[1651 logical printed p. 154 | IA leaf 172 | PDF p. 172]

and gratuitous favor of God—a theology which the sacred oracles reject.
Accordingly, just as there is an approving will and simple complacency,
revealed in Scripture, concerning a thing never future, such as the obedience
of reprobates, and a will of good pleasure concerning a thing God will
infallibly effect, so there is in God an absolute decree concerning truly
future things, and a decree of simple complacency or approval concerning many
possibles never future but posited upon a hypothesis. But it can be objected
that by this argument I rejected God's conditional decree concerning God's
concurrence with possible acts of freedom that will never be future, because
it is foreign to God's wisdom to decree as future what he foreknows will never
be future. For this is not the act of a prudent man: no provident mother
prepares a garment for a fetus which she certainly knows has died in her
womb. Therefore, it is foreign to wisdom for God to decree the Tyrians'
conversion upon the hypothesis that miracles should occur among them, when he
both foreknows and decrees never to posit that hypothesis. Reply. I reject
that conditional decree by this argument not so much because the thing will
never be future, as because God's will would be fixed and settled concerning a
thing never future—otherwise it is no decree—and, as though his intellect
foreknew nothing, God's will would be suspended upon the human will. But here,
although God foreknows that the extremes of the conditional connection will
never be future, he nevertheless foreknows the connection to be true and to
have reality

[1651 logical printed p. 155 | IA leaf 173 | PDF p. 173]

in his will. Therefore, God does not decree a nonbeing. For in God's decree
the sight of the miracles and the Tyrians' repentance truly coexist, although
neither those miracles nor the repentance exist separately and apart. But by
the conditional decree of Suárez which I rejected, God is made to intend and
decree what he sees to be plainly a nonbeing, and something is decreed by God
to be actually future which he foreknows will never be future and which he
foreknows has no being at all in his determining will. In the conditional
decree treated here, however, it is not decreed that a thing never future
should be future. For the Tyrians' conversion upon seeing signs is in reality
a being truly willed and decreed by God. 2. I do not deny that this decree is
called conditional very improperly, because the condition depends upon God's
absolute will, as does the conditioned thing. Indeed, it is a simple and
absolute volition that two things cohere with one another—namely, the sight of
the signs and the Tyrians' conversion. But in the conditional decree which I
reject, God wills something which he knows will never be future upon a
hypothesis which he knows will never be posited, as though God decreed that
Judas should believe and decreed to concur with him if he wills to believe,
while knowing both that Judas will never believe and that he himself will
never concur. We, however, say that God neither wills nor foreknows either the
miracles seen by the Tyrians or their conversion, but simply takes pleasure
in the coexistence of these two, just as God takes pleasure in Judas's
obedience, which will never be future.
