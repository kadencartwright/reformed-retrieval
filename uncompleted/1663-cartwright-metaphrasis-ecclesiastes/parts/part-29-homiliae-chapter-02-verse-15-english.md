VERSE 15. The wise man himself is like one whose eyes are in his head, but the
fool walks in darkness; yet I likewise recognized that the same outcome
befalls all these men.

He proves their opposition from their contrary subjects, because there is a
very great difference between the wise man and the fool—the kind of man he
previously affirmed the pleasure-seeker to be—which he concludes from their
contrary effects. For the wise man knows the way in which he must conduct
himself among men so that he may benefit both himself and others; he knows

what is to be pursued and what is to be avoided; he foresees future evils long
beforehand; and he joins past things with present ones and compares future
things with both. The pleasure-seeker, however, is borne only toward that
present thing by which his senses are moved, paying very little attention to
things either past or future. Hence he is exposed to innumerable troubles that
could have been avoided by wisdom. Moreover, wisdom can be joined and wedded
to true and eternal happiness, and can greatly advance that happiness once it
has been acquired, provided it confines itself within the affairs of this age.
But once it has crossed its own bounds and barriers and flies over to heavenly
things, nothing is more unwise or more mad. Folly, or the life of pleasure,
fights against that happiness diametrically and chills and dulls it once
acquired, although it does not wholly extinguish it.

He illustrates this by a comparison of counterparts when he says that the
wise man has eyes in his head. Because they are set in a convenient and
elevated place in the body, like a watchman in a citadel, the wise man can see
from afar the things that are going to meet him. By this he teaches clearly
that the pleasure-seeker's eyes are set in an inconvenient place, as though in
his knees or shins—or rather, as the Greek proverb has it, *to have one's mind
in one's shins* (`νοῦν ἐπὶ κνήμαις ἔχειν`), as we commonly say, “His heart is
in his heele”—so that the evil is upon him before he can foresee it in order
to flee it; or that his eyes are on his back, so that he cannot recognize evils
unless he has already experienced and tasted them. He further illustrates
this teaching by another like comparison, likening the fool to one who walks
at night in darkness. There he strikes against every stone, is driven headlong
at every precipitous place, falls unwittingly into deep pits and rivers, and
becomes entangled in innumerable snares. By this very comparison he indicates
clearly that the wise man, by contrast, travels as though in light and at
midday, discerning the road and a departure from it.

It is indeed true that this wisdom is darkness in comparison with the Word of
Life, and that the man endowed with it is blind in comparison with the man who
has been gifted with the Spirit of adoption. But when he is compared with a
pleasure-seeker or a miser, and so forth, he must be preferred to that man no
less than light to darkness and a sighted man to a blind man. So much, then,
for the respects in which the wisdom of this age is separated and
distinguished from folly.

It should also be noted that this perishable wisdom differs as widely as
heaven from earth from fraud, cunning, deceit, and evil arts. For this wisdom,
as has been said, is acquired through labor and trouble, whereas our corrupted
nature suggests fraud, deceit, and the like to us without any effort. There
are men who think that they cannot manage either domestic or public affairs
unless they apply their minds to fraud, lies, pretense, and dissimulation, and
who even reckon themselves wise—indeed, *supremely politically astute*
(`πολιτικωτάτους`)—as though true wisdom, even though perishable, were so weak
and powerless that it could not protect itself against frauds and render
endeavors of this kind ineffective.

It follows that he should speak about the respects in which they agree well
with each other. This is first explained generally in the latter half of this
verse and in the following verse, and then is declared by particulars in verse
16. He therefore says that one and the same outcome befalls these two. It is
just as if, when two men were standing at a fork in the road, one entered one
road and the other another, yet both at last met again in one inn; or as if,
when two men were setting sail from the same port, one directed his course
toward one quarter and the other toward another, yet they landed at the same
port.

*Translator's notes:*

1. The edition prints the coordinate construction `viam dignoscere, de via
   deflectere`, literally “to recognize the road, to turn aside from the road,”
   even though both infinitives grammatically continue to describe the wise
   man. The translation understands the second infinitive substantivally as
   recognizing “a departure from” the road rather than silently altering the
   Latin.
2. The English phrase `His heart is in his heele` is printed in English within
   Cartwright's Latin and is retained in its edition spelling.
