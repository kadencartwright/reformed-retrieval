## Chapter VI. On the Number of the Sacraments

We acknowledge only two sacraments of the New Testament. The papists thrust
seven upon us.

### Thesis I

Bellarmine. We shall show that the definition of a sacrament applies to no
fewer and no more than seven rites: namely, that there are this many outward
and perceptible signs which, by divine institution, have attached to them a
promise of justifying grace.

Protestant reply. 1. According to the position Bellarmine proposed earlier, a
sacrament additionally requires a fixed formula of the consecrating word, and
its physical efficacy *ex opere operato* must be the cause of justifying grace.
2. This very definition will never be proved to apply to any ordinary
sacraments other than baptism and the Lord's Supper.

2. Bellarmine. We have the outward rite of confirmation in Acts 8 and 19,
together with the effect of grace, from which its institution and command are
inferred. It must further be noted that the laying on of hands was joined with
anointing and the sign of the cross, and that it was performed by the hand of
a bishop.

Protestant reply. 1. Nothing is read here about anointing or the sign of the
cross. 2. Nothing can be inferred from this about an ordinary institution and
promise, because this was the privilege of certain extraordinary ministers:
that at the laying on of their—

[1632 witness | printed p. 35]

—hands the Holy Spirit was given. 3. Nothing is said here about the grace of
justification. “They undoubtedly received the Holy Spirit,” Cajetan says, “in
a perceptible effect; through baptism they had received him in the effect of
inward grace alone. At the apostles' laying on of hands believers were
baptized with the baptism of the Spirit—they were not confirmed.” So Alexander
of Hales, Part 4, question 6, member 6. 4. Nothing is said here about
confirmation as necessarily related to an antecedent baptism. For in Acts
10:44 the Holy Spirit fell in precisely the same manner upon those who had not
yet been baptized.

3. Bellarmine. In John 20:23 we have the outward, judicial rite of penance,
namely absolution, which is a sign of what takes place inwardly.

Protestant reply. 1. No outward rite is prescribed here. 2. No matter and form
of any sacrament are indicated here, because there is no element distinct
from the word. 3. Not even the slightest mention of auricular confession is
made in this passage.

4. Bellarmine. In James 5:14 we have the rite of extreme unction, together
with an attached promise.

Protestant reply. Cajetan answers concerning that passage: “Neither by their
words nor by their effect do these words speak of the sacramental anointing of
extreme unction, but rather of the anointing which the Lord Jesus instituted
in the Gospel for the disciples to administer to the sick. For the text does
not say, ‘Is anyone sick unto death?’ but simply, ‘Is anyone sick?’ It states
the effect as the relief of the sick person, and it speaks of the forgiveness
of sins only conditionally. Extreme unction, however, is given only near the
point of death and aims directly—as its formula declares—at the forgiveness
of sins. Add that James commands many presbyters, both praying and anointing,
to be called to one sick person, which is foreign to the rite of extreme
unction.”

5. Bellarmine. In 1 Timothy 4 we have, concerning the sacrament of orders, an
outward rite—the laying on of hands—and likewise the promise of grace.

Protestant reply. 1. The papists are not sufficiently consistent with
themselves in wishing the laying on of hands to be a sacramental rite both in
orders and in confirmation. It is unheard of in Scripture for one and the
same sign to occur in two sacraments. 2. Nothing is said here about a promise
of justifying grace. 3. The laying on of hands is no more the cause of any
grace than prophecy is. 4. On the papists' own position, the passage concerns
the episcopate, which is not an order. In fact, however, it concerns the
extraordinary office of an evangelist. 2 Timothy 4:5.

6. Bellarmine. For matrimony we have the very name *sacrament*. Ephesians
5:31.

Protestant reply. 1. We also have the same term for the woman and the beast in
Revelation 17:7: “I will tell you the sacrament of the woman and of the beast
that carries her.” On this reasoning, then, an—

[1632 witness | printed p. 36]

—eighth sacrament has at last been discovered. 2. Cajetan makes this note on
Ephesians 5:32: “You do not have from this passage, prudent reader, that
marriage is a sacrament. For it does not say *sacrament*, but ‘this is a great
mystery.’” 3. The apostle immediately warns that he is not speaking of human
marriage: “But I speak concerning Christ and the church.”

7. Bellarmine. The number of seven sacraments is confirmed by various
correspondences.

Protestant reply. We demand compelling demonstrations. Out of the poverty of
his case, Bellarmine retreats to childish correspondences that neither
produce nor merit—even by congruous merit—any credence.

8. Bellarmine. Bodily life requires generation, growth, and nourishment; one
remedy against diseases and another against the aftereffects of diseases;
parents to propagate bodily life and magistrates to order and govern it. In
the same way, in spiritual life regeneration comes through baptism, growth
through confirmation, nourishment through the Eucharist, a remedy against
diseases through penance, and a remedy against the remnants of sins through
extreme unction. Matrimony serves to propagate Christians, and orders to
govern them.

Protestant reply. 1. No account is taken here of clothing, housing, exercise,
and similar things necessary to bodily life. 2. Growth is perversely placed
before nourishment. 3. Two sacraments are absurdly fashioned from sins and
the remnants of sins, as though the remnants of sins were not sins. Why, then,
should there not also be two sacraments relating to nourishment and the
remnants of nourishment? 4. One and the same propagation of spiritual and
bodily life is fabricated. 5. Government and matrimony are ineptly referred
to justifying grace, which Bellarmine maintains is the effect of all the
sacraments of the New Testament.

9. Bellarmine. Seven sacraments are instituted against seven sins and wounds:
baptism against original sin; penance against actual sin; unction against its
remnants; confirmation against weakness; the Eucharist against malice;
matrimony against concupiscence; and orders against ignorance.

Protestant reply. 1. It would also be desirable for some special sacrament to
be instituted among the papists against the lust for dealing in frivolous
nonsense in sacred matters. A moment ago only two sacraments, penance and
extreme unction, were remedies; now the rest are remedies on equal terms. A
sevenfold number of sins and wounds is fabricated which, apart from this
correspondence for which it was devised, has no place anywhere, not even among
the scholastics' most idle speculations. To make the correspondence work, the
order is thrown into confusion and the nature of the sacraments altered.

[1632 witness | printed p. 37]

10. Bellarmine. The seven sacraments correspond to the seven virtues: baptism
to faith, confirmation to hope, the Eucharist to love, penance to justice,
extreme unction to fortitude, orders to prudence, and matrimony to temperance.

Protestant reply. 1. A sevenfold number of virtues has not yet been proved. 2.
Faith, hope, and love are considered jointly, not separately, in baptism and
the Eucharist, while all the other virtues depend upon these. 3. If matrimony
corresponds to temperance, then the prohibition of marriage among Roman
clerics corresponds to intemperance, which their lives also prove.

11. Bellarmine. The number seven is celebrated and frequent in the
expiations of the Old Testament.

Protestant reply. It is remarkable, then, that seven sacraments cannot be
found there which were appointed as figures of these seven. Unless this can
be shown, this argument ought to be held of no account.
