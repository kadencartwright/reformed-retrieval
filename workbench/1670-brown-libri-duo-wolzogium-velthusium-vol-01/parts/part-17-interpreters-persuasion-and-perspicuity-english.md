XXXI. *Wolzogen* next faults the *Exercitator* because he did not define the
interpreter he was seeking with somewhat more precise judgment; that is, he
did not distinguish between one who interprets the Scriptures for himself and
one who interprets them for others. And indeed, whether this ought rightly and
deservedly to be faulted in this controversy or not, *Wolzogen* himself no
less than *Paradox* is guilty of this fault. For he himself mentions this
distinction only once, and that as if in passing, on page 56. Nor does he make
any difference between them with respect to interpretation, but only with
respect to the outcome or effect of interpretation. Nor does *Wolzogen* call
to mind the distinction employed by the orthodox in this Question, namely,
between the principal interpreter and the subordinate and ministerial
interpreter. This ought to have been quite foreign to a careful disputant.

XXXII. *Wolzogen* says on page 91 that the *Paradoxical Exercitator* lays down
such conditions of an interpreter as entail certain and invincible
persuasion. I do not see that this is said by him in this first chapter. He
only says *[that it follows more clearly than midday light that to him who is
able to indicate and demonstrate the true and genuine meaning of the divine
writings, it has certainly and indubitably been granted—to him alone—to
determine something firm and immovable concerning faith and morals, etc.]* He
undoubtedly understands these things of the norm and of the person who uses a
certain and infallible norm and applies it rightly. And this norm can surely,
in its own kind, persuade; so too can the one who uses it rightly, even if he
does not actually persuade.

Nor does he say it in chapter 4, where in § 3 he says: *[The true interpreter
of the Sacred Writings is one who can elicit their true meanings, show them to
be such, and detect and refute false ones, namely, by unassailable arguments
in which a mind desirous of solid truth can and ought entirely and fully to
acquiesce.]* This

[Page 142 | ONB canvas 196 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000196]

however differs from actual and invincible persuasion. But what more does
*Wolzogen* say? *[But I deny*, he says on page 92, *that there is or ever has
been an interpreter who produces in others a certain and invincible faith; I
deny that there ought to be; I deny that there can be.]* Although no question
is being raised about an interpreter such as he had described above, it is
nevertheless evident that *Wolzogen* does not wish the Holy Spirit to be
regarded as an interpreter; for he does not dare to deny that the Spirit can,
if he wills, produce in people a certain and invincible faith, since he grants
this in the *Censure of the Censure*, page 122. Even if it seemed good to God
to ordain that heresies should arise by his permission, and therefore he did
not will to bestow upon everyone that invincible and certain faith or to give
everyone an invincible persuasion of the truth, it must nevertheless not be
denied that he can actually accomplish this if he wills. Therefore, when
*Wolzogen* denies that there can be such an interpreter, does he not deny that
the Spirit of God ought to be regarded as an interpreter? In saying this, does
he not abandon the orthodox position and oppose the Reformed doctors? The
Orthodox assert, and we with them, that the Spirit clearly discloses his mind
through the Scriptures as far as matters necessary for salvation are
concerned; and that if any difficulty about such a matter occurs anywhere, he
removes it by interpreting those obscure Scriptures through clearer and
plainer ones, so that all who read them seriously and weigh them can be
convinced and persuaded concerning that truth. They also assert that God, if
he wills, can renew the minds of everyone through his efficacious concurrence,
influx, and spiritual illumination, and actually produce in them, as an
outcome, this invincible faith and persuasion. It is no answer for him to say
in the *Censure of the Censure*, page 122, that he freely admits this; and that
when he says there can be no such interpreter, he is speaking in the most
explicit words about those who had neither the spirits of other people nor
the Spirit of God in their power. For by this reasoning he confirms what we
say, namely, that by an interpreter of Scripture he in no way under-*

[Page 143 | ONB canvas 197 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000197]

*stands the Holy Spirit, and cannot or does not wish to understand him by it,
because it is of this very interpreter that he speaks here. Let others judge
whether this agrees with the doctrine of the Reformed. But if he is speaking
of the norm itself, or of that by which one who uses reason can and ought to
be brought to allow himself to be persuaded of the truth of some
interpretation, why is there not even now such an interpreter, who can produce
in all others a certain and invincible faith, although they are not in actual
fact convinced? And this interpreter, or this norm, is by its nature fitted,
in its own kind, to remove all heresies and schisms and to settle the Christian
people, split into various parties, in a sure peace; and it would actually
accomplish this if everyone duly attended to it and the fault in human beings
were removed.

XXXIII. *Paradox* says in chapter III, § 2: *[A perspicuous discourse is one
whose meaning is apparent at first sight or first glance to the hearer or
reader; but an obscure discourse is one whose meaning is hidden at first sight
or first glance from the hearer or reader?]* He sets forth these definitions
of perspicuity so as to prepare a way for himself to infer from this the things
he has in view, namely, that all perspicuity and obscurity consist in a
relation, as we shall presently hear. Although these definitions are not true
in every respect, since a hearer and reader can be a quite incapable subject,
and it can happen that he does not read or hear attentively enough. Would no
discourse be perspicuous, but every discourse obscure, unless its meaning were
apparent at first sight or first glance even to a fool, even to someone
occupied with something else? But what does *Wolzogen* say to this? He
criticizes nothing in these definitions. He only says that the *Exercitator*
does not act in good faith in defining the perspicuity of discourse when he
maintains that all discourse is obscure. Indeed, on page 98 he says: *[The
very definitions of perspicuity and obscurity could be acceptable if he had
explained somewhat more explicitly what “to be apparent or hidden at first
glance” means?]* Nor is this indeed surprising; for, as we shall hear shortly,
he asserts with his adversary that all the perspi-*

*cuity of discourse is relative.
