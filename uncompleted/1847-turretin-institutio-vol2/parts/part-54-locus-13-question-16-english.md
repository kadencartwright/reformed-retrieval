QUESTION XVI.

*Is the descent to hell rightly referred to the infernal torments and to the
most abject state under the dominion of death in the tomb? We affirm.*

I. In the preceding question the Roman Catholics’ false position concerning
Christ’s local descent to hell was refuted. Now its true and genuine meaning
must be established. The orthodox do not entirely agree among themselves
about it. Some refer it to Christ’s spiritual anguish and the hellish torments
which he sustained, including Calvin, Beza, Danaeus, Ursinus, and others, and
indeed various confessions of the churches. Others contend that it pertains to
his burial and three-day detention in the tomb, including Zanchi, Piscator,
Pierius, and others.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. Before all else, it must be observed that the question here does not
concern the origin of this article: whether it was included in the Apostles’
Creed from the beginning and constantly acknowledged and received by the
churches. For it is evident that no mention is made of it in the Nicene and
Roman Creeds, according to Rufinus, *Exposition of the Creed,* chapter 20, and
that the ancients who issued confessions and expounded the rule of faith—such
as Irenaeus, Origen, Tertullian, Augustine, and others—are silent about it. It
is reported only by Rufinus of Aquileia around the end of the fourth century.
This article is indeed found in the Athanasian Creed, but with the article of
burial omitted, a clear indication that they were taken for one and the same
thing. Thus it is probable that this article was transferred from the
Athanasian Creed into the Apostles’ Creed, perhaps first placed in the margin
for the sake of explanation and then transferred from the margin into the
text itself, where it was afterward retained and recited in one continuous
sequence. But leaving that question aside now, the issue here concerns only
its true meaning, about which not all agree.

III. Since the cause of this disagreement is nothing other than the ambiguity
of the words שאול, *Sheol,* and ᾅδης, *hades,* and the multiple meanings of the
phrase *to descend to hell,* it must be examined briefly. The word שאול is
taken four ways in Scripture: 1. For the tomb (Ps. 16:10; 49:15). 2. For the
place of the damned (Luke 16:23). 3. For the greatest torments (Ps. 18:5;
116:3). 4. For extreme humiliation (Isa. 14:15). Hence *to descend to hell* is
used in four ways: 1. It denotes being buried (Gen. 37:35; 42:38). 2.
Descending into the place of the damned (Num. 16:33). 3. Feeling infernal
pains (1 Sam. 2:6). 4. Being extremely humbled (Matt. 11:23). According to
these four significations, this article can have a fourfold meaning. It can be
referred to a local descent into the place of the damned, which the Roman
Catholics and Lutherans hold and we refuted above; to Christ’s burial; to his
infernal pains; or to the final degree of his emptying.

[Marginal heading: Reasons for burial and the state of the dead.]

IV. Those who hold that this article does not differ from burial rely chiefly
on these reasons: 1. Peter, from Psalm 16 in Acts 2, seems openly to refer it
to Christ’s burial in verse 31: “David, foreseeing, spoke of Christ’s
resurrection, that his soul was not left in hell,” and so forth. 2. שאול is
frequently put for the tomb in Scripture and *to descend into hell* for *to
descend into the tomb,* as Arias Montanus, Emmanuel Sa, other Roman Catholics,
and the lexicographers teach. 3. In various creeds mention is made of the
descent to hell with no mention of burial, as in the Athanasian Creed, which
proves that it takes them for one and the same thing. To this can be referred
the fact that Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:3–4 indeed mentions Christ’s death,
burial, and resurrection according to the Scriptures, but says nothing about
the descent to hell. Yet this would have had to be said if he had believed
that such an article conveyed something other than burial.

V. Yet it is not probable that this article is identical with burial. 1. It
would be a ταυτολογία, *tautology,* scarcely tolerable in so concise and brief
a creed. 2. It cannot be said that this article was placed after the preceding
one concerning burial as its explanation, since it is more obscure than the
former. Thus it must not be referred precisely to burial, but to the state of
death, or his detention in the tomb under the dominion of death. The phrase is
often taken in this way to describe the state of the dead. In Genesis 37:35,
Jacob, mourning his son Joseph whom he believed had been torn apart by wild
beasts, says, “Mourning, I will descend שאולה, *to Sheol,* with my son.” He did
not mean into the tomb, because he thought Joseph had been torn apart by wild
beasts and not buried in the ground, but simply to death or the state of the
dead. So Psalm 6:5 says, “In death there is no remembrance of you; who in שאול,
*Sheol,* will praise you?” See Job 17:13–15; Psalms 30:3; 49:15; Isaiah 14:11,
19–20.

VI. Second, Peter’s passage in Acts 2:27, taken from the oracle of Psalm 16:10,
seems necessarily to require this: “You will not leave my soul εἰς ᾅδου, *in
the house of hades,*” supplying οἶκον, *house.* Peter employs it to prove the
resurrection when he says that this prophecy was fulfilled through Christ’s
resurrection. Thus the sense is, “You will not leave my soul,” that is, my life
or me, in death, but you will raise me from death through the resurrection.
To this pertains what is said in verse 24 about the pains or cords of death by
which he was held as a captive by death and from which he was released through
the resurrection, since it was impossible for him to be detained further by
it, both because of the glory of the deity and because of the holiness of the
humanity.

[Marginal heading: Reasons for the torments.]

VII. But neither should the other position be rejected, which understands
this descent as referring to Christ’s supreme anguish, endured both in the
garden and on the cross. For it agrees: 1. With the style of Scripture, which by
*hell* and *the pains of hell* customarily designates the most grievous

torments, as was already said. 2. With the creed, lest Christ’s most grievous
and principal passions be left unmentioned, as they would be unless Christ’s
descent to hell were understood of the internal passions of his soul. For the
preceding articles speak only of the body’s external passions.

[Marginal heading: Reconciliation of the two positions.]

VIII. If it is asked which of these two positions should be retained, we
answer that both can be admitted and very well reconciled with each other. The
descent to hell is understood as the final degree of Christ’s passion and
emptying with respect to both soul and body. Just as the final degree of
humiliation with respect to the body was detention in the tomb, so with
respect to the soul it was those terrible torments which he endured. Thus this
last article was placed to express the final degree of Christ’s emptying,
whether with respect to the body’s ignominy or the soul’s anguish. Nor should
it seem strange if these two parts, diverse from each other, are joined in one
and the same article, since it is not unusual in Scripture for a single sense
to assume a varied σχέσιν, *relation,* and embrace many things at once,
especially when the things are subordinated to one another and connected with
each other. Therefore, since this phrase is referred now to the body’s
abasement and now to the soul’s pains, and Christ had to sustain both
conditions, the ancients not without reason added this article to the
preceding ones in order to express this state of Christ more distinctly.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

IX. The constant and indissoluble union of the human nature with the divine in
Christ does not prevent his having been able to suffer in both soul and body
the punishments owed to us. Union with the Word indeed makes it always holy
and free of every sin, but not always blessed and glorious, since he came for
this very purpose, that he might suffer. Christ always possessed the glory of
the Person as θεάνθρωπος, *God-man,* but he did not always have to possess the
glory of the human nature, which he was to attain only after the resurrection,
because he had to be tempted in all things just as we are and become a curse
for our salvation.

X. One is properly called *damned* who in hell sustains the punishment owed to
his own sins. This name cannot be attributed to Christ, who never suffered for
his own sins but for ours, and not in hell but on earth. Yet nothing prevents
the Son of God from being said to have been condemned by God for us, just as
elsewhere he is said to have become κατάρα, *a curse,* for us. Nor is it more
absurd to say that Christ was condemned than to say that the Lord of glory
suffered and was crucified and was afflicted and chastised for us, as is so
often said.

XI. The vision of God enjoyed by the blessed in heaven through glory is one
thing; that of believers on earth through grace is another. Those who see God
in the former way can no longer be subjected to any punishments and pains,
because they are in their homeland, established in a state of happiness. But
the account of believers is not the same. Although they see God through faith,
they do not cease to be exposed to various afflictions. Christ as man on earth
saw God in the latter sense, and far more perfectly than believers, but this
vision did not prevent him from suffering and complaining that he was
abandoned by God.
