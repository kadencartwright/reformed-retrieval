CHAPTER XLI.

Sixth Corollary: Knowledge of Discipline Is Necessary for the People.

The sixth point concerns the English people, whose favor we are accused of
seeking in what is now a worn-out and obsolete reproach. For the people used
to flow to our sermons even from far away; hence we have been accused by the
Hierarchists of being seducers who draw multitudes after us, Acts 20:30. As
though the Albigensians were seducers because, as Bernard complains, there was
a great concourse to them! As though the Waldenses were seducers because,
according to their accuser Reinerius, the multitude was ready to believe
them! As though Ambrose were a seducer because he could surround himself with
a multitude of people! As though Chrysostom were a seducer because the people
were so devoted to him that he was forced to depart secretly from the city to
forestall a popular tumult! Why do I dwell upon these examples? The Lord Jesus
Christ himself experienced the same calum—

But, Whitgift says, you cannot escape popularity in your doctrine. This is the

government of the Church have to do with the people? What business do they
have with discipline?” Here I seem to hear Scultingius: “What does an
understanding of sacred prayers have to do with the people? What business do
they have with knowledge of divine worship? Surely your statement that the
Scriptures should be read publicly to the people in the vernacular language
is democratic and popular.” Does not the same Spirit speak here? Is not the
accusation of popularity the same? Just as divine worship must be performed
by the motion of the tongue, so discipline must be preached before the people
so that it may be understood by them.

First, knowledge of discipline pertains to the sanctification of the
understanding. Without that knowledge the people will not understand how they
ought to conduct themselves in the Church of God, 1 Timothy 3:15, nor will
that order flourish in the Churches which Paul praises so highly among the
Colossians, Colossians 2:5. And since right discipline is an external means
which in its own manner is necessary to salvation, the deliberate ignorance
of those who are willing neither to read books written about it nor to be
instructed in it by anyone cannot be excused. This is precisely what
Tertullian censures as follows: “They prefer not to know, because they already
hate. So completely do they prejudge that what they do not know is what, if
they knew it, they could not hate. For if no debt of hatred is found, it is
certainly best to cease hating unjustly. But if its merit is established, not
only is nothing taken away from hatred, but more is acquired for perseverance
by the authority of justice itself.”

But lest anyone think that these complaints concern doctrine alone and not
discipline, let him hear Laurentius Valla: “We distinguish counterfeit coins,
separate them, and cast them away. Shall we not distinguish a reprobate
lordship, but retain it?” Gregory of Hamburg, writing on the reformation of
the Church, inveighs most sharply against this ignorance of discipline: “No
one,” he says, “ought to be ignorant of public matters, nor is anyone excused
who, through negligence, alleges ignorance of a good, at least of those things
which he is bound to know.”

Third, knowledge of right discipline is necessary to the people for the
duties of piety. Without that knowledge the people can neither groan over the
evils of the Church nor pray for the peace owed to it. The clause in the
Lord's Prayer, “Your kingdom come,” shows that it is the people's duty to cry
out to God for the restoration of discipline, according to these words of our
John Reynolds: “Not Zerubbabel and Joshua alone,” he says, “must strive to
build the house of God and fit living stones taken from the rubble into the
building, but the whole people also.” He says that it is their duty to press
God with Daniel 9, lesson 1, verse 17, “that God's face may shine upon the
desolated sanctuary,” and to imitate the zeal expressed by Isaiah 62:3: “For
Zion's sake let us not be silent, and for Jerusalem's sake let us not rest,
until her righteousness goes forth like brightness and her salvation like a
burning lamp.”

did he not suffer from Bancroft? Yet they were undeserved. For the same
difficulty which once arose in Gregory's time through the episcopate of
Constantinople now exists in England through the Bishops' usurpation. In that
situation Gregory prescribed the following prayers: “We must pray earnestly
and implore almighty God with continual prayers that he may remove from the
unity and humility of the Church this evil of pride and confusion.” If peace
was granted to the Church in the time of Gallienus, it came through the
prayers of the faithful, for God gives nothing except through prayers,
Revelation 6:10.

Fourth, knowledge of right discipline is necessary to the people for
sanctifying their obedience toward every superior. Otherwise that obedience
will labor chiefly under two faults.

First, Paul commands us to be subject for conscience' sake, Romans 13:5. In
the cause of discipline there will be no such conscience if discipline is
unknown. Indeed, what Aeneas Sylvius once complained had happened in the papal
schism has now happened in the English schism: “I see no clerics willing to
suffer martyrdom for this side or that. We all hold the same faith as our
princes, and if they worshiped idols, we too would worship them even now. And
we would deny not only the Pope but Christ himself if secular power pressed
us, because charity has grown cold and wholly perished.”

I will not say that very many would deny Christ himself. I will say, however,
that they would certainly deny the Bishops for whom they now contend if
secular power pressed them. They would not endure martyrdom, nor even those
cruelties which the Puritans have borne bravely and steadfastly for truth
under attack. And surely from this alone the difference between the two causes
is clear. For if truth belonged to the Hierarchists, would it not sanctify
their obedience just as it has sanctified the Puritans' obedience? But, you
will say, they show no obedience. We must answer with Jewel. Harding objects
that the doctrine of the Gospel conveys rebellion against princes, which he
attempts to prove from the Scottish tumults. Jewel replies: “A prince ought
not to think it a derogation from himself when he sees God obeyed before him,
for a prince is not God but God's minister. Leo says: ‘Christ ordained
that the things which are God's should be rendered to God and those which are
Caesar's to Caesar.’” This is truly not to attack Caesar, but to help him.
