### Chapter II

#### The first argument.

Page 279.

If the operation of God’s grace by which he effects faith and conversion in
us is so great that through it not only the power of believing but also faith
itself in act is given to a person and produced in him, then it overcomes all
resistance and effects conversion by itself. But Scripture openly testifies
that the operation of grace is of this kind. Philippians 1:29 and 2:13: “It
has been freely given to you in the cause of Christ to believe in him.” “God
is the one who effects in you both to will and to accomplish according to his
good pleasure.” Therefore.

The Remonstrants answer: 1. “Even if the act of resisting itself is removed,
resistibility nevertheless remains.” This is just as if they had said that
after all resistance has been overcome it nevertheless remains capable of
overcoming, or that an efficacy by which all actual resistance is taken away
can actually be resisted. But they ought to observe that the argument does
not say the act of resistance is hindered or restrained for a time—which
could be consistent with the power of resisting—but that resistance itself
is overcome. This does not happen except by a force and efficacy that exceeds
the power of resisting, as they themselves concede on p. 276. But when a
greater force is exercised, it is not resistible with respect to the lesser.
Nor is resistance properly said to be either overcome or taken away except
with respect to the power of resisting utterly.

2. The Remonstrants object that “the argument is not universal, because it
does not conclude that the grace of conversion is irresistible in those in
whom it wills to act effectually, but only in those in whom it does act
effectually, that is, in believers.” Here 1. it will be enough to recall them
to the principles of dialectic, from which they can be taught that a general
axiom is not contradicted by another general axiom in such a way that

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 199 | printed p. 189]

truth is distinguished from falsehood, but only by a particular one. Since,
therefore, their thesis was general—concerning the resistibility of every
operation of grace in any persons whatever with respect to conversion (for
they themselves cannot deny that this is the sense of the assertion they
state indefinitely)—it was certainly most appropriate to oppose it with an
argument that was a particular, not a general, contradiction. 2. They ought
to remember that in the thesis it is the grace by which the beginning,
progress, and perfection of all good actions is produced in a person that is
declared resistible. But this manner of operation properly belongs only to
believers. Our men therefore argued correctly and according to the meaning
of the thesis expressed in its words when they insisted that the contrary
holds in believers. 3. They themselves say expressly on p. 268 that the
person considered in the fourth thesis is regenerate, that is, a believer.

3. They make an observation about that little word “by itself,” which they
do not sufficiently approve of being placed in the argument. “If,” they say,
“it signifies that grace is the sole, chief, and supreme cause of faith, it
is true; but if they mean to exclude every cooperation of the will awakened
by grace, it is an error, because no one actually believes unless the will to
believe precedes.” Yet while they appear to remove one ambiguity, they ought
not to pile several more upon it. Whether they have done this deliberately or
not, I shall not say. 1. In saying that grace is the sole cause of faith,
they either speak very ambiguously or openly contradict themselves. For
Nicolaas Grevinckhoven expressly affirms on p. 208 that “free choice is a
partial cause of faith, and grace is only a partial cause, partial, that is,
in its causation.” And in this very place, where they call grace the sole
cause, they immediately add “chief and supreme,” indicating clearly enough
that there is another cause of faith besides God’s grace, though perhaps a
less principal one. 2. A cause is called chief either according to the degree
of its caus—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 200 | printed p. 190]

—ality, as they say, or for some other reason. If they mean the former, which
is the most proper sense, then when they call grace the chief cause of faith
they appear to contradict themselves in this as well: they say that grace is
determined to act by choice, and that the will acts although grace acts
nothing upon the will itself. Nor does it do anything else as a principle
toward the will’s act except remove obstacles and entice it to act. Nicolaas
Grevinckhoven, pp. 206, 204, 300. For a cause which determines another,
possesses no power communicated by that other, and acts properly ought to be
called a more principal cause than the enticing cause, whatever it may be,
whose instigation is merely moral. 3. The awakening of the will of which
they speak is either an external knocking alone or a certain inward bringing
to life. If they mean the former, then the cooperation of the will toward the
immediately following act—that is, toward the engendering of spiritual
life—can be excluded without any error. If they mean the latter as well, then
when someone says that the proximate and immediate terminus of that action,
namely life, exists before the will’s cooperation, I do not see how he errs.
Indeed, if someone says that in the will’s very cooperation toward the act of
conversion grace alone is the adequate cause why a person is converted,
because the whole rationale of this efficacy, or of the act insofar as it has
this character—though not insofar simply as it is an act—depends upon and
flows from grace alone, he will not err—unless
Christ is an erroneous guide in John 15:5: “Without me you do nothing;
therefore, from me you do everything.” 4. But their final statement is the
most tortuous of all: “No one actually believes unless the will to believe
precedes.” For the willing to believe is believing itself, and so it is not
clear how the one precedes the other. Perhaps by “the will to believe” they
mean an influence of choice distinct from its terminus. In that sense they
can say, with no less reason, that the will to believe precedes both divine
concurrence and God’s absolute intention; for they teach that this influence
is a condition pre—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 201 | printed p. 191]

—required in order that God should concur, or should will absolutely to
concur, toward the act of faith in its determinate existence. Nicolaas
Grevinckhoven, pp. 117, 214.

4. They say that “the passages produced have nothing to do with the issue,
because they treat the operation alone, not the manner of operation, which
alone is called into question.” But 1. operation and the manner of operation
are in some cases so closely connected that anyone who treats either one
must necessarily note both. 2. They know that the third and fourth theses
are joined in that reply; there is therefore no reason for them to wonder
that the operation is joined with the manner of operation. 3. The manner of
this operation is also declared plainly enough under the title of a free
gift communicated according to God’s good pleasure.

5. On Philippians 1:29 they contend that “actual faith is mentioned, but
they deny that it is produced unless the human will has preceded it in the
act of believing.” I shall allow this answer as much force as it can have:
either it has none, or it has a force I cannot grasp. For why should this
effectual precedence of the will prevent actual faith itself, which follows
so certainly, from being God’s own pure gift?

6. They assume as conceded that “the other gift, of suffering for Christ,
is not irresistible, since the Philippians could have escaped afflictions by
denying Christ. Moreover, the agreement in expression indicates that the
character of each gift is the same.” I readily concede that the character of
each is indeed the same. For the gift does not vary with respect to the thing
itself, but is one and the same; and the gift by which we believe and the gift
by which we suffer differ no more than true faith and constant or courageous
faith. There is indeed some addition of being counted worthy through the
endurance of the cross, but it is no greater than the addition made to grace
through its fruit and testimony. Yet neither is that claim true which they
take as certain, that the Philippians could have escaped these af—

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 202 | printed p. 192]

—flictions by denying Christ. For those through whose sufferings God willed
the gospel to be glorified could not utterly deny Christ; nor, if they denied
him to some extent, would they thereby escape afflictions. They themselves
concede on p. 99 that believers are predestined to the cross. And so that
they should not shrink back because of fear, the Apostle here persuades them
by the same argument he uses in Romans 8:28: “Because all things work
together for good to those who love God,” or believe in God—and this by God’s
free and constant gift. Therefore, since this gift properly relates to
nothing other than antecedent grace and the consequent outcome, the Apostle
rightly admonishes them to stir up that grace within themselves and await the
desired outcome without fear.

7. To Philippians 2:13 they make two replies: 1. “To effect willing and to
effect it irresistibly are incompatible.” 2. “Nor is the accomplishing
effected without our will.” The first of these is manifestly false. For God
causes the will to desire the good in general, whether true or apparent,
which it nevertheless wills so necessarily that it cannot fail to will it.
He causes Christ now to will God’s glory and the church’s salvation in such a
way that he cannot fail to will it. The second reply, therefore, which
depends upon the first, rests upon no foundation; and, as its words stand, it
contains nothing contrary to our position or argument.

8. They say, “If the very accomplishing is given irresistibly, then a
person’s cooperation in the progress of conversion will be no greater than
at its beginning.” That this consequence has no force can be clear even from
the fact that they themselves teach that the mind is always illuminated
irresistibly, yet it is obvious that a person cooperates more in acquiring
knowledge for himself during its progress than at the beginning of the first
illumination.

9. “If the Philippians were sustained by irresistible grace, then fear and
trembling had no use for them,” they

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 203 | printed p. 193]

affirm. But 1. fear and trembling are no more incompatible with the
irresistibility of the cause than with the known infallibility of the outcome.
Yet we see from Christ’s example that they can accord perfectly with the latter, since
although he was utterly certain of victory, he was nevertheless deeply
affected by fear. Hebrews 5:7. 2. “Fear and trembling” ought not to be
understood as uncertain doubt, for the Apostle is not accustomed to exhort
believers to that, but as humility, reverence, and solicitous care. These can
and commonly do accord perfectly with God’s utterly certain predetermination
and even his most effectual procurement of salvation, precisely because they
are a sign, testimony, and means of that effectual procurement already at
work. 3. Someone can be affected by fear while he considers his own powers,
yet by supreme confidence while he contemplates omnipotence and the divine
promise. 4. The manner of divine efficiency is not the adequate rule by
which our actions or affections ought to be directed by us; that rule is the
revealed will of God. Those who neglect God’s precepts and wish to measure
human affections and duties by his operations, which are for the most part
unknown to us, therefore act wrongly and ignorantly.
