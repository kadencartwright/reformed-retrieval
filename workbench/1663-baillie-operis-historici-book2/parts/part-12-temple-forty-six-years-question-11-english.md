## QUESTION XI

### How is the temple said at John 2:20 to have been built in forty-six years, although the temple was completed by Solomon in seven years, 1 Kings 6:38, while the second temple was completed by Zerubbabel, Ezra 6:15, in Darius’s sixth year, after it had been undertaken in Cyrus’s second year and soon interrupted until Darius’s second, Ezra 3:8 and 4:24?

We said certain things about this question above, in book 1, chapter 10, question 13, but this is its proper place, where more points are available to add. The opinions of learned men here, both now and formerly, vary greatly; we shall weigh five or six of them. The first group thinks that people labor in vain, and that whatever doubt or difficulty may be raised here is rightly disregarded. For what John 2:20 says about the temple’s construction over forty-six years is not a statement of the Holy Spirit, but of Christ’s profane enemies. By recounting their statement, the evangelist does not support their ignorance or error. The word of God relates with perfect truth and holiness the things which the devil and the most perverse of men say with utter falsehood and perform with utter impiety. What those wretched unbelievers maliciously snarled at Christ about the temple’s forty-six years of construction proves nothing, nor does it burden anyone who disregards or denies their depraved testimony—especially in the doctrine of chronology, where the most learned Jews and even Christians profess that to this day they have been able to determine few things certainly, clearly, and without controversy. Consequently, not a few think that any questions which can arise from the statement of these unbelieving Jews should be disregarded. I find Lyra, Ribera, Emmanuel Sa, and Lucas Brugensis holding this opinion. Those who pass over all the questions here seem to belong to this class; among them are Cyril of Alexandria, Irenaeus, Cartwright, Heinsius, Hammond, the first English annotators, and others. Those who are minded to follow them safely divest themselves of all trouble, but this flight from labor does not please us, both because

the whole remaining company of learned men does not think this question should be passed over, and also because Lyra, Ribera, and Brugensis themselves, although they judge this forcible untying of the knot sufficient, nevertheless labor no less than others to establish other answers. As for the rest who raise no difficulty, it is very uncertain with what intention they do this. Heinsius, certainly, is an exceedingly learned man, however you regard him, yet throughout his notes he is accustomed to pass untouched over the points that are most doubtful.

The second opinion is Augustine’s. Apparently not satisfying himself with the literal sense, he took refuge in mysteries, following the practice far too common in his age; he professed that in this he followed his predecessors, doubtless meaning Cyprian. He observes a threefold mystery in the temple’s forty-six years. First, the formation in the mother’s womb of Christ’s body, the true temple, as the body of every human being takes forty-six days, by which time all the embryo’s members appear distinct. Then, multiplying forty-six by six produces 276 days, or nine months and six days, at which time Christ was born and every male ordinarily comes forth from the womb into the light. He draws the second mystery from the name *Adam*, which he takes to signify Christ’s flesh in the womb, inasmuch as it was received from the first Adam through his mother Mary. All the letters among the Greeks, he says, signify numbers. Thus the first letter in *Adam*, α, signifies one; the second, δ, signifies four; the third, again α, signifies one; and the fourth, μ, denotes forty. These four numbers added together make forty-six, the time in which the temple was built and Christ’s body perfected in the womb. Not content with these, he adds a third mystery: the gathering of the elect, incorporated into Christ, from the world’s four quarters—east, west, north, and south. For the first letter in *Adam*, α, denotes ἀνατολή, the east; the second, δ, δύσις, the west; the third, α, ἄρκτος, the north; and the fourth, μ, μεσημβρία, the south. Far be it from us to declare ridiculous anything which proceeded from the mouth of such a holy and learned man as either Augustine or Cyprian. Yet these mystical expositions certainly are neither produced from the text nor suited to it. Cornelius a Lapide and other papists, who frequently add and prefer mystical senses to the literal one, embrace these mysteries. But Erasmus, Maldonatus, Toletus, and even other Jesuits candidly admit that they should be rejected as contributing nothing to the verse, although the *Glossa ordinaria*, and before it Strabo’s gloss from the venerable Bede, follow Augustine step for step. The opinion would have been more tolerable had some literal sense been joined to the mysteries, as indeed the gloss, while citing Augustine, does when, before all the mysteries, it interprets the temple’s forty-six years literally of Zerubbabel’s temple, whose construction continued from Cyrus’s first year to Darius’s sixth. But this literal sense is Strabo’s addition from Bede, and you will look for it in vain in Augustine.

The third opinion is Origen’s. Although elsewhere he immerses himself far too deeply in mystical senses, here he follows the letter; but I think he has no follower on the path he takes, except perhaps the outstanding poet Nonnus. They hold that the forty-six years should be understood of Solomon’s first temple. When the seven years in which Solomon’s temple is said to have been completed at 1 Kings 6:38 are objected against them, they think they escape by extending the period of the temple’s construction back to David’s first resolve to build it at 2 Samuel 7, from which time he was continually preparing material for the work’s construction. But this escape is very flimsy, for not even from that time to the temple’s completion by Solomon did forty-six years elapse. And even if the period agreed, the temples were not the same: Solomon’s was utterly destroyed, and the Jews were speaking not of one which had ceased to exist, but of the temple then present.

The fourth opinion is the most common. It understands the forty-six years of the second temple which Zerubbabel built, but various writers explain it variously. Chrysostom dispatches the matter in a few words without descending to particulars, for he says nothing except that those forty-six years of the Jews apply to the second temple, not the first, because the latter was constructed over a period of twenty years. The conclusion, as we shall soon see, is not sound, but the reason on which it is built is much worse, for Scripture expressly assigns no more than seven years to the first temple’s construction. The invention by which Toletus and other papists excuse Chrysostom—namely, that he meant not the bare temple but all the buildings constructed by Solomon—cannot be approved, for there is not a trace or vestige of this invention in Chrysostom. As the Jesuit Pererius correctly notes, Theophylact adds new errors to Chrysostom’s. He holds that Nehemiah’s construction, by which he completed the wall of Jerusalem in fifty-two days at chapter 6:15, was the same as the temple’s construction mentioned by the Jews at John 2. Yet he holds that the building of this temple began not in Cyrus’s first year but in Artaxerxes Longimanus’s, and that the work continued until Darius Nothus’s sixth. These are manifest errors contrary to the clear Scriptures.

These slips in so great a man do not allow us to wonder at the peculiar bypaths of more recent writers. Piscator, a most exact theologian, follows Theophylact’s very erroneous calculation, asserting that the second temple was founded in Artaxerxes’s first year and completed in Darius Nothus’s sixth.

with Cambyses only nine years, to the Magus one, and to Darius Hystaspes only twenty. He next attaches Darius Longimanus, who never lived before Annius’s dreams, assigns him nineteen years, and holds that the temple was finished in his sixth. But since he finds only thirty-six years from Cyrus’s first to Darius Longimanus’s sixth, by a new calculation he prolongs the temple’s construction to forty-nine years—namely, to the end of the nineteenth and final year of that Darius Longimanus begotten by Annius.

But why should I recall Piscator’s or Rollock’s errors, when John Calvin himself, a man of supreme judgment, strays so far from the mark in his answer here? First, he not only assigns these forty-six years to the second temple, but also measures the time of its construction by Daniel’s first seven weeks. Yet in the proper place we clearly demonstrated that the first of Daniel’s seventy weeks did not begin until many years after the temple had been completed.

Second, he raises against himself the objection that Ezra assigns fewer years to the temple’s construction than the forty-nine which seven weeks require. To remove the inconsistency, his only answer is that one must count not merely the years of construction but also those of cessation, during which the work ceased. But even if this is supposed, the number does not rise to forty years, much less forty-six.

Third. He expressly writes that it was completed before the work was interrupted, indeed that the sanctuary had been finished when sacrifices were first offered. How paradoxical! At Ezra 3:5, 8, the altar was erected and sacrifices were offered in the seventh month of the first year after the return, but no foundations of the sanctuary were laid before the second year. Indeed, immediately after the first foundations the whole work was interrupted until the second year of Darius Hystaspes. So far was the sanctuary from being erected as soon as sacrifice began.

Fourth. He holds that the entire second temple was destroyed by Herod and that Herod’s new temple bore the earlier temple’s name for no reason other than common usage. But the entire earlier temple certainly was not destroyed by Herod; otherwise the later one could not by any right have borne the earlier one’s name, and popular speech would have given no foundation for this nomenclature.

Theodore Beza was more cautious here. Although he follows the common opinion and, with most writers, composes the forty-six years from thirty under Cyrus, eight under Cambyses, two under the Magi, and six under Darius, he nevertheless doubts the soundness of this common calculation. He finally professes that he wishes to suspend judgment and to await light here from those more learned in the chronological discipline. This reserve was prudent and highly commendable.

I have noted these peculiar points in several great men who support the fourth opinion. More can be noted in others, but I hasten to the opinion itself as it is defended by most papists and Protestants. Although it is the commonest opinion and has been approved by almost everyone after Bede and Lyra, it does not please me, first, because it supposes that forty-six years elapsed from the year of Cyrus in which, after Babylon was captured, he released the Jews, until Darius Hystaspes’s sixth. This is a most offensive chronological error, as we proved above, for from Cyrus’s first year of the Persian monarchy until Darius’s sixth scarcely twenty-three years are found, if we place any trust in the authors of ancient history.

Second. There is no reason why the years in which construction ceased should be counted among the years of the temple’s construction, while it is admitted that scarcely six were spent on the work. Suppose the work were interrupted for forty, a hundred, or a thousand years: must all these years in which construction was interrupted be said to have been spent on the construction itself? As to the objection from Ezra 5:16, that the temple’s construction continued from the time when Zerubbabel first founded it until Darius’s second year, the ready answer is that this was a slander by profane men against the express truth of Ezra 4:24. For there we read that the work ceased and by no means continued from the first interruption until Darius’s second year.

The fifth opinion, in which we rest, is that the Jews were speaking neither of Solomon’s construction nor of Zerubbabel’s, but of Herod the Great’s building work begun forty-six years before. For in his eighteenth year he began to build a new temple. Whether it was entirely new, as Josephus and many after him hold, or only renovated, as Hegesippus and many more recent writers hold, matters little to the present subject, provided that forty-six years before he began his work of building up the temple then present, in which the Jews were conversing with Christ. It is certainly clear from Josephus that Herod’s eighteenth year, when he began to renovate the temple, was approximately the forty-sixth year before Tiberius’s fifteenth, in which Christ was baptized and held the present conversation with the Jews. Herod did indeed complete that most magnificent work in eight years, if we believe Josephus. Yet according to the same Josephus, something in the way of new ornament was continually being added not only until Herod’s death but for a long time afterward, down to Nero’s fourth year, shortly before the destruction of the city and temple by Titus. Several thousand workmen continued in the temple, some in restoration and some in ornamentation, so that the common people correctly said that forty-six entire years had thus far been spent on the construction of that present temple, which was not yet complete.

So too do many of the more recent papists—Baronius, Estius, a Lapide, and others—for very frequently the most learned papists abandon their own writers and are accustomed to follow ours, though concealing the name.

Casaubon deservedly censures Baronius here for inconsistency and vacillation. After placing all the earlier interpreters in the preceding opinion with praise and condemning Josephus, in his usual fashion, for falsehood because he teaches that Herod built a third temple after Zerubbabel’s second was utterly destroyed, Baronius nevertheless abandons all the interpreters whom he praised and embraces the opinion of Josephus which he had condemned as the true solution of the knot before us.

There remains a sixth opinion, approved by Arias Montanus, the Jesuit Sanches, Cornelius a Lapide, and others. It holds that the forty-six years should not be composed of the years during which the interrupted work lay idle, but only of those actually spent in construction. They try to find these, however, by joining the periods spent on the temple’s restoration in the days of Cyrus, Darius, the Maccabees, Onias the high priest, Herod, and others. This new calculation does not please us, for besides the fact that even in this way the sum of forty-six years is not demonstrated from the smaller periods stated above, Maldonatus, Ribera, and others of their associates appear, not without reason, to dismiss this opinion as absurd.
