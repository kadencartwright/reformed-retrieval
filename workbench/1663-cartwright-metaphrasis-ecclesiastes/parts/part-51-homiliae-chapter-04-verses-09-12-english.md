VERSE 9. Better is a needy and wise boy than an old and foolish king who no
longer knows how to receive admonition.

VERSE 10. For the former comes out of bonds to reign, while the latter is born
poor even in his own kingdom.

VERSE 11. I saw all the living who walk under the sun joining themselves to
that second boy who will stand in the place.

VERSE 12. There is no end to any people; concerning anyone who rules them,
even posterity will not rejoice: for this too is vanity and affliction of
spirit.

The summary concerns the vanity of the royal or imperial condition.

Its parts are the vanity of the royal condition when a foolish king, or any
other such person, reigns.

He confirms the vanity of a foolishly administered royal condition by a
comparison with the greater case: namely, that the condition of an adolescent
or boy—one who is also poor, provided he is wise—is preferable to that of a
king, even though the king is advanced in age. In this comparison he
wonderfully proclaims the praise of wisdom. By wisdom alone, a petty man of
weak age and the most meager condition is preferred to a most august king who
is also to be revered for his gray hair.

Almost nothing is held in greater contempt than boyhood, or indeed youth as
well. It is regarded as so suspect for rashness that whatever counsel proceeds
from young people is rejected as trifling and contemptible. Hence Paul
earnestly admonishes Timothy, who was still young, in 1 Timothy 4, that no one
should despise his youth. That is, he should conduct himself throughout the
whole course of his ministry in such a way that, through virtue and his
vigilance in every respect, he frees himself from the contempt of men which
commonly attaches to so tender an age. For although he had been furnished with
excellent gifts, Paul intimates that he would nevertheless be exposed to the
contempt of proud men unless he exerted himself toward every excellence with
zeal and strength.

But if poverty is also added to this much-despised age, it renders a man far
more vile and abject. Mortals so scorn the poor that splendid deeds done by
them, brave achievements, and even distinguished services to the whole
commonwealth are buried in thankless oblivion. Ecclesiastes 9. What is more,
the poor man is deserted by his own brothers. Proverbs 19:7. By contrast,
those worn out with age are held in honor among all

nations that are not entirely barbarous and savage. Nor in the entire universe
of things is there any condition so exalted and venerable as that of a king or
emperor. Everyone applauds and so admires them that people set even their
faults and bodily deformities before themselves for imitation. The praise of
wisdom, therefore, must be beyond comparison, since it renders a poor boy more
commendable than an old king.

The king's folly is assigned chiefly to this: he does not acquiesce in the
wholesome counsels of others and suffers himself to be admonished by them with
resentment. The first and principal wisdom is to be wise from oneself and from
the treasury of one's own mind. The next best thing is that, if a man lacks
counsel, he allows himself to be governed by the admonitions and counsels of
others who counsel rightly. A man who has neither of these must be remarkably
foolish.

When the Prophet says that the king no longer suffers himself to be admonished,
he indicates plainly enough that princes of advanced age are more unmanageable.
No small cause of this stubbornness and obstinacy is commonly the pernicious
flattery of courtiers. Their minds are so bewitched by it that truth becomes
unwelcome to their ears. Hence the saying that princes have no one—apart from
their horses—who does not speak to them according to their heart's desire. For
horses obey a king or prince who rides them no more than they obey the vilest
slave.

Nor is it surprising that kings, swollen and elated by their greatness, stumble
at this point, since one may observe this headstrong obstinacy in almost all
old men, however poor and dishonored they may be. Hence also these outcries:
“Am I to be taught by that fellow who was only just born and has scarcely come
out of his shell? Am I to be brought under discipline? Am I to be taught from
my cradle?” Of such men it is truly affirmed: “Have you seen a man wise in his
own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him.” By contrast, if a young
man should stumble through the fault of his age, he can generally be turned to
a better opinion by sound counsels, especially when he is wise, as is the one
presented in this passage.

The question here is what, finally, is this wisdom with which a boy or
adolescent can be imbued? The philosophers deny that a young man can be
prudent. Aristotle, *Ethics* 6. Elsewhere Aristotle likewise says that a young
man is not a suitable hearer of civic discipline. Since prudence is acquired
only from long use and experience of affairs, prudence scarcely seems able to
belong to a young man, although it is especially desired in the administration
of affairs. Therefore, by “wise and prudent” in this passage I understand, in
accordance with David and Solomon's customary manner of speaking, a man who
fears God and is well versed in the knowledge of the divine law. Thus, from
this fear of God as from a fountain, other gifts of mind and body flow forth
which make men better prepared to manage affairs well.

Hence David, while still young, professes in Psalm 119, section 13, that by his
study and love of the divine law he was wiser, first, than his enemies; next,
than his teachers; and finally, than those who preceded him in age. Hence also
it is repeatedly said of David that he showed himself prudent in waging war.
To the same point belongs what is left on record concerning Daniel while still
an adolescent and his three companions: that in prudence they far surpassed
the young princes of all the nations.

Finally, the experience of our own times under the profession of the gospel
supports this. A ten-year-old boy now understands more of the arts of
navigation, painting, and architecture than a grown man did in those dark
times of the papal religion. Just as it sometimes happens that gray and white
hairs are sprinkled here and there upon the hair or beard of young men, so
young men are found who have been instructed and imbued with the prudence of
the old. Those who think that, because they are boys and adolescents, it is
not at all incumbent upon them to become godly should pay particular attention
to this.

VERSE 9. Better is a needy and wise boy than an old and foolish king who no
longer knows how to receive admonition.

Above, the Prophet rejected and assailed the miser's lone-wandering and
solitary life. In this verse, however, he prefers the solitude of a young and
poor man—provided he is wise—to a courtly retinue and its din when it is
administered by a foolish king. For everyone, even the poor man's brothers,
flees from him, as Solomon affirms in Proverbs 19:7. Yet there is this
difference: the miser chooses his solitude by his own purpose and will, whereas
solitude befalls the poor man against his will and despite his resistance.

VERSE 10. For the former comes out of bonds to reign, while the latter is born
poor even in his own kingdom.

He confirms the foregoing axiom about the superiority of a poor and wise
adolescent over an old and foolish king from the opposing circumstances of
each. As regards their proper circumstances, he affirms that the prudent young
man, however poor, leapt from prison to a royal throne. Wisdom alone, in which
he excelled, appears to have brought about so great a change. Solomon seems
here to have alluded to the history of Joseph, who was freed from the vilest
and most ignominious prison and placed at the helm of all Egypt. Neither his
captivity, his poverty, nor the low estimation of a foreigner could prevent him
from being declared second to the king on account of his wisdom. The same can
also be confirmed from Daniel, who, though both captive and foreigner, was
raised to the highest summit of the whole Babylonian empire.

What the papists build from this passage in support of their purgatory is
foolish. They interpret the prison as purgatory and the throne to which men
rise from prison as the kingdom of heaven. To say nothing of the fact that the
Prophet's sense and purpose are overturned in this way, it follows from this
interpretation that there is a return from heaven to purgatory; for, the
Prophet says, there is one who is thrust from the throne into prison.

As for the other circumstance of the old and foolish king, which is opposed
to the former, Solomon says that through his foolishness the king became poor
and wretched in his own kingdom. He intended to signify this by the verb “to
be born,” because he likens the king's change to a birth: on account of the
utter misery into which he is cast down from the highest summit of honor, the
king enters, as it were, upon a new and different life. Not a few examples of
this are found among the people of God: Manasseh, Jeconiah, Zedekiah,
Jehoahaz, and others. When they were all led away captive, the eyes of some
were put out and chains and fetters were placed on others. There was also
Nebuchadnezzar, who was cast down from the throne of supreme imperial dignity
to the grazing and lairs of wild beasts, to which his pride cast him down most
ignominiously. This abasement is all the more painful insofar as those who
formerly lived amid pleasures and ease are less able to endure hunger and
labor.

From all these things proceeds what the Apostle confirms in 1 Timothy 4: that
godliness is useful for all things and has promises of the present and future
life. The Holy Spirit's purpose in this passage is to teach that, even in this
life itself, the fear of God is the shortest road to blessedness.

VERSE 11. I saw all the living who walk under the sun joining themselves to
that second boy who will stand in this man's place.

There follows another part concerning the condition of a king who, although
judicious and wise, nevertheless has this vanity clinging to him: his subjects,
seized by a certain satiety and weariness, seek the favor and pleasure of his
son or of some other successor. Thus, gradually withdrawing from the king,

they conform themselves and their pursuits to the will of the son or successor,
so that they devote more labor and diligence to serving them than to the
obedience due to their own prince. For men are so avid for novelty that one
and the same thing, however excellent, cannot please for long. The Apostle
rebukes this vanity and fickleness of men in Hebrews 13 when he admonishes us
to be content with the things present. To the same point belongs the common
saying: “Everyone worships the rising sun and despises the setting sun.”

Hence almost all Israel deserted David and defected to Absalom, placing the
son on the royal throne while his father was still alive. From the same source
flowed Joab's desire to make Adonijah king while his father lived. Evidence of
this can be sought not only in kingdoms and commonwealths but also in great
households. One may often see the servants and attendants of nobles devote
more effort to hunting after the favor of the son and heir than to serving
their master. They follow the heir and flatter him; they corrupt and ruin him
with false and counterfeit praises; if he says yes, they say yes, and if he
says no, they say no; they resolve among themselves to assent to him in
everything. In short, they contribute more to the corruption of his character
than his tutor can contribute toward imbuing him with virtue and piety.

To this point also belongs the rashness of the people of Israel. Their
commonwealth had been most excellently established by God under the rule of
the judges and preserved for several generations, yet by their importunate
desires and rash petition it was changed into a royal government. 1 Samuel 8,
9, 12:12.

VERSE 12. There is no end to any people; concerning anyone who rules them,
even posterity will not rejoice: for this too is vanity and affliction of
spirit.

The Prophet assigns as the cause of this the people's fickleness and
inconstancy. They never rest content with any one ruler, however judicious and
advantageous. He indicates this by saying that there is no end to the people:
as soon as they obtain the prince they had expected, desire for another
immediately seizes them; not much later, once they have had their fill of him,
they make their wishes for some third man. This is what Solomon says will
happen: posterity will grow no less cold in its love toward that second man
than it formerly grew languid in its duty toward his predecessor.

What is written in Matthew 11 confirms this fickleness of the people. At the
beginning of John's ministry everyone eagerly flew to him, left the cities,
and streamed into the wilderness to hear him; afterward, when a little cloud
of affliction followed, they despised him. A still clearer example is seen in
Matthew 27. The whole people had applauded Christ with acclamations as he rode
into Jerusalem, but a few days later the same people demanded that the same
Christ suffer death on the cross. It is therefore very unsafe to place any
support in the people's favor and goodwill. The Prophet says that it is
unreliable to put hope in princes; there is even greater danger in promising
ourselves anything great from the people's favor and goodwill toward us.

It would therefore be worthwhile for a king to take care that his subjects are
instructed in the true knowledge of God. In this way they will obey and submit
not so much from fear of punishment or hope of reward as from conscience
toward God. Those whose inheritance remains suspended in hope and expectation
must also beware of people who proclaim every good thing about them. They
should instead lend their mind and ear to those who blunt their youthful
desires with harsher words. The conclusion is that even royal dignity, which
presents a grand appearance of felicity, contains a very great measure of
vanity and vexation.
