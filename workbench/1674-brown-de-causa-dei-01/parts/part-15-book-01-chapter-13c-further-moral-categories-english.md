From what has been said he gathers three kinds of precepts. Section 4: “Some
precepts,” he says, “flow immediately from the nature of God, and these are of
themselves absolutely and immutably moral, because God cannot deny himself.
Others flow from the image of God in a mediated way, with the constitution of
things intervening; these are not of themselves absolutely and immutably
moral, since they depend upon the will of God, who could have chosen not to
create those things. Nevertheless, they must be called immutable morals to the
extent that God willed the natures of things to be immutable. To that same
extent dispensation has no place in them either, for otherwise he would deny
his will or decree. Finally, some precepts are purely positive.” Reply: We
readily grant that there are certain purely positive precepts and that these
are distinguished from the others. We also concede that all morals are not
equally necessary or equally mutable. But I do not yet understand how the
former are called *mediated* because the constitution of things intervenes,
while the latter are called *immediate* because this constitution apparently
does not intervene, since all precepts presuppose the creation of the rational
creatures to whom they are given. 2. Nor do I understand how all moral precepts
can be said to flow from the nature of God, for whatever flows from God’s
nature flows from it necessarily and always flows from it equally—that is,
without variation, interruption, or deliberation. 3. Nor do I understand how
something can be said to flow from the nature of God and yet, as regards its
immutability, depend upon the nature of created things. I would like to be
instructed here.

He further adds: “It is therefore a highly improper expression, one which
introduces great confusion into the doctrine of virtue and vice and which,
when properly understood, is false and absurd, to call something positive
moral because commanding it is indeed a matter of eternal morality; to say
that there are positive precepts in the Decalogue and nevertheless say that
all those ten precepts are moral, and thus greatly to boast and τραγῳδεῖν,
‘declaim extravagantly,’ about the morality of the Decalogue.” Reply: 1. Since
he himself distinguishes between Immediate and Mediated Morals, calling the
former moral of themselves, absolutely and immutably, but not the latter,
because they depend upon God’s will, why may we not also call some things
*morals* simply speaking, or *natural morals*, and others *positive morals*,
because they depend upon God’s will and possess an agreement in themselves,
although not an absolutely necessary agreement with the created nature of the
human being? Let him show, I ask, how our manner of speaking introduces
greater confusion into the doctrine of virtue and vice, or is more false and
absurd, than his own. 2. I want to know what he understands by eternal
morality. We are not ashamed to say that a particular precept is positive as
regards its completion and particular application, but moral and natural as
regards its foundation and substance, and that it binds everyone until the end
of the world notwithstanding the varied and altered economy of the covenant.
3. We do not say that there is any purely positive precept in the Decalogue.
Thus we can say that all those ten precepts are moral without thereby
τραγῳδεῖν, “declaiming extravagantly.”

He also numbers among Morals those *means* which are necessary for obtaining
some common moral good that God commands us to pursue, or for averting some
common moral evil that God commands us to flee. He illustrates this as follows
on page 6: “Thus,” he says, “in order that justice might be exercised and each
person receive what is his, it was necessary to distinguish ownership of
property, to establish Magistrates, and to create judges who would

[1674 logical printed p. 167 | IA leaf 185 | PDF p. 185]

give each person what is his, and to arrange the other things which pertain to
the second table. They are therefore matters of natural right just as fully as
it is a matter of natural right for a person to care for a neighbor’s goods and
interests as his own, although the form of Magistrates and judgment by which
the commonwealth and justice are administered is positive.” Reply: 1. It can
be asked whether these means flow from the nature or image of God as
immediately as do the Morals for the attainment of which these means were
instituted. This seems to require a negative answer, because these means
evidently flow from the image of God not only with the constitution of things
intervening—through which those common morals flow from this image—but also
with this particular constitution intervening, by which such common moral
goods are to be pursued and evils avoided. For a means to an end always
presupposes the end. Hence it also appears that these means are still less to
be called absolutely and immutably moral of themselves, because they depend
not only upon God’s will but in part upon a free human constitution. From this
it is clear that there are three kinds of morals: some are Immediate, while
the Mediated can be subdivided into *those* which flow from the image of God
with the constitution of things intervening, and therefore depend upon the
will of God, and *those* which require the intervention of ends and thus stand
still farther removed from this image of God. Is this doctrine not fitted to
produce as much confusion as ours? 2. A free human constitution has a place in
the distinction and establishment of Ownership and in the creation of
Magistrates and Judges. For when people made ownership unequal, they could
have made it equal; and he himself acknowledges that the form of Magistrates
and judgment by which the commonwealth and justice are administered is
positive. Yet notwithstanding this free human constitution and determination,
he will acknowledge—because he affirms it—that it is moral to employ and
submit to this means; that everyone is morally bound to give each person his
right in accordance with this free human determination; and that one must
abide by the judgment and sentence of Magistrates whose form is a human
constitution. Why, then, will it not also be moral to observe the weekly
Sabbath in holiness notwithstanding that particular divine constitution
regarding the appointed day? I hope he will not deny that it is moral to spend
some time in the solemn worship of God. Why, then, will it not also be moral
to consecrate the weekly Sabbath to God, since this divine determination is
necessary as a means to that common moral good which God commands us to pursue
in nature itself? Will a divine determination concerning some necessary means
render it non-moral when it would otherwise be moral, although a human
determination can have no such effect? This I certainly cannot understand. Is
a divine determination more hostile to morality than a human determination?

The man who objects to us that we create confusion proposes still another
kind of morals in the following paragraph, when he says that some matters of
Natural Right are such not simply, but *for a particular state of affairs*.
But let us hear the explanation: “It was thus a matter of natural right,” he
says, “that all things should be common so long as nothing had been established
concerning the ownership of property, yet in such a way that people could by
common consent divide those common things among themselves. Once the division
had been established, things which previously were common became the property
of one person or another. Natural right does not indeed assign particular
goods to anyone, but neither does it prohibit such an assignment from being
made by common consent and agreement. Hence ownership of property and moral
justice or injustice concerning it arise.” Reply: 1. Can something, then, be a
matter of Natural Right which can be freely altered by a human being and is

[1674 logical printed p. 168 | IA leaf 186 | PDF p. 186]

altered, or can be altered? 2. He said above that all Mediated morals are
immutably Moral to the extent that God willed the natures of things to be
immutable. Yet here, although the natures of things remain immutable, a change
is nevertheless brought about in the relation of things, and morality thereby
introduced, through a free human constitution. 3. Since it was a matter of
Natural Right that all things should be common, was the division and
establishment of ownership made *necessarily* or *freely*? If *necessarily*,
then Natural Right, it appears, not only does not prohibit this division and
assignment but commands it. How, then, can Natural Right both command that all
things be common and that all things not be common, but that a division be
made? If *freely*, then such a division could have remained unmade. Yet he had
said correctly above that this division, or distinction of ownership, is a
necessary means of obtaining some common moral good and avoiding a common
moral evil, and is therefore moral. 4. Why may I not speak in the same way of
the Sabbath now in controversy and say that, although by Natural Right all
days are equal and equally suitable for the solemn worship of God so long as
there is no particular determination, yet when God saw fit to select a
particular day and destine it for this use, that day—which previously shared
its privileges with all the others—was, once this determination had been made,
properly destined for this use? Although Natural Right assigns no day over the
others to this use, it does not prohibit a determination from being made by
God. And if the matter stands thus, can we say that the observance of this
appointed day also belongs to natural right—not simply, but *for a particular
state of affairs*? I do not yet see what, even according to his own statements,
could prevent this from being said.

Moreover, in theorem 7 on page 7 he says that “whatever is so connected with
moral virtues as not merely to be inseparable from them—for,” he says, “time
and place are also inseparable from them—but to be inseparable in such a way
that their matter too is moral, and reason itself, derived from divine and
human attributes, necessarily requires such appendages of moral obedience,
also pertains to morals. Such appendages,” he says, “are attention, careful
diligence and investigation of God’s will, and precision in observing God’s
will with the greatest possible ἀκριβείᾳ, ‘exactness,’ and therefore working
out one’s salvation with fear and trembling.” We know that all these things
are moral and are also specifically commanded, even though Nature does not
teach them to the Gentiles. But what follows? “Since,” he says, “time and place
belong to divine worship and obedience solely by natural necessity—for, since
we are also composed of bodies and live in time, all our external actions
cannot be performed without time and place—this necessity is not so much moral
as natural.” Reply: We concede that time and place, which necessarily belong
to our external actions, are natural circumstances insofar as they belong to
them in this way. But no one will say that time and place cannot assume a
different character. For the adversaries themselves affirm that the last day
of each week had a ceremonial character and belonged to divine worship and
obedience not by natural necessity, but by constituting a part of ceremonial
worship and divine obedience. And everyone acknowledges that a place too,
namely the temple, was part of ceremonial worship. Hence it is clear that both
time and place can belong to divine worship and obedience in a manner other
than solely by natural necessity. 2. How place and time differ in this respect,
or how time can be moral while place is not, we will show later in Book II,
Chapter XX. At present we say only this:

[1674 logical printed p. 169 | IA leaf 187 | PDF p. 187]

it is certain that something particular is commanded in the fourth precept of
the Decalogue which is not commanded in the other precepts. It is also certain
that some particular time is commanded here; almost every word of the precept
proves this, and no one can deny it. Hence it is clear that this time is
prescribed here in a different way from the way time is commanded in the other
precepts. For in the other precepts which command external worship or external
acts of obedience, time too is commanded as something belonging to them by
natural necessity. Therefore it is not commanded under that conception in
this precept. Further, it is also certain that no precept of the Decalogue
specifically commands in what *place* God is to be worshiped; in this respect,
therefore, the difference between *time* and *place* appears. We have also
partly proved above that all the precepts of the Decalogue are moral, and we
will show it more fully below. Hence it is further clear that *time* has a
moral consideration here. Finally, it is certain that from the beginning of
creation God prescribed nothing concerning place, laid no foundation in
creation for such an observance, and demonstrated none by his own example. We
explained above, and will demonstrate more fully below in Books II and III,
that matters stand differently with respect to time.

“To perform worship at a certain time or place,” he continues, “is no more
moral than to pray to God with certain gestures and words, since neither one
nor the other can be deduced from the attributes of God or the human being. If
there were some gesture of this kind which naturally and of itself represented
a particular virtue, then its observance too would be moral and therefore
immutable. It could no more be omitted, or a contrary gesture employed, than
it would be lawful during prayer to use gestures which, by the general
institution or custom of the Nation in which we live, are accustomed to
express contempt or negligence.” Reply: I do not now dispute whether there are
some gestures so natural that they cannot be omitted in prayers. It is enough
for me that the adversary concedes that it is morally unlawful during prayer
to use gestures which by the general institution or custom of the Nation in
which we live are accustomed to express contempt or negligence. From this it
is clear both that some gestures must be used during prayer and that human
customs or institutions determine which gestures in particular are lawful and
which unlawful; and that, notwithstanding this human determination but by its
mediation, it becomes morally unlawful to use the latter gestures and lawful
to use the former. Hence it is evident that observing the Sabbath to God is no
less morally lawful, and failing to observe it unlawful, even though that
particular day depends upon God’s free determination, since nature requires us
solemnly to worship God at some time. For God’s determination is no more
hostile to morality than a human custom or institution is.

“Thus,” he says, “if fasting were a natural sign of humiliation, it would
certainly be a moral action and a part of divine worship, since without it
profound humiliation before God could not be performed, and it would therefore
be as necessary as that humiliation itself. But since this cannot be said of
any bodily gesture or of fasting, it is clear from this that such things must
not be numbered among morals.” Reply: 1. Since by general human institution or
custom religious Fasting is a sign of solemn humiliation just as surely as
certain gestures are indications of due reverence and a religious disposition,
why will it not be just as morally good to employ religious fasting at a time
of extraordinary and solemn humiliation, and morally evil to neglect it, as
it is morally good to use during prayer those gestures which by the custom of
the place express reverence, and evil to use those which indicate contempt and
negligence? 2. Did not nature itself teach Fasting

[1674 logical printed p. 170 | IA leaf 188 | PDF p. 188]

to the *Ninevites*? Was it not prescribed in every state of the Church, under
the Gospel as well as under the law? See Joel 1:14 and 2:15; Matthew 6:16 and
9:15; 1 Corinthians 7:5; and put into practice in Daniel 9:3 and 10:3; Ezra
8:21; Acts 10:3; 13:2–3; and 14:22.
