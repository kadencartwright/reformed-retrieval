# CHAPTER XVI

## Whether the Evangelical Protestants rightly demonstrate from the Scriptures
that God wills sin to occur while he permits it. The passage in Genesis 45
about the selling of Joseph is examined and vindicated.

How exceedingly dear Jehovah's immaculate holiness is to me, it is enough to
have a witness in heaven. But God has no need at all of the support of our lie.
The papists have certainly burdened the Reformed churches—and above all the
apostolic men John Calvin and Theodore Beza—with the gravest charge of fighting
against God. These include the Rheims Jesuits, Becanus, Gregory of Valencia,
Diego Ruiz, Gabriel Pennottus, Robert Bellarmine, as well as Johann Asselmann,
Johann Forster, Balthasar Meisner, and Mentzer. Jacob Arminius, Arnold Corvinus,
the synodists, and the Remonstrants follow them. Socinus joins their company
with his impure followers, who infer that we maintain God to be the cause and
author of sin because we teach that God foreknows all future contingents in
himself as in their cause. So Peltius reports in his *Harmony*.

predestination. *The Calvinists*, chapter 1, nos. 1–4.]

2. The adversaries distort the state of the question here. For, first,
Bellarmine falsely claims that our theologians maintain God to be the author
of sin, yet says that the question is not “whether God is the author of sin.”
He says, indeed, that Zwingli, Calvin, Peter Martyr, and Beza teach in their
writings that God is by no means the author of sin. 2. Gregory of Valencia
says that the question is “whether God is in any way the cause of sin.” We
consign such a question to the under-*

[Marginal references: Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book 2, chapter 2.
Calvin, *Institutes*, book 1, chapter 17, section 3; on Psalms 105, 25, and 55;
*Instruction against the Libertines*; on Hosea 13:11. Zwingli, *On Providence*.
Peter Martyr, commentary on Romans 1. Beza, response to Castellio, aphorism 8.
Gregory of Valencia. Arminius, *Anti-Perkins*, p. 698.]

[Chapter XVI; printed page 190; PDF page 241]

*world. 3. The same Gregory says that the question is “whether God wills the
evil of fault in the same way as he wills the evil of punishment.” This is not
the question either, for he can positively effect punishment, but he cannot
effect fault in that same way. 4. He also says that the question is “whether
God in any way incites to sin under the aspect of good, so that he wills it.”
But this question is chimerical. For God physically predetermines to the act;
the malice, however, is something consequent upon the act. 5. Vorstius says
that the question is “whether God truly and properly wills us generally to do
the opposite—and wills it by an antecedent and absolute will—of what God
ordinarily commands in his Word under pain of death.” But this question came
into his mind in dreams, since he thinks that we teach there are two
contradictory wills in God, which we by no means teach. The fathers of Trent
pretend that the question is “whether an act of piety is equally the work of
God and the Devil, and conversely”; likewise, “whether a human being bears
himself merely passively in every evil work.” But they do not touch the matter
at issue. For we too teach with them that God's concurrence is required for
the entitative act of sin. But we stand for a concurrence of God that
predetermines the will, so that the glory of governing providence may thus be
secured to God. They imagine that God concurs in the act of believing and
embracing Christ with numerically the same concurrence—though an indifferent,
universal, and collateral one—with which he concurs in the act of unbelief and
rejecting Christ. 7. Whatever they may say to the contrary, the Jesuits and
Arminians bring against us the very objection which, as Beza observes, carnal
people formerly brought against Paul in Romans 3:5: “If sins conduce to God's
glory, not only should they not be punished, but one should even devote oneself
to sin.” Paul does not deny that sins conduce to God's glory, yet they are not
to be done so that good might come from them. The same objection is made to
Paul in Romans 6:1 and Romans 9:14–15. The adversaries in this question are
therefore carnal. And Augustine had the same question with Julian, as the most
learned Dr. Pareus observes: “Whether God, while preserving liberty, governs
and inclines free wills to good and evil.” Augustine, as do we, says yes;
Julian, like the Jesuits and Arminians, says no.

Julian*, book 5, chapter 3. Pareus, commentary on Romans, chapter 3, doubt 19.]

3. There is nevertheless agreement in this question: 1. That God foreknows
sins, except that we hold the Arminians, Vorstius, Episcopius, and Corvinus not
merely suspect but guilty of denying the foreknowledge of future sins and of
all future contingents. 2. That God

[Marginal references: Vorstius, *On God*, at disputation 6. Episcopius, private
disputation 4, thesis 10. Corvinus, against Molinaeus, chapter 6, question 9.]

[Chapter XVI; printed page 191; PDF page 242]

concurs in the material acts of sin, except that the unfortunate Capuchin
Louis of Dole, who recently appeared, has revived the opinion of Durandus,
which denies God's immediate concurrence. 3. There is agreement that God
directs sins to his glory, although there is a wide difference between us in
the manner of that direction. 4. That God neither effects nor causes sin. 5.
That God permits sins willingly and knowingly. 6. The question, therefore, is
“whether God by some positive act of his permissive will wills sin as a means
through which God has decreed to declare his glory.” The adversaries deny it;
we affirm it. This is to ask: “Whether God willed and decreed that sin should
occur, with himself permitting and a rational creature committing it.” We
defend the affirmative part. They, however, wish God in no way to will sin,
but to bear himself negatively—that is, as plainly unwilling and willing with
respect to sin. 2. After sin has occurred against God's will, they say that
God's counsel concerning the direction to his glory of a sin now existing
apart from God's counsel and decree then intervenes and arises. 3. They say
that God idly permits sin to enter the world. 4. They then commit it entirely
to the creature's liberty whether sin exists or does not exist; and they
subordinate to a created free choice that is wholly indifferent and suspended,
and depends in no way upon God's bending it to the unwilling or willing of his
providence, whether the incarnation of God occurs or does not occur, whether
the glory of punitive justice, pardoning mercy, and grace that grants
repentance is manifested or is not manifested—just as it pleases angels and
little men. Calvin expressly says more than once that “God wills no evil to
occur; God is not the author of sin; sin does not please God; he is infinitely
removed from wickedness; and one must prudently distinguish between the work
of God and that of human beings.” So too Beza, Junius, Zanchius, Ursinus,
Trelcatius, and the Leiden professors. Dr. Twisse should also be consulted.

I therefore argue first from the sacred oracles as follows. If the wicked
sending and selling of Joseph into Egypt occurred by God's counsel and decree,
then sins occur because God wills and decrees them—namely, according to his
permitting, though not approving, will. But the

[Marginal summary: Argument 1, from the authority of the Scriptures.]

[Chapter XVI; printed page 192; PDF page 243]

former is true. Therefore the latter is also true. The major is virtually the
thesis itself. The minor is evident from Genesis 47:7: “Thus God sent me before
you,” Joseph says; “therefore it was not you who sent me here, but God himself,
who made me a father to Pharaoh.” Chapter 50, verse 19: “Joseph said to his
brothers, ‘Am I in the place of God?’” Verse 20: “You indeed intended evil
against me, but God intended it for good.”

The Jesuits attempt many things against this argument but make no progress. 1.
Leonard Lessius says: “God brings about evil by permission, and Joseph says,
‘I do not ascribe to your counsel as the primary cause the fact that I was sent
here, but to God's will.’ Yet it is not thereby established that the crime
itself is from God's counsel.”

[Marginal reference: Leonard Lessius, *On the Efficacy of Grace*, section 2,
on predefinition, argument 8, no. 9. First objection.]

I answer: 1. If Joseph's sending into Egypt occurred by bare permission and not
by God's counsel, then the Jesuit's gloss contradicts the text. For the text
says, “It was not you who sent me here, but God himself.” But the Jesuit's
meaning is: God did not send me here by any act of his counsel; rather, he
permitted it—that is, he did not will that you, moved by envy, and he did not
will that the Ishmaelites, having bought a slave from desire for gain, should
send me here. 2. What Joseph denies in the text should be attributed to the
brothers' counsel, he attributes by a neat and elegant antithesis to God's
counsel as the predetermining cause. This is evident from the words: “Not you,
but God sent me here.” But Joseph denies that the fact he was sent into Egypt
should be attributed to his brothers and their counsel as the determining
cause. Therefore, he attributes his being sent into Egypt to God's counsel. 3.
“I do not ascribe to your counsel,” says the Jesuit according to his own
meaning, “as the primary cause the fact that I was sent here, but to God's
will”—that is, as the primary cause. But that sending, in relation to Joseph's
brothers who sent him, was a grave sin. God's will will therefore be the cause,
and indeed the primary cause, of that criminal sending. Let the Jesuit take
notice. 4. The Jesuit adds that “it is not thereby established that the crime
itself is from God's counsel.” This conclusion has been dishonestly introduced
to make us odious, for we teach that the thing which is a crime, not the crime
itself, is to be attributed to God's counsel.

The second respondent to the argument is the Jesuit Diego Ruiz, who says: “It
belongs to God's providence that God does not allow vice to be unconducive to
the declaration of his glory, and God permitted the patriarchs to sell Joseph
so that he might thereby receive an occasion for effecting good, as Clement of
Alexandria says.” And in disputation 44, section 7, he says that Joseph in no
way

[Marginal references: Second objection. Diego Ruiz, volume *On the Will of
God*, disputation 18, section 2; disputation 44, section 7. Clement of
Alexandria, *Stromata* 1, and the same, section 7, nos. 4–5.]

[Source note: Both IA and BSB print “Genesis 47:7,” although the quoted words
belong to Genesis 45:7–8. The printed reading has been retained.]

[Chapter XVI; printed page 193; PDF page 244]

favors physical predetermination but rather conflicts with it, since he
referred the evil intentions to the brothers—‘You intended evil against
me’—and not to God as author or as one who impels to evil actions. The meaning
is that the omnipotence of the divine will is so great, and it is so impossible
to resist it, that he converted the murder and slavery which you intended
against me into dominion and majesty and into your own benefit.”

I answer: God does not properly allow or permit, but positively effects, the
good of wisdom, justice, and pardoning mercy which he elicits from sin. For, I
suppose, the conduciveness in sin to declaring God's glory is not sin; neither
is it the creature's work, but God's good work. Clement of Alexandria therefore
relates permission to sin itself, not—as the Jesuit wrongly does—to the good of
conduciveness which God elicits from sin. 2. If God “permitted the brothers to
sell Joseph so that he might thereby receive an occasion for effecting good,”
as the Jesuit says, then God in some way intended and willed that wicked sale
for the sake of the good to be elicited from it. This is precisely our point.
And by the same reasoning, he decreed to permit Adam's first fall so that he
might thereby receive an occasion for the glorious incarnation—which is to
wound so that a gracious occasion might be afforded to the art of healing. Yet
this is the very thing which they heartily condemn in Calvin, Beza, and our
theologians and banish from heaven. 3. The Jesuit preaches charmingly about
“the omnipotence of God's irresistible will in exalting Joseph and feeding
God's church.” But the whole cohort of Jesuits denies that the omnipotence of
God's irresistible will acts upon the free acts of human beings. Yet the
Ishmaelites freely bought Joseph; they could have refrained from buying him.
They freely sold him in Egypt; they could have refrained from selling him.
Pharaoh, I suppose, freely raised to honors a foreigner, a Hebrew who was
unwelcome and odious to the Egyptian nation. Joseph freely—and meritoriously,
if the papists are to be believed—fed by a work of supererogation the brothers
who had treated him badly. It could therefore have happened that all these
free agents resisted God's irresistible will, by which he willed Joseph to be
sold in Egypt and raised by Pharaoh, with this omnipotence injured and broken.
We ask, then, that the Jesuit show us God's irresistible will concerning
Joseph's being sold and, once sold, exalted—unless he asserts either that the
wills of those who freely sold and freely exalted him were physically
predetermined by God, or that—and this will satisfy us, for I do not con-*

[Chapter XVI; printed page 194; PDF page 245]

*tend about the word “predetermination,” if the reality is established—God so
acts upon their free wills that they
could no more have failed to sell Joseph or failed to exalt him than it could
be possible to resist and oppose God's omnipotent will, if the effect is
referred to God's decree.

The third respondent is Luis de Molina, creator of middle knowledge and chief
source of the disturbances. He says: “The meaning is that God indeed willed by
an absolute will to exalt Joseph—if not in that way, certainly in any other
way—and, since God foresaw that his brothers' betrayal would be a suitable
means to that end, he did not will to hinder it. For this reason Joseph's sale,
which occurred while God merely permitted it, is ascribed to God.”

[Marginal reference: Third objection. Molina, *On Concurrence*, question 19,
article 6, disputation 2.]

I answer: 1. Is this, I ask, God's inexplicable and profound providence? By a
bare act of foreknowledge God knows that Joseph is to be sold and sent into
Egypt before he wills him to be sold or sent; he wills neither that he be sold
nor that he be sent, but merely does not desire the sale to be prevented. It
is as though I exercised providence—away with the blasphemy!—concerning
tomorrow's sunrise, since I know beforehand the moment at which it will rise
and know the means of its preceding course, yet do not desire its rising to be
prevented. Such is Molina's providence. 2. How is it established for the
Jesuit that God “willed by an absolute will that Joseph be exalted”? Could
Joseph have rejected the pagan king's honors, as we read Moses formerly did?
For he accepted the honors freely. But in the Jesuit school it is absurd for
God absolutely to will and decree a creature's free acts. 3. The Jesuit's
meaning and gloss destroy the text. For Joseph was not only exalted by God's
counsel; this exaltation was advanced through those determinate means, and not
through various alternatives if these had failed—namely, through the sale into
Egypt and Pharaoh's favorable disposition toward him. And this was by God's
singular providence, as the words of chapter 45:5 proclaim: “But now do not
torment yourselves with grief, nor let it be displeasing in your eyes that you
sold me here; for God sent me here before you to preserve life.” Chapter 50:
“You indeed intended evil against me”—namely, murder and slavery—“but God
intended it for good,” namely, by his wise counsel. God therefore did not
decree Joseph's exaltation in general through means under an ambiguous
disjunction in God's mind—either these, or if these fail, others, or if those
others fail, still others. For if all the means are contingent, they can all
fail without end, and God's absolute will would thus be cut off. That is
absurd. If the-*

[Marginal references and summary: Hebrews 11:24–25. Means under a disjunction
conflict with God's perfect counsel.]

*means are necessary, they could not render Joseph's will in accepting the
honors and in pardoning and feeding his brothers uncoerced but free, in the
manner of the freest and—as they hold—meritorious obedience. But Moses refers
these determinate means to divine providence, including the sending itself:
“God sent me here.” 4. What, I ask, is the suitability of the means for
Joseph's exaltation? This suitability, of course, was not willed by God and not
made, but foreknown by a bare act of middle knowledge. God therefore foresaw
the suitability; he did not make it. O drowsy providence! The godly man is sold
from his brothers' envy; the Ishmaelites buy him in hope of a small profit; he
is carried down into Egypt, into Potiphar's household; a harlot, mad with
unspeakable lust for him and unable to attain her desire, contrives by an
invented lie to have God's innocent servant thrown into prison; and Pharaoh,
understanding him to be a divine seer and interpreter of dreams, raises him to
the highest honors after he interprets Pharaoh's dream. What does God do here?
He foreknows everything and does not hinder these most suitable means before
an act of the divine will. The Jesuit says nothing of decree and counsel,
nothing of active direction, nothing of the bending of human wills. The whole
matter occurs by the bare act of middle foreknowledge alone and by
non-hindrance. God's servant certainly ought to ascribe to the divine will
nothing of the glory of God's wise counsel and nothing belonging to faith,
hope, and patience. Everything was actual and future before God's middle
knowledge—the most suitable means that would produce Joseph's exaltation and
the feeding of the church—and thus before God's will that does not hinder but
permits. Everything occurs without God. O execrable blasphemy! O supine
providence! Yet according to Jesuit dogmas, God cannot even prevent free
disobedience.

The fourth respondent here is Theodor Smising, a Jesuit of Louvain. He says:
“Can we resist God's will? That is, can we prevent him from eliciting out of my
wicked sale the result that through me he saves many peoples? In reality,
however, that will of God is not directed to the sale.” And in the same place:
“Nor is Joseph said to have been sold by God's will, but to have been sent into
Egypt for the salvation of his family. Hence only Joseph's transfer into
Egypt, and not the fact that it occurred through the wicked sale, was God's
work.”

[Marginal reference: Fourth objection. Theodor Smising, vol. 1, treatise 3,
question 3, no. 94; 11, 45, on I Thomas, first part.]

I answer: 1. Neither are we the people who call a wicked work the work of the
most holy God. Therefore, this conclusion is not against us.

2. The free will of the Ishmaelites who bought him, Pharaoh who raised him to
honors, and Joseph himself, who accepted the honors and dutifully fed the brothers who had
treated him badly—all their free wills, I say, could have resisted God's will
according to the adversaries. They could therefore have prevented this good
event, namely the feeding of the church, from arising out of Joseph's wicked
sale. For Joseph could have refrained from feeding those whom he freely fed,
and Pharaoh could have refused Joseph the honors and wealth from which he
would supply food to the church. 3. In this way the Jesuit clouds the glory of
providence: God elicited so eminent a good—the nourishment of the starving
church—from Joseph's journey into Egypt insofar as that journey is simply good
and free of sin. But what praise is there in eliciting good from good, or life
from life? The deeper abyss of providence here is that God omnipotently elicited
the glory of the church's preservation out of Joseph's unjust sale and
slavery. 4. This gloss also conflicts with the text, because the patriarchs'
consciences were stung not by Joseph's good and just journey into Egypt, but by
the fact that through a wicked sale they had cast their innocent brother out
of their father's family and sent him there. Joseph comforts them on the very
ground that, from his singular providence, God had elicited a far more eminent
good from their wicked sale: “Do not torment yourselves because you sold me
here”—most wickedly. Nor are there two acts here, Joseph's journey into Egypt
and his journey through a wicked sale. It was one and the same journey, wicked
in relation to the brothers but willed and most justly decreed in relation to
God, so that by the singular and holy art of providence God might elicit the
straightest end—the preservation of the church—from this most crooked means.

[Marginal summary: God acted not from Joseph's sale considered simply, but from
his wicked sale.]

Fifth, Francis Cumel of Louvain replies. He acknowledges that “God absolutely
predefined and decreed Joseph's sale as regards the material and entitative
acts of sin, but not as regards the malice annexed to the acts.”

[Marginal reference: Fifth objection. Francis Cumel, *Various Disputations on
I-II*, vol. 2, conclusion 2, p. 294.]

I answer: Even so, the glory of providence is meager if God elicited his glory
from acts that are entitatively good. For it is not the entitative acts but the
malice adhering to the acts which is punishable and pardonable and which is the
intrinsic material for declaring the glory of punitive justice and pardoning
mercy. Nor did the entitative acts of hatred and envy toward their brother burn
and torment the patriarchs' consciences. Rather, consciousness of the malice
adhering to the act troubled them with grief over the deed and fear of
retaliatory vengeance.

[Chapter XVI; printed page 197; PDF page 248]

Sixth, Bellarmine and Arnold Corvinus, a follower of Arminius, must join the
rest in opposing the truth. “Nothing else is said here except that God foresaw
the evil wills of Joseph's brothers. Thus the sale itself is not attributed to
God except by bare permission; only the good event resulting from the sale is
ascribed to God.”

[Marginal references: Sixth objection. Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book
2, chapter o. Corvinus, against Molinaeus, chapter 3, question 6.]

[Source note: Both witnesses print “chapter o” in the marginal citation; the
unusual reference has not been silently changed to a numeral.]

I answer: 1. The gloss destroys the text. The text says that Joseph's brothers'
intention and God's intention concerned one and the same means, but with widely
different ends and manners: “You intended evil against me; God intended good,”
and so forth. God's intention was therefore directed to the sale itself as a
means to a good end, just as their intention was directed to the same sale as
a means to an evil end. 2. By this gloss the meaning of “It was not you who
sent me here, but God” will be: It was not God, but you; you alone sent me
here. God in no way willed the sale but merely permitted it—that is, did not
will it. And “God sent me” must be expounded as: “God neither sent me nor
willed me to be sent.” 3. Nor is any efficacy of the good event granted to God
here. For all the free agents—the patriarchs, the Ishmaelites, Potiphar, the
harlot, and Pharaoh—could have refrained from doing what is in this chain or
could have done its opposite. Thus not even the event can be attributed to God
as its efficient cause. What the sophists say tends in this direction: in
Joseph's sale, God bears himself “as the will of a sleeping peasant,” as Daniel
Chamier observes, “bears itself toward the sun's entrance into Capricorn or
Sagittarius.” The man sleeps, entirely unconcerned about the solar motion. So,
while God does not yet know and does not yet will—for the object of middle
knowledge is by nature prior to the knowledge itself and thus much more prior
to God's decree—Joseph is sold, thrown into prison, oppressed by the infamy of
adultery, and promoted to honors before God foreknows or wills all these things
to occur.

[Marginal summary and reference: According to the adversaries, the efficacy of
evil events cannot be ascribed to God. Chamier, *Catholic Panstratia*, on the
nature of sin, book 3, chapter 4, section 6.]

At last the seventh respondent, Jacob Arminius, comes down into the battle
line. He says: “In addition to that permission and the presentation of
arguments and occasions added to permission, and direction and determination,
and even concurrence, God is said himself to do the evils perpetrated by human
beings and Satan—to have sent Joseph into Egypt and taken Job's goods.”
Meanwhile he maintains that God in no way wills sins, but merely permits and
does not hinder them.

[Marginal reference and summary: Arminius, *On the Efficacy of Providence*,
disputations 11 and 12. Arminius's lax permission added to permission is a
mockery.]

I answer: 1. According to Arminius, the direction of Joseph's sale is later
than the sale itself. Therefore, because of its direction, the sale can-*

[Chapter XVI; printed page 198; PDF page 249]

*not be said to be from God. For the burning of a house is not from the resident
merely because he uses the ashes of the burned house to fertilize fields. 2.
The presentation of arguments is merely “permission added to permission.” For
it was not God, but the Devil and corrupt reason—as I know well enough—that
persuaded the patriarchs to sell their brother. Otherwise, they ought to have
obeyed God when he persuaded them. What follows? In the law, under pain of
eternal death, God dissuades us from injuring our brother. This “presentation
of arguments” was therefore made through God's permission, and the same
question will recur concerning it. 3. Nor is the “determination of sin” with
respect to place, time, and degree of malice anything other than “permission
added to permission.” Arminius thus deceives and entices us with an empty hope.
For this determination is either the bare non-willing and non-effecting of sin,
and thus is still merely “permission added to permission”; or it is some
positive act of the divine will by which God wills Joseph's sale to exist at
such a place and time and to such a measure—say, at degree 5 rather than 4 or
6. But one who positively wills sin to coexist with such a place and time wills
sin to exist, because the coexistence of sin is a certain existence. 4. In
Corvinus's definition, the determination of sin is “permission that the
creature most ready to sin should perpetrate this rather than that.” I do not
now examine how narrow and at the same time loose this definition is. But, 1.
if the determination of sin is the permission of sin, determination is merely
“permission added to permission.” If it is free for a sinner to elicit sin to
degree 6, it is also free for him to commit sin to degree 8, 100, or 1,000. And
if God can prevent sin from swelling to more degrees than seems good to God, he
can simply prevent sin from occurring. I leave the adversaries to investigate
how God can prevent sin. Is it by a physical impediment? Then the will will not
be free, for a physical obstacle is thus placed before it. Is it by a moral
impediment? But that is not efficacious and does not efficaciously determine
the will not to sin. 3. To determine sin sometimes means to set a limit to sin
so that it does not grow further. But permission by which the creature is
allowed to perpetrate this sin rather than that is not to set a limit to sin,
but to loosen its reins. 4. It is a thorny question how God determines sin,
both as to the measure of its magnitude and as to the measure of its duration.
If it is free for the will to elicit sin to degree 3, then it is also free to
elicit sin to degree 100 or 1,000.

[Marginal reference and summary: Corvinus, against Molinaeus, chapter 3,
question 18. How, by ascribing the determination of sin to God, Arminius makes
God the author of sin.]

[Chapter XVI; printed page 199; PDF page 250 — only the material above the
rule]

But if God effects that sin be committed to degree 6 rather than degree 4,
God will be the author of sin. One who is the cause of a graver sin is the
cause of sin simply, for the inference from the comparative degree to the
positive is valid. The same holds if he is the cause determining the sinner to
this object rather than that—for example, if he effects that the Jews rage
against Christ rather than Barabbas, he is the cause of the graver sin, since
the gravity of sin is often derived from its object. But let the Arminians
solve this too. God determined that the Chaldeans should hold his people
captive for seventy years rather than for two or three months. But if holding
God's people captive for one year is a sin, holding them for two is a graver
sin; therefore, holding them for seventy is the gravest. Let them see how God,
by bare permission alone, determines sin to seventy years. 5. Arminius cannot
in any way ascribe Joseph's sale to God's concurrence as something added over
and above divine permission, because an act of sin follows no more from
Arminian indifferent and universal concurrence than does an act of obedience.
