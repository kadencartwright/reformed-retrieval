CHAPTER XXIII

The Same Is Proved by Other Arguments

Whatever contributes something to the fullness, ornament, and splendor of the
universe, GOD willed to exist with himself permitting it. But sin contributes
something to the fullness, ornament, and splendor of the universe. Therefore,
and so forth. The reason for the major premise is that the fullness and
splendor of the universe is a good willed and intended in itself by him who
created all things by weight, number, and measure. Therefore all the means
conducing to this end will in some manner be willed by GOD. The minor premise
is certain, because if there had never been sin, many things would have been
lacking to the world: namely, the incarnation of CHRIST, calling by grace,
gratuitous justification, the Church of the redeemed, CHRIST’s death,
resurrection, and glorious triumph over death, sin, devils, and hell; the
repentance and perseverance of the Elect; the patience of the martyrs; and so
forth. There also would have been lacking the glory of justice which punishes,
of mercy which spares, of omnipotence which frees the Church and subdues
CHRIST’s enemies, of longsuffering, of GOD’s truthfulness, and so forth. So say
Eusebius, Lactantius, Augustine, and Isidore. Likewise Aquinas and the Coimbra
Doctors say *“that the good of the universe would have been impeded, and that
there would have been neither avenging justice nor suffering patience if
iniquity were absent.”* Corvinus answers that Thomas means *“not sin itself,
but the permission of sin, contributes to the integrity of the world.”* I
answer: from a permission of the kind the adversaries imagine—which, once
posited, permits it to occur that there is never any sin—the integrity of the
world follows no more than the world’s non-integrity. 2. Nor is it permission,
except remotely and consequently, but permitted sin itself that-*

*completes the universe. The saints are not patient because Tyrants are
permitted to persecute, but because they actually persecute; nor did CHRIST
come in the flesh to remove permission, but to destroy the work of the devil.
3. Beings are threefold. Some belong to the essential constitution of the
world, such as the elements, rational creatures, and celestial bodies. 2. Some
contribute to the integrity of the world, such as composite bodies. 3. Some
belong to the accidental integrity of the world, such as privations.* It is in
this respect that our theologians Beza, Zwingli, Festus Hommius, and
Triglandius, and also Báñez, Medina, Rispolis, and Álvarez, say that sins
contribute something to the ornament and splendor of the world. Yet here we do
not speak of the first world, created with Adam and fallen, but of the second
world, redeemed and restored in CHRIST. *“And this occurs so that nothing in
this kingdom,”* says Boethius, *“may be permitted to rashness.”* Corvinus
answers that *“if sin contributed anything to the universe, GOD would then be
obliged to promote sin so that nothing be lacking to his work; and that sin is
the cause why the world, subjected to vanity, fell from its perfection.”* I
answer: 1. GOD enriched the world with this fullness in which it now shines by
no debt, but by his wise good pleasure alone. Who are you to bind GOD with the
bond of debt? 2. As far as he can while preserving his holiness, GOD so
governs the world that sin occurs with his permission; but it is not necessary
that he effectively promote it. 3. In the genus of meritorious cause, it is
true that *“sin is the cause why the world, made subject to vanity, fell from
its perfection.”* But this does not prevent sin, in another genus of cause—
namely, a material or occasional cause, for an occasion has a certain
causality—from being a cause of the fullness and splendor of the universe to be
constituted, with respect to the number τῶν ὄντων, “of beings,” not in the first
world but in the second world restored by CHRIST. For if sin were absent, many
things would be absent from the world which are of no small benefit and
ornament to it. And although sin is evil for the sinner, it is nevertheless
good for the universe. *“Black,”* as Hugh and, with him, Ferrariensis say, *“is
not good for white but evil; nevertheless, it is good and a splendid ornament
for an image or picture composed of various colors.”*

Argument 8. If GOD has entirely committed it to the choice of the Sabeans,
Chaldeans, Assyrians, Absalom, and Shimei to choose whether to inflict grievous
evils upon the Saints—with GOD not willing but merely permitting—then it
follows that the prayers of the Saints in which they ask that those evils be
averted are ineffectual-*

[Marginal argument VIII: GOD’s idle permission conflicts with fervor and
earnestness in prayer; it also conflicts with GOD’s omnipotence.]

[Chapter XXIII; printed page 358; IA PDF page 417; BSB canvas 410]

*but the latter is absurd. Therefore the former is also absurd. The major
premise is certain. 1. For I pray to GOD in vain that he avert hatreds,
robberies, and persecutions from me if they befall me when GOD neither wills
nor decrees them. And such prayers are empty if GOD cannot prevent the hatreds
and unjust counsels of enemies unless he strips their wills of freedom, which
is a marvel. To what end would the Saints pray for the aversion of a cup which
they believe to have been mixed and commanded by GOD’s own will and hand, but
offered to them by the hatred and malice of their enemies, if it must be drunk
by no will of GOD but only by bare permission? For if it is not GOD’s will that
the wicked inflict any evil upon the saints, neither will it be his will that
they suffer any evil from them. For GOD does not will τὸ occidere justum, “the
killing of the just person,” nor does he will τὸ occidi justum, “the just
person’s being killed.” Therefore let the saint pray: *“Grant, Lord, that a
wicked person not pursue me—who have merited no evil from him—with hatred,
envy, wounds, or death.”* Yet GOD must answer: “It is not my will that the
wicked person hate you or sin against you. I cannot aid you, for when every
action of mine whatsoever is posited, freedom reigns: he can sin against you,
and he can refrain from sinning.” The Jesuit Raynaudus frankly admits this.
*“GOD,”* he says, *“cannot prevent sin while preserving the order of the
universe and the office of the most universal cause.”* Indeed, Theodore Smising
says that *“GOD can prevent sin neither physically nor morally,”* as I cited
above.
