But by what arguments, I ask—to touch on that point in passing—are we to suppose that Dr. Beza’s opinion was stabbed through, and by what solutions of Arminius his arguments were robbed of their force? I shall ensure, reader, that you form a conjecture from the analysis he composed of Romans 9. When, at verse 21, he had mentioned Dr. Beza’s interpretation, by which under the name of the lump he understands the human race not yet created, much less corrupted, please see and diligently weigh what he adds: “We know,” he says, “that Augustine holds a different opinion, interpreting it as the lump of perdition; and if anyone wishes to deny it, that proof which Beza adduces will not suffice. For otherwise the Apostle would have had to say that some vessels were left in this dishonor and others transferred from this dishonor to honor. But I am willing to grant him that unformed matter is signified by the word *lump*. For simply to say that God does not have power to make from one lump a vessel for wrath and a vessel for mercy—to make a man who is to be hardened by an irresistible will and a man who is to be attended with mercy—does not seem sufficiently safe to me.”

A splendid refutation indeed! First he brings forward just one of Beza’s many proofs. Next, concerning that proof, he suggests nothing other than that it is insufficient, but does not indicate in even a single word why it is insufficient. Then he subscribes to that same opinion to which not a few of our theologians, men at once most pious and most learned, do not yet subscribe, although in other respects they have willingly embraced Beza’s opinion on predestination. What then? Are these things to count as a proper refutation? Indeed, what follows is that refutation explained over two leaves: “Although this may be admitted, it does not therefore follow that the object of predestination is the lump not yet created.” But who other than Arminius would ever deny that inference? If from a lump not yet created God makes some people vessels of wrath and others vessels of mercy, did he not from a lump not yet created destine some for the purpose of exercising mercy upon them, and devote others to the purpose of exercising wrath upon them? “No,” he says, “it does not follow.” But do not the very words indicate this—namely, *for wrath*, God’s intention toward their destruction, and *for mercy*, God’s intention toward their salvation? “They do not indicate it at all, as I explain and teach in this way: to be created for wrath is to be created a vessel fitted for wrath; to be created for mercy is to be created a vessel fitted for mercy.” But I reply that on this reasoning God either created no people at all or indiscriminately created all people both vessels of wrath and vessels of mercy. For if he intends this to be said of immediate aptitude, no one has been created from the bare lump either a vessel of wrath or a vessel of mercy, because none are immediately fitted for wrath or mercy except sinners; yet God certainly did not create anyone as sinners. But if he is speaking of a mediate aptitude which could supervene in due time, then all people were created fitted for wrath and all fitted for mercy. For all were created in such a way that they could become sinners and consequently be prepared either for wrath or for mercy.

But let us hear him discuss the matter a little more fully: “A man existing in the state of corruption, together with all the descendants whom God had determined to bring forth from his blood in the ordinary way, deserved that God should render him wrath and punishment according to his deserts; that is, actually make him a vessel of wrath. But God’s goodness did not permit this, and so forth. Wisdom therefore found a way by which the cause might be rendered what it deserved and goodness what befitted it: namely, Jesus Christ the Mediator. But justice demonstrated that it was equitable to establish a condition according to which that benefit might or might not be communicated from mercy. This condition, however, was faith. And hence it was decreed to make some men vessels of wrath and others vessels of mercy, that is, fitted for wrath or for mercy.”

This, then, is that opinion into which Arminius was carried by “the divine rod and the leading of the Holy Spirit,” as Bertius rhetorizes, when he was laboring at a refutation of the Delft brethren’s writing and of their reply to certain arguments of Calvin and Beza from the treatise on predestination concerning Romans 9. This, namely, is to create some vessels of wrath and others vessels of mercy, and to do so from a lump not yet created, even though he expressly and pointedly indicates that this condition presupposes both the fall of men in Adam and each person’s faith in Christ or unbelief. For according to Arminius, to render wrath and punishment according to deserts is actually to make a vessel of wrath; and by the same reasoning, to render destruction to unbelievers according to desert and salvation to believers from mercy will actually be to make vessels of wrath and mercy according to him. And yet this candid and modest teacher is not at all ashamed to affirm that God makes some vessels of wrath and others vessels of mercy from a lump not yet created. Please see whether this is to exchange an old opinion for a new one when conquered by the force of truth, as those craftsmen of deceits tell the tale, and not rather to be swept away by the dizziness of error, so that he defends and maintains the opinion he had long cherished in his bosom, while at least retaining the appearance of the apostolic saying’s interpretation concerning a lump not yet created—an interpretation he was unable to oppose—and thus to be borne as if by a whirlwind into sheer drops and precipices. But let them keep their devices; let them, if they please, relish even the depths of Satan to the point of nausea and disgust. God will meanwhile keep us safe from their snares and ambushes, provided we attend to what divine truth dictates and take no trouble over what the mind of a vain man invents; he himself will also provide that we do so.

He proceeds with the same candor to profess that not every scruple has been removed for him by this writing, as if he still embraced the same opinion as Perkins on predestination, though not without a scruple. For if he held a different opinion, how absurd it would be for him to say that his mind was troubled by certain scruples arising from this opinion! Next he declares that not every scruple can be removed; he therefore signifies that some scruples can be removed. But which ones, I ask? Would that he had noted them. For throughout that examination of his I cannot discover what they were. Indeed, throughout this writing he confirms the very things which he calls scruples and uses them as proper reasons and arguments to overthrow Perkins’s opinion. Nor does he anywhere acknowledge Perkins’s solutions so far as the state and *krisis*, or decisive point, of the controversy itself is concerned. What purpose and design, then, is this—to make sport of his reader so utterly without candor or modesty? He still wants to play the part of some person they call a Calvinist, that is, a man exceedingly desirous to retain and confirm Calvin and Beza’s opinion on predestination, but in vain: a man, namely, conquered by truth and constrained by the evidence of the arguments to be led almost against his will into the opposing camp. For the mind must yield to what is true and evident as a scale yields to weights. Away, however, with these impostures of a man who plainly distrusts his own cause and strives as if from an ambush to seize among the disturbed affections of men what he cannot obtain by a just weight of reasons before the reader’s

uncorrupted judgment. For my part, I readily profess the opposite: I did not approach Arminius with the intention that he should free me from scruples. Rather, having long professed an opinion opposed to his, I approached in order to discover what novelty this new theologian might bring forward, either to undermine our opinion or to establish his own, by which he might either surpass or equal the fame of his name. For because of the reports being circulated about the man’s ability and learning, I was so far from distrusting this cause—which Bradwardine styles “the cause of God”—that on the contrary I determined that the time was approaching when truth would come forth in a form all the more splendid and effective for winning over pious minds, the more acute and rigorous an opponent it had found. Nor did my hope disappoint me. They report of him that from virtually every conference this always happened to him: once everything had been investigated, he was confirmed still more and more in the truth of his own opinion. But if something of the same sort has happened to us as we examined Arminius, we prefer to give thanks to God on that account; we do not desire by parading this fact to construct favor for our cause and odium for the opposing cause. Meanwhile, I do not proclaim that even we made some progress by applying ourselves to the refutation of these points and in the very course of this disputation advanced to certain more sublime points concerning the mystery of predestination. Rather, I am persuaded that an attentive reader will notice it.

But he adds that Perkins also wrote “certain things which deserve to be examined by the standard of truth.” But what is this about “certain things” being written in this way by Perkins? On the contrary, I say that without dispute everything deserves it. For what has ever proceeded from any Father of the Church after Christ and the apostles—however sound, however orthodox—which does not deserve to be examined by the standard of truth? Absolutely everyone’s writings must be subjected to this examination. Isaiah appeals “to the law and to the testimony”; “test the spirits,” the Apostle exhorts; “the spirits of prophets are subject to prophets”—to this extent, namely, that judgment may be passed upon them from God’s Word. Paul likewise says, “Even if an angel from heaven announces something other than what was announced by these men, let him be accursed.” Accordingly, it must be tested whether it is so or otherwise. But there is a *leptotēs*, or subtlety, here: he wants tacitly to signify and have a prudent reader understand more than he himself, I suppose in his modesty, expressly states—namely, that there are certain things which cannot endure examination because when examined they betray an unhealthy constitution. Come, let this be so. But is this something astonishing, or something to be turned into a peculiar charge against any writing, of whatever quality and whatever piety it may be, provided that it was fashioned by human effort? Yet whenever this occurs, provided that it makes no difference to the substance of the matter, it certainly will not harm the cause under discussion. This failing is common; for we are human, and in this regard “we both ask and grant pardon in turn.” We not only note matters of this sort, but also find other things in Arminius which would disgrace even an ordinary theologian. In establishing his own positions he is a lean and feeble arguer; in attacking ours he is almost always empty, though frequently pompous, especially whenever he plays the sophist or impostor. Thus he seldom proves himself a strenuous opponent unless he either rests upon his own ignorance or puts his confidence in ours.

Of the peaceful conference which he promises, I say nothing. For whenever someone departs from it, he benefits neither himself nor his cause, but rather the opposing cause, and harms his own all the more. No promise is needed from me in this regard; my part has already been done. For I am coming to these matters almost as the last of all. I am confident, however, that nowhere, either by constructing calumnies or by vomiting abuse, have I strayed beyond piously Christian manners. I acknowledge that at times I grow more heated in earnest, namely whenever I have exposed the man’s fraud and imposture. For I am indignant—and who would not rightly be indignant?—that when the cause of religion cannot be weakened with proper arguments, it should at least be buried beneath tricks and sophisms. I am also accustomed to be stirred whenever I perceive a man’s presumptuous boldness growing insolent in some pompous argumentation; yet when it has been examined, pressed, and recalled to scholastic examination, that disputation, outwardly so magnificent, is found to be empty and vain in reality. Meanwhile, I am confident that even here I have by no means exceeded the bounds of modesty. But if any sin seems to anyone to have been committed here—“if piety has swelled at some point, if something has been done more ardently than was fitting”—the reader will grant me a first favor if he pardons me, for he will find me ready to pardon others as well. If that cannot be obtained, then it will be fair for me to obtain a second favor: that he turn this deed of mine into a charge against me alone and not lay it as a foundation for harm to the cause.

As for his prayer, I desire to join him as a petitioner and freely offer myself, asking that he admit me, or at least allow me to fill the place of an unlearned man, so that I may say Amen to him. I add only this one thing: in these scholastic contests and disputations appointed for the investigation of truth, let us conduct ourselves so candidly and ingenuously, without pretense or dissimulation, without fallacies, and without sophisms, that we demonstrate the attainment of heavenly truth to be our one desire.
