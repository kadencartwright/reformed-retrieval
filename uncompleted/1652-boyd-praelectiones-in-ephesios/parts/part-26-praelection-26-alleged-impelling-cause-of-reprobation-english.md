[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 148 | De Prædestinatione p. 104 (from Lecture XXVI opening)]

Thus far we have rejected the arguments brought forward for false antecedent
causes of our election. As regards reprobation, I know that the adversaries
customarily bring forward many exceptions by which they attempt to evade and
weaken those very clear testimonies from Romans 9 that we drew upon in the
proper place to establish this doctrine. But because I have found their
evasions to be of no weight, I have therefore determined not to linger here
in examining them. For example, against the statement that the reprobate are
called by the apostle “vessels of wrath fitted for destruction,” they first
make the blasphemous exception that these vessels were fitted not by God but
by the Devil. For, they say, “to be fitted” means nothing else in that passage
than to introduce into vessels of wrath a capacity and disposition by which
they are prepared more and more for eternal disgrace, and this is done by the
Devil, the author of sin. They thus ascribe to the Devil, who is himself the
first and chief vessel of wrath, a work proper to God, a work of his most just
judgment, indeed of the separation of the human race, which depends upon his
supreme right and his sheer and utterly free authority over all flesh. They
do this with the utmost wickedness. Then, by corrupting the apostle’s words,
they also pervert his mind and meaning.

[Marginalia: Transition to the latter part, to the antecedent cause of
reprobation. Lecture XXVI. Example 1. Romans 9:22. And verse 11.]

How many frivolous and feeble exceptions do they likewise make against the
example of Jacob and Esau drawn from the same chapter? For instance, first,
they say that the passage must be understood not so much of Jacob and Esau
themselves as of their descendants, that is, the Israelite and Idumean
peoples. This is evident, they say, from examining the passages themselves in
Moses and Malachi. Yet it is evident that the apostle speaks of the persons
themselves in that passage when he says, “while the children were not yet born
and before they had done anything either good or evil.” Nor do those prophets,
even if they extend the prophecy to the nations that descended from them,
exclude the very heads and roots of the peoples.

[Marginalia: Example 2. Exception 1.]

Next they say that Paul’s passage must not be understood of eternal election
or reprobation, because it would follow that not only Jacob himself but all
his descendants had been predestined to eternal life, and likewise that not
only Esau himself but all his descendants had been reprobated for eternity.
Both assertions are absurd.

[Marginalia: Exception 2.]

But it is manifest that Paul is speaking of eternal election. Consequently,
to wish to deduce these absurdities from the apostle’s interpretation is not
so much to rise up against us as impiously to rise up against the apostle
himself. Yet the conclusions they attempt to infer from it by no means
follow. This is evident from the following rules or axioms, which must be
observed both for the right interpretation of sacred Scripture and to
vindicate its application to the apostle’s purpose from all the adversaries’
calumnies.

[Marginalia: Reply.]

First, the temporal things promised and set before the fathers in the Old
Testament were so many symbols and sacraments of greater things, that is, of
spiritual and eternal things. These must therefore not be torn apart from one
another insofar as God willed to join them.

[Marginalia: Rule 1.]

Second, when oracles or prophecies are delivered concerning certain peoples
who are comprehended under the name of their founders, the fathers or
founders themselves must not therefore be excluded. In their persons are
included the nations that sprang from them. This is evident in the example
before us, because, properly speaking, it was not so much the two nations
themselves that were in Rebekah’s womb as their fathers, founders, and heads.
The apostle therefore applied to the founders themselves what the divine
oracle had foretold concerning the nations that descended from them. He knew
perfectly well that in those very heads of the nations the first example and
the truth of all the things foretold had been exhibited.

[Marginalia: Rule 2.]

Third, this does not meanwhile prevent the prophecy itself from being applied
in one way to the head or founder of a nation and in another to the nation
that descended from him. For although the heads themselves bore the character
both of types and of the truth, this cannot truly be said of the whole
nations, that is, of every individual sprung from them. It can be said only
of the peoples themselves when considered generally, not of every individual
within those peoples.

[Marginalia: Rule 3.]

I explain this statement more clearly as follows. Jacob and Esau themselves
were types of the elect and the reprobate, and one of them was also in truth
elect and the other reprobate. But this was not so of every individual in the
two nations that sprang from them. Those two nations, the Jacobite and the
Edomite, were indeed two types when considered generally and collectively.
The former was a type of the true Church, that is, of the elect and those
predestined to eternal life, and consequently of the children of the promise.
The latter was a type of the reprobate from the same family of Jacob, who
would be children merely of flesh and blood, not of faith and the Spirit. As
Justin Martyr teaches explicitly in his *Dialogue with Trypho the Jew*, “We
must understand two houses of Jacob, one begotten of blood and flesh, the
other of the Spirit and faith.” But the nations were not themselves in all
their individuals the truth corresponding to that type. The truth is instead
discerned in the elect among Jacob’s descendants and the reprobate among the
same; otherwise this would be to confuse the types themselves with the truth.
We confess that the truth coincided with the type in the founders and heads
of the nations themselves. But the same could not obtain in the nations
viewed distributively, that is, in every individual belonging to them, unless
every distinction between truth and figure, and consequently the freedom of
the divine decree in election and reprobation, were abolished.

[Marginalia: An explanation to be noted. Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with
Trypho*. Note the distinction.]

From this it is manifest that the absurdity alleged by the adversaries does
not follow. Although in a typological sense the one nation taken collectively
designates the reprobate and the other the elect, the actual truth of eternal
election and reprobation depends not upon that carnal descent, but upon the
utterly free and secret judgment of God who elects and reprobates. The
apostle demonstrates this in that passage in the nations’ own founders and
heads.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

In the third place they make the exception that neither Isaac nor Rebekah
received or understood that oracle as speaking of Esau’s eternal reprobation.
Otherwise, they say, how could they have avoided being swallowed up and
destroyed by utterly inconsolable grief, if from the bare force of that oracle
and the divine decree they had known that this infant of theirs, still
nursing, still struggling in his mother’s womb and dependent upon her breasts,
was a brand for infernal Gehenna, excluded absolutely from every hope and
entrance to salvation for no other reason than that God had so pleased? What
else is this but to engage in foolish and empty rhetoric in order to stir up
either resentment, tears, bile, or weeping, when they are destitute of every
true and solid argument? I say bile,

[Marginalia: Exception 3. Reply. Member 1.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 149 | De Prædestinatione p. 105]

which redounds not so much to the dishonor of human beings as it becomes an
injurious and blasphemous attack upon God himself. It is as though those
godly parents had not submitted and subjected themselves in all things to the
good pleasure of the divine will, and as though we ought not to do the same
by restraining our profane reason when it rises impiously against God’s holy
counsel. Or could not Adam have perceived the same thing concerning Cain,
Abraham concerning Ishmael, and David concerning Absalom, from the outcome
and from divine revelation? Yet they were not therefore destroyed by grief,
but subjected their natural affections to the judgment of the divine will
with all reverence and submission of mind.

[Marginalia: Member 2.]

Next, even if the meaning of the oracle had at that time been known to Isaac
and Rebekah only to a certain extent—namely, only as a type and as regards
temporal things and the condition of this life, since we confess that all
things had been made known more obscurely and imperfectly to the patriarchs—
what, I ask, would follow? That it had no spiritual and higher meaning? Or
that this meaning was not afterward perceived by our apostle, the most
faithful interpreter of the Old Testament? Far be such impiety from us. We
should not even listen to those who say that Isaac, when expressly expounding
the meaning of that oracle in Genesis 27, did not understand it as concerning
spiritual and eternal things. It is as though the holy prophet of God had
fixed his mind solely upon these bodily things and did not look toward that
better and future life. Yet in Hebrews 11 the apostle brings forward this very
example of Isaac blessing Isaac and Jacob by faith concerning things to come,
in order to demonstrate the nature of saving faith. Saving faith certainly
does not cling to present and earthly things, but is borne toward things
above, things eternal and heavenly, and lays hold upon heaven itself.

[Marginalia: Genesis 27:19. Hebrews 11:20.]

Let us now see, therefore, how these same people attempt to attack the
antecedent cause of reprobation.

First, they object as follows. The principle of evil is not from God. But
eternal reprobation is the greatest evil of a creature. Therefore, its cause
or principle is not from God. We answer, first, by applying to the major
premise the familiar distinction of evil into the evil of guilt and the evil
of punishment. The major premise is true only of the former, not of the
latter. This could readily be demonstrated clearly from sacred Scripture if
necessary, but one or two statements will suffice. Lamentations 3 says: “At
whose command does anything come to pass, if the Lord has not commanded it?
Does not every evil and good come forth from the mouth of the Most High?” By
“evil” is meant the just punishment of sinners, as the prophet himself
explains in the words and verses that follow. Thus in Amos 3 the prophet says,
“Will there be evil in a city which Jehovah has not done?” This accords with
Isaiah 45: “I am Jehovah, and there is none besides me, forming light and
creating darkness, making peace and creating evil; I am Jehovah who does all
these things.”

[Marginalia: Objection 1 of this member. Solution 1. Lamentations 3:37–38.
Amos 3:6. Isaiah 45:7.]

Second, the minor premise is also false. Since reprobation is God’s work and
counsel, it does not have the character of evil but of good. It is God’s
counsel, which is not only free but also supremely holy, just, and wise.

[Marginalia: 2.]

Finally, the formal defect is sufficiently evident. It ought first to have
been observed by anyone who strictly follows the laws of argument, and the
inference must be denied, because the middle term is changed in the minor
premise.

[Marginalia: 3.]

But suppose someone presses the point in this way, in order to prove that
reprobation is something evil and therefore must not be attributed to God, or
at least that its antecedent cause must be sought outside God in the sinner
himself. The cause of the evil of guilt, that is, of sin itself, is evil. But
reprobation is the cause of sin, or its first principle. Therefore,
reprobation is evil and consequently must not be ascribed to God, who, since
he is the highest good, cannot be the author of evil.

[Marginalia: Counter-instance.]

This kind of exception may be answered briefly by distinguishing the word
“cause” or “principle.” One thing is an acting or efficient cause; another
only orders and disposes what it wisely permits to be done. In the former
sense we admit the minor premise but deny the major. In the latter, on the
contrary, we deny the major but admit the minor. For we confess that
reprobation is a permissive principle of sin and that it wisely orders sin
both to illustrate God’s glory in justice and to the most just punishment and
condemnation of sinners. But we deny that this kind of principle of sin—one
that only permits and orders, that also abandons and withholds, but does not
compel, effect, infuse malice, counsel sin, drive into evil, approve that evil,
or delight in it—is evil. Indeed, we steadfastly assert that it can be
supremely good, supremely wise, and utterly free.

[Marginalia: Answer. Note this distinction.]

Second, from Hosea 13, “Your destruction is from yourself, Israel, but your
salvation is in me,” they argue as follows. If the cause of eternal
reprobation is in the reprobate themselves, then it must not be located in
God. But the former is true; therefore, so is the latter. The minor is proved
as follows. Sin itself is the cause of eternal reprobation; therefore, it is
in the reprobate. The antecedent is evident from this testimony of Hosea,
which asserts that the Israelites’ destruction comes from themselves and that
its cause lies within them. That cause could be nothing other than their own
sins.

[Marginalia: Objection 2. Hosea 13:9.]

Before we refute this frivolous sophism, we must first examine the true
meaning of that passage, which the adversaries corrupted by a bad translation,
depraved, and thereby twisted into an alien sense.

[Marginalia: Vindication.]

It does not appear in the
Hebrew context as it is read in the Latin Vulgate. The Hebrew words instead
sound as follows: “He destroyed you, O
Israel; while in me, in your aid,” or “because in me is your help or your
helper.”

[Marginalia: שחתך ישראל כי־בי בעזרך.]

The words are concise and elliptical—something must necessarily be
supplied from what follows or repeated from what precedes in order to complete
the sense—and they have made a sentence otherwise plain and clear in itself
somewhat more obscure. Some, therefore, such as Rabbi David Kimhi, supply the
nominative from verse 6 of chapter 8: “That calf which you worshiped destroyed
you, when you had stood, or would have stood, with me as your aid.” They also
supply the verb at the end, which is no less necessary to do once the noun
that governs it has preceded. The Targum of Jonathan departs farther from the
text and clarifies the same words in this paraphrase: “When you corrupted
your works, O house of Israel, nations had dominion over you; but when you
returned to the law of my Word, I was your help or protection.” The common
Greek version of the Septuagint renders it far too freely and loosely: “To
your corruption, O Israel, who will give aid?” Others, such as Abraham ibn
Ezra, interpret it, “Your fictitious consolation destroyed you.” Others supply
the noun “king” from the following verse: “He destroyed you, namely, your king,
when he would have stood with me as your aid.” Thus the very learned Junius.
For my part, I prefer to all those interpretations the exposition of another
most acute man, who reads it as follows: “You have destroyed yourself, O
Israel, because you rebelled against me, your helper, or acted treacherously.”
On this reading the word *ki* is taken as causal or exegetical rather than
adversative, while *bi* means “against me,” as it does several times above and
below in this same book, chapter 6, verse 7, *sham baghedu bi*: “in this they
acted treacherously against me,” or “there they rebelled against me.”

[Marginalia: Thus Pererius.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 150 | De Prædestinatione p. 106]

But if there were any place among the other interpretations for a conjecture
of our own, I would certainly follow the traces of the Septuagint’s
interpretation. Its translators appear to have read *mi* in place of *bi*
when they rendered it “Who will give aid?” I would suppose that, because the
shape of the letters is not dissimilar, *beth* could easily have been formed
from the letter *mem*. If we read *mi*, “who,” the sense appears plain and
straightforward, and nothing at all needs to be supplied. After narrating in
God’s own person the divine judgments and punishments inflicted upon the
people, the prophet concludes by an apostrophe to the Israelites themselves:
“He destroyed you, O Israel”—namely, Jehovah himself, of whom he had spoken
immediately before, by justly avenging your sins. He then adds a confirming
question, *ki-mi be'ezrekha?*, “For who,” or “For who is there to aid you?” That
is, who would or could have been your helper? Who would have been your
protection or brought you help when God opposed you and was justly hostile
and angry with you? Your king? Your champions or judges? And so forth. He
mentions them in the following verse. Let everyone have free judgment from us
on this matter.

[Marginalia: Our conjecture.]

It will suffice to have observed from this that the Hebrew word in this
passage does not signify “destruction.” It is *shichet*, the Piel perfect of
the verb *shachath*, as is evident. No verbal noun of this form signifying
destruction is found, except with the servile and heemantic letter *mem*:
*masobith*, a net or snare, and *mascheth*, corruption or destruction. For
they refer *shachath*, a pit or grave, to a different root, namely *jafchah*
or *shuach*. The words “from yourself” are likewise additions and are not
found in the Hebrew truth.

[Marginalia: Correction of the Latin version.]

It is now evident that, when this passage is correctly understood, it proves
absolutely nothing for the adversaries’ position, whatever word we ultimately
repeat or supply. The sum is that the Israelites drew God’s just judgment and
vengeance upon themselves by their crimes, impenitence, and obstinacy; whereas,
if they had adhered firmly and constantly to Jehovah alone and to his law,
they would have continued in him and remained safe. But what does this have
to do with the cause of reprobation? Who denies that, as the Caecias draws a
cloud, as the philosopher says, so the impious bring evil upon themselves,
that is, divine vengeance? Or, as the apostle says, that they store up wrath
for themselves as a treasure for the day of wrath and the revelation of
God’s just judgment? Indeed, even if we admit that Latin Vulgate reading, no
difficulty will follow. We freely concede that the destruction of the
reprobate is from themselves, since they summon just condemnation upon
themselves by their sins, as was just said. But it is one thing to investigate
the cause of destruction, that is, of just punishment, and another to
investigate the cause of reprobation, that is, of that eternal decree of God.

[Marginalia: Solution of the argument. Aristotle. Romans 2:5.]

The remaining testimonies they misuse in this matter are of almost the same
kind: neither are they intended to prove the adversaries’ point, nor do they
conclude anything relevant to it. For example: That whose beginning is from
humanity’s innate malice, namely unbelief, has sin as its cause. But the
beginning of reprobation is from humanity’s innate malice, namely unbelief, in
those who remained unbelievers after hearing the gospel. Therefore, and so
forth. They prove the assumption from the apostle in Romans 11, who says that
those Jews were broken off through unbelief, and from Acts 13, where Paul and
Barnabas say that those same obstinate Jews, by rejecting God’s word from
which faith arises, made themselves unworthy of eternal life.

[Marginalia: Objection 3. Romans 11:20. Acts 13:46.]

But the assumption in this argument is false, and the cited testimonies
conclude nothing toward its proof. In the former passage Paul is speaking of
the Jews’ rejection from the Church and the communion of God’s people. For
God’s Church and the communion of the faithful was that olive tree from which
they were broken off through unbelief. The apostle rightly imputes and
ascribes this most severe punishment to their supreme faithlessness and
obstinacy as its most just cause. But what does this have to do with the
cause of the eternal decree of reprobation, which the apostle himself had
taught above to depend upon God’s sheer will, right, and freedom?

[Marginalia: Answer.]

In the latter passage there is the same fault in the proof and the same
defect, not in the testimony cited but in those who cite it ineptly for this
purpose. When the apostle asserts that the Jews by their unbelief judged
themselves unworthy of eternal life, nothing was further from his purpose
than to touch upon the cause of eternal reprobation, about which this
controversy now stands between us. He instead discloses only the cause of
their most just destruction, and sets it forth not simply but with
amplification and emphasis. In those words he censures and convicts their
wicked ingratitude plainly enough: although they had been chosen before
others by God’s gratuitous goodness as those to whom the saving grace of
Christ would first of all be announced through the gospel, they nevertheless
rejected so great a benefit maliciously, perversely, and obstinately. When
they did this, what else did they do, as if the apostle were to say, than cast
as it were by their own vote the ballot of condemnation and eternal death
against themselves? They pronounced upon themselves the sentence that they
were utterly unworthy of eternal life. But this judgment which the unbelieving
Jews declared against themselves was by no means the cause of their
reprobation, but rather its execution and fulfillment.

[Marginalia: 2.]

Likewise, most of the other passages they bring forward wander still farther
from the point: they use them to prove that sin or unbelief is a cause for the
reprobate. For example, the reprobate are invited to the banquet but refuse to
come, in Luke 14. Again, they rebel against God when he calls, as in Isaiah 65: “All day
long I stretched out my hands to a rebellious people.” Again, they spurn God’s
calling and counsel, or Wisdom’s admonitions and rebukes, in Proverbs 1. Thus
they do not remain in goodness, in Romans 11: “If you continue in kindness;
otherwise you too will be cut off.” They corrupt that testimony in two ways,
both in citing it and still more in applying it. First, it is as if the
apostle were speaking in this way about the reprobate, when the statement
instead pertains to the elect. Although they will never actually be cut off,
they nevertheless need threats and admonitions of this kind, by which their
torpor may be shaken off and they may be kept in watchful, humble modesty and
sobriety. Second, they speak as if Paul meant that goodness in which they
falsely suppose that the reprobate once were or could be. Yet by the word
“kindness” he understands God’s gratuitous benevolence in calling the nations
to his Church. Similarly, from Colossians 1 they argue that the reprobate are
not steadfast in the faith but allow themselves to be moved away from the
hope of the gospel. From these passages they conclude that the cause of
reprobation is in the reprobate themselves. All of them can therefore be
erased with one stroke by denying the inference, so that we do not linger
pointlessly over each one.

[Marginalia: Rejection of irrelevant passages. A two-part refutation. 1.]

Fourth, then, they object as follows. If sin were not the efficient cause of
the decree of reprobation, then God would have condemned innocent people who
had deserved nothing of the kind to eternal death.

[Marginalia: Objection 4.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 151 | De Prædestinatione p. 107 (through Lecture XXVI close)]

But the consequent is absurd; therefore, so is the antecedent. The solution
is easy: we deny this hypothetical proposition, which rests upon no firm
reason. The reason for denying it is evident to anyone from the doctrine of
reprobation taught above. Although that decree of God is free and depends
upon God’s sheer and absolute will as regards its antecedent cause, yet as
regards its latter act it is not absolute from every relation to sin.
Instead, it necessarily regards and includes sin in the reprobate as the
antecedent cause, not indeed of the decree itself or of any part or act of it,
but of condemnation and punishment. God therefore decreed to condemn the
reprobate and consign them to punishments only after they had first become
guilty and convicted of sin. Thus he did not decree to punish the innocent
and those free of all guilt, even though that sin by no means impelled God to
make this decree. For he could have decreed to liberate them from sin and make
them sharers in eternal life no less than the elect, all of whom were also
made or born children of wrath and condemnation no less than the reprobate,
if he had willed and had judged it expedient for his glory.

[Marginalia: Solution. Conclusion of the solution.]

Finally, some attempt to establish from the testimony of Malachi cited by the
apostle in Romans 9, “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated,” that the cause of the
decree of reprobation is the hatred of the people reprobated, that is, the
hatred with which God pursued them from eternity and continues to pursue them.
This must by no means be admitted. No passion of this kind or distress of
mind should be located in God by which, properly speaking, he is impelled to
do something; to assert this would be impious and blasphemous. Nor can he
properly hate those whom he was going to make, for otherwise he would not
have made them at all. Since in general God Most Good and Great averts and
hates nothing except sin as contrary to his purity and holiness, and sinners
insofar as they are such, that is, insofar as they are corrupted and defiled
by the contagion and stain of sin, while the reprobate did not even exist at
all, much less exist as sinners, before God determined anything concerning
them, it cannot be said without impiety and perversity that God was impelled
by hatred of them as an antecedent cause to reprobate them.

[Marginalia: Objection 5. Romans 9:13. Solution. A maxim to be noted.]

As regards the passage from the prophet cited by the apostle, however, we say
that those who interpret it in this way assign it a false sense. In a word,
God’s hatred mentioned there signifies nothing other than reprobation and
rejection itself, as is evident upon inspection, not its antecedent cause. It
is the same as if God had said, “I rejected Esau from my election and did not
honor him with that grace or pursue him with the favor with which I pursued
his brother Jacob.” Thus God’s hatred in that passage was to place Esau after
Jacob, not to honor him with the same grace and favor, but to exclude him
from his eternal election and not constitute him a vessel for honor. This is
nothing other than reprobation itself, whose antecedent cause is still not
made known from that passage. The apostle himself subsequently brings it to
light from Moses and Malachi: there is no other antecedent cause outside
God’s own self-determining and utterly free will. We established this above
from that passage in its proper place.

[Marginalia: Vindication of the passage. And the true exposition.]

And thus, with God’s help, we have resolved and explained the second class of
these objections.

[Marginalia: Transition to the third class of objections.]

As regards the third class, concerning the means that serve the execution of
both decrees, I know of few objections that can or customarily are brought
against the truth in this respect. I will therefore not linger here any
longer. As regards election, Christ himself, the eternal Son of God, was
destined or designated as Mediator and head of the elect. His incarnation
and his whole mediation, that is, the performance and execution of that
office, were accordingly and rightly established as the first and chief means
for executing election. From this also emerged the principal difficulty: how
could this work and this order which we have observed be consistent with the
dignity of Christ himself, if he as Mediator, and his coming into the flesh,
were said to be destined for human salvation as a further end—that is, if
what is greater and more worthy were destined for an end that is baser and
less worthy? Since we sufficiently anticipated and answered this above when
we spoke of Christ the Mediator as the foundation for executing election, we
will repeat nothing here at present.
