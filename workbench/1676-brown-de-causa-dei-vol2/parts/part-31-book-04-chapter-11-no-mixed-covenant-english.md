# CHAPTER XI

## The covenant made with the Israelites was not a mixed covenant

It is easy to show that the covenant which Jehovah made with the Israelites
was not some mixed or intermediate covenant which shared the nature both of
the covenant of grace and of the covenant of works, or which was compounded
and blended from both.

1. God never made such a covenant with anyone.

2. Such a covenant cannot be made with fallen humanity. For fallen humanity
has no ability to acquire life, or any good whatever, from God as a debt,
since it is not within human power to fulfill the law even in part.

3. Paul plainly teaches in Romans 11:6 that no such mixture exists, saying,
*But if it is by grace, it is no longer by works; otherwise grace is no longer
grace.*

[Printed p. 117 | IA PDF 125 | IA leaf 125 | IA decausadei02brow]

*But if it is by works, it is no longer grace; otherwise work is no longer
work.* Thus these different ways of acquiring life are incompatible and
mutually annul and destroy each other.

4. The covenant of works is made without a mediator, whereas the covenant of
grace is made with a mediator. Eternal life cannot be acquired both with and
without a mediator, or partly with and partly without one.

5. The pursuit of righteousness by faith and the pursuit of it by works of
the law are opposed and contrary to each other. Thus those who pursue
righteousness by works of the law cannot attain to the law of righteousness,
whereas those who seek it by faith obtain it (Romans 9:30–32). These two ways
therefore cannot be reconciled or establish a third way of acquiring
justification compounded from both. See Galatians 3:12.

6. The righteousness which is from the law is described in these words: *The
person who performs these things will live by them.* But the righteousness of
faith is described in very different words: *Do not say in your heart, Who
will ascend into heaven?—that is, to bring Christ down from above—or, Who will
descend into the abyss?—that is, to bring Christ back from the dead. But what
does it say? The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart; that is,
the word of faith which we preach. If you confess the Lord Jesus with your
mouth and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be
saved* (Romans 10:5–9). These things are so different that they cannot be
combined; otherwise the Apostle's argument in that passage would be entirely
empty.

7. The administration of the law of works is the ministry of the letter which
kills, the ministry of death, and the ministry of condemnation. But the
administration of the covenant of grace is the ministry of the Spirit who
gives life and the ministry of righteousness (2 Corinthians 3:6–9). There the
Apostle compares the Mosaic administration, insofar as the covenant of works
was proposed in it, with the evangelical administration. But these cannot be
combined.

8. Our righteousness which is from the law cannot be combined with that which
is through faith in Christ, which is from God through faith (Romans 10:3;
Philippians 3:9). There is therefore no covenant compounded from both.

9. The Apostle says in Galatians 2:22 that if righteousness were through the
law, Christ would have died without cause. It is therefore clear that there is
no third covenant mixed from works and faith.

10. If some such mixed covenant existed, people could begin by the Spirit and
be made perfect by the flesh. The Apostle considers it madness to think this
(Galatians 3:3).

11. As many as are of the works of the law are under the curse, for it is
written, *Cursed is everyone who does not continue in all the things written
in the book of the law*, according to Paul in Galatians 3:12. No one,
therefore, can acquire salvation even in part through works of the law.
Consequently, there is no composition of the covenant of grace and the
covenant of works, nor was there one formerly.

12. The righteous formerly lived by faith and were not justified before God
through the law, according to Paul in Galatians 3:11. But if that covenant had
been a mixed covenant, they would have been justified before God through the
law at least in part, and would not have lived by faith alone, contrary to
Habakkuk 2:4. Thus the Apostle's argument both here and in Romans 1:17 would
have been empty. In those places he proves from the Old Testament that
justification is by faith alone.

13. The believers of old shared in Abraham's blessing in the same way that

[Printed p. 118 | IA PDF 126 | IA leaf 126 | IA decausadei02brow]

Abraham himself came to share in it, because they were Abraham's children.
*Abraham himself believed God, and it was imputed to him for righteousness*
(Galatians 3:6). The Apostle concludes from this that all who are of faith are
blessed with faithful Abraham (verse 9). We must therefore say either that
believers in the time of Moses and afterward were not genuine children of
Abraham, or that they were justified by faith alone and, consequently, that
this covenant was not a mixed covenant.

14. *The promises were spoken to Abraham and to his seed. It does not say,
“And to seeds,” as of many, but as of one, “And to your seed,” who is Christ*
(Galatians 3:16). But it would have said *to seeds* if believers under the law
of Moses had been justified in a different way from the way they are now
justified under the gospel. [Tell me, then, says Beza on the passage, what is
the Apostle's purpose? It is to teach that the example of Abraham, who was
justified through faith alone, is rightly applied both to Gentiles and to
Jews, so that, without any consideration of circumcision, all believers are
blessed in Christ alone after the example of their father. Therefore, those
whom he had considered separately in verse 14, he now undertakes to join into
one body, so that they may have one common father, Abraham, with whom they are
blessed by virtue of one and the same blessing. He was not disputing with
utterly unbelieving Jews, but with those from the circumcision who believed
and did not doubt that Christ was the one in whom all would obtain the
blessing. Yet they were joining the covenant of the law with the gracious
covenant—that is, death with life. There must therefore be some common
principle by which Gentiles and Jews are signified in this compact under the
common name of *seed*. Otherwise Abraham would have several seeds, one of the
Jews and another of the Gentiles, and so the promise would have been made to
Abraham in the plural: namely, *and to his seeds*, and so forth.]

15. If the covenant which God made with the Israelites was mixed, the promise,
or compact previously ratified by God, would at least in part have been
rendered useless and abolished, because the inheritance would at least to
some extent have been from the law and not from the promise. This would
greatly diminish the force of the Apostle's argument in Galatians 3:17–18.

16. Furthermore, if this were said, the Apostle's argument in Galatians
3:21–22 would be undermined. There he denies that the law is contrary to God's
promise or that a law was given which could impart life or that righteousness
comes from the law. On the contrary, he affirms that *Scripture confined all
under sin so that the promise by faith in Jesus Christ might be given to those
who believe*.

17. If that covenant had been partly the covenant of works and partly the
covenant of grace, the law would not have been a tutor leading believers under
the law to Christ so that they might be justified by faith. But according to
Paul in Galatians 3:24, it was. The connection in the major premise is clear
from the fact that they would then have had from the law a part of the
righteousness required to appear before God. The law would therefore have had
another use than to lead them like a tutor to Christ so that they might be
justified by faith.

18. If that covenant had been a mixed covenant of this kind, the state of the
church under the law would have differed from the state of the church under
the gospel far more than a child placed under guardians and trustees differs
from an heir who is legally independent, since those covenants would have
been specifically distinct. But according to the Apostle in Galatians 4:1–2,
this cannot be said.

19. Moreover, if that covenant had been such a mixed covenant, it would never
be called a testament, and it would differ from the evangelical covenant far
more than the Old Testament differs from the New. But the Apostle expressly
calls that covenant a testament and

[Printed p. 119 | IA PDF 127 | IA leaf 127 | IA decausadei02brow]

calls it the Old Testament, while he calls the evangelical covenant the New
Testament.

20. No one can be born both from the slave woman and from the free woman.
Therefore no such mixture of covenants exists. The Apostle compares these two
covenants—the covenants of works and of grace—to the slave woman and the free
woman in Galatians 4:21–24: *Tell me, you who wish to be under the law, do you
not hear the law? For it is written that Abraham had two sons, one by the
slave woman and one by the free woman. These are those two covenants*, and so
forth. According to some, the *two covenants* there mean the covenant of works
and the covenant of grace. That interpretation is not improbable, as is clear
from Paul's statement that all the children of the slave woman are cast out,
which must not be said of the fathers under the law, and from the Apostle's
assumption that both covenants existed together and still exist (verses
28–29), which cannot be said of the Old Testament. See Calvin on the passage.

21. If that covenant was a mixed covenant, the Apostle would never have said
that all who wish to be justified through the law have been rendered void,
severed from Christ, and fallen from grace, as he says in Galatians 5:4.

22. The covenant of works is a covenant of friendship, instituted to preserve
humanity in the state in which it was created. But the covenant of grace is a
covenant of restoration and reconciliation, formed so that humanity might be
restored to the former state which it lost. One and the same covenant cannot
be both a covenant of friendship and a covenant of reconciliation. A person
cannot be considered both as a friend and as an enemy.

*Objection.* It was said above that God renewed and proposed the covenant of
works to the Israelites. Therefore that dispensation was mixed, partly legal
and partly evangelical, and the covenant itself was mixed, being compounded
from both covenants.

*Response.* I conceded, and still concede, that God proposed the covenant of
works to the Israelites, but not so that they might be justified through that
covenant either wholly or in part. Rather, it was so that, knowing thoroughly
what the law of works demanded and being convicted of their sins and their
own inability, they might flee outside themselves to the promised mediator,
Christ. This use of the covenant of works does not in the least change the
nature of the covenant which was then made, because that dispensation of the
covenant of works only served the covenant of grace, which was principally
and solely the covenant made at that time. Ministers must still propose the
covenant of works so that those who have not yet believed in Christ may see
the state they are in, perceive how much guilt they have brought upon
themselves and consequently how great their danger is, and so embrace with
greater eagerness and willingness the Christ offered in the gospel, deny
themselves, despise their own righteousness, despair of salvation in
themselves, and, driven to extremity, flee to Christ's outstretched arms and
seek righteousness and salvation in him alone. Yet the evangelical covenant
must not on that account be called a mixed covenant. This subservient use of
the covenant of works does not change the nature of the covenant of grace now
promulgated under the gospel, as is well known. Therefore neither will that
use of the covenant of works which obtained under the Old Testament change
the nature of the covenant which Jehovah made with the Israelites.
