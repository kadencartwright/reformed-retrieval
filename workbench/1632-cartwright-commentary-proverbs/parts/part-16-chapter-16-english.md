CHAPTER XVI.

Verse 1. To men belong the dispositions of the battle lines of the mind; but from the Lord is the answer of the tongue.

In מערכי there is a metaphor transferred from warfare to thoughts digested and marshalled in the mind in military order. In לשון (*tongue*) there is a synecdoche of the part.

The sum is: that meditation and reflection upon those things which we afterward speak and utter are of no profit unless God directs the tongue, so that it may bring forth and declare what was considered beforehand.

What is proposed in this Proverb may seem foreign to the doctrine of providence, since Solomon so divides human actions that he assigns one—and indeed the more excellent—to man, and the other to God. For to marshal and direct thoughts in an orderly and composed fashion seems to belong to some greater faculty than to speak out what has been thought and reflected upon, especially for those who have a swift and fluent tongue, to whom nothing seems easier than to put forward publicly whatever they wish. But first, as concerns the just and lawful thoughts of the godly, we know that they flow from no source other than God, since we are not fit of ourselves to think anything good. As concerns the thoughts of the wicked, although all things seem utterly free to them, nevertheless it is plainly evident that they are mistaken and deceived. They indeed cannot but think things that are perverse and impious; yet meanwhile, even in that perversity, it is not whatever pleases them that enters their mind, but what seems good. Hence God also is said to summon tyrants with a whistle, that they may bring war upon a nation, Isaiah 5.26 and 7.18; likewise to rouse up a nation and draw it into battle, Habakkuk 1.6; Judges 4.57. Therefore, when men are said to marshal the battle line of their thoughts and counsels, this comes about because, although God rules and moderates their thoughts and counsels, nevertheless by this direction of God human freedom in these matters is not taken away, so that what they think and deliberate they do of their own accord and will, and apply the faculties of their minds to their counsels.

That the answer of the tongue is from the Lord is proved by the strongest arguments: namely, because the godly ask of God an opening of their mouth, Ephesians 6.19, and that a guard be placed at their mouth. Next, because he prays that in their false accusations against him they may have little consistency, Psalm 55.9. This is also illustrated by examples: 1. Balaam, who, although he had set out with the purpose of cursing the people of God and had resolved upon this more than once in his mind, nevertheless not only could not curse them but, beyond his intention, blessed them, Numbers 22.35 and 23, verse 1, etc. 2. The same may be understood from Laban, who, though he had resolved to rebuke Jacob with the bitterest words, nevertheless could not accomplish it, because God cast a bridle upon him on the journey, Genesis 31.28.

In Proverbs 15.28 it is taught that the part of a just man is to meditate upon an answer. Now from this Proverb we gather another duty of a just man: that he should not only meditate upon an answer or speech, but further, in a matter of greater moment, employ prayers by which he may obtain from God that he should fittingly bring forth and set before others what he has properly considered within himself or even committed to memory;

[1632 witness | PDF p. 310 | printed columns 594–595]

since otherwise what he has meditated upon may easily escape him while speaking. This duty must be performed all the more eagerly and diligently because a speech, however fittingly delivered or framed, has no power to persuade unless God attends it with his blessing by bending the minds of its hearers. This must be done by everyone, but it must especially be performed by Ministers because of the excellence of the subject matter.

But if we cannot bring forth and declare the things we have considered beforehand—much less commit to execution what we have designed in our mind—unless God directs our hand and blesses our endeavors.

Verse 2. All the ways of each man are pure in his own eyes; but the Lord is he who weighs the spirits.

In דרכי there is a metaphor customarily used for human pursuits and actions. Likewise in עיניו there is a metaphor transferred from the sight of the eyes to the judgment of the mind. Likewise in תכן there is a metaphor from weights, by which the lightness and heaviness of merchandise are examined, to signify God’s all-knowing understanding.

The sum is: that those things in which men wonderfully please themselves do not please God.

In the former part the Prophet censures the perversity of human nature in its judgment about itself. He also marks a particular kind of perversity: namely, that it flatters itself and is its own bawd, persuading itself that all its affairs are excellent, although matters go very badly with it—just as if an ugly woman should induce herself to believe that she is beautiful and comely, or as if someone covered in rags should suppose that he walks about most splendidly clothed. For this is plainly our condition, as we are taught in Ezekiel 16 and by the Apostle, who compares the corruption of our nature to rags and worn-out garments. For although no one is so stupid that he is not conscious of some evil in himself, nevertheless, because it generally happens that we please ourselves, this is enough to maintain the truth of the Proverb. Besides, we diminish the evils that are in us and, judging them less perverse than they are, raise them to some rank and number of good things. The truth of this Proverb appears wonderfully from the fact that we hold to human inventions more tenaciously than to the genuine worship of God. Thus it happens that men judge more severely against one who eats meat on Friday than against one who commits adultery with his neighbor’s wife; and that one observes more diligently many festivals invented by men than the Lord’s Day commanded by God. See Matthew 15.

Therefore the duty of the godly is to be displeased with themselves and always to hold all their own things suspect—even each of their most excellent actions, as things which savor somewhat of the old and corrupt Adam, whom they have not entirely put off. See the example in Job, who in the holy love-feasts which his sons celebrated always suspected that something had crept in which would bring God’s anger upon himself and his family, Job 1. What of the fact that even in those things in which they are conscious of no evil, they nevertheless suspect that something lies beneath which does not agree with the divine law? 1 Corinthians 4.4; 1 John 3.20. Here let us examine ourselves, whether we are among those who kiss our own hands, sacrifice to our own nets, bend the knee to the inventions of our own mind, and as it were prostrate ourselves before ourselves—especially since Christ affirms that we cannot follow him unless we deny our own selves.

In the latter part it is taught that those things are not approved by God; and at the same time the reason is added: because he weighs and searches the spirits, because he looks not merely upon what appears outwardly but into the innermost recesses of souls, from which actions and pursuits flow; and unless these are purified by faith, everything that flows from them stinks before God. Hence it comes about that what seems to us to be gold, he discovers to be bronze; what appears to us to be a rose is before him a nettle or a thorn; and what

[1632 witness | PDF p. 311 | printed columns 596–597]

we suppose to be fragrant and aromatic is before him dung-like.

From this it is plainly evident why it happens that we delight in our ways and pursuits. For Solomon has indicated the cause in this place, and not obscurely: because we do not examine our minds to see whether they are sincere or not; whether something proceeds from fear of God or from fear of men; whether from love of God or from φιλαυτίᾳ, self-love; whether we set before ourselves God’s glory or our own κενοδοξίαν, vainglory; whether we look to our private advantage or to that of the Church, the Commonwealth, and the family in which we are. Christ seems to have alluded to this passage where he says that not only does that which pleases men not please God, but that which is glorious among men is abominable to God, Luke 16.15; there the same reason is assigned as here, namely, that God knows the hearts of men, which they themselves do not know. For human actions are just like inflated and swelling objects—for example, bladders and the like—which have an appearance of magnitude, but when the air is driven out and they are collapsed, they at once contract into a narrow space. And indeed, to return to the earlier similitude, they are rightly compared to women who, because they are painted, entertain an opinion of their beauty and are unwilling to remove that mask from themselves, lest they be compelled to behold the ugliness of their face; or to those who are unwilling to look into a mirror, lest they see their own swellings, or who look only into mirrors that are false and flattering.

Next, let us also note from this passage that we should not take too much spirit upon ourselves when we are praised by men, however good and wise, because they can behold only external things; they cannot penetrate with the sight of their eyes to the mind, which is the fountain of an action. But let us rather strive to approve ourselves to God, 1 Corinthians 4.5. For he is worthy of praise whom God praises, not men. And let us not even then lift up our head, when, though most excellently instructed, we are conscious of no fault, because God is greater than our heart, 1 John 3.20.

Verse 3. Roll your works upon the Lord, and your thoughts shall be established.

In גל (*roll*) there is a metaphor drawn from men who, oppressed by a burden, transfer it to another who is stronger.

The sum is: that we should commit to God our affairs that are too weighty for us to sustain.

There is a stupid and dull kind of men who do not understand the weight and gravity of their affairs and therefore are not solicitous about advancing them or bringing them to a happy outcome. There is also another kind of men who indeed conceive and comprehend in their mind the magnitude and difficulty of their affairs, but, captivated and enticed by the pleasures and allurements of this life, cast those affairs away from themselves so that they may fill themselves with pleasures. There is a third kind: those who comprehend in their mind the moment and weight of their affairs, suspend their accomplishment and happy success upon their own particular skill and industry, and are continually and constantly tormented and lacerated in mind by cares, anxieties, fear, and distrust, lest any of those affairs turn out otherwise than they desire. Hence it happens that they do not live a life that is, as they say, vital; do not take peaceful sleep; do not eat pleasant food; much less worship God with delight and cheerfulness. Therefore Solomon, condemning by this Proverb these corruptions and deviations from true wisdom, shows us the right way.

For when he bids us roll, he censures that kind of men who perceive and apprehend nothing. For one who is touched by no sense of a burden cannot roll it onto another. Next, he also rebukes those who, while comprehending in their mind the difficulty of their affairs, cast everything aside so that

[1632 witness | PDF p. 312 | printed columns 598–599]

they may gape after pleasures. For when he says, “Roll your works and deeds,” it is apparent that God commands this so that a man may keep himself occupied in his affairs. Next, he openly strikes those who, conscious of the difficulty of their affairs, suspend their accomplishment and happy outcome upon themselves, because they do not cast upon God burdens which they themselves are unable to bear.

Therefore it is evident that the duty of a Christian man is thoroughly to know the difficulty of all his affairs and what dangers they contain within themselves. Secondly, he must set his hand to their administration and pass over carelessly none of the things that may contribute to their accomplishment. Thirdly, he must in no way consume and lacerate himself with anxieties over the success and outcome of his affairs, although the matter may not seem free from danger and may contain no little fear within itself. Thus farmers and vine-dressers, when they have made their expenditure and labor, calmly permit all things to God and await the fruit. It is enough for us first to have taken counsel about the administration of affairs, then to have put our hand to the work and wearied ourselves with labor. Yet, because these things have been laid upon and commanded us, they can be carried through by us with strength supplied by God. But God, who knows our strength, did not wish to impose upon us care and anxiety about the outcome, since he knows us to be unequal to bearing them. Therefore, in agreement with this counsel of Solomon and of his father David, let us cast this burden upon God. For why do you wish to sink beneath a burden, or rather to be crushed and ground down beneath it? For who is there that, when heavily burdened, would not willingly transfer his burden to another stronger man who offered his aid?

But here a difficult thought occurs: how can anyone cast away from himself cares and anxieties which are so tightly fixed and clinging? Who will show us the way to do this? Someone says that this seems rather to be wished for than hoped for. I answer that Scripture shows us this way as certain and undoubted, and not a difficult one, so that we need not climb into heaven, cross the sea, or let ourselves down into the deep. “Trust in God,” says David, Psalm 37.5. Therefore he who places his trust in God will certainly have accomplished this easily. Consequently, in proportion to the faith we have, so much is taken away from these faithless cares, anxieties, and torments of mind concerning the happy outcome of our affairs. But if we cannot cease to depend wholly and altogether upon our own circumspection, that is proof that we do not trust God.

There is another manner of casting these things upon God which the Apostle taught: namely, if, gathering our cares, anxieties, and distresses from every side, enclosing and enfolding them all in the prayer of faith, we pour them back from ourselves upon God. Let us therefore ask God that, after forgiving us the errors or wanderings which we have committed in the management of our affairs, he may deign so to preside over our affairs that they may attain an outcome pleasant and salutary for his glory and our consolation. An eminent example of this exists in Esther 4, penultimate verse. Thus, as soon as Christ had prayed in John 17, he immediately withdrew to the place where the traitor might encounter him, John 18.

In the latter hemistich there is a promise that the things we have promised to God will turn out rightly, and that the counsels we have adopted will fall out prosperously for us. In this prolepsis he answers what the flesh could snarl and object in this manner: “If I cast off the care of these affairs, who will receive it?” Solomon answers that God will bring it about that all things are properly cared for. When I answer this with the prolepsis, it appears more clearly in Peter, who, after admonishing them from this passage of Solomon to cast their care upon God, adds that God cares for us.

From this it is clear that the counsels of those men will not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 313 | printed columns 600–601]

succeed who either do not consider the difficulty of their affairs or, although they comprehend it, nevertheless do not cast those affairs upon God while laboring and occupying themselves in their calling.

Verse 4. The Lord has wrought all things for his own sake, and even the wicked man for the day of evil.

The sum is: that glory is owed to God from all his works, even from those from which, in human judgment, it seems least of all to be owed.

This is a noble and distinguished Proverb, by which, beyond the work of creation comprehended to some extent within the compass of human reason, two chief mysteries of religion that surpass human and Angelic understanding are confirmed. One of them concerns God’s providence, which diffuses itself widely to all things; the other concerns the Doctrine of predestination.

In the former part he clearly and plainly confirms that all things are done by God: the great as well as the small; the fortuitous as well as those whose causes are known and comprehended; the adverse as well as the prosperous. For the case is far different in the works of God than in those of men. Builders and other craftsmen, once a house has been constructed and the work completed, permit its use and administration to others; but God rules and governs at his own will the work which he made. Even things that are evil and vicious, which he did not make and of which he is not the author, he nevertheless governs for his own glory and the advantage of his people.

And first, that all things universally are done and administered by God, Scripture openly teaches both in this passage and in other testimonies, Romans 11, last verse; Ephesians 1.11.

Nor is this only by a general administration and inspection of all things, as very many affirm, but by a particular care and administration of each thing separately, so that neither a man’s finger is raised upward nor a beast’s hoof planted downward upon the ground without God’s providence. Many proofs of this exist in the book of Jonah, where it is wonderfully confirmed in the sudden stirring up of the wind to chastise Jonah’s flight; in its sudden calming; in the preparation of the whale for that very moment at which Jonah was cast into the sea, and in the whale’s landing him upon dry ground; first in the sudden greenness of the gourd and afterward in its withering; and in the fish which, holding a drachma in its mouth, presented itself at once to be caught by Peter’s hook.

What is meant by the statement, “even the wicked man for the day of evil,” unless by this form of speech he wishes to indicate that even this very thing is done and administered by God which in the judgment and opinion of men seems foreign to God, so that the sense is altogether the same as in the Apostle’s exclamation in Romans 11.33, 34, 35? Thus those who wish to permit nothing to God unless they themselves discern its equity and justice are plainly led by a spirit contrary to that which was in Solomon and Paul. They do not begrudge Kings and Princes their secrets—if subjects were to inquire into and thoroughly investigate them, they would declare the subjects forward and excessively curious—while meanwhile they utterly shut their eyes to their own much more insolent forwardness in this matter. But God’s omniscience, foreknowing this forwardness of men, adds the particle for this reason: that he might fortify the truth more strongly at the very point from which he foresaw that it would be more grievously attacked. Hence it is that he does not say, “the elect for the day of good,” but, etc. For there are very many who maintain the gratuitous election of the faithful before the foundations of the world were laid, who nevertheless do not admit reprobation.

The uses of this doctrine are manifold. First, it renders us grateful to God and kindles gratitude, while we consider that whatever good comes to us, by whatever means—whether of parents, Princes, friends, or finally our own skill and labor—nevertheless comes from God, the supreme and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 314 | printed columns 602–603]

sole author. The same point is established by considering gratuitous election, or predestination to life.

Secondly, in adversities it sustains our patience and restrains us from immoderate anger and vengeance against men, when we reflect in our minds that this proceeds not so much from them unjustly and without cause as from God by our own desert. See the example of Joseph toward his brothers who had acted wickedly against him.

Thirdly, even in the very sins that we commit it advances our repentance and is a spur to a deeper and more grievous inward sorrow, when we turn over in our mind that by the just judgment of God it has come to pass that, because of our sins, we are cast into so shameful and foul a state of feeling.

To this is added a fourth fruit, namely that we fear God, Ecclesiastes 12:13; and lastly a fifth, that we glorify God, Romans 11, last verse, even in our sins, in which his justice shines forth supremely.

The day of evil in this place is understood as the day of death and of the last judgment, which will be an unlucky day for the wicked, but for the godly a day of gladness and joy. Therefore let us not envy the wicked those days which they esteem halcyon days, since the day of evil will come and will turn their gladness into sorrow; rather, let us endure patiently.

Verse 5. Everyone proud in heart is an abomination to Jehovah: hand joined to hand, he will not be innocent.

In יד (*hand*) there is a metonymy of the subject for the conjoined powers and strength that are in the hand. In לא ינקה (*he will not be innocent*) there is a metonymy of cause for the effect, vengeance.

The sum is that the proud man, exalted in spirit, will not escape unpunished.

Chapter 15:25 contains a grave threat against the proud and against both their descendants and also their houses and estates.

Although the same thing is taught by this Proverb, it is nevertheless set forth with interest. The interest consists, first, in this: that the cause of their punishment is rendered terrifying, namely God's abomination. Next comes the principal and original seat of pride, the human mind, where pride most especially has its dwelling. Finally comes the ineluctable and unavoidable certainty of divine judgment and vengeance.

In the matter of divine vengeance against the proud man, it must be noted that the Prophet meets a twofold temptation of the godly. With eyes fixed upon the splendor of the proud—their display and pomp in clothing, banquets, buildings, retinue, horsemanship, and dependants—they are carried so far into admiration of them that they judge them thrice and four times blessed, and almost desire to become like and conformed to them. What is said in this place ought to overthrow and utterly root out this foolish admiration: however glorious such a man may be in human eyes, before God he stinks worse than any corpse and is the more abominable to him the more he abounds in those incitements to pride which I have mentioned.

Let us therefore learn to hold as abominable those whom God abominates, and supremely to abominate those whom God especially abominates, as in Proverbs 6:16, where among those seven hateful sins he bears the greatest hatred for this one, namely among those which belong to the second table. Nor should we delight in the power of helpers, since the exalted are most easily stripped by God of the defenses of their pride.

As to the seat of pride, it must be noted that pride often resides in the eyes, Proverbs 6, and in the countenance, Psalm 10; at other times in clothing and retinue, when someone exceeds the proper measure and passes beyond the customary standard or even his means. But God hates the pride that is in the mind, even though it does not break out in the countenance or external display. For there is a class of men

[1632 witness | PDF p. 315 | printed columns 604–605]

who, although their minds are exalted beyond measure, nevertheless feign the greatest modesty in their outward countenance, bearing, and clothing. By that very fact they are highly praised among men for humility of spirit, yet God supremely abominates them. Examples are Absalom, 2 Samuel 15:2, 3, 4, 5, etc., and likewise the petty papist friars, among whom, though nothing appears more humble, in reality nothing is more proud.

The last point concerns the certainty of the divine judgment that hangs over them. Here the Holy Spirit meets both the false opinion of the proud about themselves and that of the godly about the proud. The godly think them so fortified on every side and so hedged about by friendships and dependants that they cannot fall from their prosperous condition. The proud, in turn, persuade themselves that they are so deeply rooted and founded in that fortune of theirs that no engine can dislodge them from it. Hence in Psalm 10 God's judgments are said to be far removed from their sight, and in the same Psalm they proclaim concerning themselves that they will never suffer evil. Solomon replies to both that the proud man, whatever forces he possesses and whatever friends and dependants fortify him, will never carry away his pride of mind unpunished, since God alone knows how to scatter all their forces and dependants and, with one little finger, all the hands by which they are upheld. Isaiah 2:11–17.

Verse 6. By lovingkindness and faithfulness iniquity is expiated; and by the fear of God one departs from evil.

The sum is that God, from mere grace and in accordance with his promise, forgives sins with this design: that henceforth men, imbued with the true fear of God, should not return to their old sins as to vomit.

In the first part the words are equivocal, or have multiple acceptations. Sometimes these expressions are understood of a man's lovingkindness and faithfulness; most often, however, they refer to God's lovingkindness in promising and his faithfulness in keeping what he has promised to men, as they must be understood in this place. For if sins were expiated by lovingkindness and faithfulness toward men, the sins of unbelievers also would be expiated, since among the ungodly and unbelievers not a few are found who are kind and faithful toward men; yet it is utterly absurd that their sins should be forgiven. Nor does what is cited concerning Nebuchadnezzar conflict with this doctrine: they affirm that Daniel commanded him to redeem his sins by almsgiving, Daniel 4:24. For in the Hebrew context there is nothing of the kind. There he is commanded only to break off his sins by the practice of justice and kindness toward the afflicted, not to redeem them. Then Daniel distinguishes by his intended end between the expiation of sin and that cessation from sin to which he exhorts Nebuchadnezzar: namely, that from such cessation there would come to him neither forgiveness of sins, nor its fruit, eternal salvation, but only a prolongation of the external prosperity which he enjoyed.

In the former expression it is taught that the remission of sins is purely gratuitous, depending on God's lovingkindness and good pleasure toward men alone, without the least admixture of any merit of ours, Romans 4:7, 8. To this pertains the fact that our sins are said to be remitted and our debts forgiven, and that we are justified by faith without works.

Then, when he says that expiation is by the faithfulness of God, he circumscribes within fixed bounds the lovingkindness of God—which men are accustomed to stretch according to the desire of their own mind. It extends no farther and spreads no wider than he has promised by his word and promise to be kind: in respect of place, namely in the church; in respect of persons, namely the repentant and those who fear God; and in respect of time, as long as it is the accepted time and the Gospel endures. Therefore, if we depart from the church,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 316 | printed columns 606–607]

if we harden our minds, if we carelessly and sluggishly allow the season of our calling to pass by, God is not bound to us.

In the other part of the Proverb sanctification is taught, as justification was in the former part. Here the end of cleansing from sin is taught: not that we should wallow in sin and return to the sins from which we are expiated, but that henceforth we should walk before God in justice and holiness all the days of our life, Luke 1:75. For who, having been redeemed at a great price from the power and captivity of enemies, immediately gives himself back into their slavery? [Shall we then sin, that grace may abound? By no means.] Romans 5, 6, and 7.

Hence it is also clear that those who are not imbued with the fear of God cannot but sin.

Verse 7. When the Lord is pleased with anyone's way, he causes even his enemies to cultivate peace with him.

The sum is that by far the most direct way to gather the goodwill of men is for a person earnestly to conform himself to obedience to God.

How many people we meet who find certain persons hostile whom they greatly desire to make favorable to themselves—princes, parents, wives, children, friends, masters, and mistresses! Yet how few are found who think that the cause of this alienation resides at home, in their own minds? Most accuse their fortune because they are held in little esteem by those in whose high esteem it is greatly in their interest to stand. Besides, they consider within themselves either the fickleness or the obstinacy of those who, through no fault of theirs, have alienated their minds from them. I do not deny that this may readily happen. Meanwhile they do not examine themselves or reckon themselves the principal and primary authors of this estrangement—if not by giving those persons just cause for alienation, then certainly by provoking God with their sins, by which they in some measure impelled him to effect this divorce of minds from their own people.

Hence men who apply themselves to this with strenuous and most earnest efforts, so that they may either recover lost favor or acquire new favor for their own uses, turn over everything else first and allow anything to enter their minds sooner than what is counseled here: repentance from sins and amendment of life, though this ought above all to have entered their mind and thought. Therefore they determine within themselves to accommodate themselves to the service of those whom they find more hostile, while in the meantime they do not think of God even in sleep. Yet it is the Spirit of God who bends the minds of men.

For God is Spirit, and is like glue by which things most unlike coalesce and are glued together. He is the rennet, ὀπός, by which milk, fluid and liquid, is curdled; so the minds of men, dissenting and flowing apart in discord, settle together. He alone can bring it to pass that iron and clay—that is, things utterly unfit for conjunction and mixture—are mingled with each other. He causes [the wolf to feed amicably with the lamb, the calf with the bear, the leopard with the kid, and the ox with the lion], Isaiah 11:6. Indeed, does not the Holy Spirit testify that such power and strength lies in seeking and invoking God that in this way wild beasts of this kind become allied, and even stones devoid of sense? For as those who are allied to a King have peace with all his subjects, so those who are reconciled to God have as favorable to them all his armies, both in heaven and on earth, so that they are harmed by no one subject to his rule. And this is supplied all the more by reconciliation with God than by reconciliation with any King, because none of God's subjects can move against us without God's will; whereas,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 317 | printed columns 608–609]

when a King has been appeased, we do not nevertheless immediately have the King's subjects favorable to us, as is clear in Abner, who, after being reconciled to David, was killed by Joab. To this is added the illustrious testimony in 1 Kings 8:48, 49, 50, by which this doctrine is expressly confirmed. See 2 Chronicles 7:10, 11. Examples of this matter are found in Jacob. Experiencing the mortal and irreconcilable hatred of his brother Esau and soon perceiving its sad and bitter fruits, he turned himself to God and dealt by prayer with him who changed Esau's fierce and savage mind into one mild and gentle. This example ought the more to be observed because it is difficult to reconcile dissenting brothers, especially when one is reprobate. See Proverbs 18:19. So when Jacob feared for himself because of the slaughter committed by his sons against the Shechemites, lest all the neighboring towns rush upon him, he was commanded to go to Bethel and pay his vows to God as the one remedy by which he might be safe from the danger threatening him and his household.

Nevertheless, the aids and services of obedience and gifts by which men's minds are soothed must not for that reason be neglected, as we see in Jacob.

If our enemies become our friends when we return into favor with God, what must we determine about ourselves when those who are joined to us and bound to us by the closest bond of love bear a mind alienated from us? Do we not have just cause to examine our sins, to seek why God is averse and alienated from us, and to appease him by true repentance?

Here it is asked whether, when men's hatred is stirred up against us for the sake of truth, we ought by this hatred of men to be led to consciousness and conviction of our sins, especially since Christ commands us to rejoice in the matter supremely and solidly, Matthew 5:10, 11. I answer: it is indeed in place of the greatest benefit to incur men's hatred for the sake of truth. Yet let this not be an obstacle preventing us from bringing ourselves to the examination of our sins. By their due examination God's benefit toward us shines forth the more: he has transferred the enmity that is justly due to our sins into the cause of truth.

Lastly, it must be noted that this promise, given concerning things pertaining to this life, is kept only so far as it is not adverse to God's glory and our own salvation; that is, ἐπὶ τὸ πολὺ, *for the most part*, so that it is not an invariably firm proof of our obstinacy in sins if our enemies are not favorable to us, provided that we embrace them with goodwill and pursue them with offices of charity.

Verse 8. A little with justice is better than a multitude of revenues without right.

The sum is that even a narrow but just possession is to be preferred to a large one acquired unjustly.

The Proverbs in chapter 15, verses 16 and 17, are very closely related to this one. The difference is that the first warned us not to acquire anything by violating religion. The second warned us not to obtain any advantage with loss of charity toward our neighbor. This third warns us not to take anything away by force or fraud, contrary to right, from its rightful owners, or even to withhold it from them. And the rightful owners are not only those who know how to claim something for themselves by right from patrimony or from their own labor and industry, but also the poor who need our aid, Proverbs 3:26.

That he calls this better is true for many reasons: first, for tranquillity of conscience, when we are conscious of no injury against our neighbor; then for the present use of this life,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 318 | printed columns 610–611]

1. because by the very means through which they persuade themselves that they will become rich, they are impoverished and reduced to exhaustion, Proverbs 10:4; 2. because the food and clothing procured from it neither feed nor clothe a man suitably, Proverbs 20:17; 3. because things acquired unjustly are not fitted to endure but swiftly fall apart. For if God scatters the substance of the wicked, Proverbs 10:3, even though they have acquired it justly and duly, much more do those things vanish into smoke which were acquired wickedly and unjustly.

Verse 9. The heart of man considers his way, but God disposes his step.

In דרך (*way*) and צעד (*step*) there is an allegory concerning human affairs and their progress.

The sum is that a man cannot accomplish his affairs by any industry of his own, even the most skillful, unless God applies his hand and leads them to the desired outcome.

First, thought is not so attributed to man as to exclude God, since a thought cannot enter a man's mind unless God permits it, Exodus 34:24.

This Proverb is related to the one found in verse 1 of this chapter. The difference, however, appears to be that the former concerns the direction of speech, whereas this concerns every kind of commerce and business; and indeed it concerns a way and a step taken properly and without metaphor, as is clear from 2 Kings 23:29 and 2 Chronicles 35:20, etc.

Then another distinction is established with respect to God: he governs and disposes not only the outcome of a matter but all the means that relate to its outcome and event, down to each single step. An illustrious example of this is found in Micah 1, from verse 9 to the end of the chapter, where the Prophet, long before anything is put into execution, describes the single steps, as it were, of the coming enemy in particular villages and towns. Something of this kind occurs in Isaiah 5:27, 28.

Hence it is clear how vain and proud is the boasting and vaunting of those who affirm that they will accomplish this and that without any exception for the divine will. See James 4:13, 14, 15, 16.

Verse 10. Let sagacity be upon the King's lips; in judgment let his mouth not transgress.

In קסם (*sagacity*) there is a metaphor drawn from those who search out secret things that escape common understanding and bring them forth for either the salvation or the destruction of others. Indeed, in Scripture it is most frequently used in a bad sense for those who pry into hidden things by diabolical arts; but sometimes it is also used in a good sense, as in Isaiah 3 and Ezekiel 13:6, for those who set forth some secret that they have attained lawfully, either by divine revelation or by skillful investigation. The sense, therefore, is that Kings and Princes ought to take pains so that from a hidden knowledge of affairs they may be able to determine matters falling under their administration. In פיו (*his mouth*) there is the usual synecdoche of the part; and in the word מלך (*King*).

The sum is that the King and Magistrate must labor so that, from an intimate knowledge of affairs, he may pronounce concerning those things that belong to his office—especially in judgment—what is certain.

By this Proverb Kings and Magistrates are taught to drive the vanity and falsity of words far from themselves and from their lips, since they are free from those incitements and impulses to lie and deceive to which other mortals are subject. First, we know that fear is a cause by which many are impelled to falsehood, as we learn in Sarah's example:

[1632 witness | PDF p. 319 | printed columns 612–613]

There is such authority and power in the word of kings that they ought no more to fear their own subjects than a Lion fears the other animals. Some people pollute their lips with lies for the sake of gain, as do those who sell merchandise. Princes and jurisconsults ought to shrink from this all the more, since a great abundance of every kind of thing flows round them. Some people lie so that they may be courted with honors; kings ought to be as far removed as possible from this, since they have attained the summit and pinnacle of honor. Finally, others lie so that they may either escape some evil or obtain some good; from the former kings are more widely removed, while they possess the latter more fully than other people. It is therefore plainly evident that Kings sin more grievously than other mortals when they corrupt themselves with lying, Proverbs 17. And in this way Felix and Festus sinned more grievously: the former eagerly sought both money and popular favor; the latter was willing to violate justice in order to court the favor of the Jews.

But this is not the whole of what rests upon a King. For it is not enough that he be alien in spirit and will from telling lies, and disposed in spirit to bring forth the truth; beyond this he must strive with singular labor and effort to draw out truth deeply submerged in darkness and, when it is obstructed by the many lurking places of falsehood, to unfold and disentangle it, so that matters which surpass the common capacity of people may not escape his understanding. For since God's name has been communicated to them, Psalm 82.6, they must take every pains that no word escape them which in some way does not accord with the wisdom of God. Therefore it rests upon them to handle Sacred Scripture continually and to read other writings which are especially suited to their calling. And this rests upon them all the more because all who surround them are ready to flatter them, so that, unless they are assisted by books, which do not know how to flatter, it is, for the most part, all over with them.

But still greater force of spirit will have to be exerted when a king sits before the tribunal and ascends the seat of judgment, lest he bring forth anything that departs even a fingernail's breadth from the truth, because he exercises God's judgment, not man's, Deuteronomy 1.17; 2 Chronicles 19.6. Next, God himself sits in the midst of the judges, Psalm 82.1. Therefore they must first apply time and diligence to searching out the truth, as Job declares that he did in more difficult cases, Job 29.16. Next, God must be moved by prayers so that he may give them his judgments, Psalm 72.1. Thus there will come to pass what Job declares of himself: that he was adorned with justice and judgment as with insignia and ornaments customarily worn by Kings in the administration of law, Job 29.14. See an example of this sagacity in Solomon, 1 Kings 3.

From this it is evident that in former times all Kings were judges and ought to sit before the tribunal, a duty which also rests upon them today. For although all judges are not Kings, nevertheless it is evident from innumerable testimonies of Scripture that all Kings ought to be judges.

Verse 11. The balance and scales of judgment are the Lord's; all the stones of the purse are his work.

In פלס (*balance*) and מאזניו (*scales*) there is a synecdoche for all measures and weights which are employed in the exchange or sale of merchandise.

The sum is that God is the author of weights and measures, and therefore those who abuse them for fraud sin against him.

It cannot be denied that weights and scales were invented by people for the more convenient use of commerce. But since the invention is so noble and wonderfully useful for every use of the present life, God calls it his own institution, both because

[1632 witness | PDF p. 320 | printed columns 614–615]

he supplied people with the prudence and sagacity to devise them and because he also approves this ingenious invention with his own reckoning. Thus in Genesis 3 God is said to have made tunics for Adam and Eve, because he gave them the prudence to devise those garments and approved the invention as necessary and honorable. (From this, that ὃ παρέδω, *what I handed down*, is to be noted: every excellent and useful invention must be referred to God as its true author, not to the people who devise it. The contrary is done by people who sacrifice according to their own rites.) For so long as they are used rightly and lawfully, they assign to each person what is his own, like judges, however mute. Therefore, in the use of weights and measures we must reflect that not only the authority of the Magistrate but also that of God himself intervenes, to spur us toward their lawful use and restrain us from an unlawful and unjust use. If anyone were to corrupt and falsify measures and weights sealed with the Prince's seal, danger from the violated law would threaten him; how much more when God has confirmed these things by his command as though with a seal affixed.

Therefore, to sharpen our spirits toward justice in measures and weights and to restrain us from fraud, God must be set before us as rewarder and avenger. He will punish frauds of this kind all the more grievously because they transfer this most excellent institution of God, introduced to maintain justice and equity among people, to fraud and injustice. Indeed, it would even be more tolerable to deceive someone without recourse to weights and measures than, under the appearance of justice and of doing what is equitable, to overthrow one's neighbor in commerce.

He made express mention of the beam, the scale pans, and the stones for this reason: by all these things people are deceived both jointly and separately. For unless both the scale pans and the stones are equal, and the beam is held properly and uprightly by the hand, deception is admitted.

Verse 12. Let it be an abomination to a King to commit wickedness, since the throne is established by justice.

In כסא (*throne*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct, the throne being used for its subject, the kingdom or dominion.

The sum is that a kingdom is established by the just administration of the kingdom and is weakened by the contrary administration.

First, it must be observed that it belongs to a good King not only to refrain from committing injustice, but further to hold it in hatred, indeed in abomination. For if anyone wishes to review the annals, he will find some people who exercised justice exactly and carefully and yet were far removed from love of justice; they inclined zealously toward justice and yet were induced by no hatred of injustice, but devoted themselves to the care of justice either to court the popular breeze, to protect the dignity to which they had been raised, merely because they feared for themselves the vengeance of God, or, finally, to merit eternal life. But the person who does what is just and also does it justly, from love of justice and for God's sake, while, conversely, he withdraws from injustice out of hatred, indeed abomination, of it, must be accounted just, as the philosopher reports.

Next, since he says that wickedness ought to be an abomination to a King rather than merely hated, it can be asked why he requires from Kings what is the common duty of everyone. For this is required also from subjects and private persons, not only from Kings and Magistrates. I answer that this belongs to Kings in a certain singular manner. For this reason, Kings must pursue justice and injustice with a greater and more heaped-up measure either of hatred or of love than other people, so that if others

[1632 witness | PDF p. 321 | printed columns 616–617]

hate injustice, Kings detest it; and if others love justice, Kings are bound to love and cherish it exceedingly. For just as the Apostle in 1 Timothy 3, when requiring from Ministers of the word certain virtues common to all Christians, requires those same virtues from them more abundantly and densely than from others, so that they may shine before the flock and be an example, so in this passage Solomon urgently requires from Kings not only that they hate wickedness, but further that they abominate it. This teaches that the more each person has received from God, the more he ought to be led both by zeal for justice and by hatred of wickedness.

In verse 10 the Prophet requires sagacity for the just administration of the kingly office; but in this Proverb he also requires justice, so that every lawful administration of the Commonwealth may be accomplished by these two as though by columns. For since in verse 8 he taught that not even a house or household can long endure unless it cultivates justice in all its acquisitions, it would be foolish to dream that the Commonwealth, whose administration is a matter of greater mass, could long stand without justice.

It ought to have been sufficient to lead Kings to the cultivation and love of justice that God delights in it and that these things add to them a greater confidence in the divine love toward themselves. But Solomon adds another reason by which he may kindle the spirits of kings and Magistrates to zeal for justice, when he says that the King's throne is established by justice. When proposing the eternity of Christ's kingdom, the Prophet assigns no other support and foundation of this eternity and duration than that the scepter of equity is his scepter, and that he loves justice and hates wickedness, Hebrews 1.8; Psalm 45.7. And certainly the kingdoms of this age collapse and are torn out by the roots for no other reason than that under the appearance of kingly authority they exercise robbery. The convulsion of Monarchies and their crushing into the smallest pieces are abundant witnesses of this matter. What of the fact that Solomon himself, although imbued with piety, wise to the point of wonder, and fortified by a holier and by far most prudent senate, was unable to transmit his kingdom to his son because he had burdened the people subject to him with frequent tributes and dues? See 1 Kings 2.3, 4. Princes devote themselves wholly to this: that they may fortify themselves with forces of cavalry and infantry and with chariots; if these abound, they confidently suppose themselves well protected against every hostile force. But God has determined something far different.

Verse 13. The lips of justice are the delight of Kings; and let him love the person who speaks upright things.

In רצון (*delight*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct customarily used by the Hebrews.

The sum is that it is among the foremost duties of Kings, from an inward love of truth, to delight in those who speak true things.

When it is said that a King ought to be delighted by true sayings, it must be understood that this principally means the kind of truth which is unpleasant to the flesh but acceptable to the spirit and to a godly person. For, tell me, what specimen of either piety or wisdom is it to hold pleasant and acceptable the things which are related to us according to our own spirit's choice? If someone should bring us some true report which furthers and advances the advantages we pursue and especially desire, or should be the messenger of our attaining some honor, who is so savage and inhuman as not to embrace this messenger as welcome and desired? Therefore, this Proverb is undoubtedly spoken especially concerning those truth-tellers who speak things that are sad and unwelcome, yet salutary and medicinal.

His requiring this from a king, although it is the common duty of all people, occurs for this reason: because from Kings, as was said in the preceding verse, this in a certain special manner

[1632 witness | PDF p. 322 | printed columns 618–619]

is required. The reason is readily apparent. First, there is no class of people for whom it is more important that true things be spoken than Kings, since not only they themselves but those subject to their dominion are either assisted by the telling of truth or harmed by the contrary falsehood. Second, their example in eagerly seizing upon lies is pernicious to all their subjects. Third, by this readiness to listen to lies, many of their subjects and attendants are drawn into announcing false things to them. Hence it comes to pass that although lies are harmful to everyone, they are ruinous to Kings and to those established in eminence. Fourth, while those who say pleasant and profitable things to Kings flock to them in great number, very few are found who bring salutary sorrow by bearing witness to the truth. Thus it has passed into a Proverb that sad messengers ought to be sent by someone lame or crippled in his feet. Unless, therefore, amid so great a scarcity a King or Ruler cherishes and kindly receives those who bring these less pleasant things, it is all over with him: he is one who finds himself in the whirlpool and abyss of every evil before he learns anything by which he may turn aside from the danger. This matter should admonish Kings and Princes to be assiduous in the reading of books, especially Sacred Scripture, which do not know how to lie or flatter.

An illustrious example of this matter is in Eli the priest. When he had perceived that God was offended with him to such an extent that God no longer wished to reveal himself to him, and he suspected that God had indicated something to the boy Samuel which would be grievous to him, he adjured Samuel with curses and an imprecation not to conceal anything which God had communicated to him. And when Samuel had related to him the saddest things both concerning himself and concerning his whole household, he nevertheless bore everything that was said with the greatest equanimity and kindness. Therefore, if we have conceived any opinion of a Prince's piety, let us not hesitate to perform this duty of speaking the truth. And even though they are ungodly, yet if this rests upon us by reason of our calling, let us remember that God will easily win favor for us even among the ungodly, however harsh and sorrowful the things we report. Let Daniel be an example to us: although he had told the saddest things to Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar, nevertheless he was most honorably received by each of them and was treated with magnificent rewards, Daniel 2, 4, and 5.

Sometimes, by contrast, it happens that messengers of truth are badly received, as John the Baptist was by Herod, Matthew 14, and Micaiah by Ahab, 1 Kings 21. From the history it is evident that Ahab perished for this reason: he preferred pleasant but false messengers to sorrowful but true ones. How unjust people are in this matter can be gathered from the fact that it is guaranteed to the Legates of Princes by the law of nations that they suffer no harm, however sad the things they bring. Therefore, when someone who performs God's embassy, by reason of his calling, forewarns another person of adverse things, it is contrary to right and equity that he cannot do this without danger.

Verse 14. A King's blazing wrath is a manifold messenger of death; but a wise man will expiate it.

In מלאכיו (*messengers*) there is a metaphor from animate and corporeal things to things devoid of sensation. For messengers are put for judgments and signs of the thing that will follow.

The sum is that the inflamed wrath of powerful people and of those furnished with authority is a cause of death to those with whom they are angry, and that it cannot be resisted except by godly prudence.

It can indeed happen that someone becomes angry with another and does not immediately desire his life to be taken. But if anyone, of whatever condition and rank, becomes heated and inflamed with anger against another, it cannot happen otherwise than that a desire for vengeance immediately invades his spirit, so that he desires the other person to be extinguished.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 323 | printed columns 620–621]

Therefore, if the person against whom someone bears so exasperated a spirit remains alive, this must be attributed either to a lack of power and strength to remove him or to fear of punishment. But since a King is neither hindered nor delayed by these impediments—inasmuch as instruments of death are abundantly available to him and he fears no one under his dominion—the result is that the blazing wrath of a King and powerful person is deadly.

From this are drawn teachings most useful both to the King and to his subjects. Kings are admonished by this Proverb not to loosen and relax the reins upon wrath and passion, since, if they wish to indulge wrath and affections, they will make themselves guilty of the gravest crimes and the greatest cruelty, especially because, after innocent blood has once been tasted, almost every feeling that restrains the perpetration of slaughter is afterward lost.

Subjects and those placed in an inferior station should learn what Solomon usefully admonishes elsewhere: that everyone should observe the King's mouth so far as obedience and the covenant made with God permit, so that no one readily withdraws himself from the King's command and obedience, Ecclesiastes 8.2, 3, 4. For let an account of his deeds be demanded of the King according to his own will and pleasure. And although you are far removed from the King's presence, he must not therefore be provoked with greater boldness, because his power is spread widely.

But if the indignation of a Prince, who has power to destroy only the body, is so formidable, how greatly ought we to fear the heat of God, who has power to destroy body and soul? Therefore, if it is prudent not to depart rashly from obedience to the King, it would be absurd to withdraw yourself from obedience to God.

When he says that this blazing wrath is expiated by a wise man, as almost everywhere in this book he understands by “wise” a person who has piety joined with wisdom, not carnal cunning and craftiness. Here he marks the power of true wisdom, which alone repels impending death, as he also taught above, Proverbs 10.2. For gifts cannot pacify a King who is grievously enraged, since he abounds in riches; nor can labor and industry, since he has others subject to him whose labor he may use. Wisdom and piety alone can accomplish this and extinguish the King's passion, 1 Samuel 19.4, 5, 6.

This is accomplished partly by prayers poured out to God, as we see in Nehemiah. Gravely fearing the Prince's wrath toward himself, both because he was rather sad and because he was about to seek dismissal from the court, he nevertheless had obtained from God by prayers that he found the King compliant and open to entreaty. But this can be seen most clearly of all in Abigail, who by a godly and prudent speech made David into a placid lamb when he was breathing slaughter and blood and had sworn the death of Nabal and his entire household, 1 Samuel 25. Likewise it can be seen in David, who by a honeyed speech so bent Saul when, once and then again, Saul sought him for death, that Saul was ready, as it were, to embrace him in his own arms.

Verse 15. In the light of the King's face is life; and his goodwill is like a dense cloud of the latter rain.

In אור פני (*light of the face*) there is a metaphor transferred from the sun's pleasant radiance to cheerfulness of countenance. Likewise, in מלקוש (*latter rain*) there is an allegory from late crops moistened by the same copious rain to a person cheered by the King's favorably disposed will.

The sum is that the King's cheerful countenance, proceeding from goodwill and inward affection, renders life both pleasant and advantageous.

This Proverb receives confirmation from the preceding one. For from the reasoning of opposites it is argued that life is preserved no less by the King's

[1632 witness | PDF p. 324 | printed columns 622–623]

life is preserved by an open countenance, whereas life is destroyed by a contracted brow and an angry expression. For if Jacob declares that he was refreshed by his brother Esau’s cheerful countenance just as if God had looked upon him with a loving aspect, how much more is a subject revived and cheered by the King’s cheerful aspect.

Then, in saying that the King’s goodwill is like a dense cloud which sends forth abundant rain fit for ripening the crops, he teaches that the King’s goodwill not only preserves life but renders it pleasant and comfortable.

First it must be noted here that the Stoic teaching—that one ought to keep one and the same expression at all times—is not approved. For Solomon attributes two countenances to the King: one to be a terror to the impious; the other, by which pious and faithful subjects may be refreshed, so that he is serene toward the latter and, as it were, clothed in a little cloud toward the former.

Then he teaches that affection and goodwill in the mind ought not to be separated from a serene countenance. For since it is shameful in everyone to present one thing in the face and to keep another enclosed in the mind, to display tokens of love in one way while suppressing and brooding over hatred and ill will in another, that conduct is much more disgraceful in a King and Prince.

Finally, Kings must also learn from this Proverb that they ought to be no less ready and eager to reward the good than to burden the wicked with punishments. For most Princes are keen and sufficiently bitter in punishing criminals, while those same men are exceedingly slow and restrained in following the pious with benefits. In one hand, to be sure, they bear the rod, but the other hand, in which they ought to hold a garland, is empty. Kings, however, have ample revenues granted for this purpose, so that by generosity toward the pious they may add spurs to the cultivation of virtue.

Verse 16. To acquire wisdom—how much better it is than mined gold! And to acquire prudence—how much more desirable it is than silver!

In “gold & silver” there is a synecdoche of the part; there is likewise a metaphor transferred from merchandise and from possessions that are temporary and perishable, as is clear from chapter 4, verses 5 and 7.

The sum is that true piety surpasses the most precious possession by many parasangs.

First, it is asked here what difference there is between wisdom and prudence. Wisdom appears to be knowledge of things τῶν ὄντων, while prudence is knowledge κατὰ τί δεῖ; that is, an understanding by which a person knows how to accommodate the things he knows to the circumstances of times, places, and persons. Thus wisdom properly regards the knowledge by which we recognize whether a thing is lawful, while prudence is occupied in this: that we judge whether it is expedient. The former therefore looks to understanding, the latter to judgment and discretion. For it often happens that a person understands matters well, yet does not know how to use them well and skilfully.

Then, what he teaches about its being acquired in preference to riches, gold, and silver is the same thing Solomon taught above, when he admonished that it should be acquired before all possessions—house, land, household goods, gold, and silver—Proverbs 4:7 and Proverbs 8:10. Nor is it unlike the saying Christ uttered in Matthew 6: “First seek the kingdom of heaven, & all these things shall be added to you.” In this he confronts the corruption and perversity of people who put riches before wisdom and even before the fear of God. He seems to have inserted this Proverb here, however, in order to teach a particular use of wisdom and prudence. For it is like a conclusion drawn from the Proverb set forth in verse 14, where he teaches that the King’s blazing anger can be appeased by wisdom, which this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 325 | printed columns 624–625]

Proverb amplifies by comparison, as if to say: gold & silver cannot appease the King’s anger, but wisdom can; see Ecclesiastes 7:12, 14. Therefore wisdom is better than riches. An outstanding example of this appears in Daniel 4. For when Nebuchadnezzar, burning with anger, had ordered by edict all the magicians & wise men of the Chaldeans to be killed, among whom were Daniel and his three companions, it would have done Daniel no good with the King if he had offered him mountains of gold, of which Kings do not have so much need. Nevertheless, by his wisdom & understanding he appeased the King and delivered himself, his companions, & the Chaldeans from the punishment of death.

Then, when he says “how much,” &c., he declares wisdom’s superiority to gold to be so great that no one could embrace it even in mind & thought, much less express it by tongue or pen. For besides the fact that it cannot be taken away from us, it is advantageous both for the present life and for the life to come. Added to this is the fact that it is obtained without gold or silver, by the free gift of God, Isaiah 55:1.

Verse 17. The raised road of the upright is to withdraw from evil; he who guards his way preserves his life.

In מסלה (“raised road”) & דרכו (“his way”) there is a well-worn metaphor.

The sum is that the safest course is to remain with God in the calling to which one has been called by withdrawing from evil.

What a raised and paved road is in making a journey, withdrawal from evil is in the course of the Christian life. Let us therefore see what use a raised road serves in making a journey. First this point presents itself: a road that is raised and elevated has this advantage, that it is safe and not as exposed as other roads lying in depressions to obstacles against which a traveller may strike. So he who withdraws from evil is far removed from the dangers to which other people are exposed. To this pertains the fact that those who order their life rightly & according to God’s command are said to walk in light & in the day, whereas those who enter upon contrary ways are said to walk in darkness & night. Just as those who walk in light remain free of collisions, so do those who withdraw from evil. There is this difference, however: a person can stumble on the highest raised roads and in the noonday light, whereas by withdrawing from evil he can never fall.

Therefore, by this Proverb Solomon meets the temptation by which many are tormented: they persuade themselves that by fleeing sin & wicked people they will suffer some serious loss. If someone is oppressed by poverty, he supposes that many evils—hunger and disgrace—threaten him from it unless he seeks a remedy for his poverty in theft & fraud. If he has planned a crime, he determines in his own mind that a deadly penalty hangs over him unless by a lie he forestalls the evil due to him from his crime. If, when provoked by an injury inflicted upon him, he does not repay evil with evil under the law of retaliation, he fears that both the man by whom he was injured and others, having this impunity set before them, will heap injury upon injury, &c. The Holy Spirit meets all these fears and six hundred others of this kind, and solemnly assures us that no evil can befall us provided that we withdraw from evil and from wicked people. Even if we could obtain some good by doing evil, nevertheless [evil must not be done so that good may come from it], Romans 3. But now, when we are taught by this Proverb that by flight from evil we will be immune from evil, and will obtain this good—that we shall be beyond the range of the weapon—what madness (an evil) is this, that we should be so bewitched as to suppose that we can advance by acting wickedly and can gather fruit from fruitless works?

Moreover, a raised road has this good in it: just as it is safe, so it is easy to recognize & find. By its elevation it is quickly & easily distinguished from other roads. This may

[1632 witness | PDF p. 326 | printed columns 626–627]

serve as an answer to those who complain of the difficulty of truth, as though it were buried and submerged so deep in the earth that it could never be investigated & dug out by any diligence, when it is called the King’s road and a raised road—in English, “The kinges high way, or the Fosse, or Dunstables high way.” [For all the words of God are accessible & plain to one who understands], Proverbs 8:9. [And Christ says that the man who does God’s will shall know concerning his teaching], John 7:17.

In the latter hemistich he sets forth a reward for the person who keeps to the King’s raised road by withdrawing from what is evil: namely, that the person who guards his way preserves his life. Here the preservation of life is understood as the attainment both of this present life and of the life to come. For piety is useful for all things, & has promises of the present life & of the future life. But caution is needed. For if anyone turns aside from the raised and elevated road, he suffers a more serious fall than if he were walking or riding in a valley or plain. And certainly it would be better never to have entered the way of truth than, once having entered it, to abandon it, 2 Peter 2:20, 21, 22.

He then adds another mark of the safe & easy road: not only that a person withdraw from evil, but further that he remain in the calling to which he has been called. For it is not enough that we do something good unless it is also done according to the prescription of our calling.

Verse 18. Before shattering comes pride, & before a fall, loftiness of Spirit.

In שבר, “shattering,” there is a metaphor transferred from fragile things, or earthenware vessels, which when they fall are broken into the smallest pieces, to people who fall into an exceedingly grave & sorrowful calamity.

There is likewise in כשלון (“fall”) a metaphor drawn from the body’s fall, caused by striking a stone or some other obstacle, to some grave harm which a person receives by God’s just judgment.

The sum is that pride, which has its seat especially in the mind, calls down a grave judgment of God upon the person who is puffed up by pride.

We received something of this kind in chapter 11, verse 2, embellished with the figure of personification; but here it is set forth in other words & by metaphor.

In this Proverb, then, there are two things: first, a description of pride, and then an explanation of its effects. There are likewise two things in pride: (1) the very source & foundation of pride, which is in the latter part of the verse, namely loftiness of spirit, or an inflated mind; then the elevation & self-display of that very person, betrayed by some outward sign. From this, a certain description of pride can seemingly be derived in this manner: pride is a person’s immoderate opinion of himself, driving him to undertake things that are above him. This is confirmed by the words of Psalm 131:1. For first, so that the prophet may clear himself of the slander of pride & ambition imposed upon him by the Courtiers, he declares that he is not uplifted in mind; then that he has not attempted or taken upon himself those things which were greater than he, or more arduous than suited him. To these he appropriately adds what the Apostle sets forth in Romans 12, where in τὸ ὑπερφρονεῖν he teaches this consequence: that a member of the body arrogates to itself the function & duty of other members in the same body and intrudes into matters that are not its own.

The contagion & corruption of this sin creeps far & wide and spreads itself through nearly all the actions of life. Whether you transact something with yourself or confer with another, pride has great power to ruin those things which a person undertakes through pride. This is clearly established from the contrary, ταπεινώσει. For the Apostle, seeking to raise the edifice & structure of good works, lays ταπεινοφροσύνην beneath them all as their base & foundation, Ephesians 4:2. Therefore no one can doubt

[1632 witness | PDF p. 327 | printed columns 628–629]

that pride has as much power toward the manifold birth of crimes as humility of mind has toward fruitfulness in virtues. And certainly there is no society—private or public, civil or Ecclesiastical—which pride does not either completely tear apart or at least weaken. For just as charity and the vice contrary to it diffuse themselves through all the actions of our life, so humility of mind, & the sin contrary to it, seem to spread their power & energy through everything.

We see the effects which pride produces, and these must be weighed by us so that through them we may be frightened away from the sin of pride. They must be weighed all the more and lodge more deeply in our minds because pride is among those offences for which people establish no punishment. The proud ought indeed to be banished from the holy society of men, as is clear from the εὐνομίᾳ which David established, Psalm 101:5.

But since people almost always neglect this, let us lift our eyes to God, the just judge & avenger of this crime. He assigns two things, of which the latter—the fall—is the cause of the former, namely the shattering. Therefore let us hold it settled that those who lift themselves up will be cast down & will fall, even if their heads have touched heaven. Hence it is also declared in our Proverb, “pride will have a fall”: the more they have been elevated & borne on high, the more grievous the fall by which they will be thrown to the earth. For just as people of very tall stature are shaken & battered more severely when they fall, so the more inflated a person’s mind is, the more grievous the blow with which he will be struck. No one, therefore, will envy the wicked their eminence if he considers within himself the fall that follows it. Who envies a robber climbing the ladder from which he is afterward to be hurled down?

Examples of this doctrine occur everywhere in Scripture: Nebuchadnezzar, Haman, Absalom, Adonijah, Herod. Nor were even the most holy men of God spared when lifted up by pride, as is clearly established by the examples of David & Hezekiah, 2 Samuel 24; Isaiah 38.

Verse 19. Better is one humble in Spirit among the afflicted than that one should divide spoil with the proud.

The sum is that the condition of one humble & lowly in Spirit, however afflicted, is preferable to abounding in riches while a person is swollen & puffed up in mind.

By this Proverb the Prophet fortifies & strengthens the minds of the pious against the plague of pride. For a great provocation & enticement to cast aside humility of mind & put on haughtiness is this: those who are humble and think meanly of themselves are generally held in contempt by others, so that in this manner their access to honours & the advantages of this life is closed. It has almost passed into a Proverb that no one will ever make his fortune unless, like a candidate, he begs for honours or advantages & raises himself up; that those who do not thrust themselves forward or seek honours & advantages lie neglected and are held in contempt, while on the other hand those who seek high things with proud Spirits are carried forward & promoted.

Although many replies could be made to these things—namely, that no one ought to thrust himself forward, but should calmly & patiently await God’s calling; [then, that honours & riches come neither from the East, nor from the West, nor from the North, nor from the desert, but that it is God alone who casts this man down and raises that one up, enriches this man and makes that one poor]—nevertheless, the Prophet applies another answer here: it is better for you to be humble in mind in an afflicted & meagre condition, as though vanquished & captive, than through pride to be surrounded by the utmost abundance of all things.

The Hebrew word seems to have a doubtful signification: with the consonants it denotes the afflicted or the poor, but with the vowels, the meek. Yet the matter comes to the same thing, & perhaps to embrace both kinds of people he

[1632 witness | PDF p. 328 | printed columns 630–631]

intended. For so it is generally arranged that those who are afflicted & poor are also mild & meek; hence in Psalm 10 those who in one verse are called afflicted are in another called meek. The contrary also often happens, so that those who are rich & established in honour become puffed up & swollen by their prosperity.

It is easy to establish, however, that being humble & poor is better than being rich & proud. For the very condition of the afflicted, considered apart from meekness, is fortified by God’s special protection beyond the condition of those who are not afflicted, in such a way that an injury inflicted upon them is avenged more severely than one committed against those who sit upon & rest in the dregs of their own prosperity, Matthew 11:5. Next, the afflicted are better fitted for the kingdom of heaven, 1 Corinthians 1:27. Furthermore, in the most grievous judgments of God, which overwhelm a region like a deluge of waters, the poor & afflicted are punished more lightly, as in the Babylonian captivity. But when humility of mind is added to an afflicted condition, in which God delights as in his own image, it cannot be said how greatly his protection is there doubled & multiplied. By it all the fountains of his spiritual gifts are opened, and all the chests & receptacles of his treasures are unlocked. On the other hand, God declares war on the proud and resists them to their face, especially when they abuse his gifts of riches & honours for pride.

Verse 20. He who understands concerning the word shall find good; and blessed is he who trusts in the Lord.

In דבר, “word,” there is a metonymy of the adjunct for matters comprehended by a word.

The sum is that the convenient accomplishment of every honourable matter requires both skill in the matter being handled and faith in God.

Two things, therefore, are set forth in this Proverb: one, that everyone should know well & accurately whatever matter he undertakes; the other, that he should not rely on his own skill & understanding, but on the living God.

As for the first, we learned not long ago that counsel is a singular aid for transacting our affairs, and that an abundance of counsellors brings matters to a successful conclusion. A most illustrious testimony to this also appears in Isaiah 3, where the prophet, announcing the destruction of Judaea, threatens the destruction of the mighty man, the warrior, the judge, the prophet, the sagacious man, and the elder, the captain of fifty, the honourable counsellor, the skilful artisan, & the orator—the persons upon whom the Commonwealth & Church lean like a hedge upon its stakes. This, therefore, can be a rule & guiding star for directing us in our calling: let each person practise the art that he knows, as the proverb says, “Let craftsmen handle the works of craftsmen.”

As this must be observed in all arts, sciences, & professions, in the family, the Commonwealth, and the Church, so the greater the importance of any art or profession in any society—public or private, sacred or profane—the more studiously & attentively it must be heeded. This one matter, when observed, preserves societies; when neglected, it utterly tears them apart & weakens them. If account were taken of this, a Magistrate in peace, a General in war, and a Minister in the Church would never undertake these offices unless they were well conscious of their own skill in the matters which are handled by virtue of their calling; not even when they had been elected to those offices by the votes & choice of others.

Here, therefore, the perversity & dishonesty of many are censured: though ignorant of the matters whose management they undertake, they dare to thrust themselves into some calling, even if it should be to perform the menial labours of the lowest servant. No less deserving of censure is the folly of those who, for the highest offices in the Commonwealth & Church, those men

[1632 witness | PDF p. 329 | printed columns 632–633]

they will employ, who are unfit to govern the reins of either, and that in the judgment of the very men who choose them. This arises from no other cause than that they are induced to do it through favour, or through zeal for some advantage.

But what he says—that he who understands concerning a matter will find good—can plainly be established. For ignorance and inexperience in conducting affairs cast us into great anguish and anxieties; just as, on the contrary, it greatly revives and refreshes the spirit when we handle matters that are well known and explored by us, and know both how to enter upon the way and how to be thoroughly acquainted with all its paths and byways.

And thus far, indeed, concerning the former part; the other, concerning faith in God, follows. For it is extremely difficult for a thoroughly experienced man so to draw his mind and thought away from his experience that he does not rest wholly satisfied in that experience of his. Therefore Solomon wisely meets this evil when he teaches that trust is not to be placed in one's own wisdom or understanding in conducting affairs, but in the one living God. Unless he does this, it will come about that his experience does not profit. For either he will not accomplish by the benefit and aid of his experience what he attempts to do; or, if he does accomplish it, it will nevertheless not succeed for those honourable uses to which he directs his labour. The clearest support for this matter is set down in Psal. 127. Likewise Hag. 1. verses 6 and 9; Eccles. 9. 13. Therefore that counsel of Solomon in Prov. 3. 5. 6. 7. is excellent.

Accordingly, he who trusts in the Lord, in every undertaking, especially a weightier one, although he be a most experienced craftsman, as Bezaleel and Aholiab once were, will nevertheless strive in prayers from God that the work which he undertakes may prosper well; that is, that skill may be ready and at hand for him with which to complete the matter, and that, when completed, it may be of use to those for whom it is intended. Hence it is that everything fell out so prosperously for Laban, Potiphar, and Pharaoh, who employed Jacob and Joseph as servants and administrators. If these things were well considered by fathers of families, they would never entrust even the most abject offices to men who were ungodly, wicked, blasphemers, fornicators, or drunkards.

Of a man of this sort it is said that he is exceedingly blessed: just as expertise in no subject makes its possessor blessed, not even that which concerns sacred letters and heavenly mysteries, though he should know everything to a nicety. Indeed, the commonwealth, church, or family whose affairs he manages will also be blessed to some extent.

Verse 21. The wise in heart shall be given the name of prudent; but sweetness of lips adds doctrine.

In מתק (sweetness) there is a metaphor from the sweetness of sensible things to those things perceived by mind and understanding. Likewise in יקרא (shall be named) there appears to be a synecdoche of the kind, an appellation, in place of a celebrated and renowned appellation.

The sum is: eloquence joined with wisdom is more excellent than when a man who is eminently wise in mind cannot conveniently express the thoughts of his own mind.

By this Proverb the Prophet appears both tacitly to censure men's ambition and pursuit of empty glory, and at the same time to point out the right path which they ought to hold. For almost all pursue those things and apply themselves to them, especially those which make them celebrated and renowned among men. And because wisdom and knowledge of things both human and divine are highly esteemed among men, they therefore devote themselves almost solely to that, and labour strenuously to be, and to be considered, as knowledgeable and wise as possible. This sin once prevailed among the Corinthians, so that they pursued chiefly those χαρίσματα,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 330 | printed columns 634–635]

which were most highly valued among men. Hence they set gifts of tongues before other gifts far more excellent. And this same vanity prevails among us today, so that each person, in pursuing arts and sciences, sets this alone before his eyes: to acquire for himself knowledge of those things which will render him as celebrated and renowned as possible. It is just as if a man constructing a building were to carry together those timbers and stones which displayed an appearance of beauty, passing over the solidity and firmness upon which the weight of the building must be laid.

In the latter part of the Proverb he indicates the right path which is to be entered and followed: namely, that moderate wisdom joined with the faculty of speaking is for this reason to be preferred, because it brings more benefit for instructing others than if a man, bare of this faculty, were imbued with profound knowledge of the greatest matters. Therefore, in choosing a manner of life, provided that our parents and those under whose subjection and rule we live leave and permit the choice to us, we must see first and before all things what those things are of which the commonwealth and Church have the greatest need. Next, what are the gifts, whether of nature or acquisition, by whose assistance we may attain those things which are of the greatest use to the commonwealth and Church? Hence the Apostle in 1 Cor. 14. 1. admonishes us to pursue the more excellent gifts with zeal, especially those with which we edify others; and among this kind he places Prophecy. If this had been observed, we would have fewer lawyers and physicians, and more men fitted for the ministry.

Next he notes what the end of eloquence is: not that a man should vaunt himself, or draw another to his own side by the sweetness of his speech and charm his ears, but that he may the more conveniently instruct others in those things which it concerns them to know.

Next it must be noted that there are various gifts of God, which he distributes as he wills, lest we too proudly despise others upon whom the same gifts have not been conferred. See the example of Moses and Aaron, of whom the former excelled in understanding, the latter chiefly in speech. See also 2 Cor. 11. 6.

Verse 22. Understanding is a fountain of life to its masters; but the instruction of fools is folly.

In מקור (fountain) there is a metaphor by which both perennial and abundant life proceeding from solid knowledge is designated. In אולת (folly) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for that which is most foolish.

The sum is: that from an understanding man there flow forth in every way things which lead to life; and, on the contrary, from a fool nothing but things tending toward death.

Not long ago Solomon compared a wise man to a tree of life; from this he signified, not obscurely, how highly such a man should be valued, since in an enemy's field it was not permitted to cut down a fruit-bearing tree of any kind. This metaphor likewise adorns no less that point concerning the worth of a godly man. For when God was commending the land of Canaan to the people, he attributed to it that it abounded in waters, Deut. 12. Likewise it is said of Elim, a place abounding in palms, that there were twelve fountains there, Exod. 15. 27. Accordingly, that land, that city, and finally that house is happy where there are fountains of this sort. And it is a matter of great injustice, or rather impiety, to stop up those fountains and springs, or to furnish an occasion for stopping them up. For those who closed the fountains and springs of Abraham and Isaac, and stopped them with earth and mire cast in, are no better than the Philistines. Indeed, those who dry up fountains of waters more precious by infinite degrees sin so much the more grievously. As this is understood of all the godly, so it is especially of godly Ministers of Christ, who are like

[1632 witness | PDF p. 331 | printed columns 636–637]

common channels and aqueducts for a whole town. To have a fountain of living water at one's own house is a singular benefit, but for it to be available in the marketplace, where it may serve the use of the whole city, is more divine.

From the opposed antithesis in the latter half, the Prophet appears in the former part of the Proverb to designate an understanding man who communicates his understanding in many ways, even when you would least of all expect it: not only when matters succeed according to his desire, but also when, with winds opposing, matters fall out otherwise; even amid supper and dinner; not only when he handles serious things and sits in the chair, but also when he acts in jest and gives himself to relaxation. This is gathered from the latter part of the Proverb, by which it is confirmed that even the teaching, instruction, and attempt to inform made by fools is full of folly; indeed, it often happens that the greater the importance of the matters they undertake, the more they disclose their ignorance.

Let us therefore remember what the Apostle admonishes the Colossians: that the word of God ought to dwell plentifully among us, not only in the Church but in the Court and chambers; not only in serious affairs but when we descend to the relaxation of our spirits; not only in prayers but also in familiar speech, which is to be seasoned with grace as with salt; not only at home but in the field; not only when you rise but also when you seek your bed; not only at leisure but also in business. An example of this matter is set forth in Solomon, who scattered his wisdom here and there not only in sacrifices and when he sat at the tribunal, but also in his household, his buildings, the clothing of his servants, their sitting, standing, and so forth, 1 Kings 10. 3. to 8. The same is perceived still more in Christ the Lord, who meanwhile, when he took food and when he was on a journey, seized every opportunity for teaching and instructing, even to his final breath. For if a man furnished with understanding does not employ it for the benefit of others, he is not like a fountain of springing waters, but like stagnant water and a lake, Prov. 6: 13. By contrast, a fool betrays his folly not only in speech but often in his gait and gesture.

Verse 23. A wise heart imbues its mouth with understanding, and through its lips adds doctrine.

The sum is: not only that the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart, but furthermore that great assistance for bringing forth the good thoughts of the mind is placed in this, if a man has the matter of which he begins to speak well known and grasped.

What the Saviour aptly said, that the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart, and that things which are good are brought forth from the good treasure of the heart, is confirmed by this Proverb. This indeed must be observed against those who, when they speak basely or maliciously, profess that they have thought nothing evil; for vicious speeches are like arrows stored and enclosed in the quiver of the heart, which are shot out by the tongue as by a bow.

But something more appears to be comprehended in this Proverb: namely, that the faculty of speaking is aided by a deeper understanding of the matter about which we speak, so that it is the same as that saying, that each man is sufficiently eloquent in what he understands. Yet what we taught at verse 21 is not overthrown by this Proverb: the gift of wisdom is one thing, the gift of fluency another. For although elegance in speaking is adorned and polished by wisdom, nevertheless, because the matters themselves as it were give birth to the words, nothing prevents an accurate comprehension of the matter under discussion from bringing great weight to good speech. Hence it is that folly is condemned and a mark of infamy is branded upon the man who answers before he hears the matter, Prov. 18. 13. And certainly one who

[1632 witness | PDF p. 332 | printed columns 638–639]

is most hindered and slow of tongue will speak more suitably about any matter that he understands than if he were most powerful and eloquent in speech while not having the matter well perceived and explored. Hence it is that the Apostle charges certain men who aspired to be teachers with not knowing what they said, or the things about which they spoke so assertively, 1 Tim. 1. 7. This fault today ranges far and wide among us, not only in common speech but in the pulpit, so that certain men, relying upon fluency of tongue and words, thrust themselves into the Ministry. Nor are hearers lacking who, provided such men pour forth words without hesitation, are carried away into admiration and sing the praises of heralds of this sort; but if these hearers are asked what they have learned from those men, they immediately fall silent.

Next, by frequent use of wisdom and continual discourse about those things which pertain to true wisdom, the tongue is made ready and swift.

Finally it must be noted that speech ought always to proceed from the mind and understanding, not from affections and disturbances of the spirit—from hatred, love, envy, or anger.

Verse 24. A honeycomb are words of pleasantness: each is sweet to the soul, and health-giving to every bone.

The discourse is wholly Allegorical. For צוף דבש (a honeycomb) is a metaphor transferred from the sweetness of sensible things to spiritual delights; נפש (to the soul) is a synecdoche for the whole condition of a man who looks toward eternal life, just as עצם (to the bone) is a synecdoche for the whole condition of a man insofar as it comprises this life.

The sum is: that godly and upright discourses bring great weight to the present and future life.

Since pleasant words, or discourses, are adorned by the Prophet with such splendid titles and commendation, it will first be worth the trouble to consider what pleasant words are, lest, if we mistake them, we lose all the fruit of this Proverb and, what is more serious, fall into danger. What pleasant discourses are can be sought nowhere more aptly and conveniently than from Solomon himself. First, it is defined from chapter 15, verse 26, when he says: The discourses of the pure are pleasant; and at chapter 22, verses 17 and 18: Incline your ear and hear the words of the wise, and apply your heart to my knowledge. For it is pleasant. Hence it is clear that the words of the pure and the words of the wise are pleasant words, and finally the words of the knowledge and cognition of God. If, then, they belong to the pure and wise, they ought immediately to be pleasant, of whatever kind they are—whether promises or threats, whether admonitions or reproofs. For there are many who willingly admit doctrines which produce knowledge and eagerly seize promises, but when it comes to admonitions, they bear them with difficulty. For they are so proud that they take it ill to be admonished, and in no way endure to be reproved or struck by threats.

Next it is taught how pleasant they are: namely, like honey, than which nothing is sweeter, like a honeycomb—(in English, lifi hony), Psal. 10—which is still more delicious and more pleasing to the palate, as that which flows of itself from a honeycomb without being pressed out by force and which is by far the most precious thing found in honey. Here, then, let us examine what taste the words of wisdom, that is, the words of God, have among us: whether they savour our palate, whether they are pleasing to our spirit, so that from this we may form a judgment about ourselves. For there are those to whom they savour no more than yolk—indeed, than gall.

Next, when he says that pleasant words are pleasing to the soul, he teaches that they must not merely be tasted but swallowed, and thoroughly digested by the mind and meditation, so that they may profit us. For if they are only tasted, and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 333 | printed columns 640–641]

afterwards spat out; or if they are swallowed and then vomited up again, they certainly cannot gladden the spirit. Hence it is that at Prov. 22. 17, 18. it is required that we apply the mind to the knowledge of God; and he says that they will then be pleasant when you have preserved them in the belly, or in the inmost recesses of the spirit, and afterwards set them forth with your lips once they have been inwardly assimilated and digested.

Next another benefit is conferred upon those to whom the discourses of God are sweet: namely, that it heals the bones, that is, brings health to the body and therefore restores to health those worn down by disease, even when they labour under the gravest disease. For of all diseases that one is most deplorable when the bones, which are as the foundations of the body, are corrupted or broken in pieces. For just as honey is not only sweet but also wholesome, so are pleasant discourses. See Job 32. 19. and following. See Matt. 9: Son, have confidence; your sins are forgiven you. The promises of this life are fulfilled in such a way that they conduce to God's glory and our salvation.

Verse 25. There is a right way in the sight of a man, whose end is a manifold way to death.

In דרך (way) there is a metaphor for a manner of life; likewise in ישר (right) there is a metaphor from the straightness of a way to the uprightness or usefulness of a manner of life; and in לפני (before the face) there is one transferred from the gaze of the eyes to the understanding of the mind, so that the whole Proverb is almost allegorical.

The sum is: that to men pursuing what is good and useful by the dictate of their own sense and reason, the opposite happens; and it is entirely the same as that Proverb which occurs at chapter 14. 12., in wording as in sense, by which the Prophet wished to teach δὶς καὶ τρὶς τὸ αὐτὸ γράφειν, to write the same thing twice and thrice, Phil. 3. 1., although some distinction is established here by the connection with the immediately preceding Proverb: that pleasant words are as honey, and so forth, but not all things are pleasant and truly delightful which appear so to us.

In the commandments of the second table, after the Fall, God left certain sparks of the knowledge and cognition of right and wrong, just and unjust, by whose aid we may pursue those things which are good and turn away from their contraries. But corrupt custom arising from education partly obscures these sparks shining in the darkness, and the disturbances and turbulent affections of the spirit partly extinguish them, so that what reason judges well of itself, reason, conquered and captured by disturbances, determines perversely. Hence it is that when the appearance of advantage or freedom is presented, parents and Magistrates are resisted; through desire for vengeance, the killing of a neighbour appears advantageous and salutary to us; another's adultery is numbered among good things; the diversion of another's goods is seen as advantageous to us; and slander of a neighbour is seen as fit for preserving or advancing our reputation. For although, with reason as judge, all these things have a deformed and nauseating aspect, yet through slight education and the disordered agitation of the mind judgment is made their slave, which takes away from us the aspect of deformity and, as it were, dresses them with rouge and pigments in a flowery colour; deceived by this, we are swept toward the evil things from which we would otherwise recoil.

But in those matters which pertain to the first table there is indeed a certain common, confused notion of the worship of God implanted in us, extending thus far: that we know God to exist, that he is to be worshipped, that we must speak of him only with the highest reverence, and that some time must be set apart for his worship. But who that God is; what worship, internal or external, is to be rendered to that God; how we are to sanctify his name or take care not to violate it; finally, what the time is in which he is to be worshipped and how we are to conduct ourselves during this time—not even we ourselves

[1632 witness | PDF p. 334 | printed columns 642–643]

by the rays of reason we know him. Therefore those who were masters of reason, or most highly skilled in it, yet followed reason as their guide, have erred most grossly and most shamefully of all. Hence this Proverb also has a principal place in these commandments. For whether you obey the prescription of reason, which is hostile and hateful to God, or a very ancient custom and the manner of your forefathers, or whatever it may be that you set before yourself apart from the one word of God, that way will lead you to certain death. From this it is clear how feeble is that foundation of true religion on which the Pontificians rest. They praise so exceedingly the good intention, as they call it, that they contend that in the cause of religion it is scarcely possible to stray from the way, provided that the deed be done with good zeal and a good intention, as they call it. By this reasoning the murderers of the Apostles are pronounced righteous, John 6, and even the killers of Christ himself, a great part of whom thought that they were offering worship to God, Romans 10; 1 Corinthians 1. See the example of Uzzah.

But his affirmation that the road to death has manifold ends and outcomes will also be found true in the very sin in which a person transgresses. For since the observance of the commandments is rewarded with a twofold reward in this life and the life to come, it necessarily follows that their violation is repaid with both kinds of death. Next, in one and the same sin there is not a twofold but a manifold road to destruction. For in a lustful life, when the strength has been exhausted, the health of the body is undermined, so that a person in some fashion dies every single day; next he is reduced to a morsel of bread and the utmost poverty; moreover, he is overwhelmed with disgrace. The sense and consciousness of all these things weigh a person downward toward hell like a heavy burden, besides the deprivation of this present life which the Law of God imposes on adulterers.

Verse 26. He who causes trouble to a soul causes trouble to himself. For his own mouth is turned back upon it.

In נפש (*soul*) there is a synecdoche of the part. Likewise in פיהו (*his mouth*).

The sum is: that when a person devises evil against his neighbor by treacherous words, the evil is turned back and recoils upon its author.

Those who construct evil against others through slanders, detractions, and reproaches propose to themselves that, by harming others, they will secure their own advantages, or at least that, when others have been injured, they themselves will escape untouched and unharmed. But the Holy Spirit, in order to oppose this opinion and tear out this error, teaches that the contrary happens to what they suppose. For it often happens that they do not bring the evil which they plan and contrive against others to the outcome they desired. But those who devise crimes against others always fall upon their own heads. And indeed, when their plans fail, as does the coveted crime which they intend to perpetrate, there immediately arises and sprouts a dreadful anguish of mind, by which they are tormented and lacerated because the crime designed in their minds is not accomplished by them. Next, the perpetual consciousness of the crime which they either committed or thought to commit drives them on and does not permit them to rest. Finally, the evil they were preparing against others is often turned back upon themselves, even in this life, by the just judgment of God. Of this there are countless examples in Scripture: Doeg, Judas, Haman, Adoni-bezek, and others. Hence come those frequent sayings of the Prophets: that they fall into the pit which they themselves dug; that the arrow, or the stone, or anything of that kind which someone shot into the air or hurled aloft falls back upon his own head. For he who by law provides that an eye be cut out for an eye, a foot for a foot, a tooth for a tooth, and so forth, from the person who mutilated another's body, will not be wanting to his own justice in that kind of compensation which people

[1632 witness | PDF p. 335 | printed columns 644–645]

are unable to render. To this class belongs detraction, slander, or treacherous accusation, by which another person's life, possessions, or reputation is assailed. For judges among mortals ought not to oppose slander with slander, but God most justly does this.

Therefore it is a species not only of wickedness but also of madness and fury to sow slanders and detractions against others, since, as the Proverb says, κακὴ βουλὴ τῷ βουλεύσαντι κακίστη—bad counsel is worst for the one who counsels it. Hence to some extent it may be said, as is written by the Apostle to the Ephesians, “Who ever hated his own flesh?” since the evil that a person prepares for another redounds upon himself. But if vengeance is not exacted in this life, and the injury that someone devised against another is not repaid in kind, nevertheless God, the just judge, will compensate for it with a fuller measure at the last judgment. Therefore, if anyone cannot be brought by the consciousness of obedience to God to restrain his tongue from being sharpened for his neighbor's harm, let him nevertheless do this for the sake of his own advantage. If he cannot be led by love of his neighbor to refrain from reviling, at least let him do so through aversion to harming himself.

But here it is asked how the trouble is turned back upon the mouth of the person who causes it, since a grievous evil is also brought upon those who are troubled, so that some seek a remedy by hanging themselves. What of the fact that it also redounds upon God himself, who is assailed with blasphemies and oaths by the person who is afflicted? But this also is true: that all these things return upon the head of the person who causes the trouble. Especially that which is directed against God falls back upon the person's own head with great force, just as all things borne downward do.

Verse 27. A worthless man digs up evil; and upon his lips there is, as it were, a burning fire.

In כרה (*digs up*) there is a metaphor from those who secretly lie in wait for wild beasts, so that they may ensnare them unawares.

The sum is: that a worthless person treacherously prepares evil and destruction for another, and afterward brings it forth into the open with the aid of a venomous tongue.

First let us see who it is whom the Prophet calls worthless. From the etymology of the word, it is the person who advances not even a whit. This is a lesson to us concerning the standing of those who receive no increase of piety: namely, that their condition is ruined. It is indeed true, as the Apostle affirms, that all wicked people and impostors advance daily in wickedness, 2 Timothy 3.13. But the Prophet chose to describe a worthless person in this way in order to sharpen our diligence toward good works, while he pronounces not only the person who diligently devotes himself to wickedness to be criminal, but also the one who does not persist in the tracks of uprightness—indeed, who daily renders his progress in uprightness neither firmer nor swifter. Hence the condition of a Christian person is compared to the condition of a natural person who grows day by day, until he arrives at the proper stature of manhood. Hence it is also said of John the Baptist and Christ in Luke 2 that they grew in wisdom as in stature. In this natural life it is deformed to be maimed and not to increase in bodily size with age; but it would plainly be a monster if some little boy of short stature diminished day by day. Next, they may rightly be said not to advance and not to ascend because they do not attain those things which they pursue by their wickedness and design in their minds. Hence also comes that statement of the Apostle: that the wicked will not advance, but their madness will become known to everyone, 2 Timothy 3.9.

When he says that they dig up evil, he indicates the activity of the worthless person, who does not devise evil against another through imprudence or ignorance, nor when moved by some sudden disturbance, nor through

[1632 witness | PDF p. 336 | printed columns 646–647]

weakness; nor is it the person who does this once or rarely, when ensnared and occupied by Satan. Rather, the person who knowingly and deliberately, and with constant application, practices his wickedness as though it were a trade is at length to be judged a worthless person. For it can happen that a godly man, overcome by disturbance, secretly devises evil; but the former devotes his whole self to committing crimes. Hence he is also compared to those who gain their livelihood by hunting wild beasts, to miners, to those who labor in mines, or to those who dig tunnels, all of whom have laborious work. Next, by attributing to these people the practice which they exercise, he brands them with a grievous mark of infamy, especially if by their calling they occupy a more honorable place in the Commonwealth or Church. They are like people who, with eyes fixed upon the ground, are always intent upon it and cast down to base and illiberal occupations—especially because laborers end their work with the day, while this kind of person does not rest from devising evils even by night.

Another effect of the worthless person is that fire rests upon his lips. The tongue of a wicked person is often inflamed even when something escapes him through imprudence or disturbance; but when evil has been long premeditated and digested beforehand, then at last the stroke of the tongue, like fire, is more penetrating and more fervent. And this is what is said in Jeremiah 9.5: that they taught their tongues to speak falsehood. People of this kind are those who apply themselves to burdening others, especially the righteous, with slanders. It is deservedly compared to fire, even to one kindled from hell, as in James 3, and to juniper coals in Psalm 120, because the tongue has burned cities and even kingdoms. See how great a conflagration was caused by Doeg's detracting tongue, greater than that caused by Sheba son of Bichri, 2 Samuel 20.1, who by a single word withdrew ten tribes from obedience to their King. Therefore, if it is dangerous for an incendiary to be in a house or city, let us judge that there is no less danger in a person who devotes himself to devising slanders—indeed, so much the greater because this fire, bursting from hell itself, is not so easily extinguished. Hence it is still more evident how precious a good man is, who pours forth from his mouth a fountain of perennial water in order to extinguish the fire kindled from hell.

Verse 28. A man of perversities sends in contention; and a whisperer separates a leader.

In תהפכות (*perversity*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In אלוף (*leader*) there is a synecdoche of the part for anyone who is joined to another by a close bond of fellowship.

The sum is: that a person devoted to perversities mixes contentions even among those who are most closely joined.

Here, as in the preceding Proverb, it is asked who it is whom the Prophet calls a man devoted to perversities. This question appears capable of being answered most conveniently from Solomon and the Apostle. First, from Solomon 2, verses 12, 13, 14, 15, where this perversity is located in depravity of words and abandonment of the right way in order to enter dark paths. But in Proverbs 6 a more particular description occurs, where, while depicting an idle and slothful person, he attributes perversity of mouth to him and, moreover, says that he fabricates evils in his mind and sends in strife. Most deservedly, moreover, an idle person is called a man of perversities, since he reverses the order of nature. For, although he was born to labor, as sparks of fire fly upward, he tears down this order. Hence the Apostle in 1 Thessalonians 3.11 attributes this title, τῶν ἀτάκτων, to idle and sluggish drones. Next, he is not inappropriately called a man of perversity because he twists all the words and deeds of those whom he has determined to defame into the worst construction.

The sending in of contention particularly denotes the effect of this perverse person. For since he devotes himself to no

[1632 witness | PDF p. 337 | printed columns 648–649]

honorable occupation by which he might sustain himself, and yet the mind, which is engaged in continual agitation and motion, cannot rest, it happens that he occupies his whole self in fabricating evils and spreading them abroad, so that in this manner he eats the bread of idleness and sloth. See 1 Timothy 5.13 and 2 Thessalonians 3.11. From this it is evident how greatly idleness must be avoided, especially by the attendants of nobles and the soldiers of commanders, to whom no ἐργοδιῶκται are assigned to drive them to perform work or demand accounts from them, so that, unless they are ἔφοροι over their own selves, they are utterly ruined. Besides this, they disturb the families and societies in which they live with jocular taunts, and devote themselves to biting insolence so that they may cheer themselves and others by the grief of other people.

Next he denotes the efficient, or instrumental, cause by which he mixes contentions: namely, secret mutterings and whisperings. For, like a serpent which strikes while hissing, he does everything secretly. First, because he is slothful and idle, he fears danger for himself. Next, he so defames one person before another that he is ready to defame that very other person before the one with whom he lodged the former accusation. David very often complains of whisperers of this kind, but especially in Psalm 35, where he teaches that, in his presence, they had made a show of mourning his calamity, but after departing from him they raged against him and mocked him.

Finally, the subject matter identifies those among whom he disseminates strife. For he does not only nourish and foster contentions already stirred up, but also sends in new ones, and that among those who are most closely joined and ought to be so: between a Leader and his subjects, between a husband and wife, and so forth. See the example in Doeg and Sheba, mentioned above. This is contrary to Christ's admonition that no one ought to separate what God has joined, Matthew 19.5. But if peacemakers are blessed, then those authors of contentions are accursed; and Solomon pronounces those who sow strife among brothers to be detestable to God, Proverbs 6.19.

Verse 29. A man of violence entices his neighbor and leads him down a way that is not good.

The sum is: that a person thoroughly formed by wickedness leaves nothing unattempted by which he may accomplish the evil designed in his mind against his neighbor.

A violent man is one who, in contempt of divine right and having broken through all laws of equity and goodness, commits the evil which he has desired. And he is precisely the same as the one whom David in Psalm 17 calls a breaker-in, one who breaks through every bar and gate of equity and honesty. In this there appears to be something incongruous: although he calls him violent, he nevertheless says that the man uses flattering words and enticements in order to ensnare his neighbor. For violent people seem rather to do everything openly and by force. But the Prophet refers this violence to the impotence of a mind which can never rest unless the evil it designed has been committed, so that the person cannot take his food or fall asleep unless the crime is accomplished. When one way of wickedness has not succeeded, he immediately undertakes another; he sews the fox's hide to the lion's skin in order to commit the crime he covets; and although he is proud beyond measure, nevertheless, as in Psalm 10, he bends and lowers himself like a lion so that he may suddenly seize the godly.

An example of this is Cain, who enticed his brother into the field with flattering words in order to kill him.

Verse 30. Winking with his eyes, he winks in order to devise evil; compressing his lips, he completes evil.

The sum is: that it often happens that wicked people

[1632 witness | PDF p. 338 | printed columns 650–651]

betray their wickedness by outward gestures of the body.

In chapter 6, verse 13, of this book it was said of the idle person that he winks with his eyes, speaks with his feet, and gives an indication of his sloth with his fingers. But in this place the Proverb extends more widely. For although no one can form a certain judgment from the face and the gesticulation of the body, nevertheless it often happens that signs and conjectures of the mind which are not to be despised are elicited from the body, especially from the gesture of the face, in order to obtain knowledge of the matter we seek. Hence in 1 Kings 20, the servants of Ben-hadad, already defeated in war, approached Ahab in order to court favor for the purpose of having it conferred on Ben-hadad. They were exceedingly intent on exploring not only from Ahab's speech but also from his expression and bodily gesture what disposition King Ahab had toward their master, whether frank or feigned. So also it appears in the Gospel that Christ's disciples, not knowing which of them was going to betray Christ after Christ had foretold that one of their number would be a betrayer, looked at one another's faces to see whether perhaps they might discern him from a sudden pallor or blush, John 13.22.

And indeed, as to winking with the eyes, it appears to be the gesture most suited of all bodily gestures to devising either good or evil, according as those who wink are wicked. For one who considers a matter with closed eyes remains in attentive consideration of it without any intervening interruption or disturbance. For when the eyes are open, whatever is presented to sight draws and snatches the mind elsewhere.

As to the compression of the lips, whether it is simple speech, or figuratively a metonymy of cause for speech altogether, both the biting of the lip from indignation and desire for revenge, and clipped and broken speech, indicate a person so affected that he has determined within himself to inflict punishment.

Here, just as we are taught to strengthen in ourselves the spirit of discernment and judgment, by which we may more easily guard ourselves against the wicked, so we are taught that we ought to give an account not only of deeds and sayings, but also of bodily gestures. For since much labor is spent in training and instructing boys for the use of the Royal court, so that even their gestures may be becoming and honorable, how much more must we apply ourselves so that our gestures may please God, the supreme King. And if much labor is spent on the foul stage so that a costumed actor does not offend anyone among the spectators, how much more must this be done in the theater of the Christian life, whose spectators are God and the holy Angels. From this it is evident how shamefully those people go astray who persuade themselves that God takes no account of the hair or beard and that people may therefore use and abuse them according to their own pleasure, when he has given commands concerning both the beard and the hair.

Verse 31. Gray hair is a crown of glory; let it be found in the way of righteousness.

In עטרת (*crown*) there is a metaphor from Kings encircled with crowns in order to engender greater reverence. In תפארת (*glory*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In שיבה (*gray hair*) there is likewise a metonymy of the adjunct for old age.

The sum is: that old age, especially that which is given by God to the godly, is to be honored.

First, therefore, it must be observed that the benefit of old age ought to refresh the old person who is given it against the disadvantages and difficulties of old age, and ought to stir old people to thanksgiving; and not only that person, but everyone who belongs to his family. For just as it is regarded as a curse when there is no old person in a family, 1 Samuel 2.31, 32, so it must be regarded as a benefit when there are old people in a family.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 339 | printed columns 652–653]

But above all he admonishes the younger people to show honor to them. Honor is shown to the person who wears upon his head a cap of silk or some distinguished ornament. Therefore, the person who is adorned with gray hair as with a crown is wreathed and adorned with something far more precious than a golden crown set with gems, and so forth. If, therefore, we revere the person who wears a crown and diadem, why not an old man? If we revere a person upon whose head mortals have placed a crown, how much more an old man upon whose head the immortal God has placed a crown. Therefore, by this gray hair and length of life God has vindicated from contempt the weakness in their body, the curvature in their back, and the infirmity in the other members of their body. This is a matter of such great and manifest equity that even among the Nations, which lived without God in this world, it prevailed that old people should be revered on account of their age. Hence the Prophet attributes savagery even to an enemy if he does not respect the face of an old person, Lamentations 4.34. And elsewhere the fact that those younger by birth rise up against old and aged people is adduced as evidence of a collapsed or convulsed commonwealth, Isaiah 3.5.

Now if it is asked in what manner they are to be honored, Scripture will resolve this question: first, by rising before them, stepping aside for them on the road, conceding the first place to them at table, addressing them honorably, and the other things of this kind, Leviticus 19.32; 1 Timothy 5.1, 2; then by yielding them the first part in speaking, Job 32.6, 7.

But if it is asked why they are to be esteemed and revered, I answer: first, on account of gray hair itself and their numerous years, because by longevity they approach more nearly the likeness of God, who is called the Ancient of Days, Daniel 7.9. For gray hair itself is like a crown. Next, on account of prudence, because they generally excel through experience and the use of many things. Hence also the Prophet, while declaring ruin and destruction to the people, says among other things that God will remove the old man from the midst of the commonwealth, Isaiah 3.2. But if he is destitute of prudence, just as he is not to be revered on that account as much as if it flourished in him, so he is not to be deprived of honor on account of gray hair.

Hence it is evident how ill-advised are children, servants, or subjects who seize upon old age and the infirmity that accompanies it as an opportunity to honor their parents, masters, or Kings less; as it is likely that the sons of Samuel did, 1 Samuel 8.3, and Adonijah, who, when his father was decrepit and confined to bed, attempted to seize the kingdom of his brother Solomon, 1 Kings 1. This is especially evident from the fact that an express warning is given that no one should hold in contempt a mother who is advanced in years and worn out by age, Proverbs 23.22. See how God struck down forty-two boys who derisively taunted Elisha with his baldness, 2 Kings 2.24.

In the latter hemistich, when he teaches that this crown will be found in the way of righteousness, he indicates sufficiently who it is that adorns people with this crown of gray hair, and upon whom chiefly this benefit is conferred: namely, upon those who persist in God's commandments. It is indeed promised in a certain special manner to those who obey their parents, but it is equally set forth as a reward for those who walk in the commandments of God. Many testimonies of this matter, indeed almost infinite ones, are found; but we shall be content with a few. See Proverbs 4.20, 21, 22 and 3.16.

How many people seek many years, especially years joined with peace? But how few seek them in the way of righteousness? They seek them by their way of life, in medicine, and in riches; but although those years offer themselves to them in the way of righteousness, they do not seek them there. When gray hair is found in the way of righteousness, however, the honor owed to it is doubled.

Although this does not befall all righteous people, nevertheless the promise of God is not vain and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 340 | printed columns 654–655]

void, since, to maintain the truth of this promise, it is enough if this occurs for the most part, even though not always—especially since the brevity of this life is compensated by a longer life in supreme blessedness.

Verse 32. A person slow to anger is better than a strong man; and one who rules his own spirit than one who takes a city.

The sum is that a long-suffering person, however much held in contempt and scorn among people, is more precious before God and the Angels than a strong man who is held in the highest esteem among people.

Here a comparison is instituted between two goods, to determine which is better and more desirable. For in war fortitude and strength are among good things, and those who excelled in them are praised and adorned with encomiums by the Holy Spirit himself in Samuel and Chronicles. Indeed, even the fortitude in war of the reprobate Saul is celebrated by the Holy Spirit. But when fortitude is compared with long-suffering and patience, it must, as it were, rise before them as the more illustrious and be placed after them. Each is a gift of God, from whom every good gift comes; but fortitude is sometimes conferred by God upon people through a considerable benefit of nature, because it is transmitted from parents to children, or it is sometimes acquired by imitation of others who are magnanimous. It is not so with patience and long-suffering. Further, fortitude, or strength of spirit, is conferred by God upon the wicked, but long-suffering only upon the elect. The former fights with a sword of iron, the latter with the sword of the spirit; the former with a shield of steel, the latter with the shield of faith. The former is chiefly occupied in inflicting blows; the latter always in enduring them bravely. Everyone understands how much greater courage there is in patiently sustaining wounds than in inflicting them.

Next, he proves the truth of this axiom from the effects of the two virtues compared with each other. For a patient or long-suffering person conquers his own spirit, but a strong person conquers a city. Yet it is a greater thing to conquer the spirit or mind than to subjugate the countless bodies of people, while the minds and spirits of those whom you have conquered have not been brought under the yoke. Further, although you may lead a thousand captives bound to your chariot in triumph, you may meanwhile be captive to your own passions and exist as a base slave. “For by whom a person is overcome, of him he is the servant.” What honor will consequently return to you, since you are held bound by harsher chains and thicker cords than those with which you bound others? Moreover, the long-suffering person conquers not only human beings but also the principalities and powers of darkness by patiently enduring injuries, Ephesians 6.12, whereas the person who occupied a city emerges as victor only over human beings. Therefore the song of the Israelite women who, coming to meet the Israelites returning from war, sang that their Saul had slain one thousand in battle but David ten thousand, will fit here not inappropriately. But if people do not render these due and just praises, God and the holy Angels—by whom it has been praised that they be praised—will certainly bestow these honors upon those who are patient and long-suffering.

But if conquering anger is of such great worth, let those who regard it as sport and play to provoke their neighbor—and indeed their brother in Christ—to anger by offering some injury consider what they will answer to God.

The latter part of this Proverb embraces all disturbances of the mind whatsoever and can therefore be applied to them.

Verse 33. The lot is cast into the lap, but its whole judgment is from the Lord.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 341 | printed columns 656–657]

In חיק (*lap*) there is a synecdoche of a species for a fold of a garment, an urn, or something of this kind, into which lots are cast.

The sum is that all things, even those which appear least of all to be so, are disposed by the most watchful providence of God.

When the casting of lots is permitted, people restrain their vote and judgment in the matter to be adjudicated by lot; and consequently human counsel and reason intervene in casting the lot into the lap, the fold of the garment, or the urn. But it is far otherwise in the outcome of the lot, for there God presides without any human deliberation.

A most firm proof of this matter is that all the lots whose inscriptions are mentioned came out and attained their effect in such a way that everyone who is not blind can easily understand that God governed the issue and outcome of the lot. The first lot which we read to have been commanded by God occurs in Joshua 7, for selecting Achan, who had stolen the accursed thing. There the manifold providence of God shines forth: first in the tribe, then in the family, and finally in Achan himself, who had committed sacrilege. The second lot was in Joshua 15, in the distribution of the land of Canaan, where God's wondrous providence shone forth, by which he so governed the lots that the part of the land which Jacob had foretold one hundred years before would come to each tribe did come to it. The third casting of a lot occurs in 1 Samuel 14, where, when inquiry was made by lot as to who had eaten something in disregard of the King's command, Jonathan was found by lot. The fourth lot is in Jonah 1, where the lot fell upon Jonah, who had been the cause of the stormy sea and tempest. Finally, the fifth and last occurs in the New Testament, Acts 1, where a lot decided which of two disciples would be an Apostle. From all these things, most wisely and foresightedly governed and dispensed, it is evident that the lot is governed by God.

When he says, moreover, that its whole judgment is from God, he thereby indicates that it is as much from God that this person is rejected as that the other is chosen; that it is as much from God that one person is condemned to death and hanging as that the other is acquitted of death; and that it is as much from him that this person has obtained a more excellent inheritance as that the other has received a worse one by lot. For this reason the former furnishes an occasion for praising God just as the latter furnishes an occasion for calmly and equably bearing whatever God shall have ordained by lot.

Next, by calling the lot a judgment he teaches that recourse is to be had to the judgment of a lot only in weightier matters and in a matter of some great importance, especially since through the lot God himself sits in judgment. For just as God advised through Jethro that, when the lesser cases had been adjudicated by other judges, the difficult ones should be reserved for the cognizance of Moses, so God instituted that a case which could be discerned by no power and insight of any person's understanding should be referred to him through the judgment of a lot. To this pertains the fact that these lots were always handled religiously among the people of God and holily, with the highest reverence. Hence in Joshua 7 the people are commanded to sanctify themselves before they proceed to cast the lot. And in Joshua 15 Eleazar the high priest is designated as one of those who ought to preside over the casting of lots. And in Acts 1 Peter, with the prayers of the whole Church, supplicates God to govern the outcome of the lot. Indeed Saul, however ungodly, did not undertake a lot without prayers. We read only that the profane sailors in Jonah offered no prayers to their gods before the lot.

Hence it is evident how great among us is the abuse of this most holy judgment of God, since we employ the lot for sport and play in the smallest and most trivial matters, although a lot should be applied to no matter to which prayers and fervent prayer to God should not be joined.
