ON

GABRIEL PENOTTUS’S

*Bulwark of Human Liberty*

BOOK III, CHAPTER XIX.

In which he brings forward and answers the opponents’ arguments against the foregoing argument that he
fashioned for Thomas.

PENOTTUS.

Molina and others rise up with many arguments against this argument brought forward by Thomas; they
must now be briefly reviewed and answered. The first argument is that this presence and coexistence of
things in eternity is fictitious and impossible. But to this I answer that the presence of things in
eternity, in the manner explained by us, is not fictitious but true and necessary, and that from it the
argument for divine knowledge is most properly taken.

RESPONSE.

We have already taken pains to show how foolish is this argument of yours which you fasten upon Thomas,
but without any sobriety or probability. To put it briefly, I say that there is no opinion of the other
Scholastics which we would not regard as far preferable to this one of yours, as we have already said.

PENOTTUS.

The second argument: God has knowledge of future contingents which never were and never will be. For he
knew that if David had gone down to Keilah, the men of Keilah would have delivered him into Saul’s
hands, 1 Samuel 23; and that if certain miracles had been performed in Tyre and Sidon, they would have
repented, Luke 10. Yet these things did not in fact happen, nor will they happen. Therefore the presence
of things is not necessary in order that the divine intellect may know future contingents.

I answer that Thomas’s argument does not apply to every kind of knowledge of every contingent that is
future in any fashion whatever, but only to intuitive knowledge of a contingent future absolutely and
upon a hypothesis. Although those things were not future absolutely, they were nevertheless future upon
a hypothesis.

RESPONSE.

The Novarian agrees excellently with the Jesuits in attributing to God cognition of conditional
futures; and why should we not readily allow, in maintaining so monstrous an opinion, one mule to
scratch another? Yet Penottus’s answer is by no means pleasing, since he thrusts upon Thomas a
distinction which he has not yet shown to appear anywhere in Aquinas. Whatever God knows by the
knowledge of vision, Aquinas derives the cognition of it from the eternal presence of things before
God, without any distinction between futures absolutely and futures conditionally. He ought rather to
have said, if my judgment counts for anything, that the argument brought forward by Thomas for
establishing the certainty of divine cognition concerns only the cognition of those things which God
knows by the knowledge of vision. But knowledge of conditional futures belongs rather to the knowledge
of simple intelligence than to the knowledge of vision, for the knowledge of vision embraces only
things future, present, and past. Yet the things you call conditional futures are no more future than
nonfuture; indeed, they should much rather be called simply nonfuture than future. Again, according to
the common opinion of the theologians, things that are only possible are established as the object of
the knowledge of simple intelligence. But the things you call conditional futures are merely possible.

PENOTTUS.

The third argument, also Molina’s: God, comprehending himself through natural knowledge, most perfectly
knows everything contained in himself. Therefore in himself he knows the free choice of any creature
which he can make through himself, and whatever the free choice of any creature would effect—upon the
hypothesis that God willed to create it and to constitute it in this or that order of things—and to
which side it would determine itself. He knows this before any absolute decree of the divine will about
things to be created or constituted in such an order, for it would be unworthy and impious to assert
that God is ignorant of it. Therefore, as soon as the decree of the divine will to constitute things in
this or that order is added, God sees what free choice will do before anything has emanated from it,
either in time or from eternity, according to the being of actual existence. Therefore that presence of
things according to their actual existences is wholly irrelevant, since perfect knowledge of future
contingents is conceived in God without it.

RESPONSE.

This argument of Molina is an enthymeme whose antecedent rests solely upon a fuller explanation of what
they call

[Book I, printed p. 110; BSB canvas 00136]

middle knowledge. Penottus, however, undertakes to maintain this middle knowledge, so that no other
convenient answer is available to him for this argument, according to the opinion of Penottus, who
embraces middle knowledge together with the Jesuits, than to deny the consequence. It will therefore be
worthwhile to observe carefully how Penottus conducts himself in refuting this argument.

PENOTTUS.

I answer, he says, that the argument involves many things which the Doctors commonly reject as false.
But as much as is sufficient for solving the argument, I say that the claim that God, by comprehending
free choice, sees whatever it will actually do if it is constituted in this or that order can be
understood in two ways. In one way, he would see, or sees, whatever it actually does by stopping only at
the cognition of the nature of free choice itself and of the aids in which that free choice would be
constituted, prescinding from actual knowledge of the free determination of that same choice constituted
in such an order. In this sense it is absolutely denied that God, by comprehending free choice, knows
whatever it will effect if it is constituted in this or that order. Nor does this argue any unworthiness
in God, but an impossibility in the thing. In the same way it is impossible that, by considering only
the future being which a contingent has in a contingent cause insofar as it is contingent, one should
know certainly whether it will be future. For this would destroy the nature of a contingent cause, as
was said above. In the other way, he sees whatever it will actually effect by attaining this actual
determination of free choice to this or that effect and the actual volition which would proceed from
that same free choice. In this sense it can be admitted that, by comprehending free choice, he knows
what it would do if it were constituted in such an order with such aids. Although this manner of
speaking is not proper, because in this way God does not know what will be future from free choice by
comprehending the nature of free choice, but by seeing its actual determination to such an effect and
the actual emanation of that effect from free choice itself, upon the supposition that it were
constituted in such an order. This is to return to Thomas’s opinion and to the argument assigned by him.

RESPONSE.

Although Penottus defends middle knowledge together with Molina, from which I conjectured that no other
means of meeting the preceding argument was available to him than denying the consequence—because its
antecedent rests only upon his own opinion concerning the explanation of middle knowledge—Penottus
nevertheless attacks the antecedent itself boldly and manfully, but with a distinction. To confess the
truth of the matter, this Novarian is accustomed to luxuriate in his distinctions, by which he relieves
himself as easily as a beggar shakes lice out of his bosom. Come then, let us examine this distinction.
To see what free choice will do, he says, can be understood in two ways: either by stopping only at the
cognition of free choice itself and of the aids in which it will be constituted, or by attaining the
actual determination of free choice itself. But, I say, this distinction is exceedingly absurd, since
it does not so much distinguish different ways of seeing what free choice will do as wretchedly tear
apart the members of one and the same way. For one who comprehends what free choice will do does not by
any means stop at cognition of the nature of free choice and of the aids in which it is constituted; he
comprehends all these things and also the actual determination of free choice to this or that effect as
future. In this same way Penottus himself would have God behold from eternity the being of actual
existence which future contingents will have—that is, what will be. For to see that free choice will do
this or that is to see that free choice will at some point determine itself to this effect.
Nevertheless, Molina does not signify this alone; he goes further and indicates the manner in which God
sees these things, namely from considering the nature of each free choice as placed in comparison with
the aids by which it has been appointed by God that free choice should be moved to do what it will at
some point do. Thus Molina makes the congruity of the divine aids with the condition or inclination of
free choice the root of divine cognition. I readily grant that this too is, in my judgment, impossible.
Nevertheless, those who labor to bring forth a cause of the cognition of future contingents and do
bring forth some cause conduct themselves far more learnedly than those who make a great show of
promising to do the very same thing yet bring nothing whatever to light. This indeed is the disgraceful
issue of the Novarian’s opinion.

For first he drags Aquinas to this point, as though Aquinas did not derive from the presence of things
before God a reason why they are known by God at all, but why they are known certainly and infallibly.
Yet once he has retreated to this position so that his affair might be in safe shallows, he makes
shipwreck here in the very harbor of all sobriety and common sense. For his argument concludes only
this: God therefore certainly sees the things which are future because he certainly sees the things
which are present; or, God therefore certainly and infallibly sees the things which are future because
he certainly and infallibly sees the things which are future. This proof is altogether identical and
most deserves to be hissed out of every school of the theologians. Indeed, it is customary for this
Novarian to argue in this fashion, as when he says in this very place: “In this sense it can be admitted
that, by comprehending free choice, he knows what it would do if it were constituted in such an order
with such aids”—that is, provided only that he sees the actual determination of free choice itself to do
something. And what else is this than to see what it would do if it were constituted in such an order?
Thus the conclusion which he proposes so magisterially comes to this: “Therefore, if he comprehends in
this way what free choice does—namely by knowing what it would do—in this sense it can be admitted that
he would know what it would do!”

But it surpasses all wonder that he feared he had not yet been sufficiently cautious, and so adds the
qualification, “if it were constituted in such an order with such aids,” as though, in whatever order it
were constituted and with whatever aids, it were not equally true that if God saw what free choice
would do, he would undoubtedly see what free choice would do. As for Thomas’s opinion, we have already
shown—and it can also be established from the judgment of almost everyone—that he grounds the certain
and infallible knowledge of future contingents which we attribute to God in a certain presence of
things before God. Penottus, whatever he pretends at the outset, acknowledges no presence except an
objective one. And afterward he himself returns to this objective presence of things before God.

PENOTTUS.

The fourth argument is this: God does not receive his knowledge from created things; therefore, whether
things exist or do not exist makes no difference to the certainty of divine knowledge, since that
certainty must be sought from another source. This is Scotus’s principal argument.

RESPONSE.

I judge this argument to be most solid; nor does Aquinas oppose it, since his undoubted opinion

[Book I, printed p. 111; BSB canvas 00137]

is this: God does not see things outside God in themselves, but in himself. Therefore I judge the
presence of things in which Aquinas grounds the cognition of things to be nothing other than the
presence of things before God in willed being. Thus Aquinas, Scotus, and Durandus agree exactly. But let
us see what Penottus answers here.

PENOTTUS.

The consequence is denied. For although the being of actual existence of a future contingent is not
necessary in order that the divine intellect should receive its knowledge from it, it is nevertheless
necessary in order that the future contingent itself can be an object of certain divine knowledge. For
nothing can be an object of certain knowledge which does not contain determinate truth in itself. But a
future contingent does not contain determinate truth except by reason of that being of actual existence
which it has for some difference of time.

RESPONSE.

This vicious argument is redeemed by no candor. First, one cannot easily discern in which of the two
distinctions this answer rests, for he deceitfully presses a double distinction. The first concerns the
necessity of a thing either in order that the divine intellect may receive its knowledge from it, or in
order that it may be an object of divine knowledge. The other concerns the necessity of a thing either
for knowledge of the thing or for certain knowledge of it. For only in the latter member is mention
made both of an object and of certain knowledge. Therefore, to drive this fox from its lairs, it must be
observed that when theologians say that God does not receive his knowledge from created things, they
mean only that the existence of created things is by no means required in order that God may know them
intuitively, although it is required for human and angelic intuitive cognition. Yet when angels or
human beings see created things, they still cannot properly be said to receive vision from existing
things. Therefore, if Penottus strains the phrase by dragging it to the strict propriety of speech, he
will act not as a theologian but as a sophist.

I therefore interpret Penottus as intending that the actual existence of a future contingent, although
it is not required in order that it can be an object of certain divine knowledge, is nevertheless
required in order that it can be an object of certain divine knowledge. Once his answer has been
stripped bare in this way, it appears exceedingly absurd at first sight. For, unless I am mistaken,
everyone agrees that in whatever manner God can have knowledge of a thing, in that same manner God can
undoubtedly have certain knowledge of the thing. For unless he knows certainly, he will be in doubt.
But to be in doubt whether some thing will be future or not is not to know that a thing will be future,
but rather to be held in suspense. Again, since GOD, just as he knows all things, also knows all things
certainly in himself, what sort of imagining is this which invents the actual existence of things as
necessary, not so much in order that God may know things as in order that he may know them certainly?

Second, how absurd it is to say that God must see some thing to be present in order that he may
certainly see some thing to be future! Why not proceed with equal insanity and say that God must see
some thing to be present in order that he may certainly know that same thing to be past? Third, it is
false that a future contingent does not contain determinate truth except by reason of the actual
existence which it has. For what reason can be invented why it should not contain determinate truth by
reason of the actual existence which it will have, just as much as by reason of the actual existence
which it has? Observe in passing Penottus’s customary practice: he uses various phrases expressing the
same thing like a trick rider leaping between horses, so that he may always follow the convenience of
the business at hand and cloak his frauds with a variety of phraseology. Here he philosophizes about
the being of actual existence which a future contingent has, namely as something present. Elsewhere one
everywhere sees him philosophize about the being of actual existence which a future contingent will
have, namely about something future, which is also more congruently said in accordance with Penottus’s
opinion. For he acknowledges no presence of things before God, besides presence in cognized being,
except that which occurs in time. But we acknowledge another presence, one that truly precedes divine
knowledge, namely in willed being before God. And what sober person doubts that future contingents can
obtain determinate truth from the determination of the divine will?

PENOTTUS.

The fifth argument: In God there is providence and predestination; therefore in God there is knowledge
of future contingents before they are conceived as having actual existence. The consequence is
evident. For things are not provided for and predestined because they are future; on the contrary, they
are future because they are provided for. I answer, first, that no one is pressed more by this argument
than Molina, its author, since he maintains that no free act was predetermined by God. Second, I answer
*ad hominem* that it is sufficient that acts which are provided for and predestined are foreseen as
future before the free decree through that middle knowledge which Molina himself places in God. Third,
I answer that acts provided for and predestined by God fall into two classes. Some are those which God
places in us without us and without our free consent; these he could foreknow in his efficacious divine
decree as in a determined cause. Others are those which God does not, by the ordinary law, place in us
without us and without our free consent. These were discussed above in Chapter 14, in the fourth manner
of reconciling the authorities of the Fathers. With respect to the latter, it is denied that these
effects were efficaciously provided for and predestined by God before the free determination and
cooperation of the divine will had been foreseen, as we shall say in book 4, chapter 6.

RESPONSE.

This argument of Molina by no means operates against the Dominicans, since they place the determination
of the divine will before the existence of things in eternity, as may be seen in Álvarez. Furthermore,
he proceeds far too loosely when from the predestination of things he concludes that knowledge of
future things precedes the conception of their actual existence. For undoubtedly knowledge of the
actual existence of future things presupposes their presence before God in knowable being. But as for
Penottus’s answers: just as according to Molina no free act is defined by God before it is known, so
according to him no free act seems to be known by God before it is defined. Nor do I judge it probable
that Molina holds that acts provided for and predestined by God are foreseen by God as future before
his free decree. Unless I am mistaken, what he says concerns the provision of conditionally future
acts; but what is future only conditionally must by no means be called future simply. As for the third
answer, unless my

[Book I, printed p. 112; BSB canvas 00138]

conjecture deceives me, Molina’s argument concerns future things which occur through the determination
of the divine will alone and do not depend upon human choice, though their occurrence must nevertheless
be contingent, since God freely does whatever he does. Yet the Dominicans promise the existence of all
such things in eternity, and indeed from eternity, to intuitive divine knowledge. But Molina wrongly
attempts to overthrow their opinion with an argument of this kind, as was said long ago.

Yet the chief claim of both your opinions must not be passed over here: namely, that things actually
exist before they are foreknown in some divine decree. If this is true, it follows that things actually
exist before God has decreed that they should exist. For if the decree concerning their actual
existence were prior to their actual existence, their actual existence could certainly have been
foreknown in that decree as future before it was actual. But it is a monster of an opinion that the
actual existence of things is prior to the divine decree concerning their actual existence. For on
your opinion the actual existence of things is produced only in time. Do you then also maintain that
the divine decrees concerning the actual existence of things are themselves temporal and later than
the actual existence of things? Yet I easily see what you intend: namely, that faith, a person’s
repentance, and every good work will be future before it is decreed by God. But this too nourishes a
monster of an opinion. For what need was there for God to decree that a person’s faith or repentance
would be future if he had already foreseen before his decree that it would be future?

Again, a person’s faith is by its nature merely possible, and it could not migrate from the condition of
a possible thing into the condition of a future thing without a cause. Produce, therefore, the cause of
this migration, especially since it occurred from eternity. For Peter’s faith was future from eternity.
The cause cannot be sought outside God, since outside God nothing is eternal, and what is not eternal
cannot cause something which was from eternity. It remains, therefore, that this cause be found in God
himself or be found nowhere. Yet it cannot be found in the essence of God considered simply, for the
divine essence apart from the determination of his will does not make possible things pass into the
condition of future things. Nor can knowledge apart from the will accomplish any such thing. It remains,
therefore, that the will of God, and it alone, is the cause through which things which by their nature
are merely possible become future, namely through the determination of the will itself.

But because afterward you profess to express the same thing in other words, and because it may be that
on second thought you judged your opinion should be stated more moderately, let us also examine what
that statement was like. “It is denied,” you say, “that these effects were efficaciously provided for and
predestined by God before the free determination and cooperation of the divine will had been foreseen.”
But these words are far more monstrous than your preceding statements. Take an instance: it is denied,
for example, that faith was provided for and predestined before the free determination and cooperation
of the divine will had been foreseen. Yet it is incredible that anyone could become so demented as to
cherish such monstrosities of opinion. What, then? Does the predestination of faith to be given place
before itself the foresight of the free divine determination and cooperation for producing faith? With
equal reason it could have been said that the decree to create the world follows the provision of the
free divine determination to create the world. But the predestination of faith is the divine will’s very
determination to produce faith.

Therefore, so far as I can attain by conjecture, there is a typographical error here. It should not have
said, “before the free determination and cooperation of the divine will had been foreseen,” but rather,
“before the free determination and cooperation of the human will had been foreseen.” You therefore
intend the free determination and cooperation of the human will toward believing to be foreseen by God
before the decree to give faith, or at least from some source other than God’s decree. But I prove the
contrary by a demonstrative argument—not one of the kinds which the Novarian, in his distinguished
subtlety, is accustomed to share with us, than which the world never sees anything more diluted, but
one for which neither Penottus himself nor the whole nation of the Jesuits will ever produce any
tolerable solution. I frame it as follows.

The free determination and cooperation of the human will toward believing cannot be foreseen by God
from eternity as future unless it is future from eternity. But it is not future from eternity except by
God’s decree. Therefore it cannot be foreseen as future except by God’s decree. I prove the minor. Since
everything future outside God is by its nature only possible, it must migrate from the condition of a
possible thing into the condition of a future thing. But nothing can from eternity migrate from the
condition of a possible thing into the condition of a future thing except by God’s decree. Therefore it
comes about by God’s decree that some thing was future from eternity—that is, that it passed from the
condition of a possible thing into the condition of a future thing. The major is of undoubted truth. I
confirm the minor in this way. A migration such as that of a possible thing into the condition of a
future thing cannot occur without a cause. But no cause of this migration can be given except the will
of God decreeing it. Therefore this migration occurred only by God’s decree, or by the will of God
decreeing it. The major is extremely well known, for if some possible thing could pass without a cause
into the state of a future thing, why would not every possible thing pass in the same way into the state
of a future thing?

I prove the minor as follows. If something besides the will of God decreeing can be the cause of this
migration, then it is either something outside God or something within God. But neither something
outside God nor something within God can be the cause of this migration. It remains, therefore, that it
be attributed solely to the decreeing will of God. I shall establish the minor again through its parts.
Nothing outside God, I say, can be the cause of this migration. The reason is that this migration
occurred from eternity; therefore something which also existed from eternity must be assigned as its
cause. For what did not exist when this migration occurred could not be its cause, since nonbeing
causes nothing. No action belongs to nonbeing. Nor does anything within GOD occur as the cause of this
migration except the will of GOD. For the knowledge of GOD presupposes rather than causes that something
will be future. If it is said that God’s knowledge is practical knowledge, I answer that God’s knowledge
is never practical except as joined with God’s will. Thus Aquinas, part 1, q. 14, art. 8: “His knowledge
must be the cause of things insofar as it has the will joined to it.” And q. 19, art.

[Book I, printed p. 113; BSB canvas 00139]

11, reply 1: “Knowledge is not the cause of the things which come to be except through the will. For we
do not do the things we know unless we will them. And therefore a sign is not attributed to knowledge as
it is attributed to the will.” And Cajetan, part 1, q. 14, art. 13: “Knowledge does not cause unless
determined by the will.”

PENOTTUS.

The sixth argument: God permits sins to occur, but to permit something to occur is to see that it will
be future unless it is prevented and to will not to prevent it. Therefore God foresees sins as future
upon the hypothesis that they are permitted before he sees the being which they will actually have in
the future at some difference of time.

I answer that nothing is concluded from this, because things which are future upon a hypothesis are
known by God under that aspect insofar as he sees the actual determination of causes to their effects
and the emanation of effects from their causes as future upon the hypothesis that he willed so to create
and order those causes.

RESPONSE.

Molina’s argument does nothing against the Dominicans. For according to the Dominicans, just as good
things are future from the decree to effect them, so evil things are future from the decree to permit
them; and from decrees of this kind the futurition of things is prior to the foresight of those things
under the aspect of future things. Whatever will be future in time so as to coexist with eternity,
which is wholly simultaneous, they therefore maintain by this very fact to exist from eternity before
God, at least in eternity.

As for Penottus’s peculiar opinion, to foresee sins as future from the decree to permit them is not
prior to seeing the being which they will actually have in the future at some difference of time. For
to foresee sins as future and to see the being which they will have in the future are altogether the
same. Unless I am mistaken, Penottus himself intends this, although he by no means suitably adapts his
answer. For Molina’s argument proceeded from the foresight of future sin upon the hypothesis that it is
permitted, while Penottus’s answer proceeds from the permission of future sin upon the hypothesis that
God willed so to create and order its causes. The Novarian indulges himself too much in this kind of
oblique statement through variations of phrase. But the opinion of both men, grounded in their doctrine
of middle knowledge, is wholly heterodox. For God cannot see that actual determination of causes to
effects, in whatever order the causes are placed, apart from the decree of his will. The reason is the
one I pursued above: in such an order, the determination of the human will to a designated effect is by
its nature merely possible, and it cannot migrate from the condition of a possible thing into the state
of a future thing without a cause. Yet no cause of this can reasonably be imagined except the decreeing
will of God, as was argued above.

PENOTTUS.

The seventh argument, Scotus’s: either God knows a future contingent through the actual presence of
things insofar as it is present, and this cannot be, because a future thing is not known as present
except when it is present, not beforehand; or he knows it insofar as it is future, and this too is
impossible, because what is known as future is not known as actually present.

RESPONSE.

I judge that this argument fights vigorously against those who assert that the actual existence of
things is really present to God from eternity. Yet this opinion seems to me to be exceedingly far from
Thomas’s opinion, since in my judgment Thomas establishes no other presence before God of things that
will be future than an intentional presence—not merely in cognized being, however, but in willed being.
According to this opinion there is no need to answer Scotus’s argument, since Scotus vigorously
establishes this presence of all things before God. But if there were need to answer, we would answer as
follows: God knows a future contingent when it is present, and we acknowledge that it is present before
God prior to cognition, not really but intentionally, namely in willed being. Nothing about this
prevents it from being known as future really and according to real being in time. But let us consider
Penottus’s answer.

PENOTTUS.

I answer that Thomas never said that God knows a future contingent only insofar as it is present. He
said that God has certain knowledge of a future contingent because he sees not only its future being,
which for one difference of time it has only in its causes, but also that present being which for
another difference of time it has in itself. God therefore sees a contingent both insofar as it is
future for one difference of time and as present for another difference of time. Thus he sees it as
future and nevertheless sees it certainly and infallibly.

RESPONSE.

This gradation, namely that God sees not only the future being of a future contingent but also its
present being, is nowhere, so far as I know, found in Thomas. I grant that this gradation does occur in
Thomas: God sees future contingents not only as they are in their causes but also as they are in their
presentiality. Yet the gradation invented by Penottus is no gradation but the repetition of one and the
same thing through varied phraseology. For although here he describes the present being of a future
contingent as that which it has in itself, he frequently says that being of this kind is what it will
have in itself. But there is no discrepancy whatever between the future being of a thing and the
present being which it will have.

Again, it is false that a future contingent has its present being when it is seen by God, for it is
seen by God from eternity. Yet no future contingent has or has had from eternity its present being in
itself of the sort which Penottus imagines—namely present being really in time. Meanwhile it can have,
and indeed does have, its present being intentionally before God in willed being, and does so from
eternity. In willed being God beholds all future contingents. From it he knows not only that for one
fixed difference of time they will be future and for another difference of time they will be present,
but also that for a third difference of time they will be past. But Penottus had perceived that this
answer of his needed some explanation, and so in what follows he presents it to us in this way.

PENOTTUS.

For a fuller explanation of this answer it must be noted that, just as three things are required in
order for something to be called truly future: first, that it does not have being in itself; second,
that it has possible being in its causes; and third, that through the possible being which it has in
its causes it tends toward real being of actual existence in itself outside its causes. The formal
character of the future as future consists in these three conditions: by the first, the future is
distinguished from the present; by the second, from the past; and by the third, from that which can come
to be but nevertheless has never been made and never will be. Thus three things are required in order
for something to be known as truly and properly future. First, it must be known not to have being in
itself for some difference of time. Second, it must be known at the same difference of time to have
possible being in a cause

[Book I, printed p. 114; BSB canvas 00140]

tending toward real being. Third, for the immediately following time it must be known that it will have
being of actual existence outside its causes, and it must be seen certainly and infallibly to be.
Therefore God knows future contingents not only because he sees the being which they will have outside
their causes, but because he sees that at this preceding difference of time they do not have this being
but have only possible being in their causes.

RESPONSE.

But this explanation is utterly worthless, nor does it carry any weight for making his opinion more
commendable through any illumination. Evidently this man’s superficial intellect seems exceedingly well
suited for roaming through ridiculous matters. At the end he briefly repeats the same things he had
said before, when he says: “Therefore God knows future contingents not only because he sees the being
which they will have.” Here what he formerly expressed in terms of the present he expresses again in
terms of the future, though he distinguishes it from the future as though the being which a thing will
have were present being. But what he adds is also false: that for a difference of time preceding its
existence a future contingent has only possible being in its causes. For it has not only possible being
but also future being. Although in secondary causes it has only possible being, in the first cause of
all things and in the determination of his will it has future being and determinate truth insofar as it
is future.

PENOTTUS.

The eighth argument: Just as eternity coexists with all time, so too does *aevum*. Therefore, just as
God knows future contingents because they are present to him in eternity, so an angel will be able to
know the same contingents because they are present to it in *aevum*. For just as God’s cognition is
measured by eternity, so the cognition of an angel is measured by *aevum*. I answer with Cajetan that an
angelic operation which is cognition is not measured by *aevum* but by an instant of discrete time. And
although that discrete time consists of indivisible instants, they are not all simultaneous but succeed
one another; angelic cognitions do so in the same manner. I add also that *aevum* does not contain time
as eternity contains it, which had to be proved by the arguer and was not proved.

RESPONSE.

Just as eternity is wholly simultaneous, so too is *aevum* by the confession of all the Scholastics.
It follows that whatever coexists with *aevum* coexists with the whole of it, just as whatever coexists
with eternity coexists with the whole of it. By the reasoning from which the Dominicans conclude that
all future things have existed simultaneously from eternity in eternity, it is likewise necessary that
all things future in time have existed in *aevum* from the beginning of *aevum*. If they coexisted with
angelic *aevum* from its beginning, what prevents all future contingents from having been known by the
angels from the beginning? For what? Does it not belong to an angel to know all things which coexist
with the angels themselves and with their duration? Nor is it an objection that *aevum* does not
contain time as eternity contains it. For if the account of *aevum* and eternity is alike in that each
is wholly simultaneous, whatever coexists with *aevum* must coexist with its whole just as it does with
eternity.

PENOTTUS.

The ninth argument: If the certainty of divine cognition concerning future contingents must be taken
from the presentiality of things, their knowledge should no longer be called foreknowledge but
knowledge of present things.

I answer that it can properly be called this, and that Boethius calls it this in book 5 of the
*Consolation*, final prose section; Anselm also does so in *On the Fall of the Devil*, chapters 13 and
21, and in *On Concord*, chapter 1. St. Ambrose, *On the Faith*, book 5, chapter 8; Augustine, sermon 11,
*On the Words of the Apostle*; and many others among those cited in Chapter 16 can be added to prove the
first proposition.

RESPONSE.

But Penottus acknowledges no presence of things before God other than according to objective being. I
cannot sufficiently marvel at the man’s brazen audacity when, beyond all shame, he plays the dissembler,
as though he wished to approach the defense of the Dominican opinion concerning the presence of future
contingents before God as a shield-bearing champion of the first nobility. Yet he has nothing in common
with the Dominican opinion; he plays the old fox under this name because he wishes to appear to defend
Thomas—brilliantly, to be sure—after he has so moderated Thomas’s opinion by his interpretation to suit
his own Scholastic strength that he hopes he can defend Aquinas, even according to his interpretation,
from the objections of Molina and the others. But this argument is childish enough.

PENOTTUS.

The tenth argument: Divine foreknowledge is the cause of things and of future contingents themselves.
Therefore the certainty of divine knowledge cannot be taken from the very presence of future
contingents, since every cause is understood to be prior to its effect, while divine foreknowledge is
the cause of the existence and presentiality of the things themselves.

I answer that the knowledge which is the cause of things is not the knowledge of vision, which is
merely speculative, but practical knowledge, which we said above in Chapter 12 belongs to the knowledge
of simple intelligence. I add again that not even through practical knowledge can God certainly know
future contingents, especially those which depend upon human choice, before he sees the free
determination of our choice toward them. For divine foreknowledge is not the proximate and immediate
effective principle of things and does not effect things except when joined with a free decree of the
free will, as was said above. And the absolute decree of the divine will concerning free acts which
depend upon human choice does not precede the inspected determination of human choice, as will be said
in the following book and was intimated above at the end of Chapter 14.

RESPONSE.

Penottus gives a double answer to this argument, but neither satisfies. The first, I grant, is more
congruent with the truth and offers a superficial satisfaction—I do not deny it—because this is how
things stand in human affairs. For although an artisan produces some artifact through his art and
knowledge, he nevertheless beholds it as made not through that same knowledge but through ocular
vision, and is acted upon by seeing his own work. But this is not how matters stand in divine things.
The same knowledge both shows what it is fitting to make and, when only the determination of the divine
will is added, sees that it will be future. Nor does God see otherwise than in himself those things
which are daily produced and become present in time. Nevertheless, only when the determination of the
divine will is added does a thing become knowable under the aspect of a future thing, since by its
nature it was knowable only under the aspect of a possible thing.

But Penottus cannot digest this determination of the divine will as constituting things knowable under
the aspect of future things. Evidently he intends that before God sees the free determination of our
choice, he cannot know as future those things

[Book I, printed p. 115; BSB canvas 00141; upper section]

which depend upon human choice. But then, I say, God does not decree the determination of human choice
toward the performance of any work, but foresees it as future. Thus God foresaw but did not decree the
determination of King Cyrus’s choice to release his people. He foresaw but by no means decreed the
determination of King Josiah’s choice to burn the bones of the prophets to ashes upon the altar at
Bethel. Indeed, whenever the justice or mercy of God wonderfully exerts itself through the wills of
people who are unaware of it, God foresaw but did not determine the determination of their choice. It
will follow that all things are not so much administered by God’s providence as that all things very
fortunately turn out for God’s use.

You will say: Yet it is from God that human beings are constituted in such an order of things, from
which he sees as future that they will do the things through which either God’s justice or his mercy
will shine. If this suffices to constitute GOD the author of works both of justice and of mercy, why
does it not also suffice to constitute GOD the author of sins? First, because both God’s justice and his
mercy frequently shine through human sins. His justice shone when Sennacherib, king of Assyria, perished
by the sword of his own sons, and also when Ahithophel and Judas perished by their own hands after each
had betrayed his lord. His mercy shone when both Gentiles and Jews drove the Son of God to the cross.
Second, because it is permissible for God to constitute a person both in an order in which he sees that
person will sin and in an order in which he sees that person will piously perform some good work.

But let us ask whence God sees that the free determination of human choice will be future, since by its
nature it is merely possible. Was it brought about by some cause outside God that it migrated from the
condition of a possible thing into the condition of a future thing? Indeed, this is altogether
ἀδύνατον—impossible. For this migration occurred from eternity, but outside God nothing existed from
eternity which could cause that migration; and what did not exist could not cause another thing, much
less do so from eternity. The cause of this migration must therefore be sought within God. Yet it is
not found in God’s knowledge separated from God’s will, as everyone confesses with one voice. It remains,
therefore, that the futurition of all things be grounded in the determination of the divine will as its
sole and unique cause—that is, the migration of all those things from things merely possible (which is
all they were by their nature) into the condition of future things. When Penottus has solved this
argument, it will demand a monument far more honorable than this entire *Bulwark of Human Liberty*.

At the end he is immensely pleased with himself as he embraces his cloud in place of Juno, like Ixion.
For he wishes Thomas’s argument to be understood according to the petty measure of his own
interpretation, though we have more than once explained its unhappy conclusion, to which it returns:
“Because God certainly sees the present being of a thing, therefore he certainly sees that thing to be
future.” Or, at least somewhat more offensively, it must be reduced to this: “Because God certainly and
infallibly sees some thing to be future, therefore he certainly and infallibly sees that thing to be
future.” He sells this argument as the clearest and most solid of all those which the Fathers or the
Scholastics have assigned thus far, an argument which, once its terms are understood, can scarcely—
indeed, scarcely at all—be denied except by an obstinate person. From these things you may see τῦτ᾽
χρηστήρια τ᾽ ἀνδρός, “these oracles of the man.” Yet I readily grant that, as for the latter
explanation, it is of such clear truth that once its terms are understood, not even an obstinate person
could deny it. The acumen of this Novarian should therefore be valued so highly that no sober person
would buy it even for a farthing.
