# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 24.* Romans 10:4: *For Christ is the end of the law for
righteousness to everyone who believes.* From this they wish to infer that
the law was abolished after Christ's advent.

*Answer.* 1. Although Christ is truly the end of the ceremonial law in the
sense that he completely removed and abrogated it, he was not the end of the
moral law in this way. This is more than sufficiently clear from the purpose
and circumstances of this passage. For here Paul explains the reason for
what he had said in verse 3: namely, why the Jews, ignorant of God's
righteousness, were not subject to it but sought to establish their own.
They did not understand the law's proper end and purpose, namely Christ. They
thought that the end of the law was the righteousness of works, or that God
had prescribed the law to them for the purpose of their acquiring
righteousness by their own works prescribed in the law. They consequently
attempted to establish their own righteousness and did not flee to Christ,
although the law had been enacted by God so that, convicted of sin and
subject to the curse, they might flee to Christ, through whom alone sins
could be expiated and forgiveness of sins obtained. Thus

[Printed p. 84 | IA PDF 92 | IA leaf 92 | IA decausadei02brow]

Christ was the end—τέλος, or purpose—of the law, though not *in itself.*
For the purpose of the law considered in itself was our justification through
perfect obedience rendered to it, or to justify those who observe it
perfectly. Christ was its purpose *incidentally*, because of our impotence
and sinfulness: we could not observe the law perfectly and, furthermore,
because we had violated it, were liable to divine wrath. Christ was therefore
appointed to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, so that all
believers might be justified.

Accordingly, this law had been enacted and renewed so that the Jews might
betake themselves to him: it was to demand, press, and threaten the curse, so
that they might flee to Christ for righteousness and salvation. Thus Christ
was the law's end *for the sake of which it existed.* Medicine may similarly
be called the end of disease *incidentally*, because disease directs and
drives the sick person to seek medicine. So too, by accusing them of
disobedience and exposing their guilt and impotence, the law showed that it
was impossible for them to be justified by their own works. It thereby
compelled everyone to despair of obtaining life through the righteousness of
works and drove them to seek and expect righteousness, justification, and
salvation in another. In this sense, according to the intention of that
promulgation, the law was a tutor leading to Christ (Galatians 3:23–24), so
that they might be justified by faith.

2. Christ can also be called the law's end *of perfection*, as some note,
because:

1. We are justified in him and obtain salvation, which the law properly
intended.

2. He gives the regenerate the Spirit of sanctification, by whom they are
made fit, in part, to observe the law and walk in God's commandments.

3. He himself kept the law perfectly and imputes this obedience of his to his
own (Romans 5:19; 2 Corinthians 5, final verse).

4. By his death he underwent the penalties imposed by the law, being made a
curse, so that he might redeem us from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13).

This passage therefore does nothing for the Antinomians. For here the
apostle's entire concern is to explain the true manner of a sinner's
justification before God; he says nothing here about sanctification.

*Objection 25.* Christ was a mediator, not a lawgiver. Therefore the law does
not pertain to Christians.

*Answer.* 1. Christ is King and hence the supreme governor and lawgiver of
the church. He is not, however, the sort of lawgiver the Socinians imagine:
one who gives us precepts more perfect than the moral precepts proposed to
God's people in ancient times; or who perfects the law of the Decalogue for
us by adding certain supplements to each commandment of the Decalogue; or who
prescribes precepts to us by observing which we become righteous and
justified, and so obtain salvation through our works.

2. Indeed, as was said before, Christ was that Jehovah who sanctioned and
promulgated the law from Mount Sinai.

3. Nor does this legislation of his conflict with his mediation, for the
former was subordinated to the latter.

4. Much less does it follow that by the power of his mediation believers
were exempted from moral and natural duties.

*Objection 26.* The law does not pertain to those who are not justified and
do not receive the promises through the law. But Christians are not justified
and do not receive the promises through the law (Romans 3:20; 4:13; Galatians
2:16).

*Answer.* The major premise can be distinguished as follows. The law does not
pertain to those who are not justified, and so forth, through the law, so
that they may be justified through obedience rendered to it and receive the
promises:

[Printed p. 85 | IA PDF 93 | IA leaf 93 | IA decausadei02brow]

true. The law does not pertain to them as the standard of perfect
righteousness and rule of conduct and life: false. The conclusion therefore
has no force, for it infers only what is not denied—namely, that the law does
not pertain to us so that we may be justified before God through its perfect
observance.

*Objection 27.* The law increases sin; therefore it is now abolished.

*Answer.* By this argument it can likewise be proved that the law was
abolished, or at least should have been abolished, under the Old Testament.
For whatever increases sin in itself could have had no place then any more
than now.

2. But the fallacy here is from what is incidental. For the fact that sin is
increased by the law does not arise from the law's nature, but from our
depravity and innate corruption.

3. Human corruption abuses the gospel itself to commit sin or to sleep
securely in sin. Yet it does not follow from this that the gospel should be
abrogated.

*Objection 28.* Christians ought to stand fast in the freedom with which
Christ has freed them and, without turning back, not be entangled in the yoke
of slavery (Galatians 6:1).

*Answer.* 1. The cited passage concerns the ceremonial law, as the context
proves, and we certainly ought to stand fast in this freedom.

2. To be without obligation to the moral law is not freedom but license,
which Christ never obtained for his own.
