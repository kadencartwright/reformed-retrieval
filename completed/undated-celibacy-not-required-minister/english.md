# That Celibacy Is Not to Be Required of a Minister in This Way

*A fresh translation of the complete Latin letter at CO X/1 226–229. The CO
title, page notices, and chronological footnote are editorial rather than
words silently attributed to the letter.*

[CO X/1, p. 226]

Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus, brother most
highly esteemed by me in the Lord.

When we have to write to people with whom we have had no acquaintance or
personal connection, we are accustomed at the outset to make a careful apology
and ask pardon, lest we be charged with rashness or intrusiveness in doing so.
Although I am entirely unknown to you by sight, however, I think I have reason
enough to dare address you by letter without any apology of that sort.

Apart from the fact that our Christ, whom we worship and confess in one
Spirit, ought to be a sufficiently firm bond of intimate friendship between
us, N. told me of your exceptional goodwill toward me. I did not doubt that it
was in turn my duty to attest and declare, in whatever way I could, the like
disposition of my heart toward you. What especially gave me confidence was
that he also reported that you take pleasure in my writings and assured me
that this service would not be unwelcome to you if you received a private
letter from me.

I therefore complied, and all the more gladly because I judged myself bound to
you in no ordinary degree on account of that goodwill so favorably disposed
toward me. For the present I had nothing with which to make a return except
this one thing that he requested and that was easy for me to provide. Moreover,
I placed firm trust in his testimony, because, having been educated with you
for one year, he is able to have gained a thorough perception of your whole
disposition in this matter.

Therefore, as I have said, since you have opened for me an approach to you that
is not difficult, I shall not linger any longer over a more extended apology.
First, I want you to be certain of this: I desire nothing more than to have a
closer friendship with you and to confirm it by whatever services are
possible. For this godly brother has praised your gifts to me in such a way
that I readily count you among those to whom all who

[CO X/1, p. 227]

wish to belong to Christ owe a great deal. The fewer good people there are
today, the more highly goodness itself ought to be valued by us wherever it
appears. As I hear, you furnish no slight proof of prudence, sincerity, and
holiness in this: although with immense difficulty you sustain there an office
full of danger and labor, you conduct yourself so as to satisfy fully everyone
who is godly and discerning. Therefore, all to whom the edification of the
church is dear ought rightly to admire these virtues of yours and to embrace
and honor you in the Lord.

As for me, I wish I could show by some more substantial proof how much I love
you and how highly I esteem you. As the one thing left to me, I promise you
that my prayers to God will bear witness to the love that you cannot experience
through human services from me.

Meanwhile, I sincerely congratulate your churches, upon which the Lord has
bestowed so many excellent blessings in addition to purer doctrine. It is no
small thing that they have pastors by whom they are governed and ordered; that
their morals are so well regulated; that they are established in so excellent
a form; and that they are furnished with the best discipline, which we can
rightly call the best and indeed the sole bond for maintaining obedience. To
our great distress, we experience its value precisely while we lack it and
cannot attain it by any means. This causes me often to falter in spirit and to
perform my office less vigorously. Indeed, I would despair entirely if it did
not come to mind that the edification of the church is always the Lord's work,
which he will prosper by his own power even if every aid fails us. It is
nevertheless a great and rare blessing to be assisted by so necessary a
support. I shall therefore judge our own churches properly supported only when
they have been bound together by that sinew.

As for your confession which was recently published, I have only inspected
it. I have not yet had an opportunity to read through it. It nevertheless has
the endorsement of men whose judgment I readily accept. Yet I would wish you
to be somewhat more sparing in your commendation of celibacy. Forgive my
plainness in this matter, for I do not hesitate to explain freely to you what
does not entirely please me.

I see that your position is not wholly without a rationale. Those bound up in
marriage are less free for the Lord's work, so that it is very advantageous
for those who wish to consecrate themselves wholly to the Lord to be released
from this hindrance. Then, continence itself commends no slight dignity to the
sacred ministry. Finally, you do not drive those who hold ecclesiastical
office into celibacy by force or tyranny. You simply counsel and advise it
because you judge it beneficial to the church.

Yet although I admit that marriage brings many varied impediments with it,
from which one could wish Christ's servants to be free, I will not concede
that they are of such a kind as to call them away from their duty. In turn, I
shall object that celibacy too has its own

[CO X/1, p. 228]

disadvantages, and those neither slight nor of a single kind. I am not yet
speaking about the difficulty of continence. I say that the unmarried are
distracted by diversions no less numerous or serious than those who are
married, or at least that there is little enough difference for both to be
distracted equally.

Even if it were established that nothing is freer than celibacy and nothing
more impeded than marriage, this still ought not to prevent provision being
made for necessity. It is certain that many who are more suitable for the
ministry than others cannot beneficially do without marriage.

To the second point I answer that the Lord has provided better than anyone
what gifts should adorn his ministry. Yet we see that he is very far from
having commended celibacy among them (1 Timothy 3:2; Titus 1:6). I approve the
fact that you do not impose it by a fixed law. But take care lest this itself
amount to prescribing a law: esteeming those who have married less because
they seem to have lost some distinction.

Nor was there a coercive law in the ancient church. But a misplaced admiration
for celibacy became so strong that marriage in bishops was marked with
disgrace. Afterward, the severity of law gradually crept in, and it has
produced innumerable forms of evil for us. I cannot judge what good it has
brought. I therefore always fear that it is dangerous for excessive honor to
be paid to celibacy, by which good men may be deterred from marriage even when
necessity presses.

It therefore seems to me that I have reason to desire that any austerity among
you in this matter be mitigated—not because it harms you at present, but
because it may greatly harm later generations, whose interests, as you know,
must be considered. But if your circumstances are presently such that some
danger from pastors marrying threatens you, I would wish a plan to be adopted
by which it may be met.

I am compelled again to ask pardon for my bluntness. I could not restrain
myself from briefly indicating my fear in this matter—perhaps a needless fear,
but nevertheless not conceived out of nothing. And so that you may understand
that I am not pleading a private cause here: although you see me as so hostile
to celibacy, I have never married, and I do not know whether I shall ever do
so. If I marry, I shall do so with this purpose: that, freer from many
entanglements, I may devote myself to the Lord. In taking that course, I do
not seize upon my own incontinence as a pretext, since no one can charge me
with it. I merely wished to give you an occasion for reflection. For I do not
doubt that if you once apply your mind seriously to this question, in
accordance with your exceptional prudence and long experience, you will
provide for what is most beneficial and do what appears best to be done.

As for the office over which you preside, I trust that you administer it in
such a way that you need no one's exhortation. I would rather be admonished by
you if an occasion were ever given to you and so much leisure

[CO X/1, p. 229]

were available. Meanwhile, I implore you to devote yourself to preserving
unity, as I know you do of your own accord. The times themselves teach us well
enough how necessary it is to act faithfully in concert in the Lord, so that,
our minds thus joined, we may fight against Satan and all his ranks.

May the Lord Jesus preserve you, most upright brother, revered by me in the
Lord. Please greet in my name those whom you hope will receive the greeting
favorably.

## Translator's notes

1. CO does not identify the recipient, the intermediary `N.`, the recently
   published confession, or the recipient's churches. The translation does
   not supply names from conjecture.
2. The opening *necessitudo* denotes an established relationship. Later
   *necessitas* denotes circumstances in which marriage may be necessary for a
   minister.
3. Calvin praises continence and grants that marriage brings hindrances. His
   objection is to treating celibacy as a distinguishing ministerial ornament
   in a way that becomes an indirect law and harms the supply of suitable
   pastors.
4. The comment that this must be an early letter is CO's footnote, not part of
   Calvin's closing. It is excluded from the translated body.
5. “Act ... in concert” renders *conspirare* without the predominantly
   negative sense of modern “conspire.” The following *militemus* continues
   the image with united service against Satan's ranks.
6. The English was translated afresh from `latin.txt` and checked against the
   facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not consulted or used for
   wording.
