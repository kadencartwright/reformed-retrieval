QUESTION SIX

In what sense is the church called catholic?

I. Although all agree that the church is rightly called catholic, they do not
equally agree about the sense of this word. For the papists circumscribe this
universality

[1847 vol. III printed p. 27 | IA leaf 61 | PDF p. 61]

within the boundaries of the Roman church, so that it extends only to those
who in various nations and places are subject to the Roman pontiff, with the
result that *Roman* and *catholic* are the same thing, as Bellarmine says. But
it can easily be shown that the meaning of this designation is different.

[Marginalia: 1. The name *catholic* properly denotes the universal church,
both triumphant and militant.]

II. First, the proper meaning of the word itself teaches that a body confined
to certain places cannot claim for itself the name of the catholic church.
Rather, only that society is catholic which embraces all the elect and
faithful—both those triumphant in heaven and those militant on earth—wherever
they have been or will be, and in whatever age they have lived from the
beginning of the world or will live until the end. In this sense “the whole
family of God” is said to be “named in heaven and on earth” (Ephesians 3:15),
and so in the Creed the church is properly called catholic.

Thus Augustine explains on Psalm 42: “His whole church, which is spread
everywhere, is his body, of which he himself is also the head. This includes
not only the faithful who now exist, but also those who existed before us and
those who will exist after us until the end of the age; all belong to his
body,” and so forth. And on Psalm 90: “The church is the body of this head—not
only the church in this place, but the church both in this place and throughout
the entire world; not only the church at this time, but the whole people of the
saints, from Abel himself to those who will be born even to the end and will
believe in Christ, belonging to one city, which city is Christ’s body.” Gregory
says in Homily 19 on the Gospels: “Our Creator has a universal vineyard,
namely, the church. From righteous Abel to the last elect person who will be
born, however many saints it has produced, it has sent out, as it were, that
many branches.”

III. That this conception is intended in the Creed is evident, first, from the
genuine meaning of the word. Understood in its full breadth, it must embrace
the universal church and extend to all its parts: the part that reigns in
heaven as well as the part that fights on earth; the part that has already
existed from the beginning of the world and now exists as well as the part
that will exist until the consummation of the age. These three parts constitute
the one church that is called catholic, without limitation of time or place,
in contrast to particular churches. Those churches are members of that great
body which Christ gathers for himself out of the world.

Second, the phrase concerning the communion of saints that is added
immediately confirms the same thing. For the saints are not only those who
live on earth but also those who reign in heaven and those who will be born
until the end; they ought to be joined to one another by the holy bond of
communion. Third, whenever Scripture speaks of the church under the idea of
universality, it means the whole church and not only one part (Ephesians
1:22–23; 5:25–26; Colossians 1:18; Hebrews 12:23; and frequently elsewhere).

IV. Although in ordinary usage the word *church* denotes an outward and
visible assembly, it does not therefore follow, when speaking precisely about
Christ’s church, that the proper and natural meaning of the word implies
simply a visible assembly or a merely outward profession. Spiritual and
inward communion constitutes its essence, as was seen before. Therefore, when
the universal church is mentioned absolutely and without qualification, no
meaning can be offered as more proper and natural than that it designates the
universal society of God’s children in contrast to the society of worldly
people and the children of this age.

[Marginalia: 2. The New Testament church in contrast to the Old Testament
church.]

V. Second, the catholic church is taken more strictly for the church of the
New Testament, which is spread throughout the whole world, in contrast to the
church of the Old Testament, which was enclosed within the narrow boundaries
of Judea. This is so with respect to places, persons, and times. 1. With
respect to places, because it is dispersed throughout the entire world. The
faithful are no longer bound to a particular place to worship God, as they
once were, but can worship God anywhere in spirit and truth

[1847 vol. III printed p. 28 | IA leaf 62 | PDF p. 62]

(John 4:21, 23; 1 Timothy 2:8). 2. With respect to persons, because people are
chosen without discrimination from every human race, rank, and station. For
there is no distinction of Jew or Greek, slave or free, male or female (Romans
10:12); rather, in every nation anyone who fears him is acceptable to him
(Acts 10:35). “In Christ there is neither male nor female, neither slave nor
free, neither Scythian nor barbarian, but Christ is all in all” (Colossians
3:11). Hence the elders give thanks to the Lamb because he redeemed them “from
every tribe, language, nation, and people” (Revelation 5:9).

Finally, with respect to times, because in this world it must endure until the
consummation of the age and Christ’s final advent. Christ therefore wills the
memory of his death to be celebrated until he comes (1 Corinthians 11:26). In
this sense the Fathers very frequently speak of the catholic church. “The
church is called catholic,” says Optatus in book 2 against Parmenian, “because
it is spread everywhere.” Augustine asserts the same in Letters 152 and 170.
Thus the catholic church is set in contrast to any particular churches, which
are confined within specific places and times.

[Marginalia: 3. By reason of catholic, orthodox doctrine.]

VI. Third, the church is not infrequently called catholic by the Fathers by
reason of doctrine, because it holds and defends catholic, orthodox doctrine;
“because it truly holds the whole [doctrine],” as Augustine says in Letter 48,
contrary to what heretics and innovators did. Vincent of Lérins explains this
clearly in chapter 3 of *Against Heresies*: “In the catholic church itself
great care must be taken that we hold what has been believed everywhere,
always, and by all. For this is truly and properly catholic, as the force and
meaning of the name itself declare, comprehending all things truly and
universally.”

In this sense there can be many catholic churches; indeed, any particular
orthodox churches are catholic, as they were often called. Their bishops were
called “bishops of the catholic church” so that they might be distinguished by
this title from assemblies of heretics and schismatics. The Theodosian law in
Sozomen, book 7, chapter 4, teaches this by decreeing: “Only the church of
those who worshiped the divine Trinity with equal honor was to be called
catholic; those who thought differently were to be called heretics and marked
with disgrace.” Augustine likewise speaks in Letter 152 of “not only the
overseas catholic church, but also the African catholic church,” and in Letter
166 he calls the emperors “catholic,” that is, orthodox and sound in the faith.

The Roman synod under Hilary, bishop of Rome, begins in this way: “Hilary,
bishop of the catholic church of the city of Rome.” It is clear here that
Hilary does not call himself bishop of the universal church, since he confines
his episcopate to the city of Rome.

VII. Whatever may be the rationale for this designation, it certainly cannot
belong to the Roman church, since it neither holds the catholic faith nor is
spread everywhere. Innumerable churches, both in the East and in the West,
are separated from it and have nothing in common with it.

VIII. Although Jerusalem could be the center of the Jewish church, because
that church was for the most part enclosed within the boundaries of Judea and
was bound to a particular place, Rome cannot likewise be the center of the
Christian catholic church. For this church is no longer specially attached to
any place, but can worship God anywhere in Spirit and truth (John 4:24).
Therefore Christ did not substitute for Jerusalem any fixed seat and visible
assembly, but rather heavenly and mystical Jerusalem, independent of every
visible assembly and seat, joined together by the bond of the Spirit alone and
by communion in a faith of equal honor. Neither distance between places,
intervening seas, nor any other interval can hinder this union.

IX. From the fact that heretics everywhere customarily claim the name of the
catholic church for themselves falsely, it is rightly inferred that the name
*catholic* cannot be a mark of the true church, as will be shown in its proper
place against the papists, because the title often exists without the reality.
Yet it does not therefore follow that catholicity is not a proper attribute of
the true church properly so called.
