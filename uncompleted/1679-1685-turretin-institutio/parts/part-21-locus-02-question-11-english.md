QUESTION XI

ON THE AUTHENTIC EDITION

Are the Hebrew edition of the Old Testament and the Greek edition of the New
Testament alone authentic? We affirm against the Roman Catholics.

I. Editions of Scripture are of one kind when they are prototypes
(πρωτότυποι) or archetypes (ἀρχέτυποι), the original and primary editions
which the authors of the books first used. Others are copies (ἔκτυποι), or
secondary editions, namely, versions by which Scripture was carried over into
other languages. No one denies that the Hebrew edition of the Old Testament
and the Greek edition of the New Testament are original and primordial. But

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 102 | IA leaf 156 | PDF p. 156]

whether each is authentic, deserving credence and authority of itself, and
whether all versions must be tested by it—this is disputed between us and the
Roman Catholics.

[Marginal heading: The opinion of the Roman Catholics.]

II. Although some Roman Catholics are more equitable toward the original
texts, asserting their purity, as was seen above, and ascribing authenticity
to them on account of that purity, so that all versions, including the
Vulgate, ought to be recalled to them and can be corrected from them—such as
Sixtus of Siena, book 8, heresy 2; Driedo, book 2; Andradius, book 2 of the
*Defense of the Council of Trent*; and others—nevertheless, most are more
unfair toward them. These say that the Hebrew text possesses no certainty,
that in disputations about faith no appeal should be made to the original
texts, and that the Vulgate Version should not be corrected from them. Such
are Stapleton, *On the Doctrinal Principles of Faith*, controversy 5,
question 3, article 1; Cano, book 2, chapters 11 and 13; Lindanus; and others
who maintain the corruption of the original texts. This opinion, moreover,
was drawn from the decree of the Council of Trent, session 4, which ordained
that the Latin Vulgate Version should be held as authentic in public readings,
disputations, sermons, and expositions, so that no one should dare to reject
it under any pretext. It is true that not a few Roman Catholics who are
ashamed of this decree attempt to fabricate a different meaning for it, as if
the council had decreed nothing against the authenticity of the original
texts and had not preferred the Vulgate Version to them, but had only selected
one from the Latin versions now in circulation to set above the others. So
says Bellarmine, *On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 10, and this is the
opinion of Andradius, Salmeron, Serarius, and others. Nevertheless, D. Bannes
rightly declares this to be a distortion of the synodical decree, and it is
easily gathered from the very words of the decree. For if the Vulgate must be
held as authentic so that no one dares to reject it under any pretext, is it
not made equal to the original texts and even preferred to them? If it departs
from those texts, it will not be tested by them, but they instead by it. Hence
Mariana, *On the Vulgate Version*, page 99, complains that after the
promulgation of the Council of Trent, the Greek and Hebrew fell at one stroke.
Our opinion, however, is that the Hebrew edition of the Old Testament and the
Greek edition of the New have always been and still are alone authentic, and
that all controversies concerning faith and religion and all versions must be
tested and examined by them.

[Marginal heading: What is an authentic writing?]

III. An authentic writing is one which abundantly possesses everything
required to establish credibility and to which the fullest credence of its
kind is due. It is certainly established that such a writing both proceeded
from the author whose name it bears and contains everything written in the
manner in which he himself wished it to be written. A writing, however, can be
authentic in two ways: either primarily and originally, or secondarily and
derivatively. A writing is primarily authentic when it is self-credible
(αὐτόπιστον), when its credibility is established through itself, and when it
is plainly believed and ought to be believed on its own account. In this way
the archetypal tablets of royal edicts, the decrees of magistrates, wills,
contracts, and every autograph of an author are authentic. Secondarily
authentic are all copies accurately and faithfully transcribed from archetypes
by suitable persons. For princely edicts and other public documents, only
notaries appointed to this task by public authority are such persons; but for
books and other writings, any upright and diligent scribes and copyists are
such persons. In the first sense, only the autographs of Moses, the prophets,
and the apostles are authentic. But in the latter sense, faithful and accurate
copies are also authentic.

IV. Again, the authority of every authentic writing is twofold. One kind is
founded upon the very matters which it treats and regards the people to whom
the writing is directed. The other concerns the language itself and the
written text, and relates to cognate copies and the translations made from it.
Over all these it possesses such a right that they must be examined by the
authentic writing,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 103 | IA leaf 157 | PDF p. 157]

and, wherever they vary from it, must be corrected and amended. The former
authority can be either greater or less according to the authority of the one
from whom the writing in question proceeded and according to whether he
possesses greater or less dominion over the people to whom he directs his
writing. In the Sacred Scriptures, however, this authority is by far the
greatest, of a kind which can exist in no other writing. Because of God's
supreme dominion over human beings, as over all other things, and because of
the supreme truth and wisdom which are preeminent in him, we must believe him
unconditionally and obey in everything that his most sacred Word, contained
in authentic Scripture, sets forth either to be believed or to be done. The
latter authority, however, consists in this: the autographs, as well as
accurate and faithful copies, are the standard of all other copies of the same
writing and also of its translations. If anything in these is found to
disagree with authentic writings, whether autographs or copies, it is unworthy
of the name *authentic* and must be rejected as spurious and adulterated. No
other principle is needed to exercise this judgment beyond the disagreement
itself. The former authority was treated above in Question IV, when we
disputed about the divinity of Scripture. We now dispute about the latter,
which is found in an authentic edition.

V. Finally, authenticity is considered in two ways: materially, with respect
to the things asserted, or formally, with respect to the words and manner of
asserting them. The question here does not concern authenticity in the former
sense, for we do not deny this to versions when they agree with the original
texts. It concerns only the latter, which belongs to the original texts alone.

[Marginal heading: The Hebrew edition of the Old Testament and the Greek
edition of the New are alone authentic.]

VI. The reasons are: 1. The original texts alone are “God-inspired”
(θεόπνευστοι), with respect both to the things and to the words, 1 Timothy
3:16, and therefore they alone can be authentic. For if the men of God wrote
anything, they did it while moved by the Holy Spirit, 2 Peter 1:21, who, to
keep them from error, dictated not only the things but also the very words.
This cannot be said of any version. 2. They are the norm and rule by which all
versions must be examined, just as a copy (ἔκτυπον) must be referred to its
archetype (ἀρχέτυπον), and a stream distinguished from its fountain. To this
belongs Gratian's canon from Augustine, distinction 19: “As the credibility of
the books of the Old Testament must be examined by the Hebrew volumes, so the
truth of those of the New requires the standard of the Greek language.” Jerome
deals extensively with this matter so as to defend the authority of the
Hebrew text as the fountain to which all the streams of the versions must be
referred and corrected. See letter 102 to Marcella, the letter to Vitalis, and
the letter to Sunnia and Fretela. 3. These editions were authentic from their
very origin and were always held as such by the Jewish and Christian church
for many centuries after Christ. No reason can be given why they should now
have ceased to be authentic. For the reason sought from corruption, besides
assuming what is in question, was refuted by us above.

VII. 4. If the Hebrew edition of the Old Testament and the Greek edition of
the New Testament are not authentic, no edition will be authentic, for none
besides these has had divine testimony to its own authenticity (αὐθεντίας).
Thus there would be no authentic Word of God in the church and no end to
disputes, because there would be no certain rule of faith and morals in which
complete rest must be found. Scripture, like a wax nose or a Lesbian rule,
could be bent in either direction at will. 5. The adversaries themselves admit
that in certain cases it is permissible to have recourse to the original
texts. Bellarmine, *On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 11, lists these: 1.
When an error of copyists seems to occur in the Latin codices. 2. When they
have variant readings, so that which one is true cannot be established with
certainty. 3. When they contain something ambiguous, whether in words or in
things. 4. When the force (ἐνέργεια) and proper meaning of words do not
appear to be sufficiently expressed. This certainly could not be done unless
the original texts were authentic. Arias Montanus, in the preface to the
Bible, demonstrates in various ways that errors in

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 104 | IA leaf 158 | PDF p. 158]

versions cannot be corrected except from the truth of the primary language.
Vives, on book 14, chapter 8 of Augustine's *City of God*, sets it down as
certain and indubitable that recourse must be had to the original texts.
Salmeron, Bonfrerius, Masius, Muisius, Jansenius with his school, and others
who repeatedly appeal to the original texts hold the same view.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. The variant readings which occur in Scripture do not overthrow its
authenticity, because they are easily detected and judged, partly from the
coherence of the text and partly from comparison with the better codices.
Some are even of such a kind that, though different, they nevertheless fit
the same text well enough.

IX. Although various disputes arise from the Hebrew and Greek original texts,
it does not follow that they cannot be authentic. Otherwise there would be no
authentic edition of the Bible at all to which recourse might be had, for
there is no language which does not furnish contentious people abundant
material for wrangling. Furthermore, this happens not by a fault in the
original texts, but through those who misuse them, either by failing to
understand or by twisting the words to their own opinions and stubbornly
adhering to them.

X. The Hebrew edition of the Old Testament and the Greek edition of the New
are falsely called mutilated. Nor can the passages which the adversaries
bring forward prove this. The account of the adulteress in John 8 does not
prove it, for although it is absent from the Syriac Version, it is found in
all the Greek codices. Nor does the statement in 1 John 5:7 prove it. Although
some formerly called it into doubt, and heretics call it into doubt today,
all the Greek copies nevertheless contain it, as Sixtus of Siena acknowledges:
“the words were always of unquestioned truth and were read in all the Greek
copies from the very times of the apostles.” Nor does Mark 16 prove it. It
could have been lacking in various copies in Jerome's time, as he himself
admits, but it is now found in them all and also in the Syriac Version, and it
is plainly necessary to complete the history of Christ's resurrection.

XI. In vain do the adversaries, in order to undermine the authenticity
(αὐθεντίαν) of the Hebrew edition, take refuge in the novelty of the vowel
points, as if pointing were only a human invention devised by the Masoretes,
and therefore rested on human authority rather than divine and infallible
authority, could be changed at will without danger, and thus always left the
meaning of the text uncertain and ambiguous. Several replies can be made. 1.
Bellarmine will answer here for us, *On the Word of God*, book 2, chapter 2:
“The errors which have occurred from the addition of the points do no harm to
the truth, because the points were added externally and do not change the
text.” 2. Once this hypothesis is posited, the certainty not only of the
original text but also of the Vulgate Version, which was drawn from that
fountain, collapses. This is so unless it is proved that the first author of
that version, whether Jerome or someone else, immediately received from the
Holy Spirit the necessary revelation of the vowels, which otherwise he
certainly received from the tradition of the Jews. If that tradition was
uncertain, all the authority of the sacred text will totter.

XII. 3. Even if the points had been newly added, as those contend who trace
their origin to the Tiberian Masoretes, it would not follow that the pointing
was a merely human invention dependent only upon human choice. If that were
indeed maintained, it would undermine the authenticity of the Hebrew codex
not a little. For, at least in the judgment of most who hold this opinion,
pointing is not supposed to have been made at the pleasure of the rabbis, but
according to the analogy of Scripture, the genius of the sacred language, and
the meaning received among the Jews by long use. For although, according to
that hypothesis, the points did not exist from the beginning with respect to
their shape, it cannot be denied that they always existed with respect to
their sound and value, or power. Otherwise, since vowels are the souls of
consonants, the meaning of words would always be ambiguous—indeed, no meaning
at all would arise—unless the vowels were coeval with the consonants. Prideaux

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 105 | IA leaf 159 | PDF p. 159]

observes this well in lecture 12, *On the Antiquity of the Points*: “As to
sound and value, no one will have denied that the points and accents existed
from the beginning, but only as to signs and characters.” And a little later:
“Vowels were coeval with consonants in their underlying force and sounds,
although the marks and shapes now under discussion had not yet seen the light
at that time.” Indeed, it can scarcely be doubted that the vowels, if they were
not represented in the same shape in which they are now written, were
nevertheless marked by certain small signs in place of points, for example by
the letters אוי, as some judge. These are therefore called *mothers of
reading*, so that the meaning of the Holy Spirit could be held certain and
constant. Otherwise, since it depended upon mere tradition and human
institution and memory, it could easily have been delivered over to oblivion
and adulterated. This is the opinion of the most learned Walton, prolegomenon
7 to the Polyglot Bible: “By use,” he says, “and the tradition of the ancestors,
with the help of the three letters אוי, which are called *mothers of reading*
and which before the invention of the points stood in place of vowels, the
true reading and pronunciation were preserved.”

XIII. 4. The adversaries gratuitously assume what had to be proved: that the
points are a recent and human invention. Until now the Jews have asserted the
contrary with great agreement, apart from the one Elias Levita, who lived a
hundred years ago. Many very celebrated men, grammarians as well as
theologians, Protestants and Roman Catholics, follow them here: Junius,
Illyricus, Reuchlin, Munster, Cevalerius, Pagninus, M. Marinus, Polanus,
Deodatus, Broughton, Muisius, Tailor, Bootius, Lightfoot, the greatest part of
recent theologians, and the father and son Buxtorf, who seem to have carried
every point here—the former in his *Tiberias*, the latter in the very solid
work which he opposed to *The Mystery of Pointing Revealed*. It would not be
difficult to confirm this opinion with various arguments if we were now
treating it. But because this question seems more grammatical than
theological, we do not wish to make it our quarrel. It suffices to note in a
word that the opinion which maintains that the points are of divine origin,
whether they are referred to Moses or to Ezra, the leader of the Great
Synagogue, has always seemed truer and safer to us for preserving the
authenticity (αὐθεντίαν) of the original text sound and intact against every
cavil of the profane and heretics, and for establishing a certain and
immovable principle of faith. Thus the adversaries wrongly seek to attack the
authority of the Hebrew codex from the novelty of the points.
