ROMANS 7:17.

Thus it is no longer I who do it, but the sin which dwells in me.

Hence the apostle concludes from the struggle between flesh and spirit that he
does not sin with the full consent and endeavor of the will. When he says, “It
is not I who do it,” he means, “not I insofar as I am regenerate, and insofar
as the Holy Spirit dwells in my will, mind, and affections.” The Spirit makes
the acts of sin diminished and less intense. Hence

It is asked whether, according to our theologians, all the sins of the
regenerate are merely sins of infirmity. The Remonstrants affirm this in
*Scriptum Synodale*, article 5, p. 211.

We answer by making a distinction. A sin of infirmity is understood in two
ways. First, as it is opposed to a sin committed under the dominion of reigning
and prevailing concupiscence. In this sense our theologians—Calvin, Beza, and
Zanchi, as they are cited in the *Collatio Hagiensis*, article 5, p. 31—rightly
teach that all the sins of the regenerate are sins of infirmity, because they
always sin with resistance made by the Spirit.

Second, those sins are called sins of infirmity which arise from daily
incursion, such as natural lapses and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 607 | PDF p. 656 | split word joined: labes]

stains adhering to our best works. In this sense it is false that our
theologians teach that all the sins of the regenerate are sins of infirmity.

1. David's adultery, Solomon's idolatry, and the injustice and violence of Asa
when he imprisoned God's prophet were atrocious sins.

2. The saints sometimes indulge carnal security, not without grave contempt
for Christ's calling, Song of Songs 5:2–4.

3. For their greater humility God sometimes abandons the regenerate so that,
when they have fallen into graver sins, they may perceive how insignificant
they are when abandoned by God and what thanks they owe God, through Christ
the Mediator, for strengthening grace.

It is asked whether the Remonstrants rightly condemn us because we teach that
the truly regenerate cannot sin by deliberate counsel, with the whole endeavor
of the will, full consent, and devoted malice. They condemn us on this charge
in *Scriptum Synodale*, article 5, p. 211.

We answer that sin is committed by deliberate counsel in two ways.

1. Sin is committed by deliberate counsel when it is perpetrated from
knowledge and deliberate choice, after the reasons on both sides have been
compared, the advantages and disadvantages that will follow from the sin have
been weighed, and the greatness and infinity of the divine Majesty and the
wrath of the offended Deity have also been considered. In this sense our
theologians have never denied that the regenerate sin all too frequently by
deliberate counsel.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 608 | PDF p. 657 | split word joined: Peccatur]

2. Sin is committed by deliberate counsel when a person sins with a full,
most thoroughly composed, and most freely deliberated act, with the whole
effort, endeavor, and impulse of the will, and without any act of the Spirit of
grace resisting, as it were protesting to the contrary, and struggling against
it. This counter-resistance is either habitual or the actual uprising of the
Spirit of grace against the flesh which drags a person captive into sin. We
teach that the truly regenerate never sin in this manner.

1. Sin does not reign or exercise dominion in them, Romans 6:4–6, 11, 14. The
dominion of sin, however, lies in the will's full consent to sin “with consent
in avarice,” Ephesians 4:19.

2. The regenerate are not slaves of sin or enslaved to the lust of desires,
since their old man has been crucified, Romans 6:16–21; Romans 8:9–11;
Ephesians 5:5; Colossians 3:1–3.

3. In the regenerate the Spirit and the law of the mind—that is, the soul,
mind, conscience, will, and affections insofar as they are renewed—oppose the
flesh in all their sins. Hence consent to sin is diminished, and the intense
effort and endeavor of the will is slackened and relaxed. That effort was once
most intense while the person lived in the flesh and its concupiscences. Thus
the regenerate do not sin with the whole heart, but with their renewed part
resisting, as is clear from Romans 7:17, 23. There a captive obeys the master
or tyrant who detains him with a half-willing will,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 609 | PDF p. 658 | split word joined: voluntate]

that is, with consent of the will which is by no means full. So also the
regenerate person sins with half-full consent of the will. In Galatians 5:17,
the Spirit and the flesh in every regenerate person are like two men or two
commanders confronting and contending with one another.

4. In 1 John 3:9 the regenerate person “cannot sin”—that is, with full consent,
from devoted malice and an evil habit; otherwise there is no one who cannot
sin, 1 John 1:8. It is explained in the same place, “he cannot commit sin,
because his seed remains in him”; and the anointing which teaches all things
remains in him, 1 John 2:27. He has received the Spirit of adoption, Romans
8:15. He has received the Spirit who is from God, 1 Corinthians 2:12. The
Father and the Son make their dwelling with him, John 14:23. Christ dwells in
his heart through faith, Ephesians 3:17. He has the Spirit of God, Romans 8:9.
He has in himself a spring of living water welling up to eternal life, John
4:14—that is, the Spirit of God, John 7:38–39. It is therefore plain that
habitual grace remains in him, namely the Spirit of God formerly promised in
Isaiah 44:3–4; 54:11–12; Ezekiel 36:26–27; Zechariah 12:10; Joel 2:28–29; and
John 6:45. Through this grace it is impossible for the whole heart and the
whole will to exert full consent and its complete, entire, and most intense
powers upon sin. It exerts only powers blunted, diminished, and subdued by
God's grace. So an agent whose powers have been broken, weakened, and subdued
produces actions proportioned to those subdued powers, not the most intense
actions—just as

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 610 | PDF p. 659 | split word joined: ignis]

fire warms a cold hand in one way and lukewarm water in another. Hence, because
of that resistance of the indwelling Holy Spirit, the sin of the regenerate is
lesser and lighter. From this

It is asked whether the Spirit's resistance and struggle against the flesh in
every sin of a truly regenerate person diminishes the sin and makes it lighter,
so that, because of that resistance, the sin is not committed with the full
consent of the will. The Remonstrants deny this in article 5, pp. 255–256. They
say that this resistance aggravates rather than diminishes the sins of the
regenerate. Indeed, they say it reveals how vehement the will's consent to sin
is, since not even a protesting conscience could prevent the will from giving
that consent. Resistance therefore increases and promotes the fullness of
consent rather than diminishing and retarding it.

Here, however, I think we must distinguish between a sin graver simply and a
sin graver in a certain respect and because of a circumstance—that is, with
respect to outward scandal and with respect to the sin in itself. Second, we
must distinguish a sin graver with respect to guilt from one graver with
respect to the intensity and measure of the action. Third, we must distinguish
a resistance with which the sinner joins and contributes his own influence
from a resistance which the sinner himself resists and opposes with his own
powers. Fourth, we must distinguish sin with respect to opposition from sin
with respect to dominion. Hence

Assertion 1. Sin is considered either, first, with respect to the subject in
which it exists; second, with respect to the free principle from which it
comes; or third, with respect to the circumstance around which it is occupied.
In the first way, with respect to scandal, the adultery

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 611 | PDF p. 660 | split word joined: adulterium]

and murder of David the prophet were graver and procured fouler blasphemies
against God's name among the Philistines and profane people than Absalom's
incest and the parricides of Adrammelech and Sharezer, who killed their father
Sennacherib. Second, if we speak of sin within the category of obligation
arising from the bond of God's benefits, one who knows the Lord's will sins
more gravely and is punished more severely than one who is ignorant. In this
way, within the category of sin against the obligation of beneficence, the sin
of a regenerate person is graver: he sins, first, against Christ's death;
second, against the grace of regeneration; and third, against the benefits of
saving illumination. The unregenerate person is not bound by so many benefits.
But if we speak of sin within the category of sin simply, insofar as it injures
and violates the authority of the Lawgiver, then the unregenerate person sins
with a more uplifted hand, a more secure conscience, and a lighter touch and
sense of the divine Majesty, and therefore with a more intense effort of the
free will.

Assertion 2. With respect to the free principle from which sin flows, the
unregenerate person sins more gravely, simply speaking, than the regenerate
person. The less voluntariness and the less vicious and culpable inclination
there is in sin, the less lawlessness and malice there is in it. In a
regenerate sinner there is less voluntariness and less vicious inclination to
sin than in an unregenerate sinner. For in the regenerate person there is, as
it were, half of the will—that is, the will insofar as renewed and affected by
the holy habit of grace—which inclines

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 612 | PDF p. 661]

not to sin. Thus it removes the greatest part of consent from the sin to which
the will consents insofar as it is affected by the indwelling flesh.

The Remonstrants' argument is therefore easily answered: “To sin while the
conscience protests and the spirit resists to the contrary is graver than to
sin simply.” But the sin would be far graver and the act of consenting far
more intense if the spirit which resisted were not present, as it is not
present in the unregenerate. In the unregenerate person the whole will, with
the totality of the powers that concur in sinning, supplies consent without
holy resistance to the contrary. Hence a culpable act arising from a
conscience seared with a hot iron and afflicted by insensibility is a far
graver sin.

Assertion 3. With respect to circumstance and in a certain respect, the sin of
the regenerate person is graver than that of the unregenerate. The regenerate
person sins against a greater illumination of conscience; to that extent it is
indeed graver, as they prove.

Assertion 4. With respect to guilt, the sin of the unregenerate is graver than
that of the regenerate, because, first, sinners are outside Christ; second, the
whole of such a person is flesh; and third, they sin with their whole impulse.
Yet with respect to the matter and measure of the action, the sin of a
regenerate person can sometimes be graver than the sin of an unregenerate
person. Thus David sinned more gravely in murder than Absalom did in a dutiful
lie, because the matter of the latter

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 613 | PDF p. 662]

is lighter and the sin less serious. Yet an unregenerate person, as a bondman
and slave, can serve a lighter sin as a reigning master, while a regenerate
person does not serve a far graver sin—such as adultery or murder—as a master,
but treats it as a servant subdued and conquered by God's grace.

Assertion 5. The resistance of conscience sometimes diminishes sin; this
always occurs in the regenerate. Resistance sometimes aggravates sin; this
always occurs in the unregenerate. Resistance diminishes sin when we seriously
subscribe to the holy resistance and contribute our own powers and consent to
it. This happens, first, when we stand on the side of conscience and of the
spirit resisting the flesh; second, when we grieve that the spirit has been
defeated and the flesh is victorious; and third, when the greater intensity of
the will inclines to the side of the spirit even though we cannot accomplish
the deed, Romans 7:18—that is, even if the omission of good or the actual
victory of concupiscence follows. A captive has a greater intensity of will to
walk freely than he has strength to free himself from his chains. Thus our
theologians rightly say that resistance diminishes sin. But when we oppose the
resistance of conscience and grieve that conscience and the spirit resist,
wishing that this part would acquiesce and not rise against the flesh, then
resistance aggravates sin. This occurs in the unregenerate whenever they sin
while natural reason and conscience protest, as happened in

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 614 | PDF p. 663]

Pilate when he condemned Christ against his conscience. In spiritual sins,
however—such as unbelief and spiritual security—the natural conscience does
not oppose the flesh. Yet opposition accompanied by grief that the spirit
resists but is nevertheless defeated by the flesh is not found in a truly
regenerate person. Resistance also increases and intensifies guilt rather than
diminishing it when this grief is not godly resistance but arises from the
merely natural conscience and is joined with blazing hatred of God. Thus James
2:19 says, “The demons believe and shudder.” The will of the demons resists the
God in whom they believe and pursues him with hatred; it would prefer God not
to exist and the act of believing not to exist. Herod's will resists the pang
of conscience which hinders him from killing John the Baptist and would prefer
that resistance not to exist. But the regenerate person always assents to the
Spirit's resistance.

Assertion 6. With respect to the impulse of the one who opposes, the sin of
the unregenerate is far graver than that of the regenerate, because the
unregenerate person applies more strength to sinning than the regenerate
person. It is also graver with respect to dominion, because sin exercises less
dominion and the evil inclination is weaker in the regenerate than in the
unregenerate. Yet the opposition of sin, with respect to more objects and
extensively, is sharper and more vehement against the Spirit in the regenerate
person, because it has a stronger adversary in the regenerate than in the
unregenerate. The Spirit of grace is not in the unregenerate, and the flesh in
him does not have such an adversary. Therefore the struggle is more sluggish
and

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 615 | PDF p. 664 | split word joined: remissior]

more relaxed—or almost nonexistent—unless the natural conscience opposes it;
but that conscience is polluted and yields easily. Natural grief and
resistance arise because the unregenerate person is offended by sin insofar as
it is something penal, not insofar as it offends God. In the regenerate,
resistance and remorse exist insofar as the person hates sin as sin. Romans
7:15 says, “For I do not do what I will, but I do what I hate”; and verse 22,
“For I delight in the law of God according to the inner man.”

Hence there is a certain sin in the regenerate, namely culpable opposition
made against grace and the Spirit, which is never found in the unregenerate.
In this way the sin of the regenerate is graver with respect to opposition.
One who rises against a judge personally present sins more gravely than one who
rises only against the law itself, because a person who rises against the
judge when thus present both wrongs the judge's person and violates the law.
The regenerate person, from the side of the flesh, rises against God and the
Holy Spirit who is really present through his grace and, so to speak, present
in a representative person; he also violates the law and thus commits two
sins. The unregenerate person merely violates the law. He does not violate the
indwelling Holy Spirit, because he does not have that Spirit, and thus commits
only one sin. Therefore the sin of the regenerate extends to more objects than
the sin of the unregenerate. Yet this does not prevent the sin of the
unregenerate, with respect to dominion, from being

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 616 | PDF p. 665]

far graver than that of the regenerate.

Assertion 7. With respect to the light and the spiritual quality of that
light, there is more voluntariness in the sins of the regenerate. Thus David
aggravates his sin on this account in Psalm 51:6: “In secret you had made
wisdom known to me.” Yet with respect to the impulse of choice, which enters
more intimately than knowledge into the nature of voluntariness, there is far
more voluntariness and therefore far more guilt in the sin of the unregenerate
than in the sin of the regenerate. In the unregenerate there is a total impulse
and power of choice, accompanied by delight and full consent.

Their objection has little force: “There cannot be a double and contrary will
concerning the same act when a regenerate person sins. Before the regenerate
person sins, while the mind stands at the fork and the spirit dissuades him,
his sin is perhaps lighter than that of the unregenerate. But when he actually
sins and the flesh overcomes the spirit in an actual victory, the spirit then
rests and the struggle ceases.” Reply 1. I do not know whether they profess
ignorance or malice here. When a father disinherits an incorrigible son, he had
willed the son to be corrected and desired that he enjoy his inheritance,
provided the son became upright and good. When a merchant casts his goods into
the sea to redeem his life, he nevertheless desired that they be saved. When a
person suffering from a deadly ulcer arranges for a limb to be cut off, in all
these actions both before the act itself and in the act itself there is
something voluntary

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 617 | PDF p. 666 | split word joined: voluntarium]

and something involuntary. In the act itself contrary principles contend in
the same mixed action: natural love for the son with anger and hatred toward
the incorrigible son's vices, and love of riches with love of life. Just as a
dislocated tibia actually concurs with the motive power in producing a limp,
but by a defective influence, and the hot concurs with the cold in the same
warming, so the flesh and the spirit form one and the same free principle of
one and the same action, whether good or evil. Each concurs both by acting and
by retarding the action's fervor. Hence a good supernatural action is not most
intense because of the flesh's defective influence; nor is an evil action most
intense, because the spirit removes some voluntariness from the action.

2. The Remonstrants maintain that there is no struggle between concupiscence
and the spirit except before consent. This is plainly false from Romans 7:20:
“But if I do what I would not”—there is here a measure of voluntariness on the
side of “I would not”—“it is not I who do it, but the sin which dwells in me.”
By “sin” Paul denotes the part affected by flesh and corruption, in which
there is something voluntary. Otherwise there would be no actual voluntary
sin in Paul, which is in truth to deny this spiritual struggle.
