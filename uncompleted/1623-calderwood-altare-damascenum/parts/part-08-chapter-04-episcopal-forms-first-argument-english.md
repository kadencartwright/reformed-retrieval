### CHAPTER IV

### Bishop and Presbyter Are of the Same Rank

The venerable presbyter Theodore Beza published a treatise on the threefold
episcopate: divine, human, and satanic. He says the divine episcopate is the
office of those who are called pastors by another distinctive name, for all
pastors are also called bishops in the sacred Scriptures. He defines the
human episcopate—that is, the one introduced into the church by human prudence
alone, apart from the express Word of God—as a certain authority attributed
to one particular pastor over his colleagues, yet bounded by certain canons
or rules devised against tyranny. Those who exercised this authority were
called bishops in relation to their fellow presbyters and the whole clergy,
as though they were overseers placed over the clergy. He describes the
satanic episcopate, propagated from the human, as that of men who have wholly
separated themselves from presbyteries, so that they have nothing in common
with presbyters, or who have utterly abolished presbyteries and claimed for
themselves and for officials of some sort—I know not what sort—the whole
ecclesiastical administration and, above all, election, deposition, and the
rights taken from the churches,

[1623 printed p. 113 | IA leaf 125 | PDF p. 125]

so that now they rule the clergy not merely as superiors but as its sole
lords. They have invaded temporal dominions; they have wished not only to take
part in the affairs of the commonwealth but even to preside over them; and,
against all conscience and shame, they have consumed in luxury and pomp what
was dedicated to sacred uses. In his most insolent *Paraenesis*, Tilenus
attacks this division made by the venerable man:

*As if,* he says, *there were as many forms and kinds of episcopate as there
are bishops, or as if the bishops of the Christian church differed among
themselves in no other way than Aaron's descendants differed from the priests
of Jeroboam and both differed from the Balaamites. The differences here,* he
says, *are not only those of priests but also of priesthoods. In the Christian
church, however, there is only one divinely instituted episcopate, although it
can be variously defiled by the leaven of human traditions or even by the
poison of satanic superstitions, just as the Levitical priesthood was defiled
by the sons of Eli and very many others, and likewise by altars erected
everywhere upon the high places.*

[Marginalia: chapter 7.]

Those are the missiles Tilenus drew from Saravia's quiver. We acknowledge
that only one divinely instituted episcopate ought to have standing in the
Christian church, but we affirm that in fact various kinds of episcopate were
introduced into the church in earlier ages, and that there were as many
genera of episcopate as there were men who were bishops, insofar as they were
bishops. We distinguish forms of episcopal government not by the character of
the persons, their piety or impiety, their pure or corrupted faith, or their
sincere or contaminated worship, but by the intrinsic differences and forms
of government. For who does not know that the form of government is one thing
when all pastors, as divinely instituted bishops, govern the church by common
counsel and assistance; another when one man, distinctively called bishop,
has some authority over his colleagues, bounded by canons as though by
barriers lest it break out into tyranny; and another when he alone rules the
flock and the presbyters? A man who does not know how to distinguish
aristocratic government from a moderated or an absolute monarchy needs
eyewash to heal the blindness from which he suffers. Thus Saravia and his
follower Tilenus give us `ἀντιβαλλόμενα`, substitutions—one thing in place of
another—as apothecaries sometimes do. A godly bishop does not differ in kind
from an ungodly one. A bad praetor or a bad consul does not cease to be a
praetor or consul because of his wickedness, so long as men do not overthrow
the praetorship or consulship. The apostolate of Judas the traitor was not
different from that which Peter discharged. Caiaphas exercised the same
priesthood as Aaron. Deviations arising from human traditions do not abolish
the essence of one ministry, but they defile it. Let Saravia play upon that
field; he still does not strike his adversary. If a consul claims for himself
a perpetual principate or perpetual dictatorship, will he not be different
from what he was before? A bishop differs from a bishop not only as a good
consul

[Marginalia: *On the Threefold Episcopate*, p. 21.]

[1623 printed p. 114 | IA leaf 126 | PDF p. 126]

or praetor differs from a bad praetor or consul, but also as a consul differs
from a prince or perpetual dictator. Therefore, what Tilenus builds up
collapses by itself. That Dr. Beza did not call the human episcopate satanic
must be ascribed to his moderation. He dealt more gently with the ancients,
who had introduced that episcopate with zeal indeed but without knowledge, in
order—as they thought—to preserve order more safely. Thus, although the human
episcopate is the offspring of human prudence, it could also in a certain
respect be called satanic, because the mystery of iniquity was at work in its
institution and it was that poisoned egg from which Antichrist was born.
Nevertheless, because they acted imprudently, Beza calls it human, for to err,
be deceived, or fall is human. But when one man ruled his fellow presbyters by
an exclusive authority and with the presbyters shut out, who will allege human
zeal or prudence? Who would not say that it arose, not from a man for the sake
of maintaining order, but from some demon for the sake of overthrowing order?
There is therefore no doubt that various genera of episcopate have in fact
existed in the Christian church, except that clarity is needed in describing
the human episcopate and distinguishing it from the satanic.

A bishop exercises his authority either after consulting the presbyters or
without consulting them. If without consulting them, it is plain that he
exercises exclusive authority. If after consulting them, their votes are
either determinative or advisory. If determinative, then the bishop is only
the president and is constrained as though by barriers by the judgments of
his fellow presbyters. If they are merely deliberative or advisory, he is not
bound to count their judgments; it depends upon his own choice whether he
wishes to follow the larger or smaller, sounder or less sound portion. By his
own affirmative or negative vote, he will command their affirmative or
negative votes to vanish into thin air whenever he pleases. Although this man
has not separated the presbytery from himself, he nevertheless separates it
from all his authority and jurisdiction. *It no more detracts from the dignity
of bishops,* says Downham, *to employ the counsel of prudent and learned men
than it detracts from the majesty of kings to consult prudent and faithful men
who are their counselors.* Likewise, he says, *Ambrose speaks of the
attendance of presbyters for counseling and directing, of the same kind as
the counsel of advisers to a prince, but the others speak of attendance for
governing by number of votes, as in the Roman Senate, granting not even a
negative vote to their bishop.* There is no difference here in episcopal
authority when the bishop employs the counsel and deliberative votes of his
fellow presbyters and when he exercises his jurisdiction without consulting
them, for in either case he alone is judge and prince. The same Downham says
that the bishop's authority is no less when he employs the counsel of the
presbyters than when he does not, just as the authority of a prince is not
diminished when he consults those who are his counselors. We therefore
distinguish the three genera of episcopate in this way. One is the divinely
instituted bishop-pastor, for all pastors

[Marginalia: *Defense*, book 1, chapter 7, p. 111; *Defense*, book 3, chapter
5, p. 121.]

[1623 printed p. 115 | IA leaf 127 | PDF p. 127]

are called bishops in the Scriptures, and that term is never found related to
a presbytery, but to the flock they feed. Another is the presiding bishop,
whom we may call human, who holds a perpetual presidency in the presbytery and
is distinctively called bishop. He only moderates ecclesiastical proceedings
in the sacred assembly, asks for judgments, and is bound, like the other
fellow presbyters, by their greater number. The third is the princely bishop
and judge, who alone has authority over the presbyters and flock. He can
consult or not consult the presbyters and can embrace or reject their counsel.
I would call this episcopate satanic, devised by Satan to overthrow the whole
polity of the church.

Our adversaries dispute with us about the second and third episcopates,
especially the third, when the controversy is whether bishop and presbyter
are of the same order. No one has ever doubted that a president, even a
perpetual one, and the other fellow presbyters are of the same order. They
hold that this president is a dean or archpresbyter, not a bishop. The
princely bishop is also a presiding bishop, but conversely the presiding
bishop is not a princely bishop. Therefore, the arguments our adversaries
bring forward for the presiding bishop do nothing to support the princely
bishop. They are different forms of government, and consequently their
arguments are not common. We shall therefore discuss each separately, and
first the princely bishop and judge, whom alone our adversaries recognize. For
they recognize only one episcopate, namely the third, and affirm it to be
divinely instituted.

In the hierarchical bishop, intensive authority must be distinguished from
extensive authority. By his authority he can be superior to the pastors of
one parish or of a whole diocese. Suppose, for example, that in one
congregation or parish there are several pastors and presbyters, as has often
happened in certain Reformed churches and in the primitive church. If one
pastor is superior to the other presbyters and pastors in preeminence and
superiority of government, he will be no less a bishop than one who has the
same authority over the presbyters of a whole diocese. The king of Salem is no
less a king than the king of Judea. Thus it is one thing to consider a bishop
absolutely and simply, and another to consider him insofar as he is a diocesan
who extends and exercises his authority over several churches. He can
therefore be either parochial or diocesan.

#### On the Intensive Authority of the Princely Bishop

Downham guards himself against the words *sole* and *exclusive* when he
attributes a superiority of authority to the bishop. But since he admits no
one into a share of the authority, and that superiority is not relative to
any lesser governmental authority belonging to the presbyters in the
external forum, this superiority of authority is nothing other than
exclusivity of authority. By its eminence he alone is superior to his fellow
presbyters, who cannot diminish or constrain that superiority by a number of
votes or by censures;

[1623 printed p. 116 | IA leaf 128 | PDF p. 128]

they cannot do so. Ecclesiastical authority is twofold: the authority of
ordination and that of jurisdiction. Both belong to the bishop, as they hold.
By the authority of jurisdiction, presbyters are subject to the bishop as
ruler and as judge. They are subject to him as ruler, by whom they must be led
and directed in the exercise of the authority of their order. For they cannot
exercise the acts of their order or do anything of greater importance without
his permission and knowledge. He alone is properly the pastor in the church;
the rest are only assistants taken into a share of the care, not into the
fullness of authority. They are subject to him as judge, by whom they must be
rebuked and corrected with ecclesiastical censures and penalties of
suspension, excommunication, and deposition. Thus the princely bishop is the
man who has the authority of ordination and an exclusive superiority—or the
exclusivity—of directive and corrective authority over the presbyters and the
flock.

They contend that this bishop as ruler and judge is divine, which they prove
as a consequence, namely because he is of apostolic institution. Some hold
that Christ himself instituted this difference between bishop and presbyter,
but in that way one proposition would cut the throat of the other. For if
Christ immediately instituted this difference, it is not of apostolic but of
dominical institution. If it is of apostolic institution, it is not of
dominical institution, that is, instituted by the Lord himself while living
on earth. If it is nevertheless apostolic, we shall not deny that the
institution is divine, for the Holy Spirit led and directed the apostles both
in handing down doctrine and in shaping the polity of the church. They prove
that it is an apostolic institution by three arguments, ascending step by
step.

1. Argument. That government which prevailed everywhere and always in all
churches for three hundred years from Christ and the apostles, and which was
not instituted in general councils, was without any doubt of apostolic
institution. *What the universal church holds,* says Augustine, *and which
was not instituted by councils but has always been retained, is most rightly
believed to have been handed down by apostolic authority alone.* But the
directive and corrective government of bishops over presbyters and the flock
prevailed everywhere and continuously for three hundred years after Christ
and his apostles in all churches, and was not instituted in general councils.
That is what they must necessarily assume in order to infer what we deny. *If
Christ wished the churches to be governed not by the authority of one bishop,
but by the common counsel of presbyters,* says Tilenus, singing a supporting
part for his new precentors and teachers, *if several churches and pastors can
be subjected to the one pastor or prelate of a single diocese or province only
by tyrannical usurpation, from where, I ask, did there come so great and so
sudden a consent to this tyrannical government by all the churches gathered
and constituted by the apostles throughout the whole world, and even by the
presbyters themselves?* We do not grant the proposition as necessary and
certain, since the proposition says, *without any doubt.* For all visible and
particular churches can err. Particular councils

[Marginalia: Downham, *Defense*, book 2, chapter 4, p. 67; Field, book 5,
chapter 27, pp. 135–136; *On Baptism Against the Donatists*, book 4, chapter
24; *Paraenesis*, chapter 7.]

[1623 printed p. 117 | IA leaf 129 | PDF p. 129]

and general councils can err. Doctors and fathers can err. The constant and
universal practice of the church after the apostles' times is not a certain
indication of apostolic tradition. The papists defend their traditions—the
forty-day fast, the seven orders, the observance of Easter, and so forth—by
this rule. Let us therefore go to the law and testimony in the sacred
Scriptures, where we shall find the apostolic traditions recorded, and not to
the dead. *From where is that tradition?* Cyprian asks concerning the baptism
of heretics. *Does it descend from the authority of the Lord and the Gospel,
or does it come from the commands and epistles of the apostles?* Likewise,
*If therefore it is commanded in the Gospel, or contained in the epistles or
Acts of the Apostles, that those coming from any heresy should not be baptized
but that hands should merely be laid upon them in repentance, let this divine
and holy tradition be observed.* Socrates writes as follows concerning the
feast of Easter, although it was most ancient, and many other matters: *It
certainly seems to me that, just as many other things were introduced in
various places by a kind of custom, so the feast of Easter acquired its
particular observance among individual peoples from a certain custom, because
none of the apostles handed down any law about it to anyone, as I have said.*
Tertullian writes as follows about custom: *Custom, having commonly taken its
beginning from ignorance or simplicity, is strengthened into use by
succession, and thus is asserted against truth.*

Furthermore, from where shall we learn with certainty that there was a
universal and perpetual practice for the three hundred years after the
apostles? Many fathers wrote nothing, many writings have perished, and some
men handed down certain things which, by our adversaries' admission, are not
apostolic, while our adversaries cannot show that the rest disagreed with
them, says Whitaker. There are also very many spurious and adulterated
writings inscribed with the fathers' names. For this reason Vincent of Lérins
says that truth is not always to be asserted, or heresy attacked, by human
testimonies. *But neither always nor every heresy is to be attacked in this
way, but only new and recent ones, when they first arise, before they have
falsified the rules of the ancient faith. They are constrained by the narrow
limits of the time itself, before they attempt, by the poison spreading more
widely, to corrupt the volumes of the ancients. But if heresies have spread
and become inveterate, they must by no means be attacked in this way, because
the long course of time has given them ample opportunity to steal the truth.
And therefore, as for all those more ancient profanities of schisms or
heresies, we ought by no means to do anything except either convict them, if
need be, by the sole authority of the Scriptures, or certainly shun them as
already convicted and condemned in ancient times by universal councils of
Catholic priests.* Since, then, the authority of bishops is inveterate and did
not arise in our own age, the writings of the ancients must not be trusted.
They say Antichrist corrupted the writings of the fathers in order to
establish his own primacy. To defend the episcopate too, it was necessary to
act as a forger, since if the episcopate is removed, the primacy of the Roman
pontiff will fall. The ancients who forged spurious fathers for us—

[Marginalia: epistle 74, to Pompeius; *History*, book 5, chapter 21; *On the
Veiling of Virgins*; *Controversies* 1, question 6, chapter 4.]

[1623 printed p. 118 | IA leaf 130 | PDF p. 130]

Dionysius the Areopagite, Clement of Rome, Ignatius, Dorotheus, and canons
falsely inscribed with the apostles' name, almost all breathing nothing but
episcopacy and hierarchy—we not unjustly fear also corrupted the genuine
fathers, especially after Aerius brought suit against the ranks of bishops.
For men who felt no religious scruple about publishing fictitious fathers and
spurious writings would not have regarded interpolating the genuine writings
of others as a crime. As for Augustine's rule, *If we are to admit it,* says
Whitaker, *a twofold caution is needed: first, whether something was retained
in such a way that there was always a similar reason for observing it, that
is, whether it was always observed in the same way; second, whether it was
observed as necessary.* Tilenus includes the apostles' times within
Augustine's rule: *This rule is acceptable: for what was observed in the
universal church in all past times was surely observed by the apostles
themselves and the other holy men, as members of the universal church. Either
they handed down to their disciples and apostolic men what they had received
from Christ `πολιτευσαμένῳ`—while he lived—on earth, or afterward they
instituted it by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit.*

Next, we deny the assumption as false. They did not conspire toward the
tyrannical rule of one pastor over the several parishes of a diocese or
province. The government of one man with directive and corrective authority
over other fellow presbyters did not prevail in all churches for the first
three hundred years. *Thus until those times the church remained a pure and
uncorrupted virgin, while those who attempted to corrupt the sound rule of
saving proclamation—if there were any such then—lay hidden in a certain dark
gloom until that time. But after the sacred choir of the apostles met their
different ends, and that generation which had deserved to hear divine wisdom
with its ears passed away, the conspiracy of impious error then took its
beginning through the seduction of those who handed down alien doctrine. And
because no one from among the apostles any longer survived, they attempted
with bare face to proclaim against the preaching of truth a knowledge falsely
so called,* says Hegesippus. If, once the apostles were taken away, heretics
dared to raise their head, it is no wonder that Diotrephes too, who aspired to
primacy in the apostles' age, aspired to the same still more shamelessly after
their death. The mystery of iniquity was at work in Paul's time. Mornay does
not hesitate to say that after the apostles' death ambition did not long
remain idle, but followed in their first footsteps. We therefore say that
some men fell through imprudence and some, swollen with ambition, attempted a
change in certain churches. But we nowhere say that all the churches conspired
toward the exclusive superiority of a bishop as ruler and judge, even by
acting imprudently, much less that they wantonly trampled apostolic discipline
underfoot.

To establish the assumption, they draw an argument from a continuous
succession of bishops traced in all the most celebrated churches

[1623 printed p. 119 | IA leaf 131 | PDF p. 131]

from the apostles down to the Council of Nicaea. They prove an interrupted
and perpetual succession from the catalog of bishops of the churches of
Alexandria, Jerusalem, Rome, and so forth, which Eusebius compiled. Eusebius's
testimony is weak for several reasons. *But the subject itself demands right
at the outset,* he says, *that I be pardoned. I confess that what we promise
and profess is greater than our powers, unlimited, and incomprehensible. For
we are the first to attempt this subject, endeavoring to enter as though upon
a deserted and untrodden way. And although we pray that God may be the guide
of our path and Christ's power our helper, nowhere can we find the bare
footprints of the men who entered this same way before us, except `μικρὰς
προφάσεις`, merely slight starting points, by which one man or another in his
own times left particular little accounts for us, like small torches.*

The succession of bishops must be proved from writers of each age, not from
authors who after three hundred years relied upon uncertain report and little
accounts handed from one person to another. Furthermore, Eusebius did not
read attentively and discerningly enough the writings of the ancients that
survived before his time, nor did he weigh the fabulous little traditions he
received from his forebears. The letter from Christ to Abgar, which appears in
Eusebius, was rejected as apocryphal by Pope Gelasius in a Roman council of
seventy bishops, although Eusebius reports that it was taken from the archives
and translated from Syriac. Eusebius says that what Philo the Jew wrote about
the Jewish Essenes was written about Christian monks; Scaliger demonstrates
from Philo himself in his *Refutation of the Three Heresies* that this is
false. Eusebius proves the history of Peter's crucifixion at Rome from Peter's
tomb, as if it were certain that Peter had died at Rome. How fabulous he is in
describing miracles! How many errors and anachronisms Joseph Scaliger notes in
his observations on Eusebius's *Chronicle*! Indeed, he reads the Scriptures
themselves carelessly. He says that the Cephas rebuked by Paul at Antioch was
not the apostle Peter, but another man of the same name from the number of the
seventy disciples. Gelasius rejected the *History* of Eusebius, but not those
of Socrates or Sozomen, among the apocrypha. *In the Chronicle,* says
Spalatensis, *different men find different errors, and not slight ones, as
well as an utterly false assignment of dates.* Eusebius presided over the
little council at Tyre against Athanasius and stood on the side of the Arians.
Joseph Scaliger recounts in his *Thesaurus of Times* the testimonies of the
ancients concerning Eusebius's errors and Arianism. Some relate that he came
to his senses; others, that he died in Arian perfidy. While he was weaving his
history, he was an Arian. But even if he himself were a witness greater than
every objection, there is no doubt that some unskilled impostor interpolated
Eusebius's history. For it mentions Sozomen, who was born one hundred years
later. Finally, Eusebius's catalog does indeed present us with a series of
bishops, but

[Marginalia: proem to the *Ecclesiastical History*; book 1, chapter 14;
*Decree*, 15, chapter 3, *The Holy Roman Church*; book 4, chapter 26, number
21; *Observations*, p. 268.]

[1623 printed p. 120 | IA leaf 132 | PDF p. 132 | ends before the second argument]

not bishops who were judges and princes, possessing exclusive authority or a
superiority of directive and corrective authority over presbyters and the
flock, which was the point to be proved. I hear the word *bishop*, but I do not
hear of the office or the directive and corrective authority. There is an
equivocation in the name *bishop*. Grant, therefore, that all those whom
Eusebius lists were bishops; it does not follow that they were peculiarly and
distinctively called so. Grant also that they were peculiarly and
distinctively called so; nevertheless, it does not follow that the bishops
were judges, since presidents too were distinctively and peculiarly called
bishops. But because they serve this catalog up to us like twice-cooked
cabbage in the second argument, let us advance to the second step, namely the
apostles' own times.
