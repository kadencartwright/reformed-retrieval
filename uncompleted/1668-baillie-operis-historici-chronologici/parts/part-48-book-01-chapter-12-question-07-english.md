### QUESTION VII

#### To What Times Should the Things Ezekiel Has Concerning Gog and Magog in Chapters 38 and 39 Be Assigned?

Response. The difficulty here is so great that the most learned Prideaux, in
discussing this matter, rightly declares that among Scripture’s
hard-to-understand passages scarcely any more involved oracle occurs. And on
Revelation 20:7 Paraeus affirms that John, in a passage if not the same as
ours then certainly very closely parallel, does not explain but speaks in
riddles, wrapping the matter in an enigma. “For this part of the oracle,” he
says, “is most obscure to us, so that one can scarcely divine what the prophet
means, and there are almost as many opinions among the ancients and moderns as
there are heads.” Certainly what Jerome says about Ezekiel 40 and the chapters
that follow—“We openly confess our ignorance, thinking it better to say
nothing than to say a little”—fits the passage now before us still better.
Thus, if in such difficult matters we are unable to offer anything clear and
certain, this seems rightly pardonable on account of human weakness.

> Marginalia, printed p. 270: *The question’s difficulty*.

I find six more notable opinions here; I shall not linger over the many more
obscure ones which the Jesuits Bellarmine and Alcasar scrape together.

The first is Theodoret’s opinion on the passage. He interprets the whole of
both chapters literally and historically of Scythian peoples who, immediately
after Zerubbabel’s return and before the temple was built, were attracted by
the Jews’ enemies with money and promises, marched into Judea with very great
forces, and did and suffered all the things the prophet records. Not only
Bellarmine, Alcasar, and à Lapide, but also our Prideaux, present Theodoret’s
opinion in such a way that it is quite apparent either that they never looked
at Theodoret’s passage or, if they once read it, that they consigned it
entirely to oblivion. For all four say that Theodoret refers Gog and Magog to
Antiochus Epiphanes and the times of the Maccabees. Yet Theodoret is so far
from doing this that he proves Ezekiel speaks about the times of Cyrus and
Zerubbabel before the second temple was built, several centuries before the
Maccabees were born. His argument is that Zerubbabel built that temple from
the spoils of Gog and Magog.

> Marginalia, printed p. 270: *Theodoret’s opinion not understood by the
> Jesuits*.

In any case, I argue as follows against Theodoret. Israel’s victory over Gog
and Magog was by far the greatest, and God’s people never gained a comparable
victory over any enemy. Ezra and Nehemiah, however, wrote a very accurate and
detailed history of everything that happened to the Jews in Zerubbabel’s
time, yet neither says one word about this victory.

> Marginalia, printed p. 270: *It is an error in the best sense, because: 1*.

Second. In Zerubbabel’s days, while Cyrus, Cambyses, and Darius reigned in
Asia, no Scythian peoples ruled the Persians, Egyptians, Ethiopians, and the
others from whom the prophet relates that Gog’s army was constituted in
chapter 38, verses 5 and 13. Indeed, that people did not set foot beyond the
Caspian Gates, the boundaries of the Massagetae, or the banks of the Danube,
the frontier of the western Scythians—much less did they advance into the
interior of Asia Minor and Syria toward Judea. Herodotus, Xenophon, Ctesias,
Berosus, and others give an accurate history of those centuries. From them it
is clear that no Scythians took up arms within the boundaries of the Persian
Empire while Zerubbabel lived.

Third. The war of Gog and Magog against the Jews was launched after the return
to Canaan not only of the Jews but also of the Israelites, and after all
twelve tribes had been reintegrated into one kingdom. For chapter 37 fully
describes the return of Ephraim and the ten tribes and their reconciliation
with Judah, Benjamin, and Levi before the Gogic war of chapter 38; nor to this
day have they been restored to one kingdom with Judah and Benjamin.

Fourth. After the Gogic war the state of the church is most prosperous for
ever, as we read in chapter 39, verses 22 and 29. But after Zerubbabel’s days
the church suffered its greatest calamities—from Antiochus, then from the
Romans, and then from Antichrist, the Roman pontiff.

Theodoret offers these arguments for himself, even with anger and indignation
against all Christians as though they were Judaizers, because they believe
this Gogic war is still future and has not yet passed.

> Marginalia, printed p. 270: *Theodoret’s arguments refuted*.

First. The history of the Gogic war is joined with the history of the return
from Babylon in chapter 37. Therefore these two happened at the same time.
Response.

[Printed p. 271 | IA leaf 301 | access PDF 301 | OCR object 300]

Leaving the antecedent aside, it is apparent from this similar argument that
there is no consequence. The history of the restoration of the temple and the
Christian church in chapter 40 is joined with the history of the Gogic war in
chapter 39. Therefore the most perfect reformation of the Christian church at
the end of the age is joined with the Gogic war in Zerubbabel’s days.
Theodoret recognizes that this is utterly absurd.

Second, he argues: By Gog’s destruction the nations recognized the power of
the God of Israel. But in Christian times the nations were taught the power
of the God of Israel through the gospel. Response. Although the nations came
to know God’s omnipotence through the gospel, nothing prevents them from also
beholding and magnifying divine power in that signal victory of the faithful
over God’s unbelieving enemies in the times of Gog. Gog’s destruction was not,
as Theodoret insists, a more powerful means of enlightening the nations than
the gospel; nevertheless, this means, joined with and subordinate to the
gospel, could have no small power in affecting the nations.

The second opinion is that of Lactantius and many other Fathers. Following in
the footsteps of the millenarians, they refer Gog and Magog to the final years
of their millennium, when Satan, having been loosed, rouses the pagan nations
against the saints who have happily spent a thousand years with Christ in
Jerusalem and Judea. He draws fire down from God out of heaven, which will
destroy those nations in three full days, as it once destroyed the Sodomites,
while the saints meanwhile lie safely concealed in underground caves.

> Marginalia, printed p. 271: *The error of Lactantius and the millenarians*.

The Jews differ scarcely at all, as Jerome says on the passage and Galatinus
in book 5, chapter 11, except that they wish Antichrist to be the leader of
Gog and Magog and imagine that miraculous fire is sent by their fictitious
Messiah. There is no need to linger over refuting this fable. It would long
ago have rested, exploded, in its grave, if the importunity of certain recent
Englishmen had permitted it—even though Bellarmine and the crowd of other
Jesuits do not depart in many respects from the ancient heretics and Jews in
this present component of millenarianism concerning Gog and Magog.

In any case, Lactantius’s most manifest and exceedingly foul anachronisms here,
among many other things, deter us from following him. For with the other
millenarians he wishes the thousand years to begin with the world’s seventh
millennium and Antichrist to be killed at the end of the sixth after a reign
of three and a half years. Yet he places the end of the sixth millennium in
the six-hundredth year after Christ’s birth, for he dedicates his books to
Constantine the Great, already a Christian, whom he earnestly exhorts to
expect with most certain faith the millenarians’ golden ages after two hundred
years. For owing to a bad rendering by the translators of the Septuagint,
many Fathers, Greek as well as Latin, count more than a thousand years beyond
the true number in their chronology.

From the extreme of the millenarians who press the literal sense, Jerome runs
down to the contrary extreme. He will have nothing historical in Ezekiel, for
he interprets everything in chapters 38 and 39 mystically and allegorically of
arch-heretics and heretics who attempt with great effort to destroy the
church, but whose errors are all finally put to flight by divine aid.

> Marginalia, printed p. 271: *The opinion of Jerome, Augustine, and the
> others is to be rejected*.

Both the interlinear gloss and the ordinary marginal gloss follow this
opinion of Jerome, but it is commonly rejected by both Protestant and Roman
Catholic interpreters. Certainly this commentary of Jerome can provide any
sensible reader with an excellent lesson in the great audacity with which
some of even the greatest men in the ancient church twist many passages of
Scripture toward the most alien senses without any solid reason.

Augustine is also allegorical here, though somewhat different from Jerome.
He holds that Gog and Magog do not denote any definite nations, but any enemies
of the church aroused by the devil, especially Antichrist at the end of the
age, in *The City of God*, book 20, chapter 13. Bede, Primasius, Haymo, and
most other Latin interpreters borrow not merely Augustine’s words but also his
meaning. Nevertheless, what Augustine here adds to Jerome seems no less
distorted and discordant with the text than the points on which he abandons
Jerome. He holds that the fire from heaven which devours Gog is nothing other
than the envy that torments the church’s enemies; the saints who stand firm
in persecution are heaven itself and the firmament; and the wrath, hatred,
and envy which the persecutors conceive against the saints because of their
constancy in the faith are the fire flowing down from heaven. These and still
more of Augustine’s statements in this passage are so foreign that even the
Roman Catholics cannot stomach them.

I name Eusebius and Ambrose only in passing. The former interprets Gog of the
Roman emperor and Magog of the empire; the latter interprets both of the
greatest enemies of the Roman Empire, the Goths. Against them he rouses
Emperor Arcadius with a sure hope, from Revelation 20, of a most splendid
victory, about which Jerome dares determine nothing until the event should
disclose the catastrophe of the Gothic war.

> Marginalia, printed p. 271: *Eusebius, etc.; now also Ambrose*.

Passing over these and still many other opinions, two remain for us to
consider especially. The first is that of most Protestants, who, following
Bibliander and Junius, understand Ezekiel’s Gog of Antiochus Epiphanes;

[Printed p. 272 | IA leaf 302 | access PDF 302 | OCR object 301]

the other is that of most Roman Catholics, who interpret both Ezekiel’s and
John’s Gog of a single Antichrist. I say “most” because the Jesuits Alcasar, à
Lapide, and Ribera join the former group to the extent that they concede that
Ezekiel’s Gog is Antiochus, a type of John’s Antichrist.

I would gladly rest in the first opinion because I find almost all our men in
it, nor do I know anyone who offers any argument against it. Least of all men
do I love to innovate against the Fathers and brethren in anything; yet here
I cannot induce myself not to present these points for the more learned to
consider.

> Marginalia, printed p. 272: *Points offered for consideration against the
> common Protestant opinion*.

First. The name Gog does not seem at all applicable to Antiochus Epiphanes. I
admit it proceeded from Junius’s great intellect and learning that so many
today call the kingdom of Syria and Asia Minor “Gogic,” from Gyges, king of
the Lydians and the grandfather or great-grandfather of Croesus, just as they
call a city Gygarta and a lake Gygaean. Although a great many very learned men
swallow these things on implicit faith in Junius—as they do very many other
inventions of that man—they seem foreign to the truth to me. First, since
Gyges, king of Lydia, was Ezekiel’s contemporary, it is unlikely that he could
so quickly have imposed his name upon the people of Asia Minor, and among the
Jews at that, who never seem to have heard so much as a word about this Gyges.

As for Lake Gygaea, it is apparent that this name was by no means imposed on
it by Gyges the Lydian, for many centuries before Gyges was born Homer
mentions the Gygaean lake. Perhaps that lake near Sardis was named from the
giant Gyges, brother of the centaur Briareus:

> “And hundred-handed Gyges, and the man half ox.”

But it was by no means named from Junius’s Lydian.

As for the city Gygarta, grant that according to Junius’s fancy this word is
explained as *Gog carta*, the city of Gyges, the same as Hierapolis,
Scythopolis, and the city Magog, also called Edessa and Bambyce on the
Euphrates—or the city at the foot of Lebanon where the idol Derceto or
Atargatis was worshiped. Although all these things are granted to Junius, they
do not seem to contribute anything to the present matter. For the city Magog,
situated far beyond the boundaries of the Lydians, received its name from the
Scythians who penetrated there, not from Gyges.

Furthermore, what does Gyges have to do with the prince of Meshech and Tubal?
Those peoples far to the north—the Iberians and Albanians—never obeyed the
kingdom of the Lydians. Junius admits that in Croesus’s time the Lydians were
bounded by the River Halys, far from Albania and Iberia, but he thinks that
Croesus’s father Halyattes extended the boundaries of the Lydian kingdom
farther. Yet he proves this opinion of his by no authority’s testimony. Even
granting him this freely, neither Gyges, Halyattes, nor Croesus has anything
to do with Antiochus Epiphanes. For Antiochus was neither descended from
Gyges nor born of the Lydian people, nor was he ever prince of the land of
Magog—that is, of the Scythians—or of the land of Meshech and Tubal—that is,
of the Iberians and Albanians. Alexander’s own dominion was not so northern.

Antiochus the Great, Epiphanes’ father, did indeed possess Lydia and most of
the rest of Asia Minor for a while. But after he had been broken by the
Scipios, he was forced to surrender all Asia on this side of the Taurus, so
that his son Epiphanes never attained dominion even of Lydia itself, much less
of Iberia and Albania.

We do not deny that in the Median war in the days of Cyaxares the Scythians
advanced to the Euphrates and Lebanon and named a city there which they either
found or founded Magog after themselves, and that the kings of Syria held it
after the Scythians were expelled. Yet there is no reason why those kings
should be celebrated as princes of the land of Magog merely because they
possessed that single city, since they never possessed one foot in Scythia,
the true land of Magog even by Junius’s judgment. This is especially so in
Ezekiel, who prophesied these things about Gog and Magog before that Scythian
incursion and the founding of Hierapolis, or at least before any of the Jews
knew that city under the name Magog.

We by no means admit what the Jesuit Alcasar urges in confirmation of Junius’s
opinion: that Antiochus was especially celebrated among the Jews as prince of
Magog under that name because he compelled them by the most extreme tortures
to worship the idol of Magog, Derceto-Dagon, a man—or rather a woman—who was
half fish. For although it is clear from the historians that Antiochus forbade
the gods both of the Syrians and of the Jews to be worshiped, we do not know
by whose testimony they will teach that Derceto of Magog was exempted from
this royal edict. In any case, I know no one besides Alcasar who affirms
anything of the sort—that worship of that half fish was ever imposed on the
Jews. Antiochus did indeed command the Jews and Samaritans to worship Olympian
Jupiter, but no one has yet taught us that he proposed Derceto for any of the
Syrians to worship.

Second. The Gogic war is waged after the Jews’ conversion to the Christian
faith and the return of the ten tribes, and after their reintegration with the
Jews into one kingdom, much less merely into one church. But no one doubts
that these things follow our times, much less the times of Antiochus
Epiphanes. The entire question concerns the major premise, and about it I dare
pronounce nothing categorically. Yet it seems likely that the history in
chapter 37, which precedes the events of chapters 38 and 39, conveys all the
things stated. The Jews’ final conversion is expressly called by Paul

[Printed p. 273 | IA leaf 303 | access PDF 303 | OCR object 302]

in Romans 11:15 a resurrection from the dead. What therefore prevents us from
interpreting the resurrection of Israel expressed so fully, clearly, and
movingly by Ezekiel in his thirty-seventh chapter, beyond the return from the
Babylonian captivity, of that people’s final and full restoration? This is
especially so since by repeating the type the prophet shows that he is by no
means speaking of a resurrection of Israel that would concern the Jews alone,
but of one that would recall the whole house of Jacob, all twelve tribes, from
exile—and indeed one that would place them all under the government of David
and Christ. It is certain these things did not occur before Antiochus’s days,
and whether such a resurrection of Jacob’s entire house has occurred to this
day is rightly doubted. Certainly the restoration promised by the prophet is
joined with the cleansing of the ten tribes not only from idolatry but also
from every iniquity with which they polluted themselves in the places of
their exile, chapter 37, verse 22, and especially from unbelief and rebellion
against the gospel. Yet no such cleansing even of most Jews, much less of most
Israelites, has yet appeared; so far is it from preceding the days of
Antiochus Epiphanes.

Third. King Gog, of whom Ezekiel speaks, had an innumerable army, composed
especially of northern Scythians and of Ethiopians and southern Africans. But
Antiochus Epiphanes never led an army of a particularly or extraordinarily
notable size against the Egyptians or Persians, much less against the weak
Jews. And indeed no Scythians or Ethiopians are ever noted in his army; none
of those peoples were under Epiphanes’ dominion, nor were they accustomed to
serve for pay in Syria in Epiphanes’ age.

Fourth. When Gog invaded, God’s people lived securely in open villages and
had no citadels or fortified cities, chapter 38, verse 11. But in the days of
Antiochus Epiphanes many Jews possessed very strongly fortified cities, and
Jerusalem was defended by such walls and bulwarks that it would have sufficed
to withstand Epiphanes’ force if treachery had been absent.

Fifth. Gog’s soldiers destroyed one another by mutual wounds in 38:21. But
nothing of the sort happened in Antiochus’s army. There were indeed civil wars
among Epiphanes’ successors, but no army of his was destroyed by mutual wounds
through an internal sedition.

Sixth. Gog’s army was destroyed by fire from heaven in an extraordinary
slaughter. But this did not happen to Epiphanes. The Maccabees’ victories over
Antiochus were indeed signal and exceedingly illustrious, but they were not
miraculous. Many thousands of his enemies fell in various battles, but we do
not read that the Maccabees carried out such an immense slaughter as Ezekiel
records.

Seventh. King Gog, together with his army, falls in the mountains of Israel in
a single battle in which the people do none of the fighting but God does it
all; Gog himself falls no less than his army, and he is buried in the land of
the Jews no less than his soldiers. But nothing of this sort was true of
Epiphanes.

Eighth. After Gog’s army was destroyed, the Israelites consumed all its
weapons in flames and themselves lived in great peace. But when Antiochus
Epiphanes died, the Jews consumed no weapons, but gathered as many as they
could and fortified their cities and citadels to defend themselves against
the fearsome and frequent assaults of Antiochus’s successors.

As for the other opinion, that of the Roman Catholics who hold that the Gogic
war in both John and Ezekiel is the war of a single Antichrist against the
church at the end of the age, they construct from it one of their
demonstrations of the heresy and blasphemy of us who teach that the Roman
pontiff is Antichrist. We do not wish to enter here into the commonplace
concerning Antichrist. Certainly, in the judgment of many very great
theologians the marks of Antichrist are so deeply impressed upon the Roman
pontiff that no art of the Jesuits will ever suffice to erase them. I shall
offer only a few points from the text before us against this commentary.

> Marginalia, printed p. 273: *Arguments against the common Roman Catholic
> opinion*.

First. Gog is the prince of northern nations who leads very great forces into
the land of the Israelites to destroy the Jews. But if we believe the Roman
Catholics, Antichrist will himself be a Jew and the Jews’ dearest friend—indeed,
the Jews’ false Messiah. He will defend Judea against foreign nations and by
the Jews’ arms gain many splendid victories over all nations.

Second. Antichrist is the false prophet who by his miracles deceived the
nations in the presence of the beast and the scarlet-clad whore and who was
thrown with the beast into the lake of fire in Revelation 19, before the
outbreak of the Gogic war in chapter 20. Therefore that Gogic war does not
belong to Antichrist, who long before was defeated and cast into the lake of
fire. The Jesuits themselves—Sanctius, Ribera, Alcasar, Pererius, and
others—affirm that the false prophet of chapter 19 is Antichrist.

Third. According to Bellarmine and the common Roman Catholic opinion, there
are only forty-five days from Antichrist’s death to the end of the world. But
after Gog’s death Ezekiel numbers seven years in which the Israelites burned
his weapons.

[Printed p. 274 | IA leaf 304 | access PDF 304 | OCR object 303]

The Jesuits have two evasions here. The first is that Gog’s army had so many
weapons that they could have furnished fuel for the fire for seven whole
years. This response certainly does not satisfy, for in chapter 39, verse 9,
the prophet expressly affirms that the Israelites actually burned those
weapons, not merely that they could have burned them over seven entire years.
Their other evasion is that the definite seven years are taken for an
indefinite period. But this response also fails. For if some necessity
compelled us to take a definite number for an indefinite one, that indefinite
number would have either to exceed or in some way equal the definite number.
Yet so few days—namely, forty-five—are so unequal to seven years that the
latter cannot stand for the former. Indeed, à Lapide on the passage cites for
us his associates Ribera, Pintius, Barradius, and others, to whose calculations
he adds his own.

Fourth. The prince of Magog invaded Judea to gather spoil from the Israelites
which he would distribute to the nations; but according to the Roman
Catholics, Antichrist will give the Jews the spoils he seizes from the nations
and from them build the temple at Jerusalem with the greatest magnificence.

Fifth. Gog is killed and buried in the valley of Hamon-Gog near the place
Gennesaret, as Ezekiel says. But according to the Roman Catholics, although
Antichrist will be killed on the Mount of Olives, he will be buried nowhere
but cast down into hell.

Sixth. Antichrist will make religion, God’s glory, and the good of the church
his pretext in all his wars; but in this war Gog fights with uncovered face
against God and the saints as such, for mere civil dominion, deliberately
trampling down all religion.

Seventh. Just as Sanctius and others among the Roman Catholics, so Polanus and
others among the Protestants understand the passage of Ezekiel both of
Antiochus and of Antichrist, as type and antitype. On this account the Roman
Catholics will gain little even if we concede that Gog is Antichrist, for
Polanus seems not inappropriately to apply to the Roman pontiff what either
Ezekiel or John says about Gog and Magog. But we, who think neither Ezekiel
nor John speaks anything either about Antiochus or Antichrist, shall not use
this argument.

Brightman, Paraeus, Mede, Piscator, Alsted, and Archer, while referring
Ezekiel’s Gog to Antiochus, each nevertheless has his own opinion about John’s
Gog. I shall not linger over examining them, but note only a few things in
passing.

Paraeus, who rightly holds that Gog and Magog are northern peoples, ought not
to have begun the Gogic war of Revelation 20 with the Saracens’ battles
against the Christians in the holy war, when through the treachery of Reynald,
count of Tripoli, Saladin expelled all our men from Judea and all Asia. For
the Saracens, Arabs, Egyptians, and southern Africans have nothing whatsoever
to do with the northern Gog and Magog.

> Marginalia, printed p. 274: *Paraeus’s opinion considered*.

Second. Although Brightman seems to interpret Gog and Magog of the Turks and
Tartars suitably enough, the fact that both he and Paraeus extend the Gogic
war over several centuries cannot be reconciled with Holy Scripture. For both
Ezekiel and John make the Gogic war exceedingly brief and consisting of few,
if any, battles. As soon as Gog surrounds the camp of the saints, God permits
him no victory over the saints, but before they come to blows a heavenly flame
consumes all Gog’s forces. The Turks’ and Saracens’ battles against the
Christians, however, have now been very numerous over six hundred years. In
them, with the fortune of war varying, each side has gained many bloody
victories over the other, while the strength of both still remains intact,
for today many very powerful kingdoms of both Christians and Muslims stand.

> Marginalia, printed p. 274: *Brightman’s*.

Third. Mede concludes by several sophistical little arguments that Gog and
Magog are the American peoples who, after the thousand years of the saints’
kingdom, will invade Europe, Asia, and Africa, which will then be entirely
Christian and will have been holy for a full thousand years. This conjecture,
like many of that man’s, is far too bold and very poorly founded, not to say
ridiculous.

> Marginalia, printed p. 274: *Mede’s*.

Fourth. Piscator’s comments on Revelation 20 please no more, nor do Alsted’s
additions to them, and least of all the most gross and complete
millenarianism which Archer and Goodwin imposed as a colophon upon Piscator
and Alsted. Those men would have been a great disgrace to the Reformed
churches if these innovators had not separated themselves from them all.

> Marginalia, printed p. 274: *Piscator, Alsted, and the
> Independents*.

Piscator postpones the Gogic war for a full thousand years after Rome is
destroyed and Antichrist overthrown. Alsted agrees with Piscator about the
thousand years, but keeps the resurrected martyrs, whom Piscator makes ascend
into heaven to reign there with Christ, on earth until the end of the world.
Yet Archer and Goodwin—the former lately the sole doctor and the latter the
sole pastor, with Philip Nye, of the English Independent church at Arnhem—did
nothing by halves in their millenarianism. For they bring Christ down from
heaven; teach the resurrection of the martyrs a thousand years before the end
of the world; send Gog and Magog with their army against Christ reigning in
the midst of the saints at Jerusalem; and belch forth other most foul dreams
of the ancient

[Printed p. 275 | IA leaf 305 | access PDF 305 | OCR object 304]

millenarians, as the most learned Voetius rightly observed from Archer in his
appendix on the thousand years, and as we also observed more fully from
Goodwin, Burroughs, and the other errors of his sect in our *Dissuasive*.

We do not wish at present to involve ourselves more deeply in these disputes;
after the opinions of others we shall only submit our own view in several
propositions, humbly and candidly, to the examination of the learned.

> Marginalia, printed p. 275: *We set forth our own view*.

First. In every difficult matter it is far easier to assail the opinions of
others than to explain one’s own in such a way that it is free from others’
objections. Certainly what many cite from Irenaeus in the present matter
offers every modest person an excuse for ignorance, if not for error: when he
cannot rest in the opinions of others, he presents what seems right to him in
such a way as to prescribe nothing to anyone, but waits with open ear to learn
better things from anyone else. “Every prophecy,” says Irenaeus in book 4,
chapter 43, “is an enigma before it is fulfilled; but when it has been
fulfilled, it has a manifest exposition and understanding.” No one doubts
that the things John says about Gog and Magog have not yet been fulfilled, and
we offered above many reasons for consideration why Ezekiel’s statements
about the same may likewise seem not yet fulfilled.

Second. The things first taught by Jerome concerning the names Gog and Magog,
and after him by a crowd of writers both now and formerly, do not seem solid.
Jerome’s purpose in the etymology of Gog is to mark the hidden frauds of
heretics, and in that of Magog their open hostilities. But first, this
distinction is peculiar to no time and no kind of the church’s enemies; for
from the beginning of the world to its end Gog and Magog—that is, hidden and
uncovered enemies—have fought and will fight against the church in this
manner.

Second. Jerome’s wish that hidden enemies be denoted by the name Gog is
incongruous, for Christ often uses the metaphor of a roof to denote the
opposite. And according to the construction of Jewish houses, whatever anyone
said or did on the roof he professed to say and do openly in the ears and
before the eyes of everyone. Therefore the metaphor of a roof and the name
Gog are not suitable for signifying hidden enemies.

Third. Just as I would not contend with Jerome or Junius about the change of
vowels in the derivation of words, so that *Gog* can be formed correctly
enough from *Gag* or *Gig*—although *vau*, whether *cholem*, *shurek*, or
unpointed, is more rarely changed in derivation, especially when it is
radical—so I would not so easily concede that *Magog* is formed from *Magag*.
For when the preposition *min* is prefixed to nouns or verbs beginning with a
consonant capable of taking a dagesh, such as *gimel*, it never admits beneath
it the point *patach* or *cametz*, and it always gives the following consonant
a dagesh, neither of which can be seen in the name *Magog*. Certainly, if the
root of the word *Magog* is to be sought, the theme מוג, *mug*, “to melt, to
fail,” which is very common in Scripture, is at hand. From it comes the Piel
מוגג, *mogeg*. From this, with all the consonants preserved, *Magog* could be
formed, even with the genuine meaning of a people that by its terror and
barbarous cruelty causes the spirits of others to fail and melt.

Third. The first English Annotators, who compiled their commentaries at
Geneva, took what they could from the writings of Calvin and Beza, and where
these failed—for those modest men never wished to write anything on the later
chapters of Ezekiel and Revelation—from conversation. They extend Ezekiel’s
Gogic war to Revelation 20 and do not wish it to be understood of Antiochus or
of any persecution of the church before the Messiah’s days. Following them, I
think that the things Ezekiel relates about Gog and Magog are not true of any
age before our own, and that this history of the prophet is rightly restricted
to the times to which the Spirit of God himself applies it in Revelation 20,
not as a type that once passed in figure, but as a signal promise whose
fulfillment was reserved for the times designated by John.

Fourth. We must beware of interpreting these chapters of Ezekiel, with Thomas
Collier in his recent *Apology* to Cromwell for the Jews’ petition, of Jews
who persist in their unbelief—as though it were advantageous to establish
publicly in London and the other cities of the entire island, with full and
secure liberty, synagogues that blaspheme Christ’s name and are prepared by
their example to lead as many as they can into Judaism, as Manasseh ben Israel
requests. Among other pretexts, this is urged: that since the Jews will soon,
when Gog has been defeated, become masters of all nations, it is prudent to
bind them by timely favors so that we may find them friendlier in their
imminent empire. Certainly the prophet in those passages says nothing about
unbelieving Jews. All Christians must indeed beware of them in that state of
unbelief, and we Britons more than others, especially in this season, when by
an epidemic disease of itching almost unheard of in other ages, many mortals
among us appear to hold their ears and souls far too open and exposed to all
manner of most hideous errors and blasphemies. Whatever happiness Ezekiel
promises the Jews in the cited passages is openly promised to penitent
believers who have turned from their long obstinacy to the Lord.

Fifth. Just as the middle English Annotators, together with Junius, departed
badly from the first annotators by interpreting Ezekiel’s Gog of Antiochus’s times, so
both the middle and the latest depart still worse from Junius and all the
orthodox in Revelation 21:8, when, being excessively millenarian, they
postpone John’s Gogic war a full thousand years after Antichrist has been
destroyed and the Jews converted. Far be it, far be it, that our most beloved
bridegroom Jesus should delay his wedding so long. We do indeed place the
Gogic war after the Roman false prophet is driven into Tartarus and after the
Jews and Israelites return to obedience to their David and to faith in Jesus
Christ, Ezekiel 37 and 38. But to wish to prolong Gog’s assault upon the
church for an entire millennium after that age is to indulge the
millenarians far too much.

Sixth. Since the Gogic war is in itself a great mystery and its completion is
future even after this day, it seems advisable to abstain from every
categorical determination about its time, its persons, or most of its
circumstances. For bold decrees about singular matters of this sort procure a
judgment of rashness from many and mockery of their error from others.
Nevertheless, with the learned men’s kind permission, we seem able to offer,
if not pronounce, this: Gog is not Antichrist, since the latter comes forth in
Revelation 11 before the former’s entrance in chapter 20; and the Roman
Antichrist does everything under the pretext of religion as Christ’s vicar,
whereas Gog, in both Ezekiel and John, lays aside and scorns every pretext of
religion and invades all the saints’ camps in open warfare, contending for
dominion rather than error. Gog also breathes of the north in name, manners,
and soldiers, while for many years now the Roman Antichrist has had little or
nothing to do with the northern regions. For first the separation of the
Greeks and later of the Protestants from the Romans snatched almost all the
northern peoples from Antichrist’s bosom.

Nor does the Turk seem to be indicated by Gog’s name. For the Turk is now an
old enemy of four hundred years, worn out by age and long visibly tending
toward destruction with Antichrist; he threatens ruin. But Gog looms as a new
and vigorous enemy, about to devour the whole church in one gulp like
Leviathan, with a greater and more fearsome assault than anyone before him.

Seventh. Although I would not, with the Jesuit Sanctius and others, apply the
title Magog to Magor, the by far greatest king of India and a descendant of
Tamerlane’s stock, nor seek Gog and Magog with Marco Polo in Tenduc, the
furthest eastern province of the north among the Tartars, nevertheless John’s
description of the person, joined with Ezekiel’s, seems to indicate a profane
and cruel prince from the northeast. And certainly, to anyone who prudently
examines either the present state of the world or its state a few centuries
ago, along with Tamerlane’s fearsome trampling of all Asia and the recent
subjugation of China by the Cathayan Tartars, some khan of the Tartars seems
more like Gog than any other mortal on earth today. But leaving those things
for time to disclose, we think it the duty of all the godly to strengthen
their hearts against a storm still more dreadful from the northern regions
than any that has fallen upon the holy city in any age from any quarter of the
world. Against this tempest we are fortified by the Holy Spirit, not only
with most certain faith in a far more illustrious victory and in Christ’s
return for judgment, then at the door, but also, so far as we can gather from
Ezekiel and John, with good hope that Gog’s fearsome assault will advance no
farther than threats. For we do not read that the Gogic weapons shed one drop
of any saint’s blood; instead, all the godly lie hidden in their camps during
that storm, until they are most fully delivered from every fear when Christ
sends heavenly flames upon the hostile army. We do not descend to smaller
details, leaving the questions of Revelation to others.
