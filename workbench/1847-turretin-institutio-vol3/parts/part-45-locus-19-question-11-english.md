QUESTION XI.

ON BAPTISM.

What is baptism, and how many kinds are there?

I. Having explained the nature of the sacraments in general, we must now,
according to the design of our work, treat in particular the sacraments of the
New Testament. We assume that there are only two, baptism and the sacred
Supper, rejecting the five false sacraments added by the papists, which must be
discussed later.

[Marginalia: Foundations of the number two for the sacraments. 1. Christ’s
institution. 2. The nature of the sacraments.]

II. The foundations of the number two are as follows. 1. Christ’s institution,
because no others besides baptism and the Supper are recorded as instituted by
Christ. 2. The nature of the sacraments, because the essential attributes
required for the constitution of a sacrament belong to these alone: they were
instituted by Christ to signify and seal saving grace; in them are material and
visible signs suited to signify the thing signified by analogy; and they are
perpetual and of common use in the church.

[Marginalia: 3. Paul’s testimony.]

3. Paul’s testimony, for he mentions only these two, whether in comparison
with the Israelites’ extraordinary sacraments in the wilderness (1 Cor.
10:1–3) or in relation to the thing signified (1 Cor. 12:13).

[Marginalia: 4. The analogy of the sacraments of the Old Testament.]

4. The analogy of the ordinary sacraments of the Old Testament, of which there
were only two, circumcision and Passover; the former corresponds to baptism,
and the latter to the Supper.

[Marginalia: 5. The necessity of grace.]

5. The necessity of the grace required to produce and perfect spiritual life
in believers, which God wills to seal to us through the sacraments. It is clear
that two things are especially necessary: first, that life be given to us, and
then that the life given be preserved and nourished. Birth is represented by
baptism, while nourishment is represented by the Supper. Hence baptism is
called the sacrament of initiation and the washing of regeneration, while the
Supper is called the sacrament of confirmation and nourishment. Through
baptism we are regenerated; through the Supper we are nourished for eternal
life. Through the former we are received into the covenant; through the latter
we are preserved in it.

III. Since birth comes before nourishment, and reception into the covenant
before preservation in it, baptism comes first in order as the *propylaion*
(προπύλαιον) and *prothyron* (πρόθυρον), or gateway of grace. We must therefore
discuss it first, so that we may see

[1847 vol. III printed p. 323 | IA leaf 357 | PDF p. 357]

first the origin of the word, then its homonymy and its many uses, both among
the sacred writers and among ecclesiastical writers.

[Marginalia: The etymology of baptism.]

IV. *Baptism* is a word of Greek origin derived from the verb *baptō* (βάπτω),
which means to dip and imbue; *baptizein* (βαπτίζειν) means to plunge and
immerse. Plutarch, *On Superstition*, says, *baptizon se eis thalassan*
(βαπτίζόν σε εἰς θάλασσαν), “plunge yourself into the sea”; and in the *Life of
Theseus* he recites a verse of the Sibyl about Athens that applies more aptly
to the church:

*Askys [sic] baptizē, dynai de toi ou themis esti.*
You will be plunged like a wineskin in the waters, but you will never sink.

Hence it is more than *epipolazein* (ἐπιπολάζειν), which is “to float lightly,”
and less than *dyein* (δύειν), which is to go to the bottom, that is, to seek
the depths unto destruction. Since something is commonly plunged and dipped
in order to be washed, and those who are immersed are commonly washed, it has
come about that just as the Hebrew טבל, which the Septuagint renders *baptizō*
(βαπτίζω) in 2 Kings 5:14, is also used for רחץ, which means *to wash*, so also
among the Greeks *to baptize* (τὸ βαπτίζειν) is used for washing by metalepsis
(Mark 7:4): “When the Jews return from the marketplace, they do not eat unless
they wash themselves, *ean mē baptisōntai* (ἐὰν μὴ βαπτίσωνται).” The baptisms
of cups, pitchers, and couches practiced among the Jews must be understood in
the same way, as must the “various washings” (*diaphoroi baptismoi*, διάφοροι
βαπτισμοί) prescribed for the Jews, mentioned in Hebrews 9:10, and the
superstitious washings received from the tradition of the elders, mentioned
in Mark 7:4. Hence Justin called the Pharisees *baptistai* (βαπτισταί) for this
reason. Epiphanius, *Heresies* 17, also speaks of the sect of the “daily
baptizers” (*tōn hēmerobaptistōn*, τῶν ἡμεροβαπτιστῶν), who insisted upon a
daily baptism and maintained that a person washed in this way “was cleansed
and purified from every charge” (*apolouesthai kai hagnizesthai apo pasēs
aitias*, ἀπολούεσθαι καὶ ἁγνίζεσθαι ἀπὸ πάσης αἰτίας).

[Marginalia: Homonymy.]

V. From this twofold meaning of plunging and washing, two other metaphorical
meanings have been derived. In the first, baptism is used for affliction and
calamity, according to the style of Scripture in which afflictions are
compared to watery depths (Ps. 69:1, 15–16; 18:17; 32:6). It is used in this
sense in Matthew 20:22 and Luke 12:52. Not a few also wish “baptism *for the
dead*” (*hyper nekrōn*, ὑπὲρ νεκρῶν), of which Paul speaks in 1 Corinthians
15:29, to be understood in relation to the violent sufferings of martyrs or
confessors: “for the dead,” that is, for faith in the resurrection of the dead;
or “because of the dead,” that is, because of hope concerning the dead; or
because of the dead one, namely Jesus, by an exchange of number. Others,
however, prefer to take the word *baptism* properly and refer it either to
vicarious baptism or to the baptism of the bedridden that prevailed among the
ancients. The celebrated Spanheim, with his customary precision and learning,
thoroughly treats this entire question in the excellent dissertation he wrote
on this “difficult to understand” (*dysnoēton*, δυσνόητον) passage of Paul. In
the second metaphorical meaning, baptism is transferred to the miraculous
outpouring of the gifts of the Holy Spirit, because these are commonly poured
into the soul to imbue and cleanse it (Matt. 3:11; Acts 1:5; Titus 3:5). This
comes from the Old Testament, where the communication of the Spirit is
commonly represented by an outpouring of waters (Isa. 44:3; Joel 2:28).

VI. From this homonymy arises the ancients’ distinction of a threefold baptism:
of water, of the Spirit, and of blood. The baptism of water designates the
sacrament of water. The baptism of the Spirit designates the outpouring of the
Holy Spirit, whether ordinary for conversion or extraordinary for miraculous
gifts. The baptism of blood designates the martyrdom of those who, not yet
having received baptism, contended for the faith of Christ and resisted to the
point of shedding blood. Basil says of them in his *Homily on the Forty
Martyrs* that *en tō idiō haimati ebaptisthēsan* (ἐν τῷ ἰδίῳ αἵματι
ἐβαπτίσθησαν), “They were baptized in their own blood.” The distinction is
acceptable if it resolves an equivocation; it cannot be tolerated if it
divides baptism into species, because it is not a division of a thing into its
species but only a distinction of an equivocal name into the things it
denotes. Some add a fourth species, namely the baptism of light, by which they
wish to denote doctrine

[1847 vol. III printed p. 324 | IA leaf 358 | PDF p. 358]

preached by someone. They wish Matthew 21:25 and Acts 18:25, which deal with
John’s baptism, to be understood in this sense. But this is less appropriate
if it is taken to the exclusion of the baptism of water, which the ancients
called *phōtismos* (φωτισμός), or illumination. Nor do the passages cited
prove this, because in both places the baptism of water is meant. Under it, by
a synecdoche of a part or adjunct, John’s doctrine or his entire ministry is
comprehended.

VII. Baptism is better distinguished into the external and visible baptism of
water, which is performed by a human being, and the internal and invisible
baptism of the Spirit, which is performed by God, whatever the Lutherans may
oppose here. For this distinction is expressly taught by John the Baptist
(Matt. 3:11): “I indeed baptize you with water, but he will baptize you with
the Holy Spirit and fire”; and it is confirmed by Christ (Acts 1:5). Thus Peter
openly distinguishes the baptism by which the filth of the body is washed away
externally from that which consists inwardly in the pledge of a good conscience
(1 Pet. 3:21), just as Paul distinguishes a twofold circumcision (Rom. 2:28;
Col. 2:11), one of the flesh and the letter and the other of the Spirit in the
heart. In this way, however, we do not make two sacraments. We distinguish the
earthly and heavenly thing of the sacrament, the sign and the thing signified,
which are designated synecdochically by the name of the whole. Or we designate
two operations in the same baptism, one belonging to Christ and the other to
the minister.

VIII. The ancients enumerate various kinds of baptism, some three and others
six, as does John of Damascus in *Orthodox Faith* 4.10. The first is the
universal flood that destroyed sinners, which Peter calls an antitype of
baptism (1 Pet. 3:21). Tertullian, in his book *On Baptism*, calls it “the
baptism of the world.” The second is baptism through the sea and the cloud, of
which the apostle speaks in 1 Corinthians 10:1–2, when Moses passed through the
Red Sea. There the sea was the antitype of the water of baptism, while the
pillar of cloud that gave light by night was the antitype of the Holy Spirit,
according to Cyprian, book 4, epistle 6, and Chrysostom on 1 Corinthians 10.
The third is legal baptism, by which the unclean washed themselves and their
clothes according to the Mosaic law. The fourth is John’s baptism, which was
introductory (*eisagōgikon*, εἰσαγωγικὸν), bringing those baptized only to
repentance and “not yet wholly spiritual” (*ontō [sic] de holon pneumatikon*,
ὄντω [sic] δὲ ὅλον πνευματικόν). The fifth is the “wholly spiritual” (*holon
pneumatikon*, ὅλον πνευματικόν) baptism of our Lord Jesus Christ, by which
either he himself,
though not in need of baptism, was baptized by John for our sake, or by which
he baptizes us with water and the Spirit. The sixth is the “truly laborious”
(*ontōs epiponon*, ὄντως ἐπίπονον) baptism of repentance and tears. The seventh
is the baptism of blood and martyrdom. The eighth is not saving but lethal: the
baptism of eternal punishments, in which sinners are immersed in the flaming
fires of the infernal flood unto eternal destruction.

IX. Here baptism is taken properly and preeminently for “the first sacrament
of the New Testament, by which, through external sprinkling and washing with
water, the believers’ internal absolution is declared and sealed to them, both
with respect to the remission of sins and with respect to sanctification by
the blood and Spirit of Christ.” Or, somewhat more fully, it is “the first
sacrament of the Christian church, by which the remission of sins and
regeneration through the blood of Christ and the Holy Spirit are offered and
sealed, by an external sprinkling of water in the name of the most holy
Trinity, to covenant members who thereby are received into the family of God.”
This is gathered from Matthew 28:19; Romans 6:3–4; Titus 3:5–6; and Acts 2:38.

[Marginalia: The author of baptism.]

X. The efficient cause and author of baptism is God, because it is said to be
from heaven, that is, from God, and not from human beings (Matt. 21:25). And
God sent the first person who baptized to baptize (John 1:33). But since at
first it had been instituted only for the Jews when it was administered
through the ministry of John the Baptist, Christ by a renewed institution
extended its use to all nations that were to be converted through the
preaching of the gospel (Matt. 28:19). Yet Christ is not recorded to have
baptized (John 4:2), since he was to baptize with the Spirit and fire (Matt.
3:11). The subordinate ministers of baptism are

[1847 vol. III printed p. 325 | IA leaf 359 | PDF p. 359]

pastors and preachers of the gospel, whether ordinary or extraordinary, and
they alone. For the administration of the sacraments belongs to the person to
whom the preaching of the word belongs. Nor is it lawful under the pretext of
any necessity to depart from the command and order of Christ, who joined the
public preaching of the gospel to the administration of baptism by an
indissoluble bond.

[Marginalia: The external matter.]

XI. The matter of baptism is twofold. The external and sensible matter has the
nature of a sign. Under it we understand everything perceived by the external
senses in the lawful administration of baptism, to which belong (1) the
element and (2) the external ceremonies customarily employed according to the
divine institution. Hence the sign can be distinguished into the elemental
and the ceremonial. The elemental sign is true and natural water, not oil,
salt, spittle, honey, or any other matter of this kind foreign to Christ’s
command and the practice of the ancient church. The ceremonial sign consists
in the rite, namely the washing performed with water (1 Pet. 3:21), whether by
sprinkling or by immersion. Immersion was formerly used in warmer regions, as
the practice of John the Baptist (Matt. 3:6, 16; John 3:23), Christ’s apostles
(John 3:22; 4:1–2), and Philip (Acts 8:38) teaches. But later, especially in
colder regions, when the church began to extend toward the north, submersion
(*katapontismos*, καταποντισμός) was changed to sprinkling (*rhantismos*,
ῥαντισμόν), and sprinkling alone was employed. Just as this is in no way
contrary to Christ’s institution, so it can be confirmed by examples from the
apostolic church and from the primitive church that followed it.

[Marginalia: Sprinkling is rightly employed in baptism.]

For although immersion ordinarily had a place, various arguments show that it
was not employed so universally that sprinkling was not also sometimes used,
both with respect to the apostolic church and the primitive church. Thus where
there was a great multitude of believers, as when three thousand were baptized
in one day (Acts 2:41), it can scarcely be doubted that sprinkling rather than
immersion was employed, since immersion could hardly, if at all, have been
conveniently performed in so short a time. Likewise, baptism was administered
from house to house, where it is improbable that a supply of water sufficient
for immersion was always present, especially if the matter arose unexpectedly
(Acts 16:27ff.). In the primitive church baptism was given to the bedridden and
the sick, for whom it undoubtedly could not have been performed by immersion.
Nor are various arguments for sprinkling lacking. (1) The noun *baptismoi*
(βαπτισμοί) and the verb *baptizesthai* (βαπτίζεσθαι) are used not only of
immersion but also of sprinkling (Mark 7:4; Luke 11:38). (2) The thing
signified by baptism is designated by the term *sprinkling* (1 Pet. 1:2; Heb.
10:22). (3) Sprinkling is sufficient for the analogy, and the power of baptism
depends not upon the quantity but upon the quality of the water. (4) Under the
Old Testament there were various washings and sprinklings (*rhantismoi*,
ῥαντισμοί), both of water and of blood, to which Christ looks in the
institution of baptism. Hence the blood of Christ, which is the thing
signified, is called the “blood of sprinkling” (*haima rhantismou*,
ῥαντισμοῦ; Heb. 12:24). (5) Sprinkling is far more suitable both for
protecting the health of those baptized, which could have suffered harm from
immersion in those colder regions, especially in tender infants, and for
sparing the modesty of adults, which could have been injured by the baring of
the whole body. For this reason we read that deaconesses were formerly
employed to undress women.

XII. Moreover, washing, whether by sprinkling or by immersion, is either once
or three times. It is clear that a threefold washing was formerly employed to
represent the Trinity of persons. Tertullian says in *Against Praxeas*, chapter
16, “We dip not once but three times, at the several names, into the several
persons of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.” And in *The Soldier’s Crown* he
says, “We are immersed three times.” Cyril, *Catechesis* 2; Basil, *On the Holy
Spirit* 27; Ambrose, *On the Sacraments* 2.27; and others agree with him.
Isidore of Seville, *On Illustrious Men* 27, asserts that either rite

[1847 vol. III printed p. 326 | IA leaf 360 | PDF p. 360]

can be used without prejudice to the faith. He says, “Yet in the one faith a
different custom does not harm the holy church. For because there is one
substance in three subsistences, it can in no way be blameworthy to immerse an
infant in baptism three times or once, since in the three immersions the
Trinity of persons can be designated, and in the one the singularity of the
Deity.” Today we retain a single sprinkling and do not think that we should
quarrel contentiously with anyone about it, since the number has not been
expressly sanctioned by any command. We reject as foreign to Christ’s
institution and superstitious the other strange rites commonly added by the
papists, such as exorcism, the consecration of the water, the sign of the cross
upon the forehead and breast of the baptized person, and the placing of a
lighted candle in his hands.

[Marginalia: The internal matter.]

XIII. The internal matter, or the thing signified, is Christ with his benefits:
“For as many of us as have been baptized into Christ have put on Christ” (Gal.
3:27). From that most intimate union of ours with Christ flow the benefits of
justification and regeneration, which are designated by the mystical washing
through the blood of Christ and the Holy Spirit. This twofold washing is
discussed in 1 Corinthians 6:11. The first is the washing from the guilt of
sins through Christ’s blood, treated in Acts 22:16; 1 John 1:7; Acts 2:38; and
Matthew 26:28. The other is regeneration through the Holy Spirit (Titus 3:5)
unto newness of life (Rom. 6:4), in which sense Christ is said to have come
with water and blood (1 John 5:6).

[Marginalia: The form.]

XIV. The form of baptism consists in the relation (*schesis*, σχέσει) and
analogy between the sign and the thing signified. This relation is placed in
three things: the signification, sealing, and exhibition of the internal
matter through the external, of the thing signified through the sign. First,
it lies in signification, because just as water washes away the filth of the
body (1 Pet. 3:21), so the blood and Spirit of Christ wash away the filth of
the soul (1 Cor. 6:11; 1 John 1:7). Just as in former times those to be baptized
were immersed in the water, remained under the water, and emerged from it
(Matt. 3:16; Acts 8:38), so the old man died and was buried in them, and the new
man arose (Rom. 6:4; Col. 2:12). Just as those to be baptized are now sprinkled
with water, so they are sprinkled with the blood and Spirit of Christ for the
washing away of sins (Acts 22:16; Eph. 5:26, 26–27; Heb. 4:14). Second, it lies
in sealing, because baptism is no less a seal of the righteousness of faith
than circumcision. Third, it lies in exhibition, because in lawful use God
actually furnishes what is represented through baptism, not by the work
performed, but by the most certain truth of the promise. By it we are assured
that what is done in the body through the sprinkling of water is done
spiritually within the soul of believers through the blood and Spirit of
Christ. The clarity of this signification and the certainty of the sealing
and application found in lawful use are the cause of sacramental phraseology
about baptism, when it is called the washing away of sins (Acts 22:16), the
washing of regeneration (Titus 3:5), and a cause of salvation (1 Pet. 3:21).
Peter clearly teaches in the same passage, by adding a qualification, that
these expressions are not proper but figurative when he says, “Baptism, which
saves us, is not the removal of the filth of the flesh but the pledge of a
good conscience.” Therefore, in keeping with the nature of the sacraments,
these expressions must be resolved into proper ones: baptism is the sign and
seal of the washing away of sins and of regeneration, and it saves us insofar
as through it the Holy Spirit works effectively to confirm faith in us.

[Marginalia: The subject.]

XV. The subjects of baptism are all covenant members, whether they truly are
such or are probably judged to be such because of their external calling,
profession of faith, and communion with believers, without any distinction of
sex, nation, or age. With respect to nation, although at first it was
administered only to the Jews (Matt. 3), by Christ’s command it began to be

[1847 vol. III printed p. 327 | IA leaf 361 | PDF p. 361]

administered to all nations (Matt. 28:19), to whom the gospel was to be
preached. With respect to sex, it is common to both sexes and not proper only
to males as circumcision was. Hence Philip is said to have baptized men and
women (Acts 8:12), and Paul to have baptized Lydia (Acts 16:15) and others.
With respect to age, infants and adults alike are to be baptized, yet on the
condition that adult catechumens confess their sins beforehand and profess
faith in Christ (Matt. 3:6; Acts 2:41; 8:12, 37), while infants are born to
believing parents or at least to one believing parent (Acts 2:38–39; 1 Cor.
7:14). For Scripture testifies that they are heirs of the kingdom of heaven
(Matt. 19:14), participants in the promise of grace (Acts 2:39), and holy (1
Cor. 7:14). They should no more be excluded from baptism than they were
formerly excluded from circumcision—indeed, all the less insofar as the later
grace of the New Testament is greater than the earlier grace of the Old, as we
will prove against the Anabaptists in its proper place.

[Marginalia: The end.]

XVI. The end of baptism is twofold: (1) to serve our faith before God and (2)
to serve our confession before human beings. On God’s part, the primary end is
the sealing of the covenant of grace and its benefits: our union with Christ,
and the remission of sins, or justification, and regeneration through the Holy
Spirit that flow from it. On our part, it is our obligation to worship the
true God—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—into whose communion we are received as
children of the Father, members of Christ, and temples of the Holy Spirit, and
to love our neighbor, since through one Spirit we have all been baptized by
one baptism into one body (Eph. 4:4–5; 1 Cor. 12:13). The secondary end is (1)
the external testimony of the obedience we must render to God; (2) the
distinction of the church from profane nations through this token, by which we
profess that we enroll under Christ as commander; and (3) the symbol of the
communion of the saints with one another, by which they ought to embrace one
another in the duties of reciprocal love as members of the same body.

XVII. If it is further asked why Christ willed this sacrament of initiation to
consist in baptism rather than in some other sign, and from where that rite
drew its origin, the answer is ready at hand. It was drawn from the common
practice of the Jews, among whom various baptisms were known to have been
customary, especially with respect to those who passed from paganism to
Judaism or placed themselves under teachers for instruction. Hence came the
custom of consecrating proselytes “by circumcision, sacrifice, and baptism.”
Maimonides says, “When a gentile wishes to be received and joined to the
Israelite covenant, to take refuge under the wings of the divine majesty, and
to take the yoke of the law upon himself, circumcision, baptism, and an
offering are required.” Hence the well-worn maxim among them, אינו גר עד שימול
ויטבול: “A person is not a proselyte until he is circumcised and baptized.”
This was why the ceremony of baptizing instituted by John and Christ seemed
strange to no one, since nothing was better known or more customary among
them.
