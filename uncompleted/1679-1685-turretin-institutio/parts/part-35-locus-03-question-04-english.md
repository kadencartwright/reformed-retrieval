QUESTION IV

ON THE NAME JEHOVAH

Is it so proper to God alone that it is incommunicable to creatures?
Affirmative against the Socinians.

I. Although God, who is both the most singular being and by his nature
distinct from every other thing, does not need a distinguishing name, and
although a name does not properly apply to God—neither an appellative name,
which distinguishes species of the same genus, nor a proper name, which
distinguishes individuals of the same species—hence Trismegistus calls God
“nameless” (ἀνώνυμον) in Lactantius, book 1, chapter 6. Nevertheless, because
all our knowledge begins from a name, he is accustomed to assume various
names in Scripture in order to accommodate himself to us. Some are drawn from
strength, such as El and Elohim; some from omnipotence and sufficiency, such as
Schaddai; some from loftiness, such as Elion; and some from dominion, such as
Adonai. But the first and principal of all is Jehovah, which is derived from
being or existing.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. There are two principal questions about this name. One is grammatical and
concerns its pronunciation: whether it can be pronounced, and what its true
and genuine manner of pronunciation is—whether as *Jehovah* or as *Adonai*.
The other is theological and concerns its use: whether it is proper to God
alone or is also common to creatures. Here we leave aside the first question,
which we leave to the critics, and treat only the latter.

III. This question is disputed between us and the Socinians. In order to evade
the argument we are accustomed to draw from this name to assert the deity of
Christ, they contend that this name can be communicated to various creatures
and is customarily attributed in Scripture to angels and to Christ as God's
envoys. They assume as a rule of undoubted truth that an envoy can bear the
name and person of the one who sends him. Nevertheless, they establish a
certain distinction: if the name Jehovah is set with the addition of the word
*Zebaoth*, it is proper to God alone, but if it occurs without it, it is also
common to creatures. We, however, say that this name is so proper to God that
it is wholly incommunicable to creatures. It is true that there is some
disagreement even among the orthodox about the use of this name, but it is
verbal rather than real. Some acknowledge that it belongs to no created
person, but nevertheless maintain that it is used with respect to the ark of
the covenant by a metonymy of the adjunct. Others hold that it is attributed
and belongs to no creature at all. But all agree that this name, taken
absolutely and without a trope, is proper to God alone. Nevertheless, the
latter opinion seems truer and safer for cutting off the Socinian error.

IV. The reasons are: 1. God claims this name for himself as his own and
proper name: “I am Jehovah; this is my name; I will not give my glory to
another,” Isaiah 42:8 and 48:2; Amos 5:8 and 9:6. Hence Israel sings in Exodus
15:3, “Jehovah is his name.” Thus the Jews call it absolutely *the name*
(השם), *the name of the substance* (שם העצם), and *the proper and separate
name* (שם המפורש המיוחד).

V. The etymology and signification of the word are such that it belongs to
God alone. It is clear from Scripture that this name principally implies
three things which

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 167 | IA leaf 221 | PDF p. 221]

seem to be joined together, Isaiah 44:24–26. 1. God's eternity and
independence, insofar as God
is the necessary being who exists through himself independently of any other,
the self-existent one (αὐτοὼν), Exodus 3:14, “I am who I am.” Hence he is
called simply “the one who is” (ὁ ὢν), as the ancient philosophers and Plato
especially acknowledged. John also describes him in Revelation 1:4 through
the three differences of time: “the one who is, and who was, and who is
coming” (ὁ ὢν καὶ ὁ ἦν καὶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος). To this pertains that saying of the
ancient Gentiles: “Zeus was, Zeus is, Zeus will be, O great Zeus” (Ζεὺς ἦν,
Ζεὺς ἐστὶ, Ζεὺς ἔσσεται ὦ μεγάλε Ζεῦ). 2. Causality and efficacy, because
what is first and most perfect in every genus is the cause of the others.
Because God exists through himself, he is the cause of being for all others,
Isaiah 44:24. 3. Immutability and constancy in promises, because he truly
performs and does what he promised, giving his promises “being” (τὸ εἶναι),
not only as the self-existent one (αὐτοὼν), but also as “substance” (οὐσίαν)
and “one who gives substance” (οὐσιοποιὸς). In this sense Exodus 6:3 says that
he was not known to the fathers under the name Jehovah—not with respect to the
signifying word, for the contrary is clear from the book of Genesis, but with
respect to the thing signified. For he had not yet given being to his promises
concerning the multiplication of the seed, the departure of the people from
Egypt, the entrance into Canaan, and so forth. He had revealed himself to the
fathers through his power in the creation of the world, in its government,
and in the bestowal of many benefits and marvelous protection, but he had not
yet declared in reality that he was Jehovah by fulfilling the promises given
to the fathers. Since eternal existence, omnipotent power, and immutable truth
belong to God alone, the name Jehovah, which embraces these three things, must
be proper to God alone.

VI. The name Jehovah by its own nature or grammatical construction cannot be
common to several, because it is individual in itself and is never read with
the emphatic or demonstrative ה. 2. It is always used in the singular number
and never in the plural. 3. It is nowhere read with a suffix or in the
construct state. Therefore, since it is attributed to God, it must be proper
to him.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

VII. Wherever this name is attributed to an angel, as in Genesis 16, 18, and
48; Exodus 3:2; and elsewhere, the uncreated angel is meant, not a created
one—namely, the Son of God, who is the angel of the presence and who often
appeared in this way as a prelude to his incarnation, as is clear from the
circumstances of the passages. There the properties, works, and honor of God
are attributed to that angel. In Genesis 16:10 he attributes to himself the
divine effect of *multiplying the seed*; in verse 13 he is called *the God of
vision*. In Genesis 18 the uncreated angel is designated: 1. by the *name*,
because only one of the three angels who appear is called Jehovah; 2. by
*divine properties*, when he attributes omnipotence and omniscience to
himself, foretells future things in verses 10 and 14, and is called the Judge
of the whole earth in verse 25; and 3. by the *honor*, because Abraham adores
him, yet the angel does not refuse it, although he would have done so if he
had been created, as in Revelation 19. In Genesis 48 the angel is said to have
delivered Jacob from every evil, which belongs to God alone, and a blessing
is sought from him which no one but God can give. In Exodus 3 the angel who
appears to Moses is in turn called Elohim and Adonai, is said to have sent
Moses in chapter 4, and to be the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob who heard
the cry of the people.

VIII. The angel of Jehovah is not always distinguished essentially from
Jehovah, as one sent from the sender, but only personally, so that the one who
is the angel of Jehovah is also the angel Jehovah.

IX. It is one thing to act from God's authority, which belongs to a created
angel. It is another to possess God's authority and claim his name for
oneself, which belongs to no one but the true and eternal God. The attendant
or envoy of a prince acts from the prince's authority and in his name, but he
does not therefore assume the prince's titles for himself or allow himself to
be called king or prince. Thus a created angel never assumes God's person in
such a way that he either attributes any name of God to himself or allows it
to be conferred upon him by others.

X. When the apostle in Hebrews 13:2 commends hospitality from the fact

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 168 | IA leaf 222 | PDF p. 222]

that through it angels were sometimes received as guests, he is not referring
to the appearance made to Abraham in Genesis 18, but to the one which happened
to Lot in Genesis 19, where only two were created angels. The third, who was
uncreated, remained with Abraham to speak with him and afterward met Lot as
he came out of Sodom. He is therefore called Jehovah in verse 24.

XI. The supreme God cannot be sent by command, but he can be sent by consent.
Nor is it incoherent (ἀσύστατον) to be the angel of Jehovah and to be Jehovah,
because in the former expression *Jehovah* is taken hypostatically, but in the
latter essentially. The Son can be sent by God the Father and yet be himself
the same God with the Father.

XII. The angel about whom Moses speaks in Exodus 23:20 cannot be a creature,
because God commands the people to obey him and not provoke him, since he will
avenge human sins and God's name is in him. This signifies far more than being
furnished with God's authority. Otherwise it could equally be said of Moses
and any ordinary prophet that God's name is in him, which is said nowhere.

XIII. It is one thing to be and to be called Jehovah. It is another for
Jehovah to be and dwell somewhere. The latter is said of the church in Ezekiel
48:35, when it is said that it will be called by this name, “Jehovah is there”
(יהוה שמה); not the former.

XIV. It is one thing to inscribe a symbol upon an altar, but another to
attribute some name to the altar. The former is said in Exodus 17:15, where
the title and inscription of the altar is noted: “Jehovah is my banner”—supply
*is*—in the way that certain sayings are customarily inscribed upon palaces
and temples; but not the latter.

XV. It is one thing for the ark of the covenant to be called Jehovah, but
another for the God whose presence the ark symbolized and pledged to be so
called. The latter is said in Psalm 24:7 and 47:6, not the former. Likewise,
what was customarily said when the ark was raised or set down—Psalm 68:1,
“Arise, Lord, and let your enemies be scattered”—was not directed to the ark
itself, which was not capable of that worship, but to God represented in the
ark, whose work it was to scatter his enemies.

XVI. Jeremiah 33:16 does not say that Jerusalem is to be called “Jehovah our
righteousness.” Rather, the prophet teaches that the one who would call
Jerusalem to salvation would be Jehovah our righteousness, in order to denote
the foundation of its faith and hope. Nor should it be objected that Jerusalem
can no less be called Jehovah than the church is called Christ in 1
Corinthians 12:27. For the name Christ is common to many, from which the
faithful are called Christians because of a likeness of anointing, 1 John
2:20. But the account of the name Jehovah is not the same, for it is singular
and proper to God alone.

XVII. Although the word “Lord” (Κυρίου), when taken appellatively, is common
to several, nevertheless when it is taken absolutely, insofar as it
corresponds to Jehovah, it is just as if it were a proper name. In this way
the Seventy, and the apostles after them, render יהוה by “Lord” (Κύριον),
because no other more suitable word was available, although it does not fully
express the force of the word. In this sense it can be attributed to no one
but God alone.

XVIII. Although God has spoken to us through the Son as incarnate (ἔνσαρκον)
in these last times, Hebrews 1:2, this does not prevent him from having already
spoken to the fathers through him as “without flesh” (ἄσαρκον). Otherwise it
be false that he is said to be the same yesterday and today, Hebrews 13; that
he was tempted by the Israelites in the wilderness, 1 Corinthians 10:9; that
the law was given in the hand of the Mediator, Galatians 3:19; and that he
spoke to Moses and gave him “living oracles” (λόγια ζῶντα), Acts 7:38.
Therefore in the promulgation of the law the primary lawgiver, who was Christ,
the angel of the covenant mentioned in Acts 7:31, who had appeared to Moses in
the bush and to whom divine works and honors are therefore attributed, must
be distinguished from the attendants who served as ministers. In this sense
the law is said to have been given through angels, Galatians 3:19; Hebrews
2:2, because Christ used their ministry, whether in

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 169 | IA leaf 223 | PDF p. 223]

establishing the magnificent display upon the mountain through thunder,
fire, and the sound of the trumpet, or in forming the very words. Hence
*angels* are spoken of in the plural, while the angel who is also called
Jehovah is everywhere named in the singular. The comparison established in
Hebrews 2:2 between the law and the gospel, because the law was given through
angels but the gospel through the Son, is not made simply with respect to the
primary author, who in both cases was Christ, but with respect to the manner
of acting and the ministers. In the former God willed to use the ministry of
angels, but in the latter Christ himself, incarnate, willed to speak
immediately. Therefore, just as there is a distinction between the things a
prince does by himself and immediately and those he performs through his
ministers, so the gospel is said to be more excellent in this sense, because
Christ incarnate (ἔνσαρκος) promulgated it immediately and speaks to us by
himself, whereas he willed to give us the law through Moses and the angels.

XIX. It cannot be said that the name Jehovah is attributed either to the
golden calf in Exodus 32:5 or to Micah's idol in Judges 17:3, because in both
places that name is attributed to the true God. In the former, Aaron proclaims
a feast to be celebrated to Jehovah through the calf, which was to be a symbol
of the true God according to the custom of the Egyptians, among whom Apis was
worshiped in the form of an ox. Hence in verse 4 they are called the gods who
brought the people out of Egypt. In the latter, the silver from which the idol
was made is said in verse 3 to have been consecrated to Jehovah with respect
to its end—that is, for his worship.
