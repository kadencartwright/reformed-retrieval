CHAPTER XIX.

Verse 1. Better is a poor man walking in his integrity than a rich man perverse in his lips, because he is a fool.

The sum is that piety joined with poverty is to be preferred to riches separated from piety.

Riches lay a danger before their lord and possessor because of the depravity innate in each person’s nature, by which a rich man swells and is puffed up through confidence in riches. This danger, grave in itself, is made graver by other men’s foolish opinion and immoderate estimation of the rich, which the Prophet censures and rebukes in this Proverb.

There is no gift of God which is not to be esteemed by men; and so those endowed with these gifts are to be embraced and esteemed according to the excellence and diversity of the gifts. Therefore it is not contrary to duty if a rich man is preferred to a poor man to some degree, provided they are equal in other respects. But just as all God’s gifts are to be embraced and pursued with honour, so also each

[1632 witness | PDF p. 397 | printed columns 768-769]

gift is to be valued more highly insofar as it is more excellent and better fitted to adorn the glory of God or procure the benefit of men. Hence the Apostle takes the Corinthians to task because they preferred the gift of tongues, which has more ostentation, to the gift of Prophecy, which contributes very greatly to the edification of the Church, and because they did not prefer charity to both, 1 Cor. 13 and 14.

If, when New Year’s gifts or other presents are offered to Kings, an earthenware vessel which an associate offered were valued more highly than the golden vessel which you gave, would this not burn you and sting you most sharply? How much more deservedly is God incensed when his gifts are esteemed beneath their worth?

Men therefore sin grievously in this when they place an upright poor man after a foolish rich man. In Eccles. 9 and elsewhere Solomon judges that a certain civil and political prudence, which is discerned in conveniently managing the affairs of this age, is to be preferred to riches and external power—to say nothing of that wisdom which alone deserves the name of wisdom. If, then, those who do not prefer a prudent poor man to a rich man sin, how much more grievously do those sin who do not place a godly and upright poor man before an ungodly rich man. For what will riches profit if you suffer the loss of your soul, though you gain the whole world?

Although many sin in this kind, none transgress more grievously than those who profess piety and wish to be regarded as worshippers of piety. For just as one skilled in military discipline is blamed more than everyone else if he does not esteem a brave and warlike soldier according to his merit, so citizens of the Church are most justly condemned if they take greater account of riches than of piety. Hence comes that sharp rebuke of James in chapter 2. By contrast, it is counted to Moses for immortal praise that he preferred poverty with the people of God to the treasures of Egypt, Heb. 11.

Next, another evil is met. For as a rich man is honoured beyond measure because he is rich, so if a certain perversity and foxlike cunning is added to his riches, by which he knows how to accommodate himself to everything and to serve the time as though it were a stage, they value him still more highly—at least they certainly pretend that they do—lest riches joined with fraud should harm them. By contrast, despising a poor man because he is poor, they then hold him in still greater contempt because he does not know how to dissemble or turn himself into every shape. Thus they despise a crafty and deceitful poor man who turns himself this way and that less than one who conducts himself with integrity and incorruptly.

From this Proverb let the poor learn not to lose heart. However much men hold them in contempt, they are nevertheless dear to God, the Angels, and godly men, provided they devote themselves to piety.

V. 2. Equally, that which his mind does not discern is not good, and one headlong with his feet goes astray.

Supply: from the aim which he sets before himself.

In ברגליו (*with his feet*) there is a metonymy of the subject and a synecdoche of the part. For by the feet he understands every undertaking of a business, or the execution of a matter designed in the mind. There also seems to be a metaphor drawn from the haste of feet, from which stumbling follows, and transferred to the sudden and accidental performance of some matter.

The sum is that, to conduct a matter well, there is need both of γνώσει and of σοφίᾳ—that is, of knowledge and judgment of the matter.

It is admitted that a man who lives in ignorance and lack of skill in affairs will never do anything that befits a worthy man. For, like a man deprived of sight, he wanders through uncertain places in the manner of the Andabatae, strikes against every step, or falls into a pit. Here, therefore, the Papists are held under the first charge, since they rest in the Church’s implicit faith, and especially those who did not hesitate

[1632 witness | PDF p. 398 | printed columns 770-771]

to call ignorance of the divine will the mother of devotion. Next come the Evangelicals, who confine a Christian man’s knowledge within narrow bounds, although it is required of the godly that they be full of all knowledge, γνώσει, or understanding, συνέσει, Eph. 1. 17; Coloss. 1. 9, so that we may discern from good not only evil, but even the smallest evil which bears before itself the appearance of good.

Yet it is not sufficient to know each matter unless we employ judgment and wisdom so that we may know what is to be carried out, at what time and place, with which persons, and by which means. For these are two separate and distinct gifts. In the latter of these gifts the man who is headlong in acting goes wrong.

All those who indulge their passions and allow themselves to be driven headlong by them—by anger, love, hatred, envy, and so forth—are driven into this precipice. Therefore we must keep diligent watch against these passions, lest they drive us sideways and, as it were, pierce our eyes.

The phrase is rendered *equally goes astray* rather than, as others render it, *equally sins*, because the Hebrew word embraces both senses; yet in this place the former sense seems more suitable for this reason: one who sins through rashness and lack of consideration is to be judged more at fault than one who turns from the way through ignorance, provided that ignorance is simple.

Verse 3. The folly of a man perverts his way; and his heart is indignant against Jehovah.

In דרכו (*his way*) there is a very common metaphor for designating each person’s undertakings and established purposes.

The sum is that men charge God with their misfortunes, which ought to be charged against themselves.

That men’s wickedness is the cause why matters which they manage and handle turn out badly for them is confirmed by many testimonies of Solomon and the other Prophets. In Prov. 3 it is taught that the upright man’s righteousness directs his way, and that the ungodly man falls by his own wickedness. And in Psal. 1 the highest prosperity in all that he undertakes or attempts is ascribed to the godly man who withdraws himself from every fellowship with the ungodly; but it is not so with the ungodly. See further what is written in Deut. 28 and Levit. 26 concerning the happy outcome in all things for those who fear God, and the contrary for those who cast away the bridle of the fear of God. For sin draws God’s curse upon itself. Like a moth eating garments or rust eating iron, that curse consumes our works; and like fire descending from heaven, it consumes all the works of our hands and—as is said in this place—perverts our way and purpose, making those things which are designed and undertaken by us for our good turn into evil for us.

To this folly, by which men scatter their own works and purposes, they add madness and fury when, turning their eyes away from themselves and referring the cause of their evils to God, they become angry and indignant with him. See Isa. 59. 11: we roar like bears and moan like doves. See Rev. 16. 21. See an example of this matter in Adam and Eve.

They are indignant with God not only when they are borne directly against God, but also when they are borne obliquely against his servants, Ministers, and Magistrates. See Exod. 16, where Moses openly pronounces that Israel’s murmurings against himself and Aaron were directed and hurled against God himself. Nor indeed does this happen only when indignation is directed against men, but even when it is directed against beasts, as we read of Balaam. He raged against his ass and loaded it with blows—and was then going to kill it—although the fault rested with himself, because the Angel opposed him in the way, Num. 22. What of the fact that the perversity of human disposition is such that against things

[1632 witness | PDF p. 399 | printed columns 772-773]

which are even inanimate, if men have received some harm through them because of their own carelessness, they blaze up, throw those things away, break them as though to take vengeance upon them, or cast them into the fire? To these must be added those who cast all their evils upon the sky, fortune, or the planets, and say that they were born under an unlucky planet.

These things must be observed by us all the more because the godly are sometimes taken in by Satan’s deceit and the weakness of the flesh. Thus we read of David in 2 Sam. 6, who blazed up because God had made a breach in the transport of the ark, although this came about through his own and others’ ignorance: he had directed that the ark be carried and transported not upon the shoulders of the priests but upon a cart. We see the same thing happen in Jonah throughout the whole of chapter 4. The same befell Job, who, in cursing the most excellent creatures of God, was borne obliquely against God himself.

But here someone may ask why they should not be borne against God, since God, who governs all things and directs them according to his will, is the author of this unhappiness: “[nor is there evil in the city which the Lord does not do].” I answer that God indeed does this, but does it as a just judge, and so the highest just and due praise is owed to him on that account; so far is it from being the case that men ought to be indignant with him for that cause. Therefore what is written in Lament. 3, from verse 27 to 43, and especially verses 39. 40. 1. 2. 3, is memorable.

We must beware, however, lest we deceive and flatter ourselves because of the prosperous outcome of matters which we have undertaken, since it very often happens that the way of the ungodly is prosperous. See Psal. 37 and 73; Ioh. 21; and Luke 13.

V. 4. Wealth adds many friends; but a poor man is separated from his friend.

In הון יסיף (*wealth adds*) there is a metonymy of the effect. In דל (*poor*) there is likewise a metonymy of the adjunct: namely, *a slender man* and *a poor man* for slenderness and poverty.

The purpose is that neither ought the rich to take airs from πολυφιλίᾳ, a multitude of friends, nor ought the poor to lose heart because they have been deprived of friends.

The rich, surrounded by a crowd of friends, suppose that they and their virtues procure this favour for them and lift them up to men’s admiration. Hence they take airs, grow proud, and think something greater and more magnificent of themselves than the truth allows; but they are grossly deceived. For in this Proverb Solomon teaches that it was riches, not virtue, that caused them to be held in affection and delight, so that all fly together to them as eagles to prey and ravens to corpses. By that very fact they must take care not to swell up because of the abundance of all things.

At the same time he warns the rich how unreliable and unstable is that friendship which is highly esteemed among them. A distinguished example of this is found in the commonwealth and Church of Israel. Just as she lived in the highest praise and admiration while her affairs were prosperous, so by contrast, when they were sad and afflicted, she sat solitary, abandoned by all by whom she had once been cherished and deprived of friends. What is more grievous, her friends were turned into enemies. Add Job’s example here.

By contrast, the poor, either furnished with no friends or deserted and cast aside by their own, generally enter into this reasoning with themselves: they suppose that their persons are held in contempt and that, even by searching, no gifts of God can be found in them which might place them in favour among men. Hence they think abjectly of themselves and their gifts, and this often drives those thoughts toward despair. But they ought to consider that it is not they themselves, but their slender means and poverty, which are trodden and trampled underfoot; and that they are deserted by their own people just as granaries are deserted by mice when, empty of grain, they are no longer frequented, however splendidly constructed they may be.

It must further be reckoned among a poor man’s advantages

[1632 witness | PDF p. 400 | printed columns 774-775]

that by this poverty of his he is sufficiently well fortified and armed against false and pretended friendships and against dangerous and destructive flatteries, to which the rich are liable. To this is added that, if poor men are infected with some fault, they are freely and confidently admonished about those faults of theirs under which they labour. First, enemies are restrained by no fear from doing this. And if anyone, moved by the duty and conscience of a good man, is ready to admonish him, he is hindered by no obstacles of power or terror placed in his way from performing that duty. With the rich, however, it is far otherwise. Although they have a few enemies who practise hostilities with them, those enemies are nevertheless constrained by fear from casting their crimes in their teeth. But those who are friends, since they love for the sake of advantage and are friends not so much to the men as to their riches, will take sufficient care not to say anything that might offend the rich man. Indeed, they even venture to hear wrongdoing spoken of favourably.
