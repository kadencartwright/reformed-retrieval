CHAPTER III. On the Marks of the Church.

THESIS I.

The fifth question concerns the marks of the Church, book 4.

Bellarmine. *The controversy concerning the marks of the true Church is most
useful.*

Protestants. I. It has no use at all if what Bellarmine asserts in book 3,
chapter 2 is true: *The Church is a body of people so visible and palpable as
the body of the Roman people, the Kingdom of France, or the Republic of
Venice.* For it would be superfluous and altogether ridiculous to dispute
carefully about the marks of the Kingdom of France or the Republic of Venice.
II. No use

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n262 | printed p. 45]

can it have (on Bellarmine's position) among those who know what the Christian faith and
the sacraments are and who the Roman Pontiff is, because from these they have
a definition of the Church and therefore a certain knowledge of it—provided
that the Church is, as Bellarmine contends, *a body of people bound together
by profession of the same Christian faith and communion in the same
sacraments, under the government of legitimate pastors and especially of the
Roman Pontiff* (*ibid.*). III. It has no necessary use according to
our position, at least with respect to the whole Church militant, because when
we define the Church by true faith, no one can fail to know that the outward
or visible mark of that Church is the outward profession of that true faith.
IV. With respect to a particular Church instituted by Christ, there is no use
for this dispute about marks before we have defined the Church itself that is
to be marked; this is not without cause lacking in Bellarmine. Therefore, the
controversy about marks is not of great importance in itself. Yet because of
the perversity of the Pontificians, who place everything in externals, we must
briefly consider how much truth and usefulness those things Bellarmine teaches
about these marks contain.

2. Bellarmine. *A mark ought to be proper to the thing to be distinguished,
not common, not even in the opinion of others. 2. Marks ought to be better
known than the thing of which they are marks. 3. They ought to be
inseparable.*

Protestants. A mark sometimes means almost the same as a sign or argument by
which something can be made known. But because those arguments that are
proper to a thing and easier to perceive are especially fitted to make it
known, by common approval those arguments in which these conditions occur are
not improperly called marks. Yet I. the qualification *in the opinion of
others* is introduced badly and sophistically, because if the properties of
things had to be measured by this or that person's opinion, we would have
nothing certain about them. II. It is ambiguous to say that *marks are better
known than the thing marked*, because to this or that person, in this or that
way, things may be both more and less known by a distinct or confused
knowledge.

3. Bellarmine. *The true preaching of the Gospel, the legitimate
administration of the sacraments, and legitimate discipline are in no way
sufficient marks of the Church. 1. Because according to the heretics they do
not show which is the true Church except probably, but rather where the Church
lies hidden.*

Protestants. I. We do not employ these marks to identify the true Church
militant with certainty and necessity with respect to its inward essence, but
to designate a visible body that is a particular church formed according to
Christ's institution. II. These marks, however, show both what this church is
and where it appears, not where it lies hidden, and this with certainty, not
merely probability. III.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n263 | printed p. 46]

By this reasoning these marks also show us at the same time that visible body
which we ought to regard as a
true part of the Church militant. IV. These very marks are confirmed by
Bellarmine as especially essential when he defines the Church from them in
book 3, chapter 2: *It is a body of people bound together by profession of the
same Christian faith and communion in the same sacraments, under the
government of legitimate pastors.* For the primary part of the public
profession of the Christian faith is the preaching of it when approved and
received.

4. Bellarmine. *Sincere preaching of the truth is a mark most common to all
sects, at least in their own opinion. For what sect ever existed that did not
say it possessed sincere preaching of the truth?*

Protestants. I. By this excellent invention Bellarmine could mock Christ the
Lord when he gives a mark—or, as Nazianzen calls it, a character—of a
Christian, John 13:35: *By this everyone will know that you are my disciples,
if you have love for one another.* For what whitewashed hypocrite would not
say that he loves those like himself? II. By this reasoning Bellarmine also
refutes his entire definition of the Church, apart from that tail about the
Pope which he gratuitously sewed onto it. For what sect would not say that it
possessed profession of the Christian faith and communion in the sacraments
under the government of legitimate pastors? Will he therefore say that his
definition is most common? Just as the Apostle John interprets that mark of a
disciple of Christ as love that is not *in word or tongue alone, but in deed
and truth*, 1 John 3:18, so also the preaching of the Gospel, together with the
other marks of the Church, ought to be understood not according to people's
opinion and sayings, but according to the thing itself in truth.

5. Bellarmine. *No one can know where the word is truly believed; therefore,
neither can anyone know where it is so preached that it is also received.*

Protestants. The subject here is properly the Church with respect to that
visible state which it has in a perceptible body. True and inward reception of
the word by every member of that body is not necessarily required for this,
but an outward profession of such reception is. According to this profession,
however, we ought also to determine and pronounce upon inward reception.

6. Bellarmine. *Which is the true Church is better known than which is the
true preaching of the word.*

Protestants. This is false in every respect. For I. the word is the cause of
the Church, in which respect it is called her *seed and foundation* in
Scripture; but in the order of nature a cause is more distinctly known than
its effect. II. In defining the Church Bellarmine himself explains her nature
by profession of the Christian faith, whose object is the word of God. Did he
then explain it by what is less known? III. With respect to our perception,
it is easier to know whether the Christian faith is publicly preached and

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n264 | printed p. 47]

received in some body than it is by any means to know whether the personal
profession of the Christian faith has been received by the members of that
body in such a way as to suffice for constituting a Church.

Bellarmine. *We learn from the Church which is true preaching. We cannot know
Scripture itself and its meaning except from the testimony of the Church. The
Church is the guardian and teacher of Scripture.*

Protestants. I. We learn from a craftsman, grammarian, logician, and so forth
what the art is that he professes, and yet we do not know the craftsman—except
by a vague, confused, uncertain, and wandering knowledge—unless we have first
understood his art. II. We can learn the true preaching of the word from God
through some individual messenger, not from the Church; and in this way the
first instruction of those who were alien to the Church is for the most part
customarily dispensed. Even those things we learn in the Church we do not so
properly learn from the Church as from God through his ministers. III. Even if
those things about the testimony of the Church which Bellarmine demands be
granted to him were true, it still would not follow that, when we understand
Scripture in some measure, we cannot recognize from its preaching received in
this or that body that this body is the Church of God. IV. It is not necessary
for every keeper of a treasure to be known before the treasure, or every
teacher before the teaching. The contrary is rather true if we speak of
distinct knowledge of the keeper and teacher insofar as they are such.

7. Bellarmine. *True preaching of the word is sometimes separated from the
true Church, as in the churches of the Corinthians and Galatians when the
Apostles wrote to them.*

Protestants. I. It can sometimes be absent with respect to degree of quantity
and purity of quality, but not with respect to the essence, substance, or sum
of the matter. II. The profession of the Christian faith which Bellarmine
places in his definition of the Church can equally be separated from the
Church. This, therefore, no more argues against the mark of the Church than
against his definition of her. III. As for what is said about the Church of
the Corinthians and that of the Galatians, that corruption belonged only to
parts, not to the whole churches, 1 Corinthians 15:12: *How do some among you
say?* It was *in becoming*, not *in accomplished existence*.

8. Protestants. John 10. My sheep hear my voice.

Bellarmine 1. *This passage does not teach where the Church is, but who are the
elect.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine previously objected against our marks that *we
cannot know from them who are elect, and from them we learn where the Church
lies hidden before we learn what it is.* Here, to the contrary, he admits that
these marks teach who are elect, but not where the Church is. II. If this mark
teaches who are elect, then it also teaches who are members of the Church, and
therefore, when it indicates where such people are, it also indicates

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n265 | printed p. 48]

where the Church is, at least with respect to her scattered members. Moreover, that
which makes and identifies a Christian person, if the same exists in a united
congregation, also makes and identifies a Christian congregation, that is, a
Church.

Bellarmine. *It cannot be known who hear Christ's voice in their heart.*

Protestants. It can be known who profess that they hear in their heart, and
that profession is a mark of the visible Church.

9. Protestants. Ephesians 5. Cleansing them with washing in the word of life.

Bellarmine. *This teaches not where the Church is, but what good God has
bestowed upon the Church; cleansing, however, is invisible.*

Protestants. I. It is plainly taught that the Church is where that good is
communicated through the word and Baptism. II. Cleansing is visible in some
way, according to the admission in book 3, chapter 9: *Baptism is holy;
profession is holy; union is holy; conduct is holy.* If all these things in
the Church are visibly holy, certainly there is some visible sanctification
and cleansing in the Church of God. The same Bellarmine, in *On Baptism*, book
1, chapter 13, contends that this cleansing takes place through visible
Baptism.

10. Protestants. Matthew 18. Where two or three are, and so forth, there I am
in their midst.

Bellarmine 1. *This demonstrates not where the Church is, but where Christ
is.*

Protestants. The body in which Christ is present is demonstrated when where
Christ is is demonstrated. That body, however, is the Church, for Christ walks
among the seven lampstands which are the seven Churches, Revelation 1:20 and
2:1.

Bellarmine 2. *To be gathered in Christ's name in any manner whatsoever is not
a mark of the Church, for it belongs to all heresies and schisms.*

Protestants. To be gathered for the true preaching, reverent hearing, and
reception of Christ's word is to be gathered in Christ's name. This does not
belong to heresies and schisms that destroy the Church.

11. Protestants. John 15. You are clean because of the word I have spoken to
you.

Bellarmine. *This passage is like the second.*

Protestants. It therefore follows that the second passage also is to be
understood of the outward speaking or preaching of the word and the outward
reception of it, not of inward reception alone.

12. Protestants. Romans 1. The Gospel is the power of God unto salvation for
everyone who believes.

Bellarmine. *The effect of the Gospel is invisible.*

Protestants. I. The invisible effect of the Gospel has many visible adjuncts
and effects. II. Either Bellarmine here denies that the Church is an effect of
the Gospel, contrary to the express words of Scripture, Ephesians 2:30 and 1
Peter 1:23, or he denies that the Church is visible.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n266 | printed p. 49]

13. Protestants. Chrysostom held the same opinion as we do, *Homily 49 on
Matthew*: *Whoever wishes to know which is the true Church of Christ, from
what can he know it except only from the Scriptures?* And Augustine, *On the
Unity of the Church*: *I wish the holy Church to be demonstrated not by human
documents but by divine oracles.*

Bellarmine 1. *We have already replied above to the passage of Chrysostom.*

Protestants. We would have had that reply presented here if he had ever made
any reply to the point that was not utterly shameful. Bellarmine does not
shrink from repetitions of one and the same reply as much as he wishes to seem
to do here.

Bellarmine 2. *It is proved from Scripture where the Church is because
Scripture teaches what her marks are.*

Protestants. I. Therefore, since Scripture makes known the marks of the Church,
it must be better known not only than the Church herself but also than her
marks. II. If Scripture teaches what the marks are, then wherever that doctrine
which Scripture teaches is preached, received, and practiced, the marks of the
Church, and therefore the Church herself, must appear there.

14. Protestants. Scripture is better known than the Church because the Church
is proved from Scripture.

Bellarmine. *Simply considered, the Church is prior to and better known than
Scripture. Yet sometimes, on a hypothesis, Scripture is better known, namely,
when Scripture is received and speaks clearly, and a question arises about the
Church herself; and sometimes conversely.*

Protestants. I. These statements are simply sophistical, because the subject
here is the word of God, or the essential doctrine contained in the
Scriptures, not its manner of existing in writing. The subject also is this or
that particular Church, not—not—the whole Church militant from the beginning
of the world to its end. II. They are also simply false. For since, as is clear
from Bellarmine's own definition, the Christian faith belongs to the essence
of the Church, and since that faith comes by hearing the word of God, the word
of God itself must be simply prior to the Church. III. A people becomes a
Church because it has the word of God implanted in it, James 1:21. The word,
therefore, is related to the Church as the abstract to the concrete, wisdom to
the wise person, and justice to the just person. But wisdom is simply prior to
and better known than the wise person, and justice than the just person;
therefore, the word is also prior to and better known than the Church. IV. If
Scripture is better known *when it is received*, as Bellarmine admits, then it
is always better known, for ever since it was given it has been and will be
received in the Church. V. If Scripture is better known in questions about the
Church, then it is also simply and universally better known. For the
Pontificians will not say that there is some other principle of faith about
which a question could

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n267 | printed p. 50]

arise that is better known than Scripture and the Church together. And
Scripture no less clearly or immediately instructs the Church concerning other
necessary matters than concerning the nature of the Church herself. VI. What
Bellarmine relates about Scripture received by the Church consists of
considerations dependent upon the condition of human beings. But when one must
deal with an unbeliever who receives neither the Church nor Scripture, anyone
who first proposed the Church to him so that from her he might know the word of
God, rather than the word of God so that from it he might learn the Church and
all necessary things, would be ridiculous. Therefore, in first reception, the
word of God is prior and better known; and certainly it does not lose its
nature or dignity by being received. It is therefore always and simply better
known and prior. VII. A question about the Church has arisen between us and
the Pontificians. Scripture is received on both sides, for Scripture is clear
about the Church, as Bellarmine admits in this place. It follows, therefore,
even on the hypothesis, that Bellarmine must admit that Scripture is better
known here and that the preaching of Scripture itself, as concerns the
character of its knowability, can be a true mark of the Church.

15. Bellarmine. *The legitimate administration of the sacraments cannot be a
mark of the Church, because everyone claims it for themselves; because it is
less known than the Church; and because true Churches sometimes do not
administer the sacraments sincerely.*

Protestants. I. All these claims are no less effective in proving that
communion in the sacraments does not belong to the definition of the Church
(although Bellarmine in book 3, chapter 2 wishes it to be coupled there with
profession of the Christian faith) than in excluding it from the number of the
marks. II. The true Church does not falsely claim the legitimate use of the
sacraments for herself, as other sects do, but uses and enjoys them in truth.
This use is better known to people than a Church formed according to Christ's
institution is. No such Church is perpetually without it with respect to her
essence. III. In this matter Bellarmine, from eagerness and zeal to contradict
us, contradicts the doctrine of all the Fathers and Scholastics concerning the
sacraments, which is thus explained by Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 1,
member 2, article 2: *When the division of the spiritual city Jerusalem from
the city of Babylon had begun in Abel and Cain, there had to be certain sacred
signs by which the citizens of Jerusalem would be distinguished from the
citizens of Babylon. So we see in other things: the sheep of one flock are
distinguished from the sheep of another flock by their proper mark; sacred
buildings are distinguished from those that are not sacred by their proper
signs; and a noble city is customarily denoted by some sign, as the city of
Rome by the cloak, military rank by the girding on of a sword, and office by
the handing over of a rod or keys. From all these things it is gathered that
sacraments were necessary for fallen humanity so that one might be
distinguished as a citizen*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n268 | printed p. 51]

*of spiritual Jerusalem, of the Lord's flock, and of his army.* From this use
which the sacraments have in marking the Church, the Scholastics took occasion
to raise those foolish questions *about the character impressed by a
sacrament*, in which they wretchedly torment and vex themselves together with
their followers.

Thus far the marks of the Church approved by us have been discussed. The
Bellarminean marks follow.

16. Bellarmine. *The first mark is the very name of the Catholic Church and of
Christians.*

Protestants. I. Two names of different meaning do not constitute one mark;
Bellarmine therefore sophistically joins *Catholics* and *Christians*. II.
Bellarmine acts here contrary to his pledge. His promise stands in chapter 2:
*We shall derive the marks of the Church from the Scriptures.* But the name
*Catholic Church* is found nowhere in the Scriptures, much less is any
characterizing force of this kind attributed either to this name or to any
other name whatsoever. III. It also follows from this that neither *Catholic*
nor *Christian*, as a name of the Church, can be a coextensive mark of the
Church, since the Church once existed without these names; but Bellarmine
requires a mark to be inseparable. IV. No name can be the proper mark of any
thing unless God promises that he will not permit it to be shared with other
things in any way. This cannot be affirmed about any single name either of the
Church or even of God himself. V. Even in present usage these names are not
proper to the Pontificians. The Eastern Churches are commonly called Catholic
in those regions; and not only they, but everyone who professes Christ's name
in any way, are called Christians. We, however, are prepared to show by sure
demonstrations that we are Christians not in name alone but also in reality,
and therefore Catholics.

Bellarmine. *There is no heresy which does not take its name from some human
being, its author.*

Protestants. I. The names of the Gnostics, Acephali, and so forth prove this
false. II. If this were true of particular heresies, yet the same reasoning
does not apply to a general apostasy such as that of the Papists. The Jews are
still called by the ancient name of that Church of which they are merely
branches torn away and cast off. This first mark marks only the poverty of the
Pontifical cause, since uncertain little words are seized upon so that the
very pillar of the Roman faith may be supported by them in a plainly childish
way. If one or another of the Fathers, taking account of places and times,
commended the name *Catholic* to simpler people, he no more intended by this
to establish

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n269 | printed p. 52]

a law for marking the Church than people of our confession now intend to do
with the name *Reformed Church*. The same Fathers equally gloried in the title
*Apostolic Church*, and yet among the other sects of heretics there was one
called *Apostolics*. Why could there not similarly have been a heresy of
*Catholics*?

17. Bellarmine. *The second mark is antiquity.*

Protestants. I. If antiquity is considered simply and in itself, it cannot be
a proper mark of the Church because it is a common property of all ancient
things, profane as well as sacred. If it is taken concretely together with the
Church herself, then it cannot be a mark because it is more obscure or less
known than the Church in herself. For it is much easier to know that the
Church exists here or there than to know that she now exists there or
elsewhere and also existed long ago, many centuries before. III. It can in no
way be an inseparable mark of the Church, because every particular Church and
even the whole Catholic Church herself had a beginning and at one time was not
ancient. IV. The most ancient Church is that which has her foundation laid in
the doctrine of the Apostles and Prophets who were from the beginning. The
Church of the Pontificians does not possess such antiquity, but ours does.

18. Bellarmine. *In every notable change of religion these six things can
always be demonstrated: 1. its author; 2. the new dogma; 3. the time when it
began; 4. the place where it began; 5. who opposed it; 6. the small body from
which it began. But none of these can be shown concerning us. Therefore, our
Church is that most ancient Church, without any notable change.*

Protestants. I. Of those six only one can be shown generally concerning all
heresies, namely, that they introduced a *new dogma*. *Philaster records a
certain heresy*, Augustine says in chapter 67, *without an author and without
a name.* Should that heresy therefore have been approved as ancient truth?
Our faith in rejecting errors must not be made to depend upon human histories
or knowledge of times, places, and people. II. Various serious errors had
crept into the Jewish Church before Christ's coming, yet Christ refuted the
dogmas themselves alone, without any consideration of authors, places, times,
and similar trifles. III. Although in many of the errors of the Pontificians
we could come at least very close to identifying authors, places, and times if
we thought it worth the effort, nevertheless a great part of that apostasy was
not introduced all at once and suddenly, but insinuated little by little and
through definite stages while the watchmen slept. Therefore, such an exact
account of the circumstances is not to be demanded in the whole change.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n270 | printed p. 53]

Bellarmine 1. *Luther also put forward his teachings little by little.*

Protestants. Luther's whole lifetime scarcely amounts to one stage of the
preceding decline.

Bellarmine 2. *Matthew 13 does not say that the tares grew while people slept,
but that they were sown, and then, as soon as they had grown, appeared and
became known.*

Protestants. It is expressly said that the tares were so sown while people
slept that they appeared, not *as soon as they grew*, but finally *after the
plant had sprouted and produced fruit*.

19. Bellarmine. *The third mark is long duration, never interrupted.*

Protestants. I. Duration does not differ in reality from antiquity. The same
arguments by which these things have just been demonstrated concerning
antiquity therefore show that it is not a proper, evident, and inseparable
mark.

20. Bellarmine. *The fourth mark is breadth, or the multitude and variety of
believers. For the Catholic Church ought to embrace not only all times, but
also all places, all nations, and every kind of person.*

Protestants. I. This breadth is not in itself proper to the Christian faith,
but sometimes belongs to error, as when, in Jerome's words, *the world groaned
and marveled that it had become Arian*; or, as Vincent of Lérins, from whom
Bellarmine took this mark, explains the same matter, *the Arian plague had
contaminated not merely some small portion but almost the whole world, so that
a certain darkness was cast over the minds of almost all the bishops of the
Latin tongue, some deceived by force and some by fraud.* It belongs to the
dominion of Antichrist, Revelation 17:1, 15: *The waters upon which the great
whore sits are peoples, multitudes, nations, and tongues.* The breadth even of
the true Church is less known than the Church herself. Nor is this breadth
inseparable from the Church. It therefore cannot be a mark of the Church. II.
If the Catholic Church ought to embrace *all times*, then Bellarmine certainly
acts deceitfully when in book 3, chapter 2 he puts it as an essential
condition in his definition of this Church that she be *under the government
of the Roman Pontiff*. For the Roman Pontiff was not the Church's pastor in
every age. III. *The universality of the Church*, says Bañez in 2, question 1,
article 10, page 109, and so forth, *can be considered in two ways: with
respect to the matter, which is the faithful from whom she is gathered; and
with respect to the form, namely, faith. With respect to the matter she is
Catholic, not because she is actually extended to every corner of the earth,
but because she excludes no one, calls everyone without distinction, and
binds everyone by her precepts, which are God's; and perhaps because she
successively extends herself to every nation. With respect to the form, or
faith, she is universal because faith extends itself to all things, ordering
a person with respect to all his inward and outward acts, and proposing a
remedy*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n271 | printed p. 54]

*against every kind of sin and purifying the whole person.* The explanation
of this latter respect, though it seems more ingenious than solid, shows that
any particular Church can be called catholic and universal even if it is
contained within one parish; and it is rightly so called if it professes that
faith which was preached by the Apostles throughout almost the whole world.
Bellarmine admits this: *If only one province retained the true faith, it would
still truly and plainly be called Catholic, provided it were plainly shown to
be one and the same with that which existed at some time or at different times
throughout the whole world.* Therefore, this mark requires nothing that we do
not demonstrate concerning our Church, nor can Bellarmine derive anything
from it for himself or against us except by begging the question in his usual
way.

21. Bellarmine. *The fifth mark is the succession of bishops in the Roman
Church, derived from the Apostles down to us.*

Protestants. I. Here Bellarmine too openly begs what is in question, for he
wishes an adjunct of the Roman Church to be a mark of the true Church. That he
does this deliberately appears from the reply in which he says to Calvin:
*Those who in Africa, Egypt, and Asia belong to the true Church cannot indeed
show a continuous succession of their own particular bishops, but they can
show the continuous succession of the universal bishops, who are the Roman
ones.* He therefore maintains that true succession in the true Church at Rome
is a mark of the true Church, not at Rome alone, but also throughout the whole
world. II. Personal or local succession, unless at least joined with the
continuation of the same doctrine, is not proper to the Church. Thus even
Bellarmine himself virtually admits that this mark is only, as they say, a
condition *without which not*: *There is no Church*, he says, *where this
succession does not exist, though it is not necessarily gathered from this
that the Church exists where there is succession.* He also concedes that the
succession of true pastors cannot continue where the bishops are manifest
heretics: *All the patriarchal Churches of Greece had manifest heretics for
long periods, and therefore the succession of the ancient pastors was
interrupted.* He therefore can conclude nothing from this succession in favor
of the Roman Church unless it is gratuitously granted to him, as he asks so
often, that the doctrine of the Roman Church is true. Nazianzen says
splendidly: *The same throne and the same doctrine; but the opposing throne and
a different doctrine.* III. If legitimate succession in the true Church is
understood, then it is plain that this is less known than the Church herself
and therefore cannot be a mark of the Church. IV. No succession of the Church
is simply necessary and altogether inseparable from her except the
continuation and propagation of the same doctrine and the

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n272 | printed p. 55]

form of administration which Christ is said to have established through the
Apostles in the first Churches. This is found least of all in the Roman
Church.

22. Bellarmine. *The Church can in no way exist without pastors and bishops.
Only those are true bishops who are shown to have descended from the Apostles
through legitimate succession and ordination. For this it is required that
someone succeed an Apostle, as Clement succeeded Peter; or someone whom an
Apostle made a bishop; or that he be made a bishop by one who possesses
apostolic authority, that is, by the Pope; and that he be ordained by three
bishops, or by dispensation by one bishop with abbots assisting. But the
Lutherans do not have such bishops; therefore—*

Protestants. I. Although the Church cannot be well constituted without
pastors, nevertheless the scattered members of the Church militant and this
or that particular Church can subsist for a time without a pastor, as appears
everywhere in the Acts of the Apostles. II. Diocesan bishops, such as
Bellarmine understands here, are neither the only pastors nor, as such, true
pastors. III. All those pastors who have been established in the Church
according to the apostolic canons delivered in the Scriptures have descended
from the Apostles. IV. Their perpetual succession from the Apostles and
apostolic men does not have to be shown from the uncertain testimony of human
histories—who succeeded whom, when, and how—but from that promise of Christ by
which he pledged that through every age he would raise up laborers to procure
the salvation of the elect. V. The power of ordaining was indeed conveyed by
people to bishops and abbots, but in respect of right it was granted by God to
every particular Church. VI. By God's grace, we have pastors descended from
the Apostles in this way and ordained by that right in all the Churches
constituted according to our desire. VII. The authority which the Pope claims
for himself in ordaining pastors is not *apostolic* but *apostate*.

23. Bellarmine. *The sixth mark is agreement in doctrine with the ancient
Church.*

Protestants. I. This mark is indeed true, and by itself sufficient for
distinguishing the true Church, if rightly understood. It is the same mark
which we approve and Bellarmine previously rejected in the *sincere preaching
of truth*, chapter 2. But Bellarmine acts very perversely and backwardly when
he wishes the doctrine of the ancient Church to be sought from the rivulets of
certain Fathers and not from the very fountains of Holy Scripture.

Bellarmine. *It is certain that during the first five hundred years the
ancient Church was the true Church and therefore retained apostolic doctrine.*

Protestants. I. This is indeed certain, but not to all

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n273 | printed p. 56]

those who have an interest in knowing the true Church. II. Nor is anyone
certain that no variation at all crept into any part of doctrine in the Church
during so many centuries. It is certain, and our Jewel abundantly shows, that
various dogmas of the Pontificians were wholly unheard of in the Church of God
during those five hundred years. What Bellarmine babbles about our confession
and known heresies consists of stale slanders worn away by frequent
refutation.

24. Bellarmine. *The seventh mark is the union of the members with one another
and with the head.*

Protestants. I. Union considered in itself is not proper to the Church. It is
also attributed to the kingdom of Antichrist, Revelation 17:13: *These are of
one mind and will give their strength and authority to the Beast.* II. Union
in the true faith is not better known than the faith itself. III. The union of
the Church has its breadth through various degrees, not all of which are
equally necessary to the essence of the Church. IV. The union proper to the
Church is spiritual and mystical, not visible or falling under the senses. V.
The union of the Papist Church with her head, the Pope, does not prove that she
is the true Church unless it is first proved that the Pope is the head of the
true Church. VI. That union which is maintained under the Pope is forced and
servile, like that of a motley army of soldiers variously disposed under one
leader or emperor. *Those who submit their own judgment to the judgment of one
person cannot think otherwise*, says Bellarmine. VII. And yet they cannot
glory in this union. The canonists on one side and the Sorbonists on the other
regard the Jesuits as false prophets concerning the very authority of the
Pope. The Dominicans also number them among the Semi-Pelagians because of the
doctrine they defend concerning grace and free will. Certain books of Becan
and Bellarmine were publicly condemned and burned in Paris. VIII. That unity
which depends upon one Pope, although not rarely several have also been
regarded as Popes at the same time, does not make the Church *one, but under
one, just as various kingdoms are under one king*, as Cajetan observes in
2.2, question 39, article 1. IX. *The unity of the Church*, as Bañez teaches in
2.2, question 1, article 10, *is chiefly placed in this: that she has one King,
God or Christ; is governed by the same laws; has the same faith, hope, and
love; the same heavenly pattern; one worship; and one communion, or
communication of the works of the members with one another. This is the unity
of that Church*, Bañez adds, *which includes all the faithful from the
beginning of the world to the end.* By God's grace we cherish this unity. It
also appears abundantly in the harmony of our confessions, although we do not
all hold altogether the same opinion in every matter.

25. Bellarmine. *The eighth mark is holiness of doctrine.*

Protestants. I. True doctrine is always holy. This mark, therefore, as concerns
the thing itself, is the same as that which we proposed and Bellarmine attacked
concerning the sincere preaching of the Gospel; nor can it be distinguished
by any certain reasoning unless truth is first demonstrated. II. This very
doctrine which Bellarmine teaches concerning the Church—to omit other
things—is exceedingly hostile to holiness, since he teaches without any
qualification that no true holiness is required for someone to be a member of
the Church and of Christ's mystical body.

26. Bellarmine. *The ninth mark is the efficacy of doctrine.*

Protestants. The coming of Antichrist has efficacy, namely, that of Satan, 2
Thessalonians 2:9. But the efficacy of the Spirit of God often cannot be
distinguished from it except by the truth of the doctrine.

27. Bellarmine. *The tenth mark is the holiness of life of the authors of our
religion.*

Protestants. Then let the lives of the Roman Pontiffs be read.

28. Bellarmine. *The eleventh mark is the glory of miracles.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine himself does not say that miracles are always
necessary for the Church's existence, but only *for a new faith or an
extraordinary mission*. They are therefore not inseparable marks of the
Church. II. They are not always necessary for an extraordinary mission, as is
seen in many prophets of the Old Testament about whose miracles nothing is
related. III. We profess no new faith or extraordinary mission such as that of
the Prophets. IV. The miracles of Christ, the Apostles, and the primitive
Church attest that Church which professes the true doctrine of the Gospel. V.
Most of those miracles in which the Pontificians glory are the lying wonders
and great signs which are said to accompany Antichrist, 2 Thessalonians 2:9;
Revelation 13:13.

29. Bellarmine. *The twelfth mark is prophetic light.*

Protestants. That light does not shine constantly in the Church and therefore
cannot be a mark. Yet Bellarmine cannot produce from among his own anyone
regarded by people as a prophet against whom, if we valued this mark so
highly, the single example of Girolamo Savonarola could not be set, upon the
testimony of Pico della Mirandola and Philippe de Commines.

30. Bellarmine. *The thirteenth mark is the confession of adversaries.*

Protestants. Therefore, that Church whose adversaries are obstinate,
malicious, venomous, abusive devils will not be the true Church.

31. *The fourteenth mark is the unhappy departure or end of those who have
attacked the Church.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n275 | printed p. 58]

Protestants. The unhappiness of such people is not always apparent to the
world. Yet if examples of this kind are diligently observed, even within a
single religion where the Pontificians persecuted our people with hostile
minds—as Thuanus noted many examples from France and our Foxe from England and
certain other places—the Pontificians will have from them something that can
serve as a not unprofitable mark, lesson, or warning for themselves.

32. Bellarmine. *The final mark is temporal prosperity divinely bestowed on
those who have defended the Church.*

Protestants. The matter does not always stand so. And when God blesses his
people in this way, it is an outcome that follows from knowledge of the Church
and a fitting defense of her, not a proper means of first knowing her.

COROLLARY.

*Christ's hidden Church cannot be known in the sensible particularity of her
members, but is reached only by the force of reason in a certain universality
of reason. The conjectural Church, within her conjecture received from signs,
admits those who do not adhere to Christ in the Spirit.* These are the words
of Cardinal Nicholas of Cusa in his letter to Rodrigo of Treviño. He wrote,
however, at the time of the Council of Basel. From them it is apparent that
the distinction of the Church into visible and invisible is not new, but was
once received by the most learned of the Pontificians in precisely the same
sense in which it is now used by us.
