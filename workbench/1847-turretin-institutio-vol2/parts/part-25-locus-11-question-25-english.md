QUESTION XXV.

THE ABROGATION OF THE CEREMONIAL LAW.

Has the ceremonial law been abrogated under the New Testament, and when and
how?

[Marginal heading: The state of the question.]

I. The ceremonial law can be considered in two ways: either with respect to
its *doctrine* and signification, or with respect to its *obligation* and
observance. The question is not whether it *has been abrogated with respect to
doctrine*. For we acknowledge that it still remains, that it is useful in many
ways among Christians, and that the mystical truth which lay hidden beneath
this bark is always the same and of perpetual necessity. Hence, on account of
this analogy, the names are always retained although the ancient state has
changed, and circumcision, sacrifices, altars, and incense are ascribed to
Christians. This is not because there ought to be a place for these rites
under the gospel, but because the truth of those figures always remains; in
that truth we possess the things foreshadowed by those signs.

Rather, the question is *whether it has been abrogated with respect to
obligation and obedience*: whether believers are still subject to the
ceremonial law as the Jews formerly were and are bound to observe it, as the
Jews contend. This was not only the opinion of the ancient Jews and is still
that of the Jews today. It was also the opinion of the Judaizing
pseudo-apostles in the time of the apostles, who urged the observance of the
ceremonies as necessary, thus rashly confusing law with gospel and Moses with
Christ. We, however, deny it with the apostles and the whole church.

[Marginal heading: The abrogation of the ceremonial law is proved: 1. from
Genesis 49:10.]

II. The reasons are as follows. 1. The Mosaic economy, of which the
ceremonial law was the chief part, had to be changed; therefore the ceremonial
law also had to be abrogated. That it had to be changed is invincibly gathered
against the Jews from the oracles of the Old Testament. First, Genesis 49:10
says that “the scepter shall not be taken away from Judah, nor the lawgiver
from between his feet, until Shiloh comes.” Here the necessity of the legal
economy, designated by the scepter and lawgiver, is asserted until the advent
of the Messiah, who is expressed by the name τοῦ שילה, as though it were
אשר לו, *he to whom the kingdom belongs*. Likewise, once he has been manifested,
the abrogation of that economy is necessarily presupposed. For if it had to
remain until Christ, there ought to have been no place for it after him. The
objections of the Jews will be refuted in their proper place when the advent
of the Messiah is discussed.

[Marginal heading: 2. From Jeremiah 31:31.]

III. 2. Jeremiah 31:31 promises a new covenant and a new law in the last days,
that is, under the New Testament: “Behold, the days are coming when I will
make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah, not
according to the covenant which I made,” and so forth. This notes not only the
truth and certainty of the change, because when a new covenant is said to be
made, by that very fact the former one is made old (Heb. 8:13). It also
indicates the *cause* of the change, namely, the violation of the prior
covenant, and its *manner*, in the internal inscription of the law. And lest
anyone object with the Jews that it is called new with respect to the
confirmation and consolidation of the old covenant, not with respect to the
institution of a new one, it is expressly asserted that it will *not be
according to the covenant which had been made with the fathers*. This
necessarily implies a diversity of covenant—if not with respect to substance,
certainly with respect to economy.

[Marginal heading: 3. From Daniel 9:27.]

IV. 3. Daniel 9:27 says that “Messiah” will “put an end to offering and
sacrifice and seal up prophecy,” which cannot be understood without the
abrogation of the legal economy. And Jeremiah 3:16–17 says, “In those days
they shall no longer say, ‘The ark of the Lord,’ nor shall they visit it,” and
so forth. If no further mention is to be made of the ancient ark, which was as
it were the center of the

[Printed p. 140 | Internet Archive PDF 168 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

ancient economy, then that whole economy had to be made old. Nor does what is
added in verse 17 about the glory of Jerusalem and the return to Canaan oppose
this position, because it must be understood spiritually and mystically of
the church, not literally of earthly Jerusalem, which was to be destroyed.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Psalm 110:4.]

V. 4. Psalm 110 promises a new priest according to the order of Melchizedek,
who was not only to arise from another tribe but also to administer his office
in another way, without successor and vicar, not for a time but forever. Hence
the apostle concludes that “when the priesthood is changed, a change of law
must necessarily occur” (Heb. 7:12–13). “For if,” he says, “perfection had
been through the Levitical priesthood, what need would there have been for
another priest to arise after the order of Melchizedek and not of Aaron?” The
same is gathered from all those passages in which the worship of God is said
to be extended throughout the whole world (Mal. 1:11; Isa. 54:1; and
elsewhere), which could not have taken place under the legal economy.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the end of the legal economy.]

VI. 5. It is proved from the end of that economy and of the ceremonies. For
when the causes and reasons for which laws are enacted change, the laws also
change. 1. That law was like a *pedagogue* who would lead to Christ and keep
believers in expectation of the good things to come until the time of the
Father’s διορθώσεως, *reformation*, or προθεσμίας, *appointed term* (Gal.
4:2), when the church was to become mature and be emancipated. But now that
Christ, who is the Father and Head of the church, has been manifested and the
church itself has been brought by him to mature age, there is no need of a
pedagogue, as the apostle proves in Galatians 4:1–3.

2. The law was a μεσότοιχον, *partition wall*, which separated the Jews from
the Gentiles. But since the distinction between Jews and Gentiles has been
removed through Christ, there is no further use for that intervening wall,
and Paul expressly testifies that Christ destroyed it (Eph. 2:14).

3. The law was a χειρόγραφον, *handwriting*, against us, not only by
uncovering sin but also by attesting the sinner’s guilt and condemnation. But
since our debt was paid through Christ and sins were expiated, the handwriting
was blotted out and taken from the midst—indeed, it was even affixed to the
cross, as the apostle elegantly says in Colossians 2:14.

4. That law possessed a shadow of future things (Heb. 10:1), because all its
rites, as was seen above, had this as their chief end: to foreshadow Christ and
his offices and benefits. But shadows vanish when the sun is present; there is
no place for figures once the truth has been manifested; and candles are not
needed when the day has dawned. These things are so ἀσύστατα, *incompatible*,
with one another that to wish to retain the shadows under the New Testament
would be to deny Christ’s advent. For they were employed as signs of one who
was to come, which could indeed be done truly before his advent but afterward
only falsely and wrongly.

5. The law was a φρουρὰ, *guard*, under which the people were detained lest
they turn toward the profane worship of the Gentiles and intermingle with
them (Gal. 3:23), through the Spirit of bondage which then prevailed. But now
believers have been called to evangelical liberty and given the Spirit of
adoption as a willing people (Ps. 110:3); they can no longer be detained under
that guard.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the change of things, persons, and places.]

VII. 6. The persons, things, and places which were required under the
ceremonial law for its exact observance had to be changed and have in fact
been changed. Therefore the law itself had to be abrogated, as it has been.
With respect to *persons*, the law was given only to the Israelites and did
not regard other nations. But now all peoples and nations are called to the
economy of saving grace, and that people is rejected. To this pertain the
various passages of the prophets which speak of the rejection of the Jewish
nation and the calling of the Gentiles, such as Deuteronomy 32, Leviticus 26,
Hosea 2, Isaiah 2, 49, 54, 55, 66, and so forth. Formerly only one tribe,
namely the Levitical, was admitted to sacred ministry; but now there has
arisen

[Printed p. 141 | Internet Archive PDF 169 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

another priesthood according to the order of Melchizedek from the tribe of
Judah, and priests are taken not only from the Jews but also from the Gentiles
themselves (Isa. 66:21; 1 Pet. 2:5; Rev. 1:6). Formerly sacrifices were
external and carnal, but now spiritual sacrifices are required (Rom. 12:1;
Mal. 1), and God declares more than once that he rejects and abhors the
ancient ones (Isa. 1:12; Hos. 6:6; Mic. 6; Pss. 50 and 51; Isa. 58 and 66).
Indeed, Ezekiel 20:25 calls them *statutes that were not good, by which a
person could not live*: both in themselves, with respect to their inability
to justify and sanctify, and in relation to the Jews’ abuse, because they
separated ceremonies from moral worship.

Formerly sacred worship was attached to a certain part of the world and to
one place, outside which it was not lawful to perform sacred rites. But now
the Lord’s temple is open everywhere in the catholic church; it is lawful to
lift pure hands to God everywhere (1 Tim. 2:8); and true worshipers are no
longer bound to present themselves at Jerusalem or on Mount Gerizim to perform
sacred rites, because they can worship God everywhere in spirit and truth
(John 4:21, 23). Hence an altar is said to be erected in Egypt (Isa. 19:19),
which would have been unlawful under the Old Testament; under this altar
religious worship is described. Incense is also foretold as being offered to
God in every place (Mal. 1:11). These things could not have occurred under the
law.

Thus Eusebius, in *Demonstration of the Gospel* 1.3, soundly proves that the
observance of the ceremonial law was impossible after the calling of the
Gentiles. Nor can it be conceived that God, the supremely wise legislator,
wished to enact such a law for nations remote from the place chosen by the
Lord, as though God wished the Indians, the Europeans, and the Britons,
separated from the whole world, even today to journey to Jerusalem
three times every year. What city—for I will not say what temple—indeed, what
region could be capable of receiving them all? What altars could suffice to
receive the sacrifices? What region could supply sacrifices for so many
worshipers?

[Marginal heading: 7. From the destruction of the temple.]

VIII. 7. Not only did that law, together with the whole Mosaic economy, have
to be abolished *de jure*; it has also been abolished *de facto*. For the
temple has been destroyed and Jerusalem, which was the seat of the sacred
rites, leveled to the ground, while the entire nation has been miserably
scattered and dispersed throughout the whole world. It is therefore
impossible to observe that law according to God’s prescription, because there
is neither altar nor temple where sacred rites may be performed, nor is there
any priesthood or sacrifice, any arrangement of the ancient polity, or any
distinction of tribes and families. And this has indeed been so for many
centuries, which divine providence would never have permitted if those
ceremonies ought to have remained in force.

Nor can the Jews answer here that these are indeed signs of an angry God,
which they suffered at other times, as in the Babylonian and Egyptian
captivities, and that just as they were delivered from those, so they can also
be recalled from this latest exile. For the present dispersion of the Jews
differs immensely from their other captivities. The time of those captivities
was determined, but the time of this one is not; and the period which has now
elapsed surpasses the time established for the others not twofold or fourfold
but tenfold—not seventy years or one or two centuries, but sixteen centuries
now elapsed. In the other captivities Jerusalem and the temple still stood,
although the temple was damaged; but now they have been entirely leveled to
the ground. In the others the distinction of tribes remained, but here no
true distinction can be given, whatever the circumcised may prattle. In the
others prophets and priests were not lacking, but now they have entirely
ceased.

[Marginal heading: 8. From the synodical decree in Acts 15.]

IX. 8. The synodical decree in Acts 15 openly confirms this abrogation when
it determines that nothing further is to be imposed upon Christians beyond
abstinence from what is strangled, from blood, and from things offered to
idols (vv. 28–29). From this it is clearly gathered that, with these three
exceptions, all other ceremonies and the observance of the ceremonial law are
abrogated, including the use of circumcision, which the pseudo-apostles urged
and which was the occasion of the Jerusalem Council. The reason why this

[Printed p. 142 | Internet Archive PDF 170 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

decree was made and how it is to be understood will be stated below.

[Marginal heading: The manner of abrogation is noted. *De jure*.]

X. The *manner and time* of this abrogation must now be examined. Its *manner*
is considered, first, *de jure*, when the force of the law and the foundation
of its obligation are taken away. This occurred *through Christ’s death*, in
which he blotted out—and did not merely blot out but completely tore up—the
handwriting against us, since full payment of our debts was made (Col. 2:14).

[Marginal heading: *De facto*.]

Second, it is considered *de facto*, when the law actually and in fact ceases
to bind and is abrogated with respect to human beings. The former occurred all
at once and at one time; the latter successively and by degrees. In the former
the abrogation occurred *meritoriously and causally*; in the latter,
*formally and effectively*.

XI. Hence three periods of the ceremonies must be carefully distinguished:
the first in which they were *living*, the second in which they were *dead*,
and the third in which they were *deadly*. In the first, by reason of *divine
institution*, they were not only lawful but *useful and necessary* under the
Old Testament. In this sense circumcision is celebrated as the seal of the
righteousness of faith (Rom. 4:11), and a great privilege is attributed to it
(Rom. 3:2), which can by parity be applied to the other ceremonies.

In the second, by reason of συγκαταβάσεως, *accommodation*, and *human
toleration*, although already abrogated *de jure* through Christ’s death, they
became dead and indifferent. Nevertheless, they could still be observed for a
time to gain the weak Jews, provided this was done from charity alone and not
from necessity.

In the third, with respect to the *abuse of the Jews and pseudo-apostles*, by
whom they were urged under the gospel as necessary for salvation with an
opinion of merit, they became lethal and deadly. With respect to the first
period, they are extolled as necessary from God’s command. With respect to the
second, they are depreciated as things of no value and indifferent (1 Cor.
7:19; Rom. 14:3–4). With respect to the third, they are condemned as
destructive and harmful (Gal. 5:4). The first extends from the sanction of the
law to the time of διορθώσεως, *reformation*, and Christ’s death; the second,
from Christ’s death to the full manifestation of the gospel and Christian
liberty; the third, from the promulgation of the gospel and the destruction
of the temple to the end of the world.

XII. This abrogation, however, was not a simple *abolition*, through which
something so ceases to be that nothing succeeds it. Rather, it was a
*consummation* and perfection (Heb. 8:6), in which something more perfect
succeeds that which is imperfect, as the light of the sun succeeds the dawn
and mature age succeeds infancy. Thus circumcision ἐν γράμματι, *in the
letter*, was succeeded by circumcision in the Spirit; the brute victims of
bulls and goats by the heavenly and rational victim, namely, the Lamb of God;
an offering made by a mere man, and that a sinner, by the offering made by
Christ, the most holy θεανθρώπῳ, *God-man*; earthly things by heavenly things;
a mortal priest by the immortal and heavenly priest; a worldly, handmade
sanctuary by a heavenly and ἀχειροποίητον, *not-handmade*, sanctuary; purity
of the flesh by purity of conscience; and carnal and external worship by
spiritual λατρεία λογική, *rational worship* (Rom. 12:1; 1 Pet. 2:5).

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XIII. What belongs to divine natural law is immutable, since it is founded
upon the immutable holiness of God. But what belongs to divine positive law
is not likewise immutable, because it is founded upon the decision of the
legislator, who, just as he gave the law for reasons of his own, can also
change it if he wishes. The ceremonial law does not belong to natural law but
only to positive law; it was not commanded because it was just, but is just
because it was commanded.

XIV. The *constancy and immutability of God* do not conflict with the
abrogation of the ceremonial law, because he did not give it to remain
forever, but only for a time. Thus the law was changed by the immutable will
of God, who neither began to will what he had not willed nor ceased to will
what he had willed, but fulfilled the counsel which he had undertaken: to
govern his underage church by such a law for a fixed time,

[Printed p. 143 | Internet Archive PDF 171 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

until, once Christ was manifested, it would be succeeded by the evangelical
economy which would govern the mature church.

XV. Nor does the fact that *circumcision* is called an *eternal covenant*
(Gen. 17:7), and that the law was to endure forever, conflict with this
abrogation. For the *perpetuity* is understood not as absolute, but as
*limited and periodic*. It is so designated because it had to endure not only
for a few years but throughout the whole time of the Mosaic economy until the
διόρθωσιν, *reformation* (Heb. 7). Thus elsewhere the word עולם is often
taken for a long but nevertheless limited duration. For example, 1 Samuel
1:22 mentions the ministry of the Levites as perpetual, although it was only
fifty years; and in Exodus 21 a servant who bound himself to his master until
the Jubilee was said to serve לעלם, *forever*. It can also be said that the
ceremonies are called perpetual not with respect to the *signs* and in
themselves, but with respect to the *things signified* and the spiritual truth
foreshadowed by them, which remains the same forever. Hence circumcision is
said to be established in Christ (Col. 2), and the true Passover is given to
us in him (1 Cor. 5:7).

XVI. Nor do the prophetic oracles conflict which seem to promise the
restoration of the legal economy under the Messiah, together with a return to
the homeland and the renewal of Jerusalem and the temple (Deut. 30:1–5;
Ezek. 37:24–25; 40; 41; and so forth). For, besides the fact that various of
these prophecies can be referred to the return from the Babylonian captivity,
so that it is not surprising if they treat the restoration of the temple,
altar, and the other things pertaining to them, if they are understood of the
state of the church under the Messiah—as it is certain they must be extended
that far for their full sense to be obtained—the expressions must not be
pressed literally. They are symbolic, not proper; typical, not literal; to be
explained spiritually, not carnally.

Israel is to be restored not according to the flesh and letter but according
to promise and Spirit (Rom. 9); the holy city is not Jerusalem but the church;
and the worship to be renewed is not carnal but spiritual. Nevertheless, in
keeping with the condition of the times and the capacity of the nation, it is
designated by carnal worship, to which the Jews were especially attached. In
this way the prophets call prayers incense, the conversion of the Gentiles an
ascent to Jerusalem, adoration an offering of incense, and the knowledge of
God visions and dreams, and so forth.

Therefore, whenever the prophets speak of the people’s return to the homeland
and its restoration, this can be understood partly literally, of the return
from captivity, and partly mystically and symbolically, of the constitution
of the New Testament church, which is described in legal terms. That they
cannot be understood simply according to the letter is sufficiently evident
from the fact that these things neither have been fulfilled thus far nor can
be fulfilled, since the temple was once destroyed, Jerusalem devastated, and
the nation dispersed.

XVII. If the apostles observed the ceremonies after Christ’s death and
resurrection, they did so not from necessity but from charity, to accommodate
themselves to the weakness of the Jews. They did so partly to show that they
were not opposed to the law of Moses, as malicious people wickedly slandered
Paul; partly to gain the Jews for Christ (1 Cor. 9:20); and partly to bury the
synagogue with honor. This is clearly established even from the fact that,
although they wished in dealing with weak brothers to use the ceremonies for
a time, they nevertheless consistently repudiated them when dealing with
pseudo-apostles and obstinate adversaries. Paul, who wished Timothy to be
circumcised (Acts 16:3), nevertheless refused to circumcise Titus when
disputing against the false brothers who lay in wait for his liberty (Gal.
2:3–4), lest he give them an occasion for caviling; and for the same reason he
severely rebuked Peter for Judaizing. See Augustine, *To Jerome*, Letter 19.

XVIII. *Paul’s vow* concerning the shaving of his head (Acts 18:18) was not
an *act of religion*, as though he had promised God something from the law as
a part of necessary worship. Rather, it was an act *of charity*, by which he
accommodated himself to the weak, showing that he was not a despiser of the
law but had “become all things to all people” (1 Cor. 9):

[Printed p. 144 | Internet Archive PDF 172 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

“not by deceitfully practicing all the evils of human beings, but by applying
the diligence of a compassionate physician to all the evils of all others as
though they were his own,” as Augustine says.

XIX. It is one thing for there to be some external rites in the church, but
another to retain the ceremonial law. External rites are necessary in the
church for the sake of good order, because all things in it must be done
honorably and decently (1 Cor. 14:40). But they differ greatly from the
ceremonial law, because that law signified Christ who was to come, while these
rites do not. That law was a necessary part of divine worship; these rites are
only aids and adjuncts of worship. The following cautions must always be
observed regarding them. 1. No rites are to be prescribed with an opinion of
necessity and merit. 2. They are not to possess an obligation of conscience
equal to divine laws, as though damnable guilt were incurred by any violation
of them, even if it occurred without contempt for the one commanding and
without scandal to others. 3. Such rites are not to be multiplied so that
Christians are thereby oppressed with a servile yoke and brought back, as it
were, into Jewish bondage.

XX. The apostolic decree in Acts 15:20 concerning abstinence from what is
strangled and from blood belonged not to perpetual but to temporary law. This
is proved: 1. From the *end and purpose* which was the cause of its
institution. That end was temporary, namely, the peace of the church through
toleration of the weak from among the Jews, who, being accustomed to the
ceremonies, had to be drawn away from them gradually. 2. From the *manner of
the decision*, in which the Gentiles are favored by being released from the
ceremonies of the law, while an effort is made to satisfy the Jews by
commanding certain ceremonies to the Gentiles. Thus for the sake of harmony
something was conceded to the Jews until Christian liberty became more fully
known, especially because the Jews professed themselves chiefly averse to the
Gentiles on this point. 3. Because every distinction of foods has been
entirely abrogated under the New Testament, as is evident from Romans 14:14, 1
Corinthians 8:8 and 10:27, Colossians 2:21, and 1 Timothy 4:3.

The argument presses all the more forcefully for the following reasons. 1.
Paul himself, who was present at the synod and best grasped and explained its
mind, wrote these things after the synod. How, then, could the apostle so
clearly and expressly remove such a distinction without any exception if he
considered the apostolic decree to be of perpetual law? Indeed, since a later
law derogates from an earlier, we must not doubt that he regarded this law as
abrogated or as to be abrogated. If its decree is renewed in Acts 21:25 after
the letter to the Corinthians was written, it must be understood only as an
apostolic institution and ordinance for a fixed time, not forever. 4. The
judgment and authority of the church, once Christian liberty was more fully
uncovered, gradually changed and abrogated that law. On this see Augustine,
*Against Faustus* 32.13.

XXI. The perpetuity of this decree is not proved: 1. Because *it was made by
apostolic authority*, for the apostles also decreed matters which pertain to
εὐταξίαν, *good order* (1 Cor. 14:33). 2. Because it was decreed under the
title of *necessity*, for the necessity is not absolute and simple but
relative and hypothetical, in keeping with the circumstances of the time and
for the sake of avoiding scandal. 3. Because it is joined with the prohibition
of fornication, which certainly belongs to perpetual law. For this can be
understood not of fornication itself but of a harlot’s food or the price of a
harlot, so that the word ἀλισγήματος, *pollution*, is to be repeated
ἀπὸ κοινοῦ, *in common construction*, which denotes the pollution of food and
drink from Daniel 1:8.

And if it is referred to fornication itself, it does not immediately follow
that it belongs to the same class, because disparate things are often joined,
as are moral and ceremonial matters in Ezekiel 18:6, Luke 1:6, and 1 Timothy
3:2. Moreover, things offered to idols are treated here, and abstinence from
them is not of the same necessity as abstinence from fornication, because it
is sometimes lawful to eat things offered to idols, but it is never lawful to
fornicate. Moral matters can therefore be joined with positive matters,
especially when such things are considered by human judgment to belong to the
same class, in the way that

[Printed p. 145 | Internet Archive PDF 173 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

fornication was regarded by pagans almost as an indifferent matter, or at
least as a very slight fault, especially among the Greeks, among whom, as the
comic poet says, to fornicate was no disgrace. 4. Nor is perpetuity proved
because this law was given before Moses (Gen. 9:4), for many ceremonial
things already prevailed before Moses, such as sacrifices and circumcision.
5. Nor is it proved because the reason of the law is moral and perpetual,
namely, lest people accustomed to human blood should become more cruel. For a
moral reason does not always prove a law to be moral. Thus the observance of
the seventh day has a moral reason from God’s rest, but is nevertheless not
moral. Add that this reason contained something forensic accommodated to the
hardness and ferocity of the Jewish nation and to the circumstances of that
time, when before the flood people had savagely run riot in murders and
robberies (Gen. 6).

XXII. 6. Finally, neither is perpetuity proved because this decree was
confirmed by the Synod of Gangra, canon 2, the Second Council of
Constantinople, canon 67, and Emperor Leo in the fifth century, and because it
was long observed in the Christian church. Hence Tertullian, in *Apology* 9,
reports among the trials of Christians that bladders distended with blood were
customarily brought near them. Thus today the Armenians observe that law, and
there are some among the Protestants who judge that it should be retained.
For human authority should not outweigh divine authority, which the ancients
lack here. The longer use of this law arose from a perverse exposition of
this passage and must be charged to the ignorance and κακοζηλίᾳ, *bad
imitation*, of those who did not know or did not wish to distinguish things
instituted for the sake of something else and κατὰ τί, *in a certain respect*,
from those which must be observed simply and for their own sake.
