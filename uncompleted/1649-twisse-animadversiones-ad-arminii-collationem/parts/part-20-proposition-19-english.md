PROPOSITION NINETEEN.

ARMINIUS. Besides all this, it seems necessary to consider that, since predestination, preterition,
and reprobation posit nothing actually in the predestined, the passed-over, and the reprobate, the
subject of execution in reality and of the decree in the divine mind is entirely the same and
considered in the same manner. Therefore, since God does not actually communicate grace except to
a sinner—namely, the grace prepared by predestination—does not actually pass over anyone except a
sinner, and does not condemn or punish anyone except a sinner, it seems to follow that God
determined to impart grace only to a sinner, to pass over only a sinner, and to reprobate only a
sinner.

JUNIUS. Before I speak about the matter, it is necessary to recall the equivocation which we noted
in the second proposition. Throughout this writing, “to reprobate” means to condemn, and
“reprobation” means condemnation. For me, however, reprobation and preterition, or non-election,
are the same.

You say that predestination and so forth posit nothing actually in the predestined. That is, you
say that the predestined or elect, the passed-over, and the condemned are elected, passed over, and
condemned by God without any consideration of a quality which is in them. But from Scripture I
think the rationale of these things is different. Election and non-election indeed posit nothing
in the elect and passed-over, but condemnation presupposes the sin for which the sinner is
condemned. I readily agree, therefore, that in these matters the subject ought to be considered in
entirely the same manner in reality and in the mind. For the elect is elected and the reprobate is
passed over as a human being, but one is condemned as a sinner. He who is elected or passed over as a
human being in the execution was likewise elected or passed over in the mind of God; he who is
condemned as a sinner was likewise predestined. Otherwise God’s internal and external acts would
differ, which must never be admitted.

You say that God does not communicate the grace prepared by predestination—that is, saving
grace—except to a sinner, and does not pass over anyone except a sinner. If, in the first place,
you say this generally and without qualification about saving grace, the salvation of the elect
angels and the preterition of the others teaches that it is false. Then, if what was said is
restricted to grace toward man, it follows from this very proposition of yours that the first man
in that state of integrity did not have a communication of saving grace. Therefore this grace is
communicated to man as man, although not as sinner—not only to man, but to an angel. If you say that
grace is communicated to man insofar as he is now a sinner, we do not deny it; indeed, we believe
that it is now communicated to no one except a sinner.

ARMINIUS. 1. I employed the word “reprobation” in its own sense, and so forth. You do not, however,
take my argument in its true sense. For I do not mean that the predestined and so forth are elected
by God without consideration of any quality which is in them, and so forth. How could I, when
everywhere I endeavor to prove that sin is a condition or quality required in the object of the
whole divine decree? Rather, this is the true sense: predestination and so forth, as actions which
remain in the agent or are internal, of themselves bring no passion into the external object; but
the execution of those internal acts, which consists of external acts, passes into external things
and posits some effect in them, as Thomas explains, and so forth. From this it is evident, to use
the scholastic expression, that to posit something in the elect and so forth is one thing, and to
presuppose something is another. But if those internal acts posit nothing in the object, it then
follows that the object is entirely the same and considered in the same manner both by God when he
decrees and when he executes. From this I conclude: because it is certain that by an external act
God does not communicate the grace prepared by predestination except to a sinful human being, does
not by an external act pass over anyone except a sinner, and does not by an external act condemn
anyone except a sinner, it follows that he does not prepare grace except for a sinner, did not
determine to pass over anyone except a sinner, and did not predamn anyone except a sinner. That is,
the internal act of predestination, preterition, and predamnation is concerned only with man
considered as sinner.

2. Neither of your two arguments presents any obstacle to this truth. To the first I reply that we
are not dealing with the predestination and reprobation of angels. To the second I reply that there
is an equivocation in the expression “saving grace.” It is either sufficient or efficacious; the
expression also stands both for grace bestowed on man in the primeval state and for grace bestowed
on man in the state of sin. My proposition denies that he had this latter grace before the fall.

RESPONSE. We acknowledge as a necessary consequence what Arminius says merely seems to be the
consequence—namely, that God determined to impart grace only to a sinner, by which I mean grace
which is efficacious for salvation and destined for man. Nor, I believe, has anyone since human
beings were born ever raised a controversy about this: whether God determined to impart grace of
this kind to sinful man, that is, that man should be a sinner before God imparts grace to him. But
Arminius wants to secure the claim that sin precedes not only the imparting of grace, about which
no one doubts, but also the determination or decree to impart it. This we utterly deny. Arminius
does not strive by any sound argument to secure what he desires, but only by a crafty and confused
proposition composed of heterogeneous things. He hopes that an unskilled reader will make no
distinction among them, or, if he does distinguish them, will at least readily infer that because
sin precedes the imparting of grace in the nature of things, it must therefore precede the decree
to impart grace in God’s consideration.

The rest which he attaches is both more obscure and no more pertinent—or, if pertinent, certainly
no more true. That is, when he says, “not to pass over, not to reprobate anyone except a sinner,”
these words, I say, are ambiguous. For it is uncertain whether, just as “to impart” was posed as an
object of the determination or divine decree, “to pass over” and “to reprobate” are likewise
regarded as objects of the decree, so that the text runs: “God did not determine to pass over or
reprobate anyone except a sinner.” This is more likely according to the grammatical form of the
words, because if they were to be taken for the decree itself, they would be expressed by a
perfect, rather than a present, infinitive. And just as he said that God “did not determine,” so
here too he ought to have said, “did not pass over, did not reprobate.” But according to the action
signified by the word “reprobation,” it is not fitting that it be understood as the object of the
decree, but rather as the decree itself. For reprobation signifies the decree rather than the
execution of the decree. If, on the other hand, the words are uttered about the execution of the
decree, they are said with little pertinence. For it is beyond controversy that the execution of
the decree both of election and of reprobation, at least with respect to many of its parts,
presupposes sin. The entire question, however, concerns the decrees themselves: whether they
presuppose in God the consideration of sin. And although it is Arminius’s regular practice to
advance by reasoning from the execution of the decree to the decree itself—as though, because
condemnation presupposes sin, the decree to condemn must therefore presuppose the consideration of
sin—

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 78 | BSB canvas 90]

nothing is more feeble than this manner of arguing. For salvation too presupposes good works, at
least in adults; but no Arminian has thus far dared to conclude from this that the decree to save
therefore presupposes the consideration of good works. Nor does it follow that, because
predestination and reprobation posit nothing in the predestined and reprobate, the subject of the
execution in reality and of the decree in the divine mind is therefore considered in the same
manner. Such is Arminius’s argument: to reason, for Arminius, is nothing other than to employ the
most inconsequent consequences.

For example, predestination is the decree to confer glory. That conferral presupposes not only a
man existing in the nature of things, but a faithful man who passes his life in good works. But
since the decree to save is eternal, it precedes not only faith and good works but also the
creation of man. This difference arises not because predestination posits something in reality,
but because predestination neither posits nor presupposes anything in the predestined; whereas the
execution of predestination through the conferral of glory, just as it posits something in man,
also presupposes many things in him. In the same way, reprobation, insofar as it signifies the
decree to condemn, has condemnation for its execution. Condemnation presupposes a man who is not
only created but also corrupt, devoid of faith, repentance, and good works. But since the decree
to condemn is eternal, it does not permit even creation to precede it, much less corruption of
faith or morals. The reason for this is not that reprobation does not posit something in the reprobate,
but that, just as reprobation does not posit anything, neither does it presuppose anything in the
reprobate; whereas condemnation not only posits something in reprobate man but also presupposes
many things in him.

2. I have already indicated what I find lacking in Junius’s distinction: nowhere does he mention
the decree to condemn, which alone is opposed to the decree to save by contrary opposition.
Certainly his precision here seems to me of no importance, while matters in Arminius much more
deserving of censure he passes over without touching. As for Junius’s interpretation of Arminius’s
words—as though for Arminius “predestination posits nothing in the predestined” had meant “to be
elected without consideration of a quality which is in him”—I cannot sufficiently wonder that a
prudent theologian could either grant such an interpretation or fasten upon Arminius an opinion so
foreign to and so little in agreement with Arminius’s positions. When Junius admits that the
subject ought to be considered in the same manner in reality and in the mind, his purpose is this:
just as there was no cause of election and reprobation on man’s side insofar as election and
reprobation occur in the mind of God, so there is no cause of their execution on man’s side.

But these things are expressed with little theological precision. For election and reprobation
have no place anywhere but in God himself, since God’s decrees are only immanent acts in God. Yet
although there is no cause of the decree on man’s side, there could nevertheless be a cause of its
execution on man’s side—not, I acknowledge, of the execution as a whole, but of a particular part.
For example, the execution of the decree of election is partly the conferral of grace and partly
the conferral of glory. There is no cause at all of the conferral of grace on man’s side, but there
is a cause of the conferral of glory on man’s side, and that in accordance with the institution of
God who ordains: faith and repentance, together with perseverance in faith and repentance, are
causes of salvation, not indeed meritorious causes but merely dispositional ones. Conversely, the
decree of reprobation has no cause on man’s side why it should be made, but the execution of this
decree may have a cause on man’s side. The execution of the decree of reprobation is twofold: one
part is hardening, or the denial of efficacious grace for believing and repenting; the other is
condemnation. Although hardening may seem to have no cause on man’s side, because God hardens whom
he wills, condemnation beyond doubt has a cause on man’s side, and that in accordance with the
institution of God who ordains: final perseverance in sin is a cause of condemnation which is not
only dispositional but also meritorious.

Junius’s next statement—“Otherwise the internal and external acts would differ, which must never
be admitted”—requires explanation. God never executes anything differently from the way he
decreed to execute it, and decreed it from eternity. For God is neither changeable in disposition
nor fallible in judgment. In this sense, therefore, it must by no means be admitted that the
external act differs from the internal. But it is denied that these acts will differ whenever a
difference occurs in the subject as it is compared with the decree and with the execution of the
decree. For when the decree passes to some subject understood by God, that subject does not yet
exist at all, since God’s decrees are eternal. But when the execution of the decree is reached, it
may happen that the subject with which it is concerned not only exists but is also clothed with
certain qualities, as we have just explained.

To what follows in Junius, Arminius replies that he is discussing grace prepared by the
predestination of human beings, and grace which is not merely sufficient but efficacious.

3. No support whatever sustains, or can sustain, Arminius’s inference when he gathers from the fact
that internal acts posit nothing in the object that the object is considered in the same manner by
God both when he decrees and when he executes. That is, because grace is communicated only to a
sinner and denied only to a sinner, so that someone actually exists as a sinner before grace is
either communicated or denied to him, it must therefore be the case that someone is considered by
God as a sinner before God decrees either to communicate or deny grace to him. Again, because
someone is found to be a sinner before he is condemned, he is therefore also considered by God as
a sinner before God decrees to condemn him. By reasoning entirely parallel in every respect,
because only those who have faith and perform good works are saved by God, it will likewise be
necessary that they are not even destined for salvation unless God first considers them as having
faith and performing good works.

Although Arminius regularly clothes himself in these inferences as if they were so many
indubitable principles, without bringing forward any support to confirm them, we have nevertheless
more than once overthrown this conclusion by various arguments resting upon the support of
indubitable principles. For it would follow from this that the decree to permit sin is prior to
the decree both to save and to condemn. But we have shown by two principal arguments that this
cannot be so. For if it were granted, the permission of sin would certainly be prior in God’s
intention to salvation or condemnation. Therefore the permission of sin would assume the
character of an end, while salvation and condemnation would take on the character of means to that
end. For there is no other basis for priority and posteriority in intention than the relation of
means and end. But neither salvation nor condemnation occurs for the purpose that human sins might
be permitted. It is much more suitably affirmed that human sins are permitted for this purpose:
that some might be saved from mercy and others condemned from justice. Yet even to affirm this
does not please me at all; I judge instead that some other account must be taken of the order of
these things when compared with one another.

Again, if the permission of sin were prior in God’s intention to salvation and condemnation, it
would certainly be later in execution. That is, some would be saved and others condemned before
they were permitted to sin, according to the best-known and most certain principle that what
occupies the first place in intention holds the last place in execution. Observe in passing,
however, while I disclose the source and origin of this error and reveal that appearance of truth
which either swept these theologians sideways or gave them an occasion to thrust their false
doctrines upon their readers all the more boldly as indubitable principles. I acknowledge it to be
true that God executes nothing in time differently from the way he decreed from all eternity past
that he would execute it. For example, just as he communicates grace only to a sinner, so from
eternity he decreed to

communicate grace only to a sinner. From this it follows that sin precedes the communication of
grace, which we by no means deny. But it by no means follows from this that the consideration of
sin precedes the decree to communicate grace, which the Arminians greatly desire to secure. In the
same way, God condemns no one except a sinner, and so he decreed to condemn no one except a sinner.
From this, I acknowledge, it follows that sin precedes condemnation; but from it one will never
prove that the consideration of sin precedes the decree to condemn, although the Arminians desire
this above all. Likewise, just as God saves no adult except one who believes and devotes himself to
performing good works, so he decreed to save no adult except one who believes and devotes himself
to good works. From this, I acknowledge, it is established that faith and good works precede
salvation; but no rack will wrench from it the claim that the consideration of faith and good
works precedes the decree to save. Otherwise election would openly be established not only from
foreseen faith but also from foreseen works, which our opponents have thus far been ashamed to
affirm.

Observe in passing Arminius’s second distinction concerning grace. One grace is that which God
bestowed on man in the primeval state, and another is that which he bestows on man in the state of
sin. “My proposition,” he says, “grants that man had the former grace in the state of integrity but
did not have the latter before the fall.” That is: in the state of integrity man had the grace
which God bestowed upon him in the state of integrity, but only in the state of sin did he have the
grace which God bestowed only in the state of sin. Will Arminius seem quite sober to you when he
dictates these pronouncements as if from the professorial chair? So far as I am concerned, may the
Arminian school always have professors fit for such chairs.
