QUESTION XIX

ON THE JUSTICE OF GOD

Is vindicatory justice natural to God? The affirmative against the Socinians.

I. To God’s will belong the virtues that are customarily attributed to God,
not under the concept of a habit in the manner of human virtues, but insofar as
they denote the perfection that is in a habit and a readiness to act, although
otherwise they do not differ from the essence. They can be referred to two
principal virtues, which comprehend the others under themselves: justice and
goodness. The former is that by which God is holy and just in himself and has
a constant will to render to each his own. The latter is that by which God is
conceived as supremely good and the giver of every good.

[Marginal heading: Justice is either universal.]

II. To speak first of justice, the word *justice* is customarily used in two
ways in general. It is used either for the entire complex of all the virtues,
just as injustice is taken for any sin. This is called universal justice. By
it, just as God is most just and most holy in himself, so in all his works he
preserves uncorrupted rectitude and justice. What the poet said of human
justice belongs to him far more perfectly:

“In justice all virtue is comprehensively contained” (Ἐν δὲ δικαιοσύνῃ
συλλήβδην πᾶσ’ ἀρετ’ ἔστι).

To it belong especially kindness and beneficence, and constancy and
faithfulness in fulfilling promises, which very often occur under the name of
justice in Scripture.

[Marginal heading: Or particular.]

Or the word is used for particular justice, which renders to each his own and
is occupied with the distribution of punishments or rewards, and which is
called distributive. Yet as regards the rewards of the godly, as will be said
in its proper place, God crowns not their merits but his gifts, and gives what
is his rather than repays what is ours, as the Council of Orange, chapter 22,
says. Hence this justice is attributed to God not so much properly as
analogically. As for commutative justice, which observes arithmetic proportion,
since it consists in the equality of what is given and received and by it
ownership is transferred—neither of which can be said of God—we judge with the
sounder scholastics that it must be removed from God: “For who has first given
to him, that it should be repaid to him?” (Rom. xi. 35).

III. Again, divine justice can be considered either absolutely and in itself,
in which respect it is nothing other than the rectitude and perfection of the
divine nature, by which it has been called

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 213 | IA leaf 267 | PDF p. 267]

universal justice by us and the justice of God by others, which belongs to him
as he is God; or relatively, with respect to the going forth and exercise that
it has through the divine will according to the rule of his supreme right and
eternal wisdom. In the latter respect, two kinds can be observed. One is in
the rule and government of creatures. It is called lordly justice and belongs
to him as he is Lord and the supreme Arbiter of things, who does what he wills
with his own and directs all his actions in governing and administering
created things according to the norm of his right and wisdom. For what is law
for us in the administration of affairs is in God his own right joined with a
most wise and just will. The other is in judgments, whether rewarding or
vindicating, and the latter either for chastisement or for vengeance. This is
called vindicatory or avenging justice, with which the proposed question
deals. To understand it, certain things about it must be stated beforehand.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

IV. Once a rational creature and its moral dependence upon God are posited,
the first going forth of this justice is in the establishment of a penal law,
not simply insofar as it is law, for in this respect it properly depends upon
the justice of government, but insofar as it is penal. The second going forth
is in the very infliction of punishment. Yet punishment in turn can be
considered either as regards the thing and the very substance of punishment in
general, or as regards its manner and circumstances, which are considered in
its time, degree, or persons. Again, God’s right concerning punishment is
either supreme and rigorous, which is called strict justice
(ἀκριβοδίκαιον), or it is a right tempered with a certain equity
(ἐπιεικείᾳ). Under the former, punishment is imposed not only upon sin but
also upon the sinful person himself; under the latter, manifold moderation is
given in the exercise of justice—whether in time by delay, in persons by
transfer, or in degrees by mitigation. Hence a twofold right arises concerning
the infliction of punishment: one necessary and indispensable with respect to
sin itself, and the other free and positive with respect to the sinner.
Justice necessarily demands that every sin be punished, but it does not
likewise demand that it be punished in the very person who sins or at a
particular time and degree. The scholastics expressed this not badly when they
said that punishment is necessarily inflicted impersonally upon every sin,
but not personally upon every sinner.

V. Although we maintain that the going forth of justice is necessary, we do
not deny that God exercises it freely. For this necessity does not exclude
concomitant freedom, but only antecedent indifference. Accordingly we say that,
once a sinful creature is posited, God is indeed freely—that is, willingly and
without compulsion—inclined to inflict punishment, but not indifferently, as
though he could punish or not punish at pleasure.

VI. Hence it is also clear in what sense this justice is called natural. It is
not as though it carried physical necessity with it, but because it is founded
in God’s very nature and indeed is identified with it, although it cannot
exercise itself outwardly except through an act of the intellect and will, in
the same way as the other attributes. Although these are natural, they are
nevertheless exercised only through the free will, as are goodness, wisdom,
and so forth.

VII. This justice is considered in three ways. It is considered either by way
of power (δυνάμεως), or faculty, in which sense it is God’s very will abhorring
sinners and willing to punish them; or as activity (ἐνέργεια) itself and the
act of judgment or punishment, by which he executes his judgments upon
sinners; or as the effect itself, or the punishment inflicted by justice. We
are dealing here not with the third meaning but with the first and second,
which appear to be joined in Ps. cxix. 137: “You are just, O God, and your
judgments are just.”

VIII. The question about this vindicatory justice is raised for us by the
Socinians. In order that they may more easily destroy the satisfaction of
Christ, they deny that vindicatory justice is natural to God and hold that it
is only an effect of his most free will, which can either be or not be, for the
same reason that God was free

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 214 | IA leaf 268 | PDF p. 268]

to create or not to create the world. Socinus indeed admits that such a
justice belongs to God, by which, if he willed to employ it perpetually, he
would have the right to destroy all sinners without awaiting their repentance
and so to let no sin go unpunished (*On the Savior*, book 1, chapter 1). He
also grants that God is unwilling to remit sin except to those who repent. 3.
He does not deny that God established the punishment of sin according to the
norm of his right and wisdom. But he denies that there is any attribute in God
that necessarily demands satisfaction for sin; instead, if God punishes sin,
this is a most free effect of his will from which he can abstain. Thus Socinus,
*Lectures*, chapter 16: “For,” he says, “there is no justice in God that
commands sins to be punished without exception and that he himself cannot
renounce. There is indeed perpetual justice in God, but this is nothing other
than equity and rectitude.” And a little later: “That justice which is commonly
so called and which is opposed to mercy is not a quality of God, but only an
effect of his will.” He confirms this in *On the Savior*, book 1, chapter 1,
and book 3, chapter 1; so also Ostorod, *Institutes*, chapter 31, and Crellius,
book 1, chapter 23.

IX. The orthodox, on the contrary, maintain that this justice is an essential
property of God, not merely an effect of his free will. Nevertheless, it must
be admitted here that there is some diversity of opinions among the orthodox
themselves concerning the exercise of this justice. Some maintain that it is
necessary, while others maintain that it is free and indifferent, so that God
can abstain from it if he wills. Hence they think differently about the
necessity of satisfaction. Some make it merely hypothetical—namely, from the
hypothesis of the divine will and decree—so that God could not remit sins
without satisfaction, not because his justice demanded this absolutely and
necessarily, but because he so decreed. Twisse follows this opinion after
certain of our doctors who, before Socinus arose, spoke with less caution.
Others, however, posit an absolute necessity that depends not only upon the
decree but also arises from divine justice. Although both groups agree on the
cardinal point of the question against the Socinians, who deny the necessity
of satisfaction, it is nevertheless certain that the latter opinion, which is
now nearly common to all, is far more effective for destroying that most
pernicious heresy and more consonant with God’s nature and the words of
Scripture.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

X. The question is therefore reduced to these terms: Is God’s vindicatory
justice natural to him in such a way that, once a sinful creature is posited,
it cannot fail to exercise itself and it is repugnant to God to leave sin
unpunished? Or is it so free in God that its exercise depends upon his will and
good pleasure alone? Those with whom we are dealing maintain the latter; we
defend the former.

[Marginal heading: 1. Vindicatory justice is proved from Scripture.]

XI. Four things above the rest prove that avenging justice is essential to
God: 1. the voice of Scripture; 2. the dictate of conscience and the consensus
of peoples; 3. the sanction of the law together with the whole Levitical
priesthood; and 4. our redemption through the death of Christ. Scripture
everywhere attributes this justice to God. First, it does so in those passages
in which the praise of perfect holiness and justice is attributed to God, by
which he is said supremely to detest and abhor sin: “You are a God who by no
means acquits the guilty, visiting the sins of the fathers upon the children,”
and so forth (Exod. xxxiv. 7); “Your eyes are purer than to look upon evil”
(Habac. i. 13); and “You are not a mighty God who delights in wickedness; evil
will not dwell with you” (Ps. v. 5), and so everywhere else. Hence we infer
that if hatred of sin is necessary in God, justice is likewise necessary,
because hatred of sin is the constant will to punish it, which cannot lack its
effect in one who does not lack strength. In us, indeed, abhorrence without
punishment can often occur, either because strength is not available or
because it belongs not to private persons but

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 215 | IA leaf 269 | PDF p. 269]

to rulers and magistrates to exact vengeance. But the matter stands otherwise
in God, who is furnished with both supreme power and supreme authority.
Therefore, if he necessarily hates sin, he must necessarily punish it. That he
necessarily hates sin is evident from his love of the justice opposed to it.
For just as he necessarily loves justice so that he cannot fail to love it, so
by the nature of contraries he must necessarily abhor sin and, in abhorring it,
punish it. For just as one who loves a thing or person wills good to it and, if
he can, benefits it, so true hatred and abhorrence cannot exist without drawing
after themselves the removal and destruction of the contrary. Otherwise, if
God could fail to punish, he could fail to hate; if he could fail to hate, he
could love, which is incoherent (ἀσύστατον). Nor does the fact that he does not
punish the sins of the elect entail that he leaves them unpunished, because he
punished them in Christ.

XII. Second, Scripture does so in passages that attribute to God the relation
(σχέσιν) of a just Judge, such as Gen. xviii. 25: “Would the Judge of the whole
earth not exercise justice?” Rom. iii. 6: “Is God unjust who inflicts
punishment? By no means; otherwise how would God judge the world?” Rom. i. 18,
32: “The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and
unrighteousness of men,” who also acknowledge this to be God’s right, namely,
that those who do such things are worthy of death. And 2 Thess. i. 6: “It is
just with God to repay affliction to those who afflict you.” For since the
office of Judge and Ruler belongs to God naturally, the corrective right
(ἐπανορθωτικὸν) concerning punishment that depends upon that office must be
essential, and therefore so must the avenging justice that rests upon it.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the sense of conscience and the consensus of
peoples.]

XIII. Second, the dictate of conscience and the consensus of peoples confirm
Scripture’s testimony. Conscience does so when it places man before God’s
tribunal and either accuses or excuses him in his good or evil deeds (Rom. ii.
14, 15). The consensus of peoples does so through the persuasion that has
prevailed among all peoples, even the fiercest, that God is the just Judge of
crimes and that, if he did not exercise this justice, he would not be God.
Hence they imagined Justice (δίκην) as the daughter of Jupiter and wished her
to be placed over human affairs to avenge injuries and inflict punishments upon
those who had sinned against the gods. From where, then, comes this public
voice of conscience that reproves men and strikes terror into them? From where
come those unseen blows by which they are tormented after committing a crime,
even one known to no one, if not from the firmest persuasion of avenging
justice, through which, as they know “that God has an avenging eye” (θεὸν ἔχειν
ἔκδικον ὄμμα), they tremble and quake at his vengeance? They testified to this
public consensus in various ways, but proved it especially by sacrifices, in
which Pliny affirms that the whole world, though divided and unknown to itself,
agreed. For since sacrifices were instituted chiefly to this end, that the
wrath of the divine Majesty might be averted by them, it is evident that this
preconception (πρόληψιν) was implanted in them: the just God cannot fail to be
the avenger of crimes. And this notion put down such deep roots in their minds
that they were driven to the unspeakable crime of human sacrifice
(ἀνθρωποθυσίας), so that by a more excellent sacrifice they might obtain more
favorable acceptance from the wrathful Deity.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the law.]

XIV. Third, the moral and ceremonial law themselves establish the same thing.
For since the precepts of the former are in large part indispensable, resting
upon God’s eternal and natural right, the penal sanction added to them must be
of the same nature. This is all the more so because, as was proved above, there
is a necessary and natural connection between sin and punishment, moral and
physical evil, just as moral and physical good are joined to each other by an
indissoluble bond. The ceremonial law tends to the same conclusion, since the
whole apparatus of legal sacrifices aimed at nothing other than demonstrating
the necessity of expiating sins so that divine justice might be satisfied. Nor
would Paul so often insist that those sacrifices could not remove the guilt of
sin if it could have been removed by no necessity of justice but by God’s will
alone. For what depends upon God’s free will could have been accomplished by
the intervention of legal victims no less

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 216 | IA leaf 270 | PDF p. 270]

than by other blood. Since it was therefore impossible for the blood of bulls
to remove sins (Heb. x. 3, 4), not only because of God’s will but also from the
very nature of the matter—because sin is so foul a thing that it cannot be
washed away by any blood of goats—it follows that we must say the necessity of
such sacrifices depended not upon the will alone but upon God’s essential
justice itself.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the death of Christ.]

XV. Fourth, the death of Christ demonstrates this no less evidently. For if
God were free and entirely at liberty to punish or not to punish sin without
impairing his justice, so that no reason besides his will alone moved God to
send into the world the Son who would die for us, what legitimate reason can
be conceived for which God willed to subject his most beloved and holy Son to
a most cruel and accursed death? Does God’s supreme goodness and wisdom allow
us to believe this was done gratuitously and without some very great
necessity? Would God, who does not delight in the death of a sinner, have
delighted in the death of his most innocent Son if this depended absolutely
upon his mere choice and he was moved by no other reason to give him over to
death? Therefore, Christ’s offering of himself for us was not a work of the
bare will but of justice, which demanded this sacrifice for its satisfaction.
Hence he is said to have been “appointed by God as a propitiation to demonstrate
his justice” (Rom. iii. 24, 25), and “to have reconciled us to God through his
blood” (Col. i. 20).

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVI. It is not always permissible to waive one’s own right. It is permissible
to waive a positive and free right, but not a natural right. It is permissible
to waive the private right of a debt, which regards one’s own advantage, but
not the public right of a ruler and judge, which regards the majesty of the
laws, rectitude, and the execution of duty. Thus a magistrate would sin if,
through excessive indulgence, he waived the right of punishing sins so as to
grant impunity to crimes. But the penal right of which we speak is not a
positive right but a natural one founded upon God’s very holiness and justice.
It arises from the creature’s essential dependence, which subjects it to its
Creator both with respect to obedience by reason of his precepts and with
respect to punishment by reason of their sanction. Nor is it the private right
of a debt. Although sins are sometimes compared to debts, they are also crimes
that cannot remain unpunished without prejudice to the laws. Rather, it is the
public right of the Ruler, because God punishes not as an offended party but as
Judge and supreme Ruler of the world.

XVII. Although by not punishing sin God would wrong no one outside himself, he
would wrong his own justice, which ought not to be offended with impunity; he
would wrong the laws he has enacted, whose majesty he must preserve safe and
sound; and he would wrong the public good, whose guardian and administrator
God is. Thus he cannot deny himself or divest himself of the natural dominion
that he has over the creature, although, if he did so, he would wrong no one.

XVIII. Although the effects of justice depend upon God’s free will, it does
not follow that justice itself, as it is conceived in first act, is likewise a
free act of the will. It follows only that it is an essential property that,
in its exercise and going forth, requires the intervention of the free will,
which determines the manner, time, degree, and persons upon whom he wills to
inflict punishment.

XIX. It is one thing to punish sins from physical necessity, such as that in
fire for burning; it is another to do so from moral and rational necessity. If
God punished sins according to the former, it is true that he would always act
in the same manner and punish equally and to the fullest extent possible. But
this does not hold for moral necessity, because the exercise of this virtue
depends upon God’s most wise will, by which he can, according to equity
(ἐπιείκειαν), apply varying

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 217 | IA leaf 271 | PDF p. 271]

modifications in the infliction of punishment without prejudice to justice.
Justice indeed necessarily demands that
every sin be punished, but not that every sinner be punished immediately or
to this or that degree.

XX. Although the effects of justice and mercy can be contrary because of the
diversity of the objects with which they are concerned, it does not follow
that the virtues themselves are contrary in themselves, any more than the sun
is contrary to itself when it melts wax and hardens clay.

XXI. The basis of God’s justice and mercy in their exercise is different. For
mercy is free in every way, so that it can exercise itself or not exercise
itself without wronging anyone, because it consists in a purely gratuitous act
that God is not bound to exercise toward anyone. For whether he had taken
mercy upon us or not, he would have wronged no one, because he owes this to no
one. But although an act of justice is most free, it is nevertheless necessary
because it is owed, for it consists in rendering to each his own. And although
God properly owes nothing to the creature, and properly speaking the creature
has no right over him in this regard, nevertheless he owes this to himself and
to the preservation of the laws he has enacted, so that if he did not do it,
he would not be just.

XXII. If no sinful or miserable creature had existed, justice and mercy would
not have existed as to related being and in exercised act, but they would
nevertheless always have existed as to absolute being and in signified act,
just as omnipotence existed in God from eternity even if he had produced
nothing.

XXIII. Although God can admit some equity (ἐπιείκειαν) concerning the degree
of punishment, it does not likewise follow that he can remove every degree of
punishment, because justice has a certain range of degrees outside which, if
it departed, it would not even be justice. Although such a degree cannot be
defined by us, God nevertheless has it most precisely defined and thoroughly
known.

XXIV. The question here is not simply whether God by his power can fail to
punish sin, but whether he can do so by his justice, which is properly
concerned with punishment. Because this is repugnant to justice, it is rightly
said that God cannot do it. Although to say that sin is not punished is not a
formal contradiction in terms, it is nevertheless a contradiction in the
thing itself, because it thereby follows that God does not hate sin and can
love it.

XXV. Although by absolute right God can annihilate a creature, he cannot
likewise inflict infernal and eternal torment upon an innocent creature,
because the former is an effect of supreme dominion, while the latter
necessarily involves a relation (σχέσιν) to justice, which does not permit such
torment to be inflicted upon one who does not deserve it, just as it cannot
permit the whole punishment to be removed notwithstanding any amount of
demerit. See *Disputation on the Necessity of Satisfaction*, Parts I and II.
