1. As Chrysostom observed, throughout the continuous course of his argument
the Apostle looks to that one particular sin of the one Adam. “Through one
man sin entered the world,” verse 12. In what follows he explains which sin
it was from which all the others, and through them death, passed to all:
“through the trespass of that one,” verse 15; “through one who sinned,”
verse 16; again, “through the trespass of that one,” repeated twice in
verses 17 and 18; and finally, “through that one man,” verse 19. All these
expressions most clearly speak not of the descendants' actual sins but of
that originating and primary original sin of Adam, from which all the
remaining actual and personal sins of all men proceeded, and death from
them.

2. Verse 14 also speaks of those “who had sinned after the likeness of
Adam's transgression,” that is, actually and personally—namely infants. Yet
they nevertheless died. They therefore sinned, for “the wages of sin is
death”; but they did not sin actually in their own persons. Therefore they
sinned in another, and consequently in Adam: “upon which all sinned,” or “in
whom all sinned”; or, if they prefer, “because” or “since they sinned,” yet
still in Adam, namely as in their common stock—not

[1679 printed p. 243 | IA leaf 251]

formally but originally, seminally, and representatively, and thus by
imputation—which was to be demonstrated.

One may deny the inference: our being said to have sinned “through him” (or
“because of him,” as Erasmus speaks, if *epi* is used for *dia*, as Grotius
holds, so that “upon which” means “through whom”) does not prove that we
sinned imputatively in Adam. We can be said to sin through him if we sin
through him occasionally, as Theodoret holds; exemplarily by imitation,
according to Pelagius; or originally by propagation alone and also by
merit, as some Romanists hold—yet not by imputation, such that Adam's own
sin becomes ours and we are truly guilty of it.

I answer:

1. As for Pelagian imitation, if we considered the phrase alone, we might
quite suitably be said to sin through someone if his example caused us to
sin by imitating him. Yet that cannot fit the present matter. Adam's sin
causes some to be born sinners and to die in whom imitation of Adam's sin
has no place: infants, who lack the use of reason and have not yet learned
to imitate Adam or his sin; and many pagans who, though they are most
shameless sinners, have never even heard of Adam and his sin so as to
imitate it.

2. As for Theodoret's “occasionally,” I do not understand in what sense or
on what ground Adam and his sin could be said to have given us merely an
occasion for sinning, without real influence upon it and causality properly
so called, unless either:

He gave us an example of sin to imitate, as Pelagius held. We have already
overthrown that; and I add that even examples possess the force and influence
of a moral cause, and therefore causality.

Or Adam's sin, by its demerit, gave God an occasion to deny or withdraw from
his descendants grace, or that original righteousness with which he was
created but lost by this sin, and so became an occasion of our sinning.

But if this occurs because of the demerit of Adam's sin, then the withdrawal
of grace and our sinfulness flowing from it assume the character of a
punishment. Punishment presupposes sin. If we pay a penalty, we most
certainly sinned beforehand. God's supreme justice—and much less his
goodness—does not allow us justly to pay any penalties for a sin which is in
no way ours. Since it is worse to be evil than miserable, and since the most
just and merciful God does not inflict on us the penalty of misery, which is
the lesser thing, without our own preceding and demeriting sin, then if some
sin itself is imposed as a penalty, he will certainly never inflict that
greatest of all penalties without an antecedent sin of ours as the
meritorious cause of that penal sin. Adam's sin was therefore ours in some
way. It was not ours personally or actually. Therefore it was ours by
imputation alone. For:

3. If we say, as the Romanists do, that sin is propagated to us from the
guilt of Adam's sin, it is true thus far that the propagation of original
sin comes from the guilt of Adam's actual sin, or originating original sin.
But if it is further said that this happens without the intervention of the
imputation of Adam's sin,

[1679 printed p. 244 | IA leaf 252]

the same stone is merely rolled back again and the same absurdity follows:
this propagation of sin assumes the character of a punishment. But that we
should pay penalties for a sin which is in no way ours is incompatible with
God's justice, much less his goodness. Therefore Adam's sin becomes ours in
no way except through imputation.

As for Peter Lombard's fiction—namely, “that a certain morbid quality
inhered in Adam's flesh and seed and afterward infected and daily continues
successively to infect the souls of his descendants with original sin”—it
is rejected even by the Romanists themselves.

Estius says: “When Adam sinned by his own will, and all were in him as the
origin of the race, a cause was given through which the whole race would
thereafter be infected and individuals constituted sinners, each, that is,
from his own sin contracted from that origin. In the same way, if a father
infected with leprosy begets leprous children, the children are said to have
become leprous in their father, although each contracts his own leprosy from
him.” Although I think this is true, his statement that Adam's sin supplied
the cause through which individuals would be constituted sinners, each from
his own sin, must be understood carefully. For:

1. “Each person's own sin” can be understood consequently, insofar as it
denotes a sin actually committed by him. In this sense not everyone is
constituted a sinner by such a sin of his own, since infants are born
sinners but not because of such a consequent actual sin of their own, which
their age does not yet allow them to commit. Or it can be considered
antecedently, insofar as a sin is called his own which is derived to him
from an earlier sin as from a common fountain. As others participate in it
for their respective parts, so does he. In this way each is constituted a
sinner by his own sin, that is, by his own particular participation in
Adam's sin. In Estius's example of leprous children born of a leprous
parent, each is constituted leprous by his own particular participation in
his father's leprosy, while the father's leprosy remains the cause of the
leprosy inhering in him and the others.

2. That someone is constituted a sinner from birth can also be understood
either formally or efficiently. Formally, he becomes a sinner insofar as,
on his own part and subjectively, the corruption of nature is actually
derived to him and Adam's actual sin is actually credited to him. Yet this
flows efficiently in the first instance from the imputation of Adam's sin,
and then from the propagation of that natural corruption, so that the
imputation always necessarily comes first. Thus, although in the former
sense Estius truly says that each is constituted a sinner from his own sin,
this does not exclude Adam's sin from being its efficient cause insofar as
it is credited to him.

We have said these things as a passing excursus on Romans 5:12 and the
following verses. Meanwhile, I hope we have not strayed far from the path,
because all this confirms our third scriptural argument for our
justification by the imputation of Christ's righteousness, drawn from Romans
5. It was this: we are made participants in Christ's righteousness for
justification in the same manner as we participated in Adam's sin for
condemnation; but we participate in his sin for condemnation through its
imputation to us. Therefore we also participate in Christ's righteousness
through its corresponding imputation to us.
