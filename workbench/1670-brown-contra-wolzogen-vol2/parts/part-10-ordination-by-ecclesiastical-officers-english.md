### Assertion X

This ordination, separation, authoritative sending, and

[Volume II; printed p. 156; ONB canvas 164]

appointment of a pastor is not performed by the people, but instrumentally by
ecclesiastical officers. I set aside here that other question: whether this act
of ordaining a presbyter or pastor belongs to prelate-bishops, or rather to
presbyters or pastors and doctors of the church, for this is not the place to
treat that controversy. Both prelatic writers and Presbyterians agree in this:
this power does not belong to the people. The first part of our assertion,
namely, that this power does not belong to the people, is confirmed as follows.

First, no trace or vestige appears in Holy Scripture of a minister’s ordination
to ecclesiastical office performed by the people; neither a command nor an
example exists. Therefore the point stands. We must see what the adversaries
object.

1. They say that Matthias was appointed and ordained by the common votes of
the people, because Luke says in the last verse of Acts 1, καὶ
συγκατεψηφίσθη—“and he was numbered together”—and so forth. *Reply 1.* It can
never be proved from this that the people can ordain a minister by themselves
or without ecclesiastical governors, because the eleven apostles were present
here and played some part. 2. Indeed, it cannot be proved from this that the
people contributed anything to the act of ordination. For (1) it cannot with
any reason be said that Mary and the other women who were perhaps then present
joined authoritatively in the act of ordaining. (2) Those who put forward the
two in verse 23 were not everyone then present, but the apostles, whose names
we have in verse 13. Judas had obtained a share in the ministry among them and
was numbered with them in verse 17. Those from whom one was to be received had
accompanied them, verse 21, and the one received had to bear witness with them
to Christ’s resurrection, verse 22. They alone, I say, put forward the two,
and they cast the lots, verse 26. 3. This whole action appears extraordinary,
and in it not only the people but even the apostles themselves can scarcely be
said

[Volume II; printed p. 157; ONB canvas 165]

to have ordained Matthias. For (1) they were not free to put forward whomever
they wished, much less to ordain him, but were restricted to those who had
accompanied them throughout the time in which the Lord Jesus had lived among
them, and so forth, verses 21–22. (2) They named and put forward two because
they were not free to name or choose one particular man, much less to ordain
him. (3) Matthias was chosen and appointed by lot, therefore not by the
apostles, much less by the people. (4) Matthias was an apostle, and apostles
were called extraordinarily and immediately by Christ, Galatians 1:1. Let the
most learned interpreter Calvin be heard on the passage: “One person had to be
substituted in Judas’s place; they present two. It may be asked why they were
not content with one. The lot was therefore interposed instead, so that it
might be known and attested that Matthias was created not so much by human
votes as by divine judgment. The apostles differed from pastors in this: while
the latter were simply elected by the church, the former had to be divinely
called. For this reason Paul, in the preface to the Epistle to the Galatians,
declares himself an apostle made neither by men nor through men. Therefore,
just as the dignity of this office was singular, so it was fitting in the
election of Matthias to leave the supreme judgment to God.” 4. As for the word
συγκατεψηφίσθη, it denotes nothing other than that this outcome of the lot was
approved by the common judgment of all. So Calvin, Beza, Aretius, and the
Belgic annotations on the passage.

*Objection II.* Acts 14:23: “And when they had created presbyters for them by
votes in every church,” and so forth. There, they say, presbyters were created
by the votes of the people, not by those two apostles, for nothing is normally
decided by voting between two persons. *Reply 1.* Nor can this passage prove
that the power to ordain rests with the people alone, for the apostles Paul and
Barnabas were present.

[Volume II; printed p. 158; ONB canvas 166]

2. It is conceded that *cheirotonein* means *to extend, raise, stretch out,
lift up, or hold out the hand*, and thus can be said of the people. But then it
means nothing other than *to elect*, or *to elect by votes*, and is thereby
distinguished from *cheirothesia*, which denotes *laying on hands* and
*ordaining*. It will therefore not be proved from this that the people ordained
or created presbyters here, but only that the apostles ordained presbyters
while the people consented by their votes. Calvin’s *Institutes*, book 4,
chapter 3, section 15, is not foreign to this sense: “For Luke relates that
presbyters were appointed throughout the churches by Paul and Barnabas”—citing
this same passage in the margin—“but he notes the manner or mode at the same
time when he says that it was done by votes: ‘They appointed presbyters,’ he
says, ‘in every church.’ The two of them, therefore, created the presbyters,
but the whole multitude, as was the Greek custom in elections, declared with
raised hands whom they wished to have.” He has similar statements in his
commentaries. 3. Even if the word “appointed” were said here of the church,
nothing else could be concluded from this except that the church elected its
presbyters. This does nothing against us, for we freely concede this power to
the people. Here we are speaking not about *election*, but about *ordination*;
and the people’s election ought not to prejudice ordination by the presbytery.
For at Athens, where the people created their magistrates by votes, those
elected were nevertheless not ordained or solemnly appointed by the people,
but by other judges called *hēliastai*, according to Demosthenes, *Against
Timocrates* 4. 4. But it is better to construe the participle “appointing” with
Paul and Barnabas, who are expressly named. It therefore denotes an act of the
apostles and consequently ordination, because the same word *cheirotonein*
sometimes means *to create or appoint*, whether a law or a magistrate. For the
*thesmothetai*, according to Julius Pollux, book 8, chapter 9, section 1, are
said to promulgate public accusations and votes. In Acts 10:41 it means *to
determine or appoint*. Moreover, Henry Stephanus, citing Demosthenes,

[Volume II; printed p. 159; ONB canvas 167]

confirms this: “Whichever of the laws the lawmakers vote for is valid.” The
word is likewise used of creating a magistrate: “when either the council or
the people had elected him.” However the matter is taken, it is clear from
what has been said that this passage proves nothing against us. Consult the
Belgic annotations on the passage.

*Objection III.* Numbers 8:10–11. *Reply 1.* Nor does this passage prove that
the power to ordain pastors rests only with the people, or that the people can
ordain ministers where no pastors or ecclesiastical governors are present, for
priests were present here and joined in the action. 2. Whatever was done here
was done at God’s command; but nowhere do we read in the New Testament that
Christ commanded the people to lay hands on presbyters and ordain them. 3. The
whole congregation did not lay on hands, but certain select persons did, as
Junius, Piscator, Pareus, Ainsworth, Cajetan, Lorinus, and the Belgic
annotations hold—whether rulers, as Lyra, Cajetan, and Lorinus hold, or the
firstborn, who once enjoyed an ecclesiastical office, as Ainsworth and others
hold. For the firstborn were sanctified and consecrated to the Lord, Exodus
13:1, and God took the Levites in their place, verse 17–18. It was therefore
necessary that the firstborn lay on hands. 4. It also follows from this that by
this laying on of hands the Levites were not ordained, or set apart for the
ministry, but rather set apart as a sacrifice. The people were commanded to
lay hands on their sacrifices; but the Levites, in place of the firstborn, were
now to be presented to God as a wave offering, and therefore the people laid
hands on them. Thus the people offered the Levites as an offering to the Lord,
as Vatablus says. Menochius says that by this laying on of hands the people
testified that they were relinquishing the Levites, their fellow tribesmen,
and offering them to God as a kind of gift and sacrifice, transferring them
into his dominion and ministry. 5. By this laying on of hands, the Levites
were not ordained, nor were they admitted or rendered qualified immediately to
perform the office to

[Volume II; printed p. 160; ONB canvas 168 (upper portion)]

which they were called. For in verses 13–15 the Lord says: “Thus you shall
present the Levites before Aaron and before his sons, offering them as a wave
offering to Jehovah; and you shall separate the Levites themselves from the
midst of the children of Israel, that they may be my Levites. Afterward let the
Levites come to minister at the tent of meeting, when you have purified them
and offered them as a wave offering.” Hence it is clear that the Levites were
not permitted to undertake this ministry before they had been consecrated,
offered, and separated by Aaron. It was therefore not the people but Aaron who,
at Moses’s command, ordained them, while the people consented, testified to
their consent by laying on hands, and approved, as Junius says on the passage.
6. With what effrontery can the adversaries press this passage from the Old
Testament when they say that the calling of the Levites and of pastors under
the New Testament differ greatly, as does the New Testament church from the
Jewish church, and on that account reject our arguments drawn from the Old
Testament?
