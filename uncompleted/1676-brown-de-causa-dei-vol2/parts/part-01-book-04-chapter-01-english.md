# CHAPTER I

## Certain general points proposed concerning the nature of the Decalogue

Δεκάλογος—*Decalogue*—properly denotes ten words. Here, however, it is used
for the ten commandments, עשרת הדברים—“the ten words”—Deuteronomy 4:13 and
10:4; Exodus 34:28. For דבר—“word”—sometimes signifies a concise statement,
as in Matthew 21:22, or a command proposed in a few words, as in Esther 1:12.
Hence the Septuagint sometimes translates it ἐντολή—“commandment”—Deuteronomy
17:19, and sometimes λόγος—“word”—Psalm 119:57. And in the New Testament what
is λόγον τοῦ Θεοῦ—“the word of God”—in Mark 7:13 is ἐντολὴν τοῦ Θεοῦ—“the
commandment of God”—in Matthew 15:6. Paul also says in Galatians 5:14, ὁ γὰρ
πᾶς νόμος ἐν ἑνὶ λόγῳ πεπλήρωται—“for the whole law is fulfilled in one
word.” This “word,” however, is this complete statement: ἀγαπήσεις τὸν
πλησίον σου ὡς ἑαυτόν—*“You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”* Therefore,
although fourteen statements or commands are found here, nevertheless there
are only ten primary commandments, to which the rest are reduced.

Those ten commandments which God pronounced from Mount Sinai contain in
summary the whole moral law. This law of the Decalogue is materially the same
as the natural law originally inscribed upon the human heart. Thus the very
law of creation was renewed here for a particular end and with a particular
relation to the Jews, to whom it was then promulgated and sanctioned, with
motives specially pertaining to them employed. In this respect the law was in
a certain way peculiar to them, or an extraordinary benefit by which God
separated that nation from all others, selected it as his special possession,
and in a singular way bound and consecrated it to himself, Psalm 147:19–20;
Romans 3:2 and 9:4. Hence Jehovah was also in a special way their Lawgiver and
King, and their government is called a *theocracy*. Therefore, when they asked
for a king after the custom of other nations, the Lord expressly says that
they rejected him, 1 Samuel 8:7. See also Judges 8:23.

All the other divine commandments given to regulate our conduct, whether
toward God or toward other people, can be reduced to this epitome of the moral
or natural law. Thus all ceremonial precepts which concerned God's worship can
be reduced to the first table, and especially to the second commandment, which
commands us to worship God in the manner he himself has chosen. Judicial
precepts are for the most part reduced to the second table. Thus the
ceremonial and judicial laws, properly so called, are as it were appendices to
this moral law. Various institutions and precepts also occur in the Mosaic law
which are so many explanations or particular applications of this moral law.

[Printed p. 5 | IA PDF 13 | IA leaf 13 | IA decausadei02brow]

This law of the Decalogue is called the law of love, and so love is called
*“the fulfillment of the law,”* Romans 13:10, while one who loves another is
said to have fulfilled the law, verse 8. Love is called *“the end of the
commandment,”* 1 Timothy 1:5, and love itself is called *“the bond of
perfection,”* Colossians 3:14. Christ himself reduces all the commandments of
this law to love, Matthew 22:37–40: *“You shall love the Lord your God with all
your heart, with all your soul, and with all your mind. This is the first and
great commandment. The second is like it: You shall love your neighbor as
yourself. On these two commandments hang the whole law and the prophets.”*
Love of God and neighbor is therefore the fulfillment of the entire law. This
love of God and neighbor is joined by an indissoluble bond. For one who does
not love his neighbor, whom God commands him to love and in whom God's image,
which must be highly esteemed, shines forth, does not love God either, 1 John
4:19–21: *“If anyone says, ‘I love God,’ and hates his brother, he is a liar;
for he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God
whom he has not seen? And we have this commandment from him, that he who loves
God should also love his brother.”*

We must recall the distinction in the natural law, given earlier in Book I,
chapter VI, between its primeval and its post-primeval forms. The former bore
a special relation to unimpaired nature, first instituted by God according to
the pattern of his image, such as it was in our first parents before the fall.
The latter regards the same human nature now vitiated by corruption, deprived
of natural righteousness, and in need of another's righteousness. It is thus
accommodated to the state of fallen humanity, which needs a mediator. In this
law faith in Jesus Christ the Savior—Christ now having been revealed through
the covenant of grace—and repentance from evil works were commanded, because
the law in its commandments was accommodated to fallen humanity. Those
virtues, I say, were commanded under a particular designation and
determination which they could not have had in the state of innocence.
Nevertheless, the same virtues were required materially or virtually, though
not formally and specifically, even before the fall. Apollonius says in *On
the Law of God*, p. 258:

> After the fall the moral law was given in such a way as to be accommodated,
> in its mandates and commands, to the salvation of fallen and sinful
> humanity. Consequently, it ordered and enjoined all those virtues which the
> law requires for the salvation of a sinner. Indeed, the law of God in the
> state of integrity also established the same virtues that are virtues after
> the fall; only the same designation and determination attributed to them
> after the fall would not then have obtained. Thus repentance, as to the
> thing itself, also existed in the state of integrity. For it is nothing
> other than performance of the law, which is called repentance after people
> return to it following the fall. Modesty also existed in the state of
> integrity, for as to the thing itself it is nothing other than love of
> chastity opposing every impurity. It is now called modesty because of that
> particular determination after the fall by which it opposes the shameful
> exposure of members that ought to be covered. Hence there was a place for
> the virtue of repentance under the perfect state, although the condition of
> that state did not admit such a designation and determination.

This law of the Decalogue is the perfect rule of life and conduct, for the
Lord reduced all the precepts of the natural law, whether primeval or
post-primeval, to these ten heads. Hence it is called perfect, Psalm 19:8.
Nothing could therefore be added to it, Deuteronomy 4:2: *“You shall not add
to this word which I command you, nor take away from it.”* See also Deuteronomy
12:32. Indeed, nothing at all

could be added, because, as the renowned Hoornbeek says in *Refutation of
Socinianism*, book 2, chapter 2, section 2:

> Whatever virtue and good works can be stated or proposed are contained in
> it. For the law commands us to love God with all our heart, soul, and
> strength, and our neighbor as ourselves. Can anything in love be devised
> beyond this, whether toward God or toward another person? For since there
> are three who are to be loved—God, ourselves, and our neighbor—and there is
> no duty which is not contained in that single “word” of love, and since, as
> regards the degree of love, nothing more perfect can be expressed than to
> love God above all and with all our strength, and then ourselves and our
> neighbor as ourselves, it follows that this law is the most perfect rule of
> all duties and good works. Hence Christ says in Matthew 22:37–39, “You shall
> love the Lord your God,” and so forth. The words “to love God,” and so
> forth, present the sum of the law, the sum of things to be done. This sum is
> said to be comprehended in the Law and the Prophets. Here, if ever, there
> was occasion and opportunity to speak of additional commands, if Christ had
> possessed or delivered others more perfect than these. But when the scribe
> asked about the greatest commandments, Christ set forth no others than
> those of the law. He offered or indicated none of his own, signifying that
> enough commandments were contained in the law and that outside it he had
> none—none his own and none greater. Mark 12:31 says that Christ declared,
> “And there is no other commandment greater than these.” As regards the
> second table, or love toward one's neighbor, nothing greater than what the
> law dictated about loving one's neighbor as oneself can be prescribed; nor
> did Christ propose anything in this respect beyond the law, Romans 13:8–10.
> Can anything be required above love—above a love equal to love for oneself?
> Whatever you propose is either contained in that commandment or is not. If
> it is not, the apostle erred when he said that every commandment is summed
> up in it. But if it is, then every commandment is contained in the law, for
> that commandment is a commandment of the law, and outside it there is no
> other commandment. The words “and if there is any other commandment” signify
> that no other particular mandate about any matter can be given which is not
> included in that general command of the law, Galatians 5:13–14; James 2:8.
> Read what follows as well.

*sui ipsius*; the terminal errata directs *sibi ipsi*, adopted in the Latin
and reflected by “for oneself” above.]

This will be confirmed still more if we consider certain particular things
enjoined in this law, such as: 1. that we love God with all our heart, all our
soul, and all our strength, as has already been confirmed from Christ's own
commentary; 2. that we worship God and serve him with the heart and inward
spirit—hence he so often required the heart to be given to him, Proverbs
23:26; see also Proverbs 3:1, 5; Jeremiah 29:13; Joshua 22:5; Deuteronomy 11:13,
13:3, 26:16, and 30:2, 10; 1 Samuel 12:20; 3. it required inward mortification
and sanctification more than outward works of religion, as may be seen in
Isaiah 1 and Psalm 50; 4. further, it requires that we perform all duties in
faith and love, which the first commandment had enjoined; 5. indeed, it
commands us to love God above all with the most intense love; 6. and to be
aroused by spiritual motives to perform all duties—hence the prophets
complained that the people fasted for themselves, not for God; 7. it further
enjoins contentment of soul and affection placed in God alone, Psalm 130:7;
Isaiah 26:4; Psalm 73:24 and often in Psalm 119; 8. in a word, it required
perfection in every respect: first, perfection of the subject, so that the
whole person—mind, soul, affection, and entire heart—should be holy and
devoted to God; second, perfection of the object, so that there should be no
virtue or good action not required in it; and third, perfection of degrees, so
that

[Printed p. 7 | IA PDF 15 | IA leaf 15 | IA decausadei02brow]

duties should be performed without any defect, weakness, lukewarmness, or
sloth. See the explanation of the commandments of the Decalogue in the
*British Larger Catechism*.

The Socinians therefore must not be heard when they claim that Christ
prescribed another law more perfect than this one, or interpolated and
corrected this law. For, as Apollonius says at the place cited above, p. 241
and following:

> Christ subjected himself for us to the most perfect law. For if he had
> subjected himself for us to an imperfect law, either our redeemer would be
> imperfect, because he had not satisfied a more perfect law for us, or we
> are not obligated to a more perfect law. For he redeemed us from the law to
> which we are subject and obligated. But he subjected himself for us to the
> law of Moses, as is clear from Galatians 4:4, 12, 13. Nor was it necessary
> that he subject himself to any law other than the law of Moses in order to
> redeem us, because that is the law which threatens its transgressors with
> the curse which the Redeemer bore in our place, Galatians 3:13, and promises
> eternal life to those who keep it perfectly, Romans 10:5. Therefore, just
> as Christ was subject to no other law than Moses' in transferring to himself
> the curse owed to us, so also in acquiring the right to eternal life through
> a holy life conformed to the law he was obligated to no other law than
> Moses'. But if some other, more perfect law now obligated or constrained us,
> to which Christ had not been subject in acquiring for us the right to
> eternal life, he would not have saved us perfectly. For Scripture testifies
> that he was subject to the law of Moses, that by this subjection he redeemed
> us, and that he redeemed us from that law.

6. Apollonius continues:

> Christ asserted this with a solemn affirmation and declaration: that he had
> not come to dissolve the law but to fulfill it. And although Christ is said
> to have fulfilled the law in different ways, nevertheless here he is said
> primarily to have fulfilled the law by doctrine, perfectly teaching the law
> of Moses in its integrity and perfection. For he opposes the teaching of
> the law, which is set forth by teaching, to its dissolution, Matthew
> 5:17, 19. Therefore our Savior affirms that by his teaching he did not
> dissolve God's law but fulfilled it, setting it forth perfectly and
> completely according to the intention of the lawgiver, not correcting or
> abolishing it.

7. He adds:

> Certainly it is sufficiently evident from the summary of the law set forth
> under the Old Testament—“You shall love the Lord your God with all your
> heart,” and so forth—that nothing could be added to the law. This is also
> the summary of the law under the New Testament, as appears in Luke 10:27.
> Now if the summary of the law is the same, and the moral commands of the Old
> and New Testaments are equally perfect, then the law of the Old and New
> Testaments is equally perfect. For if the precepts of the New Testament were
> more perfect than those of the Old, one and the same summary could not be
> gathered from them, since the latter would not command everything to be
> done and prohibit everything to be omitted. Nor can greater love toward God
> and neighbor be exercised than is commanded in the summary of the law.

That discourse of Christ on the mount in Matthew 5, 6, and 7 does not charge
this moral law with imperfection. This is sufficiently evident from the fact
that Christ restored the law to its original purity, perfection, and
integrity, vindicating it from the Jews' perverse glosses and corrupt
expositions. He did not oppose his own propositions to the words or writings
of Moses, but rejected the meanings and interpretations of the rabbis and
Pharisees. For 1. if this were any addition of greater and more excellent
commandments, he would be opposing not himself to Moses, but God himself, and
indeed himself, who first gave this moral law. 2. It is sufficiently clear
that Christ here opposes himself to their opinions—μὴ νομίσητε, “do not
think.” For the Jews thought that their teachers were lawfully able to
dissolve the law by their expositions and by a life composed according to
them. Therefore they expected something of the kind from Christ. He, however,
manifestly opposes this opinion which they

[Printed p. 8 | IA PDF 16 | IA leaf 16 | IA decausadei02brow]

had imbibed concerning the dissolution of the law, not so much by the life as
by the doctrine of the rabbis, as appears in verse 19. 3. He says that he *did
not come to dissolve the law*, namely, by life or doctrine, but to fulfill it;
not by adding some complement, as Socinus wishes, for this cannot be said of
the Prophets. Yet he says that he came to fulfill the Prophets as well as the
law—rather by confirming it, as this same word is used in Romans 15:19; or by
rightly interpreting it, as it is used in Colossians 1:25; or by accomplishing
it in deed, as in 2 Thessalonians 1:11. What follows also confirms this when he
adds: *“Until heaven and earth pass away, not one iota or one point will pass
from the law until all things take place.”* Thus he fulfilled the Law and the
Prophets when he fulfilled the predictions of the Prophets and the
prefigurations of the law, or, finally, by restoring it to its original
integrity. 4. What he adds in verse 18 abundantly confirms this, for there he
plainly indicates that nothing will ever perish from the law which does not
come to pass. But how can this be consistent with amending and correcting the
law? For by the very fact that something is amended or corrected, it is
declared not to be firm. 5. The words found in verse 19 likewise confirm it:
*“Whoever therefore dissolves one of these least commandments and teaches
people so shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven.”* Here we see that
these commandments can be dissolved even by doctrine, and that Christ directs
his discourse not against Moses or the law itself, but against those who by
their corrupt doctrines and interpretations dissolved the force of the law.
We see further that Christ did not intend to diminish the authority of this
law, which he would undoubtedly have done if he had condemned it as imperfect.
6. The words of verse 20—*“For I say to you, unless your righteousness abounds
more than the righteousness of the scribes and Pharisees,”* and so forth—plainly
indicate that Christ here directs his discourse against the scribes and
Pharisees, who also sinned in doctrine and dissolved the law. 7. The words of
verse 21, *“it was said to the ancients,”* prove this. For he does not say “it
was said by Moses” or “in the law of Moses,” but “it was said to the ancients”
or “by the ancients.” As Hoornbeek notes, the Jews used this formula when
presenting their traditions or expositions of the law under the name of the
Ancients and Forefathers: *“The Masters taught. Our Rabbis said.”* This denotes
the same thing as *“it was said to the ancients.”* But when Christ speaks of
the law, he never uses this formula; he names the law itself or Moses, as
appears in Matthew 8:4, 12:5, and 19:7–8; Mark 12:26; John 10:34; Luke 10:26;
1 Corinthians 9:9. 8. Moreover, he says *“and it was said,”* not *“it is
written”* or *“it is read.”* 9. These words do not appear verbatim in the law:
*“Whoever kills will be liable to judgment.”* Therefore he reproves not the
law itself but the teachers' perverse invention. 10. Christ's response also
confirms this, for it indicates that he adds nothing to the law, since he
condemns the same things that the law itself had condemned, as may be seen in
Genesis 4:5 and 49:7; Psalm 5:5; Ecclesiastes 7:10; Proverbs 15:18, 26:21, and
29:23; 2 Samuel 16:5; 1 Kings 2:23; Leviticus 19:17–18. 11. Furthermore,
nowhere in the law do we read what is found in verse 43: *“You shall hate your
enemy.”* Indeed, Socinus himself concedes that Christ wished to reject a false
interpretation of the law here. 12. And Christ's addition, *“Love your
enemies,”* was not added by Christ to the law, as the Socinians wish, for the
law itself required it, Exodus 23:4–5; Proverbs 25:22. Enough on these matters.

What the Socinians advance to the contrary is abundantly refuted by the
orthodox, especially by the celebrated Dr. Hoornbeek in the place cited above,
book 2, chapter 2, part 1, whom the reader may consult.

The Decalogue is commonly called *the immutable rule of divine justice*. This
is not said absolutely with respect to God, as though all its commandments so
flowed from God's nature that he neither could have willed nor ever could will
or command the contrary, or as though none of those commands flowed from
God's most free good pleasure. See what we said above in Book I, chapter III;
consider also Genesis 22:2 and Exodus 11:2–3. Rather, it is said with respect
to human beings, for whom it is not lawful to alter the law or deviate from
it. It is also said in this respect: that God established it with the purpose
that it should be the immutable and perpetual rule of human conduct and life,
not one given only for a time, as the ceremonial and judicial laws were.

The Decalogue can be considered in two ways: either insofar as it presents to
us an inflexible rule of righteousness, or insofar as it has a covenant joined
to it—that is, a covenantal agreement contracted between God and human beings.
For these considerations are distinct and separable by their nature. The
covenantal relation can be removed while the rule remains unchanged, or the
covenantal relation could never have been added to this rule. For a creature
can be under the law of its creator and nevertheless not be called into
covenantal fellowship.

The Decalogue, insofar as it has a covenant joined to it, can be considered in
two ways, according to the twofold covenant which God made with human beings:
that is, either insofar as it relates to the covenant of works, or insofar as
it relates to the covenant of grace. This relation to the covenant of grace is
in turn twofold, according to the twofold administration of this covenant,
namely, legal and evangelical. The Decalogue can thus be considered either
insofar as it relates to the covenant of grace legally administered, or to the
covenant of grace evangelically administered. Or, to put it more briefly, it
can be considered insofar as it is abstracted from every covenantal relation;
insofar as it had a place in the covenant of works made with Adam in
innocence; insofar as it had a place in the covenant which God made with the
Israelites at Mount Sinai; or, finally, insofar as it has a place under the
Gospel.

Considered in the first way, it has the force of directing—that is, of
indicating God's will—and of obligating to obedience. It never loses this
force. It retains this power even under the Gospel, as we shall show more
fully later. In relation to the covenant of works, it contained the stipulated
condition of that covenant, but to that extent it is abolished for believers,
as we shall show later. In the covenant made with the Israelite people it was
indeed proposed according to the status it had under the first covenant, but
not with the purpose that they should finally be justified in this way, nor
that by obedience to it they should seek or expect salvation and righteousness
before God. Rather, the purpose was that, reduced to bondage in that state of
infancy, they should all the more ardently long for salvation through the
promised Messiah, renouncing all their own works. For, as we shall make clear
later, this covenant made with the Israelites was the covenant of grace, but
administered legally, so to speak. To that extent it is indeed now abrogated
under the New Testament, but it nevertheless retains the force of directing
and obligating even believers, as will be proved.

We must distinguish between the Decalogue taken strictly and broadly. Taken
strictly, it includes only the bare commandments, or those ten words, which
contain the matter of the covenant of works, or the matter of that
righteousness which is from the law, or the condition of life promised in that
covenant, according to the saying, *“The one who performs them*

[Printed p. 10 | IA PDF 18 | IA leaf 18 | IA decausadei02brow]

*will live by them,”* Romans 10:5. Taken broadly, it also includes the preface,
the promises, and things of that kind, together with the appendices, namely,
sacrifices and other exercises of religion. In this way it also connotes the
covenant of grace, which was then legally administered. Thus it does not
contain the condition of the covenant, but prescribes the duty of the
covenanted people according to God's intention, although the carnal Jews
abused it and turned this covenant of grace into a covenant of works, as Paul
indicates in Romans 9:31–32 and 10:3.

The Decalogue is commonly divided into two tables, Exodus 31:18; 32:15–16, 19;
34:1; Deuteronomy 4:13. The first contains those commandments which enjoin
piety toward God, while the latter contains those which enjoin justice toward
one's neighbor. The former enjoins love of God and the latter love of one's
neighbor, as Christ testifies in Matthew 22:37–38. Therefore, the first table
contains four commandments and the latter six, whatever certain Jews may say
who maintain that each table contains five commandments. They therefore claim
that the fifth commandment also relates immediately to God, because parents
in some way share with God in their own formation, so that one who does not
honor them is just as one who does not honor God. Thus speaks Rabbi Abraham
Aben Ezra in his exposition of the Decalogue, cited by Gerardus Joannes
Vossius in his *Thesis on the Division of the Decalogue*.

Roman Catholics and Lutherans err greatly in distributing the commandments of
the Decalogue. To strike out the Second—the commandment against forming and
worshiping images—so that it will not be regarded as a distinct commandment,
they assign only three commandments to the first table and seven to the
second. To establish these and so preserve the number ten intact, they divide
the Tenth, concerning covetous desire, in two. The renowned Zanchi does not
hesitate to say that this division was made by the devil's cunning. For, he
says, it gradually resulted in the commandment against making images being
finally struck from the Decalogue among many people. They hide this
commandment under the First so that it will not appear that God expressly
condemned images other than those by which Jehovah God is represented.

It is true that Augustine held this same opinion in *Questions on Exodus*,
question 71; Letter 119 to Januarius; Sermon 148, *On Time*; and elsewhere,
and that certain others followed him. But his childish reasoning is quite
apparent, for he speaks as follows in *Questions on Exodus*: *“It seems more
fitting to me that those three and these seven be accepted, because to those
who look more carefully the three which pertain to God seem to intimate the
Trinity.”* Nevertheless, in book 3 of *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians*,
chapter 4, he distinctly names the commandment against worshiping idols
separately from the First, concerning serving the one God with religious
obedience, which Roman Catholics are not accustomed to do.

The contrary opinion, as one agreeable to Scripture, must instead be held. The
ancient Hebrews assent to it, namely Philo Judaeus, writing *On the Decalogue*,
and Josephus, *Antiquities*, book 3, whose words may be seen cited by Vossius
at the place above; as do the Fathers before Augustine cited by Vossius there,
namely Origen, Homily 8 on Exodus; Athanasius, book 2, *Synopsis of Holy
Scripture*; Gregory Nazianzen, Poem 35; Ambrose on Ephesians 6; the author of
*Questions from the Old and New Testaments*; Chrysostom, Homily 49 on Matthew;
Jerome on Ephesians 6; Sulpicius Severus, book 1 of the *Sacred History*; and
Procopius of Gaza on Exodus 2.

The words *“I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt,
out of the house of bondage”* do not constitute the first commandment, as Aben
Ezra in his exposition of the Decalogue and other Jews maintain. Their
reasoning, as Burgensis explains in his addition to Lyra on Exodus, is that in
order to observe all the divine commandments one must presuppose a belief that
the one commanding was their God, who had brought them out, and so forth.
Consequently, they

[Printed p. 11 | IA PDF 19 | IA leaf 19 | IA decausadei02brow]

claim that faith in God is commanded in these words. Yet it is sufficiently
clear that these words do not contain a command, because they contain no act
commanded, but are a merely declarative statement, which is not the case in
the other commandments. Burgensis at the place above, as cited by Vossius at
the place above, abundantly refutes the Jews' arguments. These words therefore
contain a preface placed before all the commandments, in which reasons are
given to urge obedience to the commandments more strongly. They nevertheless
have a special relation to the first commandment, which says: *“You shall have
no other gods before me”*—others, that is, besides me, who am the Lord your
God and brought you out of the land of Egypt.

In this preface God manifests, as stated in the *British Larger Catechism*, his
preeminence: that he is Jehovah, the eternal, immutable, and almighty God,
Isaiah 44:6; that he has existence in himself, Exodus 3:14, and gives existence
to all his words, Exodus 6:3, and works, Acts 17:24, 28; and that he is a God
who entered into covenant, formerly with Israel and likewise with his whole
people, Genesis 17:7 compared with Romans 3:29. Just as he brought them out of
their bondage in Egypt, so he liberates us from our spiritual bondage, Luke
1:74–75. Consequently, we are bound to accept him alone as our God and observe
all his commandments, 1 Peter 1:15–18; Leviticus 18:30 and 19:37.

Thus, in brief: 1. he shows us what the true object of worship is, namely
Jehovah Elohim—that is, the one God in essence and threefold in persons; 2.
how we ought to regard this God in our worship and service, namely, as our God
by reason of the covenant, for apart from this relation the sinner can perform
nothing that can be pleasing to God; and 3. what the right motive of obedience
is, namely, the benefit of redemption. He thus teaches that our obedience must
flow from love, gratitude, and a sense of the benefit of redemption, and must
be rendered to God, who is Jehovah Elohim and is joined to us by covenant in
Christ.

Some commandments have threats attached to them, which nevertheless imply
promises, as do the Second and Third; others do not, although they all imply
threats for transgressors. Threats are expressly attached to these two,
perhaps because God foresaw that people would make light of violating them.
Thus, so that they might see how severely he would regard transgressing them
and how dangerous it is to violate them, he saw fit to attach express threats
to them.

Some commandments have express promises attached, which also imply threats,
as do the Second and Fifth; others have none. Perhaps this is because obedience
rendered to these commandments is more opposed to people's corrupt affections
and exposes them more to dangers, persecutions, and the like. For those who
faithfully adhere to the outward profession of true religion and to the
worship prescribed by God, rejecting all others, are often vexed by
persecutions and overwhelmed with slanders. Inferiors also subject themselves
to their superiors with a sufficiently reluctant spirit.

Some commandments have other reasons attached to persuade obedience, as do
the Second and Fourth; others have none besides the one contained in the
preface common to them all. Perhaps this is because natural light itself,
though now darkened, can convict of sin more readily in the others than in
these. For corrupted nature can with difficulty

[Printed p. 12 | IA PDF 20 | IA leaf 20 | IA decausadei02brow]

perceive what the worship instituted by God is and which day he wishes to be
observed as the Sabbath. And because God foresaw that people would be more
inclined not only to violate these commandments but even to strike them from
the list of moral commandments, he therefore saw fit to fortify them with the
most effective reasons. This also makes apparent how greatly and irrationally
those people rave who violate these commandments and teach that they ought to
be violated.

Some commandments are conceived and expressed affirmatively, such as the
Fifth; some negatively, as are all the others except the Fourth, which is
stated both negatively and affirmatively. It should nevertheless be noted that
affirmative commandments include negative ones and negative commandments
include affirmative ones, and therefore all are in fact both negative and
affirmative.

Even the least sin and the least violation of a divine commandment deserves
God's wrath and curse, both in this life and in the future, Ephesians 5:6;
Galatians 3:10; Luke 3:39; Deuteronomy 28:15 to the end; Matthew 25:41. Nor can
it be expiated except by Christ's blood, Hebrews 9:22; 1 Peter 1:18–19. For it
is committed against God's supreme dominion, James 2:10–11; his goodness, 1
Samuel 2:27–29; 2 Samuel 12:7–8; Exodus 20:1–2; Ezekiel 16:18–20; his holiness,
Habakkuk 1:13; Leviticus 10:3 and 11:44–45; Psalm 5:5–7; his just law, 1 John
3:4; Romans 7:12; and his justice, because the sinner either does not believe
it or makes light of it, just as he does God's power and omnipresence, Job
22:13 and 24:15. Therefore every sin involves contempt of God, Jeremiah 11:21;
Isaiah 57:11; 2 Samuel 12:9–10; 1 Samuel 2:30.

It should first be noted that not every omission of an action which is
sometimes commanded is a transgression of the law. This is because: 1. the
moral duties of the first table must be preferred to the moral duties of the
second table, and the latter yield to the former when both cannot be
performed, as is evident from Luke 14:26; Matthew 10:37; Acts 4:19 and 5:29;
Ephesians 6:1. 2. Positive duties of the first table yield to moral duties of
the second table. For on a particular occasion I can omit the public worship
of God to save a brother in danger, extinguish a fire, and do similar things,
because God desires mercy, not sacrifice, Hosea 6:6; Matthew 12:3, 4, 10 and
15:3–6. 3. Although affirmative commandments always bind, Deuteronomy 4:8–9,
they do not bind *for every moment* with respect to acts, Matthew 12:7. For
every duty is to be performed at the proper time: when it tends to God's
glory, 1 Corinthians 10:31, and the edification of others; when divine
providence calls and God's Word directs; when no harm will follow from
discharging this duty; and when no obstacle will be placed before another,
greater duty. Negative commandments, however, bind *at every moment*, Job
13:7–8 and 36:21; Romans 3:8; Hebrews 11:25, provided they are not positive,
for, as was said, positive duties yield to natural duties.

It should be noted, second, that not all transgressions of the divine law are
equally serious, but some are more serious than others in God's sight, both by
their nature and because of various aggravations, John 19:11; Ezekiel 8:6, 13,
15; 1 John 5:16; Psalm 78:17, 23, 56. These aggravations can be reduced to four
heads, as may be seen in the *British Larger Catechism*, question 150. They are
taken 1. from the persons committing the offense, Jeremiah 2:8: namely, if
they are older, Job 32:7, 9; Ecclesiastes 4:13; if they have more experience
or grace,

1 Kings 11:4, 9; if they are more eminent in their profession of religion, 2
Samuel 12:14; 1 Corinthians 5:1; in gifts, James 4:17; Luke 12:47–48; in
dignity, Jeremiah 5:4–5; or in office, 2 Samuel 12:7–9; Ezekiel 8:11–12; if
they are leaders of others, Romans 2:17–25; and if their example can invite
more people to imitation, Galatians 2:11–14.

Or 2. they are taken from the persons injured, Matthew 21:38–39: namely, if
the sin is immediately against God, 1 Samuel 2:25; Acts 5:4; his attributes,
Romans 2:4, and worship, Malachi 1:8, 14; against Christ and his grace, Hebrews

Or 3. they are taken from the nature and quality of the sin, Proverbs 6:30 to

In rightly explaining this law, the following rules must be observed.

1. The law is perfect and exceedingly broad, Psalm 119:96. Hence:

1. It requires all duties and duties of every kind, public and private,
whether toward God, ourselves, or others, in actions whether perfect or
imperfect, in gesture, words, thoughts, and things endured.

2. It obligates absolutely everyone, whether good or evil, superior or
inferior, younger or older, members of the Church or outside the Church, male
or female.

3. It obligates the whole person: not only his actions, speech, gestures, and
the like, but his very soul, understanding, will, affections, memory, and
conscience.

4. It requires perfect duties, complete in every respect, not only as to their
matter but also as to their manner, principle—that is, motives—end, and
circumstances.

5. It requires entire and supreme obedience, or the highest degree of
perfection in each duty.

6. It also requires such obedience to be perpetual.

7. It prohibits absolutely every evil, public and private, committed against
God, others, and ourselves, in actions, words, gestures, thoughts, and things
endured.

8. It prohibits not only the actual commission of an evil, but also: 1. it
condemns the very source of evil, original corruption and inclination toward
evil, Romans 7:7; 2. even involuntary stirrings or inclinations toward evil,
where there is not yet consent or delight, for those stirrings are contrary to
the holy law, signs of disagreement with the holy law, and flow from an evil
source, Matthew 5:28; 3. every delight in evil, even if there is not yet
consent to perpetrate it, displeasure at impediments to that evil, and the act
by which we wish that such a thing were not prohibited; 4. every headlong rush
into some occasion of evil or into temptation to evil; 5. every desire or
consent to perpetrate evil; and 6. finally, every appearance of evil.

2. The law is spiritual, and therefore it reaches the understanding, will,
affections, all the other powers of the soul, and the very first stirrings no
less than outward words, deeds, and gestures, Romans 7:14; Deuteronomy 6:5
compared with Matthew 22:37–39; Matthew 5:21–22, 27–28 and chapter 6 to the
end. Hence it requires: 1. spiritual duties such as faith, fear, and love of
God; 2. spiritual habits and dispositions no less than actions; and 3. that
all things be performed spiritually, in the Spirit, Galatians 5:16; 1
Corinthians 14:16—that is, that they flow from a spiritual principle, be done
in a spiritual manner and for a spiritual end, and be works of the Spirit.

3. One and the same thing is commanded or prohibited in several commandments
under different respects, Colossians 3:5; Amos 8:5; Proverbs 1:19; 1 Timothy
6:10. Such are drunkenness, ignorance, and similar things, which render people
fit for every evil and unfit for the performance of any good. Conversely,
knowledge, sobriety, vigilance, and similar things are commanded in all the
commandments.

4. Where a duty is commanded, the sin contrary to it is prohibited; and
conversely, where a sin is prohibited, the duty contrary to it is also
commanded, Isaiah 58:13; Deuteronomy 6:13 compared with Matthew 4:9–10;
Matthew 15:4–6 and 5:21–25; Ephesians 4:28. Thus, where a promise is appended,
the contrary threat is implied, Exodus 20:12 compared with Proverbs 30:17; and
where a threat is attached, the contrary promise is understood, Jeremiah
18:7–8; Exodus 20:7 compared with Psalms 15:1, 4–5 and 24:4–5.

[Printed p. 15 | IA PDF 23 | IA leaf 23 | IA decausadei02brow]

5. Where any duty is commanded, there are also commanded: 1. all duties of its
kind; 2. all causes and means, Leviticus 19:18; Hebrews 10:24–25; and 3. the
very profession of that duty, Romans 10:9–11.

6. Where the duties of one relation are commanded, the duties of the
correlative relation are also included. Thus, the duties of parents include
the duties of children, and the duties of magistrates and pastors include the
duties of subjects and members of the Church, and vice versa.

7. Where any sin is prohibited, there are also prohibited: 1. all sins of the
same kind; 2. all their causes and occasions; 3. all their means and
enticements; and 4. all their species. See Matthew 5:21–22, 27–28 and 15:4–6;
1 Thessalonians 5:22; Jude 23; Galatians 5:26; Colossians 1:21. 5. Much more
are prohibited all evil customs and habits acquired from many acts; 6. also
greater sins; and 7. likewise every delight in them and every minimizing of
them, whether in ourselves or in others.

8. Whatever is prohibited or commanded to us, we are bound, according to the
station in which we are placed, to strive that it should also be avoided or
performed by others according to their station and office, Exodus 20:10;
Leviticus 19:17; Genesis 18:19; Joshua 24:15; Deuteronomy 6:6–7.

9. Whatever is commanded to others, we must assist them in it according to
the nature of our station and calling, 1 Corinthians 1:24, and carefully guard
against becoming participants with them in things prohibited to them, 1
Timothy 5:22; Ephesians 5:11.

10. One who violates one commandment is virtually guilty of all, for they are
so closely joined and linked that when one is violated, the authority by which
they are all enjoined cannot but be despised and scorned, James 2:10; 1 John
4:20.

11. None of these commandments contradicts another, for nothing is commanded
in one that is prohibited in another. The duties here certainly differ and
are distinct, but they are not opposed. Where one yields to another, as we
indicated above, the one which yields is not on that occasion a commanded duty
or a prohibited sin.

12. This law obligates everyone, but each person according to the nature of
his calling, function, and station, as well as according to his qualifications
and other circumstances. It requires one thing from magistrates and other
superiors and another from subjects and other inferiors. Although everyone is
bound to edify others, the magistrate is bound to edify in one manner, the
pastor in another, the private person in another, and the servant in another.
Thus a servant is bound to reprove differently than a lord or master. The rich
are obligated to different things than the poor, and the wise than the
foolish. For the law is just and observes such proportion. It does not remove,
alter, or confuse different relations and stations, but confirms and limits
them.

13. All God's commandments are ordered toward love of God and neighbor, for
Christ himself reduces them all to these two heads, Matthew 22:37–38; Luke
10:27. The end of the commandment is love from a pure heart and good
conscience, 1 Timothy 1:5, and love is called the fulfillment of the law,
Romans 13:10. See also Galatians 5:14; James 2:8.

14. We must diligently guard against misusing the law, 1 Timothy 1:8. We
misuse the law both in practice and in doctrine. We misuse it in doctrine
when: 1. we turn it into useless questions which do little or nothing to
promote edification, and thus render it

[Printed p. 16 | IA PDF 24 | IA leaf 24 | IA decausadei02brow]

obscure; 2. we handle or press the law in order to exclude Christ; 3. we
handle it in such a way that we break godly spirits; 4. we handle it for
carnal considerations, so that we may serve the stage, and not sincerely and
with a pure spirit; 5. we assert that it is abrogated; 6. we interpret it
badly and perversely—that is, not so as to make evident that it condemns: 1.
covetous desire, the source of all evils; 2. the first stirrings, even though
involuntary; 3. all corrupt and unrighteous affections; 4. the will's consent
to every evil stirring of covetous desire; 5. all outward expressions of evil
desires and affections, whether made by words, gestures, or nods; 6. much more,
the outward completion of sin; and 7. especially custom, stubbornness, and a
habit of perpetrating evil. Conversely, we interpret it so as not to make
evident that it commands: 1. the perfect righteousness of nature; 2. holy
stirrings perpetually tending toward doing God's will; 3. godly affections
agreeing with God's law; 4. the will's consent to all the good and holy
stirrings of regenerate nature; 5. holy words and upright conduct breathing
true and sincere piety; 6. holy outward obedience; and 7. godly habits and
constant practices in doing good.

We misuse it in practice when: 1. we seek in it a righteousness in which we
can appear before God, as the Jews did, Romans 9 and 10; 2. it is pressed to
approve unlawful things; 3. we are not moved by its authority to do good; 4.
its authority is altogether repudiated; 5. it is opposed to Christ and the
Gospel; and 6. we do not kiss and embrace it as good, just, holy, spiritual,
and perfect.
