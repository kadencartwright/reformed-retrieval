## QUESTION IV

### When was the dying Jacob’s prophecy concerning the departure of the scepter from Judah and the coming of Shiloh fulfilled?

We said something about the meaning of the prophecy’s words at the fifth of our Hebrew questions. Concerning the time of its fulfillment, besides the ancients a great many of the most learned men among both Protestants and papists dispute altogether too much: Paraeus, Rivetus, Spanhemius, Scaliger, Casaubon, Cunaeus, and others from our side; Baronius, Torniellus, Pererius, Galatinus, Petavius, Serarius, and others among the papists greatly vex the present question. From the great variety of opinions we shall touch upon only a few, and those briefly.

The first opinion is Julian the Apostate’s. To prove that this most illustrious prophecy pertained in no way to Jesus Christ, he said that the scepter departed from Judah at Zedekiah’s death, after whom there was no king in Israel from the seed of Judah. Cyril of Alexandria opposed an entire treatise to the apostate’s pernicious cavils. Among other things he replied that the scepter and some measure of rule continued after Zedekiah in the house of David, or at least in the tribe of Judah, down to the days of Christ. Casaubon, Cunaeus, Scaliger, and Pererius assail this reply of Cyril’s as utterly false. Yet so excellent a father ought certainly to have been treated more kindly, especially in his *Apology for the Christian Faith against Julian the Apostate*. It is acknowledged that Jacob speaks not only of the kings’ scepter but also of the rule of governors. Casaubon admits that the rabbis affirm this rule continued in Zerubbabel’s son and grandsons long after Zedekiah. Cunaeus also declares from Jewish writers that in the chamber of Gazith, where the great Sanhedrin sat within the temple courts, a scepter hung as a sign until the days of Herod—if not of supreme authority, certainly of great authority in that council of the Jews. Moreover, no argument, nor even the shadow of an argument, is offered by anyone for the rule of any high priest from the tribe of Levi before the days of Alexander the Great, who sent his commands to the high priest Jaddua as to a ruler. Yet what all Christians assert in order to reconcile Judah’s majesty with the government of priests in that tribe could easily have been employed to reconcile even Cyril’s answer with Jacob’s oracle, had this been permitted with the goodwill of the men named.

The second opinion is that of the Jews. In order to elude the divine force of this prophecy despite their astonishing unbelief, they turn themselves into many shapes; and because they can give no solid answer, they have invented many futile things, chiefly these three. First, that Jacob speaks not of Christ but of Nebuchadnezzar, who was sent by God to remove the scepter from the tribe of Judah and to destroy the kingdom of the Jews in the ruin of the first temple. Reply. Although Julian the Apostate embraced this invention from the Jews, the most learned Jews, both ancient and recent, reject it. All the Chaldean paraphrases, as cited by Casaubon—those of Jonathan and Onkelos, as well as the Jerusalem paraphrase—expressly expound the passage of the Messiah. The Jews of the present day, and for some centuries past, interpret the passage no differently. Nebuchadnezzar certainly was not Shiloh, a peaceful man, but was most warlike and especially cruel to the Jews. Nor was he the expectation of the nations, but the abhorrence of peoples, as a notorious devastator of the world. It is true that the Latin translator renders *Shiloh* as “he who is to be sent,” but elsewhere we demonstrated how corruptly this is done against the Hebrew text.

Another Jewish misinterpretation of the oracle is that Jacob says nothing about the kingdom of Judah, but about Judah’s cross, or the rod that was always to lie upon his back until the days of the Messiah, when all the Jewish people’s adversities were to be changed into prosperity. Reply. This exposition is plainly false, for the tribe of Judah often enjoyed prosperity before the Messiah. In the days of David and Solomon, and at other times too, the Jews enjoyed such peace and every kind of earthly felicity as any nation could rightly desire. The word שבט does indeed mean a rod, but more often a scepter; here it can mean nothing but a scepter. For the whole pro—

The Jews’ third “golden refuge” is that the scepter has always remained in the tribe of Judah down to the present day and will always remain until the days of the Messiah. For, they say, from the captivity under Nebuchadnezzar onward there has been in Babylon an *aichmalotarches*, or Rosh Galut, from the tribe of Judah and the family of David: a head of the captivity who exercises royal authority over all Jews everywhere, especially those of the eastern dispersion. Reply. Astonishing is this effrontery of Maimonides and of other Jews now commonly, who dare shamelessly to affirm this brazen lie. How false it is that throughout the time of the second temple Judah’s scepter remained in Babylon or exercised even the least jurisdiction over free Jews in Jerusalem as though they were Babylonian captives! And today does either the Turkish or the Persian emperor permit any Jew royal power over the Jews in his dominion? Let Apella the Jew believe it. Certainly many things that Benjamin of Tudela relates in his *Itinerary* about the condition of the Jews under the Muslim caliphs seem close to fables. What he says about the ruler of the house of David and his glory is all ludicrous and childish, and implies no jurisdiction over anyone’s life or death, much less signifies supreme authority or a scepter. Finally, even if some Jew possessed royal and exceedingly illustrious dominion in Babylon or anywhere in the world outside Canaan, this would have no bearing on Jacob’s blessing, which was fulfilled entirely within the boundaries of the promised land, as Cunaeus rightly notes from Maimonides.

The third opinion, which we too embrace, is the common opinion of the ancient Jews, the Christian fathers, and most doctors in the Church among Protestants and papists alike. Spanhemius, Rivetus, and others defend it at length: namely, that Jacob’s prophecy was fulfilled when Herod the Great obtained the kingdom of the Jews from the Romans. Then the scepter departed from Judah, a foreign yoke was imposed upon it, and no lawgiver from its seed remained. The Sanhedrin was removed by Herod, and the Jews were left no judgment over anyone’s life for any crime whatever.

To make this nearly universal opinion of Christians more evident, we shall raise various difficulties concerning it.

First: there was no scepter and no lawgiver in the tribe of Judah before the late age of David. Reply. Jacob’s oracle expressly concerns later ages, namely the last days; therefore it did not have to be fulfilled immediately and in the earliest ages. In the days of the judges, indeed, two rulers—Othniel and Abesan—were from the tribe of Judah, and that tribe always possessed the foremost dignity. For just as the double portion of the birthright from which Reuben fell belonged to Joseph, so honor and preeminence belonged to Judah from the beginning. Therefore in the arrangement of the camp, in the temple sacrifices, in the allotment of the land, and in war against the common enemy, the tribe of Judah always held the first lot. Nevertheless, before King David no scepter or settled rule was granted to it either by God or by men. It is more than sufficient for fulfillment of the prophecy that the scepter came into Judah’s hands in David’s days.

The second difficulty is that Judah retained the scepter no longer than until Zedekiah, the last of the Davidic kings, was extinguished, not quite five hundred years; whereas throughout the time of the second temple the Levitical high priests exercised supreme power over the Jews, at least until Herod’s reign. Reply. No adequate argument proves the high priests’ supreme power before the days of the Maccabees, or at least before the days of Alexander the Great. Thus the royal scepter remained in Judah’s hands much longer than with any other tribe. Furthermore, the oracle contains an express distinction: the scepter did not have to remain continually in Judah, but after the kings were removed, lawgivers, governors, and rulers of lesser power were to succeed them. How long these persisted in supreme authority is uncertain. Governors from the house of David certainly seem to have presided in the days of Malachi and the last writers of the Old Testament. Concerning some following centuries we possess nothing besides the testimony of the Apocrypha and of human writers—and very few of those—which does not produce divine faith.

The third difficulty: did the scepter not depart from Judah when Maccabean rulers from the tribe of Levi exercised supreme and royal power over the Jews? Various men give various answers to this very grave doubt. First, some maintain that the Maccabees were truly from the tribe of Judah on their father’s side. Not only Genebrardus but also Baronius held this opinion. Baronius professes that this was his view in the earlier editions of his *Annals*, although he retracted it in the later ones. This is indeed an exceedingly foul error, for in 1 Maccabees—which is authentic Scripture for Baronius—Mattathias’s lineage is most expressly traced to the priest Jehoiarib and the patriarch Levi. Baronius did not retract the argument for his former belief, nor can any papist rightly retract it, for in what they hold as authentic Scripture, 1 Maccabees 5:63, the Maccabees are called “men of Judah.” We readily reply that the Latin text is corrupt here, for the Greek has Καὶ ὁ ἀνὴρ Ἰούδας & οἱ ἀδελφοὶ αὐτοῦ, “Judas the man and his brothers,” words that have no bearing upon the tribe of Judah, although the Louvain editors write them in the Latin margin. Second, others answer that although the Maccabees were not of Judah on their father’s side, they nevertheless touched the tribe of Judah on their mother’s side and possessed Judah’s scepter by maternal right. Baronius professes that he persisted in this opinion even in the final revision of his *Annals*, volume 1, page 3. Reply. This gratuitous assertion concerning the Maccabees’ Jewish mothers, lacking any competent witness, is as easily denied as affirmed. Some well-known examples in Scripture put it beyond doubt that priests were permitted to marry wives from the family of Judah. But nowhere do we read that this was often done, or that the Maccabean priests did so. And even if it were granted, the point in question still would not be proved. Women possessed a different right among the Jews than among other nations: they did not transfer a private inheritance, much less a kingdom, to husbands from other tribes. The Salic law of the French—by which lilies are not spun, women do not reign, and do not transmit the right of the kingdom either to husbands or children—always prevailed among the Israelites. Athaliah’s tyranny and Deborah the prophetess’s extraordinary rule do not weaken the ordinary law.

The sole argument Baronius offers for this answer is as follows. Unless the Maccabees had been from the tribe of Judah at least on their mother’s side, their power would have rested on no right and would indeed have turned into outright tyranny. Reply. What difficulty would there be if, just as William Wallace restored the scepter of the Scots, redeemed by his heroic valor, to its lawful heir Robert Bruce, so Judas Maccabaeus by his God-given valor had restored the liberated kingdom of Judea to the Davidic house, to which alone it belonged? The calamities and tragedies that first afflicted and then utterly exterminated the Maccabean family perhaps demonstrate that their rule was not altogether lawful and acceptable to God. But if anyone wishes to defend their government as entirely lawful, he can do so on this ground: in the final oppression under Antiochus, heroic men were raised up by an extraordinary call from heaven; miraculously aided by God in vindicating the people, they received the government most willingly committed into their hands by the people. This is expressly stated at 1 Maccabees 14:41: until a prophet should arise to signify God’s mind concerning what ought to be done for the people’s welfare amid the Davidic family’s extreme weakness and the Maccabees’ supreme valor, glory, and success.

Finally, fourth, others offer two points that do not displease us, especially if they are joined. First, the Sanhedrin, in which almost the highest power resided, consisted in large part of the tribe of Judah. We indeed do not admit that the Sanhedrin’s power was greater than the ruler’s. The argument some offer from Josephus to prove it is of little weight: that the Sanhedrin summoned Herod himself to answer a capital charge. Josephus indeed relates this fact, but two things weaken the argument’s force. First, the Sanhedrin at that time exercised jurisdiction in the name of Hyrcanus, who was high priest and ruler, indeed king. Second, at that time Herod was only a young man and a mere private person, subject by right to the Sanhedrin’s sentence no less than anyone else among the people.

But if the oracle is not satisfied by judges from the tribe of Judah sitting in the Sanhedrin under the Maccabean rulers and high priests and continuing until the days of the Messiah, add the second point: although the Maccabean rulers were from the tribe of Levi, they nevertheless belonged to the tribe of Judah. For after the return, especially from Babylon, all the Israelites were inserted and incorporated into the tribe of Judah, or at least attached as appendages of that one greatest tribe, and all passed into the name of Jews. Thus the Maccabees were willingly chosen by the Jews as heads and rulers of the nation; they governed in the name and strength of the Jews, and from Jerusalem, their capital city, exercised dominion over the remaining territory according to Jewish laws and decrees. Their scepter could therefore rightly be called Judah’s scepter, and they themselves could rightly be named Jewish rulers and men from the tribe of Judah—that is, the tribe enlarged by appendages of Benjamites, Levites, and others incorporated into it after Jeroboam’s schism, and especially after the return from Babylon. We rest in this fourth answer.

The fifth difficulty: after the scepter was removed from Judah to Herod, Shiloh had not yet come, for Christ was not born before Herod’s thirty-fifth year. Reply. Sacred periods often admit some latitude and do not consist in a mathematically indivisible moment. If Shiloh came in the days of Herod, who first removed the scepter from the seed of Jacob, it is quite rightly said that after the scepter was removed from Judah, Shiloh came and the Messiah was born.

There is still a fourth opinion here, belonging to highly learned men: Scaliger, Casaubon, Cunaeus, Torniellus, Gomarus, and others. They hold that Jacob’s oracle was fulfilled by no means in the days of Herod, but in the final and complete ruin of Jerusalem and the entire Jewish commonwealth. In his interpolation of Scaliger’s opinion, Casaubon distinguishes three moments in the Jewish ruin and thus in the fulfillment of the oracle before us. Besides needlessly confronting almost all the ancients and moderns with an overly open and troublesome front, it is also untrue that Pompey took either autonomy or rule away from the Jews—although he punished them severely—much less reduced them to a province. Moreover, their teaching that the scepter remained in Judah for a full seventy years after Shiloh’s coming and forty years after the Messiah’s death is an innovation so expressly contradicting the oracle that whoever can digest it must have a strong stomach.

To defend his opinion Scaliger introduces a very novel argument, which Casaubon develops at length: Herod was truly a Jew, not an *allophylos*, or foreigner; thus while he reigned a Jew reigned, and the scepter was not removed from Judah. Against this argument the Jesuit Serarius wrote a whole book filled with the bitterest abuse of Scaliger. So much labor was unnecessary, for the whole dispute about this argument is a war of words and therefore easily reconcilable, as Petavius rightly observes in *The Doctrine of Times* 12.5.

There were three ancient opinions concerning Herod’s stock and family. The first was that of Nicolaus of Damascus, a most learned historian who lived on familiar terms with Herod. He asserted that Herod’s ancestors sprang from the Babylonian Jews and therefore that Herod himself was a true Jew, a Hebrew of Hebrews. Josephus teaches that this opinion is false and was fabricated by Nicolaus for the sake of his friend Herod. No mortal embraced it before the Jesuit Torniellus, who at his world-year 4,028 defends it in many words against everyone. Spanhemius, however, demonstrates the weakness of its arguments by answering each one.

The second opinion is diametrically opposed to the first: so far was Herod from being a Jew of Jewish descent that neither he nor his ancestors touched the Jewish nation by any kinship, since they were mere gentiles, Philistines, and Ashkelonites. Eusebius and nearly all the other fathers of the Church uphold this opinion of Africanus. Baronius, Serarius, and very many others cling tenaciously to it. Scaliger attacks it too vehemently, using against Africanus and the Lord’s relatives, who were called the *Desposyni* and from whom Africanus affirms that he learned it, invectives more unworthy than was fitting.

The third opinion was Josephus’s middle position: Herod was neither strictly a Jew nor a gentile, but a half-Jew from the Idumean stock. After John Hyrcanus subjugated them, the Idumeans all embraced circumcision and the rest of the Jews’ religion; having become proselytes, they passed into the Jewish name and rights until Jerusalem’s devastation by Titus. Scaliger and others defend Josephus’s opinion against Africanus and his followers, but he by no means thereby attains his purpose. Grant that Herod was a Jew and proselyte in a broad sense, and thus not strictly a gentile and simply an *allophylos*. It still does not follow that the scepter did not depart from Judah when he reigned. Idumean proselytes were not from the seed of Israel but remained foreigners and outsiders, even in their proselyte condition, so that they possessed no right to the Jews’ scepter or any rule. Indeed, the defenders of this opinion acknowledge that proselytes could not be admitted into the Sanhedrin itself. Therefore, since Herod the Idumean—although an *Idoumaios*—was so foreign and alien to rule over the Jews that he was incapable even of the honor of sitting in the Sanhedrin, when such a man ascended the throne, tyrannically invaded and seized the scepter, and exercised government through the strength of mere Roman gentiles, he burned and destroyed all the native Jews’ courts, not only the supreme Sanhedrin but also the lower courts, and substituted in their place whomever he pleased. Since matters stood thus, one ought not deny that through Herod the scepter and all judicial authority were removed from Judah.

Peter Cunaeus, a most learned man, in *The Commonwealth of the Hebrews* 1.8 and the following chapters, declares in magnificent words, as is his custom, a fifth and new opinion, in which various things displease us. First, he attacks without cause what he calls the fathers’ wretched delusions, even those of Eusebius himself, whom he so highly praises in this very argument, as though they had placed the scepter in Judah’s hands immediately when the oracle was first pronounced. Certainly whoever carefully reads Eusebius, either at the beginning of his *History* or in book 8 of his *Evangelical Demonstration*, will see that Cunaeus wrongly censures Casaubon for asserting that, according to Eusebius, Judah’s scepter began in David’s days, while before then he ascribed to that tribe only a prerogative and preeminence over the others.

Second, Cunaeus’s vaunted invention does not appear solid: namely, that “scepter” and “judge” in Jacob’s oracle signify nothing other than the Jewish people’s condition, commonwealth, and majesty. Jacob certainly does not speak of the whole people or tribe but of rulers and lawgivers sprung from that tribe, namely from Judah’s seed and, as Jacob says, from his thigh.

Third, Cunaeus very wrongly denies that the scepter was given to Judah in the days of David and Solomon, when Judah’s scepter flourished supremely, as Spanhemius rightly proves.
