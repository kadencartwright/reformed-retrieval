23. I now prove the efficacy and predetermination of divine grace by the
following arguments. 1. If God does not efficaciously predetermine the will,
all the promises made by God the Father to the Son could be vain and false:
namely, that he would see his seed, Isaiah 53:10; that a people from the north,
the west, and the islands would come to him, Isaiah 49:1, 12; that by
hereditary right he would possess the nations and the ends of the earth, and
thus even us Britons, entirely separated from the rest of the world, Psalm
2:8; that his dominion would extend from sea to sea and from the river to the
ends of the earth; that the inhabitants of the desert would serve him, Psalm
72:8, 9, 10; that a willing people would hasten to him, Psalm 100:3;

[1651 logical printed p. 466 | IA leaf 484 | PDF p. 484]

that in his days Judah would be saved, and Israel, Jeremiah 23:5, 6; and that
he would set the one shepherd David over his flock to feed them, Ezekiel
34:23. What? Will all these promises be false unless men have determined
themselves to render free worship to God? Will God’s covenant with the Son
(which according to Arminius is inviolable and confirmed by God’s oath) be
void? And will God not be truthful unless human choice has made God’s promise
efficacious? But on this account Christ might have performed what belonged to
him in the covenant—namely, laying down his life for the captives—while God
might not give the people and the seed; and so Christ might have been cheated
of his reward. What do the Arminians say here? That God promises the Son a
voluntary people, not absolutely but from foreknowledge, because he foresaw
that the people would freely serve the Son—that is, if this is worthy of
belief, after the people have served Christ, God promises that they will serve
him. Surely they have rheum in their eyes when they flatter themselves with
such inventions. But Christ is promised this reward for his labors: that a
willing people will obey him and that a seed will minister to him.

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Oration on the Priesthood of Christ*, p. 16.]

24. 2. Argument. If the efficacy of grace with respect to the outcome depended
on free choice, then salvation would belong to the one who wills and runs, not
to God who has mercy, nor would God in this way have mercy on those whom he
wills. Tilenus replies: *I have mercy* (he says) *on those—namely, those who
acknowledge their misery—I have mercy, and I harden whom I will—namely, those
who deserve hardening, because of*

[Marginalia: Tilenus, *Conference with Cameron*, part 1, p. 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 467 | IA leaf 485 | PDF p. 485]

*because of contempt for commoner grace.* So also Corvinus. Reply 1. Faustus
says exactly the same: *I shall have mercy on the one on whom I will—that is, the
one whom I have recognized to be just, whose ready faith I have seen, whom I
have perceived obeying my precepts, and whom I have approved as doing my
will.* 2. God’s freedom in showing mercy will in no way stand here. For if this
man has determined his choice to believe, then by the order of his own justice
and truthfulness God will be necessitated to have mercy on this man rather than
the other.

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 15, fol. 8.]

[Marginalia: Faustus, book 1, ch. 15.]

25. 3. If God decrees to make grace efficacious in these people but not
likewise in those, then the efficacy of God’s grace depends on God’s freedom,
not men’s. But the former is true from the Scriptures. Ephesians 2:10: *We are
his workmanship, created for good works, which God prepared that we should
walk in them.* The Arminians reply that God does not decisively decree that
either Peter or Judas in particular should believe, but only conditionally,
from the prerequisite condition that Peter should will. Reply 1. The question
will regress to infinity: whether the prerequisite condition is decreed by
God, and whether absolutely or conditionally. 2. This decree is foolish, by
which God decreed that Peter should will to believe, provided he should will to
believe. For this willing to believe is no less a supernatural act than
believing itself. 3. Far more foolish is the decree by which God decreed that
Peter should believe, or not, provided he should will, since antecedently to
such a decree God by middle knowledge had foreseen that Peter would believe

[Marginalia: Thus Grevinchovius, pp. 114–115.]

[1651 logical printed p. 468 | IA leaf 486 | PDF p. 486]

finally and that Judas would finally not believe.

26. 4. The use of prayers and thanksgiving for conversion is removed. *What
is more foolish* (Augustine says) *than to ask God that you may do what you
have in your own power?* Why should I, the apostle, give thanks to God for
the faith and love of the Thessalonians? 2 Thessalonians 1:3. What? Should
Peter not ascribe the glory and thanks for his conversion to God more than
Judas?

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins*, book 2, ch.
6.]

27. 5. God’s providence is entirely removed. For here no dominion of God shines
forth by which he might convert Jacob or Peter rather than Esau and Judas. For
if the efficacy of the outcome depends on Peter’s willing, and the failure of
the outcome on Judas’s not willing, then even through God’s providence and
dominion it cannot come about that Judas is converted and saved and Peter is
not converted and is damned. Even the Jesuit Suárez agrees with me here. *For
according to* (he says) *that opinion, it happens as if by accident with
respect to the divine will that in one man sufficient grace was efficacious
and not in another, solely because one is unwilling to cooperate and the other
is willing. Although this cannot be said to happen by chance with respect to
God, since it comes from foreknowledge and from the general will by which he
wills to permit the creature to exercise its own motions, nevertheless with
respect to the divine will that difference cannot be especially attributed to
it, even with respect to the actual efficacy that grace has in the one who is
converted.* Prick up your ears, Arminians: that master Doctor Suárez, to whom
you owe your teaching above all, marks your opinion about the efficacy of
grace as

[Marginalia: Suárez, book 3, *On the Aids of Grace*, ch. 6, fol. 8, p. 117.]

[1651 logical printed p. 469 | IA leaf 487 | PDF p. 487]

ignominious to God’s providence with his censor’s stroke. Indeed, because this
efficacy from the outcome, which depends on human choice, had its futurition
antecedently to God’s middle knowledge and decree, I say that God cannot even
through his omnipotence convert more people than are actually converted, or
leave more unconverted than actually remain unconverted. Hence 6. this
efficacy conflicts with God’s omnipotence; 7. with God’s absolute freedom; 8.
with God’s wisdom and freedom together. For God cannot convert men in another
order or manner, or later or earlier, than they are converted. For antecedently
to God’s middle knowledge and decree, their conversion—in the manner, order,
place, year, day, and hour in which it occurs—had eternal futurition in
eternity. Thus God is constrained by an utterly inexorable fate that precedes
his own will. O miserable fortunes of the church! O the times!

28. 9. The entire rationale of faith perishes, and thus the use of the
ministry. For no one can trust in God for growth in faith, patience, and love
if God does not efficaciously determine the will to these acts. For if the will
itself efficaciously loves, believes, and patiently submits itself to God, a
man trusts in himself rather than in God—indeed, he cannot trust in God at all,
since, with all the strongest actions of grace posited, he is just as able not
to believe, hope, and love as to believe, hope, or love. But they say that a
man ought

[1651 logical printed p. 470 | IA leaf 488 | PDF p. 488]

to trust in God because God performs what belongs to him and is ready and
disposed to perform all these acts, unless the man himself fails himself. Most
empty trifles! What does this mean except that man will fail? But ought we not
to believe that God brings it about by grace that a man does not fail himself?
What an excellent faith and hope: God will bring it about that a man perseveres
in acts of faith, hope, and love, provided that he himself has persevered. But
this itself is the wavering of faith and hope: whether I shall fail myself.
How, then, could I trust in God and in his most powerful grace that I may not
fail myself? Should I trust that God will help me not to fail myself, if I do
not myself fail? Alas, empty consolations, empty and ludicrous hope! But this
is the mystery: I am not to trust in God and his grace for myself, by which I
may persevere to the end in the grace given. But the contrary is found in 1
Corinthians 1:8; Philippians 1:6; Jude 24.

29. 10. It overturns all the solid consolation and patience of the saints in
those evils that befall them from free causes, if the efficacy of a free
cause’s determination comes from free choice. For I would submit myself to God
in vain on account of the hatred, insults, and injuries that men freely inflict
upon me, if God moves their wills by an indifferent and collateral influence,
so that men are just as able not to do those evils as to do them, and God’s
motion contributes no weight to the actual efficacy in those evils.

[1651 logical printed p. 471 | IA leaf 489 | PDF p. 489]

30. 11. It conflicts with the nature and perfection of God’s grace that its
influence and actual efficiency should depend on man’s free choice in order to
exist or not exist. For in this way God has mercy and saves according to the
works we do and human merits; we are not converted according to rich mercy and
God’s gratuitous φιλανθρωπία, or because God graciously has mercy, but because
we will.

31. 12. It conflicts with true humility. For who would not grow proud against
God and likewise over men if he believed that he believes in such a manner and
converts himself so that the fact that he is actually converted and separated
from the sons of this age and the slaves of the Devil must be attributed not
to God but to himself and his own choice?

32. 13. If every effect depends on God in its coming to be because it is a
being, then by the same reasoning every cause depends on God in its causing,
because the τὸ *facere* in a cause is just as much a being dependent on the
first being as the τὸ *fieri* in an effect. But the will’s actively determining
itself is a being no less than the volition itself. Molina, Fonseca,
Grevinchovius, and Tilenus reply that [the determination of the will is the
volition itself and the formal act of the will, and therefore can no more
properly be attributed to God than the τὸ *velle*. Answer. First, the τὸ
*velle* with respect to coming to be is attributed to God as its efficient
cause (for anyone is insane who would have God be the formal cause of

[Marginalia: Molina, *On the Concord*, question 23, article 4.]

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 7, ch. 2, question 5, section 13.]

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, p. 291.]

[Marginalia: Tilenus, *Conference with John Cameron*, p. 89.]

[1651 logical printed p. 472 | IA leaf 490 | PDF p. 490]

volition or even efficiently produce it vitally). Therefore the τὸ *velle* with
respect to causing is attributed to God. For if passive efficiency immediately
depends on God (as William Twiss most soundly observes), why does active
efficiency not also depend on him? For causing is just as much a being in the
category of action as coming to be is in the category of passion. 2. God’s
equivocal, external, and effective determination is not the same act as vital
volition, as I prove thus. God’s external determination and volition differ as
cause and effect. For by external determination God effects the τὸ *velle* in
us; but for volition to effect the τὸ *velle* is absurd. 2. These acts differ in
their formal termini. For the formal terminus of the external determination
that we attribute to God is the will constituted in the first act of its
influence; but the formal terminus toward which formal determination, or
volition, is directed is the eligible good that the will chooses. 3. By this
determination God turns a man’s heart wherever he wills as the absolute lord of
free acts, but the will itself does not turn itself wherever it wills as an
absolute mistress. For then it would not be subject to divine providence. E.
4. Indifference is opposed to determination, but indifference is not formally
opposed to choice; rather, unwillingness or rejection is.

14. Thus Alvarez and the other Dominicans. Every principal cause determines

[1651 logical printed p. 473 | IA leaf 491 | PDF p. 491]

the instruments subordinated to it to their acts. But all creatures, and thus
the human will, are God’s instruments. Therefore they are determined by God to
their acts. The minor premise is from Scripture. Isaiah 10: *Asshur* (God says)
*is the rod of my anger and an axe in God’s hand.* It is said there, I know that
William Twiss judges this Alvarezan argument very acutely. For he says this
cannot be concluded from the peculiar notion of an instrument, even if that
notion is applicable to all secondary causes, because rational instruments
move themselves, and do so as principal causes, whereas passive instruments do
not. But I think it is unnecessary for Alvarez and the others to derive the
argument from the common nature of an instrument, but rather from the nature
of such an instrument. For a servant is his master’s instrument, but the master
need not determine every single act of a servant who happens to be working on
a country estate twenty miles away from his master. But the argument concludes
very well in this form: Every cause is so subordinated to God as every passive
instrument is to a free agent—as an axe to a carpenter or a staff to one moving
a staff—and is determined by God in its action. But all creatures are thus
subordinated to God, from Isaiah 10. Therefore all creatures are determined by
God to their acts. The reason for the major premise is that passive instruments
devoid of life and sense have no causal motion independent of the free agent
in any respect as to the τὸ *facere*. What? Would an axe that was not bent and
moved by the carpenter cut?

[Marginalia: Twiss, *Vindication of Grace against Arminius, Antiperkinian*,
book 2, charge 3, digression 7, ch. 23, p. 190.]

[1651 logical printed p. 474 | IA leaf 492 | PDF p. 492]

The reason for the minor premise is that, on account of the perfection of
divine prudence, all creatures are more subordinated to God in acting than any
creature is to another creature; otherwise God could not exercise absolute
dominion over every act of a creature, which is absurd.

15. The first cause cannot be necessitated to contribute its concurrence to a
secondary cause, since it is a free agent. For in this way any wicked man would
necessitate God to an act of sinning, and the creature would determine the
Creator’s influence toward all the acts of the secondary cause. But? If
secondary causes move first and determine themselves to their acts, either God
would necessarily concur in all the acts of the secondary cause—so that I
could bring it about that God, who does all things according to the counsel of
his will, Ephesians 1:11, would do everything I will at the nod of my will—or
there would be an action of the secondary cause without the action of the first
cause.

16. Finally, the Fathers and Scholastics attribute the determination of the
human will and the efficacy of grace to God, not to freedom. Augustine: *It is
certain that we will when we will, but he causes us to will, supplying the most
efficacious powers, who said, I shall cause you to walk, etc.* And the same
author: *The will of those* (who are converted) *is kindled by the Holy Spirit
to such an extent that they are able because they so will, and that they so
will because God works that they will.* And the same author: *God must be
prayed to will, for if he wills, it must be.* And the same author: *Since one
person is moved to faith in one way and*

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Grace and Free Choice*, ch. 12.]

[Marginalia: *On Rebuke and Grace*, ch. 12.]

*another in another way, and the same matter stated in one way often moves
one but stated in another way does not move him, and moves one person but does
not move another, who could say that God lacked a manner of calling by which
he might apply even Esau’s mind to that faith and join his will to it, in which
Jacob was justified?* And the same author: *What does [Everyone who has heard
and learned from the Father comes to me] mean except that there is no one who
hears and learns from the Father and does not come to me? For if everyone who
has heard and learned comes to me, then assuredly everyone who does not come
has not heard or learned from the Father. For if he had heard and learned, he
would come.* And below: *This grace, which by divine bounty is secretly

deceives nor is deceived. Far be it, then, that anyone who has heard and
learned from the Father should not come.* The same author: *Although we say
that obedience is God’s gift, nevertheless we exhort men to it; but to those

not been given.* And in the same place: *For if everyone who learned comes,
then assuredly the one who does not come did not learn. This is a manner of
speaking according to which God so teaches through the Spirit’s grace that
what each person has learned he not only sees by knowing but also seeks by
willing and completes by acting.* And the same author: *That deep and secret
calling so acts upon the sense that it accommodates assent.* Let anyone who

*On Predestination*, ch. 16; *On Rebuke and Grace*, ch. 11; and *On the Gift of
Perseverance*, ch. 6. Council of Orange: *We profess this salutary teaching:
in every good work we do not begin, but God works in us to will.* They are not

willed?* Here Suárez acknowledges exciting grace that morally predetermines
the will; and although Chrysostom was less versed in Pelagian teaching,
nevertheless it is certain that he acknowledged moral predetermination.
