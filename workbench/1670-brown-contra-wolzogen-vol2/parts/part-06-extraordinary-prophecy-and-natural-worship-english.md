From these arguments it is also abundantly clear that these prophets were not
private members of the church. This can be proved further by the following
arguments:

1. Nowhere in the Scriptures is the word *prophet* used for any private
individual, but it always designates a particular minister: Exodus 7:1;
Deuteronomy 18:20; 1 Samuel 22:5; 2 Samuel 7:2; 1 Kings 11:29; 13:11; 16:7;
18:6; 20:22; 2 Kings 5:3; 6:12; 9:4; 14:25; 19:2; 23:18; and countless other
passages. This is so even in the New Testament: Matthew 21:11; Luke 1:76; 7:16,
28, 39, 40; 24:19; Matthew 21:26; Acts 21:10; 13:1; and 15:32.

2. A prophet is expressly distinguished from a righteous person in Matthew
10:42.

3. They are numbered among the ministers instituted by Christ in the church
in 1 Corinthians 12:28 and Ephesians 4:11, 12.

4. Paul establishes a certain comparison between them and himself in 1
Corinthians 14:37: “If anyone thinks himself a prophet or spiritual, let him
acknowledge that the things I write to you are the Lord’s commandments.”

5. In the same verse he distinguishes a *prophet* from a *spiritual person*:
“If

[Volume II; printed p. 90; ONB canvas 98]

anyone thinks himself a prophet or spiritual.” He wanted everyone, whether
they were ministers or brothers, to hold it as certain and be persuaded that
the things he wrote were the Lord’s commandments.

6. Prophecy is nowhere attributed to private persons by reason of ordinary
gifts. Something very different is implied even when it is attributed to
private persons, Acts 2:17, 18.

7. In 1 Corinthians 12:7–11, *prophecy* signifies not only a gift but also an
ecclesiastical function. In verses 4–6 the apostle says that the gifts, or
*charismata*, are diverse; that the functions, or *administrations*, are also
diverse; and finally that the *operations* are diverse. Then in verses 8–10 he
gives a particular enumeration of them all in relation to these three things.
Therefore *administration* or diaconia belongs no less to prophets than to
other ministers. This term frequently denotes ministry, as in Ephesians 4:12;
Colossians 4:17; 2 Timothy 4:5, 11; Acts 1:17, 25; 12:25; 20:24; 21:19; Romans
11:13; and 2 Corinthians 4:1; 5:18; 6:3; 9:13. *Operation* and *gifts* also
belong to them. They are therefore ecclesiastical ministers, not private
members.

8. These prophets are credited with the effects that elsewhere are credited
to ecclesiastical ministers: public edification, consolation, conviction, and
the conversion of souls in verses 1, 3, 4, 5, 12, 24, 25, and 31, compared
with Romans 10:14, 15 and 1 Corinthians 4:1–3.

Let us now see what the adversaries reply, whether to weaken our position or
to confirm their own.

*Reply 1.* This prophecy belongs to everyone who is bound to edify, exhort,
and console others. *Response.* 1. This is plainly false, for women are
expressly excluded in verses 34 and 35, although they are nevertheless bound
to edify and exhort other Christians, and so forth. 2. All ministers, whether
ordinary or extraordinary, were given for this end, Ephesians 4:11–13, because
everything ought to be done for edification.

*Reply 2.* As long as the end—namely, *edification* and so forth—endures, the

[Volume II; printed p. 91; ONB canvas 99]

means will also endure. *Response.* Then all the institutions of the Old
Testament will still endure: *circumcision, the celebration of Passover*, and
so forth; indeed, the *apostolate* and all the extraordinary gifts, such as
*tongues, interpretation*, and so forth, will endure as well. 2. Grant that
prophecy remains and endures even to the present day. It still will not follow
that it is not something extraordinary, much less that it belongs to any and
all members of the church or to every private person.

*Reply 3.* No other means remains, and Paul urges both private persons and
ministers to this from the common duty of love. *Response.* On the contrary,
there are very many other means of edification, both public and private. 2. By
an argument drawn from the common duty of love, every member of the church,
whether a minister or someone else, can be urged to the works proper to each
one. Yet it does not follow from this that all those works are common and must
be performed by everyone. Nor, likewise, does it follow that this prophecy is
common to everyone.

*Reply 4.* It does not appear that there were as many prophets of this sort in
one church as can be gathered to have been here, especially since
extraordinary prophecy had then begun to vanish. *Response.* Many splendid
things are said of this church in 1 Corinthians 1:5, 7. From this we may gather
that there could have been several prophets of this kind in it. 2. Although at
that time the Old Testament prophets, who foretold many things about the
Messiah to come through visions and similar manifestations, had begun to
cease, that peculiar prophecy of the New Testament had not yet vanished.
Indeed, Justin Martyr says in the *Dialogue with Trypho* that prophets of this
kind continued in the church until his own time. 3. Even if they had not been
extraordinary, it still does not follow that they were merely private persons
furnished with gifts, for they could have been ordinary ministers, as
Gualterus and others think. But if they say it is no less improbable that
there were so many ordinary ministers here, when a scarcity of sacred
interpreters was experienced almost everywhere: since this epistle was not
written before AD 64,

[Volume II; printed p. 92; ONB canvas 100]

it is not probable that there was such a great scarcity of sacred
interpreters. 2. It appears from chapters 1, 2, and 3 that there were several
ordinary laborers there. The words of chapter 4:15 intimate something of this
sort, and the Presbyterians have proved that this church was not one
particular congregation.

*Reply 5.* This is attributed to each and every person in verse 31; and just as
everyone was permitted to learn and be edified, so everyone was permitted to
exercise prophecy. *Response.* 1. They will nevertheless not deny that an
exception must be admitted for women, who were permitted to learn. Therefore
the inference is null. 2. The apostle plainly indicates in 1 Corinthians
12:28, 29 that not everyone was a prophet. 3. What should be judged concerning
this mark of universality is clear from the disputes against the Arminians. 4.
Therefore this must be restricted to the prophets, namely, those who possessed
this gift. See verse 5.

*Reply 6.* When he prohibits women from exercising prophecy, he grants this
liberty to all men, because he sets sex against sex and in this respect
ascribes the prerogative to the man. *Response.* Nothing follows from this, for
we grant that this liberty was conceded to certain men, namely ministers. 2.
Whenever women are prohibited, the permission is not extended to all men. For
each and every Frenchman is not permitted to rule the whole kingdom of France,
although under the *Salic law* a woman is not permitted to steer the helm. 3.
Indeed, the adversaries themselves will deny the inference. For they grant
this faculty only to men furnished with gifts. But those not furnished with
gifts are men and, with respect to sex, are opposed to women no less than the
most richly gifted men are. Who does not know that in a contingent matter an
indefinite proposition is equivalent to a particular one? How weak,
therefore, is the inference: this is permitted to men; therefore it is
permitted to all men without exception? 4. Women are not permitted to
administer the sacraments. Will all men without exception therefore be
permitted to administer them? The inference is null.

[Volume II; printed p. 93; ONB canvas 101]

*Reply 7.* Nevertheless, when women are prohibited from this exercise, it is
clear that these exercises were not extraordinary, or that these prophets
were not extraordinary, because prophetesses were permitted to prophesy
publicly, and such women are mentioned in Holy Scripture. Miriam was one, and
there were others, as is clear from Judges 4:24 and Luke 2:36. *Response.* 1.
Pareus says on the passage that “women prophetesses are not excepted, because
the rationale is universal: the law subjected women universally to men and by
that very fact removed from women the authority to teach men.” 2. Nowhere do
we read that these women prophetesses were permitted to set forth their
prophecies in the churches. If they object with 1 Corinthians 11:4, 5, it can
be answered:

(1.) From this it can be proved, even against the adversaries’ will, that in
chapter 14 the apostle understands extraordinary prophecies or prophetic
exercises.

(2.) Many understand by a man or woman prophesying one who hears and
participates, as Hoornbeeck does above on page 118. Beza says on the passage:
“I understand these things of public prayer and prophecy, that is, the
treatment of Scripture. I do not refer it merely to the person who frames the
prayer or interprets the passage of Scripture but also to the assembly of
hearers. For even if one person speaks, the action is nevertheless common, so
that the whole assembly can in this way be said to pray and prophesy.” Pareus
does the same.

(3.) There is also another response given by some, which Hoornbeeck above
expresses in this way: “It can also be explained that in chapter 11 the apostle
does indeed speak of a woman prophesying but does not for that reason approve
it. Rather, there he was content to censure in women the preceding custom of
attending sacred rites ἀκατακαλύπτῳ τῇ κεφαλῇ, since that is what he
expressly treats there; but the fact that they prophesied besides this, the
apostle reserved for its own place, namely this one, and thus here openly
reproves in women what he passed over before.”

[Volume II; printed p. 94; ONB canvas 102]

(4.) If women prophetesses were permitted to set forth their prophecies in
public sermons, why would they not also be permitted to speak and exercise
ordinary gifts? If the greater is permitted to them, why would the lesser not
also be permitted?

3. Although this rule about women in verse 34 is understood of ordinary
exercises, it does not follow that the exercises mentioned in verses 26–28
were of the same kind, that is, also ordinary. For he does not permit women
even to propose questions publicly in the churches but tells them to learn at
home. But to prophesy in verse 26 and following was something very different
from proposing questions.

*Reply 8.* From verse 37: “If anyone thinks himself a prophet, let him
acknowledge,” and so forth. For if they had been extraordinary prophets, they
would not have been ignorant that Paul’s writings were canonical. *Response.*
The prophets did not know everything but were ignorant of the mind of God in
certain things, for some things they understood only as human beings.

*Reply 9.* From verse 36: “Did the word of God go forth from you?” and so
forth. If the word of God did not go forth from them, they were not
extraordinary prophets. *Response.* Nothing is concluded. For the sense of the
question can be: did it go forth from you alone and not also from us apostles?
Or: did it go forth from you in the way it did from the apostles?

*Reply 10.* Judas and Silas are called prophets in Acts 15:22. Yet they are
distinguished from ministers in verse 22. *Response.* Silas is regarded by
many as an evangelist because of his journeys, Acts 16:17; 17:4, 10, 14, 15;
and 18:5. (2.) They are indeed called *brothers*, but ministers too were
sometimes so called in 2 Corinthians 8:18, 22, 23. Thus Apollos is so called in
1 Corinthians 16:12, although he was a minister according to 1 Corinthians
3:5. So too were Timothy in 2 Corinthians 1:1 and Hebrews 13:23; Sosthenes in 1
Corinthians 1:1; Silvanus in 1 Peter 5:12; Tychicus in Ephesians 6:21 and
Colossians 4:7; and Philemon in Philemon verse 7. (3.) They are not so much
called *brothers* as *chief men among the brothers*, which denotes something
different.

*Reply 11.* From Revelation 11:3. Those witnesses are said to prophesy,

[Volume II; printed p. 95; ONB canvas 103]

and they are private believers. *Response.* Junius, Pareus, our Durham, and
Couperus understand those witnesses as ministers. 2. If it is understood of
private persons who offer testimony by their life and martyrdom, nothing is
concluded from it. 3. The adversaries do not prove that these witnesses were
mere private persons, nor do they prove that this act of prophesying was an
act of public preaching. But they must prove these things.

*Reply 12.* From chapters 12:7 and 14:26, this liberty was given to each
person. *Response.* (1.) Although a manifestation or disclosure of the Spirit
is given to each person for usefulness, not all the same gifts are given to
each, as verses 8–10 prove. (2.) First Corinthians 14:24 presupposes that not
everyone prophesied; otherwise the greatest confusion would follow. (3.) As
for verse 26: 1. The sense may be that each possessed these gifts objectively,
as in 1 Corinthians 3:21, 22; Luke 16:29; Philippians 3:17; Hebrews 13:10; and
2 Peter 1:15. 2. If it is taken subjectively, it must then also be said that
everyone possessed gifts of tongues and so forth, and thus that extraordinary
case should not be drawn into an example. 3. The adversaries themselves
restrict it to brothers furnished with gifts. We will be no less permitted to
restrict it to prophets, as in Ephesians 5:33; Isaiah 9:17; and Luke 12:15. 4.
“Ἕκαστος,” says Beza on the passage, “that is, one has a psalm, another has a
doctrine, and so forth. For τὸ ἕκαστος must be resolved in this way, lest
anyone think that each person was furnished with all those gifts. Another
example of this kind of expression occurs above in chapter 1, verse 12.” And
indeed that other example very well explains this passage. The Syriac and
Arabic versions also confirm this sense.

*Reply 13.* The Presbyterians therefore wrongly gather the subordination of
ecclesiastical judgments from verse 32, “The spirits of the prophets are
subject to the prophets.” *Response.* On the contrary, the argument presses
more strongly, for if prophets immediately inspired by God were subject to the
judgment and examination of others, much more ought pastors and other

[Volume II; printed p. 96; ONB canvas 104]

ordinary ministers to submit themselves to the judgment of the presbyterial
college.

*Reply 14.* Everything here is ordinary, and ordinary exercises are
regulated. *Response.* Then the gift of tongues, about which verses 6, 14, 23,
and 26 speak, and other things of that kind, were ordinary.

*Reply 15.* Prophecy is distinguished from ministry in Romans 12:6, 7.
*Response.* 1. Some understand prophecy even there as an extraordinary gift,
including Gomarus on the passage and our Gillespie. 2. Others, however,
commonly understand it as the office of pastors and teachers, and so it in no
way favors the objection. See the commentators. But no one understands it as
an ordinary gift common to any private believer or as the private gifts of
believers.

*Reply 16.* The gifts required in these prophets were ordinary and were
acquired or to be acquired by private study and industry, verses 1 and 26.
*Response.* This is denied and is not proved from the fact that they were given
for edification. 2. Does not the same verse 26 mention *tongues* and
revelations? 3. Prophecy was indeed to be preferred to the gift of tongues in
this respect: the church was more edified through it. But despite this,
prophecy could be extraordinary and not acquired by private industry and
study, but given by revelation and inspiration. Whatever tends more to
edification is not therefore ordinary. The office of the apostles tended to
this end, but it was not ordinary.

*Reply 17.* In verse 1 the apostle wanted them to seek eagerly to prophesy. But
if the gift was extraordinary, how could they seek it eagerly? How could they
ask for it in faith? *Response.* 1. In the same passage he also wanted them to
seek other spiritual gifts eagerly. 2. The prophet Elisha eagerly sought and
asked for a double portion of Elijah’s spirit in 2 Kings 2:9, and Daniel
obtained an extraordinary interpretation by prayers in chapter 2:17–19 and so
forth. 3. The interpretation of tongues was an extraordinary gift, yet he

[Volume II; printed p. 97; ONB canvas 105]

commands in verse 13 that the person who “spoke in a tongue should pray that
he might interpret.” They could therefore then seek such gifts eagerly by
which they would edify the church, chapter 12:7, especially since the promise
of Mark 16:17 stood: “These signs, moreover, will follow those who believe:
through my name they will cast out demons, speak with new tongues, pick up
serpents, and if they drink anything deadly, it will by no means harm them;
they will lay hands on the sick, and they will be well.” Why, then, could the
prayer of faith not obtain this gift just as well as health for a sufferer in
James 5:15? 4. In former times prophets also used means by which they disposed
and prepared themselves to prophesy, as can be seen in 2 Kings 3:15. By that
instrument, as Cajetan says on the passage, the prophet stirred up the inward
gathering of his mind and consequently the elevation of his mind to God,
although the hand of God caused him to prophesy. Paul’s sense here can also be
that not everyone without exception should seek this gift eagerly, but that
those moved to it by the Spirit should ask for it and seek it eagerly, as our
Rutherford says in *On the Presbytery*.

*Reply 18.* These prophets were rebuked by Paul for ἀταξίαν; therefore they
were not extraordinary prophets. *Response.* Then Peter was not an
extraordinary apostle, because Paul rebuked him, Galatians 2:2. We will show
immediately that the prophets erred not as such, actually, or insofar as they
were inspired, but as human beings.

*Reply 19.* But the apostle also subjects their doctrine to the examination of
others in verses 29 and 32. It appears that he would not do so if here he had
signified doctrine flowing from a pure, immediate, singular, and extraordinary
revelation of the Holy Spirit, as it did in the prophets. *Response.* 1. If
their doctrine was not to be examined, what was the purpose of “Beware of
false prophets” and similar passages, Matthew 7:15; 24:11, 24; and 1 John 4:1?
2. Are not the Bereans praised because they examined Paul’s doctrine

[Volume II; printed p. 98; ONB canvas 106]

in Acts 17:11? 3. Pareus says on verse 32: “It will happen sometimes that one
prophet has something to rebuke or amend in another’s doctrine.” And a little
later: “The Holy Spirit did not dictate everything to everyone, but it was
given to each according to a certain measure. It could therefore happen that
the prophets mingled certain things of their own with the gift of the Spirit;
it was the task of the other prophets to judge this.” Beza says, “That is, the
doctrine that the prophets bring forward when inspired by God’s Spirit. For
there are many false prophets and false inspirations that may deceive even
otherwise true prophets.”

*Reply 20.* It would then follow that extraordinarily inspired prophets could
also err in doctrine. *Response.* 1. Although they could not err insofar as
they were inspired by the Spirit, they nevertheless had their errors and
lapses at other times, as appears in Samuel, 1 Samuel 16:6; Nathan, 2 Samuel
7:3; and Elijah, 1 Kings 19:14 and Romans 11:3. The same is true of those in
Acts 21:4. Beza’s words should be noted: “What then? Does the Holy Spirit
contradict himself? By no means. But those men, understanding from the Spirit’s
revelation how great a danger threatened Paul, begged him out of love, not
from any particular command of the Holy Spirit, not to go up into the city,
ignorant of what the same Spirit had commanded Paul in turn.” Therefore,
although the prophets were infallible insofar as they were inspired—not
infallible simply, for in that sense God alone is infallible—just as even false
prophets had been while they were being inspired by God, though they were
habitually inspired by the spirit of falsehood, nevertheless at other times
they could err and did err. Furthermore, it must be noted that there is one
account of these prophets, who were endowed with the extraordinary gift of
interpreting the Scriptures, and another account of those who foretold future
things that were merely contingent. Although the latter in the act of
foretelling were not, as it were, their own masters—Ezekiel 2:14; Jeremiah
20:9; 2 Kings 3:15—the former nevertheless appear to have had a greater use of
their free choice and therefore could add or mingle in certain things of their
own by way of illustration or application. And

[Volume II; printed p. 99; ONB canvas 107]

they should not be said to have been infallible in these things. But enough
concerning this objection.

We now come to examine what Velthuysen has in his vernacular book *On Idolatry
and Superstition*, page 290 and following. He distinguishes between *natural*
and *instituted* worship, and on pages 291 and 292 says there is no part of
natural worship that can be administered by one person and not another. We do
not deny that *natural* worship, which is also called internal and is commanded
by the first precept of the Decalogue, is distinguished from *instituted*
worship, which is enjoined by the second precept of the Decalogue. Nor do we
say that this internal or natural worship, as such, should be performed by any
order of people to the exclusion of others, or by any people particularly
required or specially set apart for it and not by all others. Velthuysen,
therefore, wanders beyond the bounds. For whether before the law of Moses was
given or afterward, the exercise of this internal and natural worship was
never particularly assigned to any ministers or any order of people to the
exclusion of others. Rather, it was always required of every human being that
they know and acknowledge God as the one true God; worship and glorify him;
think and meditate on him and remember him; esteem, honor, choose, love,
desire, and fear him; believe him and trust in him; hope and delight and
rejoice in him; and finally invoke and praise him, obey him in everything, and
subject themselves to him. Yet it would have been much more appropriate to
distinguish worship into *public* and *private*. Whether or not Velthuysen
acknowledges this distinction, it is certain that both exist and that both are
taught by nature: namely, that God should be worshiped not only privately but
also publicly, not only internally but also externally. Hence the Gentiles,
guided by the light of nature alone, instituted external worship. But it is no
less true that certain parts of this natural worship can

[Volume II; printed p. 100; ONB canvas 108]

and should be performed in public assemblies—namely, prayers, thanksgivings,
and similar actions—and it is certain that the principal parts in these
actions belong to pastors and leaders of the flock. For they lead in them;
the others join, follow, and assent. It is also clear that in the performance
of natural worship insofar as it is done publicly, something particular has
been laid on the shoulders of ecclesiastical ministers. But the things that
follow must be examined briefly.

On page 292 he has certain things that are neither relevant nor sound. For he
says, *Een Vader heeft*, and so forth, that is: “A father has the right and
power to prescribe a form of worship for his family, as a master does for his
servants and a magistrate for his subjects. Yet no one’s conscience is bound
by this prescription in any way except that it is not permissible to resist
by force; and no one is bound to order his worship according to this
prescription when in his mind he understands that it is not rational and
right.” These things, I say, have no relevance to the present matter, because
his subject now is natural and internal worship, not external worship; but the
form that parents, masters, or magistrates prescribe concerns external and
instituted worship, whether true or false. Next, it is false to say that
children, servants, or subjects are not bound to observe the form prescribed
by parents, masters, or magistrates if they judge it false or not conformable
to God’s word. For if that form really is entirely conformable to God’s word,
they are bound to embrace it even though their erroneous conscience has
determined the matter to be otherwise. The reason is that although an
erroneous conscience binds, it does not impose an obligation, nor does it
free anyone from the obedience owed to God’s law. For God’s law obligates
whether our conscience judges rightly or wrongly. Therefore, if that form is
divinely prescribed, it obligates those inferiors both by reason of its matter
and by reason of the authority with which those superiors are endowed.

[Volume II; printed p. 101; ONB canvas 109]

In what follows he anticipates an objection no less ridiculously when he says,
*Oock mach een Vader als Vader*, and so forth, that is: “A father as father, a
master as master, and a governor as such can do something religiously that
another person not clothed with the same circumstances cannot do. But from
this,” he says, “it does not follow that there is any part of natural worship
to be administered by some and not by others. For,” he says, “a son, servant,
or subject can do many things by way of worship that a father, master, or
magistrate cannot do. Of this kind is obedience itself, which is to be
rendered to superiors for conscience’s sake; for it is a good work and highly
pleasing to God, which nevertheless only a subject can perform.” Who does not
see that Velthuysen plays with trifles here and accomplishes nothing? And who
does not also see how he cuts his own throat with his sword? For if the
obedience of inferiors, rendered to superiors from the force of conscience and
religiously, is proper to them, and consequently it belongs only to superiors
to command inferiors from the force of conscience and religiously, why will
the same not hold for ecclesiastical ministers and the flock, so that many
things can be performed by the former from the force of conscience and
religiously that cannot be performed by the latter?

But he continues on page 293, *veel minder volgt het*, and so forth, that is:
“Much less does it follow that there is any part of natural worship that by
the nature of the thing can be administered only by some, because certain
people for legitimate reasons free themselves from the exercise of this part
and assign it to others. It is the office of a father, master, or magistrate
to care for the welfare of a son, servant, or subject and therefore to teach
them the true worship of God. But if for the gravest reasons they themselves
cannot do this and, being freed from it, commend it to others, it by no means
follows,” he says, “that in natural worship only certain persons ought to
discharge such a religious office,” and so forth. As we see, Velthuysen is
prolix in proving what no one denies. Yet the man’s craftiness must be
observed, for by arguing in this way he wants to insinuate

[Volume II; printed p. 102; ONB canvas 110]

that it can in no way belong particularly to pastors to teach the flock
natural worship of God publicly and authoritatively. We do not deny that the
right to teach that natural worship of God belongs in its own way to parents,
masters, and magistrates. Nevertheless, it by no means follows that it does
not belong principally to pastors, who have been expressly commissioned by
God to declare his whole will to the flock and who for that reason are called
*pastors* and *teachers*. Furthermore, I would like Velthuysen to explain to me
what that part of natural worship is which a person cannot lawfully administer
but can commission to another. And if human beings can commit and commission
to others the actual exercise of natural worship, can God also commit some
part of it in public assemblies particularly to persons specially designated
and set apart by him for it? And in that case, will it not be unlawful for
anyone else to undertake this part of worship? Finally, he either plays with
trifles or supposes that teaching others the true worship of God is a part of
natural worship. If he supposes this, we have what we wanted: namely, that the
office of publicly, officially, authoritatively, and ordinarily teaching the
lawful worship of God and the whole revealed will of God belongs only to
pastors lawfully called and sent, no less than the right to administer certain
parts of instituted worship belonged to priests and Levites under the Old
Testament, as is abundantly clear from the reasons alleged above.

He has not yet had enough of trifles, for on page 294 he continues and says:
“This does not follow from the fact that we are bound to show great deference
to those whom we judge to be especially wise in matters concerning the
worship of God.” Who, I ask, has ever inferred or could infer from such an
antecedent the consequent that certain functions concerning natural worship
have been assigned only to certain persons?

[Volume II; printed p. 103; ONB canvas 111]

Velthuysen invents these things from his own brain, as though accomplishing
nothing; but by thus wasting all his effort and oil and devising such
ridiculous things, he cannot avoid exposing himself to the laughter of
everyone endowed with judgment. The things that follow on pages 295 and 296
quite sufficiently demonstrate his erratic and wandering mind, since they
have no relevance whatever to the present matter. They also sufficiently
indicate the end for which this anticipation was brought forward: namely, to
provide himself an occasion to censure those theologians who, when asked,
offered their judgment about a book published for the public or an opinion on
a theological matter proposed by someone. They were bound by their office to
give this judgment so that God’s truth, which they are bound to preserve safe
and sound and transmit to posterity, might suffer no harm. Nor does he act
candidly when he insinuates that such judgments are offered so that others,
having been prejudiced, cannot even exercise the judgment of discretion. He
errs no less when he thinks that, once this judgment of the theologians has
been given, the magistrate can judge the same case in his own manner only in
vain. For the latter judges it only politically, in relation to his own act,
namely, approving or disapproving, tolerating or eliminating this dogma. And
if, resting on no solid foundation, magistrates determine otherwise than the
theologians or some ecclesiastical synod have determined according to God’s
word, they have something for which they must answer to God. Yet the divinely
approved theological or ecclesiastical decision remains firm and fixed. And
if any disadvantage follows from it, this is by no means to be imputed to the
theologians or to the ecclesiastical judgment, since they contend for the
truth, than which nothing is dearer to all the godly, in keeping with the
general as well as the special office commissioned to them; they testify,
pronounce their opinion, and defend it to the best of their ability.

“Just as,” he says on pages 296 and 297, “in natural worship there is no part
that either cannot be administered by each person,

[Volume II; printed p. 104; ONB canvas 112]

or whose administration, for the sake of order, cannot be committed to this
person or that, without any discrimination, provided he is furnished with
gifts, so that with respect to this administration there are no special
persons, families, or nations whose work alone must be employed, so that
administration which belongs to anyone is nevertheless exercised by some
only for the sake of order. It does not render them holier than others, since
no one can give another greater — holiness than he himself possesses.”
*Response.* Velthuysen here supposes that there are certain parts of natural
worship that can be administered by particular persons designated for them.
And if he speaks of the exercise of this worship, or of those parts of natural
worship, in public or in public ecclesiastical assemblies, to whom, I ask, are
these parts to be commissioned if not to public pastors and ecclesiastical
ministers?

2. But to whomever this office devolves, he wants it to be done only *for the
sake of order*. Smalcius also concedes this against Frantzius on page 379. “I
say,” he writes, “that they”—that is, those who truly can teach—“can teach,
that is, nothing in reality prevents them from possessing the right to speak.
The question of fact is whether they ought to teach. To this it has already
been answered that sufficient order should be employed, not from necessity
but on account of εὐταξίαν and propriety.” Therefore he acknowledges no
divinely instituted ecclesiastical ministry to which this exercise belongs
*by right*, but he wants this office to be numbered among the ἀδιάφορα.
Indeed, he recognizes no office at all as required for performing that
worship, for he affirms that this burden can be imposed on anyone who is
furnished with gifts, without any distinction. Therefore it makes no
difference whether one is a man or a woman, a pastor or a sheep, although
Smalcius denies this above. He therefore acknowledges no one specially called,
sent, and instituted by God and set apart for this office. What follows also
confirms this, where he says that this office was commissioned to *no persons*
in particular. No one, not even a papist, has ever asserted that God has now
chosen any family or nation for this work,

[Volume II; printed p. 105; ONB canvas 113]

so Velthuysen himself knows why he also mentions families and nations. Yet it
should be noted that the Socinians say this same thing. See Socinus, epistle 3
to Radec, page 125, as though we said anything of the kind.

3. We freely grant that no one is rendered holier by the office he
discharges. Nevertheless, whoever denies that God sanctifies certain people—
that is, sets them apart, ordains, institutes, and sends them to discharge a
certain ecclesiastical office—does violence to the Scriptures and right
reason, as we have already shown.

4. Nor is the added reason of any force. For human beings only instrumentally
set others apart for this office; all force and power is from God.

On pages 297 and 298 he continues, *Oocken kan men*, and so forth, that is:
“Similarly,” he says, “in natural worship no ceremonies can be found by which
any special holiness is applied to anyone, nor anything ceremonial that
possesses any holiness beyond the foundation of natural worship on which its
lawful ceremonial use must rest. The laying on of hands is merely a sensible
rite borrowed from the Jews, who used to lay on hands in all blessings or
inaugurations. But there is no greater holiness in that ceremony than in the
ring, gown, or cap with which those who are promoted are clothed. And if there
is any special holiness in the laying on of hands, this ceremony was wrongly
applied under the New Testament, or it must be shown that it too was
instituted by God under the New Testament and that it was no part of
ceremonial worship.” *Response.* 1. Although no such ceremony can be found in
natural worship, does it therefore follow that no ceremony whatever exists or
can exist?

2. As for the laying on of hands, whatever its use was under the Old
Testament, we will show later that it must be employed in every ordination
properly performed.

3. Some say that the rite of baptizing was also borrowed from the Jews. Will
the rite therefore not have been instituted by Christ under the New Testament
and have to be perpetually observed?

[Volume II; printed p. 106; ONB canvas 114]

4. It smacks of profanity to insinuate that there is no greater holiness in
Christ’s institutions than in human institutions. By equal right Velthuysen
can say that there is no greater holiness in baptism, which is the solemn rite
of reception into Christ’s family, than in the ring, gown, and cap, which are
human rites invented by human beings to render more solemn a promotion in
some science or faculty, since he says there is no greater holiness in the
laying on of hands. For we will show that it was instituted by Christ, so that
it cannot lawfully be neglected in the ordination of pastors when no physical
or similar impediment stands in the way. And when we have demonstrated this,
we will sufficiently show that it was not part of ceremonial worship, which
was entirely abolished under the New Testament.

On pages 299 and 300 he offers another anticipation, saying, *Men is niet
beter gefondeert*, and so forth, that is: “It rests on no better foundation to
conclude, from the fact that some exercise of worship is assigned to certain
people, that those particular offices designated for certain functions of
worship—to which each person has a right but which for the sake of order are
committed to some—were always and among all nations distinct, administered by
distinct persons, and therefore by no means to be confounded in the persons.”
*Response.* Although we might not be able to prove—though it is not
improbable—that offices designated and instituted for certain functions and
administrations of worship were administered among all nations by certain
distinct and specially set-apart persons, it would by no inference follow from
this that these ecclesiastical offices are not now under the gospel to be
performed by distinct and set-apart persons, since we have already proved
clearly enough that the matter was so constituted by God. And therefore we say
that magistrates constituted to administer justice to subjects are no more
permitted to seize the office of teaching for themselves than pastors are
permitted to assume for themselves the office of a political judge,

[Volume II; printed p. 107; ONB canvas 115]

because each is prohibited in the new law. Velthuysen, who appears to
insinuate the contrary in what follows, is no less injurious to magistrates
than to pastors and subverts all political order no less than ecclesiastical
order. But let him be heard on page 300, *Al is aen een Collegie gegeven*, and
so forth, that is: “Although the administration of justice has been committed
to some college, nevertheless anyone can pronounce judgment for his neighbor
and act as judge in deciding controversies between neighbors, provided this
exercise does not conflict with the function of judges, impede it, or render
it empty.” Was I not right to say that he overthrows the magistracy no less
than the ministry? I do not doubt that anyone summoned by his neighbors and
chosen to act as arbiter can settle disputes on that occasion. But it is
something very different to pronounce judgment and constitute oneself a
judge, or to act the part of a judge by even passing sentence against an
unwilling person and compelling the guilty party to do what is equitable, and
so forth. I do not judge that this should be tolerated in any constituted
commonwealth, nor do I see how it can be done in such a way that the office of
the magistrate is not at the same time rendered empty. *Soo oock, al heeftmen
Arm-besorgers gestelt*, and so forth, that is: “Similarly, although people have
been appointed to care for the poor, nevertheless anyone can give his alms to
others; and the alms those deacons distribute is not of a different nature
from what someone gives privately.” *Response.* In this way he tries to render
the deacons’ office empty too, but in vain. Although any private person,
whether male or female, whether a member of the church or not, is permitted to
exercise mercy toward the poor and give them alms, they are nevertheless not
permitted to assume the office of deacon for themselves. And although the
alms that the private person gives is not of a different physical nature from
what the deacons distribute, the private person’s action of giving alms is
nevertheless very different from the deacons’ action of distribution. For the
deacons distribute alms officially and

[Volume II; printed p. 108; ONB canvas 116]

by reason of their special office and particular commission, while private
persons give alms not officially or by the force of an office or special
commission, but by the force of a general command and calling, insofar as they
are bound by reason of God’s moral law to exercise mercy. What follows is
similar: *En al heeft men*, and so forth, that is: “Although certain people are
chosen who ought specially to teach and instruct the flock in religion, it
does not nevertheless follow that others are by no means permitted to do so,
or that the act of teaching by those who have not undertaken this office is of
a different nature and less efficacy than that of the others when they teach
the same thing. For this is the same as if someone said that the same medicine
given by a pharmacist has less efficacy than when it is given by a physician.”
*Response.* By this reasoning Velthuysen will abolish all order both in the
commonwealth and in the church; indeed, he will destroy every institution of
Christ. For it can be asked: How does baptism administered by a woman, child,
or insane person differ from the baptism of a lawfully called pastor? Is the
sprinkling of water performed by a child, monkey, or fool of a different
nature or less efficacy than the sprinkling performed by a pastor? Is the
distribution of bread and wine performed by a woman, child, or perhaps a
trained dog of a different physical nature or less force and efficacy than the
distribution performed by a pastor? What will Velthuysen say to these things?
Does he see how greatly they smack of profanity? But what, I ask, came into
Velthuysen’s mind when he adduced such a dissimilar comparison? Do God’s
institutions operate physically as a medicine operates? What? Was Velthuysen
ignorant of this, or did he conceal it? If he concealed it, he shows plainly
enough how desperate his case is. If he truly and sincerely said this, he
should be sent back to elementary schools. What? Is Velthuysen ignorant that
one and the same action considered physically can be very diverse and
distinct when considered morally and ethically? Is the physical action by
which

[Volume II; printed p. 109; ONB canvas 117 (upper portion)]

a magistrate condemns a guilty person to death and finally puts him to death
the same as that by which a private person kills someone perhaps guilty of
death out of private anger? Will this, therefore, be of the same nature and no
less efficacy, and for that reason no less lawful, than the former action? Who
does not see where this man’s irrational principles and assertions tend?
