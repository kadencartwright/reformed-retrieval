TOPIC ONE—THEOLOGY

QUESTION ONE

Should the Word *Theology* Be Used in Christian Schools, and in How Many Ways
Is It Understood?

[Marginal heading: The word THEOLOGY is rightly used.]

I. Since the rules of an accurate method require us “first to examine the
names,” that is, to weigh the usage and force of names beforehand, as the
Philosopher wishes, because words are signs of things, certain points about
the word *theology* must be set out before we come to the thing itself.
Although the question proposed may seem hardly necessary, given this word's
common and accepted use among nearly everyone, who thinks that it should be
retained as a technical term that properly and emphatically declares its own
subject, we must nevertheless meet the opinion of certain people who dislike
the name because it does not occur in Scripture, is commonly used to signify
the heathen's vain discourse, and, they judge, should more suitably be
replaced by other words taken from Scripture.

II. Although the word *theology* is not written verbatim in Scripture, it is
not entirely unwritten, since the simple words from which it is composed
frequently occur there, such as “word of God” and “oracles of God,” Romans
3:2; 1 Peter 4:10; Hebrews 5:12. Thus it is one thing for something to be in
Scripture as to sound and syllables, or formally and in the abstract; it is
another for it to be there as to sense and the thing signified, or materially
and in the concrete. Theology is not present in Scripture in the former way,
but it is in the latter.

III. Although it is not lawful to fashion any dogmas outside Scripture, it is
nevertheless sometimes lawful to use words not found in Scripture if they
are serviceable either for explaining divine things or for guarding against
errors. For this purpose theologians have used the words *Trinity*,
*consubstantial*, *original sin*, and the like.

IV. Although the heathen often abused this word to designate their own vain
discourse, this does not prevent the name *theology*, which they wrongly
ascribed to their falsely named theology, from being claimed for our true and
saving doctrine. So the name *God*, which among the heathen signified a false
and fictitious god, and the name *church*, which designated a profane
assembly, are understood in Scripture in a sounder sense, of the true God and
the assembly of the saints. Thus the word *theology*, which is Greek in
origin, was transferred from the schools of the gentiles to sacred uses, just
as the vessels of the Egyptians were once designated by the Israelites for
sacred uses.

V. We do not deny that Scripture has various synonyms by which heavenly
doctrine is designated, as when it is called “wisdom in a mystery,” 1
Corinthians 2:7, “a form of sound words,” 2 Timothy 1:13, and “knowledge of
the truth according to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 4 | IA leaf 58 | PDF p. 58]

godliness,” Titus 1:1; it is expressed by “doctrine,” Titus 1:9, and by other
words of that kind. Yet the name can and should no less be retained, both
because it has been accepted through long use and because it is most suitable
for expressing this saving doctrine.

VI. It is established that the word *theology* was in use among the gentiles.
Those who spoke sublime things about God, explained the worship of the gods,
or recounted their births, marriages, offspring, dominions, and deeds were
themselves called *theologians*, and their science *theology*, as Lactantius,
*On the Wrath of God* 2; Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 3; Isidore, book
5; and Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 3, observe.

[Marginal heading: Uses of the name.]

VII. Among Christians, the name *theology* is understood either inadequately,
from the efficient cause, as it signifies “God's discourse,” and from the
object, “discourse about God”; or adequately, insofar as it denotes both God's
discourse and discourse about God. These two must be joined, because we cannot
speak about God without God. Thus it denotes the doctrine that originally
comes from God, objectively treats of God, and terminatively is directed to
God and leads to God. Thomas expresses this not badly, 1a 1ae q. 7: “Theology
is taught by God, teaches God, and leads to God.” This designation accordingly
embraces theology's twofold principle: one of being, which is God, and the
other of knowing, which is his Word.

VIII. Again, authors understand it in three ways: 1. broadly; 2. narrowly;
3. according to the proper breadth of its meaning. In the first way it is
applied to metaphysics, in which sense Aristotle calls first philosophy
*theology*, *Metaphysics* 6.1, and in book 10 divides theoretical philosophy
into three parts: physical, mathematical, and theological. Second, by the
name *theology* the Fathers particularly designate that part of Christian
doctrine which treats of Christ's divinity. In this sense John is called *the
Theologian* in a singular way because more forcefully than the rest he
asserted “the divinity of the Word,” as Revelation 1:2 seems to explain; and
Gregory Nazianzen obtained the name *the Theologian* among the other Fathers
because he demonstrated Christ's divinity in various orations. From this
arose among them the distinction between *theology* and *economy*: by the
former they understand doctrine concerning Christ's divinity, by the latter
doctrine concerning his incarnation; and for them “to theologize Jesus” is to
discourse about Christ's divinity. Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 5.28;
Basil, book 2 against Eunomius; and Nazianzen, Orations 42 and 49. Third, and
most properly, it denotes a system or body of doctrine about God and divine
things, revealed by God for his own glory and human salvation, in which sense
we understand it here.

IX. The use of the name *theology* is either equivocal and abusive, when it is
ascribed to the false theology of the heathen and heretics; less proper, when
it is applied to the original and infinite wisdom which we conceive in God as
he knows himself in an ineffable and most perfect manner—for this name cannot
attain the dignity of the thing itself—or when it is applied to the theology
of Christ and angelic theology; or proper, when it is ascribed to the theology
of human beings who are wayfarers, which is distinguished into natural and
supernatural, as will be said below.

QUESTION II

Does Theology Exist, and How Many Kinds Are There?

[Marginal heading: The existence of THEOLOGY is proved.]

I. Many things prove that theology exists. 1. The nature and goodness of God:
since he is supremely good, he is supremely communicative of himself, and he
communicates himself to a rational creature in no more suitable way, nor in
one more fitting to human nature, than through the knowledge and love of
himself. 2. The consensus of peoples and the inborn desire in everyone to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 5 | IA leaf 59 | PDF p. 59]

know God, which cannot be vain. For although they strayed shamefully from true
theology, by seeking it they nevertheless proved that it exists. Thus no
nation has ever been found so barbarous that it did not have its own
hierophants occupied in knowing and teaching divine things. 3. The end of
creation: God created rational creatures so that he might be acknowledged and
celebrated by them, which cannot occur without theology. 4. The nature of the
thing: the two requirements for constituting a discipline occur here—an
object that can be known and a subject capable of knowing—namely, God, “the
most knowable of all knowable things,” and rational creatures endowed with
intellect to know him. 5. The necessity of salvation: since a human being is
ordained to a supernatural end, a supernatural means by which to attain that
end must be given to him. This is nothing other than faith, which necessarily
entails the knowledge of God.

II. Although all beings are treated in philosophical disciplines, they are
not treated according to everything that can be known about them, but only
according to what can be perceived about them naturally. Thus the breadth of
philosophy's object brings no prejudice against supernatural theology, which
treats certain beings not insofar as they are known by nature but insofar as
they are known by revelation. 2. Although all natural beings are taught in
the lower sciences, this does not remove the necessity of theology, in which
various supernatural mysteries are taught that no human science has ever
reached.

III. Although the senses need no supernatural knowledge for their perfection,
it does not follow that the intellect does not need it, because the intellect
is ordained to a supernatural end that exceeds the comprehension of reason,
while the senses are not. Although this need of the intellect bears a mark of
imperfection with respect to an end not yet attained, and insofar as the
absence of the end is signified, it also designates perfection with respect
to the capacity to obtain the end.

IV. Metaphysics is the highest of all sciences in the natural order, but it
acknowledges theology as superior to itself in the supernatural order. The
philosophers' statement that sciences are distinguished by greater or lesser
abstraction, and therefore that the science which abstracts most of all from
matter, namely metaphysics, is superior to all, must be understood of sciences
that are merely theoretical, deal only with universal things, and belong to
the natural order. For they constitute their own object through the mind's
abstraction, and the greater the abstraction they use, the higher they are.
But this cannot be applied to theology, which is mixed from theory and
practice, is superior to the whole natural order, and does not constitute its
own object for itself through any abstraction, but receives it as constituted
and distinct from revelation.

[Marginal heading: How many kinds of THEOLOGY there are.]

V. Theology is customarily distinguished in various ways. 1. Into true and
false. False theology—so called equivocally, being erroneous doctrine about
God and his worship—is manifold. First is the theology of the gentiles, which
is known to have been manifold. Thus Plato, *Republic* 2, establishes it as
twofold: symbolic or mythical, which was contained wrapped in signs, under
which it was the custom of the gentiles, especially the Egyptians, to teach
divine mysteries; and philosophical or demonstrative, which was occupied in
reflection upon divine things. Varro, in Augustine, *City of God* 6.5 and
8.1, makes it threefold: the mythical or fabulous theology of poets, which
was intended for stages and theaters; the political or civil theology of
priests and the people, which was publicly practiced in temples according to
the rites of each city and nation; and the physical or natural theology of
philosophers, which was taught in schools.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 6 | IA leaf 60 | PDF p. 60]

Hence poets, on account of their myths about the gods, philosophers, and
priests were called *theologians* by Justin, *Exhortation to the Greeks*, and
Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 5. The chief part of their theology was the
exposition of the gods' genealogy, because they thought the gods had been
begotten. Second is the theology of unbelievers and heretics, who either
reject Christ entirely, such as Jews, Muslims, and so forth, or retain the
name of Christ but fight against God's Word on fundamental matters, as does
the theology of the papists, Socinians, and other heretics of that kind.
Although their theology has something true in it, because it is for the most
part false and errs in fundamentals, it is rightly called false by a
designation taken from the predominant element.

VI. True theology is divided, first, into infinite and uncreated theology,
which is both God's essential knowledge of himself, Matthew 11:27—in which
God alone is simultaneously the knower, the knowing one, and the thing
known—and that which he decreed to reveal to us about himself, commonly
called *archetypal*; and finite and created theology, which is the image and
copy of that infinite original, namely, the knowledge rational creatures have
of God and divine things, fashioned by that supreme theology and communicated
to intelligent creatures. This is communicated either to Christ's soul
through the hypostatic union, whence arises the *theology of union*; to angels
and the blessed through the beatific vision, who walk not by faith but by
sight, which is called the *theology of vision*; or through the revelation
made to wayfarers, namely, those who have not yet reached the goal, which is
called the *theology of revelation*, or *of the course*.

VII. Second, the theology of revelation is again divided into natural and
supernatural. Natural theology concerns what can be known about God. It is
both implanted, from the common notions impressed upon each person, and
acquired, from reasoning through creatures. In upright Adam it was indeed
refined, but in corrupted humanity it is most disordered. Supernatural
theology transcends our reason and is communicated to us by God through the
new light of grace so that we may attain the enjoyment of the highest good.
It was revealed to the patriarchs both before and after the flood, and was
then delivered through Moses by God to the people of Israel and is called the
Old Testament; or it is the New Testament, which is peculiarly called
Christian because it has Christ as author and object, is from Christ, John
1:18, and treats of Christ, Acts 1:1; 1 Corinthians 2:2. It is strictly
called *revealed* because its principle is divine revelation strictly so
called, made through the Word, not through creatures.

VIII. Supernatural theology is considered either systematically, insofar as
it denotes a connected body of saving doctrine about God and divine things
expressed from Scripture and arranged, in the manner of a discipline, into
its precepts by a fixed method—which is also called abstract and objective;
or habitually, in the manner of a habit residing in the intellect—which is
called concrete and subjective. Again, habitual theology is either the habit
of principles, by which each believer apprehends things foreign and remote
from reason, or the habit of conclusions, by which from the principles known
by the light of faith we explain and confirm saving doctrine.

IX. Just as there is a threefold school of God—of nature, grace, and glory—and
a threefold book—of the creature, Scripture, and life—so theology is commonly
distinguished three ways: the first natural, the second supernatural, and the
third beatific. The first comes from the light of reason, the second from the
light of faith, and the third from the light of glory. The first belongs to
human beings in the world, the second to believers in the church, and the
third to the blessed in heaven.

[Marginal heading: The unity of THEOLOGY.]

X. Although theology treats diverse things belonging to diverse sciences, it
does not cease to be one, because it treats them under the same formal aspect,
insofar as they are divine things revealed to us by God's Word. The unity of
a doctrine depends upon the unity of its object considered not materially but
formally. Hence, if other disciplines

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 7 | IA leaf 61 | PDF p. 61]

treat various things contained in theology, they do not treat them in the
same manner or under the same formal aspect. For theology treats them insofar
as they are revealed to us by God's Word. Furthermore, it considers them
according to remote causes—namely, the first efficient cause from which they
are and the ultimate end to which they are referred—not proximate causes, and
according to supernatural, not natural, properties.

XI. It is one thing for theology to be one as to the substance and kind of
doctrine, and another for it to be one as to its manner of treatment. In the
latter sense it can be called manifold according to the varied mode of
instruction in which it may be delivered, and thus it is divided into
didactic, problematic, elenctic, casuistic, and so forth. But in the former
sense it neither is nor can be manifold, because one and the same kind of
doctrine is always delivered in it.

XII. Hence it is also evident that theology can differ by degrees with
respect to the various degrees of revelation, insofar as it is either more
obscure under the Old Testament or clearer under the New, or more or less
perfect according to the capacity of its subjects. Yet it does not follow
from this that it differs in species, because the same substance of doctrine
is retained in both, as Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever,
Hebrews 13:8.

XIII. Theology does not lose its unity although it is called partly
theoretical and partly practical. For any science is called one not by simple
and absolute unity, which is called numerical and individual unity, as if it
were one simple quality—for example, whiteness in a wall—but by aggregate
unity, which is called a unity of collection, insofar as many special habits
are combined and ordered to constitute one total habit of science. Thus the
image of God is one although it embraces newness of mind and affections, and
free choice is one although it is situated in intellect and will.

QUESTION III

Does Natural Theology Exist?

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. The question does not concern theology in general, but natural theology in
particular. Nor does it concern natural theology as it existed in Adam before
the fall, for that it existed in Adam is sufficiently established even from
the image of God alone, according to which he was created. It concerns it as
it remained after the fall.

II. The question is not whether there is a natural theology that exists in
actuality as soon as a person is born, as the act of life exists in the living
and sensation in the sensing from the womb. For it is certain that no actual
knowledge of anything is born with us, and in this respect the human being is
like a blank tablet. Rather, the question is whether such a theology exists at
least by reason of its principle and potentiality, or whether there is in a
human being a natural and implanted faculty which spontaneously and of itself
exerts itself in every adult of sound mind and which embraces not only the
power of understanding but also the natural principles of notions from which
both theoretical and practical conclusions are deduced. This we affirm.

III. The question is not whether this knowledge is perfect and saving. We
acknowledge that after sin it was so greatly obscured that it became wholly
insufficient for salvation. The question is only whether some knowledge of
God survives in a human being, by which he is persuaded that God exists and
is to be worshiped religiously.

IV. Here our dispute is with the Socinians, who deny that such natural
theology or knowledge of God exists and maintain that, if any seems to exist,
it has flowed partly from the tradition of the ancestors from Adam onward and
partly from various revelations made at different

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 8 | IA leaf 62 | PDF p. 62]

times. Socinus, *Theological Lectures* 2; Ostorodt, *Institutes of the
Christian Religion* 3. The orthodox, however, consistently teach that there
is a natural theology, partly implanted, which is drawn from the book of
conscience through common notions, and partly acquired, which is drawn from
the book of creatures through reasoning. They prove it by the following
arguments.

[Marginal heading: NATURAL THEOLOGY is proved from Romans 2:14.]

V. Because there is in a human being a natural law, which is written in each
person's conscience and which excuses and accuses him in his good and evil
deeds. It therefore necessarily includes knowledge of God the Lawgiver, by
whose authority it binds human beings to obedience and announces rewards or
punishments to them: “The gentiles who do not have the Law,” that is, the Law
of Moses, “by nature do the things of the Law, and so, although they do not
have the Law, are a law to themselves, showing the work of the Law written in
their hearts, while their conscience also bears witness and their thoughts
mutually accuse or excuse them,” Romans 2:14–15. This could not be said unless
conscience dictated to every person that some deity exists who approves right
deeds and disapproves and punishes evil deeds. Nor can it be objected, 1. that
not the Law but the *work of the Law* is said to be inscribed, because these
are synonyms for the Apostle: “to be a law to themselves,” verse 14, and “to
have the work of the Law inscribed on their hearts,” verse 15. The nature of
the thing also proves this, because by such a work of the Law is understood
one by whose impulse a person not only distinguishes the honorable from the
base but is also driven to perform the former and avoid the latter. 2. Nor can
it be objected that the Law is not called *inborn* but *inscribed*, that is,
known, as the Law of Moses was known to the Jews from revelation. For
inscription entails the natural revelation of this Law in the human
conscience, opposed to the external revelation made to the Jews by inscription
on stone tablets. Hence verse 15 is explained through conscience, which
exerts itself both in preservation and in consciousness.

VI. Because God gave human beings both an implanted and an acquired knowledge
of himself. To this pertain Psalm 19:1; Acts 14:15–17 and 17:23; Romans
1:19–20. Nor can Socinus's audacious corruption be admitted, by which he
refers Paul's words to the second creation made through Christ, as if the
Apostle suggested that those things which had been invisible and unknown to
human beings from creation were now seen and known by the deeds of God and of
divine men, that is, Christ and his apostles. Paul's words and the sequence of
his discourse proclaim that he is speaking of the first creation. For he
wishes to prove that the ungodly human beings against whom God's wrath is
revealed from heaven, verse 18, hold down the truth in unrighteousness—namely,
the true notions about God contained in natural revelation. He shows this in
verse 19 when he says that what can be known about God is manifest “in them,”
because God revealed it to them, partly in their hearts and partly in the
works of creation. 2. Paul's purpose teaches the same thing, which is to
demonstrate that neither the gentiles could be justified from nature, chapter
1, nor the Jews from the Law, chapter 2, because all are sinners, but only
from the gospel revealed by Christ. 3. “Things made” cannot here be referred
to miracles done by the apostles, because they are never so called in
Scripture and were not known to the gentiles of whom Paul treats. They refer
instead to the works of the world's creation, because God's invisible things
are said to be revealed in them “from the creation of the world.”

[Marginal heading: From universal experience.]

VII. Universal experience confirms this. What inheres commonly and
unchangeably in every human being must inhere naturally, because natural
things agree with everyone and are unchangeable. But knowledge of divinity
inheres unchangeably in everyone, because no nation is so barbarous that this
persuasion—that God exists—does not reside in it. Cicero, *On the Nature of
the Gods* 1. Thus they would rather worship the most worthless things, even
a demon itself, as deity than have no

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 9 | IA leaf 63 | PDF p. 63]

deity. And if any have tried to shake off this sense, they have never been
able to do so. Although for a time they may seem to have grown callous, terror
of deity nevertheless returns, especially in adversity.

[Marginal heading: From the institution of religions.]

VIII. The institution of religions in the world proves natural theology most
clearly. For whence comes that hidden inclination of human beings toward
religion, according to which a human being is for Plato “the most
God-revering animal,” unless from a sense of deity which they acknowledge
must be worshiped? Peoples would not have been so prone to embrace even the
most hideous idolatry and so easily receive the false and fictitious religions
which impostors devised by political cunning to enslave people unless they
had been driven by a certain natural instinct toward religion and the worship
of some deity. Nor can it be said that the gentiles did this not so much from
instinct as from imitation. For if there had been no natural instinct, the
human being, an animal desirous of glory, would not have cast himself down
before the most worthless creatures merely to avoid being thought devoid of
all deity; nor can what arises from imitation alone be so common and universal.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

IX. Although there are some extremely savage peoples who seem destitute of
every sense of deity, they nevertheless do not lack all knowledge of God.
The seeds of religion remaining in them can indeed be slight because of the
great blindness and stupor by which they seem to approach nearer to wild and
brute animals, yet some seeds survive in them, as among the Americans and
Brazilians whom Socinus objects to us here. Although Jean de Léry of Burgundy
observes that no gods are acknowledged among them, he nevertheless indicates
not obscurely that traces of divinity survive in them. He relates that they
have their *caribes*, or priests, whom they are persuaded both impart military
courage and produce all the earth's fruits because they have commerce with
spirits. He also relates that they acknowledge that the souls of those who
cultivated virtue fly beyond the highest mountains and pass a happy age amid
perpetual delights in the loveliest gardens, but that the souls of cowards are
carried away to *Stignan*, as they call the evil demon, and live with him
amid eternal torments. The same writer, *History of a Voyage to Brazil* 6,
relates that the supreme god of the Mexicans is Hoizili Pochitli. José de
Acosta, *History of the West Indies* 5.2–3, says that the Peruvians have their
gods and among them their Piracocha, whom they call Pachacamak, creator of
heaven and earth. Similar things are read in Girolamo Benzoni's *History of
the New World*, Bartolomé de las Casas, and others.

X. It is not contradictory for one and the same thing to be known in
different respects both from the light of reason and believed from the light
of faith, so that what is gathered only dimly from one is held more certainly
from the other. Thus we know that God exists both from nature and from faith,
Hebrews 2:6: more obscurely from nature, but more certainly from faith. So
the special knowledge of true faith, by which believers please God and have
access to him, which Paul treats there, does not exclude but presupposes the
general knowledge derived through nature.

XI. The human mind is a blank tablet not absolutely but in a qualified sense,
with respect to discursive and dianoetic knowledge, which is necessarily
acquired by inferring one thing from another, but not with respect to
apprehensive and noetic knowledge. For, as Paul himself bears witness, the
work of the Law is so engraved on the hearts of the gentiles that they do by
nature the things of the Law. Thus a twofold writing is read in the human
heart: one from God in the remnants of his image and in the natural law, the
other from the devil through sin.

XII. What is natural subjectively and constitutively always remains in the
same manner, but not what is natural qualitatively and consecutively, for
qualities

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 10 | IA leaf 64 | PDF p. 64]

admit of greater and lesser degrees. Natural theology is called natural not
in the former but in the latter sense. Hence it is no wonder if it varies in
degree according to its subjects, who possess greater or lesser acuteness.

XIII. Although we do not deny that natural theology also depends upon human
instruction, it is nevertheless certain that this method would have been
insufficient unless both inborn and acquired natural knowledge of God had
been presupposed.

XIV. Although the knowledge of God is natural, it does not follow that no
mortal could deny God. For if any denied him, they denied him not so much from
ignorance as from malice, while their own conscience reproved them. David
testifies to this concerning mocking atheists among God's people, Psalm
14:4–5, and Paul asserts it concerning the philosophers, Romans 1:18–19,
teaching that they held down the truth—that is, true notions about God—in
unrighteousness. The cause of their denial, then, was not so much absolute
ignorance of God as corruption and malice that choked and almost extinguished
the implanted knowledge so that they might sin more freely.

QUESTION IV

Is Natural Theology Sufficient for Salvation; That Is, Is There Some Common
Religion through Which All People Indiscriminately Can Be Saved? We Deny It
against the Socinians and Remonstrants.

[Marginal heading: Occasion of the question.]

I. The occasion for the question was furnished by the impious dogma of the
Pelagians: that every person who is well established in his own religion is
saved. This is held not only by the Libertines, David Jorists, and similar
people, who are content with honorable and civil conduct and consider every
religion indifferent, but is also approved by today's Socinians. They do so
partly directly, when they teach that those who worship God according to the
light of nature as a kind of inward word please him, are acceptable to him,
and experience him as rewarder, Socinus, *Lectures* 2; and partly indirectly
and obliquely, when they reduce the heads of religion strictly necessary for
salvation to very few and make them common to everyone in their own manner
and degree, as will be said in its place. The Remonstrants approach them not
obscurely. Some do so more openly, such as Curcellaeus and Adolph Venator,
who in his *Apology against the Ministers of Dordrecht* expressly denies this
proposition: “No one can be saved who is not ingrafted into Christ through
true faith.” Others do so more cautiously, such as Arminius, Corvinus, and
Episcopius, who admit the gentiles and others to salvation not immediately
but mediately when they maintain that by the right use of the light of nature
the light of grace is acquired and through grace glory is reached. Arminius,
*Response to Articles* 15–17, and Arnoldus against Tilenus. Various papists
who dared defend the salvation of the gentiles without the knowledge of
Christ labor under the same error, such as Abulensis, Durandus, Capreolus,
Andradius, Vega, Soto, Erasmus, and others.

II. The orthodox, however, consistently maintain to the contrary that there
is only one theology or true religion by which salvation can come to a human
being after the fall, the one revealed in the word of the Law and the gospel;
and that all other religions besides this one are either impious and
idolatrous or false and erroneous. Although they retain certain obscure and
imperfect notions of the Law and of what can be known about God, they help no
further than to render the human being without excuse.

III. The question is not whether there are certain principles of religion
common to everyone. For we acknowledge that in natural theology certain such
principles are given from the light of nature, upon which supernatural
theology is built—for example,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 11 | IA leaf 65 | PDF p. 65]

that God exists, that God is to be worshiped, and so forth. Rather, the
question is whether the adequate and proper principles of true religion can
be possessed by everyone. This we deny.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

IV. The question is not whether natural theology is useful to human beings.
For we acknowledge its various ends and uses: 1. that it may be a testimony
of God's goodness toward sinful human beings who are unworthy even of these
remnants of light, Acts 14:16–17; John 1:5; 2. that it may be a bond of
external discipline among human beings, lest the world turn into a den of
robbers, Romans 2:14–15; 3. that it may be a subjective condition in the
human being for receiving the light of grace, because God does not address
brute animals and tree trunks, but rational creatures; 4. that it may be an
incitement to seek a more illustrious revelation of God, Acts 14:27; 5. that
human beings may be rendered inexcusable, Romans 1:20, both in this life at
the judgment of their accusing conscience, Romans 2:15, and in the future at
the judgment of God, who will judge human secrets, Romans 2:16. Rather, the
question is whether it is sufficient of itself for salvation, and whether the
end intended by God in that revelation was the salvation of the human beings
to whom it was made. This we deny.

[Marginal heading: The insufficiency of NATURAL THEOLOGY for salvation is
proved.]

V. The reasons are various. 1. Because there can be no saving religion
without Christ and faith in him, John 3:16; 17:3; Acts 4:11–12; 1 Corinthians
3:11; Hebrews 11:6. But Christ is nowhere revealed except in the gospel, and
faith is not given without the Word, since it comes by hearing, Romans 10:17.
Nor should it be objected that from this it follows only that the Christian
religion is the one ordinary way of salvation, but not that God cannot
extraordinarily confer salvation upon those who have lived uprightly according
to the law of nature even though Christ was not announced to them. For since
Scripture testifies that Christ is the one way of salvation without whom no
one comes to the Father, an extraordinary way without Christ cannot be
imagined without guilt. 2. Because the condition of the gentiles and all who
are destitute of Christ's Word is called a “time of ignorance,” Acts 17:30,
in which God disregarded them as if overlooking them and permitted the nations
to walk in their own ways, Acts 14:16; in which they served an unknown God,
Acts 17:23; and in which they were atheists in the world, Ephesians 2:12.
This could not be said if natural revelation possessed the things that could
suffice for salvation. 3. Because if salvation could be obtained from common
religion, there would have been no need for the gospel and the preaching of
the Word. Yet Paul testifies, 1 Corinthians 1:21, that after the world in the
wisdom of God did not know God through wisdom, it pleased God to save
believers through the foolishness of preaching, that is, through the word of
the gospel, which is foolishness to the ungodly.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

VI. It is one thing to have some knowledge of God as Creator and Preserver,
but an imperfect, corrupt, and obscure knowledge; it is another to have a
full, entire, and clear knowledge of God as Redeemer and of the lawful worship
owed to him. Natural theology has the former in what can be *known* about
God; revealed theology alone has the latter in what must be *believed*, which
is obtained only from the Word. And though God has not left himself without
witness in nature by doing good to human beings with respect to the things of
this life, Acts 14:17—which he often bestows on those whom he hates and has
devoted to destruction—it does not follow that the external call was
objectively sufficient for salvation. For verse 16 says that he “allowed the
nations to walk in their own ways,” and chapter 17:30 calls that period the
“time of ignorance,” which refers to nothing but the lack of the external
call, since it contrasts that time with the New Testament period in which he
calls human beings to repentance through the Word.

VII. It is one thing to seek the favor and grace of God as revealed through
his Word, by virtue of his promises in Christ; it is another to seek an
unknown God in the works of nature and providence, in case he might perhaps
be found by groping.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 12 | IA leaf 66 | PDF p. 66]

The latter is rightly said of the gentiles in Acts 17:26–27, but not the
former. And if elsewhere in Scripture *to seek God* signifies fleeing to
faith in him and desiring the protection of his grace, and *to find him*
denotes obtaining his desired protection and experiencing his most holy
deity as propitious, it does not follow that the words are to be understood
in the same sense in this passage. Both the objects and the manner of seeking
are entirely different. In the other passages cited, God's covenant people
are treated, but here only those who were strangers to the covenants of
promise. In those, people who knew God in the Word and abominated idols are
treated, but here idolaters to whom God was unknown. In those, the favor of
the known God is sought and found through Christ, but here the unknown God is
sought through the works of nature and providence so that he may be known and
distinguished from idols. Finally, in those it is said simply and absolutely
that believers ought to seek God so that they may find him unto salvation;
but here it is said that in creating and governing the world God gave such
proofs of his power and divinity that they were led to seek the Creator of
all in this manner—“if perhaps they might find him by groping.” No one would
say this about those saints mentioned in the Old Testament who were accustomed
to approach God, relying upon his most certain promises in Christ.

VIII. Romans 1:19–20, concerning what can be known about God, does not favor
a common religion through which all can be saved and which suffices for
salvation. 1. Because only the *knowable*, not the *believable*, which alone
is saving, is treated here. 2. Paul says that “what can be known” of God was
manifested to the gentiles, but not “everything knowable”—namely, only what
can be obtained from the book of nature, not everything that can be known
about God from the Word and that it is necessary to know for salvation, such
as the mystery of the Trinity and of Christ the Redeemer, and so forth. 3.
The *knowable* is restricted by the Apostle to God's power and divinity, that
is, to knowledge of God's existence and those attributes which most readily
strike the senses in the works of creation and providence. This is commonly
referred to natural theology. But it is not extended to knowledge of his will
and mercy in Christ, which can be obtained only from the Word and by no means
from God's works, and without which there is no salvation. 4. The knowable is
such that it can pertain to nothing beyond rendering human beings inexcusable,
Romans 1:20. Nor should the phrase *so that* used there be understood only
eventually, as denoting an accidental outcome, but also finally with respect
to God's purpose. For that outcome cannot but be intended by God, since this
passage treats a work God performs by decree, not merely something he commands
by law.

IX. What suffices for inexcusability does not immediately suffice for
salvation if you use it well, for more is required to bring about salvation
than to incur condemnation justly and without excuse. Evil arises from any
defect, but good requires an entire cause. For example, whoever stumbles in
one point is guilty of all, James 2:10, but one who does well in one point is
not immediately righteous in all. The commission of one sin can render a
person inexcusable, but the fulfillment of one good work does not suffice to
save him. Thus the gentiles were inexcusable because in place of the one God,
whom they could know from the light of nature, they fashioned innumerable
gods for themselves. Yet knowledge of the one God cannot for that reason be
said to suffice absolutely for salvation. Thus this inexcusability must be
restricted to the subject matter which the Apostle treats—namely, idolatry,
which sufficed for their condemnation, although avoiding it would not suffice
for salvation.

X. It is one thing for a person to be excusable or deserving of excuse; it is
another for him to be savable or destined to be saved, if he is excusable
only from so much and not from the whole. The former

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 13 | IA leaf 67 | PDF p. 67]

would belong to the gentiles if, per impossibile, they used the light of
nature well. For although they might rightly order their outward actions by
abstaining from subsequent sins, they could not obtain pardon for preceding
sins, especially original sin, or reform their corrupt condition and nature.
The actions they performed would be merely outward and good as to the
substance of the work, but not as to manner and principle, since they would be
destitute of the Holy Spirit. And if they benefited the person, they would
benefit him only with respect to the present or future, not the past, by
removing preceding guilt, without which removal no salvation can be given to
a human being.

XI. It is wrongly said that in what can be known about God there is given,
objectively, a revelation of grace and the Redeemer sufficient for salvation,
if not clear and explicit, at least obscure and implicit. On this account God
would be known in it as merciful and thus, in some confused manner, as a
Redeemer who has accepted satisfaction, who calls to repentance, and who
promises the remission of sins. For first, it is one thing for God to be
known as merciful with a general mercy directed toward some temporal good and
a delay of punishments; it is another for him to be known as merciful with a
special and saving mercy in Christ and upon the establishment of satisfaction.
It is one thing for God to be known as capable of being appeased and as
kind; it is another for him to be known as actually appeased, or certainly to
be appeased. We grant that the gentiles could have had the former from the
light of nature, but not the latter, which is nevertheless necessarily
required to quiet the conscience. For what would it help to know that God can
be appeased unless it were also established that he wills to be appeased and
the means of such appeasement were known? Since conscience is burdened by the
guilt of sin and the sense of divine justice, it can never be quieted unless
both God's good will and the manner of appeasing his justice become known to
it. Who would say that this can be obtained from the book of nature, in which
God presents himself only as Creator and Preserver? Who, on the contrary,
would not acknowledge that it must be sought only from the word of the gospel,
which reveals God's mercy in Christ to us? Otherwise, why would Paul call
this a mystery which was unknown through all ages, Romans 16:25? Why would he
say that the gentiles were strangers to the covenants and outside Christ,
Ephesians 2:12, if they were always in some measure under the covenant of
grace, in which some knowledge of saving mercy in Christ, though confused and
implicit, had been given?

XII. Those who seek another covering and distinguish mediate and immediate
sufficiency extricate themselves no better. They suppose that although the
gentiles could not have had a sufficient revelation immediately, they
nevertheless had one mediately, insofar as if anyone had used the light of
nature well, God would have added the light of grace. They say that his mercy
requires this and that it can be inferred from Christ's saying, Matthew 13:11,
“to him who has, it will be given.” For besides the absurdity of calling a
revelation sufficient which requires another revelation to be added—the same
reason for which we criticize the papists' opinion that Scripture can be
called sufficient mediately because, although it does not contain everything,
it sends us to tradition from which those things can be sought—it is here
gratuitously supposed, when it needed to be proved, that God would have added
the light of grace to one who used the light of nature well, as if God bound
himself to anything, owed anything to a human being, or that connection were
proved anywhere from Scripture. Indeed, this seems drawn from Pelagius's
springs, for he maintained that God would not deny grace to the person who
does what lies within him. Matthew 13:11 cannot be dragged here, because it
treats gifts of grace, which God is accustomed to crown with new gifts, not
gifts of nature.

XIII. The passage in Romans 2:4, where Paul says, “The kindness of God leads
to repentance,” and so invites to repentance by God's kindness, does not
pertain here, because he does not

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 14 | IA leaf 68 | PDF p. 68]

treat the gentiles but the Jews. In this chapter he wishes to convict them of
sin, just as in chapter 1 he had already convicted the gentiles of the same
guilt. This is evident, 1. from the things he ascribes to the man he addresses,
which fit no one more suitably than a Jew, such as that he judges others for
the things he himself commits, and so forth; 2. from verse 17, where the
discourse is addressed by name to the Jew, who is said to be that man whom he
had addressed: “Behold, you are called a Jew.” Those words are not the
beginning of a new discourse to another person different from the preceding
one, but the continuation of the former discourse with a clearer designation
of the person. Therefore the kindness treated here denotes the revelation
made to the Jews and the benefits bestowed upon them; it has nothing in
common with the works of general providence.

XIV. Although the conscience of the gentiles is sometimes said to excuse
them, Romans 2:14–15, it does not follow that in that condition they can enjoy
true and solid peace and the perfect salvation that follows it. For it is one
thing to excuse in some matters or from so much, which it does; it is another
to excuse in all things and from the whole, which is impossible for it. It is
one thing to excuse from more grievous crimes and by comparison with others
who are worse; it is another to bestow upon us sure and constant peace, which
flows from the sense of God's love and of our reconciliation with him and
cannot exist in a gentile.

XV. The rationale of the constituted state is one thing and that of the
destitute state another; the rationale of the Law considered in itself and in
its own nature is one thing and that of the Law with respect to us another.
The Law was initially given to upright humanity unto life, and in itself it
also led to life according to the sanction, “Do this and you will live.”
Romans 2:13 is to be understood in this way. But after the fall, when it was
rendered powerless through the flesh, it was given not unto life but as a
mirror of sin and misery, to render the sinful human being inexcusable,
Romans 3:19–20.

XVI. The *work of the Law* is spoken of in two ways: either formally, as the
thing which the Law itself performs, or imperatively, as the thing which it
commands a person. The former is the work or office of the Law concerning a
person, which consists in precept, promise, prohibition, and threat; the
latter is the person's work concerning the Law. The former is the proposal
and revelation of the Law; the latter its observance and fulfillment. The
gentiles are said to do “the things of the Law,” Romans 2:14, with respect not
to the latter but the former—not by fulfilling the commands prescribed by
the Law but by doing what the Law itself does, commanding honorable things
and forbidding base ones. This is evident, 1. from Paul's general purpose,
which is to convict the gentiles of being liable to death from the natural
law even without the written Law; 2. from the following words, which are
explanatory: “although they do not have the Law, they are a law to themselves.”
Therefore, to do the things of the Law is to be a law to themselves.

XVII. Although the gentiles, considered comparatively and in relation to one
another, were some more righteous than others, and although their works can
be called virtues civilly and morally speaking, so that they did not lack a
twofold reward for a rightly ordered life—a positive reward, because this
generally resulted for them in some temporal good and some tranquility of
conscience in this world, and a negative reward, because the condition of
their punishment became more tolerable—nevertheless, speaking theologically
and in relation to God, their works which were best in appearance were nothing
but more splendid sins, which could contribute nothing to their salvation
before God.

XVIII. The examples adduced of Melchizedek, Job, the centurion, and similar
people are irrelevant, because all of them did the things for which they are
praised in Scripture by the help of special grace and revelation and through
true faith, not from the simple light of nature.

XIX. Although Cornelius was a gentile by origin, he was nevertheless a
proselyte by religion. Although he did not believe that the Messiah had come
and was the Jesus whom Peter announced, he could nevertheless believe with
the Jews from the prophetic oracles that he would come.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 15 | IA leaf 69 | PDF p. 69]

Thus he should not be numbered with the gentiles but with the fathers, who
awaited salvation from the Redeemer not yet manifested. Hence from Peter's
arrival he received not the beginning of faith but its increase.

XX. The two articles in Hebrews 11:6 are to be understood not physically,
insofar as they can be perceived by the light of reason, but hyperphysically
and theologically, insofar as they are manifested to us through Scripture by
saving knowledge. By this knowledge we know not only that God exists and is
the omnipotent Creator of all things, but who he is—Father, Son, and Holy
Spirit, and, after the fall, Redeemer; and not only that he is a rewarder in a
legal manner of those who seek him by merit, but that he is a rewarder by
grace of those who seek him evangelically through faith in the Mediator. That
this is the Apostle's meaning is proved, 1. by the adjunct of saving faith,
which he proclaims throughout the whole chapter; 2. by the examples of the
saints reported there. Thus the faith in God of which the Apostle speaks is
not a general knowledge of God diffusing his goodness in any manner whatever,
but knowledge of the true God bestowing heavenly goods on account of Christ.
For the Apostle, *to approach God* is nothing other than to obtain communion
with God in Christ, as everywhere else in the same epistle, Hebrews 4:16;
7:25; 10:22. Hence Curcellaeus wrongly distinguishes faith in God from faith
in Christ, so that the former is absolutely necessary to salvation but the
latter only upon divine revelation. For there can be no true and saving faith
in God that is not joined to faith in Christ, John 14:1, since we cannot
believe in God except through Christ.

XXI. We do not deny that various ancient writers hoped well for the salvation
of gentiles and philosophers who ordered their lives according to reason,
such as Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* 7; Justin, *Apology* 2; Chrysostom,
Homily 37 on Matthew; and others cited by Casaubon, *Exercise* 7 against
Baronius. But it is less surprising that those writers labored under error,
since they spoke more incautiously before Pelagius arose, than that many
scholastics should stumble over the same stone after the necessity of grace
in Christ was so forcefully asserted by Augustine and his disciples.

XXII. Zwingli enrolled Hercules, Theseus, Numa, Aristides, Socrates, and
similar heroes in heaven in the epistle to Francis I prefixed to his
confession of faith. There, after naming saints of the Old and New Testaments
while describing the fellowship of the heavenly ones, he adds: “Here you will
see Hercules, Theseus, Socrates, Aristides, Numa, and so forth. Here you will
see your ancestors and all your forebears who departed from here in faith.”
Besides the fact that this is not approved by our writers, it is certain that
he erred in fact rather than in principle. He did not think that the gate of
salvation stood open outside Christ and faith. Rather, he hoped that in a
manner hidden from us but known to God, divine mercy had instilled faith in
others whom God had otherwise adorned so remarkably with heroic virtues. This
is evident from the fact that he expressly speaks of those “who departed in
faith,” which should not be restricted to the king's forebears but extended to
all those of whom he had spoken above. That this was his meaning is taught by
his declaration about original sin to Urbanus Rhegius, volume 2, p. 109,
where, after saying that those who assign to damnation everyone we call
gentiles are mistaken, he adds: “For what do we know of the faith each person
may have written in his heart by the hand of God?”

XXIII. Although the gentiles offered various sacrifices, it does not follow
that they acknowledged God's mercy in Christ. Their sacrifices were made not
so much to obtain God's saving grace, which cannot become known to a human
being without revelation because its exercise is entirely free, as to

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 16 | IA leaf 70 | PDF p. 70]

appease his justice, which is naturally known and whose exercise is necessary.

QUESTION V

THE OBJECT OF THEOLOGY

Are God and Divine Things the Object of Theology? We Affirm.

[Marginal heading: The object of THEOLOGY.]

I. The object of any science is everything treated principally in it and to
which all its conclusions are referred. It can be regarded either materially,
with respect to the thing considered, or formally, with respect to the manner
of considering it.

[Marginal heading: God and divine things.]

II. Although theologians speak in different ways about the object of theology,
the more common and true opinion is that of those who refer it to God and
divine things, so that God is the primary object and divine things the
secondary object—whether things made by God or things to be believed or done
by human beings. That is, God directly and indirectly: God himself, the things
that are God's, such as his works, the things subject to God, such as
creatures, and the things tending toward God, such as human duties. Thus all
things are treated in theology either because they are God himself or because
they have a relation to God as first principle and ultimate end.

III. That God is the object of theology is evident both from the very names
*theology* and *godliness*, from Scripture, which acknowledges no other
principal object, and from the conditions of an object found in him: 1. that
it is something noncomplex; 2. that certain things, such as affections or
properties, are predicated denominatively of it; 3. that everything treated
in the discipline has regard to it. For God is a noncomplex and supremely
simple being. Certain things, such as his attributes, are predicated
denominatively of him. All things are referred to him and have a relation of
origin, preservation, and dependence upon him.

[Marginal heading: God as revealed and covenanted.]

IV. But when God is proposed as the object of theology, he must not be
regarded simply as God in himself, for so he is incomprehensible to us, but
insofar as he has been revealed and has deigned to disclose himself to us in
the Word. Thus divine revelation is the formal aspect to be regarded in this
object. Nor must he be considered precisely under the aspect of deity, as
Thomas and most scholastics after him wish. In this manner knowledge of him
cannot be saving, but is destructive to sinners. Rather, he must be considered
as our God, that is, covenanted in Christ, in the manner he has disclosed
himself to us in the Word not only to be known but also to be worshiped. These
two comprise the true religion taught by theology.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

V. The unity of a science and its distinction from another are not always
taken from the unity of the material object, or the thing considered, but
from the unity of the formal object, or the manner of considering it. Although
physics, ethics, and medicine treat the same subject, they do not cease to be
different sciences, because they consider the human being under different
aspects: physics insofar as he is a species of natural body; ethics insofar
as he is capable of virtue and happiness; medicine insofar as he is to be
cured of diseases and restored to health. Thus although theology treats the
things of which metaphysics, physics, and ethics treat, its manner of
considering them is far different. It treats God not as metaphysics does,
insofar as he is being or can be known by the natural light, but insofar as he
becomes known through revelation as Creator and Redeemer. It treats creatures
not insofar as they are things of nature but insofar as they are things of
God, that is, insofar as they have a relation and order to God as their
Creator, Provider, and Redeemer, and this according to the revelation

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 17 | IA leaf 71 | PDF p. 71]

made by him—a manner of considering them which the other sciences either do
not know or do not employ.

VI. If theology endeavors to prove that God exists, it does not do so from
its first and proper intention, but almost accidentally from an adventitious
necessity, namely, to refute profane people and atheists who, having cast off
all shame and sense of conscience, dare to deny it. 2. The axiom “A science
does not prove but presupposes its subject” is true in human sciences of an
inferior order. But the case is different for theology, which is of a higher
order. For it extends itself to prove everything that can be proved by the
means proper to it, namely, divine revelation, and it does this not
instrumentally but architectonically.

VII. For the habit of a science it is not necessary that it grasp its object
in such a way that it knows perfectly everything that can belong to it. It is
enough if it knows many things from the object and can deduce them from its
own principles. Therefore a science need not be made equal to its subject
according to an exact and arithmetic equality. It is enough if it is equal
according to some proportion of equality, and such adequation is found in
theology. For it is occupied with God and his infinite perfections not by
knowing them infinitely, but in a finite manner, and not absolutely to the
degree they can be known in themselves, but to the degree he has deigned to
reveal them concerning himself. Thus theology can be said to be adequate to
its object according to the formal aspect of revelation, not by equaling God
himself, but only the revelation given concerning God.

VIII. The common saying “Science does not concern singulars but universals”
must not be admitted without qualification. For metaphysics, physics, and so
forth are sciences, and yet they nevertheless treat singulars: God and the
world. Therefore the axiom must be understood of singulars composed of matter
and constituted under the lowest species. If such things are treated in
theology, such as Adam, Noah, and others, this is done not principally but
only to disclose the origins of things, to offer an example of life, to bear
witness to divine providence, and thus on account of general causes. But if
there is some immaterial singular in pure act, there is no doubt that there
can be a science concerning it, because being is the object of the intellect.
Therefore, the more perfect a being is, the more it can be known and
understood; and it is more perfect to the extent that it is more in act and
less in potency. God can deservedly be numbered among universals, for he is
universal in causing, since he is the universal cause of all things. He is
also universal in predication, not indeed directly but indirectly: all things
are not God, but all things nevertheless are God's, from God, or directed to
God. Thus the subject of theology does not lack every aspect of universality
even in this respect.

IX. It is certain that in inferior sciences the principles of the science
are different from its subject, so that properties and attributes may be
demonstrated concerning the subject through their proper principles. Since
the subject of every human science is of finite essence and power, there must
be certain principles from which it flows or is composed. But in theology,
which is of a higher order, an entirely divine subject is given, infinite in
both nature and power and therefore prior to every thing, so that it cannot
have the character of something principled. Thus by reason of that infinity
it contains these two characters at once: it is both the subject about which
theology treats and its principle.

X. Theology treats sin not insofar as it belongs to God, but insofar as it
has a certain relation to God—either under the aspect of something opposed
and contrary to him or insofar as it is subject to his providence and justice.
So medicine treats diseases and poisons although its principal subject is the
human being who is to be healed.
