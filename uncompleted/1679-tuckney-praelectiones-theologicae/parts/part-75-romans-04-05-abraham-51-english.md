The tenth and final argument belongs to the same class and runs as follows:
“Christ suffered in order to sanctify his people. But if he sanctifies his
people only imputatively and not truly, he suffered in vain and could not
accomplish what he desired. For truly to sanctify is not to free people from
the punishment of sin so that we are regarded as holy although we really are
not, but to cause us truly to be holy, clean, and spotless.”

We answer:

1. It is absurd and slanderous to oppose what is true to what is imputed,
since the utmost truth underlies divine imputation.

2. Christ suffered in order both to justify and to sanctify his people
equally, and in neither of these does he fail to attain his end. For,

He truly justifies through the imputation of his righteousness alone, which
is the justification we are now discussing.

He also truly and inherently, not imputatively, sanctifies those whom he
justifies, according to the apostle's words: “Those whom he justified, he
also glorified.” Here this occurs initially and by degrees, and at last
perfectly. Concerning this perfection of sanctification in heaven,

[1679 printed p. 302 | IA leaf 310]

Augustine, Bede, and even Aquinas interpret some of the passages Bellarmine
cites here. So too with all those passages which proclaim the church
perfectly sanctified and cleansed from every stain. Aquinas writes in part
3, question 8, article 3, reply 2: “The ultimate end to which we are brought
through Christ's passion; hence this will be in the state of the homeland,
not in the state of the journey.” Christ therefore truly justifies through
the imputation of his righteousness and just as truly sanctifies through the
infusion of inherent grace. The latter is indeed true, yet it occurs step by
step, and sometimes slowly, as Calvin and Pezel express it; it is imperfect
in this life, but will most certainly and most perfectly occur in the life
to come. Christ both can and wills to accomplish each, and he accomplishes
each in this manner. There is therefore no frustration of his purpose. Let
me add two points here by way of a conclusion:

1. Although Christ attains his end in both ways, he does not will his
benefits to be confused, since this confusion substantially obscures God's
grace and at the same time disturbs the peace of conscience. For,

2. Although our sanctification is true and inherent, it is imperfect in this
life. We therefore judge it by no means safe to make our justification
before God depend upon it. Accordingly, we judge it safe—even with
Bellarmine himself—to rely wholly for our justification upon Christ's
righteousness alone imputed to us. This was what had to be shown.

And so at last I think all Bellarmine's arguments to the contrary have been
answered. At the close of his chapter he has these final words: “Many other
reasons could be offered, but these seemed to us the chief ones.” How well
that turns out! If these are the chief ones, we need fear the rest less,
since those that are chief for Bellarmine have proved so feeble.
