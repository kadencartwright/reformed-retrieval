# If Every Tolerable State of Discipline Is Refused

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
[`latin.txt`](latin.txt). The printed-page markers correspond to the 1871
*Calvini Opera* witness.

**[CO X/1, p. 207]**

Even if the disorder that moved you to this struggle had until now been
tolerable, the matter has nevertheless been brought to such a point that, in
my judgment, you are no longer free to remain on the condition that no remedy
be applied to such grave evils. But because you are still uncertain, in this
perplexing affair, what moderation ought to be maintained, and because you
wish to hear my judgment, take it as follows. If it is wicked to profane the
sacred mysteries, then certainly no obstacles that present themselves will be
enough to excuse sacrilege. A definition must therefore be sought by which it
can be determined with certainty how far this profanation extends. There is no
need to labor so much over a plain and obvious matter. I do not doubt that a
man who admits all sorts of people indiscriminately and without any selection
to the sacraments profanes them.

I admit, indeed, that sacramental practice in the church is never so pure as
to correspond in every respect to their holiness. We ourselves experience
more than enough how far our own practice falls short of the purity that
should be desired. But it is one thing to prostitute the sacred treasures to
those who are utterly alien; it is another to permit them to unworthy people
who cannot be barred by a lawful judgment. There was excessive severity among
the ancients. Yet it is more than shameful for less reverence to be shown to
our holy things than we know was once shown to impure superstitions.
Therefore, unless some discrimination prevails among us, so that those who
cannot be admitted without gross injury to God are kept away, I take it as
admitted that both baptism and the Supper are profaned. For I do not see why a
sponsor alien to the church should be received. Yet it is unjust for the
godly to be deprived through the fault of the wicked.

I know how Augustine shrank from public excommunication. For when drunkenness
then prevailed widely in Africa, he judged in a certain letter to Valerius
that this disease should be healed by gentle remedies rather than by an
excommunication that might tear apart and scatter the entire church.
Nevertheless, care must always be taken that freedom to sin is not encouraged
by loosening the rein. Though I grant that excessive severity ought to be
moderated, impunity must in no way be tolerated. Therefore, to have no rule
guarding the holiness of the Supper and baptism, lest both mysteries be
exposed to contempt and mockery, is an utterly inexcusable laxity.

You object that nothing should be attempted privately. Nor do I approve a
pastor's resigning his office and voluntarily deserting his post before the
matter has been investigated. But one endowed with a sincere zeal for
edification will at least testify that it is not lawful for him to admit
indiscriminately to Christ's sacred Supper those who openly show themselves to
be dogs and, all shame cast off, make a point of insulting God's mysteries.
Nor

**[CO X/1, p. 208]**

indeed will he rest content with the judgment of a few men until he has
learned that the people themselves refuse to submit to the yoke. Then the
question will follow: by what right do those who openly reject the order
prescribed by God hold Christ's servant bound? Certainly, when requests and
exhortations accomplish nothing, and when we have also protested in vain that
we are constrained in conscience not to continue knowingly and willingly to
profane God's sacred mysteries, I do not know how we are permitted to remain
amid such a confused disruption of order.

Although the delay is not free from blame, and it must frankly be admitted
that this cause was taken up later than it ought to have been, or was not
defended with enough vigor and courage, no charge can be brought except that
of excessive leniency. That leniency doubles the guilt of all who obstinately
resist rather than loading Christ's servant with odium, for by his delay he
showed that, in order to care for the people's welfare, he had been too
fearful.

If I were in your place, I would wish to refute the charge of zeal and
rashness by no argument better than this frank confession: that from the
beginning you had indeed seen how shameful and disgraceful this profanation of
holy things was, but had hoped for some correction as they matured; that this
was the reason for your tolerance, since you preferred them to be formed
gradually through progress toward teachability rather than suddenly forced
into order; that meanwhile you had not kept silent about the straits pressing
you until you saw a better condition of affairs; and that now you press the
matter later and more forcefully because necessity compels it from you.

If you now testify to this freely, then stand firm afterward and do not stop
pressing the matter: either God will find a remedy for these incurable evils,
or he will extricate you, so that whatever others may clamor, you can know
with certainty in your own conscience that nothing was rashly attempted by
you. But if this opportunity slips away from you, it is to be feared that
afterward you will discover yourself constrained by some bond other than that
of your holy calling.

## Translation notes

1. The Greek *ἀταξία* means disorder, lack of discipline, or irregularity and
   sets up the document's repeated concern with ecclesiastical order.
2. *Fideiussor* is a baptismal sponsor or surety. The syntax of *qui
   recipiendus sit ab ecclesia alienus* is compressed; the translation gives
   its most natural force in the surrounding argument about admission.
3. CO prints *ad Valerium*. The historical identification of the particular
   Augustinian letter is not supplied by this edition and is left unresolved
   here rather than silently corrected.
4. The subject of *quum adolevissent* (“as they matured”) is implicit. It most
   naturally refers to the people whom the addressee hoped to form gradually,
   but the construction is compressed enough to warrant caution.
