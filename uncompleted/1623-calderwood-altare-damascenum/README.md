# David Calderwood, *Altare Damascenum* (1623)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,
> diplomatic-normalized Latin transcription and an independent close English
> translation. Neither has received human review, and neither is
> publication-ready.

## Work identity

- **Author:** David Calderwood (1575–1650), writing in the first edition under
  the anagrammatic pseudonym “Edwardus Didoclavius”
- **Title:** *Altare Damascenum, seu Politia Ecclesiae Anglicanae obtrusa
  Ecclesiae Scoticanae*
- **Edition used:** 1623, without stated place or printer
- **Structure:** Calderwood's large examination of English ecclesiastical
  polity, with Daniel Tilenus's *Paraenesis* quoted and refuted at relevant
  points, followed by separately authored or anonymous Scottish-polity
  documents

## Controlling witness and rights

The controlling witness is the 1623 first Latin edition in the Princeton
Theological Seminary Library, digitized by the Internet Archive as
[`altared00cald`](https://archive.org/details/altared00cald). The volume and its
scan are public-domain source material. Internet Archive page images, not its
OCR, control the transcription.

The cleaner Leiden reset of 1708,
[`altarasce00cald`](https://archive.org/details/altarasce00cald), is a comparison
witness only. It explicitly identifies David Calderwood and calls itself an
edition “more elegant and corrected than the former,” but the digitized copy
is textually defective near the beginning: it jumps from printed p. 3 to
printed p. 6. The p. 3 catchword `Capituli` does not join p. 6 (`Hoc caput
alterum`), so printed pp. 4–5 are absent. Nothing is silently supplied from
that reset.

The 1621 English
[*Altar of Damascus*](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/e/eebo/A17571.0001.001?view=toc)
is a much shorter antecedent, not a translation of this expanded Latin work,
and it has not been used as an English wording source. The selected modern
translation of propositions identified in the
[research inventory](../../research/untranslated-latin-treatises.md) is both
partial and CC BY-ND; it likewise has not been used as a wording source.

## Composite map and attribution warning

The controlling volume has 1,088 access-PDF pages (1,090 IA frames). Its
principal boundaries are:

- PDF pp. 1–4: cover, binding, and ownership matter, excluded.
- PDF p. 5: 1623 title page; PDF p. 6 is its verso.
- PDF pp. 7–12: Calderwood's unnumbered address `Lectoribus benevolis`.
- PDF pp. 13–967: the main *Altare Damascenum*, printed pp. 1–955.
- PDF pp. 968–973: index to the main work.
- PDF pp. 974–976: Andrew Melville's separately attributed
  `Anti-Tami-Cami-Categoria`.
- PDF pp. 977–992: the separately attributed letter `De regimine Ecclesiae
  Scoticanae`, by “Hieronymus Philadelphus.”
- PDF pp. 993–1084: the anonymous *Vindiciae* of that letter against John
  Spottiswood.
- PDF pp. 1085–1088: blank or binding matter, excluded.

The main work is itself composite in presentation. Calderwood incorporates and
refutes Daniel Tilenus's *Paraenesis ad Scotos*, and the final chapter directly
addresses Tilenus's taunts and accusations. Quoted adversarial matter must not
be attributed to Calderwood merely because it appears within his book. The
letter on Scottish polity, Melville poem, and anonymous *Vindiciae* are also
distinct documents, not silently part of Calderwood's continuous authorial
prose. Parts 01–31 contain none of the appended documents. Parts 01–26 do not
reach the embedded Tilenus refutation; Part 27 opens Chapter V by reporting
and quoting Tilenus’s claims before Calderwood answers them. Parts 07–31 also
include long passages that Calderwood expressly quotes from Thomas Brightman,
Tilenus, Eusebius, Scaliger, Luther, German theologians, Francis Bacon,
English jurists,
patristic writers, imperial and conciliar legislation, English canons,
canonists, and other named writers; the paired files retain their quotation
boundaries and attributions.

## Editorial method

Latin long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are
normalized. Edition spelling and substantive wording are retained. Running
heads, signatures, catchwords, ornaments, and bare printed page numerals are
omitted. Marginal references and quoted documentary material are retained;
source markers record printed page, IA leaf, and access-PDF page.

The English is a fresh, full translation of the paired Latin, not a summary.
It has not been copied from either the short 1621 English antecedent or a
modern selection.

### Part 10 bounds and evidence

Part 10 begins exactly where Part 09 ends, in the lower part of shared printed

### Part 15 bounds and evidence

Part 15 begins exactly where Part 14 ends, in the lower portion of shared
printed p. 203 / IA leaf 215 / PDF p. 215, at the sixth argument:

`6 Nullum est discrimen iure divino inter Episcopum & Presbyterum.`

It includes Calderwood's opening three proofs that bishops and presbyters do
not differ in rank by divine right: Scripture nowhere establishes such a
difference, no analogous inequality exists within the other ecclesiastical
functions, and Scripture uses the names and descriptions of bishop and
presbyter interchangeably. It then includes the beginning of his induction
from shared powers, through the power to preach and administer the sacraments
and the question whether presbyters exercise that power as Christ's immediate
ministers or as episcopal assistants and vicars. The pair retains the extended

Nullum habemus praeceptum in novo testamento vel determinandi statum & certum
locum in unaquaque paroecia pro conventibus ecclesiasticis, vel erigendi
aedificia. Licitum est fidelibus coire in unum ad publicum cultum, ubicunque
commode possunt, & sub dio etiam celebrare conventus, si libitum fuerit. Sed ob
commoditatem tamen, expediebat, ut statum frequentarent locum, ne loca
conventuum aut absque ulla necessitate multiplicarentur, aut incerta redderentur,
iis qui extra ecclesiam & alliciendi sunt in gremium ecclesiae. Et licet interdum
sub dio conventus celebrari possunt, non tamen semper id fieri commode potest,
ideo aedificia extrui ad depellendas aeris & coeli injurias expediebat.
Necessitate igitur flagitante & commoditate suadente erecta & erigenda sunt
aedificia in usum civilem, nempe ad propulsandas coeli injurias a coetu, &
ministrandas sedes commodas, non ad colendum Deum templis & aedificiis. Suarez
dicit locum sacrum dupliciter spectari posse, vel sub ratione loci dicati ad
continendum Dei populum ad divinum cultum congregatum, & sic peculiarem habere
rationem sanctitatis, vel quatenus est res quaedam consecrata & destinata ad
cultum divinum, sub qua habet convenientiam cum aliis rebus sacris, ut vasis &
vestibus.

[Marginalia: De relig. tom. 1. lib. 3.]

Ratione priore nullam, posteriore superstitiosam habet sanctitatem, & si hoc
sensu fabricae illae dictae a veteribus κυριακὰ non probarem. Templum
Hierosolymitanum erat pars cultus divini, non ita templa in ecclesia Christiana.
Alioqui instituisset templa exstruenda in eum finem. Nam nullum alium vult
cultum, quam quem nobis praescribit. Aedificia ecclesiastica sunt annexa
spiritualibus, nempe praedicationi verbi, sacramentorum celebrationi orationi &
aliis publicis pietatis exercitiis, sicut & alia bona ecclesiastica. Annexa autem
sunt spiritualibus materialia aedificia ad protegendum coetum fidelium ab aeris
injuria, & ut commode possint vacare cultui divino & spiritualibus exercitiis.
Nec arctius est connexionis vinculum inter aedificia illa & cultum divinum, quam
inter bona alia ecclesiastica, & eundem cultum. Nam separari possunt aedificia
ista a publico cultu, imo Christiani saepe deseruerunt splendida illa aedificia,
ut purum retinerent cultum publicum, cum tamen difficulter possit sustentari
cultus divinus absque aliis bonis ecclesiasticis. Ubicunque coierit in unum
coetus piorum, Christus pollicetur praesentiam suam, sive in aede consecrata,
sive in solitudine, sive in monte. *Primò & per se promittit nobis praesentiam
suam, ut templis suis in omni loco; locis vero, in quae convenimus, sive areis
sive templis promittit per accidens & secundario propter nos: imo verò non
promittit eis, sed nobis in eis.*

[Marginalia: Jun. de cultu sanct. cap. 4. not. 35.]

Et certe nescio an locus ullus dicendus sanctus etiam praesente ecclesia. Nam
templum Hierosolymitanum sanctum erat propter typum, propter aspectabilem Dei
praesentiam & quia dicatum ipsi Deo immediate, &

propterea sanctum erat etiam extra tempus divini cultus non ita synagogae
Iudaeorum nec templa nostra materialia. Utcunque est, cur, quaeso, dedicantur,
verbo & precibus. Mitto jam nugas alias, & ritus histrionicos. Annon ut
separentur ab omni usu profano, & dicarentur actionibus solummodo sacris. Nam
benedictio duplex est, inquit Suarez alia constitutiva rei sacrae, nempe quae
permanentem producit effectum & constituit rem permanenter sacram, ut
consecratio Ecclesiae, aut calicis, benedictio aquae aut vestimenti, res autem
sacras quae per benedictionem constituuntur appellari sacramentalia permanentia.
Alia benedictio est invocativa, sive privata, ut benedictio mensae, benedictio
paterna & quam senex dat juveni. *Aliae vero sunt,* inquit, *benedictiones, quae
licet rem propriè non consecrent, nec separent illam a communibus & prophanis
usibus, nihilominus fiunt speciali ritu a ministris Ecclesiae, & ex speciali ejus
institutione, ut habeant specialem effectum, saltem per modum impetrationis in
nomine totius Ecclesiae, & ideo merito inter sacramentalia numerantur. Et tales
sunt benedictiones nuptiarum, item benedictio navis vel domus aut aliquorum
ciborum, prout in Missali vel Pontificali habentur.*

[Marginalia: Suarez. de virtute & statu religionis tom. 1. lib. 4. cap. 14.
sect. 1. 2.]

Et has benedictiones vocat sacramentalia transeuntia. Benedictio in
consecratione Ecclesiae est benedictio constitutiva rei sacrae, sicut in
consecratione altaris, calicis, patinae & similium. Non jam distinguo more
Pontificio benedictionem sive verbalem, sive cum aqua benedicta a consecratione
per unctionem, siquidem eadem vis est benedictionis simplicis secundum
adversarios etiam absque illa mixtura aquae, signo crucis, & unctione Chrismatis,
quae cum illis, nempe constituere in esse sacro. Et qua ratione sic consecrant
Ecclesias, consecrare etiam debent poculum Eucharisticum, mensam Eucharisticam
& similia. Christus certè cum celebravit coenam, consecravit panem & vinum, non
materialem mensam, mappas, calices, catinos, parietes imo nec panem & vinum
consecravit, ut permanerent res sacrae extra usum, sed in usum praesentem. Dicunt
se sacrare templa verbo & precibus, sed ubi oratio fidei, ubi promissio Christi,
ubi verbum institutionis, unde ergo authoritas constituendi res sacras &
sacramentalia permanentia templa, altaria, calices, patinas, & similia. Addicere
bona mobilia & immobilia Ecclesiae pium & laudabile est, sed sacrare ea, adeo ut
nefas sit eis uti, cum non utitur Ecclesia, etiam ad res licitas sed prophanas,
ac si eis pollueretur Ecclesia, vel mensa, vel calix, & similia, execranda est
superstitio. Et certè haec est benedictionis & consecrationis eorum vis, nempe ut
constituant res illas in esse sacro, quam vim non promisit Dominus precibus &
orationibus nostris, nisi in consecrandis iis, quae ipse consecranda instituit,
ut pane & vino in Eucharistia, aqua in baptismo. Parietes, calices, patinas,
mensas consecrari non instituit. Possunt & muscariis id est flabellis ad

eventilandas muscas a vino in poculo comparatis eadem ratione eandem operam
impendere. Ordinantur enim, ut vides, ad abigendas muscas a vino. Tantam tamen
inesse putant vim suis consecrationibus, ut si quis de re prophana, licet licita,
in sacrata aede tractaverit, rem sanctam & sacram prophanasse existiment.
Ecclesiae vox 1 Cor. 11 propriè & simpliciter coetum notat, locum vero illius
connotat, qui forte fuit in privatis aedibus. In constitutionibus Ecclesiasticis
provinciae Cantuariensis anno 1604 Canone de *Ecclesiarum religione prophanis
usibus non polluenda,* statuerunt, ut Ecclesiarum Oeconomi & inquisitores non
patiantur *curias saeculares, Visus Franci plegii Leets, juratus laicos Layjuries,
lustrationes militum, vel prophanum ullum usum sive ritum in Ecclesiis, capellis,
aut coemiteriis suis fieri, aut celebrari.*

[Marginalia: Can. 88.]

ac si templa & coemiteria hisce curiis & aliis rebus licitis polluerentur. *Tanta
est Ecclesiarum religio & reverentia, ut nedum illicita in Ecclesiis prohibeantur,
verum etiam quaecunque prophana, etsi alibi legitimè exerceantur,* inquit
Durantus. *Quare judicia & arbitria in Ecclesiis exerceri vetitum est in Concilio
Lugdunensi.*

[Marginalia: De ritibus Ecclesiae cathol. li. 1. cap. 26. num. 7.]

Abutuntur verbis Christi *Domus mea, domus orationis,* quae de Hierosolymitano
templo dicta sunt. Non sinebat Christus, ut vas deportaret quisquam per templum
Marc. 11. 16 quod etiam fieri audio in Anglia praesertim per Cathedrales
basilicas. Sed a templo Hierosolymitano argumentum duci non potest ad tuendam
basilicarum nostrarum dedicationem, religionem, & immunitatem, ut supra
diximus. Non polluitur Ecclesia Curiis aut arbitriis. Unum idemque aedificium
indifferens esse potest, & ad cultum divinum, & ad curias saeculares, modò non
impediatur exercitium cultus divini, ob quem primariò addictus est locus
Ecclesiae. Unum idemque poculum indifferens esse potest ad communem & sacram
potationem, non tamen polluitur, nec opus est execratione ulla, ut loquuntur, ut
licitè id fieri possit. Non sunt enim vasa Ecclesiae sacra ut fuerunt vasa templi
Hierosolymitani quae deportata Babylonem, & prophanata in convivio Belschatzaris.
Nam ut templum illud sanctum, sic sancta erant & ejus vasa. Qui templi
Hierosolymitani exemplo suas consecrationes & immunitates tuentur, etiam
sacramenta extra Ecclesias celebrata damnare debent. Nam sacrificia nullibi
oblata, nisi in templo voluit Dominus. Inutilis est consecratio templi, si extra
templum consecratum consecrare quis potest panem Eucharisticum, sicut inutilis
fuisset consecratio templi Hierosolymitani, si extra illud templum ubique licitum
fuisset offerre sacrificia. An sanctior Eucharistia quae celebratur in aede
consecrata, quam quae extra, quam quae a Christo celebrata, quam quae administrata
ab Athanasio in Ecclesia nondum dedicata Apolog. 2 ad Constantinum. Hookerus
Anglus tamen dicit *quodammodo videri Ecclesias consecratas lugere, quasi suo
jure fraudatas, cum praeferuntur*

[Marginalia: De politica lib. 5. sect. 16.]

*eis alia loca non consecrata ad exercitium cultus publici absque ulla
necessitate: cultum divinum non eam gratiae & decoris perfectionem habere, quam
haberet, si concurreret dignitas loci quem desiderat, majestatem & sanctitatem
loci esse ratione nostri magnae virtutis & efficaciae, inservire, ut adminiculum
sensibile ad excitandam devotionem, eaque ratione praestantiores efficere
actiones nostras etiam sanctissimas & praestantissimas.*

[Marginalia: De cultu sanctor. c. 4.]

Nonne eadem dicit quae Pontificii. *In Ecclesia sunt omnia accomodata ad
excitandam devotionem, quae orationi maxime necessaria est. Nam altaria, cruces
imagines, reliquiae, ipsi etiam parietes Deo dicati movent ad pietatem; quae
certè alibi non inveniuntur* inquit Bellarminus. Unde infert, *Ecclesiae
Christianorum rectè ad orandum oratione etiam privata, instituuntur.* Idem certè
sequitur ex dictis Hookeri verbis. Et sic frequentanda erunt templa privatae
orationis causa, quia majestas & sanctitas loci excitant devotionem, & ipsi
parietes Deo dicati movent ad pietatem. In coetibus Ecclesiasticis, verum, omnia
fieri debent ordine & decenter. Sed annon ordine & decenter possunt sacramenta
ministrari, verbum praedicari extra locum consecratum, in aliis locis aequè
commodis, ubi nihil deest nisi parietum consecratio. Parietes consecrati movere
fortè possunt superstitiosum, veram devotionem excitare non possunt; Nam parietum
& locorum consecrationem non mandavit Dominus: & figmentis cerebri nostri non
benedicit, nec ut mediis utitur ad devotionem excitandam vel augendam. *Quid
supplicaturus Deo locum sanctum requiris? volens in templo orare in te ora, & ita
age semper, ut Dei templum sis, ibi enim Deus exaudit, ubi habitat,* ait
Augustinus.

[Marginalia: Lib. sententiarum.]

Non est sacratior & commendatior nobis Dei praesentia in templis quàm alibi: &
Christus monet ut oraturus *intret εἰς τὸ ταμεῖον* in conclave & oret *ἐν τῷ
κρυπτῷ* in occulto, quando orationes privatas concipit; non ablegat ad
consecratos parietes. Et in publicis coetibus major erat fervor cum nullas
habuerunt consecratas Ecclesias. Dedicationem id est deputationem loci in usum
Ecclesiae per tabulas donationis aut aliis modis, quibus rerum dominia
transferuntur vel fiunt juris publici, probamus. Suarez. dicit oratorium per
simplicem voluntatem ad sacra ministeria destinari posse tom. 3. disput. 81.
sect. 2. Cur non etiam Ecclesia. Ex Ecclesiarum consecratione natae sunt earum
immunitates & libertates, execrationes, reconciliationes, reconsecrationes,
aliaque absurda, quae omnia per se corruunt sublato isto consecrationum
principio.

Sublata Ecclesiarum consecratione coemiteriorum tollitur etiam benedictio &
dedicatio. Nam si templa non sunt consecranda, multo minus coemiteria. Usus
sepulchrorum, est usus civilis ad excipienda defunctorum corpora Iudaei in suis
praediis sepulchra habebant Iudic. cap. 2. 9. Iosu. cap. 24. 30. 32. Gen. 23. 19.
Si quis locum sepulchrorum sanctum dixerit eo sensu, quo leges & jura

[1623 printed p. 343 | IA leaf 355 | PDF p. 355 | split `ma-` / `gistratuum` joined]

magistratuum, muros & portas civitatum, valla propugnaculorum & arcium, speculas
excubitorum, & legatos sanctos & sacrosanctos dicimus, nempe quia res hae non
violandae sunt, & ab injuria Magistratus authoritate, legis sanctione, & poenae
irrogatione muniuntur, & defenduntur, non contradicimus. Sed nulla hic opus erit
benedictione constitutiva terrae sanctae, ac si sanctior esset terra illa ad
excipienda defunctorum corpora, quam alia. Nam naturaliter videtur ad mortuum
pertinere locus in quem ipse infertur *locus in quo sepelitur corpus efficitur
religiosus* Codi. lib. 3. tit. 43. l. 2. Cadaveris igitur ipsius praesentia efficit
locum dicto sensu sacrosanctum & religiosum absque ulla benedictione Pontificali.
Et propterea qui sepulchrum violat vel diruit, vel inhabitat, cadavera mortuorum
sine permissu in alium locum transfert, saxa, marmora aliaque ornamenta aufert,
defunctorum reliquias contrectat, puniatur. Sed armilustria, curias saeculares,
juratus laicos, non pati in Coemiteriis, ut supra vidimus in Canone Anglicano,
ac si iis locus aliquis sanctus pollueretur & prophanaretur, est superstitionis
pollutio non levis. Quiescunt cadavera in locis subterraneis in orbe illo
invisibili, nec polluuntur negotiationibus, judiciis, arbitriis, magis quàm
deambulationibus. Descenderunt ad inferiora terrae, relicta superiora viventibus
in usus licitos. Mynsingerus in Iustiniani institutiones in locum illum
*Religiosum locum unusquisque sua voluntate facit, dum mortuum infert in locum
suum,* sic commentatur, *Ex hoc igitur textu notandum est, religiosum locum eum
dici, in quem illatum est hominis mortui corpus vel totum, vel quod in eo
principale est, id est caput. Tametsi enim sacrum, ut aedes sacra, nonnisi publica
authoritate constituebatur: religiosum tamen locum cuilibet privata authoritate
olim facere permissum erat, hodie vero secus est propter jus pontificium de
coemiteriis consecrandis proditum, quo non sit locus religiosus, nisi per
Pontificis consecrationem.*

Purus locus erat, qui nec sacer, nec sanctus, nec religiosus habebatur. Exarari

commodè fieri potuit ad evitandum aeris insalubrioris periculum.

alioqui illi pueri nondum erant baptizati, neque ad hunc finem Christo

curarentur, ut significat Anselmus.*

In ministrorum ordinationibus manus impositae 1 Tim. 4. 14. Paulo item &

caecitas, & impleretur spiritu sancto Act. 9. 17. Item in curandis aegrotis &

manus dum orabant Act. 8. 15. 17. Erat igitur gestus apud Iudaeos familiaris
manuum impositio, quoties solennis erat precatio aliqua aut benedictio, praesertim
ubi persona singularis erat, cui benedicendum erat. Nam si numerosior esset
coetus, tum attollebantur manus. *Tum attollens Aaron manus suas versus populum
benedixit eis* Leu. 9. 22. Et Christus sublatis manibus suis benedixit discipulis
Luc. 24. 50. Observat Drusius, Sacerdotes cum populo benedicerent, si in urbe
essent, levasse manus suas versus scapulas. Veteres etiam in Ecclesia Christiana
variè hoc gestu usi sunt in ministris ordinandis, in Catechumenis admittendis, in
conversis haereticis amplectendis, in lapsis reconciliandis, ut supra diximus pag.
238. Cum

[Marginalia: Commentat. in loca difficiliora pentateuchi pag. 370.]

tam variè usurpatus hic ritus sit tam in veteri quàm in novo Testamento, & apud
veteres, & nihil aliud sit quàm gestus orantis indicans personam pro qua orat quis
nomine Ecclesiae ut impetretur gratia, non significans multo minus obsignans &
exhibens gratiam quam petit orans vel benedicens, sacramentum dici propriè non
potest. Nulla est analogia inter gestum istum & gratiam quam petit Ecclesia vel
baptizato vel reconciliando, vel ordinando, vel ad Eucharistiae sacramentum
admittendo adulto. Et si analogiam quandam comminisci possimus, tamen deerit
Christi institutum & promissio, nihil obsignabit. Nam Christi solius est, qui
potestatem habet excellentiae instituere sacramenta. Sed, ut dixi, usurpatus hic
ritus ad indicandam personam illam singularem pro qua gratiam petimus, non ad
significandam gratiam ipsam eamque obsignandam.

Pontificii excogitarunt materiam sensibilem hujus sacramenti, chrisma ex oleo
olivarum & balsamo compositum, tanquam materiam remotam & unctionem ex chrismate
factam in figura seu signo crucis, tanquam materiam proximam nec aliam manuum
impositionem agnoscunt Pontificii essentialem huic confirmationis sacramento,
quàm quae includitur intrinsecè in ipsa unctione. *Dicendum est,* inquit Swarez,
*nullam manus impositionem distinctam ab unctione Chrismatis manu Episcopi facta
esse de essentia hujus sacramenti: ipsam verò unctionem esse essentialem &
utrumque satis constare ex dictis, & ex usu Ecclesiae, neque huic veritati obstare
Apostolicum factum nempe Act. 8. quia illud non fuit juxta legem & institutionem
sacramentorum, sed praeter illam ex speciali omissione vel dispensatione, &
consequenter signum illud simplicis impositionis manus non fuisse verum
sacramentum Confirmationis quoad substantiam, fuisse vero quoad effectum. Item,
Simplicem manuum impositionem, qua usi sunt Apostoli Act. 8 & 9 revera non fuisse
sacramentum Confirmationis quod nunc habemus, quia in ritu sensibili valde
differebat, imo nec simpliciter sacramentum fuisse, quia non erat ceremonia firma
& stabili lege instituta.*

[Marginalia: Swarez. tom. 3. disput. 33. sect. 4.]

Bellarminus, etsi capite secundo de Confirmatione dicat non rejectam esse manus
impositionem. *Bis enim Episcopus manus imponit confirmandis, ut ex Pontificali
Romano intelligi potest, semel cum super eos manus extendit atque orat; & rursum
cum signat in fronte atque ungit: ipsa enim unctio & signatio cùm manu fiat,
manus impositio rectissimè dicitur.* Capite tamen nono, *idem esse unctionem
Chrismatis & manus impositionem, qui enim ungit, manus imponit, & idcirco
utrumque significari, etiamsi alterum tantum exprimi videatur. Nam constat
Apostolos in Confirmatione usos esse Chrismate.* Ad primum locum Bellarmini sic
Spalatensis. *Atqui illa extensio manuum valdè impropriè dicitur impositio, & si
sit impositio, non est tamen sacramentalis sed purè deprecatoria: neque in ea
quisquam nostrum posuit vim*

[Marginalia: De republ. l. 5. ca. 5. n. 29.]

*sacramenti: non ergo illa extensio aut impositio est materia proxima sacramenti.
Pollice verò duntaxat frontem tangere esse manum imponere nimis coactum est,
nimisque violentum. cum manus imposita semper intelligatur expansa, & desuper
applicata. Pontifex autem tantummodo tangit uno digito adversam frontem hominis
confirmandi, idque non ut ei manum imponat, sed ut crucem imprimat.* Ad
posteriorem sic, *Mira certè sacrae scripturae declaratio an detorsio? Manus

manum facta, quae sit praecipua & Sacramentalis propriè? Certè si manus adhibetur

unctio verò erit actio principalis, & principaliter intenta. Ineptè scilicet Lucas,

### English translation

[1623 printed p. 323 | IA leaf 335 | PDF p. 335 | begins at `In Ecclesia ratione Vocationis`]

In the church, with respect to calling or function. With respect to the
external calling to the episcopate, in which some things concern the prince
and some the bishops.

[1623 printed p. 324 | IA leaf 336 | PDF p. 336 | split `Princi-` / `pem` joined]

As regards the prince: before the election, to nominate and grant the power
of electing; after the election, to give royal assent, command the archbishop
to confirm and, with two other bishops, to consecrate him, require the oath of
homage from the new bishop, and restore the possessions of the episcopate. As
regards the bishops: the archbishop who confirms the election, and he and two
others who consecrate according to the prescription of the Book of
Ordinations.

Mocket says: *After bishops in England, by the king's license and command, are
elected by the dean and chapter of the cathedral churches within the dioceses
over which they are to be set, those elected are approved by his assent; once
confirmed they are consecrated; and when, after consecration, they have sworn
homage to the king, the king in turn restores to them the possessions of
their episcopates.*

[Marginalia: *De Polit.* pag. 313.]

The defender of true and Christian ecclesiastical polity describes the order
of proceeding as follows. When a church such as Canterbury, Winchester, and
so forth is vacant, the dean and chapter notify the king that they have been
bereft of a bishop, and humbly ask the royal majesty to grant them power to
elect another. By his sealed patent the king grants the power, admonishing
them *to elect as bishop and pastor one who is devoted to God, necessary to
his church, and useful and faithful to us and our kingdom.*

[Marginalia: Pag. 274. 275.]

But with the patent he sends letters commendatory naming the bishop to be
elected. After the election they ask again that he give royal assent to the
lord elect, *to the Lord elected*. He then sends another patent to the
archbishop, or to another bishop whom he has delegated, to confirm by his
assent the approved man and consecrate him according to the prescribed order.
After confirmation and consecration, and after homage and the oath of
allegiance have been given, a royal writ is sent from Chancery to the official
who returns forfeited goods to the Exchequer or prince's treasury, ordering
that the temporalities of the episcopate be restored to him. If he wishes,
the bishop can also obtain another writ from Chancery directing tenants and
stewards to acknowledge him as their lord. This imaginary form of election
has entirely taken away the church's own liberty in electing. There is
nothing here about the consent, much less the votes, of the people and leading
citizens, or of the presbyters of the diocese. When the Council of Trent
treated the qualifications of bishops, there was no controversy, since all
with one voice declaimed against the vices and enormities of prelates and of
the ecclesiastical order. But Juan de Salazar, bishop of Lanciano, cast all
the blame upon the Roman court as the source of the evil, since in
distributing episcopates it considers not the merits of persons but the

[Marginalia: Petri Suavis *histor. Conc. Trid.* pa. 277 edit. Francofur.]

[1623 printed p. 325 | IA leaf 337 | PDF p. 337 | split `ope-` / `ram` joined]

service rendered to itself. *The bishop of Bitonto answered him very
prudently that this charge was undeservedly laid upon the Roman court when it
ought to be transferred elsewhere: in Germany episcopates are conferred by
election, and in France, Spain, and Hungary by royal nomination; in Italy most
are subject to a right of patronage, while in conferring those which are free
the recommendations of princes—whose requests are armed—must receive the
greatest weight. Since they admit no excuse, they deprive the pontiff of the
freedom to confer them according to merit.* Cardinal Lorraine, when about to
give his opinion on episcopal election in another congregation of that
council, *openly condemned the concordat, saying that Pope Leo and King
Francis had divided between them the collation of the kingdom's benefices,
which ought to belong to chapters; and he came close to saying that booty is
customarily divided in this way among hunters.*

[Marginalia: Pag. 790.]

Paul IV said that *once he had purged his court of this leaven, simony, so
that no one could object to him, “Physician, heal yourself,” he was determined
to show the princes themselves that the evil of simony raged more violently
in their courts, and that he, who stood above princes as well as prelates, did
not lack zeal to remove it.*

[Marginalia: Pag. 447.]

On this evil of simony in promoting English bishops, see what I transcribed
above at p. 95 from Brightman. The French turn to lamentation and prayers in
their synods. At the Synod of Rouen in 1581 they say: *We all desire from God
and humbly pray our most Christian king that elections be restored together
with the ancient and uncorrupted form of electing. But since no hope of
obtaining this yet appears, whenever a see becomes vacant we command prayers
and solemn supplications to be made to God that he give the flock a suitable
pastor.* Likewise at the Council of Bourges in 1584 it was decreed *that when
an episcopal see is vacant, public prayers and supplications be appointed by
the chapters.*

[Marginalia: Bochellus in decretis Eccl. Gallican. pag. 771.]

They desire the right of elections to be restored to the clergy, as is clear
from the Council of Reims in 1583. The church's affairs would indeed be more
prosperous if a free election were permitted to the clergy than when princes
arrogate the whole or complete election to themselves, or nomination and
approval, leaving the chapter only a shadowy election of the nominee, which
cannot properly be called an election. Every election properly so called is
comparative: from several, the most suitable one is elected. Nor should this
election by the chapter be approved, even if complete; neither is it so
ancient. We saw above from Bellarmine and Field that for many centuries no
difference was recognized between canons and non-canons, cardinal and
non-cardinal presbyters, and that in Gregory's age all clerics participated
in election. But Bellarmine says, *during the last five hundred years all
clerics have been admitted neither to councils nor to election, because they
would be too many, but only the cardinals.*

[Marginalia: *De clericis* cap. 16.]

Yet in Gregory's age there were many presbyters in the Roman church. For if
in Cornelius's time, about the year 250, amid persecutions and under idolatrous
rulers, Rome had forty-six presbyters, there were far

[1623 printed p. 326 | IA leaf 338 | PDF p. 338]

more in Gregory's time, when there was no pagan in the city; so Bellarmine
himself infers there. Nor is a numerous body of presbyters a just reason to
exclude anyone from the right of election. By the same reasoning only a few
bishops should be summoned to general councils, and parliaments of the estates
would consist of fewer members than is fitting. All the ministers of London,
therefore, ought to be summoned to elect the bishop, not only the members of
the chapter. Even if the English form of election by the chapter were
complete, it is more recent and arose only after the institution of canons and
chapters was introduced. Calvin says, *The entire power of electing bishops
was transferred to the canons alone. They confer the episcopate upon whomever
they wish and soon present him before the people, not to be examined but
adored.*

[Marginalia: *Institut.* lib. 4. cap. 1. sect. 2.]

In Germany bishops are sometimes elected by only six or eight men, as
Bellarmine admits.

[Marginalia: *De cler.* ca. 7.]

When I say that all presbyters have the right of election, I mean not only
those of the city but also those of the whole diocese. Before the whole body
of a particular diocesan church was divided into parts or parishes, all the
presbyters elected. It is not equitable that after the body has been divided
they should be so bound to their particular parishes as to be excluded from
common affairs, or that one part of the church should have a stronger right
of election than another. But there is also an older form of election, by
clergy and people. Cyprian says of Cornelius, bishop of the Roman church:
*Cornelius was made bishop by the judgment of God and his Christ, by the
testimony of almost all the clergy, by the vote of the people then present,
and by the college*—some manuscripts read *consent*—*of ancient priests and
good men.* He also relates that Cornelius *neither sought nor desired the
episcopate, nor invaded it as others do who are swollen with arrogance and
pride, but was quiet and modest, as those divinely chosen for this place are
accustomed to be. Because of the modesty of his virginal conscience and his
native and guarded humility, he did not, as someone did, use force to become
a bishop; rather, force was used upon him so that he was compelled to accept
the episcopate.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 4. ep. 2.]

The British suffer no force in accepting the episcopate, but canvass for it
by many devices. Cyprian also relates concerning Sabinus that *the episcopate
had been conferred upon him by the vote of the whole brotherhood and the
judgment of the bishops who were present.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 1. ep. 2.]

When a successor to Anteros was to be elected, Eusebius reports that all the
brethren assembled in the church, and while many considered several
distinguished candidates, the people proclaimed Fabian worthy of the
episcopate.

[Marginalia: Euseb. lib. 6. cap. 29.]

In the letter of the Council of Nicaea to the Alexandrians and the brethren in
Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis, preserved by Theodoret, it was decreed that
those created by Meletius should be confirmed by a more sacred ordination.
*But if some ruler of the church should die, let those recently received have
the right to succeed to the place of the deceased, εἰ ἄξιοι φαίνοιντο, καὶ ὁ
λαὸς αἱροῖτο,*

[Marginalia: Hist. l. 1. c. 9.]

[1623 printed p. 327 | IA leaf 339 | PDF p. 339 | split `modo` carried from catchword]

*provided they should appear worthy and the people should elect them.* After
the death of the Arian Auxentius, the Milanese disputed because they did not
all choose one man to undertake the episcopate of Milan, *οἱ τὸν αὐτὸν
αἱρούμενοι*; at last by common votes they conferred the episcopate upon
Ambrose, as Sozomen reports.

[Marginalia: Sozomenus li. 6. c. 24.]

He also reports that Chrysostom succeeded Nectarius, *ψηφισαμένων δὲ τοῦτο τοῦ
λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ κλήρου, after clergy and people had cast their votes for this.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 8. cap. 2.]

Theodoret relates that Nectarius himself was elected *παντός τε κλήρου καὶ

whole city.

[Marginalia: Th. li. 5. c. 9.]

After the death of Eudoxius of Constantinople, *the Arians put Demophilus in
his place; but the supporters of the consubstantial faith, thinking they had
found an opportune time, προεβάλλοντο, nominated, a certain Evagrius, a
supporter of their faith,* says Socrates.

[Marginalia: Soc. l. 4. c. 14.]

And Sozomen says that they *ἐψηφίζοντο, elected by their votes, a certain
Evagrius.*

[Marginalia: Soc. l. 6. c. 13.]

The Homoousians *προχειρίζονται, choose,* a certain Paul for the episcopate of

[Marginalia: Soc. li. 2. c. 6.]

After Nestorius was deposed, many Constantinopolitans *ἐπιλέγοντο, elected,*
Philip, and more elected Proclus.

[Marginalia: Soc. l. 7. c. 35.]

*Thus in the early church the people proposed, nominated, elected, and decided
just as the clergy did. Although presbyters had greater power of judgment in
discernment, the people had the power to elect their pastors, so that if the

*Let it be done, let it be done,* twenty-five times.

[Marginalia: Epist. 110.]

[Marginalia: Lib. *de divinis officiis*.]

Why accumulate more testimonies, when the adversaries admit the very thing?
Bellarmine says: *This manner, in which the clergy and people of the city
elected, was in use in the times of Chrysostom, Augustine, Leo, and Gregory.*

[Marginalia: *De Cleric.* ca. 9.]

But Pamelius is less candid: *We do not deny the ancient rite of episcopal
election in which bishops were customarily elected in the people's presence,
and indeed by their votes; but the people were granted votes alone, not the
election, which was customarily made by subscription.*

[Marginalia: In Cyprian. ep. 68. not. 16.]

When Leo distinguishes the duties of each in election, assigning votes to
citizens, testimony to peoples, judgment to leading men, and election to
clerics—or subscription to clerics, testimony to leading men, and consent to
order and people—he speaks rhetorically in his usual fashion rather than
drawing a logical distinction.

[Marginalia: Leo epist. 87.]

[1623 printed p. 328 | IA leaf 340 | PDF p. 340 | split `Cleri-` / `cis` joined]

For he assigns testimony now to peoples and now to leading men. Moreover, he
perhaps speaks of the custom of his own age. The testimonies cited above show
that the people proposed, nominated, elected, decided, and designated. We
heard above that the first nomination, ἀποβολήν, rested with the people.
Whenever some candidates were designated for the episcopate by clergy or
council, one was nevertheless elected by the votes or assent of the people.
If others made the nomination, the people made the election; conversely, if
the people made the nomination, others gave consent and election. Whether
they testified their assent by acclamation, raising hands, or subscription is
immaterial: the people walked in step with the clergy, as Bilson says.
Pamelius says: *The chief object then was that no bishop be given to an
unwilling people; yet not so that bishops were compelled to admit whomever the
people requested, as appears from canons 1 and 2 of the Greek synods collected
by Martin of Braga, from the cited letter of Celestine, and from Pope
Stephen's decree at distinction 63, part 2, chapter Nosse. If the people
requested someone contrary to the canons, clergy and people were customarily
summoned again and more often until they agreed upon a suitable person, as may
be gathered from Pope Gelasius's letter to Philip and Gerontius cited by
Gratian at distinction 63, chapter Plebs. If an objection arose from some,
the objectors were first examined, and only then, if their objections were not
true, was the bishop ordained, as appears from canon 40 of the third Council
of Carthage.*

[Marginalia: *De gubern.* c. 15. p. 434.]

The people were not compelled to receive whomever clergy or other bishops
nominated. Neither clergy nor people could compel others contrary to the
canons. Those who elected Proclus desisted when some taught that the canons
forbade transferring one nominated bishop of a city to another city.

[Marginalia: Socrates lib. 7. cap. 35.]

It is enough that Pamelius concedes that no bishop was given to an unwilling
people; without the people's votes, the subscription Leo mentions was
useless. Bellarmine says that this manner of election, with the people's
testimony and consent, lasted a long time.

[Marginalia: *De Cler.* c. 8.]

Pamelius admits that it lasted to Gregory, indeed to the emperors Charlemagne
and Louis. Baronius reports that episcopal election, usurped by Charlemagne's
predecessors, was restored to its former liberty by a law enacted with Louis,
his partner in empire, found among their ecclesiastical and civil capitularies
at book 1, chapter 84, and inserted in Gratian, distinction 63, chapter 34.

[Marginalia: Ann. 813. nu. 7.]

That capitulary assigns election to clergy and people according to the sacred
canons. The reader has its words above at p. 8. Cyprian says that popular
election descends from divine authority: *Therefore a people obedient to the
Lord's commands and fearing God ought to separate from a sinful prelate and
not*

[Marginalia: Li. 1. ep. 4.]

[1623 printed p. 329 | IA leaf 341 | PDF p. 341 | catchword `miscere` omitted]

*mingle in the sacrifices of a sacrilegious priest, since it especially has
power either to elect worthy priests or reject the
unworthy. We see that this too descends from divine authority: a priest is
chosen in the people's presence before everyone's eyes, and one worthy and
suitable is approved by public judgment and testimony.* Here the people have
power to elect the worthy and reject the unworthy. This letter is not
Cyprian's alone, but that of an African synod and many bishops. They bring
examples from the election of Matthias in Acts 1:23 and of the deacons in
Acts 6:5, where the multitude of believers receives the role of discerning
and electing suitable men. Acts 14:23 may be added, where Paul and Barnabas
are said to create presbyters by votes in every church. For χειροτονέω
properly means to stretch out, extend, or raise the hand, as though τὴν χεῖρα
τείνω, which was done in voting; hence it means not only extending the hand to
vote, but electing a magistrate by votes when construed with an accusative.
As Athenian citizens signified their vote by raising hands at the herald's
request in the forum, so believers did in the church at the presiding
officer's request. It never means laying hands upon someone being ordained or
inaugurated, whether in pagan writers or elsewhere in Scripture. In 2
Corinthians 8:19 it is properly used of one elected by votes; elsewhere it is
metaphorical for deciding or designating in any manner, as in Acts 4, for God
did not choose the apostles by any stretching out or imposition of hands. The
Septuagint expresses inaugural imposition by another term, ἐπίθεσις τῶν
χειρῶν. Luke departs as little as possible from the Septuagint's familiar
words and phrases, then current among Hellenists. We should not believe he
attached a new meaning never before used by Greek authors, and one rejected
by the very custom of voting through outstretched hands. They raised their
hands when signifying a vote, for the herald said, “Whoever disapproves of
this or that, ἀέρτω τὴν χεῖρα, let him raise his hand.” Who could aptly call
placing and therefore lowering hands upon the ordinand's head a raising of
hands? The fathers nevertheless departed from the word's genuine meaning and
called the imposition of hands in ordination χειροτονίαν. Sometimes they
retain its proper meaning, as Pseudo-Ignatius does in the letter to the
Philadelphians, and as the Council of Laodicea does according to Balsamon. Its
canon says: *It is not proper for τὰς χειροτονίας to be made in the presence
of hearers.*

[Marginalia: Can. 5.]

“By Χειροτονίας,” he says, *he here means τὰς ψήφους, that is, votes or
election. Since in elections some disgraceful things are said about those
elected, the synod does not wish hearers to be present.* So he explains it.
Zonaras follows the same interpretation and says on Apostolic Canon 1: *Now
χειροτονία is called the completion of prayers and invocation of the Holy
Spirit when*

[Marginalia: Zonaras in Can. 1. Ap.]

[1623 printed p. 330 | IA leaf 342 | PDF p. 342 | catchword `sacerdotio` omitted]

*one is initiated into priesthood and receives consecration. It is so called
because the pontiff extends his hand, blessing the man selected for sacred
things.* (But that extension is not the rite of consecration from which
ordination should be named; it only precedes it and is not an upward extension
such as was used in voting.) *But formerly αὐτὴ ἡ ψῆφος χειροτονία ὠνόμαστο,
the vote itself was called chirotonia. For when the people of cities were
permitted to elect sacred rulers, they assembled, some choosing one and some
another. For the majority's vote to prevail, the electors are said to have
extended their hands. From the hands thus extended they counted those who had
chosen one or the other, and appointed to the high priesthood the man elected
by the votes of more. Hence the name chirotonia, which the synodical fathers
are also found to use in this way, calling a vote, ψῆφον, chirotonia.* The
church's power in elections to other offices indicates that it possessed a
right in electing pastors. Paul's travelling companion, *χειροτονηθεὶς ὑπὸ τῶν
ἐκκλησιῶν, elected by the churches' votes,* was chosen to carry their gift for
the relief of the Jerusalem church, 2 Corinthians 8:19. And Acts 15:22 says,
*Then it pleased the apostles and presbyters, with the whole church, to send
to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas ἐκλεξαμένους ἄνδρας ἐξ αὐτῶν, chosen men
from themselves, Judas and Silas, with the synod's letter. In elections of
ministers in the apostles' time and by their institution, the people and the
whole multitude had a great part. This is democracy's first and perhaps chief
effect: the multitude itself elects its magistrates and governors,* says
Spalatensis.

[Marginalia: *De rep.* li. 1. c. 22. num. 10.]

He continues: *The church has after its manner something of a democratic form
in itself—not only because men may and customarily are taken even from the
lowest people for ecclesiastical offices and ministries, including the
episcopate if suitable, though this scarcely has the least savor of democracy,
and in this the people are merely passive and derive no power or authority
from it.* He therefore asserts that they actively and executively concur in
their manner in excommunication and had an active part in elections by
apostolic institution; he teaches this more fully in book 3 and proves by many
examples that it was the ancient church's custom and lasted almost to our
times.

[Marginalia: Cap. 3.]

*Indeed, after the Council of Nicaea the church perpetually maintained the
same ancient custom in elections almost to our time, so that election was
made by clergy and people, as is now to be proved from the fathers, events,
councils, and laws, and from the attestations and decrees of Roman pontiffs.*

[Marginalia: *De Rep. Eccl.* l. 3. c. 3. n. 12.]

That the people's part was not only to testify to the candidate's life and
conduct, but also to decide, elect, propose, request, and sometimes assent to
a request, we proved above from Eradius, Ambrose, Flavian, Nectarius,
Evagrius, Proclus, and Alcuin. If the people requested an unworthy or
uncanonical man, Celestine taught in his letter to the bishops

[Marginalia: Dist. 62. c. 2.]

[1623 printed p. 331 | IA leaf 343 | PDF p. 343 | split `Episco-` / `pos` joined]

of Calabria and Apulia that they should be instructed, not deprived of their
right. Spalatensis asserts that this and no other is the meaning of the
Laodicean canon: *the people must not be permitted absolutely to fulfill their
will in electing persons, when they choose even unqualified persons contrary
to the canons.*

[Marginalia: Laod. Concil. can. 13.]

*Martin of Braga understood this canon in that sense. In his collection he
places it at distinction 63 in these terms: “It is not lawful.” The people may
not elect those promoted to the priesthood, but the bishops must judge and
approve whether he is instructed in speech and faith and in the spiritual
life. By election, therefore, he also understands approval. Simple election
is left to the people by the most common right, provided approval or rejection
of the elect belongs not to the people but to the ordaining bishops.*

[Marginalia: Spalat. *de Rep.* l. 3. c. 3. num. 50.]

The church's practice before and after that canon shows its genuine meaning.
No one is so foolish as to advise the clergy to follow the multitude or be
swept away by its errors. But common sense teaches that a bishop must not be
forced upon an unwilling church, *lest an unwilling people despise or hate an
unwanted bishop and become less religious than is fitting because they were
not permitted to have whom they wished.* The Council of Paris in 559 decreed:
*Because ancient custom is neglected and the canons violated in some matters,
it has pleased us that the canonical decrees be observed according to ancient
custom. Let no bishop be ordained against the citizens' will unless the
election of people and clergy has sought him with fullest consent; and let him
not be intruded by princely command or on any condition against the will of
the metropolitan and the bishops of the province.* Princes had then begun
gradually to invade the church's rights when they saw bishops become powerful
in worldly affairs and rivals of magnates in many places. At first they wished
only to be counted among those consenting and ratifying a completed election,
and initially not even that; but finally they arrogated all power to
themselves, leaving in some places to the chapter a shadowy election—or rather
approval—of the nominee. Someone may say that the people's power was
transferred to the king with the people's consent, since all the kingdom's
citizens consent through their delegates in parliament to the law enacted
about this form of election. So Whitgift of Canterbury replies. But
Cartwright answers that this is precisely the question: whether they did so
lawfully. Has this egg of papal election lost its poisonous nature because
Parliament sat upon it? The citizens' consent is given once in parliament
when the law is enacted; particular consent is required whenever a pastor is
elected. A parliament of the estates is not an assembly of the church. The
national assembly of the church—that is, a lawful and free national council,
especially where the church is reformed—must define this form of election as
lawful before anything concerning it is enacted in parliament; that is, this
form

[1623 printed p. 332 | IA leaf 344 | PDF p. 344 | split `electi-` / `onis` joined]

of election can be enacted in parliament if it was first instituted by a
council of the church, where the church is represented as an ecclesiastical
and spiritual community, not in a parliament of the estates. I say this on
the supposition that particular churches can surrender their right and can do
so only following a decree of a general synod. But they cannot transfer that
right—especially a perpetual and almost hereditary right of election—to a
national council, much less to the prince. For, as Cartwright says, the right
of election is part of those liberties which Christ purchased by his blood;
it may no more be transferred or alienated than the inheritance of the
kingdom of heaven to which it is attached.

[Marginalia: 2 Repl. 1 part. p. 226.]

The church must take care concerning all means of salvation. It ought,
therefore, to elect a pastor who is one among a thousand, as Job says—the most
excellent, holy, learned, and vigilant it can—and not depend on others'
judgment, especially that of princes, or rather courtiers. Canonists and
imperialists fight each other, the former for the pontiff and the latter for
the emperor. On either side there is flattery; few stand for the church.
Because pontiffs despoiled churches of this liberty, emperors and princes
should not claim it for themselves but, as the church's foster-fathers and
advocates, if they delight in these titles, restore it to the churches. Let
them exact taxes and receive regalian rights or other civil jurisdictions
annexed to the temporalities of episcopates, even those of lower courts; but
let them leave the election of pastors—no small liberty of the church, on
which its welfare depends—to the churches, lest they be charged with greater
sacrilege than if they plundered the estates of a thousand episcopates.
Experience loudly declares that churches flourish more or less according as
election is more or less free, and flourish most where, under the presbytery's
instruction and guidance, leading citizens and faithful commoners together
request and elect, or possess the power Cyprian describes of receiving the
worthy and rejecting the unworthy. But when, while they sleep unwillingly,
groan, yet dare not speak, a bishop is set over them whom the papal or princely
court has sent, anyone who does not see that Epicureanism is brought into the
church from one side and popery from the other—or popery from the first and
Epicureanism from the second—is blind. Let the reader see more on episcopal
election above at pp. 6–9.

We spoke of royal assent at p. 21. We do not condemn royal assent simply, for
we know that emperors and princes sometimes gave their assent to the free
election of clergy and people. But we wholly deny that election so depends
upon the prince's assent that, if assent is denied, every election becomes
void, or only the election he strengthens by assent has force. The former
grants the prince a privative power in matters Christ granted primarily and
immediately to the church, so that the church would have no pastors if the
prince so pleased. But the prince has only a power

[1623 printed p. 333 | IA leaf 345 | PDF p. 345 | split `potesta-` / `tem` joined]

cumulative, not privative, in the liberties Christ delivered to the church.
The latter, if it does not remove every election, at least removes free
election; it makes no difference whether the prince elects beforehand or
gives assent afterward, for they come to the same thing. Pamelius said above
that if the people requested or elected someone contrary to the canons, clergy
and people were customarily summoned repeatedly until they agreed upon a
suitable man. But if someone is canonically elected by clergy and people, it
would be unjust for the prince to render the election useless at his mere
pleasure. The election of city magistrates does not so depend upon the supreme
magistrate's will. Moreover, what need is there of the prince's particular
assent in every election when the lawful form of election previously received
by the church can be enacted by authority of laws—that is, by consent of
prince and estates in parliament?

We treated the bishops' confirmation by the metropolitan at pp. 50 and 54.

Three matters must be considered in the consecration of bishops: (1)
consecration itself, (2) the consecrator, and (3) the rite of consecration. As
to the first, we showed that bishop and presbyter have one and the same
ordination, and that no essential difference has yet been introduced into the
church, not even under popery itself. We proved bishop and presbyter to be of
the same order; therefore a bishop does not need a new ordination, or, as they
call it, consecration. Bonaventure says: *A man is not so properly said to be
ordained when promoted to the episcopate as when promoted to the priesthood,
but is more properly said to be consecrated,* because, as he shortly before
said, *just as he receives no other keys, so he receives no other order.* And
below: *A priest is not anointed from necessity of order, but rather for
solemnity, that he may worthily consecrate the most sacred body of Christ.*
Again: *As conferral of the pallium does not give an archbishop a new order
beyond that of bishop, so conferral of staff, ring, mitre, or anything similar
does not give an order beyond that of priest. Such things are sacramentals
rather than sacraments. For sacramentals do not only precede a sacrament; they
can also follow it.*

[Marginalia: In lib. 4 dist. 25. qu. 3.]

The bishop therefore received the sacrament of order or ordination when he
was ordained presbyter. What more does he now receive but certain theatrical
sacramentals instituted not by Christ but by the church? In the first century
after the apostles there was no promotion to the episcopate other than by
election. Willet also gathers this from Ambrose and Jerome: Ambrose says,
*There is one episcopate and presbyterate and the same ordination*; Jerome
says, *At Alexandria the presbyters called one elected from themselves and
placed in a higher rank the bishop.*

[Marginalia: Synop. pag. 241.]

Albinus Flaccus Alcuinus gathers from the same words of Jerome that a bishop's
consecration was nothing other than election. *What episcopal consecration was
in antiquity is shown*

[Marginalia: Libro de Divinis officiis cap. 36. vel pag. 53.]

[1623 printed p. 334 | IA leaf 346 | PDF p. 346 | split `Hierony-` / `mus` joined]

*by Jerome in his letter to Evagrius, saying: At Alexandria, from Mark the Evangelist to
Heraclas and Dionysius, the presbyters always called one elected from
themselves and placed or stationed in a higher rank the bishop, just as an
army makes an emperor and deacons elect from themselves one they know to be
industrious and call him archdeacon. An archdeacon has the same consecration
as the other deacons, but is placed over them by the brethren's election.*
Jerome likewise says on the epistle to Titus: *Before schisms arose in
religion and it was said among peoples, and so forth, it was decreed
throughout the world that one elected from the presbyters should be set over
the others.* As an archdeacon with the same consecration as other deacons is
set over them by the brethren's election, so a bishop with the same
consecration as the other bishops—that is, presbyters whom Scripture calls
bishops—was set over them by the brethren's election. This Albinus Alcuinus,
Bede's disciple, flourished around 780. As to the second point, the ordainer
or consecrator: granting without conceding that a new consecration must take
place, we say that any bishop can be consecrated by any bishop and this is not
peculiar to the metropolitan. We proved this above at pp. 54–55, and there
showed from Bede that presbyters also consecrated bishops. Indeed, if we
granted the hierarchists that Timothy was an ordinary bishop, 2 Timothy 4
would prove that presbyters also laid hands upon bishops in their ordination,
for in the previous chapter we taught that *presbytery* means an assembly of
presbyters. The Rhemists admit that from this passage of Paul was derived the
canon of the fourth Council of Carthage permitting presbyters in a
presbyter's ordination to place their hands beside the bishop's hands. Their
claim that the lesser must be blessed by the greater is therefore worthless.
And how does a bishop consecrate a bishop, even an archbishop, and the bishop
of Ostia the pope? Let Bonaventure answer: *A bishop blesses a bishop. It must
be said that there is no impropriety in this, because he is blessed only
insofar as he lacks episcopal order or dignity.* The consecrating presbyters
are greater than the presbyter being consecrated bishop.

[Marginalia: In 4 sentent. Dist. 25. q. 1.]

The theologian of Auxerre says in book 4 of his *Summa*: *If there were only
three simple priests in the world, one of them would have to consecrate
another bishop and the other archbishop. Presbyters, like archbishops, receive
the keys of the kingdom of heaven in their ordination, for they too are
successors of the apostles.* Presbyters could do this in no case unless they
possessed that power of consecration by virtue of their presbyteral order; but
enough was said about this in the preceding chapter. As to the number of
consecrating bishops, three are required here. But it was not so among the
ancients. *They said that bishops' ordinations and depositions must take place
in councils. If any such thing were done elsewhere, they held it null. Divine
tradition,* says Cyprian, *and apostolic ordination must be observed*

*and maintained, as is maintained among us and through almost all provinces:

With respect to function, in matters pertaining either to the episcopal order
or to jurisdiction. As to the episcopal order:

1. To ordain deacons and ministers according to the prescription of the Book
of Ordinations.

We proved in the preceding chapter that the power of ordination is not a right
peculiar to bishops but is common to them with presbyters. We shall discuss in
their own places below the prescribed form for ordaining presbyters and deacons,
and whether the diaconate is an order. Here only one thing must be observed: all
these things which are here

[1623 printed p. 337 | IA leaf 349 | PDF p. 349]

listed are of the same right and institution, namely human—that bishops confer
orders, dedicate churches, and confirm children—and were reserved to the bishop
more for the honor of the priesthood than from necessity of law. The Council of
Aachen, celebrated under Emperor Louis in 816, says that ordination was reserved
to the bishop solely on account of authority. *Presbyters, however, are
interpreted as elders, because the Greeks call elders presbyters by reason of
age. For to them, as to bishops, the dispensation of God's mysteries has been
committed. They preside over Christ's church and are partners with bishops in
the confection of the Lord's body and blood, likewise in teaching the peoples
and in the office of preaching; and only on account of authority were the
ordination and consecration of clerics reserved to the chief priest.*

[Marginalia: Cap. 8.]

The same things are found in Isidore's second book *On Ecclesiastical Offices*.
*It should further be known that after this partition and distribution of the
churches,* Duarenus says, *the power and authority of the bishop in his large
diocese and of the presbyters in their churches were not equal, since the
ordination of presbyters and the other ministers of the church, the consecration
of basilicas, and certain other things were permitted only to the bishop, not
also to inferior presbyters.*

[Marginalia: Cap. 7.]

[Marginalia: De Benef. & minist. l. 2. c. 7.]

It is as if he had said that before the distribution and division of parishes
these offices were common to bishop and presbyters. He takes Dionysius of Rome
to be the author of the distribution of parishes through cities and villages,
on the report of the second epistle of Pope Dionysius to Bishop Severinus. *But
this very thing produced from Pope Dionysius was fabricated many years after
Dionysius. For that epistle is apocryphal and of no authority,* Spalatensis
says. *When the Roman pontiffs had assigned individual churches to individual
presbyters and divided parishes among them, Honorius, archbishop of Canterbury,
around the year of restored salvation 636, first began to distribute England
into parishes, as is read in the history of Canterbury,* Camden reports.

[Marginalia: L. 9. c. 7. num. 34.]

[Marginalia: B it. p. 100. edit. Francof.]

However that may be, whether before or after the division, Duarenus says that
ordination was reserved to the bishop in the same manner and by the same right
as the consecration of basilicas; and he cites Isidore's words, which appear in
Gratian, distinction 25, chapter *Perlectus*. Thus Leo, as we saw above at p.
232, said that ordination and the consecration of basilicas were reserved to
bishops by ecclesiastical canons and rules.

2. To dedicate churches and places of burial.

Mocket says: *To dedicate churches and places of burial to sacred uses by the
word and thanksgiving; which,* he says, *as Eusebius testifies in book 10,
chapter 3, the ancient bishops performed with great solemnity in the temples
magnificently built by Constantine.*

[Marginalia: De polit. ca. 5. p. 319.]

And why, I ask, do they not produce examples of dedications before Constantine?
For even before Constantine Christians had their ἐκκλησιαστήρια, as Isidore of
Pelusium calls them in book 2, epistle 247—that is, places appointed for the
church to assemble—which were also called

[1623 printed p. 338 | IA leaf 350 | PDF p. 350 | split `εὐκτή-` / `ρια` joined]

εὐκτήρια and προσευκτήρια, that is, oratories.

[Marginalia: Euseb. Hist. l. 8. cap. 1.]

Eusebius reports that before the persecution raised under Diocletian, the
assemblies were so numerous and the gatherings at the oratories so notable that,
not content with the old buildings, in each city they constructed larger and
more spacious churches from the very foundations. We do indeed read of church
dedications before Constantine's age in the spurious epistles of Clement,
Evaristus, or similar men. In their age Christians had no temples, much less
consecrated ones, but met in private houses and sometimes in caves and
underground crypts when persecution was raging. Constantine introduced the
dedications together with the luxury and magnificence of temples and basilicas.
Buildings destroyed by the machines of impious tyrants and leveled to the
ground began under Constantine to rise again, brighter and loftier in renewed
construction. *At that time a spectacle desired and longed for by us was
presented: celebrations of dedications throughout the cities and consecrations
of newly constructed oratories,* Eusebius says.

[Marginalia: L. 10. cap. 3.]

At first light and humble buildings were erected, and as long as they were light
and humble no one thought them worthy of consecration. But when they surpassed
royal halls in splendor, size, and ornament, then they began to dedicate them,
as if religion inhered in them by reason of ornament and splendor. Yet those
theatrical rites of dedication and consecration which appear in the Pontifical
and Roman Order had not yet been introduced into the church in Constantine's
age. By what rites the Anglicans dedicate churches is not yet clear to me. They
doubtless have a prescribed form and office of consecration. For although Mocket
says that among them churches and cemeteries are dedicated by the word and
thanksgiving, I do not think it is done without a special office, namely a
prescribed number and form of readings, prayers, litanies, canticles, and other
ceremonies. If I had found the Anglican office of consecration described
anywhere, I would doubtless have discovered many things agreeing with the Roman
Pontifical. Hooker denies that the laying of the foundation stone by the bishop
is practiced among the Anglican rites of dedication.

[Marginalia: De Polit. lib. 5. sect. 12.]

Nor is that necessary, for, as Piacesius observes from the gloss on chapter
*Nemo Ecclesiam*, distinction 1, *On Consecration*, *he can subdelegate this
action, namely the laying of the foundation stone, to another.* Hooker covers
the other rites of dedication with silence.

[Marginalia: Prax. episc. part. 1. c. 5.]

Granted, then: let no crosses be painted on the walls, no lamps be lit before
crosses, no door be struck and opened, no alphabet be traced on the pavement
with the pastoral staff, no mixture of water, wine, salt, and ashes be sprinkled
on the temple, and let it not be anointed with chrism. Nevertheless, many other
follies and superstitious rites remain. But leaving particulars aside, since, as
I said, I have nowhere found the Anglican office of dedication described, let
us see what this dedication and consecration of churches and cemeteries by the
word and thanksgiving means.

[1623 printed p. 339 | IA leaf 351 | PDF p. 351 | split `Nul-` / `lum` joined]

We have no command in the New Testament either to determine a fixed and certain
place in each parish for ecclesiastical assemblies or to erect buildings. It is
lawful for the faithful to gather for public worship wherever they conveniently
can, and even to hold assemblies in the open air if they please. For
convenience, however, it was expedient that they frequent a fixed place, lest
places of assembly be multiplied without any necessity or rendered uncertain
to those outside the church who are to be drawn into its bosom. And although
assemblies can sometimes be held in the open air, that cannot always be done
conveniently; buildings therefore had to be erected to ward off injuries of air
and weather. Thus, with necessity demanding and convenience counseling,
buildings have been and should be erected for a civil use: to ward off injuries
of the weather from the congregation and provide convenient seats, not to
worship God by means of temples and buildings. Suarez says that a sacred place
can be regarded in two ways: either as a place dedicated to contain God's people
assembled for divine worship, and thus possessing a peculiar character of
sanctity; or as a thing consecrated and appointed for divine worship, in which
respect it agrees with other sacred things such as vessels and vestments.

[Marginalia: De relig. tom. 1. lib. 3.]

In the former respect it has no sanctity; in the latter it has a superstitious
sanctity. If those buildings were called κυριακὰ by the ancients in this sense,
I would not approve. The Jerusalem temple was a part of divine worship; temples
in the Christian church are not. Otherwise Christ would have instituted the
erection of temples for this end, for he desires no worship other than what he
prescribes for us. Ecclesiastical buildings are adjuncts to spiritual things—to
the preaching of the word, celebration of the sacraments, prayer, and other
public exercises of piety—just as other ecclesiastical goods are. Material
buildings are joined to spiritual things to protect the assembly of the
faithful from injury of the air and allow them to attend conveniently to divine
worship and spiritual exercises. The bond between those buildings and divine
worship is no closer than that between other ecclesiastical goods and the same
worship. For those buildings can be separated from public worship; indeed,
Christians have often abandoned splendid buildings so as to retain pure public
worship, though divine worship can scarcely be maintained without other
ecclesiastical goods. Wherever an assembly of the godly gathers, Christ promises
his presence, whether in a consecrated building, a wilderness, or a mountain.
*Primarily and in itself, he promises his presence to us, as to his temples in
every place; to the places in which we assemble, whether open sites or temples,
he promises it accidentally and secondarily on our account—or rather he does
not promise it to them but to us in them.*

[Marginalia: Jun. de cultu sanct. cap. 4. not. 35.]

And indeed I do not know whether any place should be called holy even when the
church is present. The Jerusalem temple was holy because of the type, because
of God's visible presence, and because it was dedicated immediately to God
himself, and was

[1623 printed p. 340 | IA leaf 352 | PDF p. 352 | split `prop-` / `terea` joined]

therefore holy even outside the time of divine worship. The synagogues of the
Jews and our material temples are not so. However that may be, why, I ask, are
they dedicated by the word and prayers? I now omit the other follies and
theatrical rites. Is it not so that they may be separated from every profane use
and devoted solely to sacred actions? For a blessing is twofold, Suarez says.
One is constitutive of a sacred thing: it produces a permanent effect and
constitutes a thing permanently sacred, as in the consecration of a church or
chalice and the blessing of water or a vestment; things constituted sacred by a
blessing are called permanent sacramentals. Another blessing is invocative or
private, such as a blessing of the table, a father's blessing, or one an old man
gives a young man. *There are yet other blessings,* he says, *which, although
they do not properly consecrate a thing or separate it from common and profane
uses, are nevertheless performed with a special rite by the ministers of the
church and by its special institution, so that they may have a special effect,
at least by way of supplication in the name of the whole church; and therefore
they are rightly numbered among the sacramentals. Such are the blessings of
marriages, likewise the blessing of a ship or house or certain foods, as they
appear in the Missal or Pontifical.*

[Marginalia: Suarez. de virtute & statu religionis tom. 1. lib. 4. cap. 14.
sect. 1. 2.]

He calls these transient sacramentals. The blessing in the consecration of a
church is constitutive of a sacred thing, as in the consecration of an altar,
chalice, paten, and the like. I do not now distinguish, in pontifical fashion,
either a verbal blessing or one with blessed water from consecration by
anointing, since according to our adversaries a simple blessing, even without
that mixture of water, the sign of the cross, and anointing with chrism, has the
same power as these: namely, to constitute something in sacred being. And by the
reason by which they thus consecrate churches, they ought also to consecrate the
Eucharistic cup, the Eucharistic table, and similar things. Certainly, when
Christ celebrated the supper, he consecrated the bread and wine, not the
material table, cloths, chalices, dishes, and walls. Indeed, he did not
consecrate the bread and wine to remain sacred things outside their use, but for
the present use. They say they consecrate temples by the word and prayers; but
where is the prayer of faith, where Christ's promise, where the word of
institution? Whence, then, comes the authority to constitute sacred things and
permanent sacramentals—temples, altars, chalices, patens, and the like? To
assign movable and immovable goods to the church is pious and praiseworthy, but
to consecrate them so that it is a sin to use them for lawful but profane
purposes when the church is not using them, as though the church, table,
chalice, and the like would be polluted by those uses, is execrable
superstition. And surely this is the power of their blessing and consecration:
to constitute those things in sacred being. The Lord did not promise that power
to our prayers and supplications except in consecrating the things he instituted
to be consecrated, such as bread and wine in the Eucharist and water in baptism.
He did not institute the consecration of walls, chalices, patens, and tables. By
the same reasoning, they can bestow the same labor on fly-whisks, that is, fans
provided to

[1623 printed p. 341 | IA leaf 353 | PDF p. 353 | split `even-` / `tilandas` joined]

waft flies away from the wine in the cup. For, as you see, they are appointed to
drive flies away from wine. Nevertheless, they think such power inheres in
their consecrations that if anyone treats a profane though lawful matter in a
consecrated building, he has profaned a holy and sacred thing. In 1 Corinthians
11, the word *church* properly and simply denotes the assembly but connotes its
place, which happened to be in private houses. In the 1604 ecclesiastical
constitutions of the province of Canterbury, in the canon *That the reverence
of churches is not to be polluted by profane uses*, they decreed that churchwardens
and questmen should not permit *secular courts, views of frankpledge or leets,
lay juries, musters of soldiers, or any profane use or rite to be held or
celebrated in their churches, chapels, or cemeteries*;

[Marginalia: Can. 88.]

as if temples and cemeteries were polluted by these courts and other lawful
matters. *Such is the religious character and reverence of churches that not
only unlawful things are prohibited in churches, but also any profane things
whatsoever, even if lawfully practiced elsewhere,* Durantus says. *Hence
judgments and arbitrations were forbidden to be exercised in churches by the
Council of Lyon.*

[Marginalia: De ritibus Ecclesiae cathol. li. 1. cap. 26. num. 7.]

They misuse Christ's words, *My house is a house of prayer*, which were spoken
of the Jerusalem temple. Christ did not allow anyone to carry a vessel through
the temple, Mark 11:16, something I hear is also done in England, especially
through cathedral basilicas. But no argument can be drawn from the Jerusalem
temple to defend the dedication, sacred character, and immunity of our
basilicas, as we said above. A church is not polluted by courts or arbitrations.
One and the same building may indifferently serve divine worship and secular
courts, provided the exercise of divine worship, to which the church building
is primarily assigned, is not hindered. One and the same cup may indifferently
serve common and sacred drinking, yet it is not polluted, nor is any
deconsecration, as they call it, needed for this to be done lawfully. For the
church's vessels are not sacred as the vessels of the Jerusalem temple were,
which were carried to Babylon and profaned at Belshazzar's feast. As that temple
was holy, so its vessels were holy. Those who defend their consecrations and
immunities by the example of the Jerusalem temple ought also to condemn
sacraments celebrated outside churches, for the Lord willed sacrifices to be
offered nowhere but in the temple. The consecration of a temple is useless if
someone can consecrate the Eucharistic bread outside the consecrated temple,
just as consecration of the Jerusalem temple would have been useless if it had
been lawful to offer sacrifices everywhere outside it. Is the Eucharist
celebrated in a consecrated building holier than one celebrated outside, than
the one celebrated by Christ, or than one administered by Athanasius in a
church not yet dedicated, *Apology* 2 to Constantius? Yet the Englishman Hooker
says that *consecrated churches seem in a certain manner to mourn, as if defrauded
of their right, when*

[Marginalia: De politica lib. 5. sect. 16.]

[1623 printed p. 342 | IA leaf 354 | PDF p. 354]

*other unconsecrated places are preferred to them for the exercise of public
worship without any necessity; that divine worship does not possess the
perfection of grace and beauty it would have if the dignity of the place it
desires concurred with it; that the majesty and sanctity of a place possesses
great power and efficacy with respect to us and serves as a perceptible aid to
stir devotion; and for this reason it makes even our most holy and excellent
actions more excellent.*

[Marginalia: De cultu sanctor. c. 4.]

Does he not say the same things as the papists? *Everything in a church is
fitted to stir devotion, which is especially necessary for prayer. For altars,
crosses, images, relics, and even the walls themselves dedicated to God move us
to piety, things certainly not found elsewhere,* Bellarmine says. From this he
infers, *Christian churches are rightly instituted for prayer, even private
prayer.* The same certainly follows from Hooker's words. Temples must therefore
be frequented for the sake of private prayer, because the majesty and sanctity
of the place stir devotion, and the very walls dedicated to God move one to
piety. It is true that everything in ecclesiastical assemblies must be done in
order and decently. But cannot the sacraments be administered and the word
preached in order and decently outside a consecrated place, in other equally
convenient places where nothing is lacking but consecrated walls? Consecrated
walls may perhaps move a superstitious man; they cannot stir true devotion. For
the Lord did not command the consecration of walls and places, and he neither
blesses the inventions of our brain nor uses them as means to stir or increase
devotion. *Why, when about to supplicate God, do you seek a holy place? Desiring
to pray in a temple, pray within yourself, and always act so that you may be
God's temple; for God hears where he dwells,* Augustine says.

[Marginalia: Lib. sententiarum.]

God's presence in temples is no more sacred or commendable to us than elsewhere.
When Christ addresses private prayers, he instructs the one about to pray to
*enter εἰς τὸ ταμεῖον*, into his room, and pray *ἐν τῷ κρυπτῷ*, in secret; he
does not send him off to consecrated walls. And there was greater fervor in
public assemblies when they had no consecrated churches. We approve dedication,
that is, the assignment of a place to the church's use by deeds of gift or other
means by which ownership is transferred or things become public property.
Suarez says an oratory can be destined for sacred ministries by simple intent,
vol. 3, disputation 81, section 2. Why not also a church? From the consecration
of churches arose their immunities and liberties, deconsecrations,
reconciliations, reconsecrations, and other absurdities, all of which collapse
of themselves when that principle of consecrations is removed.

When the consecration of churches is removed, the blessing and dedication of
cemeteries is likewise removed. For if temples are not to be consecrated, much
less are cemeteries. The use of graves is a civil use for receiving the bodies
of the dead. Jews had graves on their estates, Judges 2:9, Joshua 24:30, 32,
Genesis 23:19. If anyone calls a burial place holy in the sense in which we call
the laws and rights of

[1623 printed p. 343 | IA leaf 355 | PDF p. 355 | split `ma-` / `gistratuum` joined]

magistrates, city walls and gates, the ramparts of fortifications and citadels,
watchmen's towers, and ambassadors holy and inviolable—namely, because these
things are not to be violated but are secured and defended from injury by the
magistrate's authority, the law's sanction, and the imposition of punishment—we
do not object. But there will be no need here for a constitutive blessing of
holy ground, as if that earth were holier than another for receiving the bodies
of the dead. For the place into which a dead man is put seems naturally to
belong to him: *the place in which a body is buried becomes religious*, Code,
book 3, title 43, law 2. Thus the corpse's presence itself makes the place
inviolable and religious in the stated sense without any pontifical blessing.
Therefore one who violates or destroys a grave, inhabits it, transfers the
bodies of the dead to another place without permission, carries away stones,
marble, or other ornaments, or handles the remains of the dead should be
punished. But not to permit musters, secular courts, and lay juries in cemeteries,
as we saw above in the Anglican canon, as though some holy place were polluted
and profaned by them, is no slight pollution of superstition. Corpses rest in
underground places in that invisible world and are no more polluted by business,
judgments, and arbitrations than by walking. They have descended to the lower
parts of the earth, leaving the upper parts to the living for lawful uses.
Commenting on the passage in Justinian's *Institutes*, *Each person makes a
place religious by his own will when he brings a dead man into his own place*,
Mynsingerus says: *From this text it should therefore be noted that the place
into which the body of a dead man, either the whole or its principal part, that
is, the head, has been brought is called religious. For although something
sacred, such as a sacred building, was established only by public authority,
each person was once permitted to make a place religious by private authority.
Today it is otherwise because of the pontifical law issued concerning the
consecration of cemeteries, by which a place does not become religious except
through the pontiff's consecration.*

[Marginalia: Lib. 2. tit. 1. sect. religiosum.]

You see, therefore, that the dedication of graves was introduced solely by
pontifical law, whereas the consecration of sacred buildings was introduced
partly by the κακοζηλία of Judaism and imitation of the dedication of that one
typical temple, namely the Jerusalem temple, and partly by imitation of paganism.
*For in former times a consecration of this kind was performed by the consul at
the people's command; but when rule was transferred to the prince, it was done
by Caesar himself or, on his authority, by the pontiffs, who summoned an
assembly and, employing a certain special rite, lit a brazier with their heads
veiled, over which they recited a formula, and with a piper leading, pronounced
certain set words. This was done so formally and religiously that they did not
miss even a word or syllable. Today among us the consecration of sacred things
and places is performed only by the chief ministers of our religion and bishops,
with certain solemn words first spoken and a special rite which pontifical law
prescribes.*

[Marginalia: Mynsing. ib. Parag. Sacrae res.]

Justinian distinguishes sacred things from religious and holy ones as they were
distinguished in civil law, and calls

[1623 printed p. 344 | IA leaf 356 | PDF p. 356 | split `se-` / `pulchrorum` joined]

the place of graves properly religious. But that place has no proper religious
character other than that of other things: it is inviolable, as we said above
of the things called holy in civil law. *It is evident from Cicero, book 2 of
On the Laws, that places into which a dead man had been brought might neither
be plowed nor cultivated. Thus religion is thought by Servius Sulpicius and
Massurius Sabinus to be named from leaving behind, for in plowing that burial
place was left behind. Hence, if the pontiffs decreed a place pure, they ordered
it to be plowed up.*

[Marginalia: Calvin in lexico voce Religiosus.]

A pure place was one regarded as neither sacred, holy, nor religious. We judge
it inhuman to plow cemeteries or violate graves; but to grant cemeteries, and
even temples, immunities from secular courts belongs to the class of
pseudo-apostolic precepts which Paul condemns in Colossians 2:21: *Do not eat,
do not taste, do not touch.* The consecration of temples somewhat increased the
religious character of the neighboring cemetery, so that to be buried outside
the cemetery was regarded as no slight danger to the soul. Hence some chose a
burial place in the temple rather than the cemetery, others in the chancel
rather than the nave; and the nearer the high altar, the more blessed they
judged themselves. Yet it would have been more useful for cemeteries to be
separated from temples, removing every occasion of superstition, and, if it
could conveniently be done, for fixed places in the fields outside cities and
towns to be designated and assigned for this use, to avoid the danger of
unhealthier air.

[Marginalia: Lege Lycurgi.]

*By the decemviral law, however, it was lawful to bury the dead only outside the
city. For the Twelve Tables and other laws forbade making a grave in the city or
a public place, and by a rescript of the divine Hadrian a penalty of forty
solidi was appointed for transgressors,* Mynsingerus says in the place cited
above. *When Christ approached the gate of the city called Nain, behold, a dead
man was being carried out*, Luke 7:12. Chrysostom says in a sermon *On Law and
Faith* that every city and town has graves before its entrance. Plutarch reports
that Lycurgus made a law that the bodies of the dead should be buried in cities
and around temples. Perhaps this custom flowed from Lycurgus into the church.
We have many things concerning the dedication and blessing of cemeteries in the
Roman Pontifical. It does not assert that the blessing of a cemetery is
necessary, but only that it is not superfluous. *Although most assert that the
blessing of a cemetery is unnecessary, saying that the circuits outside the
church and sprinklings of the cemetery which occur when the church is dedicated
suffice, it is nevertheless not superfluous for a blessing of this kind to be
performed.*

[Marginalia: Folio 147.]

The reader can learn from the same Pontifical the rites the papists use in
blessing a cemetery.

There is scarcely any dispute between us and our adversaries about the minister
of the consecration or dedication of churches and cemeteries. For they
themselves admit that this consecration was reserved to the bishop solely by the
church's institution and more for the honor of the priesthood than

[1623 printed p. 345 | IA leaf 357 | PDF p. 357]

from necessity of law. Certainly, if these consecrations were to be admitted, a
presbyter could consecrate no less than a bishop. For one who can consecrate
bread and wine in the Eucharist can consecrate vessels and temples by the power
of the presbyteral order. We heard above from Leo, Isidore, and Duarenus that
this consecration was reserved to the bishop by ecclesiastical rules and the
church's institution. Azorius also says that *by the Roman pontiff's authority
the consecration of a church can be committed to a simple priest.*

[Marginalia: Instit. moral. tom. 1. lib. 10. cap. 25.]

There is no need for more in so evident and insignificant a matter. If someone
asks who shall represent the church when ownership of the site and building is
transferred by a solemn legal formula, that is another matter. I answer: anyone
whom the assembly of the faithful chooses for this end, whether bishop,
presbyter, deacon, someone from the people, or the consistory. For the community
of the faithful is owner of the building and adjoining cemetery, not any lord
bishop or presbyter.

I would proceed to the following matters if our adversaries did not also approve
the costly ornament of temples and their Jewish form. Hooker defends the
ornament of temples by the example of the Jerusalem temple.

[Marginalia: De polit. l. 5. sect. 15.]

Likewise Bellarmine and the other papists infer from the most magnificent
construction of the temple and tabernacle that those insane structures of
temples are works pious in their own kind. But *there was a command of God for
each: for the magnificence of the tabernacle a legal and extraordinary command,
and for the magnificence of the temple a special command by analogy with the
law, given extraordinarily through his prophets. This magnificence belonged to
a legal and figurative, or typical, institution, by which the outward ornament
of the tabernacle and temple in the Old Testament represented the inward glory
of God and Christ in the church, and the church in Christ. Therefore the
argument from unlike things does not follow.*

[Marginalia: Iunius de cultu sanct. cap. 6. not. 4.]

The construction of synagogues did not compete in magnificence with the temple.
Hooker next adduces examples of the ancients, who built magnificent temples once
peace was granted to the church. Not all the ancients' κακοζηλία are to be
approved; the more prudent among the ancients did not approve them. We do not
condemn cleanliness, simple and modest ornament, or a size suitable for each
assembly, but excess and redundancy of ornament. Contrary to Hooker's claim
above, that ornament does not impart majesty to divine worship. *The sacraments
do not seek gold, nor do things not bought with gold please by gold,* Ambrose
says. See Gratian.

[Marginalia: Caus. 12. qu. 2. cap. 70.]

Persius, though a pagan, says, *Tell me, pontiffs, what gold does in a sacred
place. Holiness befits God's house,* Bernard says, *which delights not so much
in polished marbles as in adorned conduct, and loves pure minds more than
gilded walls.*

[Marginalia: Bern. ad milites templi cap. 5.]

The same author says in his *Apology to Abbot William*: *I pass over the immense
heights of oratories, their immoderate lengths, superfluous widths, costly
polishing, and elaborate pictures, which, while turning the gaze of those
praying upon themselves, hinder their devotion also, and somehow represent to
me the ancient*

[1623 printed p. 346 | IA leaf 358 | PDF p. 358]

*Jewish rite. Many,* Jerome says, *build walls and erect church columns; marbles
gleam, ceilings shine with gold, the altar is distinguished with gems, and no
selection is made of Christ's ministers. Let no one object to me that the temple
in Judea was rich, with its table, lamps, censers, dishes, cups, mortars, and
other things made from gold. These were approved by the Lord when priests
sacrificed victims and the blood of livestock was the redemption of sins. But
now, when the poor Lord has dedicated the poverty of his house, let us think on
his cross and regard riches as mud.*

[Marginalia: Epi. ad Nepotian.]

*An evil love of walls has seized you,* Hilary says; *you wrongly venerate God's
church in roofs and buildings and wrongly introduce the name of peace under
them. Is there any doubt that Antichrist will sit in these? Mountains and
forests, lakes, prisons, and chasms are safer for me. For in these the prophets,
either remaining or submerged, prophesied by God's Spirit.*

[Marginalia: Contra Auxentium.]

Once priests were golden when chalices were wooden; now, on the contrary,
priests are wooden when chalices are golden. Bellarmine says magnificent temples
are a great splendor to cities. Granted, but such magnificence neither befits
the church nor is useful. As was said, the sacraments do not seek that ornament.
Moreover, the poor, who are living temples and lack not merely ornament but
necessities, must not be defrauded; and we shall always have the poor. *O vanity
of vanities, no more vain than insane! The church gleams in its walls and is in
need among its poor; it clothes its stones in gold and abandons its children
naked.*

[Marginalia: Bern. Apol. ad Guilielm. Abbat.]

Palaces, praetoria, and other public places can be a splendor to cities through
magnificence and ornament; and because of human weakness these outward means
are needed to protect the dignity of secular powers and cities. As for the
church, simple and modest ornament of buildings, support of the ministers who
are golden priests, supply of things needed to advance divine worship, and
clothing of the poor are the church's ornament and splendor, not the insane
construction of temples.

As to form, the papists say churches consist of three parts in imitation of
Solomon's temple, in which were the court, the holy place, and the holy of
holies. The first part is the porch before the temple entrance, the second the
nave, and the third the sacrarium or sanctuary, the inner part of the temple
where the altar stood. It was divided from the rest of the temple by steps,
screens, and veils or curtains, and none entered it but the priests with their
clerical ministers.

[Marginalia: Bellarm. de cult. sanct. cap. 3.]

Hooker answers: Granted; and why can churches not be built in the likeness of
Solomon's temple, just as royal palaces can be built in the likeness of
Solomon's palace?

[Marginalia: De Polit. l. 5. sect. 14.]

I answer that the reasoning is not parallel: there is not the same liberty in
ecclesiastical matters as in civil ones. The divisions of the Jerusalem temple
were patterns and types of heavenly things. Two things must be avoided in the
form of material churches. One is superstition, lest a shadow of the shadows of
the Old Testament seem still to remain;

[1623 printed p. 347 | IA leaf 359 | PDF p. 359]

the other is hindrance to the people's edification, as happens through those
divisions in papist churches, where the people can scarcely hear the priest's
words uttered from the sanctuary. The same thing also happens among the
Anglicans while they attend to liturgical matters, as we shall show below.
Bellarmine says Christian churches were generally turned toward the east
because the Jews prayed and pray toward the west, so that we might signify that
theirs is the letter which kills, while ours is the spirit which gives life.

[Marginalia: De cultu sanct. c. 3.]

But pagans, on the contrary, prayed toward the east; and wherever the Jews were,
they looked toward the temple, whether that was toward the east or the west.
Neither should we turn toward the east in prayer in the manner of idolaters,
Ezekiel 8:17, nor toward the west in the manner of the Jews—or rather toward
the temple or its site—but, as Augustine says, *When someone seeks prayer, let
him arrange his limbs as it occurs to him.*

[Marginalia: Ad Simplicianum lib. 2. quaest. 4.]

But grant that they prayed toward the east with this consideration alone—which
is false, for Bellarmine provides other reasons—it follows that we should not
Judaize in the form and divisions of the temple either. The ancients refused to
celebrate Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, but celebrated it on the
Lord's Day nearest the fourteenth day after the spring equinox, lest they seem
to Judaize; those who did otherwise were called Quartodecimans. Epiphanius
reports that a certain most wicked Jewish false prophet named El Xai (or, as
Scaliger more correctly says in the *Elenchus Trihaeresii*, chapter 27, a certain
Essene who was so called in Arabic with the article *El* added, κατ’ ἐξοχὴν)
forbade *praying toward the east, asserting that one ought not to direct oneself
thus, but to turn one's face toward Jerusalem from every region, so that those
in the east direct themselves toward Jerusalem in the west.*

[Marginalia: Epiph. haeres. lib. 1.]

See Baronius's *Annals*. Epiphanius condemns this.

[Marginalia: Anno 105. num. 3.]

We approve every form suited to the people's edification by contingent decorum.
We approve no divisions that hinder the people's edification, least of all
those deliberately made in studied imitation of Solomon's temple, as the
papists do. They teach that temples were built principally for the sacrifice of
the Mass, as Solomon's temple was built for sacrifices offered to God, and thus
that the sacrificing priest must sacrifice in a holier and separate place.

3 To confirm children.

Mocket says: *To confirm by prayers and the laying on of hands children who have
been taught the Catechism.* Tilenus says: *Concerning the confirmation of the
baptized, I marvel at the quarrel instituted by your people, since Calvin
himself acknowledges that this rite was approved by purer antiquity, approves
it himself, and wishes it restored, Institutes, book 4, chapter 19, section 4.*
But he wishes rather than hopes that the simple use of the laying on of hands
will be restored. We approve the thing itself—namely, that children should be
instructed in catechetical doctrine before they are admitted to participation
in the Supper,

[Marginalia: De Polit. p. 319.]

[Marginalia: Paraenes. cap. 15.]

[1623 printed p. 348 | IA leaf 360 | PDF p. 360 | split `oran-` / `dum` joined]

and that prayer should be made for them, as we also do whenever they are
examined. But we judge the rite of laying on hands to be indifferent in its own
nature;
for what is it but a gesture of one praying, as we shall hear below? And since
this rite degenerated into the spurious sacrament of confirmation, the Reformed
churches rightly omitted it. We, however, resist its restoration, partly to
assert Christian liberty in indifferent rites, and partly to remove from our
church the Anglican sacrament of confirmation.

The laying on of hands is a very simple rite, common to the church under the Old
and New Testaments: namely, the gesture of one praying or blessing, by which the
person for whom prayers and blessings are poured out is indicated with a hand
laid upon him. Jacob laid his hands upon Joseph's sons when blessing them,
Genesis 48; Moses upon Joshua, Numbers 27. Hands were also laid upon those who
were enrolled among the senators of the Great Sanhedrin and those who were
promoted by the rabbis to the teaching office. Thus the use of this rite was
common in sacred and civil affairs: in patriarchal blessings and in the
inaugurations of senators, teachers, and leaders. In the New Testament we read
that Christ, when little children were brought to him that he might lay hands
upon them and pray, laid his hands upon them, Matthew 19:13, 15. The papists
themselves acknowledge that Christ did not institute the sacrament of
confirmation here. *For no promise, no command, and no designation of matter or
form is found there, without which the institution of a sacrament cannot exist.
Likewise, it is unlikely that Christ confirmed those children, because, as we
said with St Thomas, it was fitting that this sacrament should not be ministered
before Christ's glorification. Moreover, those children had not yet been
baptized, nor were they offered to Christ for this end, but that they might be
blessed by him, as Jerome says, or that they might be healed, as Anselm
indicates.*

[Marginalia: Suarez. in 3 part. Sum. disput. 32. sect. 2.]

Hands were laid on in the ordinations of ministers, 1 Timothy 4:14; likewise on
Paul and Barnabas when sent out by the church of Antioch, Acts 13:3; and on Paul
when his blindness was healed and he was filled with the Holy Spirit, Acts 9:17.
Hands were also laid on in healing the sick and infirm, Matthew 4:18; Mark 5:23;
Mark 16:18; Acts 28:8. The apostles also laid on hands while praying when
conferring the extraordinary gifts of the Holy Spirit, Acts 8:15, 17. The laying
on of hands, therefore, was a familiar gesture among the Jews whenever there was
some solemn prayer or blessing, especially where there was one particular person
to be blessed. For if the assembly was more numerous, hands were raised. *Then
Aaron, raising his hands toward the people, blessed them,* Leviticus 9:22. And
Christ blessed his disciples with his hands raised, Luke 24:50. Drusius observes
that when priests blessed the people, if they were in the city, they raised
their hands toward their shoulders. The ancients in the Christian church also
used this gesture in various ways: in ordaining ministers, admitting
catechumens, embracing converted heretics, and reconciling the fallen, as we
said above at p. 238.

[Marginalia: Commentat. in loca difficiliora pentateuchi pag. 370.]

[1623 printed p. 349 | IA leaf 361 | PDF p. 361]

Since this rite was used in such varied ways both in the Old and New Testaments
and among the ancients, and since it is nothing other than the gesture of one
praying, indicating the person for whom one prays in the church's name that
grace may be obtained—not signifying, much less sealing and exhibiting, the
grace that the one praying or blessing seeks—it cannot properly be called a
sacrament. There is no analogy between that gesture and the grace the church
seeks for a person who is baptized, reconciled, ordained, or, when an adult,
admitted to the sacrament of the Eucharist. Even if we could devise some
analogy, Christ's institution and promise would still be lacking, and it would
seal nothing. For it belongs to Christ alone, who has the power of excellence,
to institute sacraments. But, as I said, this rite was used to indicate the
particular person for whom we seek grace, not to signify that grace itself and
seal it.

The papists devised sensible matter for this sacrament: chrism composed of olive
oil and balsam as its remote matter, and an anointing made from chrism in the
figure or sign of the cross as its proximate matter. Nor do the papists
recognize any laying on of hands as essential to this sacrament of confirmation
except what is intrinsically included in the anointing itself. *It must be
said,* Suarez states, *that no laying on of a hand distinct from the anointing
with chrism performed by the bishop's hand belongs to the essence of this
sacrament; but that the anointing itself is essential, and that both points are
sufficiently established from what has been said and from the church's practice.
Nor does the apostolic act in Acts 8 stand against this truth, because it was not
according to the law and institution of the sacraments, but apart from it by a
special omission or dispensation. Consequently, that sign of the simple laying
on of a hand was not the true sacrament of confirmation as regards substance,
though it was so as regards effect. Likewise, the simple laying on of hands used
by the apostles in Acts 8 and 9 was not in fact the sacrament of confirmation
that we now have, because it differed greatly in its sensible rite; indeed, it
was not a sacrament simply speaking, because it was not a ceremony instituted
by a firm and stable law.*

[Marginalia: Swarez. tom. 3. disput. 33. sect. 4.]

Although Bellarmine says in chapter 2 on confirmation that the laying on of hands
has not been rejected—*For the bishop twice lays hands on those being confirmed,
as can be understood from the Roman Pontifical: once when he extends his hands
over them and prays, and again when he signs and anoints them on the forehead;
for the anointing and signing itself, since it is done by the hand, is most
properly called a laying on of the hand*—nevertheless, in chapter 9 he says that
*the anointing with chrism and the laying on of the hand are the same thing, for
he who anoints lays on his hand, and therefore each is signified even if only one
seems to be expressed. For it is certain that the apostles used chrism in
confirmation.* Spalatensis answers Bellarmine's first passage thus: *But that
extension of the hands is very improperly called a laying on; and if it is a
laying on, it is nevertheless not sacramental but purely deprecatory. Nor has
anyone among us placed the power*

[Marginalia: De republ. l. 5. ca. 5. n. 29.]

[1623 printed p. 350 | IA leaf 362 | PDF p. 362]

*of the sacrament in it. Therefore that extension or laying on is not the
proximate matter of the sacrament. But to say that merely touching the forehead
with the thumb is to lay on a hand
is excessively forced and violent, since a hand laid on is always understood as
spread out and applied from above. But the pontiff merely touches the front of
the person being confirmed with one finger, and he does this not to lay a hand
upon him but to imprint a cross.* To the later passage Spalatensis replies: *A
marvelous exposition—or distortion—of Holy Scripture indeed! “They laid on
hands”; that is, “they anointed with ointment.” I ask which of two actions
contributing to one effect—namely, the application of the hand and the anointing
performed by such application through the hand—is properly the principal and
sacramental action? Surely, if the hand is applied not for its own sake but for
the sake of anointing, then the hand's application will be instrumental, while
the anointing will be the principal action and principally intended. Luke, then,
absurdly expressed only the instrumental action when he ought to have expressed
the principal action to which the effect was due, namely the anointing; and he
expressed one that was altogether general, uncertain, and indefinite, because
many things can be done to a person by a hand: he can be washed, anointed,
struck, painted, and so forth by another's hand. Since, therefore, Luke expressed
only the laying on of the hand, he certainly expressed it as the principal
action intended in itself, not as an instrumental one.*

[Marginalia: Num. 30.]

It is clear from Cyprian and Tertullian that a proper laying on of the hand was
used in addition to that action of the hand included in anointing with chrism.
*The flesh is anointed,* Tertullian says, *that the soul may be consecrated; the
flesh is signed that the soul may be fortified; the flesh is overshadowed by
the laying on of the hand that the soul may be illuminated by the Spirit.* And
in his book on baptism: *When we have come out of the laver, we are anointed with
a blessed anointing*; and a little later: *Then the hand is laid on, through the
blessing calling and inviting the Holy Spirit.*

[Marginalia: Lib. de resur. carnis.]

Cyprian says in his letter to Jubaianus on baptizing heretics: *This is also what
is now done among us, so that those baptized in the church may be offered to the
church through the presidents*—Bellarmine reads “through the presidents,” not
“to the presidents,” in chapter 6 on confirmation—*and through our prayer and
the laying on of the hand may obtain the Holy Spirit and be consummated with the
Lord's seal.* Similar statements about the laying on of hands occur everywhere
among the fathers. Hence some call it the laying on of hands, while Eusebius of
Emesa—or whoever was the author—calls it the Sacrament of the Laying On of Hands
in the sermon on Pentecost. *Anointing with chrism was joined to baptism in much
the same way as the cup of the Lord's blood was joined to the sacrament of the
Lord's body,* Cassander says in his *Liturgics*. *And just as those baptized were
said to be confirmed with chrism by the bishop, so those who had received the
Lord's body were said to be confirmed with the cup of the Lord's blood by the
bishop, presbyter, or deacon.* He proves this from the Book of the Roman Order.
Thus the word *confirming* was common to both actions, that is, to anointing with
chrism and *the distribution of the blood. Although today the name confirmation
is applied only to this action in which the baptized are anointed with chrism.*

[Marginalia: Pag. 218.]

Spalatensis teaches that in the ancient church, for many centuries, the ceremony
of confirming or signing was

[1623 printed p. 351 | IA leaf 363 | PDF p. 363 | split `ceremoni-` / `alem` joined]

a ceremonial rite of baptism, not a distinct sacrament in itself. He
demonstrates that this is evident from
the statements of Clement in the *Constitutions*, Dionysius the Areopagite,
Tertullian, Cyprian, the Council of Elvira, Optatus of Milevis, the author of the
questions attributed to Justin, Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine, Theodoret, Severus
the patriarch of Alexandria, John of Damascus, Alcuin, and Rabanus. From these,
and from Melchiades and Fabian, he draws the following conclusions. *Confirmation,
therefore, is not an absolute term signifying one sacrament in itself but a
relative one, so that anointing with chrism is the confirmation of baptism, that
is, the final ceremony and last rite by which the whole rite of baptism is
completed and consummated.* From Tertullian: *Therefore both the anointings and
the layings on of hands for invoking the Holy Spirit belonged to the very
religion of baptism.* From Cyprian: *The laying on of the hand was a ceremonial
part of true ecclesiastical baptism and its final rite, by which the sacrament
of baptism was consummated and received its final completion.* From Jerome
against the Luciferians: *Jerome further intimates and indeed explains that
confirmation through the laying on of the bishop's hand is indeed a ceremony
belonging to baptism, yet in such a way that it is neither necessary nor
accomplishes anything spiritual by itself. Rather, what is customarily
attributed to it by some—that it gives the Holy Spirit—is possessed most
perfectly in baptism alone; nevertheless it is employed by ecclesiastical law
solely for a certain outward honor of episcopal dignity.* From Augustine:
*Confirmation is a ceremonial and ecclesiastical part of baptism, because the
church anoints the baptized.* From Alcuin: *This signing, therefore, is not the
sacrament of confirmation but the confirmation of the sacrament, namely baptism,
and consequently, according to Alcuin, it too belongs to baptism.* Then in
general: *And from all these testimonies let the first argument by which I
excluded confirmation from the number of sacraments properly so called remain
confirmed, because it was not instituted by Christ but by the church to complete
the baptismal rites.*

[Marginalia: De republ. lib. 5. cap. 5. num. 10. & sequ. ad 26.]

[Marginalia: Num. 25.]

We heard above from Tertullian that those who had just come out of the laver were
anointed and that hands were then laid upon them. Thus anointing and the laying
on of hands concluded the administration of baptism. Until the time of Sylvester
there were not two anointings after baptism, one on the crown by the presbyter
and another on the forehead by the bishop, but one. Before Sylvester's time,
bishops could easily sign the baptized with chrism so long as their dioceses were
not spread far and wide but enclosed within narrow limits, and so long as the
custom endured of celebrating baptism at the fixed seasons of Easter and
Pentecost and in one and the same place, namely the city. But when the dioceses
extended more widely, that custom began to fall out of use, and infants were not
baptized in one place such as the city, the bishop—who had now acquired for
himself, for the honor of the priesthood and not from any necessity of law, this
prerogative of anointing with chrism and laying on hands—could not hurry out to
each person baptized in the villages and country settlements. Yet lest the
baptized should be wholly deprived of chrism and

[1623 printed p. 352 | IA leaf 364 | PDF p. 364]

depart this life before the
bishop's arrival, it was instituted that the chrism with which the baptized are
anointed on the crown by the presbyter should be used in every ordinary baptism,
while the anointing with chrism on the forehead was nevertheless reserved to the
bishop. He did not, however, defer it until the seventh or tenth year of the
baptized person's age, as is now done among the papists, but administered it as
soon as a suitable opportunity was given.

Augustine says: *When we laid our hands upon these infants, each of you watched
to see whether they would speak with tongues.*

[Marginalia: Tractat. 6. in 1 Canonicâ Iohan.]

And elsewhere he says that when little children are confirmed, those who present
them answer for them just as in baptism.

[Marginalia: De Eccles. dogmat. cap. 52.]

You see that the bishop did not wait until they could recite for themselves, in
the manner of parrots, a few heads of a short catechism. *They attribute to
Sylvester this institution, that the baptized should be anointed on the crown
with chrism by the presbyter,* Cassander says. Bede, after mentioning two
anointings, one before baptism and the other immediately after it, says: *It must
be known that the anointing performed by the laying on of hands by bishops—as if
it were different from the two already mentioned and is commonly called
confirmation—is the same as the second. Yet because of arrogance it has not been
granted to individual priests, just as many other things have not.*

[Marginalia: Tom. 8. in Psal. 26.]

Thus there was formerly one anointing after baptism, *and not separate
anointings with chrism on the crown and with chrism on the forehead,* Spalatensis
says. But because of the arrogance of the bishop, who formerly used to anoint
and did not wish to lose that prerogative granted to him, and yet lest the
baptized should depart this life altogether without chrism, a double anointing
with chrism after baptism was devised. Of these, the latter was reserved to the
bishop because of arrogance, just as many other things are reserved because of
arrogance, Bede says.

[Marginalia: Lib. 5. ca. 5. num. 31.]

*Before Tertullian's time I find no authentic remembrance of chrismations, and
ecclesiastical writers before Tertullian said nothing about the anointing of
confirmation. Justin especially, at the end of his second Apology, recounts all
the rites of baptism and the Eucharist exactly but says not a word about the
consecration.* Spalatensis, number 31. Justin mentions neither anointing nor the
laying on of hands, although he then professes that he will omit nothing, *lest,*
he says, *we seem to act with any malice or concealment in this account.* This,
then, seems to have been the origin and beginning of confirmation. Those
concluding the administration of baptism prayed for the baptized person, just as
we in Reformed churches immediately after the sprinkling itself frame a short
prayer for the baptized person: namely, that the Lord would protect him; that
the grace sealed in baptism would display itself when he reached a more mature
age and would increase daily to his final breath; and similar things. As they
prayed in this way to conclude baptism, they laid on hands. Chrism and the sign
of the cross were added. We retain the thing itself, namely the short prayer for
the baptized person as described, but we do not employ the rite of laying on
hands, nor the chrism and sign of the cross that were added, because from them
arose the error that the Holy Spirit is not given in baptism itself except for
the

[1623 printed p. 353 | IA leaf 365 | PDF p. 365 | split `remissio-` / `nem` joined]

remission of sins. If the bare laying on of hands without chrism and the sign of the cross
had remained when they concluded the celebration of baptism—as a simple gesture
of one praying and blessing the baptized person, just as that gesture had been
variously used in personal blessings under the Old and New Laws and was employed
for other personal blessings too, as when converts were received or penitents
reconciled—that custom would have been tolerable. This and no other is the place
for laying on hands if the custom of the ancients is to be preserved, although
it was later torn away from baptism together with the anointing of the forehead
and was finally deferred until the seventh or tenth year, as is now done in
England, where they have rejected chrism, left the sign of the cross at the close
of baptism, and deferred the laying on of hands until the seventh or eighth year.

The Church of England does not observe the ancient custom of laying hands on
baptized infants, either the earlier form at the close of baptism or the later
form immediately after baptism at the first opportunity. But what if they say
that they imitate instead the example of the primitive apostolic church? The
laying on of hands mentioned in Hebrews 6 was used for those of more mature age
who were then to be admitted to the Holy Supper, received into the church of
adults, and undertook to subject themselves to ecclesiastical discipline. Some
interpret that passage of the extraordinary laying on of hands by which the
apostles conferred extraordinary gifts; others, of the ordaining or consecrating
laying on by which ministers are inaugurated to discharge the ministry; still
others, of the admission of catechumens into the church of adults—either adult
children of the faithful who had hitherto belonged to the church's covenant, or
converts from paganism or heresy. Both groups knew well the heads of catechetical
doctrine, such as faith toward God, repentance from dead works, the resurrection
of the dead, and eternal judgment. I now grant this third interpretation, yet I
say that it favors neither papist nor Anglican confirmation, but rather those
Reformed churches that approach more closely to the thing itself: they admit no
outsider into the church's bosom or to the Holy Supper without a solemn
profession of faith, examination, covenant engagement, and the church's prayers.
Where anything is lacking, we wish it restored, although they do not employ the
indifferent rite of laying on hands, which is free in its own nature—for it is
only the gesture of one praying—and has been so foully defiled in former and
present centuries, both by papists and by Anglicans, as we shall soon see. If it
were employed, we would nevertheless not call it confirmatory on account of the
Holy Spirit's confirming, but on account of the church's approving and, by its
approval, confirming the examined person and admitting him to that true and
twin sacrament of confirmation. Properly and primarily it would be the gesture
of one praying at the time of prayer, and consequently a gesture of the church
confirming and approving. Thus what the counterfeit Clement in

[1623 printed p. 354 | IA leaf 366 | PDF p. 366]

the *Apostolic Constitutions*, book 3, chapter 17, attributed to his
ointment—namely, that it is βεβαίωσις τῆς ὁμολογίας, the confirmation of the
profession, that is, of the profession of faith—we would attribute
to the rite of laying on hands insofar as it is the sign of the church confirming
and approving the catechumen's confession, not a sacramental power of confirming.
But because, as I said, the rite itself is indifferent and has been foully
defiled, it has rightly been omitted in Reformed churches, both at the close of
baptism, where the ancients used it in the time of Cyprian and Tertullian, and in
the admission of catechumens into the church of adults, when they are about to
become participants in the Holy Supper and subject to ecclesiastical discipline.
