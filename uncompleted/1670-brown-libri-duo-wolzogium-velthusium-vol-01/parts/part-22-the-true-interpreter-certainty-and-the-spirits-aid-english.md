XLI. After *Paradox* had indicated to us in § 2 of chapter 4 who is to be
regarded as a true Interpreter in general, in § 3 he describes for us the true
Interpreter of the sacred writings, namely, as one *[Who can draw out their
true meanings and show them to be such, and can detect and refute false ones.]*
And he adds: *[Thus his office is discharged and consists, as it were, of two
parts: namely, the investigation and confirmation of true Interpretations,
and the detection and refutation of false ones. It will certainly not have
been sufficient to have performed these things with some probability or
likelihood, as is commonly ob-*

[Page 168 | ONB canvas 222 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000222]

*tained in profane writings, but with unshaken certainty and impregnable
arguments, in which a soul desirous of solid truth can and ought fully and
completely to rest, etc.]* It cannot fail to occur to anyone that this can
easily be answered: namely, that this argument proves instead that the Holy
Spirit, or God, is the sole supreme and infallible Interpreter of the sacred
writings, because it belongs to him alone to satisfy and persuade the soul
desirous of solid truth concerning the true meaning of Scripture with
unshaken certainty and impregnable arguments. But if the discussion concerns
a ministerial and public interpreter, he cannot actually persuade another
(which *Wolzogen* could easily have said, since he himself had asserted it
above), although with the Spirit’s help he can search out the meaning
according to his measure and propose it to another. And if the discussion
concerns a subordinate and private interpreter, it is also the Holy Spirit
who persuades him of the truth and convinces him with unshaken certainty and
impregnable arguments, when and insofar as he is thus convinced. Philosophy
does not supply these arguments; rather, the Spirit speaking in and through
Scripture effects—indeed, effectually and efficaciously produces—this
reception of and persuasion concerning the truth in souls. But *Wolzogen*
wanders exceedingly far afield here and fills many pages, no fewer than
fourteen, with statements which for the most part (if I possess any judgment)
have nothing to do with the matter, and with others which are hardly sound or
Orthodox. It would take too long to cite the complete text and refute it with
proper replies. It will suffice to extract certain things and attach
observations, as follows.

1. He begins his reply in this manner on page 129: *[All these things can
indeed be approved if they are correctly understood; but if otherwise, they
will be discovered to be full of error.]* *Wolzogen* would certainly have done
something worthwhile if

[Page 169 | ONB canvas 223 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000223]

he had explained to us how those words could be correctly understood. But
perhaps everything that follows makes them plain. Let us therefore see. *[For
we admit*, he says, *that faith depends upon this question, because faith has
nothing to believe unless the Interpreter has taught it; but we deny that it
is itself a question of faith. For faith in Theological matters rests upon
divine authority alone, whereas our question about Interpretation is of a far
different nature. It touches faith only insofar as it indicates the things
which we must believe by faith. It has nothing of faith in itself. For God
does not speak here, so that because he affirms this to be the meaning of this
passage, we ought to believe it. If he did so through his Spirit, as the
patrons of the Enthusiasts contend, faith would certainly not be removed.]*
*Reply.* Was *Paradox* saying that this is a question of faith? On the
contrary, he asserts the opposite, as your senior *Velthuysen* witnesses in
*On the Use of Reason*, page 181. Was *Paradox* saying that God explains the
meaning of Scripture through the Spirit? Is *Paradox* an *Enthusiast*, or does he join
himself to the Enthusiasts? What, then, is the purpose of all this? What do
these things say against the *Exercitator*? What do they contribute to the
matter at hand? Is this what it is to refute the *Paradoxical Exercise*? You
will therefore ask, reader, why *Wolzogen* said these things. I will tell you
what I think: *Wolzogen* said them, of course, so that he might lash the
Reformed and overthrow the Reformed position. For *Paradox* says nothing
about the Spirit. He has no regard for the Spirit; he exalts Reason and wishes
Philosophy to be the sole norm for Interpreting the sacred writings, because
he thinks that the Interpreter thereby possesses unshaken certainty and
impregnable arguments in which a soul desirous of solid truth can and ought
fully and completely to rest. *Wolzogen* ought to have replied to this. But he
makes no reply whatsoever here because he had something else to do: namely,
he wished, if he could, to defeat the Reformed. Is this how you champion the
truth and defend the orthodox, *Wol-*

[Page 170 | ONB canvas 224 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000224]

*zogen*? Is this your faith? Is this your orthodox faith? Therefore, since
*Wolzogen* is not fighting here against his professed adversary—namely, the
*Paradoxical Exercitator*—but against the Reformed under the name of
*Enthusiasts*, it is our business to defend the teaching of the Reformed and
to examine somewhat more closely what *Wolzogen* says about this matter. Let
*Wolzogen* tell me: Does God not work effectually through his Spirit in the
hearts of his own both to will and to perform every good work? Is grasping the
true meaning of the Scriptures, or drawing truth from the Scriptures, not a
good work? Is persuasion not an act of faith? Must God not be said to speak
and to affirm that this is the meaning of this passage when through his Spirit
he leads the believer into all truth, and in particular demonstrates to him
this or that truth from these or those passages of Scripture and effectually
works this persuasion? Does he believe no one says this except the patrons of
the *Enthusiasts*? If not, how can the things he says here stand together? If
so, does he not openly desert all the Reformed and attack their position? What
does he say next? *[Now*, he says, *when he proposes to us only words and a
Discourse, which are then explained through laws of Interpretation, and those
laws are established by men, I do not see what need there is for faith in
order for me to judge according to human laws that this meaning agrees with
this discourse.]* *Reply.* If the Spirit of God did nothing other than what
*Wolzogen* reports here, there would be no need for faith. But what Reformed
writer says this? Namely, that God only proposes words and a Discourse to us,
which are explained through laws established by men? Does the Spirit perform
nothing more in Interpreting the Scriptures than in explaining *Curtius* or
*Livy*? Are there no other operations of the Spirit? *[And although*, he says,
*the Holy Spirit brings us some assistance in making this judgment, it is
nevertheless not of such a kind as to be able to change the very nature of
Interpretation.

[Page 171 | ONB canvas 225 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000225]

For he does not pronounce anything as though it proceeded from himself which
we could or ought to believe. He only brings it about that our mind is able to
perceive without a cloud the things contained in the actual context of
Scripture.]* *Reply.* 1. Alas, how fearful he appears lest he attribute too
much to the Spirit in this business, when he says that the Holy Spirit brings
*some* assistance! But perhaps he explains himself better in *Censure of the
Censure*, page 144. Let us hear him: *[I maintain that the entire power of the
Spirit about which dispute can be held here is twofold: the first is that by
which he so affects our minds that we can perceive the things which are in the
Scriptures; the other is that by which, Scripture being set aside, he teaches
us by an inward inspiration. In the former manner, it is evident that all the
material of faith is sought from Scripture and contains the whole object which
ought to enter our minds; while the Spirit acts by illuminating our minds,
arousing, moving, and leading them, so that, with all the darkness of natural
ignorance dispersed, we may savingly perceive the divine object. In the latter
manner the Spirit teaches by immediate revelation what must be believed and
what must be done, and as if by a certain divine voice, without any aid from
the Scriptures or any external thing, insinuates truths into the souls of
men.]* *Reply.* This is not indeed to explain himself, but rather to
contradict himself. For if the Spirit does these things, he does far more than
Wolzogen wished. This is something other than *bringing us some assistance*
in judging, according to laws established by men, that this meaning agrees
with this passage—an assistance which God provides in the explanation of all
books. But does *Wolzogen* believe that God persuades no one of any scriptural
truth—that is, any truth which is according to or conforms with the
Scriptures—except those whom he guides in Interpreting the Scriptures by these
human rules, or rules established by men, from which that truth is elicited?
In that case *Wolzogen* regards as certain something that contradicts the
experience of many truly pious people who are in no sense *Enthusiasts*. But
let us now suppose that God follows this one method which *Wolzogen* himself
describes for us.

[Page 172 | ONB canvas 226 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000226]

Will it follow from this that we can in no way say that God through his Spirit
affirms this to be the meaning of this passage, and that on this account we
ought to believe this to be the meaning of this passage—even when he so
affects our minds that we can perceive the things which are in the Scriptures,
or when by illuminating, arousing, moving, and leading our minds he causes us,
with all the darkness of natural ignorance dispersed, savingly to perceive
the divine object, and when he works powerfully in our minds so that we can
perceive the force of those arguments and be effectually moved by them? If he
denies that these things follow, what is the purpose of such an expenditure
of words? What else, I ask, had he attempted to prove? If he affirms it, he
must prove the consequence, for it can easily be denied. 2. What does it mean
to change the nature of Interpretation? Or does he think that when God
Interprets the Scriptures, he affirms nothing, speaks nothing, and by his
efficacious operation asserts nothing—even when he works persuasion concerning
some truth? It would certainly be astonishing if the matter were so. 3. Does
God pronounce nothing as proceeding from himself when through his Spirit he
makes the Scriptures clear and perspicuous and Interprets them? Or is the
reasoning this: because he pronounces nothing new or distinct from scriptural
truth as proceeding from himself, therefore he pronounces absolutely nothing
as proceeding from himself; and therefore nothing that he pronounces ought to
be believed by faith, because it is not new or distinct? A remarkable
consequence indeed! 4. He says that God only brings it about that our mind
*can* perceive without a cloud the things contained in the actual context of
Scripture. But, I ask, must we attribute nothing else to God than τὸ *posse*,
the ability to do this—that is, the ability *to perceive these things*? Must
we not also attribute to him τὸ *operari*, the actual working, or τὸ *actu
perspicere*, the actual perceiving? If not, he will never be able to free
himself from heterodoxy. And yet he at least intimates something of the kind,
not only here but also in *Censure of the Censure*, pages 145–146, where he
says: *[Nor does he cause one to judge otherwise concerning whether a meaning
agrees or disagrees,

[Page 173 | ONB canvas 227 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000227]

except because he purges the mind so that it can judge.]* Yet Scripture
testifies that God effects in us and himself *works*, Philippians 2:13. *It is
the same God who works all those things in all*, 1 Corinthians 12:6. He has
similar statements in the same place, pages 146–147: *[The Holy Spirit
certainly illuminates*, he says, *and causes us to discern when, the clouds
being wiped away, he renders our minds fit for judging.]* In that case he does
not effect in us τὸ *actu discernere*, the actual discerning, or the judgment
itself. Therefore he does not lead us into all truth; he does not actually
teach us all things. And when he illuminates minds, he only renders them fit
for seeing or perceiving, and that not by implanting any new principle, but
only by wiping away the clouds. There is therefore no consequent,
cooperating, arousing, moving grace of God which applies us to the act of
perception. In this way he will oppose not only the truth, but also himself;
for he had just said that God *arouses, moves, and leads* our minds—unless he
says that he added, *so that, with all the darkness of natural ignorance
dispersed, we may savingly perceive the divine object*. And thus this
arousing, moving, and leading by God produces only τὸ *posse*, the ability,
but not the *perception* itself. But you will say that he explains himself
better in *Censure of the Censure*, page 147, when he says: *[It must certainly
be said that the Spirit moves and admonishes in the Interpretation of the
Scriptures; yet not that he arouses by an impulse, as it were, perceptible to
the senses, or admonishes by a certain inward voice, which is the pleasant
dream of the Enthusiasts. Rather, he arouses, teaches, and leads inwardly by
the hidden power of singular providence through all those things which are in
the Scriptures themselves.]* But the question always concerns the effect:
that is, whether he so arouses, teaches, and leads by this hidden power of
singular providence that the person whom God thus arouses, teaches, and leads
is actually aroused and taught. If not, he says the same thing here as before.
If so, then he does not merely teach and arouse in such a way that we *can*
perceive; rather, he so arouses, moves, and leads that we actually perceive.
And if the Spirit so *mo-*

[Page 174 | ONB canvas 228 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000228]

*ves*, arouses, and teaches, he does far more than Wolzogen above affirmed
that he does in this business. For this is something other than bringing some
assistance in making a judgment according to human laws. Moreover, he who so
moves, arouses, and teaches that the person who is thus led and taught becomes
persuaded concerning the meaning—does he not effectually bring forth, express,
declare, utter, and, as it were, speak his mind inwardly? And when the soul is
so moved and effectually led actually to receive and embrace the truth, can it
not be said to exercise faith, or to believe that these are truths revealed
there by God?

2. On page 131 he says that the *Exercitator* derives that certainty without
which faith will have nowhere to plant its foot from impregnable arguments
sought from reason, but that he himself derives it from the laws of
deep-rooted custom. *Therefore*, from those laws there can be obtained
unshaken certainty and impregnable arguments in which a soul desirous of
solid truth can and ought fully and completely to rest; and that usage of
speech will be an unshaken and immovable rock upon which the entire structure
of our faith must be built. *Therefore*, that usage will be the sole and
infallible norm for Interpreting Scripture. Therefore, Scripture itself will
not be the infallible norm for interpreting the Scriptures, unless in the
first place by Scripture we understand nothing other than this usage of
speech. *Therefore*, *Wolzogen* does not stand on the side of the Reformed,
but fosters an individual opinion different from the position of the
Orthodox. *Therefore*, he is no less injurious to the Holy Spirit speaking in
Scripture than the *Exercitator* himself. *Therefore*, in this question he is
not to be regarded as Orthodox or as a Patron of the Orthodox. But he says in
*Censure of the Censure*, page 151: *[Since all certainty of salvation depends
upon the certainty of faith, while faith has nothing to believe except what
is drawn from Scripture; and since it cannot be drawn from Scripture except
through interpretation, and finally Interpretation depends upon usage, that
usage must possess something certain so that certain

[Page 175 | ONB canvas 229 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000229]

salvation may be possessed.]* But if interpretation depends upon usage as
upon the sole and infallible norm,
and can be elicited from it with unshaken certainty and impregnable arguments,
that usage must possess not merely something certain, but certainty in every
respect. Otherwise it will remain uncertain whether the Interpretation is
true; and if the Interpretation is uncertain, it will also be uncertain
whether what faith believes is drawn from Scripture. And if this is uncertain,
faith too will be uncertain, and with faith uncertain, salvation will be
rendered uncertain. He himself admits on page 138 that this usage is sometimes
uncertain, as we shall hear presently.

3. On page 132 he says: *[We, who derive the meaning from the Discourse
itself, judge that the matter must be handled not by arguments sought from
reason, but by the laws of deep-rooted custom. For it is a question of fact,
not of right; it depends upon the circumstances of the Discourse, not upon the
evidence of demonstrations. The question in it is what God said, not whether
what he said is true or false.]* If these things are true, he spoke badly above
on pages 20–21 when he said that one rule for Interpreting Scripture must be
sought from Reason, for Reason proves not fact but right—not the existence of
a thing but its essence, as he himself says on pages 134–135. Next, if the
matter is so, then whatever contributes to proving this question of fact has
its own testimony, and this testimony is to be trusted if it is true and
faithful. *Therefore*, faith must also be given to God when by his operation
he makes this question of fact clear. *Therefore*, because God affirms by his
testimony that this is the meaning of this passage, we ought to believe it;
yet he denied this above on page 130.

4. *[Someone will indeed say*, as he does on page 138, *that usage is perhaps
not very certain, so that a certain Interpretation could rest upon it. And I
admit that it is sometimes uncertain. Hence it also comes about that an
interpretation is not always equally certain and that we are unable to attain
some of the things delivered in the Scriptures.]*

[Page 176 | ONB canvas 230 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000230]

That usage, therefore, will not be a sufficiently solid, fixed, and certain
foundation or norm of interpretation; and it will follow from this that our
faith, at least to that extent, does not rest upon an unshaken and immovable
rock. Does *Wolzogen* not here furnish the *Paradoxical Exercitator* with a
very ample occasion for boasting and triumphing? For by his own testimony
Reason, with respect to certainty, is far to be preferred to usage. He admits
here that usage is not altogether certain, but he never said this about
Reason. On the contrary, he compared Reason with God himself, if he did not
prefer it to him, when he said above on page 29: *[Thus we do not rest more
certainly even upon reason than upon his words.]* And on page 30: *[Because he
who says these things can no more deceive or be deceived than Reason itself.]*
Could *Paradox* not infer from this that *Reason*, which can no more deceive or
be deceived than God himself (for I think the *Wolzogenian* proposition can be
converted in this way), should be regarded as the infallible norm rather than
usage, which is not so certain? If he had intended his usage of speech to be
only a means, all would be well, for even if a means is not certain everywhere
and always, it may nevertheless in some respect be preferred to another
means. But this is by no means tolerable in a norm, for that which is always
more certain ought to be regarded as the norm rather than that which is
sometimes uncertain. Does he not, therefore, refute his adversary excellently
here? Or rather, does he not surrender his bound hands to the adversary? It is
indeed true that he says in the same place: *[The usage of a language employed
in human speech or writing cannot be so doubtful that it does not define the
more common notions which fall under that language, and this usage is more
certain in Scripture.]* Nevertheless, the doubt remains whether usage which
is not always certain can be called the norm of interpretation, or its solid,
fixed, and certain foundation.

5. On pages 140–141 he says: *[But now it must finally be investigated how the
mind of God who speaks agrees with

[Page 177 | ONB canvas 231 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000231]

the words according to that meaning, and it is agreed that we can learn this
with certainty. We can do so without great difficulty if we discover that all
the parts of the Discourse, examined according to the laws of Interpreting,
conspire toward the same meaning. This knowledge is of that kind and also of
that degree of certainty which can be the greatest certainty we are able to
possess concerning any matters of fact. It possesses in addition this
prerogative of firmness: that it is very often confirmed by reason when the
matters are of such a nature as to be subject to reason, and is generally
corroborated by the Holy Spirit when it pleases him so to purge our minds that
they can perceive the entire truth of the Interpretation without any
impediment.]* From these words it is clear: 1. That without the Spirit’s help
we can perceive when God’s mind agrees with the words and what the true
meaning of Scripture is; and that with the greatest certainty, or as certainly
as we can know any fact. 2. That whenever there is need of the Spirit’s help,
it is needed only to corroborate this certainty. 3. That this help for
corroborating certainty is not always needed, but only *generally*, and when
it pleases him so to purge our minds that they can perceive the entire truth
of the interpretation without any impediment. *Therefore*, without this
purging of the mind, we can be most certain of the true meaning, since this
purging of the mind is required only to corroborate the true meaning.
*Therefore*, we can be most fully persuaded of the true meaning even if we
cannot perceive the entire truth of the interpretation. For this whole truth
is not perceived before our mind has been purged; yet, as has been said,
before our mind has been purged we can possess the most certain knowledge of
the true meaning of Scripture. And in *Censure of the Censure*, page 166, he
says that *[he did not dare affirm that the Spirit’s assistance is necessary
for each individual act, lest he detract anything from his freedom.]*
*Therefore*, from God’s freedom

[Page 178 | ONB canvas 232 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000232]

detract all who say with the Apostle that we *live and move and have our being
in God*. But he adds in the same place: *[I understand here an extraordinary
aid of grace, not the common aid of providence.]* And a little afterward: *[I
nevertheless said “generally” because, since I was dealing with believers, I
judged that chiefly to be considered which contributed to their salvation.]*
*Reply.* If you understand here an extraordinary aid of grace proper to
believers, then without this extraordinary aid the true meaning can never be
corroborated, nor can this meaning be corroborated for anyone except a
believer. *Therefore*, without this extraordinary aid our minds cannot be so
purged that we can perceive the entire truth of the Interpretation without
any impediment. *Therefore*, no one except a believer assisted by this
extraordinary aid can perceive the entire truth of the Interpretation.
*Therefore*, no one except him can learn with certainty when the mind of God
who speaks agrees with the words, or discover whether all the parts of the
Discourse, examined according to the laws of Interpreting, conspire toward the
same meaning. I do not see how these things agree with what was said above.
But he adds in the same place: *[That aid of grace is not necessary for
explaining many matters which do not pertain to the hidden mysteries of
Religion. An accurate examination of the words alone suffices here; nor is it
necessary where there is no question of a saving and efficacious
persuasion.]* *Reply.* 1. I would like to know whether usage of speech is the
same in the hidden mysteries of Religion as in other matters. If it is the
same, must not all the parts of this Discourse be examined according to the
laws of Interpreting in the same manner as the parts of another Discourse
which treats of something other than hidden mysteries? And if this Discourse
must be examined in the same manner as another, can we not learn with
certainty how the speaker’s mind agrees with the words according to that
meaning in these matters no less than in others? I would like him to explain
these things to us, since

[Page 179 | ONB canvas 233 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000233]

he sets so much value upon usage of speech. 2. If that aid of grace is not
necessary in explaining matters which do not pertain to the hidden mysteries
of Religion, then it is not *generally* necessary, because far more often it
is unnecessary. For he himself admits in the same place that *there are more
intermediate matters than mysteries*. 3. If he says that it is necessary
where there is a question of a saving and efficacious persuasion, then this
aid is not required *generally* but *always*—unless he says that without that
aid of grace we can sometimes arrive at a saving and efficacious persuasion.
And if he says this, to whom among the Reformed Orthodox will he turn himself?
It is clear, 4. that he attributes as much here to Reason as to the
extraordinary assistance of the Spirit. For he says that this certainty is
*confirmed* by Reason and *corroborated* by the Spirit; I do not know what
difference there is between these. Does he also ascribe saving and efficacious
persuasion to Reason in matters which are subject to it? Indeed, he appears to
attribute more to reason than to the Spirit, because he says that Reason
confirms *very often*, while the Holy Spirit only corroborates *generally*. It
is clear, 5. that the true meaning can be possessed without the mind having
been purged, and that in all matters which do not pertain to the hidden
mysteries of Religion. For he says this purging of the mind is required only
to corroborate the true meaning, and that this corroborating assistance is not
necessary in explaining matters which do not pertain to the hidden mysteries
of religion. Do these things agree well with the teaching of the Reformed?
