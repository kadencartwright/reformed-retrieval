7. The Arminians ask what kind of faith, on our position, God requires from
the reprobate in the visible church, for want of which they are guilty of
eternal condemnation. Reply. He requires them to assent to all God's Word and
promises and to rest by a fiducial act upon Christ as the sufficient author of
salvation. They are in no way bound, however, to believe that Christ died for
them. What says Arminius here? He rises up thus: “No one can acknowledge
himself guilty for rejecting a promise made in words if the speaker's mind has
resolved not to perform the promise to him—or rather, if the one promising in
words resolves by a sure decree that the promise should not belong, or be able
to belong, to him.”

[Marginalia: *Against Perkins*, p. 746, Bor edition.]

[1651 logical printed p. 253 | IA leaf 271 | PDF p. 271]

Reply. 1. On Arminius's principles, therefore, no one can acknowledge himself
guilty for rejecting a promise. For he holds that God calls the reprobate with
ineffectual grace, which he foreknows will produce no effect. Thus God
addresses the reprobate: “I will, decree, and from eternity intend that you
believe the evangelical promises, obey, and be saved. Yet from eternity I
decreed that, among many possible callings which would least injure your
freedom, I would employ only the one which I foreknew from eternity would be
useless: you would never obey but incur far heavier torments which would never
end. And I decreed to deny the one calling which, if employed, would bring it
about that you obey and are saved.” We appeal by God to fair readers as judges
and to the Arminians' own conscience: let them speak freely whether they do
not here blasphemously ascribe true hypocrisy and contrary wills to God. 2. To
make clear what universal promises are, I wish it noted that these promises
are considered in two ways. First, formally, as signifying the divine will
which rewards believers with eternal life. In this respect they are nothing
other than God's wise will, by which he most freely determined so to join
faith and eternal life that he holds faith and obedience so intensely dear as
to will to reward it with eternal life. This

[1651 logical printed p. 254 | IA leaf 272 | PDF p. 272]

connection is revealed equally to elect and reprobate by the promulgated
promises. It obliges everyone in the visible church to assent, trust
fiducially in Christ, and obey diligently, whether God has determined by his
eternal decree that the promised goods belong to them or not. Nothing is
therefore more false than this: “No one is guilty for rejecting a promise if
the one promising has determined by a sure decree that the promise should not
belong to him.” Arminius dictates this; would that he had proved it. But his is
a royal and dictatorial theology. What? God says to all who are now bound by
the necessity of sinning, “Do this and live,” and determines by a sure decree
that eternal life rendered to legal obedience shall not belong to us mortals
if we except the Savior. Will no one then be guilty for violating God's law?
Again, it is certain that God never intended to reward the actual sacrifice
of Isaac, yet no sane person would say that Abraham would not have been guilty
if, when God commanded it, he had refused to sacrifice his son. 3. This
argument holds that the formal ground of guilt consists in our will's not
conforming to God's intention and eternal decree. Arminius holds that a man is
not guilty for rejecting a promise which, by God's intention, does not concern
him. If, therefore, the rejected promise does not concern him by God's
intention, its rejection binds him neither by crime nor guilt. Receive,
Jacob, this distinguished theology!

[1651 logical printed p. 255 | IA leaf 273 | PDF p. 273]

Abraham's will intended Isaac's sacrifice, for nothing was more certain or
firmly resolved by Abraham; but God never intended or decreed Isaac's
sacrifice. Here is an opposition between Abraham's intention and God's.
Therefore, I say, by intending to slay his son Abraham was guilty and bound
himself by a crime. Really? Yet God located Abraham's extraordinary obedience
precisely in this, that he resolved to sacrifice his only-begotten son for his
Creator. 2. Promises are taken concretely, as means of predestination which
connote the divine intention to bestow the promised benefits. In this way they
are not bare signs of the approving will, but also connote God's decree. In
the former sense I assert that evangelical promises concern the reprobate no
less than the elect. In the latter sense they belong only to the elect. For in
Scripture only the elect are called covenanted people, children, and heirs of
the promise, Romans 9:8. And the promises are made only to Christ, the prince,
standard-bearer, and heir, Galatians 3:16; Psalm 89:26–27—and in him to his
seed and the children given him by the Father, Hebrews 2:13.

8. Indeed, by God's intention eternal life is not offered to the reprobate
even conditionally. Nothing is more false than John Cameron's novel opinion,
which holds that Christ died for all alike—reprobate and elect—on condition of
faith, but absolutely only for believers. For Christ, Cameron says, “made
satisfaction for you, but on this condition: if you believe it was done. It is
as if I redeemed a captive by paying the price, yet so that unless he
acknowledges himself redeemed by my benefit, he is regarded as not redeemed.”

[Marginalia: Cameron, vol. 3, *Miscellanies*, p. 574, letter of 1611.]

[1651 logical printed p. 256 | IA leaf 274 | PDF p. 274]

Against this I argue: 1. If Christ died conditionally for the reprobate, he
was bruised for their sins and necessarily paid in body and soul the eternal
death owed to them. But the latter is absurd. Therefore so is the former. The
major is evident. What is it for Christ to die for someone except to sustain
the wrath of God owed to the person for whom he dies? I prove the minor. If
Christ sustained even conditionally the death owed to the reprobate, then
according to the order of divine justice the reprobate who never believe
cannot sustain eternal death in themselves. Yet the sacred oracles condemn
this latter proposition as palpable falsehood on almost every page. Cameron
will say that Christ made satisfaction conditionally for the reprobate—namely,
if they believe. But did he not conditionally satisfy for all the sins of all
the reprobate? I believe no one will deny it unless he asserts that Christ
satisfied only for original sin, contrary to the Scriptures which teach that
Christ's blood cleanses us from all sins, 1 John 1:8; 1 Peter 1:18–19; Isaiah
53:6. If he sustained the penalty for all the sins of the reprobate, then also
for the final unbelief and impenitence of the reprobate. According to the
order of justice, therefore, the reprobate ought not eternally to pay in hell
the penalties of final unbelief, for God's justice does not demand two

[1651 logical printed p. 257 | IA leaf 275 | PDF p. 275]

satisfactions for the same sin. 2. God does not impute sins to those for whom
Christ made satisfaction, 2 Corinthians 5:19. But he imputes sins to the
reprobate. Therefore, etc. Cameron says that God does not impute sins to the
reprobate on the hypothesis that they believe. Nonsense. Imputation is an
immanent act of the divine will, but no volition in God is conditional or
hangs undetermined upon the created will. 3. If Christ intended, by his own
and his Father's counsel, conditionally to pay the price of satisfaction for
the reprobate, then he also conditionally destines them for eternal glory. But
what is this other than a universal election of everyone without exception on
the hypothesis of faith, from which the Arminians themselves profess at least
in words to recoil? 3. If Christ died for the reprobate, he embraced them with
a love than which none greater can be given, John 15:13. But this cannot be
said unless he also merited for them the grace of faith and regeneration and
interceded for them before the Father—which the Savior himself denies in John
17: “I do not pray for the world.” For if God delivered his Son to death for
them, how would he not give them all things with him? Romans 8. Yet he does not
give them faith, nor did Christ merit it for them. 4. This is Paul's inference
in Romans 8: Christ died; therefore, who condemns? He thereby indicates that
this connected proposition is true: none for whom Christ died are condemned—
namely, those for whom he died absolutely

[1651 logical printed p. 258 | IA leaf 276 | PDF p. 276]

are not condemned. If, however, they do not believe, they will be condemned.
But faith is not the meritorious cause of non-condemnation; Christ's
satisfaction alone is its meritorious cause. Nor, consistently with Scripture
or reason, is faith an instrumental cause of Christ's death—as though by
believing we made Christ have died, or by believing made Christ regard us as
not sinners. 5. The payment of the price and Christ's death are absolute. He
died for many who were not yet born, much less believing. But to pay the price
and not redeem the captive conflicts with justice. You will say that it does
not conflict with justice where, by God's special command, our faith is
required for the actual acceptance of the paid price. But I answer thus: God's
εὐδοκία, complacency, and acceptance of sinners is everywhere in Scripture
ascribed to God in the name of Christ's satisfaction, Matthew 3:17; Ephesians
1:6; Colossians 1:21–22; Romans 5:10. It is not said that God is well pleased
with the one making satisfaction on account of our faith. 6. When Christ died,
he foreknew that the reprobate would never believe, indeed could not believe,
since he absolutely did not merit for them the grace of faith. God the Father
also foreknew this from eternity. But it is absurd for Christ to die and God
to accept his death on the hypothesis of a condition which he foreknows will
never occur and cannot occur, as though Christ addressed the reprobate in the
gospel thus: “I lay down my life for you,

[1651 logical printed p. 259 | IA leaf 277 | PDF p. 277]

which belongs to my most intense love toward you, with the intention that if
you are willing to believe, you will be saved. Yet I know certainly that you
will never believe; indeed, by my absolute will I decreed neither to give nor
to merit for you the grace of believing.” But not even a prudent man
conditionally intends what he foreknows will never occur. 7. Whoever intends
an end also intends the means to it. But God does not intend faith, the means
of salvation, for the reprobate. Therefore by Christ's death he does not
intend salvation for them either. 8. Christ cannot be said to have died for
those who, by God's absolute will, have never heard so much as the faintest
rumor of his death. For how would he provide such a benefit for mortals whom
he absolutely willed not to inform of that beneficence? 9. Nowhere is it said,
“Believe, and Christ will have died for you.” What is more absurd? He died for
us before we believed. Nor are Cameron's arguments worth a straw; God willing,
we shall refute them more fully elsewhere.
