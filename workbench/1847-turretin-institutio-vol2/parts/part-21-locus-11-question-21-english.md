QUESTION XXI.

THE TENTH COMMANDMENT.

What concupiscence is forbidden by the tenth commandment? And are the
first-first motions sins? Affirmative.

[Marginal heading: Concupiscence is threefold: natural, good, and evil.]

I. God has implanted in the soul two faculties for fleeing evil and desiring
good: τὸ θυμικόν, *the irascible appetite*, and τὸ ἐπιθυμητικόν, *the
concupiscible appetite*. When considered physically within the *genus of
being*, they are neither good nor evil but intermediate and indifferent. They
derive their entire moral goodness and evil from the quality of the objects
with which they are occupied.

Thus *concupiscence* is sometimes praised as good, when it is occupied with a
lawful and good object. In this sense the *Spirit* is said to *desire against
the flesh* (Gal. 5:17), and Paul wishes the faithful to desire spiritual gifts
(1 Cor. 12:31). Sometimes it is condemned as disordered and vicious, when it
has an unjust and unlawful object. Sometimes it is considered intermediate
and natural and is placed under neither praise nor blame.

[Marginal heading: It is either original or actual.]

II. The vicious and disordered concupiscence treated here may be considered
with respect to its source and *habit*. It is then called original
concupiscence. James speaks of it in chapter 1, verses 14–15, and Augustine
frequently designates original sin by the name concupiscence. The Jews
customarily designated it by יצר הרע, *the evil inclination*, and the
Schoolmen commonly express it by *fomes*, *tinder*.

Or it may be considered with respect to its vicious *acts and motions*, and
is then called *actual*. This in turn is either *determinate* through the
formal *consent of the will* and what they call *lingering delight*, or
*indeterminate and incipiently stirring* in its first motion, which does not
proceed beyond velleity and suggestion.

III. Since the commandment not to covet is general, it must extend to every
vicious and disordered concupiscence, both original and habitual as well as
actual, whether it is determinate and voluntary or still indeterminate and
only incipiently stirring. In this way the supreme purity and holiness of the
Lawgiver are marked: he will not allow even the smallest stain of vicious
concupiscence in a human being but demands complete righteousness and
holiness.

[Marginal heading: Whether original concupiscence is included under the tenth
commandment.]

IV. The Roman Catholics raise two questions here: the first about original
and habitual concupiscence, and the second about its first-first motions. On
the first, they deny that concupiscence, or the tinder, is forbidden by the
law, because they judge that it is not sin. The Council of Trent, session 5,
canon 5, does indeed assert “that concupiscence, or the tinder, remains in the
baptized; yet this concupiscence, which the Apostle sometimes calls sin, is
not called sin in the sense that it is truly and properly sin in the
regenerate, but because it is from sin and inclines toward sin; and if anyone
thinks the contrary, let him be anathema.” The orthodox, however, judge both
that concupiscence is properly sin and that it is forbidden by the law.

[Marginal heading: It is proved to be included.]

V. First, this is proved from the words of the law, which expressly forbid
concupiscence, and do so generically and indefinitely, so that every
disordered concupiscence is understood—not only in its act but also in its
habit and root. It is not for us to limit where the law does not limit.

Second, the end of the law entails this very thing. Its end is to demand
complete holiness from a human being, not only with respect to act but also
with respect to habit. Since it requires both kinds of holiness, habitual and
actual,

it must likewise forbid both kinds of concupiscence, actual and habitual.

Third, because that concupiscence is vicious and evil and is *not from God*
but *from the world* (1 John 2:16), it had to be forbidden by the law, which
prohibits every vice.

VI. Fourth, Paul expressly asserts both that it is forbidden by the law and
that it is sin in Romans 7:7: “I would not have known sin without the law; for
I would not have known concupiscence unless the law had said, ‘You shall not
covet.’” He does not deny without qualification that concupiscence was known
to him. Rather, he says that he knew it to be sin, which he learned at last
from the law forbidding it.

Thus Paul supposes concupiscence to be sin because it is forbidden by the
law—not only actual concupiscence but habitual as well. For in verse 8 he
immediately adds that “sin,” namely the concupiscence forbidden by the law,
“having taken occasion, produced every kind of concupiscence.” He confirms
this in verses 9, 11, 13, 17, and 20, where he likewise calls it “sin,”
“indwelling sin,” and “sinful beyond measure”; and in verse 23 he calls it
“the law of the members which fights against the law of the mind and leads a
person captive under the law of sin.”

Who could believe that Paul would have called it sin so often and so
expressly if it were not truly and properly called sin, but received the name
only because it flows from sin and inclines toward sin, as the council wrongly
maintains? On the contrary, who does not see that Paul’s intention was to
ascribe to it the true character of sin, which he had learned from the written
law alone, since the light of reason among the Gentiles did not rise to this
height? See above, Locus IX, Question 11, theses 20 and 21.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. Although the law proximately and immediately forbids only the acts
themselves and is expressed in words signifying actions, nothing prevents the
habits and principles of sins from being mediately forbidden under those
acts, as are original sin and habitual concupiscence. Indeed, this must
necessarily be implied, because a cause is included in its effect and
principles in the things arising from them. Thus the axe is laid to the root
of sin, and complete holiness is demanded from a human being.

VIII. Although original sin is removed in the regenerate with respect to
*guilt* through justification, so that there is no condemnation in them (Rom.
8:1), and with respect to *dominion* through sanctification, so that it no
longer reigns in them, it nevertheless always remains in them, as long as they
live in this mortality, with respect to *presence and habitation*. They must
therefore crucify and mortify it continually.

IX. When James says in chapter 1, verse 15, that “concupiscence gives birth
to sin,” he does not deny that concupiscence is sin. He is not discussing when
sin begins to exist or to be reckoned as sin before God, but when it emerges
into the light and is consummated through an act. His sole concern is to
teach that the root of our destruction is in us.

So far is it from being possible to infer lawfully from this that
concupiscence is not sin, that the opposite is elicited from it by necessary
consequence. An effect must correspond to its cause, and concupiscence could
not give birth to actual sin unless it too were sin.

[Marginal heading: Whether the first-first motions are sins.]

X. The other question concerns the first-first motions of concupiscence. The
Roman Catholics deny that these are forbidden here because they are not
sinful. They call those motions *first-first* which arise by surreption and a
certain incipient stirring, but without the deliberate judgment of the mind
and the formal consent of the will. They therefore customarily call them
προπάθειαν, *prepassion*. These differ from determinate and deliberate
affections, which occur with the consent of the will and are therefore called
*lingering*, because the mind and will linger over them with delight.

The Roman Catholics judge that only the latter are forbidden by the law,
because they are voluntary and within our power, but not the former, which
are neither free nor within our power and thus are not sins. Toledo speaks in
this way on Romans 7, and the others agree with him. We, however,

maintain that concupiscence is evil in its root and forbidden by the law, and
likewise so with respect to all the acts and
motions that arise from it, whether they follow the formal consent of the will
or precede it.

[Marginal heading: They are proved to be sins.]

XI. First, the law condemns those motions of concupiscence which a human
being could have known only through the written law, as the Apostle testifies.
Yet the voluntary motions of concupiscence were not unknown to the Gentiles
as vicious and disordered. Hence Seneca’s saying, “A person is a robber before
he stains his hands,” and Juvenal’s lines:

*For one who merely contemplates any crime within himself*
*bears the guilt of the deed.*

Second, such motions do not agree with the holiness and purity God requires
of us or with the requisite perfection of loving God with the whole heart and
all our strength. Therefore, they cannot but be vicious and ἄνομοι,
*lawless*.

Third, other motions have already been forbidden in the other commandments.
In them the law so forbids external acts that, because it is spiritual, it
also condemns internal ones. Thus Christ calls the man who violates another
man’s wife’s chastity with his eyes and covets her by looking an adulterer
(Matt. 5:28); and for John, “the one who hates his brother is a murderer” (1
John 3:15).

Therefore, if only such motions were forbidden, nothing new beyond the other
commandments would be commanded or forbidden by this commandment, which is
absurd. We do not deny that this commandment was added to the preceding ones
as a general rule to declare and apply them more expressly in the court of
conscience. Nevertheless, the distinction among the commandments necessarily
requires something further to be signified. This can be nothing other than
the habit of concupiscence together with its first motions. In this way it is
shown how much the divine law differs from human laws, which regard only
external motions and do not ascend to the source of evil.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XII. Although τὸ ἀκούσιον, *the involuntary*, which is against one’s will
through a positive act of refusal, is not sin, it does not follow that
nothing can have the character of sin unless it is ἑκούσιον, *voluntary*,
formally and strictly through a deliberate motion and consent of the will.
For then neither original sin nor sins of ignorance would be true sins. It is
sufficient that something be called voluntary in the broad sense, because it
affects the will and inheres in it. Therefore, although the first-first
motions are not voluntary in the former sense, they are rightly called so in
the latter, because they affect the will and have their subject in it.

XIII. Although such motions are not within our power, they are no less
disordered. Neither is every good work within our power, yet its omission is
no less vicious and disordered. The law therefore regards not a person’s
powers but his duty—not what he can or cannot do, but what he is bound to
perform in moral matters.

XIV. Motions of concupiscence can be called *natural* only with respect to
the corrupted nature from which they flow, not with respect to sound and
integral nature. For if Adam had never sinned, he would never have experienced
such motions, which cannot be free from viciousness.
