# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 7.* It is not within believers' power to observe this law.

*Answer.* Yet it does not follow from this that this law has now been
abrogated. For under the old dispensation too it was not within any believer's
power to observe it perfectly; nevertheless, it was not then abrogated, as
the adversaries themselves admit. Furthermore, the obligation of a law does
not depend upon its subjects in such a way that if it is within their power
to fulfill it, the law has binding force, but if it is not, the law has none.
Therefore, although believers cannot perform everything that God's law
requires, they are nevertheless bound to admire that law, esteem it highly,
and regard it as good,

[Printed p. 62 | IA PDF 70 | IA leaf 70 | IA decausadei02brow]

just, and holy, even when they themselves feel their carnality, as Paul did
in Romans 7:12, 14. They are therefore bound to regard it as the norm of life
and conduct and to set it before themselves as the rule according to which
they ought to order their conduct. And this is what all who are led by the
Spirit of Christ do.

*Objection 8.* God works all things in us, namely, both *to will* and *to
accomplish* (Philippians 2:13–14). And it is not we who live but Christ in us
(Galatians 2:20). Therefore, we have no need of any external law by which we
may be directed in our obedience toward God.

*Answer 1.* This reasoning would prove no less that believers under the old
law had no need of any external law than that believers under the New
Testament do not, and consequently that this moral law was abrogated for them
as well. For it was also truly said of them that *God had accomplished all
their works in them, or for them* (Isaiah 26:12).

2. Subordinate things do not conflict. For although it is God who, in the
manner of an efficient cause, effects in us both the willing itself and the
acting itself according to his gracious goodwill, we are nevertheless bound
to work out our salvation with fear and trembling and consequently to order
all our actions according to the moral rule of life. The Spirit living in
believers leads and directs them according to that rule, for it is the same
Spirit who dwells in believers, moves, directs, arouses, and leads them, and
who prescribed that rule. These two things therefore by no means conflict or
destroy one another. For it is one thing to ask by what efficient cause,
mover, guide, and director we live, and another to ask by what rule our
actions are to be ordered.

3. From this it will follow not only that the moral law, which was in force
under the old law, has been abrogated, but also that all Christ's precepts
must likewise be rejected as now entirely useless. Consequently, it is not
now permissible to administer the sacraments in obedience to Christ's
precept. For here too we are led by the Spirit of Christ if we perform those
duties rightly and acceptably.

4. From this it will also follow that there is now no need for us to comfort
ourselves by faith in the promises given, or to be directed by those promises
in exercising faith or pouring out prayers, for Christ fulfills all the
promises.

5. But Paul himself rejects this objection when, by this very argument, he
urges care, diligence, and obedience, and admonishes us to attend to the
things that concern our salvation, not sluggishly but vigorously, eagerly,
and diligently. See Calvin and Bucer in Marlorat on the passage.

*Objection 9.* Believers have an anointing which teaches them. Therefore,
they have no need to be taught by any other rule (1 John 2:27).

*Answer 1.* From this the Anabaptists and Libertines infer with no less
apparent clarity that there is now no need for the ministry or for pastors to
teach and instruct, because the apostle says, *“You have no need for anyone
to teach you, and you know all things”* (verse 20). I do not know whether the
Antinomians will affirm this.

2. But neither conclusion follows from this. For although John said that
they knew all things, in verse 13 of chapter 5 of the same epistle he
nevertheless says that he wrote what he had written so that they might know
that they had eternal life. And although he said that they had no need for
anyone to teach them, he himself nevertheless teaches them many things.
Therefore, just as they needed instruction and instructors notwithstanding
this anointing, so too, notwithstanding that same anointing, they needed

[Printed p. 63 | IA PDF 71 | IA leaf 71 | IA decausadei02brow]

an external law by which they could know what that good, pleasing, and
perfect will of God was.

3. Therefore, the anointing of the Spirit, which teaches everything necessary
to be known for salvation, does not remove the use of the law but instead
confirms and establishes it. For it teaches them by explaining the true
meaning of the law, and so leads them to the law and directs them by the law
as by a norm.

4. It can similarly be said that believers under the Old Testament had no
need of the law, because they too were not altogether devoid of this
anointing but were endowed with it at least in part. Yet they were instructed
by the law. See Psalm 119:98–100.
