## Part III: Infant Faith and Sacramental Efficacy

[Part II | PDF page 102 | logical source scan 101 | printed page 100 | continuation begins after preceding unit]

What then? Hear patiently what I think here, Christian reader, and—

[Part II | PDF page 103 | logical source scan 102 | printed page 101]

—judge. I acknowledge that infants not only can but ought to be baptized, from Christ’s command no less expressly given than circumcision was formerly commanded. From this it follows that infant baptism is not a vain and playful thing, but one which has, by divine appointment, the promise both of forgiveness of sins and of renewal joined to the signs. I also concede that, although not everyone born in the church is necessarily of the number of the elect and those to be saved—in whom alone the promise of salvation is effectual both in the preached Word and in the administration of the sacraments—nevertheless, because God has reserved to himself alone an unimpaired judgment of that distinction in judging others, pastors are commanded to administer baptism to each infant born in the church; and all such infants are also to be presumed indefinitely to be elect.

I agree as well with these three propositions as most certain and immovable: the kingdom of heaven belongs to infants born within the body of the church; no one enters the kingdom of heaven unless justified in Christ; no one is justified in Christ except by faith alone. Therefore, you will say, infants must also employ faith, and indeed that true and actual faith which alone apprehends Christ’s righteousness. Here, however, that perplexing knot presents itself, upon whose explanation the true decision of this controversy depends. Each person’s *own* faith is properly said to be that with which he himself is endowed as something inhering in him. To this is opposed an *alien* faith, that is, a faith which is in another. The former is said to be necessary to each person for salvation, while the latter can no more benefit anyone than if someone were said to live by another’s soul. Here, as it seems to me, is the rock upon which all who posit actual faith in infants strike—with most certain error, as Augustine, the scholastics, and indeed all the Fathers before Dr. Luther bear witness.

For since knowledge and the assent of the understanding—which also affects the will—precede faith, actual faith cannot possibly be attributed to infants without our establishing as many miracles as there are infants born and baptized in the church. And since very few are baptized at adult age, faith would be born in almost no one but would merely begin to be perceived from hearing. All these things are so false that they cannot be defended without obstinacy. Yet an alien faith is attributed to infants in such a way that, although it does not inhere in them, it is nevertheless reckoned as their own in them by God’s covenant: namely, that faith by which parents, through God’s mercy, received not only for themselves but also for their descendants the right to eternal life in Christ—God always reserving to himself, as I have often said, the particular judgments by which this offspring is distinguished. For when he covenants with us he says, “I shall be God to you and to your seed,” even to a thousand—that is, as he interprets himself elsewhere, to a thousand generations. Are some of them therefore not seed merely because they are called out of this life either before baptism or after baptism but before adult age? On the contrary, in this especially they are blessed seed: without the conflict between flesh and spirit, they immediately receive the prize in him who strove and conquered for all his own. It is therefore unsurprising that Christ blessed the little children whom he so kindly received into his arms—

[Part II | PDF page 104 | logical source scan 103 | printed page 102]

—assigned the kingdom of heaven especially to them, and commanded us to strive to become like them (comparatively, of course, and in a certain respect—namely, in malice, not in understanding). For otherwise they too are born flesh from flesh and, because they sinned in Adam, are in themselves liable even to eternal death. Yet just as they are sinners by a sin at once alien and their own, because they were in the one who sinned for himself and his descendants, so they are justified by a faith at once alien and their own, because they were in believing parents who, by the covenant formula, believed for themselves and their descendants. The former comes from themselves by nature unto death; the latter comes by grace from Christ unto life. The former is because they are born from sinners; the latter because they are born from believers.

For holy parents sustain a twofold person: one as children of the first Adam, flesh begetting flesh; the other as those adopted through faith into the latter Adam, transmitting that gift to children who are now not their own but God’s. For why should the offspring of the saints be in a worse condition under this most holy and eternally immutable covenant, by which eternal salvation is granted without limit to parents and children, than the children of any men are in every civil contract in which fathers have covenanted for themselves and all their descendants concerning some matter—unless perhaps they should lose the right of inheritance through some misfortune, or it were stipulated in the contract of donation that the donor should be free to choose from the recipient’s offspring those to whom this benefit should finally pass? And why, just as by a fiction of civil law a person’s direct heirs are reckoned to be one person with their father, should the same not also hold before God in this spiritual generation?

The comparisons of the firstfruits and the whole lump, and of the root and branches, lead us here. This is not because believers are born from believers through faith propagated—for nothing here is hereditary by nature—but because holy people, by grace, beget holy people from that same grace by virtue of the covenant apprehended through faith. This holds so far that when the Apostle speaks of the nation whose branches have been broken off and which is now cut off, he nevertheless testifies that, on account of the Fathers and with respect to election, it has not fallen away forever. This, then, is that faith of the parents: alien in such a way that it becomes the own faith of their elect infants at birth, for the sealing of the covenant in baptism, to which otherwise they would not be admitted.

Nor is there any reason for someone to think that the doctrine of *ex opere operato* is thereby established. Baptism is not the first entrance to salvation for the infants of the godly; their very descent from godly parents is. And the sealing of that covenant by baptism arises from nothing other than the power of the parents’ faith intervening according to the covenant formula. If anyone asks further what, then, elect infants born from godly parents receive at birth from God’s grace in Christ, and what of Christ is sealed in them through baptism, I answer that the matter itself demands that, since infants who reach adult age suffer from more diseases which in themselves are lethal than do those who die either before baptism or after baptism but before they grow up, the former need more remedies sought from Christ than the latter—

[Part II | PDF page 105 | logical source scan 104 | printed page 103]

—a distinction which, when people fail to notice it, sometimes causes them to pour out not a few things in this argument which are insufficiently apt. Infants, therefore—even infants of the elect—are born corrupt and guilty because of the sin propagated from Adam, at once alien and their own; but they have not yet been polluted before God’s face by any of that vice’s most bitter fruits. It is therefore sufficient for them that there be this wonderful and hidden and inscrutable, yet most certain, purely passive work of the Holy Spirit within them, by which it comes about that they are born members of Christ. His obedience even to death on the cross, imputed to them, absolves them from the guilt of death and makes them worthy in him of eternal life through the same imputed righteousness of Christ.

As for the corruption to which they are born liable, their spirit, called out of this life into that blessed life before it is polluted by the fruits of that corruption, certainly does not need that form of renewal from which the conflict between spirit and flesh arises in adults. Instead it passes directly to that far higher and happier degree of regeneration to which, in God’s presence in Christ, all who die in the Lord are raised. Their tender bodies, moreover, even then receive the seed of incorruption by the power of the same Holy Spirit in the same Christ. This, then, is the justification of elect infants dying in infancy; and this is that seed of regeneration in them. For this reason we rightly regard them as most blessed—and not merely suppose it, as Andreae slanders us, but truly affirm it.

Yet since among Abraham’s children not all are heirs, as the Apostle says, but only the children of promise—for God is debtor to no one, has mercy only upon those whom he wills, and even if he had mercy upon no one would injure no one, since all are born in themselves children of wrath—and since it is not ours to distinguish some from others with certainty, not even among adults, we can and ought, relying on God’s will, to presume the application of the promise to individual persons. But to affirm it otherwise than probably and with a condition unknown to us would belong not to any knowledge but to sheer audacity and rashness. Thus I determine the matter according to my measure.

And lest I appear to introduce a new dogma, let us hear Augustine writing very plainly about this in Sermon 14 on the Words of the Apostle: “How do infants believe? By their parents’ faith. If they are cleansed by their parents’ faith, they were polluted by their parents’ sin. The body of death in the first parents generated them as sinners; the Spirit of life in the later parents regenerated them as faithful.” So he writes, judging most rightly also in this: he denies that from this holiness in which the Apostle testifies that the infants of godly parents are born, it ought to be concluded that they do not need baptism. Bernard too says in Letter 77: “Among the nations, however, as many faithful as were found before circumcision, we believe that adults were expiated by faith and sacrifices, but for little children—”

[Part II | PDF page 106 | logical source scan 105 | printed page 104]

“—the parents’ faith alone availed—indeed, sufficed.” And in the same place: “Because infants, their age preventing it, cannot have this faith, which is conversion of the heart to God, and consequently cannot have salvation if they die without receiving baptism—not because, when they are baptized, they are altogether without faith, without which it is impossible even for them to please God, but they too are saved through faith, not their own but another’s. For it is worthy, as regards God’s dignity, that grace should allow another’s faith to benefit those whose age denies them their own. Almighty justice does not think that their own faith must be required from those whom it knows to possess no fault of their own.” It is clear from their catechisms and very many other writings that, with the exception of Dr. Luther and those for whom “he himself said it” is enough, all the most learned theologians of our age think the same as Augustine: namely, that they establish the foundation of infant baptism not in any actual faith of infants but in the formula of the eternal covenant which godly parents have apprehended by faith.

### The Grace of Baptism Is Offered to Everyone in a Baptism Rightly Administered, but Is Received Only by the Elect

What could be truer than this proposition: in baptism rightly administered, the grace of baptism is offered by God to all who are baptized, even if it is not received by everyone? Yet Andreae thought even this must be gnawed at. He complains that we thereby exclude those whom, he says, we call created and ordained by God from conception and birth for eternal damnation and whom God is unwilling to regenerate and save. But in this reply I did not touch with even a word upon who would or would not receive the grace offered to them. Yet he thought that no opportunity should be omitted to bring us into odium among the unlearned. It is well, however, that he has struck his tooth against something solid.

If he maintains—as he certainly does, and says expressly in the preceding observation—that the grace of baptism is not merely offered but conferred upon all, that is, upon every individual, then faith too is conferred, especially since he stubbornly maintains that even all infants must be endowed with actual faith or else will be without the grace of baptism and consequently perish. Faith, therefore, will belong to all the baptized. Whom, then, shall we believe? Andreae rather than Christ, who declares, “Many are called, few are elect”? Rather than the Apostle, who expressly writes that faith does not belong to all? Rather than the universal experience of every age? If faith belongs to all, then salvation too belongs to all. “No,” Andreae will say, “for most fall away from faith.” Indeed, if this exception is true and the gifts of perseverance and repentance do not necessarily cohere with true faith, however often it may be struggling and dormant, where is the firmness of our hope? Where is our glorying in all our trials and chastisements? Where is that apostolic word, “Who will separate us from the love of Christ?” Where are those irrevocable gifts? Where is the immutability of the divine decree, if it is suspended from our instability? Finally, if faith belongs to all—

[Part II | PDF page 107 | logical source scan 106 | printed page 105]

—who are rightly baptized, and faith comes from gracious election rather than from us, then all rightly baptized persons are elect. But the assembly of the elect is the church. Therefore every member of the church is elect. Yet who can be said to choose if he accepts everyone? And how will all those comparisons of Christ stand, concerning pure and impure things gathered together in the visible church? Again, if this faith begins from baptism, all who die before baptism will necessarily perish, since, if Andreae is believed, no one can please God without his own personal faith. Nevertheless, in this colloquy Andreae did not dare to exclude all the unbaptized from God’s kingdom, but only those who despise baptism. The opinions of people who wander from the truth must thus conflict with themselves. And do you not yet see, Germany, what sort of theology those men teach in your bosom?

But to say something also about what is charged against me here: first, it reveals this man’s great ignorance that he thinks we make God’s decree begin from the conception and birth of those being born. For if matters stood so, the preexistence of those being born would precede God’s decree; whereas, on the contrary, whatever exists does so because God decreed that it should exist. Unless this is established, it will follow that whatever exists exists from itself—that is, there have been, are, and will be as many gods as there have been, are, and will be things. We would then have to agree with those madmen who believed the world to be a kind of body coeternal with God and animated by God as by a soul.

Second, the cause of the decree concerning some who are to be condemned in due time on account of their sins will be plain and evident: namely, that they are conceived in sins. But if this is true, why, I ask, does the Apostle rise to that eternal and immovable will of God? On the contrary, reason entirely demands that when the elect are under discussion, some corresponding thing either preexisting in reality or foreseen in God’s mind in those whom God decreed to save must be laid beneath his decree that they be saved in due time. All the orthodox agree that this dogma is purely Pelagian, and from it would also follow that blasphemy that we were elected before eternal times not in Christ but in ourselves. Here again I am compelled to cry: Germany, how does it happen that you do not perceive what theology’s doctors you foster in your bosom?

In the other two places of this observation, brief as it is, two shameful slanders display themselves. We have never said and do not say, crudely and simply as it is written here, that some were created and ordained to eternal damnation. We add that the cause—not of making this decree, but of executing it—inheres in the people themselves: namely, the corruption and guilt of those whom God condemns and, in most, the fruits of that corruption itself. For this reason they too are called vessels of wrath by the Apostle, the term *wrath* demonstrating that the just cause of God’s wrath is located in the vessels of wrath themselves, not in God. The other slander lies in these words, “Whom God—”

[Part II | PDF page 108 | logical source scan 107 | printed page 106]

“—is unwilling to regenerate and save,” which Andreae phrased so ambiguously as though these people indeed desire to be saved, but God resists their will and opposes them as they grasp after the path of salvation. On the contrary, although it is true that God, who is debtor to no one, is unwilling to have mercy on those whom from eternity he decreed to leave in their own sins, this too is true: some of them are condemned for no other reason than that they held the truth of God—that is, the implanted knowledge of him—in unrighteousness; others because they either utterly disdained the further light of the gospel or vomited out what they had tasted; and still others because they resisted both the truth and the knowledge of God with hostility. Every mortal would certainly do these things unless God had mercy on those upon whom he wills to have mercy. What in these statements, then, ought not to be conceded as most true?

Just as we previously heard Andreae maintain by the emptiest reasoning that baptism is separated within itself unless we confess, without exception, that each person baptized with water is also baptized with the Spirit, so now he contends by an even more futile argument that baptism is not regarded as a washing but merely as a sign unless we confess that all who are baptized are regenerated at the very moment when they are baptized. The man is remarkably confident. Has he been endowed with some spirit not merely capable of measuring but given in such measure that he can measure the very moments of the Holy Spirit’s efficacy? I think that, among people of sufficiently sound mind, this utterly empty dream does not even need refutation.

As for infants born in the church and divinely elect—of which kind, I have said not rashly, all are to be presumed—and who will die before attaining the use of understanding, I would readily judge, relying upon God’s promise, that at birth they are grafted into Christ, as I explained more fully above. But concerning the rest, what else can we determine without the most open rashness except that they are regenerated when they are finally endowed, through hearing, with true faith—unless in some individuals the Lord has exercised that extraordinary power of his inspiration? Yet who among men will define this time?

But so that everyone may understand the force of that utterly empty argument, I ask: would a seed not be a seed, but some vain and fictitious thing, and would sowing not be true sowing, but some empty game, unless what was entrusted to the field immediately germinated, grew green, flowered, and bore fruit? Was not the man who was summoned by his father to go into the vineyard and for some time so greatly scorned his voice that he stubbornly declared he would not go there, only later, after repenting, finally affected by the father’s voice which he had despised? Does Andreae dream that all who are divinely called are given the same readiness of faith and eagerness of mind as Abraham, the sons of Zebedee, Matthew, and others of that kind? Indeed, by what appearance of any reasoning does he imagine that faith is actually given either to infants formerly circumcised or to infants now baptized who will reach adult age—

[Part II | PDF page 109 | logical source scan 108 | printed page 107]

—before, having grown up, they hear the voice of the gospel and, the Lord opening their heart, begin to believe? For which one among so many myriads will Andreae produce who, after becoming an adult, possesses anything concerning the mystery of salvation except what he received through his adult ears? Yet if Andreae is believed, we must affirm that all who were baptized in infancy, so that baptism may always retain its power, were endowed at the very moment of baptism with true actual faith and regenerated—in some incomprehensible manner, of course—and that all of them then lose that same faith and regeneration, likewise in an incomprehensible manner, as they advance from infancy to a more mature age.

Finally, to untie this knot once for all: will Andreae dare say that those baptized with Christ’s baptism either with no faith, with feigned faith, or as heretics in a heretical church either obtain the grace of baptism especially at that moment, can never repent, or, if they do repent and come to the orthodox faith, must be rebaptized? I think not. Therefore their baptism finally benefited them not at the moment when it was administered to them, but at the time when they repented—and perhaps not before the last breath of life. That dogma is therefore false and ridiculous. Augustine, speaking expressly on this subject in very many places against the Anabaptists and Cyprian’s error, says: “Although Christ’s baptism is the same among heretics or schismatics, nevertheless it does not work remission of sins there because of the foulness of discord and the unrighteousness of dissension. But that same baptism begins to avail for the remission of sins when they come to the peace of the church—not so that the former baptism is rejected as belonging to another or as different, or another baptism given, but so that the very same baptism which outside, because of discord, worked death”—understand: incidentally, for death does not arise from baptism itself but from its abuse—“should work salvation within because of peace.”

If Augustine was truly a theologian, what must be thought of Andreae, whose following words on page 454 certainly deserve to make him deeply ashamed? “That the Holy Spirit, in a baptism legitimately administered, does not exercise the power latent in baptism at the very time when a person is baptized is something very new and astonishing for me to hear.” As though the unworthiness of the person approaching either abolished the Holy Spirit’s power or detracted anything from baptism’s ordinance.

*In this question our assertion is this: baptism is not merely a sign, but truly is the washing of regeneration.*

### Antithesis V: Theodore Beza

*You do not indeed assert it in words, but in explaining the words you do so in fact, since you place the substance of the sacraments merely IN SIGNIFYING.*

1. *Although the external action of baptism and the inward power of the Holy Spirit are distinct things, nevertheless, on account of God’s ordinance, the power of the Holy Spirit, whose proper effect is regeneration, is rightly attributed to the external action. But this separation savors of the Schwenckfeldian error.*

2. *They make two baptisms when they exclude water from baptism’s proper effect, which is the Holy Spirit.*

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Marginal Notes

We do not teach that the sacraments are bare signs, but we nevertheless deny that what belongs to the Holy Spirit alone must be attributed to the external action. The two are not indeed separated, but distinguished, by John in Matthew 3:11 and Peter in 1 Peter 3:2. The Apostle also says concerning the whole ministry that the one who plants and waters is nothing.

Andreae slanders us as though we established water and its sprinkling as bare and empty signs—a thoroughly rotten slander which he also repeats in the colloquy itself. If we said that those things were NOTHING, how great a clamor would he raise? Yet the Apostle speaks in this way about the very ministers of the divine Word and sacraments, who elsewhere are nevertheless called “God’s fellow workers.” I concede that he speaks comparatively, but not at all hyperbolically, if someone compares the inward work, which belongs to God alone, with what ministers do outwardly. For all things are as nothing beside the work of God alone.

But here too, I beg, let the Christian reader see with how much knowledge and conscience we are being treated. In the discussion of the Lord’s Supper these men cry that we establish bare signs unless we set forth in reality Christ’s very essential body in, under, or with that bread and likewise that very blood shed for us in, under, or with that wine. Yet it would be curious and even profane to dispute where that very blood, dripping from Christ’s wounds, went and whether the risen Christ took that same blood back again—although this “according to the letter” interpretation carries human minds into just such thoughts. But in baptism these men admit precisely what they accuse us of in the Lord’s Supper, since they establish as the thing of baptism not Christ himself but merely his benefits: cleansing from sins and regeneration.

Yet, as we declared in its place, neither the fruit is ever to be separated from the field nor the field from the fruit; and no one can share in the fullness of benefits overflowing from Christ into his members—Christ being apprehended through faith both in the simple Word and in the sacraments—unless he has been made one with Christ. Accordingly, the one who called baptism the washing of regeneration also said that Christ himself is put on—

[Part II | PDF page 111 | logical source scan 110 | printed page 109]

—through baptism. Although, in the special sacramental form, we are said in baptism to be washed by his blood, and in the Supper to be fed by his flesh and blood, nevertheless, indicating elsewhere that the two sacraments have one and the same common goal—namely, our spiritual union with Christ—the Apostle says by a most elegant interchange that we are baptized into one body and given one Spirit to drink. Indeed, Christ himself says that even in the simple Word his flesh, which he was to give for the life of the world, is eaten. Nothing more expressive or majestic can even be conceived concerning the mystery of the Lord’s Supper. Thus it is sufficiently clear to all who are not contentious that nothing more must be sought in the sacraments than in the simple Word. Sacramental offering and reception do not differ in the thing itself from that which occurs in the simple Word—the thing which is divinely offered in each case and must be received through faith—but only in the mode of offering. In the simple Word the Holy Spirit teaches us through the ears alone; in the sacraments he employs along with the Word other corporeal and sensible signs and, unless the obstacle lies with us, binds us more and more to our head by a spiritual bond.

But behold the new bareness of signs which Andreae devises in baptism, describing it on page 454 thus: “If the substance of the things signified and also their effect is excluded from the sacraments, are not bare signs left?” Hear this, Christian reader: if there are things signified in baptism, then there are also signs which signify them. The latter are outward and seen; the former inward and understood. Yet to Andreae the man who distinguishes outward baptism from inward and thus makes a twofold baptism is a heretic. How many errors there are in so few words! For who can take from the sacraments what God joined to the signs unless perhaps he is stronger than God? Further, if the things signified were removed, not bare signs but no signs at all would remain, since the nature of correlatives is that when one is posited both are posited, and when one is removed both are removed. Moreover, how absurd it is not to distinguish the thing itself from its abuse and, from an effect impeded through a defect in the recipient, to think something has been diminished from the efficient cause. The gospel, then, would not be God’s power for salvation because its rejection is deadly to unbelievers; the sun would not be bright because it does not shine for the blind.

Far more rightly, Dr. Herbrand writes on page 615 of his Compendium: “One must diligently distinguish between baptism itself, or the substance of baptism, and the use or faith which must accompany the use of baptism if we wish it to be saving.” And shortly afterward: “For the substance of the sacraments does not depend upon the worthiness and faith of the giver”—that is, the minister—“or recipient, but upon the divine ordinance or institution, whose truth human unbelief does not nullify.” The grace of baptism, therefore, is not separated from the sign, but, when neglected by one who approaches unworthily, is not received. For such a person—

[Part II | PDF page 112 | logical source scan 111 | printed page 110]

—the water is consequently not Christ’s blood, nor is washing with water the washing of regeneration effectually, that is, unto salvation; but contempt for both turns to his death. Why should I produce more reasons or witnesses? Let Andreae himself be heard, who on page 487 praises as piously and rightly said this statement of Augustine: “Those who despise the sacraments are deprived of the grace of the sacraments,” that is, they are without the grace of baptism. Otherwise no one is deprived of what he never possessed. But if Andreae truly approves this, and if signs thereby become bare, how can he rightly approve in Augustine the very thing which he condemns in us as a grave and almost inexpiable sin? Since matters stand thus, what shame and conscience does it show to charge us with placing the substance of the sacraments in signification ALONE?

If Andreae, contradicting himself, understands by the term *sacraments* only water and the sprinkling of water, we are so far from what he imputes to us that, with respect to God’s ordinance, we teach that not even the fault of those who approach unworthily detracts anything from the signs themselves, but only prevents their fruit. But if he considers the sacraments as wholes, how could we rest in the signification of signs which, if the things signified were excluded, would cease to be signs? Indeed, how could we separate the inward thing from the outward, when, against Andreae, we defend the two essential parts of baptism insofar as it is one thing: namely, outward baptism—that is, washing with water—and the inward washing of the Holy Spirit? The other stripping bare of the signs, which is not merely ineptly devised but also sins most gravely against God, will soon be explained by us.

I shall state what must be attributed to this water and its sprinkling in my observations on the antithesis to the first and second dogmas. For now, let the Christian reader observe only that Andreae attributes action to water, but the power by which the water acts to the Holy Spirit. Thus, with respect to the power of regenerating, regeneration belongs to the Holy Spirit, while the water itself regenerates through that power which the Holy Spirit exercises in and through it. I do not know whether what he repeats about Separation savors of the Schwenckfeldian error—for I have read very little from that fanatical heretic—but, as I have shown several times, it breathes either great ignorance or shameless slander.

I would be surprised that Andreae laid against us this sophistry about two baptisms if I did not remember that in the discussion of the Lord’s Supper he likewise establishes one oral eating of two utterly different things: bread and body, wine and the Lord’s blood. How he can finally accommodate that to his unity of baptism, let him see. From it, so far as I can infer, one must certainly conclude that by the outward washing of baptism both water and something essential along with the water are received, just as he contends that in outward reception by the mouth the body is eaten with the bread—

[Part II | PDF page 113 | logical source scan 112 | printed page 111]

—and the blood drunk with the wine. Yet that Andreae cannot appear consistent with himself is evident from his stubborn denial—as the principle of this comparison makes clear—that water is used in baptism as a thing signifying the Lord’s blood. I ask: what else is it to deny that water is the sacramental sign of the Lord’s blood than openly to do the very thing he falsely attributes to us, namely, to exclude water from baptism? For how could something that is neither sign nor thing signified be a part of baptism? But this error could be pardoned in him because, coming to his senses in some fashion on page 456, he acknowledges that if the parts of baptism are compared with one another, water is the sign of the blood and its sprinkling is the signification and representation of spiritual mortification and regeneration.

If the person who distinguishes the essential parts of a single composite whole neither tears it apart nor multiplies it, what better witness and judge than Andreae himself could I use to refute his false charge that we introduce two baptisms in place of the one baptism considered as a whole? On the contrary, we teach that within that one baptism these are to be distinctly considered: the material and visible sign, namely the water; the thing signified, namely the Lord’s blood, visible to the mind alone; the one outward rite exposed to the bodily senses, which is properly called baptism, namely the sprinkling of water by which bodily filth is washed away; and the application of Christ’s blood, improperly called baptism, which faith alone sees and apprehends and by which we are regenerated. Far from us, therefore, is any tearing of the sign from the things signified.

### Thesis VI of the Württemberg Doctors

*These are the foundations of this faith and confession of ours: Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her, that he might sanctify her, cleansing her by the washing of water in the Word. Unless one is born FROM WATER and the Spirit, he cannot enter God’s kingdom. Not from works of righteousness which we performed, but according to his mercy he saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewal of the Holy Spirit. As many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ. All of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death; we were buried with him through baptism into death, so that, just as Christ rose from the dead through the Father’s glory, so we too might walk in newness of life.*

[Part II | PDF page 114 | logical source scan 113 | printed page 112]

### Antithesis VI: Theodore Beza

*We approve all these testimonies of Holy Scripture, by which both the institution of baptism and its effects are declared—in those, certainly, who do not reject through their own unworthiness the benefits offered to them.*

### Andreae’s Marginal Observation

1. *Here these men exclude those persons, whether baptized or to be baptized, who by God’s eternal and hidden decree, before they have done anything good or evil, were ordained to eternal damnation. For they affirm that these, even if baptized a thousand times, will never be regenerated—a horrible thing to hear.*

### Dogmas Which the Württemberg Doctors Judge to Conflict with Holy Scripture

1. *It is taught that no latent power is attached or affixed to the sacraments by which the Holy Spirit confers grace upon those baptized, but that only this office has been divinely entrusted to them: to testify and confirm God’s good will toward us.*

2. *Those are foolish who bind the Holy Spirit and his effects in us to the sacraments, since the sacraments are instead used for this purpose: that in the children of believers they may seal adoption, and in adults faith also.*

### To the Dogmas Placed beneath the Preceding Theses

#### To I and II

1. *If every latent power is taken away from the water of baptism, what, I ask, is left but bare signs? This conflicts with the substance of baptism. For neither does the Holy Spirit regenerate without water nor water without the Holy Spirit, but the Holy Spirit regenerates through water and the water by the Holy Spirit’s power, from God’s ordinance, in baptism.*

2. *The water of baptism was ordained by God not merely for signifying, but as an instrument for the real exhibition of regeneration. Therefore this assertion is openly false and impious.*

3. *We do not in the least doubt that this is blasphemous against Christ, the author of baptism. For to think about baptism as was set down shortly before is not idolatry but worship of God.*

4. *This latent power of regenerating is not attributed to water simply or in itself, but because it was divinely ordained for this purpose in baptism.*

### Reply to the Four Observations on Dogmas I and II

Do we not regard it as manifest idolatry to attribute to the water some other latent power—even if sacramental—than sacramental signification, which is never vain or empty as far as God who promises is concerned? For the power that belongs not even to their angels—namely, the power of forgiving sins and renewing human hearts—would thereby be transferred to water, however sacramental.

[Part II | PDF page 115 | logical source scan 114 | printed page 113]

The question here is why the Lord employed water and immersion in water, or its sprinkling, in the sacrament with which we are dealing—whence the action itself received the name of baptism—and what and how much is to be attributed in this action to the water and its sprinkling, lest more or less than is right be attributed to them. The former would be superstition, the latter manifest contempt. We teach that the proper measure is finally maintained by those who attribute to water and its sprinkling in baptism no other power than to that bread and wine in the sacred Supper of the Lord: namely, the power of sacramental signification, that is, of representing to the minds of those approaching, and as it were placing before their eyes, those things which by divine ordinance are declared through the promise attached to those visible signs concerning eternal salvation to be sealed in Christ.

But Andreae is so far from content with this use of sacramental signification that he is unwilling even to call water a sign—a matter discussed in my observations on our first antithesis—and contends that some power is implanted in the water itself and its sprinkling through the Holy Spirit and that it is an instrument effecting regeneration. Lest I appear to wrong him in this matter, although it is sufficiently clear that this is his meaning from these four observations to which I now reply, I shall add in order the things which he repeated about it in our colloquy with various perplexing phrases.

On page 441: “The Holy Spirit is included in the word of baptism and joined to water, the external element.” On page 443: “Baptism, just as it is not water alone, so is not the Spirit alone, but water and Spirit together.” On page 444: “Christ joined water with the Spirit and attributes regeneration to each, namely, because the Spirit regenerates to eternal life through water.” In the same place: “Water is the instrument of the Holy Spirit through which he works regeneration.” On page 445: “Although the rite of sprinkling in baptism is a certain representation of spiritual mortification and regeneration, it was instituted as a cause not only FOR REPRESENTING—”

[Part II | PDF page 116 | logical source scan 115 | printed page 114]

“—but FOR EFFECTING regeneration.” On page 447: “Although water does not reach the soul, nevertheless the Spirit exercises his power through water, and the whole action is spiritual.” On page 451: “The Holy Spirit is the principal efficient cause, which works through water as through an instrument in Christ’s blood.” Again: “We do not attribute this work to bare water, but to the Holy Spirit through water.” On page 452: “The washing is properly understood when a person is washed with water and the Holy Spirit is present in that water of baptism, who performs what bare water without the Holy Spirit could not perform. We too confess that the Holy Spirit is present, indeed presides over baptism, but so as alone to accomplish what water—indeed, what not even any angel—can accomplish in any manner.” On page 453: “It is openly said in your theses to be manifest idolatry if we attribute to water any power other than sacramental signification, however sacramental. We leave to the hearers’ judgment whether this is not to establish bare signs without the thing signified.” On page 456: “One and the same effect is attributed to both together, namely to the Spirit and water.”

So he writes. From these things I think what I said above is plainly established, especially from the fact that Andreae cannot endure a distinction between water and its outward sprinkling—whose minister is a man and which is properly called baptism—and the inward sealing in the faithful mind of sprinkling with Christ’s blood through the Holy Spirit. The latter itself is metaphorically called the “sprinkling” of Christ’s blood in 1 Peter 1:2 and an “appeal” in 1 Peter 3:21. Accordingly, whenever Andreae repeats in this same colloquy that regeneration is not merely signified and represented by water and its sprinkling but conferred in reality in baptism—which we too acknowledge in believers—it must not be understood in the sense in which he speaks.

For the signifying thing and the thing signified are two things altogether distinct in number. They are not properly subordinated, as when a craftsman uses a tool, but coordinated for their respective uses, although they concur in the common result according to the nature of the things themselves, namely, the regeneration of the baptized person. The water and its outward sprinkling announce and affirm through the outward senses to the mind what divine power alone applies to the baptized through faith: the forgiveness of sins in Christ’s blood, the abolition of the old man, and the creation of the new. The work of the divine Spirit in sealing Christ and his benefits in the baptized person proceeds not through any power communicated in any way to the water and its sprinkling—or “caused,” as the scholastics speak—but comes entirely from God himself. This is declared by the words of John the Baptist, who does not indeed disjoin himself and his action from Christ and the action of Christ’s Spirit, but openly distinguishes them in number, persons, and effect. Thus it is false when Andreae says on page 446 that Christ cleansed his church not significatively but really. For the church is cleansed significatively by the baptism of water and really by the baptism of the Spirit, both concurring in their distinct actions toward the common effect of regeneration, just as John the Baptist establishes two baptizers.

[Part II | PDF page 117 | logical source scan 116 | printed page 115]

The contrary dogma was drawn from the scholastics’ stinking cisterns. In order to introduce their satanic character, as they call it, they attributed a causative power of conferring grace—principally to God, instrumentally to the sacraments. Whoever wishes may learn this from their writings, especially Thomas, *Summa*, Part III, Question 62, Articles 1, 3, and 4. I do not deny that some were led into this palpable error by badly understood statements of the Fathers. The Fathers certainly did not mean to attribute to signs in any way what is the Holy Spirit’s work alone. But because they wished to commend the use and efficacy of the sacraments, they often spoke of the signs in such a way that what belongs to divine power alone seems to be attributed to them as if they were subordinate instruments, as these men wish.

Thus Paul calls the bread which we break a communion of Christ’s body and the cup which we drink a communion of Christ’s blood. Thus he calls the baptism of water the washing of regeneration. Thus faith is said to come from hearing the preached Word. Thus the Word itself which sounds in our ears is called by Peter the seed of life. Thus Paul, writing to Timothy, says, “By teaching these things you will save both yourself and those who hear you.” Why should they not speak in this way, and the Fathers after them too, when Christ himself, to show that sacramental signification is not vain, attributed to that bread and wine the names of his own body and blood? Yet Andreae, however stubbornly he retains the real and essential presence of the body with that bread and of the blood with that wine, should consider whether he dares attribute to that bread and wine and to the oral eating of that bread and wine as such some latent power toward participating in the Lord’s body and blood like the one he assigns to baptism’s water and its sprinkling.

That speech affects hearers does not establish that any power inheres in the audible words themselves other than that of signifying the other things about which they are spoken. That the things signified by words are understood by hearers arises not from the words themselves but from the faculty of understanding which inheres in the hearer’s mind—whether from nature in natural things, or from grace in those divine things which flesh and blood do not reveal but that teacher of truth who gives ears to hear. Much more must the same be established concerning the assent of faith.

What more? The very people in whom God placed the message of reconciliation are indeed something more than the sounds which—

[Part II | PDF page 118 | logical source scan 117 | printed page 116]

—they utter. They speak, teach, exhort, rebuke, and console, and therefore deserve the designation “instruments that work together,” so that God is even said to work together with them. Yet this is so that the ministers of the Word and sacraments have their own distinct actions which must in no way be mixed with the Holy Spirit’s action: namely, the outward planting and watering, while divine power alone inwardly gives to the good seed sown in the field of a faithful mind the power to germinate, grow, and bear fruit. I should like to illustrate this by a familiar comparison.

God alone is both the author and, properly, the preserver of our natural life. He attributed natural powers suited to feeding and preserving us to the foods we eat and the various things created to maintain or restore our health, because this seemed good to him—not because he necessarily needs any aids to effect anything. He therefore feeds us, but by a power latent in the foods themselves; and he preserves or heals us by medicines into which he instilled one property or another. These are properly instrumental and at the same time efficient causes.

But in the regeneration of a person, on account of which the Apostle says we are God’s workmanship, anyone is greatly mistaken and wrongs God himself who thinks that God attributed even the smallest portion of his divine power for renewing people and preserving them unto eternal life in Christ either to the people through whose mouth he speaks, to the outward Word of God itself, or to the sacramental signs. Such power does not belong even to the angels. Just as God alone created the world and humanity by his own sole power—not, as Plato supposed, through certain lesser gods—so the same power of God alone works the inward regeneration of a person.

Nor must we suppose that, just as in generating Adam’s posterity God implanted in fathers the power to beget and in mothers the power to conceive, through which as a secondary cause he brings people into the world, so also in spiritual regeneration he attributed some intrinsic power either to the ministry of the people who plant and water, much less to the preached Word itself or to the sacraments. That would be to transfer his glory in some way to people, and still more to inanimate things. Paul, I confess, nevertheless says that he travails in birth and begets through the gospel. But elsewhere the same Paul says that he is nothing, not only insofar as he is a man but even insofar as he was a faithful apostle of God. In the former place he considers the gospel ministry as a whole, but does not distinctly consider his own labor or a power proper to the preached Word. In the latter, by contrast, he distinguishes both of these from God’s own power and never transfers to himself even the least portion of divine operation. Not even the flesh of the Son of God has in itself a life-giving power inhering in it as in its proper subject. That entire power resides in his deity, although it conveys that spiritual life into the members spiritually adhering to that head only through the intervention of the same flesh apprehended by faith—

[Part II | PDF page 119 | logical source scan 118 | printed page 117]

—This is what the Apostle declares in Ephesians 5: gifts are distributed not by people, not by the Word itself insofar as it sounds in the ears, and not by the sacraments, but by the Spirit alone, to whom and as he wills, as the same Apostle teaches extensively in 1 Corinthians 12. Let that divine doctrine indeed illuminate minds. Let God’s Word be living and sharper than any sword. Nevertheless, we must not think that any spiritual and intrinsic power has been implanted in these things as we said concerning natural foods and medicines. These things are said of them in view of their sacred, divinely ordained relation: God’s Spirit alone, by his own inward power, accomplishes what he declares through the human voice and signifies both by words and by the visible signs joined to them.

The sayings of the Fathers which commend the gospel ministry must be understood in no other way. They do not attribute anything divine to their planting, watering, or building, nor to their words or to the sacraments themselves beyond the use of signification, as distinct from the blessing which belongs entirely to God alone. Such is Augustine’s statement which the scholastics misuse: “The water touches the body; it washes the heart,” and the statement which Thomas perhaps cites from a certain sermon by the same author on Epiphany. But lest anyone think I have invented this, behold the explanation of all these sayings drawn from the genuine Augustine, who says in his sixth tractate on John’s Epistle: “The Spirit is one thing; the sacrament’s visible water another. The Spirit is invisible. The water washes the body and SIGNIFIES what occurs in the soul; through the Spirit the soul itself is cleansed and nourished.”

But come, let us also hear Chemnitz writing about this in his examination of Canon 5 of the Council of Trent on baptism: “Some think that by the external Word a certain supernatural power is conferred upon the outward elements of the sacraments. This would not be God’s relation or ordinance that, through the sacraments as instrumental causes, God wills to confer grace by the Spirit’s efficacy, but a spiritual power inhering absolutely in the corporeal elements of the sacraments, through which they flow into and act upon the soul, either effectually or dispositively. Others have established that grace is contained essentially in the elements themselves, as medicine in a box. Others wish the sacraments to be merely signs and marks of Christian society by which we are distinguished from pagans and excited and bound to mutual duties. Others wish them merely to be allegories and significations of Christian death and rising. Still others say they are merely signs of grace already conferred and exhibited before and outside the use of the sacraments. Against these errors are set these testimonies, Titus 3, ‘the washing of regeneration,’ and so forth.” So he writes. Let us now weigh these points distinctly. I say that in the first two errors he rightly condemns the very dogma of Jakob Andreae—

[Part II | PDF page 120 | logical source scan 119 | printed page 118]

—with which I deal here, but not as sincerely and openly as he ought. Chemnitz, as he was accustomed to do, exposes and refutes the manifest errors of his associates by painting them over, as it were, and disguising them rather than acting as otherwise befits a learned man. He rightly and deservedly rebukes Andreae for denying that sacramental conjunction is relational. Why then did he obscure the term *relation* by the more broadly applicable term *ordinance* without explaining it? Why did he call the sacraments instrumental causes while leaving unsaid what thing and use they instrumentally serve? We showed from Augustine himself just above that this whole ordinance and instrumental causality is nothing other than the sacramental signification of the promise joined to the sacraments, represented to the minds of those approaching through the divinely ordained analogy between signs and things signified.

We ourselves detest the next two errors, even though some men with shameless slander never cease to fasten them upon us. The fifth and final error remains, in which I demonstrate from principles Chemnitz himself establishes that he errs most gravely concerning both infants and adults. He condemns—and with the best right—the doctrine of *ex opere operato*. From this it follows necessarily that faith must precede reception of the sacraments for the reception of the promised grace joined to them. He attributes this actual faith even to infants. But whoever believes in Christ becomes at that very moment a participant in Christ and his benefits unto eternal life. Therefore no one is baptized—especially no adult, who, as even the scholastics agree, must first be catechized and profess his faith—and no one approaches the sacred Supper of the Lord in order then for the first time to participate in Christ and his benefits. Rather, through the use of the sacraments the faith he already has is confirmed and increased, Christ and his benefits are sealed in him, and he thus grows more and more in Christ.

I go still further in the case of elect infants who die before they grow up. I locate their salvation in Christ from their very entrance into this life, as the first threshold to eternal life, on the authority of the Apostle who declares that they are born holy. I do so not from the work worked, nor from any actual faith implanted in them, but—as I showed more fully in its place—because they arose from parents either immediate or more remote who, through God’s grace and by true faith, apprehended Christ according to the covenant formula both for their descendants and for themselves. As Augustine shrewdly and truly declares in the passage I cited above: “The body of death in the first parents”—that is, Adam and Eve—“generated infants as sinners; but the Spirit of life in the later parents”—namely, those to whom it was given to believe for themselves and their descendants—“regenerated them as faithful.” In sum, therefore, I again say and affirm what I said before: it is manifest idolatry to attribute to the water itself or its sprinkling any power other than sacramental signification—

[Part II | PDF page 121 | logical source scan 120 | printed page 119 | clean boundary before Dogma III]

—I fear nothing from Andreae’s thunderbolts, who does not hesitate here to fasten the terms falsehood, impiety, and blasphemy upon the perfectly pure doctrine by which that incommunicable majesty and glory is preserved inviolate for God Almighty. Nevertheless, in this matter as in the rest I am prepared to submit to the judgment of truly learned and godly theologians.

To dispose once for all of every slander about the bareness of signs, let Andreae know that those signs alone are bare to which no true offering of the thing signified has been divinely joined. Of this kind are all those truly empty mystical rites devised by humans. Opposed to them are the sacraments both old and new, to which the Lord joined the promise of the gracious saving covenant, and the visions, types, and figures which we read the Lord used in order truly and effectually to signify many particular and singular things to the Fathers.

Let Andreae also know that the truth of the sacraments which is opposed to this bareness never depended upon the real existence of the things promised. For the Fathers received Christ’s true body and true blood in the Word and their sacraments just as truly, although those things did not yet exist in the nature of things, as we now receive them by the instrument of the same faith. Nor does that truth depend upon the real coexistence of the signs with the things signified in the recipients, or upon the faith or unbelief of those who approach the sacraments. It depends upon God’s ordinance, which is always true and firm, even if unbelievers receive only the signs. To them, however, those are not even signs of grace but seals of their destruction, while the sacraments themselves nevertheless retain their integrity, as we said in its place.

Finally, let Andreae know that even if some interval of time, small or great, intervenes between reception of the sacraments and the Holy Spirit’s efficacy in the hearts of some recipients, the signs are not thereby made bare. The efficacy of the promise joined to them necessarily begins from the time when it pleases God to instill it in the recipients’ hearts. Likewise, although signs are offered and received at a definite passing moment of time, as Augustine rightly writes, their power remains and extends throughout the entire life of the elect. For what Andreae either does not know or does not weigh is that it is far different for something to lose its power than for it not to exercise that power held within itself—whether because someone is unworthy of or incapable of it, or because the time for its exercise, which rests in God’s grace alone, has not yet come.
