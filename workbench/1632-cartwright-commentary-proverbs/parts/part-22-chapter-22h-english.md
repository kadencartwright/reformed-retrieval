Verse 16. He who oppresses the poor in order to increase himself, and he who gives to the rich [in order to increase his own substance]—both will certainly come to want.

The purpose is to restrain the minds of the godly from unlawful arts of becoming rich.

The Holy Spirit sets forth in this Proverb a teaching utterly

[1632 witness | PDF p. 502 | printed columns 988-989]

opposed to the judgment of the flesh; yet it is proved most true by the surest reasons and daily experience. For when people have accumulated riches obtained either by fox-like fraud, lion-like force, or any other means whatever, they judge that they have looked after themselves very well. The Prophet, however, determines that not only has nothing been added to their estate by this injustice, but something has departed and been taken away from it. For since by this course they deprive themselves of the blessing of God by which riches are obtained and of the protection by which riches already obtained are preserved, what wonder is it if they not only fail to become rich but are even reduced to poverty and beggary?

Isa. 5.8, 9, 10 teaches how little those people advance who, by oppressing the poor, join house to house and field to field. And at 1 Kings 21.12 Elijah ἐμπρακτικῶς (*in deed*) denies that Ahab will enjoy the vineyard which he had torn from Naboth's hands by force and oppression. To this also pertains what is celebrated in Solomon's Proverb: the ungodly man will not eat the prey which he has caught. But if riches obtained through injury to one's neighbour will not be profitable, and will not merely fail to profit but will actually do harm, how much less will those riches profit which are scraped together through injury against God, such as violation of the Sabbath or any other kind of impiety? See Hag. 1.4, 5, etc., and 2.16, 17, etc.

But if oppression of others so prevents people from becoming rich, it seems that no small power for acquiring riches lies in munificence and liberality. Entirely so—provided that you give to those for whom it is fitting. If you are bountiful and liberal toward God by promoting his worship, and if you open the fountains of liberality to the poor and exhausted, it will undoubtedly be a great shortcut to riches. But if you are beneficent from your own possessions to the rich, it will be just the same as if you snatched from the poor man what is his. For it is as much a fault to bestow badly the things that are yours as to draw to yourself, by force or fraud, things which belong to another. For you must remember that you are a steward, not a master who may dispense resources according to your own judgment.

To this is added a notable hypocrisy in giving to the rich. For while you pretend that the gift is gratuitous, you are in fact carrying on profitable commerce. When the rich detect this fraud, they cast aside all care of repayment. However it may be, God, who keeps watch to perform all that he has said, will grant in actual effect what he has threatened in his word.

Here it is asked whether it is altogether unlawful to confer a gift upon a rich man. To answer this question, it will be useful to remember what it is that the Holy Spirit condemns in this Proverb. He condemns not the gift itself, but the purpose of giving: that one may increase one's own substance. For this must be repeated from the former hemistich ἐπὶ τὸ κοινὸν (*as common to both*). Therefore, if someone gives something to a rich man in order to testify to his goodwill or for the sake of honour; if he does so in order to guard himself against some evil which he fears from that man, as we read that Jacob did to his brother Esau; or finally, if he does so not to become rich but to relieve his own poverty, he will by no means be arraigned as guilty of this offence.
