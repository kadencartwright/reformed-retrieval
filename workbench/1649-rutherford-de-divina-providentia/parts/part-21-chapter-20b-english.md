The basis of the major premise is in the text. It is also confirmed: every evil
inflicted because people did not receive the love of the truth is a punishment
of sin; and every evil which in actuality incurs condemnation by just desert
is sin. For it is contrary to the justice of GOD that people should be
condemned for something that is not sin. I then argue as follows. Either God
handed the Gentiles over to a reprobate mind and their shameful passions
because of idolatry, and handed those who did not receive the love of the truth
over to the efficacy of error, with the positive intention that the former
should obey GOD and the latter should believe the truth, so that both groups
might be saved; or he did so with this permissive intention: that the Gentiles
should do τὰ μὴ καθήκοντα, “the things which are improper,” and pollute
their bodies with unnatural desire, and that those who did not receive the
love of the truth should believe a lie so that both the former and the latter
might be condemned. But the former alternative is plainly absurd—namely, that
in the blinding and judicial hardening of people God intended their obedience
and salvation. Moreover, it plainly conflicts with the text. The basis of the
major premise is that GOD'S will in these ends—ποιεῖν τὰ μὴ καθήκοντα, “to do
the things which are improper”; εἰς τὸ πιστεῦσαι τῷ ψεύδει, “to believe a
lie”; and ἵνα κριθῶσι, “so that they might be condemned”—does not comport
itself purely negatively, by intending neither this nor that. I add that the
permission that the Gentiles should pollute themselves with abominable desire,
and the bare permission that those who loved the truth less should believe or
not believe a lie, is not the compensatory punishment for such crimes. Rather,
that actual pollution itself and the actual belief given to a lie is the
punishment. Finally, committing fornication is the punishment of the person
whom JEHOVAH detests, and it is also sin. The sons of Eli's refusal to listen
to their father's voice was a serious sin and the punishment of preceding

Nor do the adversaries teach the contrary. Arminius, Vorstius, and Corvinus
teach that God wills sin insofar as it is punishment, because he “willed
Pharaoh's disobedience to continue, so that his punishment might be all the
more just.” Diego Ruiz writes in *On Predestination*, disputation 38, section 3:
“In the most formal character of malice imputable as fault is founded a certain
formal character of supreme punishment, insofar as that malice is the greatest
evil and the greatest punishment. Indeed, it formally deprives one of the
highest good, which is GOD and friendship with him; it deprives one of the good
of reason, which is GOD and friendship with him, and also of virtue alone. It
therefore renders its author utterly vile and shameful, distorted from his
natural disposition. And these things are in a certain sense involuntary,
because the sinner would wish, if he could enjoy the temporal good which he
seeks by sinning, not to admit that shamefulness at the same time.” Alfonso
Curiel likewise says: “Being sin belongs to it”—namely, to sin—“in relation to
the law which it opposes, and being a stain in relation to the subject for
which it is unsuitable.” Hence nothing is clearer than that sin, insofar as it
is opposed to GOD'S law, is sin, but insofar as it is opposed to the rational
soul as an accident unsuitable and harmful to its subject, it is punishment.
Thus the sourness of wine is either related to its contrary—namely, sweetness
and an elegant taste—and is contrary to sweetness; or it is related to the
subject in which it inheres, namely, the wine, and thus is an accident harmful
and destructive to the wine. In the same way, disease is related to health as
a privation is related to a form, or to a sick person as an accident
destructive and harmful to the person. Augustine says the same thing, and
nothing is more false than Theodor Smising's claim that “Augustine did not
rebuke Julian because he acknowledged only bare permission in GOD. For what is
it,” Augustine says, “that you say, Julian: when they were handed over to their
desires, they are to be understood as abandoned through divine patience, not
driven into sins through power? As though the Apostle could not have posited
both these things together—patience and power—where he says, ‘But GOD, willing
to show his power,’ and so forth.” “Most of the fathers,” Diego Ruiz says,

evil one, just as his blindness is to a blind man.” Lombard says: “Sin, insofar

Now let us hear the arguments by which the Jesuits and Arminians contend that
sin cannot be the punishment of sin. Luis de Molina says: “When GOD permits one
person to be unjustly killed by another because of his offenses, he can in no
way will that the killer should kill him. But once the wickedness of the unjust
killer has been posited, God can will that the person killed should suffer such
a death because of his crimes, and for that reason can permit the evil action.”
Ruiz says the same: “We can gather that the formal character of punishment in
the malice of sin is caused by God, not because he caused the malice, but
because, once the malice caused or to be caused by free will and the action
itself is presupposed, God most justly wills that this malice should be an
utterly shameful, sordid, and grievous punishment to the sinner himself.”

[Marginal citation: Molina, *On Concord*, question 14, article 13, disputation
34.]

[Marginal citation: Ruiz, *On the Will of God*, disputation 43, section 6.]

[Marginal summary: The objections of Molina and Ruiz are removed.]

I answer: 1. If GOD takes pleasure in an unjust killing as a just punishment
after that killing has been committed, then God can will that the killing be
committed as the just punishment of the person killed. If a judge wills a just
punishment already imposed, why, I ask, can he not will it to be imposed before
it is imposed? For a punishment already imposed is no less incompatible with a
holy will than a punishment yet to be imposed. Next, if, once the unjust
killing has been committed, God wills the person killed to suffer it, he wills
that killing as existing, though he did not will it to exist—as if an existing
unjust killing ceased to be unjust because it exists, and would not also have
been unjust while yet to exist. In this way they say that GOD wills sinners to
be punished after they have already been punished by sin, but that GOD in no
way wills them to be punished by sin before they are punished by sin. This
conflicts with sober reason and with all GOD'S threats in which he threatens to
punish sin with sin. 3. Nor do we teach that GOD causes the malice in sin, as
Ruiz imputes to us. We teach only that GOD permissively wills sin to exist
insofar as it is the punishment of another sin, or insofar as it is its own
punishment, although here we deny an idle non-action of the divine will.

[Marginal citation: Ruiz, *On the Will of God*, disputation 26, section 5.]

2. Ruiz objects: “GOD wills the entire-*

[Chapter XX; printed page 277; IA PDF page 337; BSB canvas 329]

*entire essence of sin and its properties to belong to sin on the supposition
that it is sin. Therefore this proposition may be granted: ‘God wills sin
insofar as it is another sin's punishment.’ But this proposition is false:
‘God wills sin so that it may be another sin's punishment,’ because God does
not will sin for this end, but only wills to permit it, intending it to be the
punishment of sin.”

[Marginal summary: God wills all the penal qualities in sin both while the sin
exists and before it exists.]

I answer: we require nothing other than that GOD wills sin insofar as it is the
punishment of sin. Let the Jesuit see how we make GOD the author of sin, since
this and nothing else is what we teach. Nor do we speak in this way: that GOD
“wills sin to exist so that it may be punishment”—as though sin were a means to
punishment as its end. For since sin is essentially punishment, God no more
wills sin to exist so that it may be punishment than he wills a human being to
exist so that he may be a rational animal.

2. The Jesuit absurdly says that “GOD most justly wills sin, once caused by the
will, to be a punishment to itself.” For nothing essential belongs to a thing
through the intervention of GOD'S free decree, nor does sin receive from GOD'S
free decree the fact that it is a harmful and penal evil for a rational
creature. The one exception is that punishment has its relation to guilt as
deserved through GOD'S free will. For by the very fact that this is essentially
sin, without the intervention of any free decree of GOD, it is harmful and
penal to a rational creature, and it also removes the honorable good by
privation. 2. It makes the creature base. 3. It leaves a stain. 4. It deprives
the creature of friendship with GOD. 5. It adds liability to punishment. But
all five of these penal qualities belong to sin in themselves.

3. Ruiz objects: “God can in no way will an inward work that is intrinsically
evil, such as blasphemies and lies, but he does will an outward work insofar as
it is punishment. God does not will the inward volition, but only permits it.
Nevertheless, presupposing some will inclined toward evil, he uses it as an
instrument for the aforesaid outward work insofar as it is a punishment to be
inflicted by God's just judgment—for example, that Christ be killed and Paul
suffer—because these things contain no incompatibility with right reason. God
can will them for the highest ends.”

[Marginal citation: Ruiz, *On the Will of God*, disputation 33, section 2.]

[Marginal summary: God cannot will sins which are intrinsically sins; yet, if
we believe Ruiz, he wills sins that are intrinsically sins.]

I answer: we therefore have an accused man confessing that a sinful outward
work is the punishment of sin and is also sin. 2. If GOD uses the will of a
sinning person to perpetrate an outward sin, Calvin and Beza are wrongly
condemned, since they teach the same thing. 3. Inward works that are
intrinsically evil are just as capable of being ordered to GOD'S glory, are
just as penal, and consist just as much of an entitative act and malice as do
outwardly evil sins. Therefore God can will both the former, insofar as they are
punishments of sinners, and the latter. And it is just as incompatible for GOD
to be the author of sin in outward things as in inward things and those which
are intrinsically sins.-*

I answer: 1. He who can efficaciously determine the will of the devil and the
Jews to crucify the Savior rather than Barabbas determines people to sin
simply, because crucifying Barabbas, a notorious robber, is not a sin, whereas
crucifying the innocent Son of GOD is a most serious sin. And because the
positive degree is most properly inferred from the comparative degree, if God
wills the devil and wicked people to perpetrate this sin rather than that one,
then God wills the devil and wicked people to perpetrate this sin. 2. For the
Jesuits, the will of good pleasure and the will of the sign are the same with
respect to external objects willed. God says: “By my will of the sign, Pilate, I
neither will nor ever decreed that you should condemn my Son to death rather
than the robber Barabbas. Yet by my will of good pleasure I positively and
efficaciously decreed—indeed, determined—that you should condemn my Son to death
rather than the robber Barabbas.” Let the Manichaeans, Florentines, and
Libertines hear and prick up their ears at this. Calvin, Beza, and our divines
neither thought nor wrote any such thing about the holy majesty of GOD.

*inflicted by GOD as supreme judge and offended Lord has the character of

it cannot be an object of vindicatory justice.”

I answer: 1. Simply to suffer evil as such is not punishable when the one who
suffers is not an agent either by habit or by act; this is indeed true. And
from this it is proved that suffering evil, or the evil of punishment, is not
formally the evil of guilt. That is not against us. But it is false to say that
suffering penal evil in such a way that the sufferer is at the same time an
agent in another respect—whether by habit, a depraved inclination, or an
act—cannot be evil. For in different respects it is both good and evil. 2. I
acknowledge as true what Aristotle says, that “every punishment is inflicted by
GOD as just Judge”: either by a positive action, as happens in those things
which are simply punishments, or by a permissive action, as happens in those
actions which are both sins and punishments in different respects, as stated
above. They are sins in relation to their terminus—namely, insofar as they are
against GOD'S law—and punishments in relation to the subject in which they
inhere, as accidents harmful and unsuitable to the rational creature in which
they exist. And so the Jesuit begs the question.

[Marginal summary: Insofar as sin and injury are inflicted upon God, it can be,
and is, both vengeance and injury.]

3. “No just judge avenges a transgression and injury done to himself by another
transgression or injury done and committed by himself.” This is true, but the
minor premise is false. God does not avenge sin by another sin perpetrated by
himself; this is indeed true, because God perpetrates no sin either in himself
or in another. But as for the claim that no judge avenges an injury done to
himself by another injury which he permits someone else to commit: in this form
the major premise is false, and still begs the question. I acknowledge that it
is true of an earthly judge: he cannot avenge an injury done to himself by
permitting another injury which he is obligated to prevent. Eli could not
punish the sins of his sons by permitting them to perpetrate other sins which
Eli was obligated to prevent. But God is not bound by such a law, because God
can punish Eli's sons by permitting them to perpetrate other sins, since God
has ordained that every sin should be a harmful, unsuitable, and penal evil to
the sinning author. 4. I also distinguish the statement: “Sin cannot please GOD
as a good, but punishment pleases GOD as the good of vindicatory justice.” For
there is a twofold good and a twofold pleasure. Sin cannot please GOD as an
honorable and moral good by the approving pleasure of the will of the sign.
This is entirely true.-*

[Chapter XX; printed page 280; IA PDF page 340; BSB canvas 332]

*But nothing follows against us, because sin insofar as it is sin and in
relation to GOD'S law is neither punishment nor good, but a dishonorable evil
and an object of divine hatred and displeasure, not of love and pleasure. But
the statement that “sin cannot please GOD as a good”—as a conducive and useful
good and the good of justice, harmful and unsuitable to the sinner, and indeed
by the permissive pleasure of the will of good pleasure—is, I say, entirely
false. For by the very fact that sin is the worst evil in the class of
dishonorable evils, it is a useful good and an object of vindicatory justice,
because it is harmful and penal to the sinner. And it is just, good, and
equitable that sin should be penal to the sinner.

6. They object: “It belongs to the nature of punishment,” Maeratius says, “that
it be evil to the one punished; but sin is not evil to the person punished, but
pleasant and useful. Therefore sin is not punishment.”

[Marginal summary: In what sense punishment is something evil to everyone
punished.]

I answer by distinguishing the major premise. It belongs to the nature of
punishment that it be evil to the person punished either in itself and in first
act, or as the person punished thus judges it and in second act. True; but in
this latter sense the major premise is not universally true, but is a mere
begging of the question. For a dishonorable evil is by this very fact a more
serious penal evil to the sinner when he does not perceive it to be punishment
but instead esteems it useful and pleasant. Thus a physician permits a sick man
to drink wine as the punishment of his intemperance, though drinking the wine
is exceedingly pleasant to him and at the same time a serious punishment, even
if he does not perceive it to be punishment. So too, in Hosea 4:14, GOD
announces in express words that his not punishing the fornications of the
Israelites' daughters is a punishment for their fornications. Yet not to be
punished by GOD is above all pleasant, just as being punished is unpleasant and
unwelcome.

essence of punishment that it harm the agent in himself, that is, in those
things which pertain to the integrity of the supposit, or that it injure the
agent's essence and good disposition. But it belongs to the nature of sin that
it injure the human being, not in his essence-*

*and in his good disposition as a supposit, but in his moral operations, which sin
perverts and deprives of moral rectitude. 3. Punishment consists in undergoing
something unsuitable, whereas sin consists only in the moral defects of our
actions. Therefore guilt and punishment are formally distinguished, and
consequently guilt according to its formal character cannot be punishment.”
James Arminius argues the same, but far more meagerly and with less force than

[Marginal citation: Arminius, *Against Perkins*, p. 728.]

I answer: the conclusion is not against us, for we teach that sin and
punishment are different quidditative concepts, and that the formal character
of sin is one thing while that of punishment is another. Sin is formally the
transgression of divine law, whereas punishment is not formally the
transgression of divine law, but a harmful and penal evil to a rational
creature because of the transgression of divine law. Guilt therefore is not
formally punishment in the way that a human being is a rational animal. But our
thesis is established if sin is both sin and punishment and if it is
permissively willed by GOD insofar as it is sin, which the adversaries deny.

[Marginal summary: In what sense sin and punishment are different quidditative
concepts, and sin and punishment differ.]

2. The Jesuits' arguments tend toward the claim that punishment is essentially
involuntary. But I set Jesuits against Jesuits. Thus Becanus says: “Although
punishment is sometimes not contrary to the will, it is nevertheless contrary
to nature.” And in the same place: “It is not necessary for punishment to be
the kind of thing which the will actually resists. It is enough that it be the
kind of thing which the will would resist if it judged and weighed it
correctly.” Gregory of Valencia says: “It is enough that punishment by its own
nature oppose a person's inclination and so be involuntary in first act.” And
Vásquez says: “The act of sin, insofar as it is delightful and useful, moves the
will directly, and thus sin is voluntary. But insofar as it is evil with the
malice of guilt, it does not entice the will, and so is involuntary and can be
punishment.” Therefore Arminius's agreement with the Jesuits is empty when he
says: “Sin is action; punishment is passion. Sin is voluntary; punishment is
involuntary and always inflicted on someone unwilling. Therefore sin is not
punishment.” For first, original concupiscence, the vicious tendencies of the
soul's inclinations against the law of nature, and habitual sin are sins, but
they are not actions. 2. The Gibeonites' carrying water and cutting wood are
actions and yet are also punishments, as Dr. Pierre Du Moulin correctly
observes. 3. Samson, while sleeping, suffers punishment through the shaving of
his hair; and after many people have died, their family and offspring are
destroyed root and branch as the punishment which GOD had threatened them with
while they were alive. Yet with Samson-*

[Marginal citation: Vásquez, *On 1–2*, volume 1, disputation 40, chapter 1, no.
2.]

*asleep, and with the dead, neither the voluntary nor the involuntary has a place. A
drunkard overwhelmed by deep sleep is carried to prison as punishment, though
he neither feels it nor suffers it unwillingly. Hence, contrary to sense and
reason, conscious awareness of punishment is most vainly made part of the
essence of punishment. 3. Punishment is indeed contrary to the will in first
act, and so sin insofar as it is punishment—or the penal qualities in sin—is
contrary to the will. Thus (1) the stain of sin, (2) aversion from the highest
good, (3) deprivation of friendship with GOD, (4) the baseness resulting to the
creature from sin, and (5) liability or obligation to eternal punishments are
all penal qualities and true passions contrary to the will in first act and
involuntary to the sinner. For the sinner, as Ruiz said above, would wish both
to enjoy the temporal good which he seeks by sinning and to be released and
freed from all these penal qualities in sin. 4. The Jesuit's distinction and
begging of the question is likewise empty when he maintains that sin harms the
integrity and good disposition of the supposit, while punishment harms the
supposit's second acts and operations. For sin also harms the integrity of the
supposit, since, as the adversaries teach, it removed the bridle of original
justice, disturbs the integrity and harmony of the rational soul with GOD as
its final end and the union of man with his sole blessedness, and topples
reason from its throne.

8. Maeratius and Vásquez argue as follows: “Every punishment can be honorably
loved and efficaciously sought by a just judge, because punishing is an act of
virtue and vindicatory justice, whose object is a good. But sin cannot be
honorably loved by a just judge. Therefore sin is not punishment.”

[Marginal summary: The arguments of Lodovico Maeratius and Gabriel Vásquez are
overturned.]

[Marginal attribution: Maeratius.]

I answer: the disputed negation is not concluded, because only this follows:
therefore sin insofar as it is a transgression of divine law cannot be
honorably loved. We do not deny this. But the following does not follow:
“therefore sin according to its being harmful and as an unsuitable penalty to
the sinner himself cannot be honorably loved.” For GOD'S will does not
terminate upon sin insofar as it is sin, but upon sin insofar as it is
punishment, or upon the penal quality harmful to the sinner which adheres to
every sin.

9. Maeratius argues as follows: “It belongs to the nature of punishment that it
repair the honor taken away from the one sinned against. But it does not belong
to the nature of sin to repair GOD'S honor, but to take GOD'S honor away.”
Arminius argues thus: “Through punishment sin is brought into order; but through
sin, sin is not brought into order but is permitted to rage in a disorderly
fashion. Therefore sin-*

[Marginal attribution: Maeratius.]

[Marginal citation: Arminius, *Against Perkins*, p. 728.]

[Chapter XX; printed page 283; IA PDF page 343; BSB canvas 335]

*is not punishment.” I answer: 1. The disputed negation is still not established. For
it is true that sin and punishment have different concepts, just as a human
being and the capacity for laughter have different formal concepts. Yet it is
by no means thereby established that a human being is not capable of laughter;
neither, therefore, is it concluded from this that sin is not punishment. 2.
The repair of the honor of the person sinned against belongs to the nature of
punishing rather than to the nature of punishment. 3. The minor premise is
false. Although sin takes away the honor of active obedience owed to GOD,
nevertheless through sin insofar as it is a harmful penalty to the sinner, the
honor of vindicatory justice is repaired. For the glory of divine justice
shines forth in this: that sin is a harmful and penal evil to the sinner
himself. Sin therefore does not rage in such a way that it is not brought into
the order of justice, because it is a serious punishment to the sinner. 4.
Against Scripture and the torrent of the doctors, this argument would prove
that original sin is not punishment.

10. I know that Scotus's statement is not unlearned: “Sin, insofar as it flows
actively from the will, is sin; but insofar as it is in the will as in a
subject, it is involuntary and the punishment of sin.” Yet I do not know whether
Vásquez and Alfonso Curiel soundly attack Scotus as follows: “Malice,” they say,
“insofar as it exists subjectively in the soul, is malice and formally
denominates the sinner unjust, for a subject is denominated such by the form
inhering in it. Therefore, if GOD is the author of sin as punishment, he will
be the author of malice insofar as it is passively received in the soul, and
GOD will be the cause why the will or soul is formally evil through the malice
of sin. Thus GOD will be the cause why malice actively flows from the soul, for
the reception of malice in the soul has an intrinsic dependence upon the
active procession of malice from the soul.”

[Marginal citation: Scotus, book 2, distinction 37, sole question, reply to
question 3.]

[Marginal citation: Vásquez; Curiel, *On 1–2*, question 87, article 2, sole
doubt, § 3.]

I answer: 1. Although the malice of sin, insofar as it inheres subjectively in
the soul, is malice, sin, and moral evil which denominates the soul morally
evil, nevertheless GOD is not the cause why the soul is evil through the
malice of sin, nor is GOD the cause why malice actively flows from the soul.
Although GOD wills sin to be harmful and penal to the sinner, since it accords
with divine justice that sin be penal and harmful to the sinner, GOD is neither
the cause of malice's inherence in the soul nor the cause of its active
emanation from the soul. 2. GOD can positively will sin as punishment, or
rather, can will the penal quality in sin, and nevertheless in no way will sin,
the commission of sin, its reception, or its subjective inherence in the soul.
The formal cause of whiteness's inherence-*

[Chapter XX; printed page 284; IA PDF page 344; BSB canvas 336]

*in milk is whiteness itself; the cause of malice's inherence in the soul is
malice itself, not God, who hates it.

11. André Duval says: “Four things concur in the punishment of sin. 1. That it
be a privation of good. 2. That it be in some way from an extrinsic agent.
Afflictions voluntarily undertaken to make satisfaction to GOD for sins are
not properly punishments. 3. That it be inflicted because of sin. If it is
inflicted for testing, it is medicine, not punishment. 4. That it be simply
involuntary. Sin, insofar as it is received in the will and has deformity
annexed to it, is at least accidentally and interpretatively voluntary. It is
the punishment of sin only accidentally, and in two ways: (1) because it is the
cause of the weakness and corruption of nature and of depraved habits; and (2)
because it is the cause of the withdrawal of grace.”

[Marginal citation: André Duval, on the *Secunda Secundae* of Thomas, treatise
*On Sin*, question 5, article 3.]

[Marginal summary: The evasions of Duval, Becanus, and Philippe de Gamaches are
removed.]

I answer: 1. According to the first condition, because sin is a privation of
the honorable good, it is punishment. 2. It is not necessary for every
punishment to be from an extrinsic agent. The despair of the damned is sin and
punishment properly so called; and here too GOD is the extrinsic inflictor. And
according to the second condition, all punishments voluntarily undertaken
cease to be satisfactions for sins, because they are not even punishments.
Being involuntary is not the essence of punishment but its second act, and
belongs only to the punishment of sense, not the punishment of loss. Indeed,
the more spiritual the will is, the more penal is what is inflicted upon it;
and the more something has the character of evil—that is, of the voluntary—the
more it has the character of punishment. 3. Sin is punishment not only
accidentally but also in itself. 4. Pharaoh's sin of refusing, at the eighth
miracle, to release GOD'S people is a punishment inflicted because of the
preceding hardening and obstinacy in him. It was inflicted by GOD in the manner
in which the punishment adhering to sin is inflicted, that is, permissively.

12. Philippe de Gamaches says: “Punishment is later than sin. Therefore sin is
not punishment.”

[Marginal citation: Philippe de Gamaches, Doctor of the Sorbonne, on Thomas's
*Summa*, 1–2, question 87, chapter 2.]

I answer: the consequence is diseased. The capacity for laughter is by nature
later than a human being. Does it follow, therefore, that a human being is not
capable of laughter?

13. Becanus argues as follows: “A sinner can be compelled to punishment, but a
sinner cannot be compelled to guilt. Therefore sin is not punishment.”

[Marginal citation: Becanus, *Theology*, part 2, treatise 2, chapter 8,
question 4.]

I answer: the question is always begged. A sinner cannot be compelled to every
punishment, but only to one that is purely and simply punishment, not to one
which has both guilt and the character of punishment-*

[Chapter XX; printed page 285; IA PDF page 345; BSB canvas 337]

*in it. 2. How a person is an involuntary sufferer with respect to the penal quality
in sin was explained above.

14. Thus reads a Jesuit manuscript which I saw at Aberdeen in the possession
of Dr. Robert Baron: “A father has two sons. Although he loves both, he
nevertheless loves the younger more, and therefore readily permits the
firstborn to go to sea or to war—not indeed with the direct intention that the
inheritance should pass to the second son, but because, on account of the
affection he has for the younger, he does not judge it so grave an evil if the
firstborn should happen to die. Therefore, by an oblique and indirect will, on
account of his affection for the glory of mercy and justice in CHRIST, he wills
the sin by which punishment is merited.” But it is also impious to fashion in
God intentions and affections which are suspended and wavering. I therefore
answer: according to this position, God is imagined to have an indirect
affection and will toward the glory of punishing justice and of mercy in CHRIST
the Mediator to be incarnate, and consequently toward sin, just as “a father
permits his firstborn to go to sea or to war,” not caring greatly and indeed
half-willing that he should die so that the inheritance may pass to the younger
son. For “the greater good,” the Jesuit says in the same place, “causes the
firstborn's death to appear a lesser evil, because it is reparable with a
greater gain, namely, with the inheritance passing to the younger son whom the
father loves more intensely and more earnestly desires to be his heir.” It
follows that the existence of sin, which is the intrinsic matter of punishing
justice, appears to God a greater good than the nonexistence of sin. Yet the
other Jesuits will not say this, because a greater good which God wills to
exist rather than a lesser good is, here and now, simply eligible to God. But
according to the adversaries' doctrine, even sin as punishment is not more
eligible to GOD than obedience.

[Marginal citation: Manuscript of a Jesuit, treatise 3, *On Predestination*.]

The manuscript's author also adds: “It is praiseworthy to permit lesser evils
in order to avoid graver ones. Thus it is permissible to counsel theft in
order to avoid homicide, as Sánchez says. In the same way, forest guards can
hide themselves and deliberately give robbers an opportunity to enter and
steal, so that, once caught on this occasion, they may repent and abstain from
robbery in the future. And so God can have an affection toward sin, not simply,
but from hatred of a greater sin; and this argues not affection but hatred of
the greater sin. Hence it is inferred that some can piously ask God for the
suffering of martyrdom, which nevertheless is not obtained apart from the sins
of tyrants. But we cannot ask anything from God except what he himself can will
and in the manner in which he can will it.”

[Marginal citation: Sánchez, *On Marriage*, book 7, disputation n., article
15.]

[Chapter XX; printed page 286; IA PDF page 346; BSB canvas 338]

I answer: 1. It is the impious dogma of certain casuists that in some case it is
permissible to counsel sin, for counseling sin is itself sin. But we are never
permitted to sin for the sake of an end or outcome, however supremely good it
might be—even the eternal salvation of all human beings and demons. For it is
never permissible to do evil for the sake of good. Romans 3:8. 2. I deny that
forest guards can licitly permit a theft which they are obligated to prevent
for the sake of an uncertain end—namely, the robbers' repentance. For repentance
is a singular gift of GOD given by CHRIST alone. Acts 5:31. God indeed can
permit sin for the sake of a good far more excellent than the sin itself is
evil, because GOD is here above the law. But Eli is not authorized by moral
permission to allow his sons to perpetrate even the smallest sins for the sake
of the most eminent good. And certainly, if God can will a lesser sin for the
sake of avoiding a greater sin, then God can will the greatest sin for the sake
of a good which is far more eminent than the avoidance of a greater sin—if,
namely, the end for which he permits sin is GOD'S glory and the redemption of
the human race. 3. It is not permissible to ask for martyrdom absolutely, but
only conditionally and with godly cautions employed.

[Marginal concession: The Jesuits admit that God can will sin for the sake of
a far more eminent good.]

What will the Romanists say to Pedro de Ledesma, professor at Salamanca:
“There is no absurdity,” he says, “in GOD'S causing
sin—not indeed insofar as it is sin, but insofar as it assumes a certain
character of punishment—and predetermining it without causing the very
character of sin.”

[Marginal citation: Ledesma, *On the Divine Helps of Grace*, p. 396.]

This is what Isidore said: “As the punishment of sin, sin is committed when,
according to the desert of each sin and with GOD abandoning the sinner, one
goes into another sin, by which the one who has committed it is further
defiled.”

[Marginal citation: Isidore, *On the Highest Good*, book 3, chapter 29.]

“The interpretation therefore stands,” Pererius says, “that Absalom's rebellion,
insofar as it was his action, was undoubtedly sin and was not from GOD; but
insofar as it was David's suffering and punishment, it was intended and willed
by GOD, who justly willed David to suffer such things because of his adultery.”
But the distinction has no place in hardening.

[Marginal citation: Pererius on Exodus, chapter 11, disputation 4, no. 28.]

And Cajetan acknowledges and confesses that that curse against David, insofar
as it was penal to him, proceeded from GOD because of his sins. “GOD said,
‘Curse David’—not by a word, but by the deed of bringing about Shimei's curse,
not insofar as it was sin, but insofar as it was David's punishment,” as he had
said by Nathan's mouth, “I will raise up evil from your house.”

[Marginal citation: Cajetan on 2 Samuel 16:10.]
