QUESTION II.

*Is Christ mediator according to both natures? We affirm against the Roman
Catholics and Stancarus.*

I. We have this question with the Roman Catholics. To establish more easily
that there can be several mediators, they contend that Christ was mediator
according to the human nature alone, as Lombard, *Book 3, Distinction 19,
Question 1,* Thomas, Part 3, Question 26, Article 2, Bellarmine, *Controversy
1, On Christ,* Book 5, chapter 3, Becanus, *Manual,* Book 3, chapter 2, and
others assert. Stancarus follows them here. While he opposed more vehemently
his colleague Osiander in the Academy of Königsberg, who maintained that
Christ is mediator only according to the divine nature and therefore that we
are justified by his eternal and essential righteousness, Stancarus did not
sufficiently distinguish Christ’s efficacy from his merit. He fell into the
opposite error of the Roman Catholics by affirming that Christ is mediator
only according to the human nature.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question is not whether the mediator’s person is divine, for the
adversaries do not deny this. Rather, it is whether the mediator’s actions or
passions proceeded from that person according to the human nature alone, or

[Printed p. 335 | Internet Archive PDF 363 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

according to both. The Roman Catholics distinguish between the *principle
which* and the *principle by which,* or the formal principle. They say that
the former is the supposit or person, not the nature, but they wish the latter
to be the human nature alone. “For although it was God,” says Bellarmine, Book
1, “who suffered, obeyed, and made satisfaction, nevertheless he did all these
things according to the form of a servant, not according to the form of God.”

III. Four things must be accurately distinguished here concerning Christ’s
actions, as Damascene observed in *On the Orthodox Faith,* Book 4, chapter 18.
1. There is *the one acting* (ὁ ἐνεργῶν), the agent or the principle which
acts, namely the supposit or person of Christ. 2. There is *that which is
operative* (Τὸ ἐνεργητικόν), or the formal principle by which he acts—that
through which the agent or person of Christ operates—namely the two natures,
each of which operates without any confusion. 3. There is the *operation*
(Ἐνέργεια), which depends upon the principle by which and reflects the nature
of its principle, so that it is divine if the principle by which is the divine
nature, but human if it is the humanity. 4. There is the *thing effected*
(Ἐνέργημα), or result (ἀποτέλεσμα), which depends upon the principle which and
is the external work that we call mediation. Thus there is a twofold principle
of mediation, common and proper. The common principle is one, namely the
mediator’s person, but the proper principle is twofold, namely the divine and
human natures: the divine nature is the proper and formal principle of divine
actions, and the human nature of human actions. Yet the proper actions should
not be attributed to the formal principles or natures as though they acted
separately. Each form acts in communion with the other what is proper to it:
the Word working what belongs to the Word and the flesh carrying out what
belongs to the flesh, as Leo says in *Letter 10 to Flavian.* Thus there is one
principal agent, namely Christ’s person, and one result (ἀποτέλεσμα), or
mediatorial work. But he operates through the two natures as two principles,
from which flow two operations (ἐνέργειαι) concurring toward that one work.

IV. Again, Christ’s actions can be considered in three orders, according as
Christ can be regarded under a threefold relation (σχέσει): either as God, as
man, or as the God-man (θεάνθρωπος). Some are purely divine, which he performs
only as God, such as creation and preservation. Others are purely human, such
as eating, walking, and sleeping. Others are mixed and called *theandric*
(θεανδρικαί), such as redemption, for whose accomplishment both the divine and
human natures had their own parts. Thus the question is to which class of
these actions mediation belongs: to truly human actions, as the Roman
Catholics would have it, or to theandric actions (θεανδρικάς), as we assert.

[Marginal heading: Christ is proved to be mediator according to both natures.
1. From Acts 20:28;]

V. The reasons are: First, because Scripture refers Christ’s mediation to
both natures. In Acts 20:28, “God” is said “to have acquired the church by his
own blood”; in 1 Corinthians 2:8, “the Lord of glory” is said to have been
crucified; and in Hebrews 9:14, to have “offered himself through the eternal
Spirit.” These things pertain not only to indicating the majesty of the person
but also the efficacy of the sacrifice, which flows from the divinity. For
although they are explained through the communication of properties, they
nevertheless also signify that the divinity had its own parts no less than the
humanity, each nature contributing what is its own: the human nature the
substance of the work or passion, but the divine nature its infinite value and
price.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the parts of Christ’s mediation:]

[Marginal heading: 1. Prophecy;]

VI. Second, all the parts of the mediatorial office require both natures.
According to whatever nature Christ is prophet, priest, and king, according
to the same he is mediator. But he could and had to exercise that threefold
office according to both natures. This is clear first concerning prophecy, for
as prophet he had to reveal the Father to us and disclose his will. But who
could reveal the Father to us except the Son, who alone knows him?

[Printed p. 336 | Internet Archive PDF 364 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Matthew 11:27. Who could recount his will and disclose the divine wisdom hidden
in mystery except the one who is in the Father’s bosom, John 1:18, and is the
Word (Λόγος) and eternal wisdom of God? Human beings indeed interpret the
divine will and report it to us, but only ministerially. Yet to act as the
supreme and unaccountable (ἀνυπεύθυνον) interpreter of heavenly things, and to
do so not only by outward persuasion and preaching but by inward illumination
and persuasion, belongs to God, not to man. Again, as prophet he must inscribe
the law upon hearts and teach so efficaciously that he gives a new heart and
opens minds to understand the Scriptures, so that believers are truly taught
by God (θεοδίδακτοι), John 6:45. But he could not do this as man, but as God,
to whom alone it belongs much more to write upon the heart than upon stone.

[Marginal heading: The priesthood;]

VII. As priest he had to make perfect satisfaction for us to divine justice
and intercede for sinners. By his death he had to abolish death and him who
held the dominion of death, and to reconcile us who were alienated from God
and unite us to him forever. But who except God could oppose an infinite merit
to the infinite demerit of sins and pay to God’s justice a counter-ransom
(ἀντίλυτρον) of infinite value? Who except God could bear the unbearable
(ἀβάστακτον) weight of wrath without being swallowed up by it? Who except God
had the power to lay down his life by a voluntary death and take it up again
by resurrection, John 10:18? Who except God could offer himself through the
eternal Spirit, fulfilling at once the parts of priest and victim, Hebrews
9:14; conquer death by his own death; vanquish the devil; obtain eternal
redemption by his blood; and save his own forever, Hebrews 7:25?

[Marginal heading: And the kingdom;]

VIII. As king he had to govern the universal church with the scepter of his
word; send the Holy Spirit with all his gifts; call efficaciously; open hearts;
crush the devil; place all the enemies of salvation as a footstool under his
feet; and glorify the church. But who could have done this except one endowed
with invincible power, that is, true God? Therefore, in whatever way Christ
the mediator is considered, he had to discharge this office according to the
divine nature no less than the human.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the emptying;]

IX. Third, he had to be mediator according to that nature according to which
he emptied himself and assumed the form of a servant, because he emptied
himself for no other reason than to discharge this office. But he emptied
himself according to the divine nature, not by a real diminution of essential
glory but by an outward concealment of accidental glory.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the mediation of the Old Testament;]

X. Fourth, he had to be mediator according to the nature according to which
he could already operate as mediator from the beginning of the world, since
he had to be the same yesterday, today, and forever. But he could not operate
according to the human nature, but only according to the divine. According to
the divine nature, as he already existed, so he could govern his church and
enrich it with all gifts necessary for salvation.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the effects of mediation.]

XI. Fifth, because the principal effects of Christ’s mediation pertain
equally to the divine nature and the human, such as redemption and salvation,
the remission of sins, and the gift of eternal life, which are such that they
are the works and benefits of God alone. Nor should it be said that these
actions depend upon Christ the man as upon an instrument, but upon the Trinity
as the principal cause. For Christ possesses not only the character of an
instrument—otherwise he would accomplish nothing more than ministers, to whom
such works are sometimes attributed—but also of a principal cause, both
meritorious and efficient. Hence Augustine says in his *Discourse on the
Sheep:* “The divinity without the humanity is not mediatrix, nor is the
humanity without the divinity; but between divinity and humanity alone, the
human divinity and divine humanity is mediatrix.”

IX. [sic; XII.] Sixth, because the incarnation took place with the properties unconfused,
and so it is with the actions no less than with the natures.

[Printed p. 337 | Internet Archive PDF 365 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Therefore, the actions pertaining to mediation must be ascribed to the
divinity as their formal principle. For Christ was incarnate in order to be
mediator, and it is absurd for Christ’s divinity to have withheld itself from
the work for which Christ was especially sent. And if it exerted itself in
miracles, it shone forth much more in the work of redemption, which is more
illustrious than every miracle. Nor did he shine with so many miracles for any
other reason than thereby to prove himself the true Savior.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XIII. When Christ the man is said to be mediator in 1 Timothy 2:5, it is not
proved that he is mediator only according to the human nature. 1. Because the
exclusive expression *as man alone* is not added. 2. This is added by way of
explanation, not to exclude the divinity but to include the humanity, so that
we may approach him with greater confidence, since he is a participant with
us in the same nature, and because Paul was immediately about to speak of his
being delivered up to death. 3. The Roman Catholic argument concludes nothing
unless it is supposed that wherever the concrete name *God* or *man* occurs in
Scripture as attributed to Christ, his divine or human nature is designated
there. This cannot be said, since a concrete name denotes the person, not the
nature. 4. The name *God* placed here is either taken personally to designate
the Father economically, between whom and human beings Christ is mediator; or,
if taken more broadly for the whole Trinity, the divine nature is nevertheless
not excluded from mediation. For it is one thing for Christ to be mediator
according to the divine nature considered absolutely, insofar as it is common
to the three persons; another according to that nature viewed economically by
reason of his voluntary emptying.

XIV. There is no absurdity in the injured party voluntarily becoming a
mediatrix under a different relation. Christ presents himself as such to us
in the parable, Matthew 21:37, and wills that his own do this, Matthew 5:24 and
18:16. Nor is it unusual for the offended party to institute reconciliation
among the offenders, as God did among our first parents, Genesis 3 and 2
Corinthians 5:18–20; the Israelites among the Benjamites, Judges 21; and David
among his rebellious subjects, 2 Samuel 19:12. So too a senator may plead the
case of a defendant who must be reconciled to the senate, as Menenius Agrippa
once did between the senate and the Roman people. Hence nothing prevents
Christ, economically considered as the God-man (θεάνθρωπον), from being
mediator to himself insofar as he is God essentially (οὐσιωδῶς). For although
God the Son, or the Word (Λόγος), does not differ from Christ the mediator in
either person or nature considered in themselves and absolutely, he does
differ in economy and the relation of the mediatorial office. It is one thing
to be God the Son and another to be mediator; one thing to be offended as God
and another to act as mediator, the God-man (θεάνθρωπον). Thus mediation will
always be toward another: both because the Son and Word differs from the
God-man (θεανθρώπῳ), if not by nature (φύσει), at least by economy (οἰκονομίᾳ),
and because the Son differs from the Father, before whom he is appropriately
said to be mediator. This is first personal, because he is a person other than
the Father; and second dispensational, because the Son alone, not the Father,
became man and emptied himself.

XV. Although the mediatorial office belongs to Christ according to the divine
nature, it does not follow that the Father and Holy Spirit are equally
mediators with the Son. This is because mediation belongs to the nature not
insofar as it is common to the persons, but insofar as it is characterized and
subsists distinctly in the person of the Son. External operations indeed are
common to the whole Trinity and to all the persons insofar as they possess the
character of one common principle. Nevertheless, the person in whom a certain
operation terminates does not cease to possess the character of a singular
principle. This is clear from the incarnation, which, although it belongs
originatively to the whole Trinity, nevertheless terminates in the Son alone,
from whom as its terminus this work is named. The same must be said of
mediation.

XVI. The unity of the result indeed argues for the unity of the principle
which, but not always of the principle by which. For the same work can arise
from various formal principles, but in one respect and another (κατ’ ἄλλο καὶ
ἄλλο). For example, speech is one work produced by a human being as its common
principle, yet it has two formal principles, the body and the soul. Thus here
there are distinct operations (ἐνέργειαι), but one thing effected (ἐνέργημα).
Hence you would wrongly infer that a confusion of operations, and consequently
of natures, follows.

XVII. Although the task of a priest and intercessor is to minister, it does
not follow that the divine nature in Christ is made inferior to the Father.
It follows only that Christ became less than the Father not by nature (φύσει)
but by economy (οἰκονομίᾳ), not absolutely and in himself but by reason of the
voluntary dispensation through which he willingly emptied himself.

XVIII. Although to lay down one’s life passively is to die, and to take it up
is to rise again, and both are rightly said of the human nature, nevertheless,
if these phrases are taken actively, as they must necessarily be in John
10:17–18, where the power of laying down and taking up his life, or authority
over life and death, is being treated, then to lay down his life is freely to
dispose of his life as lord of his own members, and to take it up is to raise
himself. This belongs not to the human but to the divine nature.

XIX. In Christ’s sacrifice the substance of the work must be distinguished
from its value and dignity. The human nature alone contributed the former,
but the divine nature had to contribute the latter. Thus the humanity acted
the part of the victim, but the divinity the part of the priest, in which sense
Christ is said to have offered himself through the eternal Spirit, Hebrews
9:14. Now although the efficacy and value of the sacrifice is not in itself a
real and physical action, nevertheless it must be acknowledged always to
proceed in a real action from a real agent. Therefore, if the efficacy is from
the divine nature as such, it must be concluded that the divine nature as such
operated in Christ’s sacrifice and that the divinity has its own parts in the
mediatorial work.

XX. A mediator must be intermediate between the contending parties, not
negatively by removal from the extremes but positively by participation in
their natures. For he could not be mediator to unite human beings with God
unless he had communion with both. Therefore, as he united the two natures in
the person through the hypostatic union, so he had to unite them in the office
through a mystical union of wills and operations. Thus it is not enough for
mediation that someone be distant from each party, for then angels could be
mediators, since they are distant from both God and human beings. Rather, an
equal distance and proximity is required—that is, that he approach and recede
as much from the one before whom mediation occurs as from the one for whom it
occurs. Now Christ as a righteous man is not equally distant from God and man,
as Bellarmine wishes. For he differs from God in essence, but from sinful
human beings only in qualities. Therefore, to find both the mediator’s
distance and proximity, it must be sought in Christ the God-man
(θεανθρώπῳ), who, as he approaches God and recedes from us with respect to the
divinity, so approaches us and recedes from God with respect to the humanity.

XXI. When the Fathers deny that Christ is mediator insofar as he is God, they
wish to indicate nothing other than the necessity of the hypostatic union in
Christ the mediator, so as to show that he is mediator not simply as God, but
as God manifested in the flesh.

XXII. Although Moses, the mediator of the Old Testament, was only a mere man,
it does not follow that Christ too could discharge this office according to
the human nature. For there is one rationale of a typical mediator, who is a
mere intermediary, and another of him who is a true peacemaker (εἰρηνοποιός),
who could reconcile us to God only by an infinite ransom (λύτρῳ), as Christ
had to be.

XXIII. Although Christ is maintained to be mediator according to the divine
nature, the Word (Λόγος) is not thereby maintained to be less than the Father,
nor is his consubstantiality (ὁμοουσία) denied. For it is one thing, with the
Arians, to make the persons unequal according to essence; another to
distinguish the Son from the Father according to the voluntary economy in
which he emptied himself. Those who falsely charge us with Arianism improperly
disregard this distinction.
