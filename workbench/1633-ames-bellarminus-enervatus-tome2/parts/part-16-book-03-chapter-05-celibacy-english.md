CHAPTER V. On celibacy.

The fourth question is whether celibacy is a necessary adjunct of clerics or
of holy orders. The Pontificians affirm it; we deny it.

1. Bellarmine. *Celibacy and the vow of continence are annexed to holy orders,
not indeed by divine law, but nevertheless by apostolic law, a law most
ancient, most right, and most equitable, and one which it is in no way
expedient to relax at the present time.*

Protestants. I. Bellarmine proceeds obscurely and sophistically. Although he
previously defended the ecclesiastical orders indiscriminately, without any
distinction between *holy* and *non-holy* orders, here he suddenly, without any
explanation—much less proof—of that *petty distinction* as legitimate, thrusts
upon us a question about *holy orders*. By this name he understands not all the
orders which he wishes to be ecclesiastical and conferred by a sacred rite, but
only presbyters, deacons, and subdeacons, according to the doctrine of the
Schoolmen on book 4 of the *Sentences*, distinction 37. II. He also
sophistically confounds *celibacy annexed* to these holy orders with *the vow
of continence*. For, as Durandus teaches on the *Sentences* in the same place,
*continence is imposed upon the ordained whether they consent or not; order
does not hinder or dissolve marriage by reason of the vow annexed to it,
because by no statute can a vow be annexed to any act unless the one vowing
consents voluntarily, even if he should say it in
words and not consent in his mind.* III. Without any reason or sense he
imagines an *apostolic law* which is universal and perpetual, yet not *divine*.
By the same reasoning, *prophetic law* would also not be divine, and the
prophetic and apostolic writings could be distinguished from divine Scripture.
IV. There is absolutely no universal, right, and equitable institution which
it can in no way be expedient to relax at this time, in any place, that is not
of divine law. Bellarmine therefore contradicts himself when he ascribes all
those qualities to this celibacy and at the same time takes away the authority
of divine law. V. If a relaxing dispensation had a place in this matter or at
this time, it is expedient at least somewhere, or it can never be expedient;
for no cause of relaxation can be imagined which would not equally suit this
and some other

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n317 | printed p. 100]

time. This clause is therefore added gratuitously, as they say, and in what
follows it has no semblance of proof annexed to it.

Bellarmine's arguments.

2. Bellarmine. *It is proved from the Scriptures, and first from Titus 1:8: a
bishop must be chaste and continent.*

Protestants. That which can be proved from the precepts of Scripture is without
doubt of divine law. Bellarmine himself admits in chapter 7 that *God's
precepts make a matter one of divine law*. Since, therefore, he previously
conceded that this celibacy is not of divine law, he seems to have given
advance notice that he adduces passages of Scripture of this kind in this
cause either merely for the sake of strife or against his own conscience. II.
That word *chaste* is not read in the authentic Vulgate version, nor can any
reason be given why the Greek word (σώφρονα) should signify the chastity of
celibacy there any more than in chapter 2, verse 5, where it is ascribed to
married women, or in verse 12, where it denotes a duty necessarily to be
performed by all. III. Continence also is not peculiar to virginity, but
belongs to all Christians in all things, 1 Corinthians 9:25; and in this place
*it extends to all things which need a bridle*, as Cajetan observes, for it
concerns married men and fathers of children in verse 6.

3. Bellarmine. *Second Timothy 2:4: No one serving as a soldier for God ought
to entangle himself in secular affairs; but marriages are such affairs.*

Protestants. I. Marriages are not of themselves affairs which necessarily
entangle. For there are those who *have wives* and yet, as far as entanglement
is concerned, *are as though they did not have them*, 1 Corinthians 7:22. II.
A godly wife is given as *a help*, not as a hindrance, both in the spiritual
life and in household affairs. III. By the same reasoning it could be concluded
that a minister of the Church ought not to preside over his own household,
contrary to 1 Timothy 3:4. IV. Bellarmine contends elsewhere (*On the
Pontiff*, book 5, chapter 10) that *political government is not prohibited by
these words, but only that excessive anxiety which is prohibited even to the
laity*. Bellarmine is a remarkable interpreter of Scripture: he places a
worldly kingdom and principality outside secular affairs and includes marriage
within them; by the same words he contends that nothing is prohibited which is
approved in the laity, yet he wishes marriage to be forbidden to the ministers
of the Church.

4. Bellarmine. *First Corinthians 7:5: Married couples ought by consent to
abstain for a time from the work of marriage so that they may devote themselves
to prayer. Consecrated men must therefore abstain perpetually from the work of
marriage, because they ought to devote themselves to prayer every day.*

Protestants. I. What the apostle says must be understood of extraordinary
prayer, which has fasting joined to it: *that you may devote yourselves to
fasting and prayers*. Consecrated men do not engage in such prayers every day.
II. By this

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n318 | printed p. 101]

reasoning, consecrated men would also have to abstain perpetually from food:
*fasting and prayers*. III. In the same way all Christians would also have to
abstain perpetually from marriage, for it is said to all, *pray without
ceasing*, 1 Thessalonians 5:17. David, though certainly married, was more
frequent and more spiritual in prayer than any of the papal consecrated men.

5. Bellarmine. *In the Old Testament continence from wives was required in
those who were about to draw near to God.*

Protestants. I. Continence from marriage was required of no order of priests in
the Old Testament. II. No special continence was prescribed to those praying,
prophesying, and so forth, but only to those handling certain ceremonial parts
of worship. To infer from those ceremonial observances of the Old Testament
not only some similar outward thing, but something much greater in the New, is
to trifle with holy things.

Bellarmine. 6. *It is proved by the testimonies of councils.*

Protestants. I. Not all decrees of councils are of apostolic law. II.
Bellarmine produces no single ancient general council expressly confirming the
point in question.

7. Bellarmine. *It is proved by the testimonies of the ancient pontiffs.*

Protestants. They were not so ancient that they were not forerunners of
Antichrist.

8. Bellarmine. *It is proved by the testimonies of the ancient Fathers.*

Protestants. That is, of those who had contracted from the Essenes,
Marcionites, Encratites, Origenists, Montanists, and other heretics the praise
and admiration of a masked and imaginary holiness, through a certain perverse
emulation, as though through a contagion. Yet from all these it is not proved
that celibacy was universally prescribed to pastors in the primitive Church by
apostolic law.

9. Bellarmine. *Marriage hinders the duty of sacrificing, because it carries
with it impurity and pollution—not an impurity which is sin, but one which has
nevertheless arisen from sin.*

Protestants. All the sons of Adam always carry at least such impurity about
with them, Christ alone excepted; and in truth Christ alone was fit to offer a
truly and properly expiatory sacrifice.

10. Bellarmine. *It hinders the study of prayer and reading.*

Protestants. Yet David, though married, prayed seven times a day and had his
meditation in the law of God by day and by night.

11. Bellarmine. *It hinders the efficacy of preaching.*

Protestants. Of that preaching, to be sure, which is occupied with human
presumptions, not divine precepts.

12. Bellarmine. *It hinders the*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n319 | printed p. 102]

*pastoral care.*

Protestants. Just as it hindered it in the apostles and prophets.

13. Bellarmine. *It hinders care of the poor, hospitality, and kindness, and
drives clerics through avarice to sell the sacraments.*

Protestants. Scripture everywhere testifies that those virtues are found in
married people. Solomon once saw the contrary vices: *one who has no second*,
Ecclesiastes 4:4; and today the whole world sees them with execration in the
Roman merchants.

14. Bellarmine. 1. *It would hinder the administration of the sacraments, and
so clerics would scarcely be distinguished from the laity.*

Protestants. Among the Pontificians, therefore, almost the one distinction
between clerics and the laity consists in this: the laity alone may have wives,
while clerics may have only concubines.

That all these hindrances were invented by Bellarmine to impose upon the
unskilled is sufficiently evident from what he himself admits in chapter 18:
*that the Roman Church has for many centuries permitted Greek priests the use
of wives*. If there were such hindrances in marriage as Bellarmine imagines,
this would be nothing other than permitting them *impurity or pollution
opposed to the duty of sacrificing; a carnal disposition repugnant to prayer
and reading; an obstacle to effective preaching; the corruption of pastoral
care; shameful avarice and simony; and the confusion of the whole order*. Yet
the Pope is not accustomed to parade such permissions and dispensations, nor
the Jesuits to boast of them. The same thing is also manifest from the fact
that for the Pontificians marriage is a sacrament conferring sanctifying grace,
which certainly is not the effect of a cause that hinders the works of grace in
this way, however Bellarmine trifles here.

Our arguments.

15. Protestants. First Timothy 4:1, 3. It is the doctrine of demons to prohibit
marriage.

Bellarmine. 1. *The Church does not prohibit marriage, but only requires that
condition in those who wish to be initiated into the sacred mysteries.*

Protestants. Those who require celibacy prohibit marriage; but the Pontificians
require celibacy; therefore they prohibit marriage.

Bellarmine. 2. *The Church is not reckoned to prohibit forensic judgments or
earthly office, even if she is unwilling for ecclesiastics to perform such
things.*

Protestants. Indeed, if by authority or command she is unwilling for such
things to be performed by ecclesiastics, she prohibits ecclesiastics from
performing them.

Bellarmine. 3. *The apostle marks those who absolutely prohibited marriages as
evil.*

Protestants. I. The apostle marks the very doctrine of prohibiting marriage as
the doctrine of demons. Now just as

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n320 | printed p. 103]

poison is poison whether it is offered absolutely or conditionally, so also a
diabolical doctrine remains diabolical even if it is proposed conditionally.
II. In verses 3 and 4 the apostle condemns the prohibition which infringes the
liberty granted by the law of creation and divine law; but Bellarmine himself
concedes that by divine law marriage is free together with the condition of
ecclesiastical ministry. III. In verse 1 the apostle expressly marks as
diabolical that doctrine which is opposed to the doctrine delivered by him in
chapter 3: *But the Spirit expressly teaches*. Yet in that chapter he had
taught that *the husband of one wife can be a bishop*, verses 2 and 4. The
Pontificians absolutely condemn marriages in that reasoning of Pope Syricius:
*Those who are in the flesh cannot please God*, to Himerius, chapter 7.

16. Protestants. Paul numbers marriage among the virtues of a bishop, 1
Timothy 3; Titus 1.

Bellarmine. *It is false that Paul places marriage among the gifts and absolute
virtues of a bishop.*

Protestants. He does not indeed place it as a species of virtue, nor is this
what we mean; but he places it as a condition which agrees very well with the
highest perfection of a bishop.

17. Protestants. In Hebrews 13:4 the apostle pronounces without exception that
marriage is honorable among all.

Bellarmine. 1. *If the marriages of all are honorable, then so are those of
close relatives and those contracted without their parents' authorization.*

Protestants. I. It does not follow, because some marriages among all people can
be lawful and honorable, though not such marriages. II. Marriages permitted by
divine law are meant, and Bellarmine does not deny that the marriages of
priests are such.

Bellarmine. 2. *It is honorable among all who are lawfully joined.*

Protestants. Yet in the apostle's judgment all those are lawfully joined who
are not *fornicators and adulterers, condemned* by divine law in the same
place. Therefore priests are also lawfully joined.

18. Protestants. The apostles had wives, 1 Corinthians 9.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is not altogether certain which of the apostles had wives
and which did not.*

Protestants. As though the force of the argument lay in these or those apostles
rather than others having had wives, and not in the fact that some of them had
wives and that it was therefore free for the rest to have or not to have them.

Bellarmine. 2. *It is certain that those among the apostles who had wives
renounced conjugal duty after they were called by Christ. For they left all
things—father, mother, wife. Matthew 19.*

Protestants. They therefore renounced conjugal duty in the same way as domestic
and filial duty. Yet afterward they performed the duty of sons and the domestic
duty insofar as these did not draw them back from following Christ, Matthew
8:14; John 19:27. Therefore they also performed conjugal duty.

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n321 | printed p. 104]

Bellarmine. 3. *In 1 Corinthians 9:5 the apostle speaks not of wives but of
women who followed the apostles and ministered to them. If he were speaking of
a wife, he would say τὴν ἀδελφὴν, “the sister.”*

Protestants. I. The apostle did not apply that article τὴν, “the,” to the
individual persons, because he was speaking not only about himself but also
about Barnabas. II. No reason permits us to believe that the other apostles led
women who were not wives through various regions. III. *Sister woman* is not a
phrase agreeable to reason, but *sister wife* is, because all sisters are
women, but not all are wives. IV. *The right to lead about* denotes that right
which a husband has in relation to his wife. V. Not leading a rich woman about
would not have been to seek relief for the churches, which nevertheless the
apostle intends to testify concerning himself in that place; there would have
been no use for a poor woman who was not a wife.

Bellarmine. 1. *Paul said “sister woman” in place of “woman sister.”*

Protestants. That is, without cause and without example he changed a clear
phrase into one whose order is reversed.

Bellarmine. 2. *That power concerned the action of leading about, not the
women; or it concerned the women as disciples.*

Protestants. There is no power concerning an action without power over the
object of that action. Nor does a teacher have the power to lead a female
disciple, as such, wherever he wishes.

Bellarmine. 3. *Those women either did not minister money but service, or they
were not always with the apostles.*

Protestants. No common ministry of service was necessary to the apostles which
could not have been expected from the widows of the churches with greater
honor and edification. Nor is the question whether such women were always with
the apostles, but whether they could always have been with them.

19. Protestants. Marriage is of divine, natural law, Genesis 1. It therefore
cannot be taken away by any human law in such a way that a man is bound to the
contrary.

Bellarmine. 1. *That precept is affirmative and does not bind except in a case
of necessity, when the danger that the human race would perish was imminent.*

Protestants. I. The whole force of the argument is sophistically evaded. For
the question does not concern a precept properly so called, but right and
power; it concerns not an obligation to marriage, but the exclusion of an
obligation to the contrary. II. Besides propagation, there is another
necessity of marriage after the fall: avoiding fornication and impurity, 1
Corinthians 7:2.

20. Protestants. Marriage is prescribed to all who do not have the gift of
continence, 1 Corinthians 7.

Bellarmine. 1. *The apostle speaks of married people.*

Protestants. He speaks expressly of *the unmarried and widows*, verse 8.

Bellarmine. 2. *It is not a precept, but a counsel.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n322 | printed p. 105]

Protestants. If it is a counsel, then according to the opinion of the
Pontificians it is of greater perfection than celibacy.

Bellarmine. 3. *It is said to those who do not have a vow of continence.*

Protestants. It is said to all to whom the apostle writes; yet they are
prohibited from having a vow of this kind. Verse 35: *Not that I may cast a
snare upon you.*

21. Protestants. Continence is a most difficult thing and is not given to all,
Matthew 19. It ought not, therefore, to be imposed indiscriminately upon all
clerics.

Bellarmine. 1. *Only those who appear to have this gift ought to be priests.*

Protestants. Nor does there appear to be anything in the Pontifical
sacrificers from which it could be gathered that they have this gift more than
others. Certain burdens and vows ought not to be imposed upon the consciences
of Christians on the basis of uncertain conjectures.

Bellarmine. 2. *Those who wish have the gift of continence.*

22. Protestants. No more than the apostle Paul was able to accomplish what he
wished, Romans 7:15. Even if one possessed it, who could promise that in
matters of this kind that will would remain constant in him forever? Cajetan
speaks rightly on Matthew 19: *Where it says “it has been given,” it does not
say “those who have wished”; for it is not of him who wills, nor of him who
runs, but of God who has mercy, by whom the gift of preserving virginity is
given. He does not say, “Let him who wishes receive it,” but, “Let him who is
able to receive it, receive it”; as though, if you wish but do not feel
yourself to possess such constancy that you are able, you are not among the
number of those who are able to receive it.*

Bellarmine. 3. *Married people are often compelled to abstain from a wife for a
long time.*

Protestants. When God imposes a necessity of this kind upon us, then he tests
us and at the same time gives a way out. But when we impose such a necessity
upon ourselves, then we put God to the test and so cannot rely upon any promise
of his.

23. Protestants. The celibacy of clerics gives rise to monstrous lusts.

Bellarmine. 1. *It is not celibacy, but the abuse of celibacy, which gives rise
to these things.*

Protestants. I. Let Bellarmine answer himself in the same way concerning the
hindrances to ministry for which he previously accused marriage. Who has
bewitched him so that he cannot distinguish equally acutely between use and
abuse in marriage as in celibacy? II. Celibacy indeed is not the cause of
monstrous lusts, but compelled celibacy is. In the same way, abstinence is not
the cause why people eat excrement and drink their own urine; rather, compelled
abstinence from natural and wholesome food and drink is the cause of so
monstrous a feeding, Isaiah 36:12.

Bellarmine. 2. *Yet we do not admit that there are as many keepers of
concubines and lepers among ecclesiastics as the heretics say.*

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n323 | printed p. 106]

Protestants. For the confirmation of the argument and the exposure of
hypocritical and impure Papist continence, it will be enough if we admit what
is said about this matter by those who are called Catholics. Thus Emperor
Maximilian, in a letter to the Pope found in Thuanus, *History*, book 36: *In
this age scarcely one among many is found who maintains chaste celibacy. Almost
all are public fornicators, with the greatest danger to souls and offense to
the people.* Espencaeus, a bishop, also says on Titus, chapter 1: *It is most
shameful that officials permit clerics to live together with concubines,
prostitutes, and mistresses, and allow them to procreate children, after
receiving from them a fixed yearly payment; indeed, elsewhere they take it
from those who are continent, for “let them have them,” they say, “if they
wish.”* To these let the very weighty testimony and reflection of Polydore
Vergil be added, *On the Inventors of Things*, book 5, chapter 4: *So far is it
from being the case that that compulsory chastity has surpassed conjugal
chastity that the crime of no other offense has impressed greater disgrace
upon the clerical order, inflicted more evil upon religion, or laid more grief
upon all good people than the stain of priestly lust. Perhaps, therefore, it
would be as much to the advantage of the Christian commonwealth as to the
advantage of the clerical order if the right of public marriage were someday
restored to priests, so that they might cultivate it holily without infamy
rather than most foully defile themselves with a natural vice of this kind.*
