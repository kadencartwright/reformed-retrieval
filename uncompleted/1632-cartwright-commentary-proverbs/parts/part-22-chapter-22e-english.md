Verse 10. Cast out the scoffer, and contention will depart; and strife and ignominy will cease.

The purpose is to indicate the damage which arises for societies from a scoffer.

Here first an exhortation is set forth to all those who preside over the societies of human life, and then the reason on which the exhortation rests. The exhortation is directed to fathers of households in household government, to magistrates in the commonwealth, and to rulers of the church, that they cast the scoffer out of their bounds: namely, whoever holds wholesome teaching and admonitions in mockery (if we leave marriage out of account, whose

[1632 witness | PDF p. 496 | printed columns 976-977]

knot cannot be dissolved except because of adultery), even if he should otherwise be very closely joined to us by the bond of blood or affinity; even if he should be as a right eye or a right hand; even if he could also otherwise be exceedingly useful and fruitful to us. An example of this exists in Ishmael, whom Sarah judged should be cast out of the household, and whom his father Abraham, admonished by an oracle, cast out. From this passage the use and necessity of excommunication is confirmed.

A twofold reason is added. The first is that the scoffer is a murderer and destroys the peace and concord of human society, partly by domestic dissensions and partly by bringing it about that the matter is disputed openly and in a public court. For since the proud man raises quarrels (as we learned at Prov. 21.24), and every scoffer is proud, as one who in the loftiness of his mind rejects and spurns all wholesome teachings, that same man must sow quarrels. Moreover, since he hates the person by whom he is admonished and reproved, Prov. 9.8, it cannot happen that he does not devise something by which he may cause him trouble.

The second reason is that one who casts out the scoffer will avoid the mark of ignominy. For since he burdens his admonishers with insults and rends them with ignominious words, unless someone wishes to foster contentions and allows himself to be afflicted with ignominy, the scoffer must be cast out.

Verse 11. He who loves purity of heart—because of the grace of his lips, the King will be his friend.

The purpose is both to arouse subjects to sincerity in their speech before princes and to arouse princes to love subjects who speak truly and sincerely.

It is customary for those who are subject to others, partly terrified by fear and partly led by hope of reward, to accommodate their words to the favour of those whom they obey. But in this way they not only bind themselves with wickedness, and first prick and stab their own consciences, then transfix them by long practice of falsehood and induce shamelessness in them; they also drive those whom they obey to a foolish opinion of themselves, whence they puff up their spirits and construct for themselves a passage to destruction. 1 Kings 22.12.

He describes the speech which ought to be employed, first, from its efficient cause, namely, purity of heart. One must speak not so much with the lips and tongue as with the heart itself; and it is not enough to say true things unless we speak further from the heart. For many people, although they think otherwise, nevertheless say true things because they judge that the truth will be pleasing to those before whom they speak. Then speech must be brought forth not only from the heart, but from a pure heart. For there are people who indeed speak the truth from the heart, according to what they think, but not from a pure heart. They do not speak in this way for the sake of truth, but in order to benefit themselves, to disadvantage others, or to contradict the opinions of others whom they hate.

Then he describes speech from the accompanying grace with which the truth of an address must be seasoned and sprinkled. For it is not enough to bring forth the truth from a pure heart unless account is taken of time, place, persons, and other circumstances of that kind; we read that this is marked by the term *grace* at Col. 4.6.

From the accompanying reward of truth-telling he incites people to love it, when he says that people of this kind will be dear to the king and his friends. By these words the Prophet teaches that God, by his most powerful Spirit, by which he governs the minds of all according to his will, will cause the speech of truth to be pleasing and acceptable even to kings themselves. Indeed, we read that he did this not only among godly kings, but sometimes

[1632 witness | PDF p. 497 | printed columns 978-979]

among barbarians also, and the ungodly. For Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar raised Daniel to the highest dignity while he pronounced all the most grievous things.

Hence it is evident how meanly those people think of their lords, princes, and magistrates who suppose that, by saying everything before them for favour, they will thereby carry away a reward for themselves. For in their own estimation they make them far worse than those profane and ungodly kings.

Hence, finally, we are taught that in choosing public pastors and even private friends we should above all admit those who will say from the heart what they think.
