VIII. That Wolzogen attributes far too much to Reason is also evident from
what he has concerning consequences on pages 21 and 22. *The consequences of
things*, he says, *have something perhaps more involved, though not less
certain. What is easily understood in the lighter and evident consequences,
and in those of matters which properly belong to Reason, is resolved with a
little more difficulty—although because of its supreme usefulness it must
nevertheless be resolved—namely, how Reason judges this truth in the
consequences of those matters which do not proceed from reason. For this must
be said in passing: many doctrines are contained in Scripture which, although
they themselves depend upon divine Revelation alone, nevertheless have their
consequences debated at the judgment of*

[Page 57 | ONB canvas 111 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000111]

*reason too. For if anyone has demonstrated from Scripture the doctrine of
justification by faith, Reason will afterward examine the very extensive and
widely diffused analogy of this doctrine. If anything is nevertheless brought
from Scripture which appears contrary to that analogy, well founded and
confirmed as it is, Scripture must suitably be explained wholly according to
that reason. For since the reason resting upon that analogy of saving doctrine
is judged to contain truth, nothing can be opposed to it which is not false;
and since it is false, it must by no means be believed to have been set before
us in Scripture.* Here it must be noted that he again wishes Reason to be
regarded as the norm and Scripture to be suitably interpreted according to it.
If he had not proposed to draw unwary readers into error and desert the cause,
he could have explained himself more soundly and plainly in this way: namely,
that the other Scripture passage whose true meaning is disputed should be
suitably explained according to that analogy demonstrated from the
Scriptures—or rather according to Scripture, or according to the Spirit in
the Scriptures, who clearly sets forth and explains that doctrine of
justification. Why should one not rely instead upon the far more solid
foundation of Scripture than upon that reason, even when it rests upon that
well-founded analogy, since it can be called into doubt whether the reason is
well founded and firm?

His argument no more concludes that the meaning of Scripture must be judged
from that Reason than does the same or an altogether similar argument advanced
by Smalcius against Frantzius in the preface: *No one ought to doubt that
whatever things conflict with reason are more certainly than certain false,
for a small light cannot in any way be contrary to a great one.* For although
it is most true that *nothing is true in one place which is false in another,*
and that *nothing in the Scriptures is opposed to sound and true reason,*
nevertheless Wolzogen's consequence is nothing. From this it follows only that
no true conflict must be conceded here and that reason must be reconciled with
the Scriptures, not that the *Scrip-*

[Page 58 | ONB canvas 112 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000112]

*tures* must be bent to Reason or examined and judged according to reason's
dictate, whether positively or negatively—that is, that something in the
Scriptures is true because Reason says the same, or false because reason
appears to deny it. See the celebrated Hoornbeeck at the place cited above,
page 115.

But the chief thing I press here is that when Wolzogen speaks about joining
consequences, he attributes too much to Reason. I freely concede that the
ministry of Reason is needed for inferring and rightly joining consequences
from the Scriptures, whether for explaining the truth more particularly and
confirming it more powerfully, or for assailing and defeating the adversaries'
dogmas. But Wolzogen says not only that Reason judges the truth in these
consequences, but also that *the consequences themselves are debated at
Reason's judgment*. These words certainly imply something far different from
the consequences being formed with Reason's aid: namely, that they must be
judged and determined according to the sentence of Reason as arbiter. Thus, if
Reason, as it is now blind and corrupt, does not approve a consequence firm in
itself—such as Christ's consequence in Matthew 22:31–32—it must be rejected.
Can Wolzogen deny that there are, or can be, far more consequences or streams
of Scripture than our blinded and corrupt Reason perceives or knows how to
derive? If he can, he must admit that our reason, covered in darkness, can
easily penetrate the abyss of the Scriptures, and that no more truths can be
elicited and deduced from it than are now elicited and drawn out. But if he
cannot, he will also necessarily admit that Scripture itself depends upon
reason, because those streams must be acknowledged as sacred Scripture. For he
wishes them not only to be deduced by reason's operation, but also debated at
reason's judgment. Thus there will be no more streams of Scripture than our
reason wills.

He states the same thing plainly enough on pages 38–39: *For what is enclosed
and bounded within narrow limits, if you consider the sources and the very
heads and founda-*

[Page 59 | ONB canvas 113 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000113]

*tions of doctrine, grows without measure and has no end if, by employing the
Use of Reason, you pursue those outflows and certain emanations, as it were.
For reason teaches how the former things are transcribed into Scripture
alone.* Here he says not only that consequences depend upon reason, but the
consequent things themselves do so too, and not as upon a means but rather as
upon a cause. His own comparison, which he appends, with the game of chess
sufficiently confirms this. For those arts, stratagems, and snares by which
the superior player emerges victorious depend upon human ingenuity and
judgment, not as upon a means, but rather as upon a productive cause. The same
is true of that other comparison on pages 37–38, drawn from the varied and
infinite conjunction of the elements of letters, which are very few. For the
varied and indeed infinite words which arise from the varied conjunction of
these elements acknowledge people as their authors.

Thus on page 68 he says that *the things which have a consequence flowing from
grace, although their source is in revelation, must nevertheless render to
Reason the acknowledgment of whatever flows from it.* It is clear from page
67, however, that he wishes Reason to be considered here in a way far
different from an instrumental cause or means. There he has these words, which
we also cited in part before: *We do not wish here to assert the instrumental
use of Reason, as they call it, which we presuppose. For interpretation itself,
which joins the meaning of an utterance with its words, must be accomplished
by judgment and understanding and therefore cannot be accomplished without
reason. And whatever we assert from whatever source, the same judgment of
reason must be applied to joining those two things fittingly to one another.
For almost nothing can be done rightly and in order by a person unless it is
done with reason's aid. But the principal difficulty is by no means situated
in this. We seek a leader, while reason serves as minister.* Therefore a
different and superior use of Reason is being treated here than its
instrumental use, without which, he says, no interpretation joining the
meaning of an utterance with its words can be made. But enough about these
things here; let us proceed.

IX. The third point which he undertakes to prove on page 24 and following is
that *God does not will to deceive anyone*. This is certainly most true. But
who will not recognize the disposition of a betrayer here when he reads what
follows? *I say*, he writes, *that he does not will to deceive, lest you
believe that he cannot if he wills.* What more could he have said to overturn
the foundation already laid without openly contradicting himself than what he
says here? He does not dare say that *God wills to deceive*, since he had
already said that *he does not will to deceive*. Nevertheless, he is so bold
as to assert, and indeed with a note of emphasis, that *he can deceive if he
wills*. From whom he learned this, if not from Descartes, is hidden from me. I
also do not know to what end he openly asserts it here, unless it was to betray
the cause as far as he was able. No necessity compelled him to bring this
forward; indeed, he would have defended the true cause sufficiently, and
better, if he had kept silent about it.

I need not show how childishly he behaves in confirming this scandalous and
false assertion of his, since it is obvious to anyone. For by no consequence
can he prove that God can deceive because he possesses wisdom, power, and the
other infinite attributes. Even of a man it can scarcely be said that he can
deceive another because he surpasses the other in acuteness of intellect,
power, or any other faculty, unless we further suppose that he is endowed with
malice and able to approach the matter with disguise and deceptions. Since
Wolzogen does not dare say this of God, because it argues imperfection, does
he not act far more unjustly when he nevertheless asserts that God can
deceive? Is it fitting even to suppose such things of God, or to say that he
*can do* things which we nevertheless do not dare say that he *wills*, *wills
to do*, or *can will to do*? Is it fitting to say that God can sin if he
wills, or change himself into a corporeal and dependent substance, or into an
accident inhering in some substance, and so forth? I pass over other things
which the most illustrious Voetius enumerates in *Selected Disputations*, part
5, page 422, and innumerable other things which cannot be recited without
horror. *Right-*

[Page 61 | ONB canvas 115 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000115]

*ly* does Voetius say in the same place, page 423: *Idly, profanely, and
audaciously thus to trifle and make sport concerning the tremendous,
terrifying, incomprehensible preeminence and Majesty of our God belongs
neither to the wisdom nor to the godliness of those who tremble at God's word,
Isaiah 66, whose dread is God, Isaiah 8, and who work out salvation with fear
and trembling, who hear that saying: It is dangerous even to speak true things
about God.* Thus he speaks.

But Wolzogen's words must be noted: *I say that he does not will to deceive,
lest you believe that he cannot if he wills.* What danger, I ask, would there
be if we believed that God cannot deceive? Would it therefore be necessary to
believe that God is not infinite in wisdom, power, and the other attributes?
By the same argument, lest we believe that God is not infinite in these
things, we must also believe that he can lie, sin, and make contradictories
true, and so forth; and this must be openly said so that our faith concerning
God's infinite attributes may remain safe and sound. But how absurd, how
ridiculous, all these things are! How ridiculous and blasphemous Wolzogen's
conclusion is! Since both God's wisdom and power and all his other attributes
are infinite, can God therefore, if he wills, lead even the most perfect
creatures into error—that is, deceive them? Perhaps this conclusion appeared
horrible even to Wolzogen, and so he thinks it better to express the matter
differently and say: *Who does not see that even the most perfect creatures,
because they are always finite, can by that very fact, as creatures, be led
into error by the infinite Creator?* The question is not about human
imperfection. We know that even the most perfect people are so imperfect that
they can be led into error even by other creatures. Rather, the question is
whether God, the most perfect, best, and most truthful—indeed, Truth itself—
should be said to be able to deceive and lead into error. Who does not see that
this is so far from divine perfection that it openly denotes imperfection? It
denotes imperfection even in a human being, much more in God. Is not *ly-*

[Page 62 | ONB canvas 116 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000116]

*ing* and saying false things an imperfection? But what is deceiving another
by words—and this kind of deception is being discussed here—other than lying
or speaking falsely? Therefore, if God can deceive by words, he can also lie,
which expressly conflicts with Scripture, Titus 1:2 and Hebrews 6:18, and the
falsehood of which even Balaam can teach Wolzogen, Numbers 23:23. It also
manifestly conflicts with God's nature, because in God there neither is nor
can be ignorance, error of judgment, malice, or anything of that kind from
which a lie draws its origin.

Indeed, if we said this, we could also say that God can become not-God if he
wills. How blasphemous that is! For to deceive by words, or to lie, is a sin.
But if God could sin, he could be not-God, for one who sins is not God.
Therefore one who can sin can become not-God. But one who can become not-God
is not God, because he is not immutably most perfect and most perfectly
immutable; indeed, one who can sin is not God. You see where this profane
assertion of yours leads, Wolzogen. Wolzogen could have devised nothing more
effective for overturning all the foundations of our salvation and evading
the whole of Scripture, because all those foundations were laid and Scripture
itself was given to us by a God who, according to Wolzogen, can deceive. And
how will it be established for us that he does not deceive? What will atheists
say to these things? How will Gentile and atheist adversaries be induced to
embrace our Religion, hear our God, or read our Scriptures, when they hear
that this God of ours can deceive? In vain will you say that nevertheless he
does not will to deceive. For how will this be established for them? Not from
the Scriptures, where he expressly says so; for they can reply: This statement
is from that God who can deceive. And how will we know that he does not here
put into effect what you say he can do? Not from God's nature, for it is not
contrary to God's nature to put into effect what he can accomplish. You will
also say in vain that willing to accomplish it conflicts with God's nature.
For

[Page 63 | ONB canvas 117 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000117]

if being able to accomplish it does not conflict with God's nature, neither
will being able to will to do it conflict with God's nature. For shame! Where
are we now? But how does Wolzogen show us that God does not will to deceive?
*For that will to deceive*, he says, *can scarcely be understood without being
judged to have something joined either with malice, by which someone attempts
to circumvent another whom he does not undertake to approach without disguise
and deceptions, or with weakness of mind, through which he distrusts his
ability to emerge superior in another way. Since both of these imply great
imperfection, they must be removed altogether from him whom we understand as
the most absolute perfection in the combination of all things.* Reply. 1. If a
will to deceive can scarcely be given without malice joined to it, it is
nevertheless not impossible, according to you, for it to occur; for what can
scarcely occur is not impossible. 2. If that will to deceive can scarcely be
understood without those imperfections, then that will can also scarcely be
put into effect without those imperfections. But if the execution of such a
will necessarily implies imperfection, we cannot say without the greatest
impiety that God can deceive, because to be able to deceive is the same as to
be able to put a will to deceive into execution. For every act of God is the
execution of his will, and he cannot do anything unless preceded by an act of
will.

3. If God can deceive and cannot will to deceive, then he can put into
execution what he cannot decree. I know that God forms no new decrees and
cannot form them now; but we are speaking here of the point of reason prior to
the decrees, and we take *to be able to will* or *to decree* in the divided
sense. Therefore, since forming a decree to deceive, or willing to deceive,
conflicts with the divine essence and its essential attributes, how can we
suppose that he can put such a decree into execution? Or how can we imagine
that any such thing can be done by God without that conflict? 4. How can it be
conceived that it does not *con-*

[Page 64 | ONB canvas 118 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000118]

*flict* with divine power to do what nevertheless conflicts absolutely with
his will to will? I know well enough that God can do many things which he does
not will, but until now I have never heard that God can do what he cannot
will. For what conflicts absolutely with the will to will cannot fail also to
conflict absolutely with his power to do it, since God's simplicity requires
this.

It must now be seen how he varnishes these things in *Censure of the Censure*,
pages 37–38. He says that he does not take the phrase *can deceive* *for that
power which denotes a certain natural faculty and infinite force in matters,
as well as an intrinsic disposition from which actions flow*. Reply. When you
said that God can deceive, you undoubtedly understood by it that power in God
by which he does or produces something when he wills; otherwise your words
must not be interpreted according to ordinary speech. Who can suppose that
you understood something else after reading your very words? For you speak in
this way: *It is sufficient that God has said it for us to know that he does
not will to deceive. I say that he does not will to deceive, lest you believe
that he cannot if he wills.* When we speak about God's *power* and God's
*Will*, and say that God can or cannot do certain things, or that God can do
what he wills or what he does not will, who will not certainly believe that we
always speak about that divine power by which he accomplishes the things he
accomplishes or wills, and by which he could do this or that which he
nevertheless does not will? This corresponds analogically to that natural
faculty, implanted force, and disposition from which actions flow in
creatures.

But tell me, Wolzogen: when in the third or fourth preceding line you said
that God *cannot speak falsely*, of what power were you then speaking? Was it
not the one which you describe here and which you removed from God? Now tell
me again whether you do not attribute to God, when you say that he *can
deceive*, the same power which you deny should be attributed to him when you
say that he *cannot speak falsely*. Tell me candidly: do you understand one
faculty in the negative proposition and another in the affirmative
proposition? I know well enough that you will not be *say-*

[Page 65 | ONB canvas 119 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000119]

*ing* this, provided you do not recall that you contradict yourself when you
confess the truth. But whether you confess this or not, it makes no difference;
the matter speaks for itself, and here your water will run aground. Yet you
say that this was not your meaning, in saying which you expose yourself to
ridicule. But let us hear how you explain yourself: *In constructing every
deception*, you say, *two things must be distinctly considered: the very will
to deceive, and the faculties which we employ in deceiving.* Reply. But there
is a third thing which you have forgotten: namely, the execution of that will,
or the actual use and application of those faculties to produce the deception
in act. Although the will to deceive and those faculties are present, without
this third thing no deception will exist.

What further? *The will*, you say, *must be present—the appointed design; it
possesses the principle and, as it were, the very form of the deception. If it
is absent, although someone is led into error and deceived, the one by whom he
is led into error nevertheless does not deceive him. He is the occasion of
the error, not properly its cause, because he is not voluntary, as is required
here.* Reply. I concede that the will must be present and that the principle
of deceiving comes from it. But I do not grasp how this will can be called the
form, or as it were the form, of the deception. Since the deception has not
yet been committed, a nonexistent thing has no form. But let this pass. Since
you yourself do not dare say that God possesses this will, or that in which
the principle and quasi-form of deception consists, how can you say that he
can deceive? Moreover, since you admit that as long as this principle is
absent, even if someone is led into error, the one by whom he is led into
error nevertheless does not deceive him but is merely the occasion of the
error, not properly its cause, how can you say that God can deceive, who at
most can be merely the occasion, not the cause, of deception? How can one be
said to be able to deceive who cannot be the cause of deception? It can
scarcely be said suitably enough even of a man that he can deceive if, or
insofar as, he is merely the *oc-*

[Page 66 | ONB canvas 120 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000120]

*casion* and not the cause of deception. And yet, with all godliness
protesting, you will not be ashamed to ascribe to God what is less suitably
ascribed even to a man. What do you have next? *But after someone has resolved
in his mind to circumvent another by fraud, he then summons all his faculties
to execute what he has devised. But which faculties? He excels either in
strength, ingenuity, experience of matters, or the arts of simulation and
dissimulation. He exercises either all these faculties, each of them, or some
few, in order to entangle by deceits the one who is by this very fact his
inferior.* Reply. I do not see how strength is to be numbered among these
faculties which you say have a place in circumvention or deception. *Now*, you
continue, *these faculties cannot be called a faculty or power of deceiving,
for they are faculties of judging and remembering.* Reply. This is indeed true
when speaking properly; but meanwhile I ask, whose faculties are the arts of
simulation and dissimulation? Can one who is skilled in these arts not be said
to be able to deceive? But I very much doubt whether one who possesses only
ingenuity, memory, and experience of matters can for that reason properly be
said to be able to deceive.

He proceeds: *A deceitful man employs only those faculties obtained from
elsewhere to construct snares and put his deception into execution.* Reply.
He rather abuses those faculties obtained from elsewhere. And God cannot
abuse his faculties. He is indeed rightly said to employ the arts of
simulation and dissimulation, because they most properly pertain to putting
deception into execution; but we acknowledge no such arts in our God. *But
the proper power of deceiving*, you continue, *is in the mind of one who has
enough malice to will to employ frauds.* Reply. One in whose mind this proper
power of deceiving is absent can by no means be said to be able to deceive.
Therefore you speak wrongly when you say that God can deceive. In what sense,
then, did you say that God can deceive? *That ability to deceive*, you say,
*must be understood of that force and wisdom, and those infinite divine
faculties which are certainly suffi-*

[Page 67 | ONB canvas 121 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000121]

*ciently ample to put an effected will to deceive into action, if such a will
could exist; not, however, of those faculties and will jointly, in which way a
faculty and power of deceiving would at last exist, and thus it could happen
that God sometimes deceived.—Therefore no power from which an act of deceiving
would follow is given in God, because no will to deceive, which is required
for that power, is given. But the other power of which I speak, which consists
of divine strength and wisdom, is brought into act whenever God produces
works of infinite strength and wisdom. Those acts, however, are not acts of
deceiving, because they do not proceed from a power of deceiving, since God
never employs his force or wisdom to deceive, but are acts of simple power or
wisdom.* Reply. Here is everything, and yet nothing! For force, wisdom, and
the other infinite divine faculties do not put, and cannot put, deception
into execution, since deception requires the arts of simulation and
dissimulation, fraud, cunning, and similar things which are not in God.

2. You speak here about deception which is accomplished in speech and words,
for executing which neither force nor wisdom is required. 3. The subtle
Doctor says here that God can deceive but does not possess the power of
deceiving. Therefore God possesses τὸ *posse*, or the faculty, as Wolzogen
himself speaks in *Censure of the Censure*, page 60, but not the power. He can
put an effected will to deceive into action, but does not possess the power
to do so. Mere dreams! 4. But the argument is subtler still: according to him,
*to be able* has regard only to faculties, but *power* states a regard to the
will and the faculties jointly. O marvelous invention! Yet what else is said
here about God than can be said about people? With respect to his faculties a
man can deceive, but as long as the will is absent, he does not possess the
power of deceiving? 5. Who does not know that a man possesses the power of
deceiving before he actually wills, intends, or resolves in his mind to
circumvent anyone by fraud? How, then, is the will *re-*

[Page 68 | ONB canvas 122 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000122]

*quired* for that power? I know how the will is required for the exercise
itself or for the execution; but how it is required for the power itself is
something Wolzogen must teach us, not merely assert. Is your will required for
the power of moving from place to place, writing, and so forth? Or, as long as
you do not will to move from place to place, speak, and so forth, do you not
possess the power? This is certainly a new doctrine, unheard of until now!

6. Even if we granted all these things, absurd though they are, what would
Wolzogen conclude from them? Does it follow, because God possesses those
infinite faculties, that he can deceive? Does it follow, because he possesses
that power and those other faculties by which a deception can be made, that
he can deceive? Far from it. Otherwise it must be said that he can also
perpetrate many other things which a godly mind can neither recount nor hear.
Finally, he proceeds on page 39: *From this it is easily resolved how God's
will and power do not conflict with each other, even if I say that God can
deceive, yet does not will to do so. For it is evident that the one is said of
intention, the other of execution; will is referred to the former, faculty to
the latter.* Reply. We know that God can do many things which he nevertheless
does not will. But we speak of those things which he not only does not will,
but cannot will, or which conflict absolutely with God's will. And it is asked
whether God can execute what he cannot intend. Will not God's will and power
conflict with each other if he can be said to put into execution what it
conflicts with his own nature to will or intend? You yourself admit that God
cannot will to deceive because this conflicts with his own nature. Show,
therefore, how God can put into execution what it conflicts absolutely with
his will to will. And if you suppose that God can execute what he cannot
intend, show, if you can, how God's Will and Power will not by this argument
conflict with each other. Enough now about these things, since it is evident
that Wolzogen has thus far treated everything pertaining to this question
fraudulently.

X. On page 26, where Wolzogen treats the discourse in which the Scriptures
are composed, he says that it is superfluous to explain every question raised
about it, but that it is by far most necessary to remember that *the
Scriptures were written in an entirely human manner*, and that it follows
from this that *in explaining them one must proceed no differently than in
interpreting other books*. Yet if he had willed to say nothing else here than
that God employs verbal signs, he had no need to assert so universally that
*the Scriptures were written in an entirely human manner*. Who would not
affirm that Wolzogen here employs deceptions and prepares a road for excluding
the Spirit entirely from interpretation? For if one must proceed no
differently in explaining the Scriptures than in interpreting other books,
what need will there be for the Spirit's peculiar aid?

Moreover, who does not see this confirmed when he speaks so meagerly about the
majesty and peculiar style of the Scriptures from which their divine origin
is demonstrated? He says: *And although this discourse can possess a certain
peculiar character of gravity of its own, so that even Theologians believe
that its divinity can be known from the majesty of the style.* He does not say
that he believes this, although other Theologians believe it. Nor does he
satisfy by saying on page 45 of *Censure of the Censure*: *Because I never
departed from the Theologians' position, when I named them I willed myself to
be comprehended too.* For just as he has often departed from the Theologians'
position, as will become evident, so he could not comprehend himself here;
nor could the reader judge him comprehended, especially since Wolzogen
indicated nothing of the kind. Nor does he say that the Scriptures *possess*
this peculiar character of gravity, but that they *can possess* it. And thus,
from this point as if it were granted when it is not, he infers: *Nothing
prevents the sacred codex in other respects, just like all other books, from
being composed according to the usage of its language.* But since the style
or phraseology of sacred literature—to say nothing of the agreement of its
parts and that marvelous fabric which appears there—possesses a peculiar
*empha-*

[Page 70 | ONB canvas 124 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000124]

*sis* of its own, and since far more divinity shines in the terms employed
and used here than anyone not endowed with the same spirit can grasp—for just
as they spoke not in words taught by human wisdom but in those taught by the
Holy Spirit, comparing spiritual things with spiritual things, 1 Corinthians
2:13, so too those amanuenses wrote. For *all Scripture is divinely inspired*,
θεόπνευστος, 2 Timothy 3:16, and *no prophecy of Scripture is of its own
explanation*, or impulse and inspiration; *for prophecy was not once brought
by the will of man, but holy men of God spoke as they were moved by the Holy
Spirit*, 2 Peter 1:20–21. They were moved and impelled by that Spirit to write
each individual word—it appears unwary enough and dangerous to say that the
Scriptures were written in an entirely human manner. They were rather written
in an entirely spiritual and divine manner, and in a manner far different
from that in which human writings were written. Therefore one must proceed in
explaining them far differently than in interpreting other books, even if the
nature of this language is the same as that of other languages. For the force
and value of words employed in other writings need not be weighed, considered,
and examined so anxiously and scrupulously; nor need their style be weighed so
accurately, or their phrases and connections of sentences be sought out and
considered with such divine, spiritual, and heavenly attention, disposition
of mind, and reverence, as all the godly will readily acknowledge. Who,
therefore, will pronounce Wolzogen free from every deception and betrayal when
he speaks so generally and with so little accuracy?

XI. When on page 27 and following he proceeds to the third of the things he
had to say about Scripture—namely, the matters themselves which are explained
to us in sacred discourse—he stumbles in more than one way. For, 1., he says:
*If we universally comprehend in the mind the nature of those matters, all of
them, with none excepted, must be regarded as historical, such as those can*

*be which some Livy or some Polybius committed to writing. There is,
nevertheless, this difference: the authority of the Scriptures is as much
greater as God is above every Livy or Polybius. Once that difference has been
posited, I judge that no diversity in the nature of the matter must be
posited.* Yet everyone knows that Scripture contains God's Will revealed to
us and must be regarded as law and norm, according to whose prescription our
life must be ordered, our conduct reformed, and our faith bounded. Who can say
here that all Sacred Scripture must be regarded as history, when no mention is
made either here or anywhere of a directive norm? Who does not know that
history as such, especially the kind which Livy or Polybius committed to
writing, demands from the reader only belief proportioned to the authority of
the one who wrote it, but not obedience? Yet the sacred Scriptures are God's
law and exhibit to us statutes, God's commands, ordinances, judgments,
testimony, and doctrine which demand obedience from us. For they must be
considered insofar as they were given by the Lawgiver and Supreme Lord.

Thus they were given principally for this end: that they might be our one norm
of faith and conduct, just as promulgated edicts and rescripts of princes are
given to regulate the conduct of subjects and direct it according to the will
of the Prince or Legislator. Why Wolzogen made no mention here of this aspect
or principal end of sacred literature, let the reader inquire and judge; and
whether he can be regarded with suspicion in this matter is left to the
reader's judgment. I certainly do not deny that the history of many deeds is
contained in the Scriptures. But in some places it is also manifest that pure
dogmatic precepts, promises, threats, and things of that kind are exhibited to
us. These pertain most properly to practice, to the right ordering of our
conduct, and indeed to regulating faith. But on that account to say that
everything contained in sacred literature must be regarded as historical

[Page 72 | ONB canvas 126 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000126]

is to speak without any reason. It is most true that in all things the
greatest faith must be placed in God, and that the authority of God speaking
alone prevails here. But on that account to say that everything contained in
the Scriptures must be regarded as historical is to give us mortar without
sand. For who has ever before heard it said that doctrines, precepts of the
Decalogue, and similar things which contain no narration of a deed done assume
the nature of historical matters because we believe and do them on the
authority of God speaking? Thus Wolzogen speaks in *Censure of the Censure*,
pages 51–52. And when on page 52 of the same work he says that not all are
historical by their own nature, but only by the mode of disclosure, he
contradicts himself. For in his book he said, as we have seen, that all of them
must be regarded as historical *if we universally comprehend in the mind the
nature of the matters*.
