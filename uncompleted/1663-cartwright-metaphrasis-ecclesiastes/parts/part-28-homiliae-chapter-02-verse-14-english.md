VERSE 14. I saw indeed that there was advantage in wisdom over folly, just as
there is advantage in light over darkness.

In the former point, he meets a certain difficulty which arises from the
preceding judgments that he delivered. For he delivered an equal judgment
concerning those three shadows and images of happiness in this age, enclosing
them all within the scope and embrace of vanity. From this it was easy to
suppose that there is no more good in one than in another, and no more
usefulness in the knowledge of natural, political, and moral philosophy than
in a life of pleasure, which he charged with folly and madness. Here, however,
he removes this difficulty. For although neither has sufficient power for
living well and happily in this life, much less in the life to come,
nevertheless there is as great a difference between the one and the other as
between light and darkness. Here it should be noted that although a thing is
unfit for establishing happiness and tranquility of mind in this world, it
should not therefore be rejected altogether as useless.

This is very far from the Papists, who, when they hear that works are rejected
by us Evangelicals as a cause of justification, immediately cry out: Therefore
good works are of no use, and it makes no difference whether you are pious or
impious, upright or depraved, a murderer or a preserver of life, and so forth.
Precisely the same conclusion could have been drawn from Solomon's words, for
he teaches that wisdom and knowledge of things no more make a man blessed than
a life of pleasure does, and hence that wisdom and knowledge of human and
divine things are utterly useless. But just as this man would here be acting
contrary to Solomon's intention, so that man would be acting contrary to the
intention of all the Apostles and Prophets.
