2. Second, concerning Bellarmine's rejection of Christ's merits being
imputed to us so that through them we are formally called and made
righteous, I answer that we are not excessively concerned with these
formalities. Since justification, whether taken actively or passively, is
not a substance properly capable of a form, we judge that it has no form
properly so called. We owe this manner of speaking first to scholastic
subtleties, not to the writings of our theologians. Chamier affirms that no
one among them can be found who says that through Christ's righteousness
thus imputed we are formally righteous or are called so; rather, the phrase
was arbitrarily imputed to us by the papists themselves. This, I think,
must be understood of the phrase's first invention and use. Otherwise,
after the Roman theologians located the form of justification in inherent
righteousness, nothing became more frequent among our theologians,
especially the methodical ones, than locating the form or formal cause of
justification in the imputation of Christ's righteousness. The term is
tropical and alludes to the forms of substances: a form is attributed to
God's action, and thus the imputation of Christ's righteousness is regarded
as the form of this action of God when he justifies the ungodly. That is,
what God regards in our justification is not a quality inherent in us, but
Christ's imputed righteousness. Accordingly, this justification formally,
if it is permissible to speak this way, is not the infusion of inherent
righteousness but the imputation of Christ's righteousness.

3. Bellarmine says that this manner of speaking conflicts with right reason,
but it does not conflict with Holy Scripture, to which right reason is not
opposed, or even with customary human speech. Though human forms of speech
do not abolish the forms God uses in his word, nothing here is foreign to
common speech. We are said to be formally righteous through Christ's
imputed righteousness in no different sense than a person whose debt is
paid by another is called and is free from that debt; or than an accused
person whose surety has paid the penalty owed by him and imputed to him
stands upright in court and by the judge's sentence is pronounced, is, and
is called free from guilt and punishment. Since this frequently happens
among human beings, why should it be denied to God?

Why could he not rightly call us righteous through the imputed righteousness
of Christ our surety, so that through his imputing it to us we are and are
called righteous?

Just as Adam's sin imputed to his descendants makes them formally sinners
before God, both in being and in name—a point Bellarmine himself admits in
book 4, *On the Loss of Grace*, chapter 10—so Christ's righteousness can be
imputed to believers in such a way that through it they are regarded and
called righteous. Bellarmine also concedes in this very passage that a
designation is sometimes, and indeed very frequently in ordinary usage,
derived from an extrinsic form. Thus a thing is called known from the
knowledge in the knower's mind, and visible from the viewer's sight. Why,
then, may a person not be called righteous at least by the sentence of the
most righteous and supreme Judge?
