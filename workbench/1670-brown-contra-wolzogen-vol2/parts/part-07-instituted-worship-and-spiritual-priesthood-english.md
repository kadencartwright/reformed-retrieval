At last, having finished what he had to say about natural worship, on page 303
and following he comes to explain instituted worship. He says that it contains
two things: first, the doctrine of faith and the exercise of religion; second,
certain ceremonies and duties connected with that exercise. What he says
about the ceremonies of the Old Testament does not bear on the matter, for no
one denies that they have now been abolished. And on pages 305–306 he rightly
says that under the Old Testament the Levitical priesthood was instituted and
assigned by God a distinctive duty concerning divine worship that no one else
was permitted to perform (Numbers 1:51; 3:10; 4:15), and that those who
violated this institution paid the penalty—namely Uzzah, 2 Samuel 5:6, and
Uzziah, 2 Chronicles 26. Moreover, on page 307 he says that no one was
permitted to sacrifice or offer his own sacrifice. But he adds there: “This
has now been abolished under the New Testament, and there is now no part of
divine worship consisting in a special approach to God that cannot, and
indeed ought not, be administered by individual Christians.”

Response. We readily grant that Christians now need no typological mediator
when they worship God or approach him in the exercise of worship. But
Velthuysen will never prove that in God’s instituted worship they need no
ministry, or that God has not now separated anyone and set him apart from
others to administer instituted worship; and we have proved the contrary. Let
us hear Velthuysen’s arguments: “We are now all a spiritual house, 1 Peter
2:5, 9, a holy priesthood to offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God
through Jesus Christ. But you are a chosen peo-”

[Volume II; printed p. 110; ONB canvas 118]

“ple, a royal priesthood, a holy nation—to proclaim the virtues of him who
called you into his marvelous light. Likewise Revelation 1:6: ‘He made us
kings and priests to God and his Father’; and chapter 5:10: ‘You made us kings
and priests to our God.’”

Response. This is precisely the argument used by all the fanatics who contend
for this liberty—or rather license—to prophesy. The Socinians or Photinians
also used it in former times, as may be seen in Gerhard, *On the Ecclesiastical
Ministry*, section 67. We need use no other answer than the one Gerhard gives
there, which is as follows:

“Response. 1. Just as it can by no means be inferred from the fact that
believers are called kings that any one of them can perform the office of a
magistrate without a calling, since the apostles speak of spiritual kings, so
neither can it be inferred from the fact that believers are called priests
that any one of them can perform the ecclesiastical ministry without a
calling, since the discourse is likewise about spiritual priests.

“2. For they are called spiritual priests not in respect of an ecclesiastical
office. Theophilus Nicolaides himself, in the *Defense of the Treatise on the
Mission of Ministers*, chapter 1, page 130, acknowledges that wherever the
word ‘priest’ is read concerning the Christian people, it applies to hearers
as much as to teachers; he adds that this can be visibly demonstrated from the
circumstances of each passage.

“3. Rather, it is in respect of spiritual sacrifices to be offered to God, as
Peter himself says in the preceding verse 5: ‘You are a holy priesthood to
offer spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Christ Jesus.’ Such
sacrifices are prayer, Psalm 141:2; Revelation 5:8; 8:4; thanksgiving, Hebrews
13:15; beneficence toward the poor, Philippians 4:18; Hebrews 13:16; the
mortification of the old man, Romans 12:1; and martyrdom undertaken for
Christ, Philippians 2:17; 2 Timothy 4:16, and so forth. Such sacrifices can be
offered by all the godly”

[Volume II; printed p. 111; ONB canvas 119]

“as spiritual priests. Augustine on Psalm 94: ‘If we are God’s temple, our
soul is God’s altar. What is God’s sacrifice? We place a sacrifice on the
altar when we praise God.’ The same author, book 20 of *The City of God*,
chapter 10: ‘We are called priests because we are members of the one Priest.’

“4. Although the preaching of the gospel also belongs among the spiritual
sacrifices, Malachi 1:11; Romans 15:16, nevertheless it cannot be inferred
from the title ‘spiritual priests,’ attributed to all the godly, that this
spiritual sacrifice—namely preaching the gospel undertaken in the public
assembly of the church—belongs to everyone. The reason for the title is drawn
from the other spiritual sacrifices, which everyone can offer, and not from
this one, which is by no means common to everyone.

“5. This is clearly inferred from the apostle’s words in 1 Corinthians 12:29:
‘Are all prophets? Are all teachers?’ All believers are spiritual priests,
yet they are not all immediately teachers or prophets, since not all are
furnished with the gift of prophecy or called to the church’s ministry.
Ephesians 4:11: ‘He gave some as apostles, others as prophets, others as
teachers and pastors, for the work of ministry.’ Therefore, just as not all
are prophets or apostles, so also not all are pastors and teachers.

“6. Nor is there any force in the objection that Peter adds that the godly are
a royal priesthood in order to proclaim the virtues of him who called them
from darkness into his marvelous light. A distinction must be made between
the general mandate and calling that all the godly receive when they are
invested with Christianity, and by which they are required to render God the
praise due to him; to confess in words and deeds the one who called them into
the fellowship of the church; to instruct their own households privately in
true godliness, Deuteronomy 6:20; to take care that Christ’s word”

[Volume II; printed p. 112; ONB canvas 120]

“dwells richly among them; to teach and admonish one another with psalms,
hymns, and spiritual songs, Ephesians 5:19; Colossians 4:16; and to comfort one
another with God’s word, 1 Thessalonians 5:18, and so forth—and the special
calling by which the ministry of word and sacraments in the public assembly of
the church is entrusted, with the church’s public consent, to particular
persons fit for it. That this calling is not common to all Christians is clear
from 1 Corinthians 12:29; Ephesians 4:11; James 3:1. 7. The administration of
the sacraments belongs to this special calling, as is gathered from 1
Corinthians 4:1. Nowhere, however, is the mutual administration of the
sacraments either commanded or permitted to all believers. Therefore the
public ministry of the word does not belong to everyone either.” So far
Gerhard. We have nothing to add to this.

We must nevertheless see what Velthuysen says next on page 38: “And it is
especially ascribed to Christ that he made believers under the New Testament
priests to God.” Response. This is entirely true, and it is also entirely true
that the same Christ made them kings. Does it therefore follow that everyone
may perform the pastoral office? I deny this consequence, and together with me
it will be denied by all who do not wish the office of the magistrate itself
destroyed. For it would follow no less firmly that anyone may ascend the
king’s throne, administer justice to everyone, put to death those whom they
judge guilty, and imitate Knipperdolling and the other Anabaptists, fanatics,
and madmen of Münster. Therefore what he next infers is no less worthless:
“so that now the whole congregation truly is holy.” This is nothing other than
what Korah and his brothers once objected in that conspiracy, Numbers 16:2–3:
“And rising up they stood before Moses—Korah and two hundred and fifty men of
the sons of Israel, leaders of the assembly, men summoned from the assembly
and men of renown. And gathering themselves against Moses and”

[Volume II; printed p. 113; ONB canvas 121]

“against Aaron they said to them, ‘You have gone far enough! For this entire
assembly, all these people, are holy, and Jehovah is among them. Why then do
you raise yourselves above Jehovah’s congregation?’” To this I will answer
nothing other than what we read there in verses 8–11: “Moses said to Korah,
‘Hear now, sons of Levi. Is it a small thing to you that the God of Israel
separated you from the congregation of Israel and brought you near himself to
perform the ministry of the Lord’s tabernacle, to stand before the assembly,
and to minister to it? He brought you near, and all your brothers, the sons of
Levi, with you; and do you seek the priesthood also? For this reason you and
your entire assembly conspire against the Lord. And what is Aaron, that you
murmur against him?’” And verses 19–21: “When Korah had gathered his entire
assembly against them at the entrance of the tent of meeting, Jehovah’s glory
appeared to the whole assembly, and Jehovah spoke to Moses and Aaron, saying,
‘Separate yourselves from the midst of this assembly, that I may consume them
in a moment.’” And verses 25–33: “Moses arose and went toward Dathan and
Abiram, with the elders of Israel following him, and spoke to the assembly,
saying, ‘Withdraw now from the tents of these wicked men, and touch nothing
that belongs to them, lest you perhaps be consumed in all their punishments.’
So they withdrew from the tabernacle of Korah, Dathan, and Abiram all around.
But Dathan and Abiram came out and stood, each at the entrance of his tent,
with their wives, sons, and little ones. And Moses said, ‘By this you will know
that the Lord sent me to perform all these works and that they did not come
from my own heart: if these people die as all other people die, or are visited
with the visitation of all people, the Lord did not send me. But if Jehovah
does something new, and the ground opens its mouth and swallows them with
everything that belongs to them, and they go down alive into the grave, then
you will know that these men have contemptuously provoked Jehovah.’ When he
finished speaking all these words, the ground beneath them split apart. The
earth opened its mouth and swallowed them and their households,”

[Volume II; printed p. 114; ONB canvas 122]

“and also all the people who belonged to Korah and all his possessions. Thus
they went down alive into the grave with everything that belonged to them, and
when the earth opened its mouth they perished from the midst of the
congregation.” If this answer is not enough, let him read Jude 11 and know that
God is of an unchangeable nature, Hebrews 13:8.

But he continues and says: “And all and each of us now, in our own person,
have the freedom to enter the sanctuary; therefore all believers now also
share in the altar and the sacrifices.” Response. Velthuysen’s case is utterly
wretched, since it cannot be defended by any arguments but those whose
consequence everyone acquainted with the terms cannot help denying. What?
Does Velthuysen think anyone can be deceived by such rotten arguments? Who
does not see that from this premise it could with equal right be inferred that
women and infants can perform all ecclesiastical duties, administer the
sacraments, exercise discipline, and so forth? And who would not marvel at a
man who infers from this that “to wish to introduce in the New Testament a
particular kind of worship or administration for whose celebration some, and
not others, are sanctified is to wish to introduce the Levitical priesthood”?
Nothing more absurd could have been inferred. For the administration of
instituted worship by people separated and ordained according to God’s
prescription was not peculiar to the Levitical priesthood, but something
common to every state of the church—whether before the law, under the law, or
under the gospel. What was peculiar to the Levitical priesthood was that this
worship was celebrated by such a family, namely the Levitical family, a
custom that does not now obtain. And we have proved that under the New
Testament Christ appropriated these administrations to certain persons who
were, as it were, consecrated and separated. Indeed, nature itself teaches
that this ought to be done. Did not all nations that had any worship of God
also have their own priests to celebrate it? But he adds another argument
there on page 309: “If”

[Volume II; printed p. 115; ONB canvas 123]

“perhaps one Christian applies himself to one sacred work and another to some
other work, no difference in holiness is thereby produced between one work
and another, or between one person and another.” Response. O marvelous
insight! What a subtle disputant we have in Velthuysen! But, my Velthuysen, if
Christ applies some people to one sacred work and forbids others to perform
that same work, would not some difference follow between this work and other
common works, and between these persons and others?

He continues: “No one now blesses, forgives sins, or distributes the Supper
insofar as he is a priest, nor is there any longer a power of priests.”
Response. Everyone grants that there is now no typological sanctuary and that
there are now no priests properly so called. Yet there are gospel ministers,
stewards of God’s mysteries, and public ecclesiastical ministers instituted
by Christ. Instrumentally, as God’s servants, though not as Levitical priests,
they bless, forgive sins (Matthew 18:18; John 20:23), and administer the
Supper. “All Christians,” he says, “know themselves worthy and approach the
Supper; people no longer go to God through the preacher. And the preacher who
says that the assembly approaches God through his prayers, over which he
presides for the sake of order, either reintroduces the Levitical priesthood
or intrudes himself into the office proper to Christ. People are not now
unclean for several days, nor do they need to be freed from that uncleanness
before they approach the public worship of God, as lepers and women after
childbirth once did.”

Response. It is true that Christians now need no typological mediators, yet
they do need ministers. Absurd would be the pastor who, in public prayer,
either thought or said that the people approached God through him as mediator
or type of the Mediator. If Velthuysen knows any such men, let him try to name
them so that they may pay the penalty. Otherwise,

[Volume II; printed p. 116; ONB canvas 124]

he insinuates a mere and utterly absurd slander here. 2. But what do these
things have to do with the matter? Will it follow from them that there are no
pastors distinct from the flock, or that there is no duty proper to pastors
that others may not perform? He will not prove this consequence in a thousand
attempts. 3. There is now no legal or ceremonial purity or impurity. Yet there
is moral purity and impurity, and one who is unclean with this impurity ought
not to approach the sacred assembly until he is cleansed from it in Christ’s
blood through faith and repentance. If he is scandalous, he ought not to be
admitted even if he approaches, but must be resolutely kept away.

He continues on pages 310–312: “It is impossible to imagine what kind of
holiness, if not ceremonial, one action must have above another, or one person
above another, under the New Testament. But now under the New Testament there
are no instituted ceremonies for consecrating persons; no place, time, or
person is now designated, chosen, or sanctified for any part of worship under
the New Testament. As for the laying on of a hand, it possessed no peculiar
holiness under the Old Testament. Many who were not specially holy used it,
and those upon whom this ceremony was used possessed no peculiar holiness,
Numbers 8:10.”

Response. 1. That holiness which you cannot grasp even in imagination is the
state or fixed relation a thing or person has when separated from common use
and consecrated, assigned, and dedicated to God, because “everything holy is
holy to God.” It is separated to God either for some active use—that is, to
perform some particular action for God—as the priests and Levites under the
Old Testament and the pastors and teachers under the New Testament were
separated and consecrated. These latter are separated from the body of the
faithful, dedicated and appropriated to God, consecrated and appointed,
assigned and sanctified to perform a sacred office—

[Volume II; printed p. 117; ONB canvas 125]

see Acts 13:2; Ephesians 3:5—or for a passive use, that is, to be used for God,
as the temple, altars, and similar things once were. And because the
Israelites were peculiarly separated to God, they were said to be sanctified,
and God was their Sanctifier, Exodus 31:13; Leviticus 20:7; 21:8; 22:32. Thus
God sanctified the firstborn in Israel to himself, Numbers 3:13. The Sabbath
was sanctified because it was separated to God, Genesis 2:3; Exodus 20:11.
Jeremiah was sanctified, Jeremiah 1:3. The priests were sanctified—that is,
consecrated to perform the priesthood—Exodus 28:3, 41; 29:1, 21, 29; 30:30;
40:13; Leviticus 8:12, 30. Hence the priests and Levites were also called holy
because God had peculiarly sanctified, separated, and ordained them to perform
the office, Exodus 22:31; Leviticus 21:6–7; 2 Chronicles 23:6; 35:3; Numbers
16:5, 7. So also the apostles, Ephesians 3:5. Velthuysen’s imagination can be
corrected to some extent by these things.

And although very many things under the Old Testament were ceremonially holy
because they testified to people that all holiness must be had in Christ and
the curse removed through him alone, many things were nevertheless not called
holy for that reason. Rather, very many were so called because they were
consecrated to God, God’s worship, and sacred use, Exodus 30:33, 37; 40:9;
Leviticus 12:4; 16:4; 19:8, 24; 21:22; 22:2, 3, 10; 23:20; 24:7; 27:9. See
Ravanella under the word *holy*. Hence a holy convocation, holy day, holy city,
holy mountain, holy house, holy land, and holy vessels were called such for
reasons other than the one Velthuysen names. “But it must be noted here,” says
Ravanella there, “that it was unlawful to use holy things—that is, things
consecrated to God—for common uses, as is clear concerning a firstborn ox,
which is holy to God,” Deuteronomy 15:19. See also the material he cites there
from Origen, Homily 9 on Leviticus. Read what Gillespie has in *Ecclesiastical
Polity*, part 2, book 2, treatise 1, chapter 4, section 9. Moreover, there is
also a certain instituted ceremony—

[Volume II; printed p. 118; ONB canvas 126 (upper portion)]

namely the laying on of hands—in separating pastors, as we shall afterwards
make plain. Whatever its use under the Old Testament may have been, and of
whatever character those who laid on their hands may have been, those upon
whom they were laid in Numbers 8:10 were nevertheless consecrated and
separated to God, and thereby sanctified. Finally, although Velthuysen now
recognizes no person as holier than another by reason of office, he ought not
therefore to say that absolutely all actions are of the same nature and that
none is holier than another. Is not the act of baptizing infants holier than
the act of washing one’s hands? Is not the celebration of the Lord’s Supper
holier than the act of eating supper at home every night?

What follows will be examined point by point in its proper place. We now
propose another assertion.
