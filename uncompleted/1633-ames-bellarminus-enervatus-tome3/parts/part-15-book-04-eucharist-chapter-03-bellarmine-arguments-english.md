## Chapter III

On the possibility of the presence of the Lord's body and blood under the
species of bread and wine, the first question is whether it does not imply a
contradiction for one whole body to be in multiple places at the same time.
Bellarmine denies it; we affirm it.

#### Bellarmine's arguments

### Thesis I

Bellarmine. Matthew 19 says, “With God all things are possible.”

Protestant reply. 1. It no more follows from this that it does not imply a
contradiction for one body to be in multiple places at the same time than that
it is not a contradiction for the same body at the same time to be and not to
be. 2. Bellarmine himself, in chapter 2, teaches that this statement must be
limited in the following manner: “God can do all things which can in any
manner exist—that is, that which does not imply a contradiction.”

2. Bellarmine. Those things imply a contradiction which conflict with the
essence of a thing. But being in a place is not of the essence of a body.

Protestant reply. 1. It is not only those things which conflict with the
essence of a thing that imply a contradiction. Bellarmine quibbles
sophistically in this passage, and Bellarmine argues soundly and truly in this
passage: a contradiction is implied here, and yet sophistry is not of the
essence of Bellarmine. 2. Being in some space is of the essence of a body, for
a body is defined by its dimensions: it has length, breadth, and height. Thus
Bellarmine himself says in chapter 5, “What is a body but extension in length,
breadth, and depth?” But a body's spatial extension does not differ in reality
from these dimensions of it.

3. Bellarmine. Christ appeared to the apostle Paul in Acts 9 and 22. He was at
the same time in the highest heaven and on earth, or in the air near the
earth.

Protestant reply. 1. In this argument Bellarmine contradicts the weightiest of
his own scholastics. For although they contend most absurdly that the same
body can be in one place in its own natural, or circumscriptive, mode and in
many other places in a sacramental mode, they nevertheless deny that it can
be in a natural mode, even by God's absolute power, at the same time in

[1632 witness | printed p. 95]

multiple places. So Thomas, Part 3, question 35, article 1, reply 4; Quodlibet
4, article 2; and on Book 4, distinction 10, question 1, article 1, and
distinction 44, question 2, reply 4. So likewise Bonaventure, on Book 4,
distinction 44, *On the Dowry of Subtlety*, question 1; Capreolus, distinction
10, question 1, article 3; Tallud, question 2; Durandus, question 1, number 3,
and 34, question 3, number 8, and distinction 44, question 6, reply 1; Henry,
Quodlibet 9, question 32; Giles, Quodlibet 1, question 1; Hervaeus, Quodlibet
11, question 3; and Ferrara, on Book 4 of *Against the Gentiles*, chapter 68,
as cited by John de Rada Arragonius, bishop of Patti, *Theological
Controversies*, p. controv. 8, article 1. For Bellarmine will not say that
Christ appeared to Paul at that time in a sacramental mode. 2. The sounder
papists do not dare to affirm how Christ appeared to Paul. Lyra says that this
is not written in Acts 2. Dionysius the Carthusian, on 1 Corinthians 9, seems
to interpret it as concerning the intellectual eye in a rapture. Thomas and
Cajetan, on 1 Corinthians 9 and 15, leave it in doubt. 3. A little afterward
Bellarmine affirms—and we do not entirely deny—that Christ could have
descended privately and for a brief time from heaven. 4. Christ nevertheless
seems to have appeared to Paul in the same manner in which he appeared to
Stephen in Acts 7, while remaining in the heavens.

Bellarmine 1. His light shone around Paul so as to make him entirely blind.

Protestant reply. 1. Nothing is said in the text about “his light,” that is,
light proceeding from Christ's body. 2. From the fact that Paul was blinded,
the contrary follows instead. Thus Cajetan, on 1 Corinthians 15: “Although in
the Acts of the Apostles it is narrated that Jesus addressed Paul and was
heard by Paul, it is nevertheless never narrated that Jesus was seen by Paul,
but rather the opposite, since it narrates that at midday a light from heaven
shone around Paul and his companions, and that so great a light overwhelmed
Paul that it deprived him of the act of seeing.”

Bellarmine 2. A certain light was also seen by his companions, and they heard
the sound of a voice as well, which is a most certain argument that Christ
appeared on earth or in the air nearby.

Protestant reply. It is a most certain argument that a singular light appeared
on earth or in the air, not Christ.

Bellarmine 3. If the voice had come from heaven, all would have heard it
equally.

Protestant reply. Scripture testifies to the contrary in John 12:28–29.

Bellarmine 4. Paul doubted who the Lord was, and therefore asked, “Who are
you, Lord?”

Protestant reply. 1. Paul asked who it was who was speaking. 2. Paul, a
stranger to Christ and ignorant of his majesty, could doubt whether that was
Christ whom he was seeing in the heavens.

Bellarmine 5. If Paul had seen Christ only in heaven, he would not have been a
suitable witness of the resurrection, for people could easily have slandered
his testimony.

Protestant reply. There were other suitable witnesses whose testimonies
people nevertheless slandered. 2. The vision of Christ in heaven was no more

[1632 witness | printed p. 96]

exposed to slander than it was on earth.

4. Bellarmine. The fathers teach that one thing can be in multiple places at
the same time.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine himself testifies to the contrary in Tome 1,
*On the Incarnation*, book 3, chapter 11: “Very many of the fathers deduce from
that passage in John 3—‘No one ascends into heaven except he who descended
from heaven, the Son of Man who is in heaven’—that Christ is God and man,
because when he was in body only on earth, he nevertheless said that he was
in heaven, namely in his deity. But if the opinion of the ubiquitaries
(papists) is true, this argument concludes nothing. For the Eutychians,
instructed by the ubiquitaries (papists), will say that Christ was then also in
heaven and on earth in his flesh.”

5. Bellarmine. God is at the same time in infinitely many places, and by the
same rationale the soul is whole in every part of the body.

Protestant reply. 1. Concerning God, Bellarmine himself acknowledges in the
passage just cited that God is distinguished in the Scriptures from all
creatures by this: that he is everywhere. Therefore to argue from God to a
body in the question of presence in many places is excessively carnal and
crass. 2. Concerning the soul, what is commonly said—that it is whole in the
whole and whole in every part of the body—is false and implies a
contradiction. 3. The same soul is not in different bodies at the same time,
is it? Therefore neither is it in different places.

Bellarmine. No reason at all can be devised why God could not preserve the
soul in a severed arm as it was before the severing.

Protestant reply. Supposing that the rational soul was in the arm before and
still remains in the body, then the reason is not imaginary: it cannot happen
that one and the same soul is at the same time divided and undivided, and that
with respect to itself.

6. Bellarmine. There are mysteries of the faith either more difficult or
equally difficult, and this is what we are treating.

Protestant reply. This begs the question. For unless it is conceded to be
possible for one body to be in multiple places at the same time, no true
mystery can be equally difficult. No possible thing, much less a true thing,
however great it may be, is as difficult as that which is altogether
impossible, however very small the thing may otherwise be.

7. Bellarmine. One place can contain two bodies commensurate with itself at
the same time. Therefore one body will also be able to fill two places.

Protestant reply. 1. It can in no manner happen that one place contains at the
same time two bodies commensurate with itself. For if the capacity of the
place is commensurate with one body, the other body added to it must exceed
that same capacity. 2. Thomas, with the whole cohort of Thomists whose names
we reported a little earlier from John de Rada Arragonius, concede the premise
and nevertheless contend sharply that one body cannot be in two places
locally, because

[1632 witness | printed p. 97]

it would be divided from itself. They therefore deny the consequence of the
argument advanced.

Bellarmine. If one body could not be locally in two places because it would be
divided from itself, then certainly it could not be there sacramentally
either, for the same reason. For to be somewhere sacramentally means to be by
a real and true presence in multiple hosts or altars, because a real presence
in so many altars, but not in the intervening places, would seem to remove the
indivision of the thing no less than filling multiple places would.

Protestant reply. We acknowledge and embrace both this consequent and this
consequence. On that account we say that thanks are owed to Bellarmine,
because he not only acknowledges but also demonstrates that the principles
of Thomas and all the Thomists conflict with the real presence of Christ's
body in the Eucharist, and consequently that all of them—who nevertheless
were the principal defenders of this fabrication—contradict themselves in
defending a presence of this kind.
