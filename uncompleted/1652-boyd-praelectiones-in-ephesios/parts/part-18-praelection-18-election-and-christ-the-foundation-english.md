It follows now that we should descend to its parts or species, namely election
and reprobation. We begin with election, as the principal species, on which we
will need to dwell and in which various matters must be considered: its
causes, its adjuncts, its subject about which it is concerned or the object it
occupies, its effects, and other things of this kind which are otherwise
common to the decree of predestination generally.

[Marginalia: Lecture XVIII.]

The word *election* has many meanings in Scripture. Sometimes it signifies
that common and outward election by which the bodies of certain nations are
separated from others and sanctified to the Lord as a peculiar people through
a common calling by the word and sacraments, as means appointed for the
particular calling. Thus Scripture speaks throughout of the people of Israel
as chosen by Jehovah from the remaining Gentiles “as his own peculiar
portion” and “the line of his inheritance.” Sometimes it means appointment to
some particular office, whether in the civil order, as Saul and David were
chosen for the kingdom, or in the Church, as the twelve were chosen for the
apostolate. Sometimes it means that particular election by which God from
eternity set some apart and appointed them as his children, which is our sole
subject here. Sometimes it means the execution in time of that election made
from eternity. In this sense Christ’s statement in John 15 seems to be
understood when he says that he chose and appointed his disciples to go and
bear fruit and that their fruit should remain. He indicates that by his
effectual calling he had actually separated them from the rest. Yet this use
of the word is certainly improper, and we will need to discuss it among the
effects of election. Finally, election is sometimes taken metonymically for
the elect themselves, according to the metonymy of adjunct for subject common
among the Hebrews. Thus Romans 11 says that “election attained what it
sought,” namely the righteousness that is by faith—that is, the elect
themselves attained it, while the rest were hardened. In the same way we
commonly use the whole of “Christianity” to mean all Christians.

[Marginalia: The various meanings of election. Deuteronomy 7:7 and 32:9. Luke
6:13. John 15:16. Romans 11:9.]

Election in that third sense, then, which is the sole subject of what follows,
can be and commonly is defined in various ways, with a difference in words
rather than in the thing itself. Augustine, sometimes taking predestination
for election since he was speaking of the predestination of the saints, says
that it is “the preparation of grace,” and elsewhere that it is “the
foreknowledge and preparation of God’s gifts, by which all who are delivered
are certainly delivered, while the rest are left in the mass of perdition.”
Lombard, book 1 of the *Sentences*, says not ineptly that it is “the
preparation of grace in the present and of glory in the future.” Others say
more briefly that it is “the predestination of a creature to a gracious and
blessed end.” Others define it at greater length as “God’s decree by which he
destined certain people to glorious grace in their salvation and in eternal
life to be obtained through Christ.” Finally, others call it “God’s counsel
by which he wisely, freely, and immutably purposed and decreed within himself
to choose certain ones from the whole body of creatures for their saving
communion with God and for the praise of his glorious grace.”

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 10.
Lombard, book 1, distinction 41.]

While we especially approve and embrace the judgments of others, we will say
that the decree of election is “that firm, free, gracious, and eternal purpose
of God the Father concerning us—all of us who are believers in heaven or on
earth and all who will ever be believers—concerning all, I say, both those
already saved and those to be saved hereafter: that from his own mere good
pleasure and gratuitous benevolence they are to be called out of sin and death
to life, adopted to himself as children in Christ, sanctified, and glorified
eternally, to the praise of his glorious grace.”

[Marginalia: Definition.]

Here we place *purpose* as the genus and take it as equivalent to counsel or
decree, that is, for what someone has purposed or determined within himself
that he will do. For what, I ask, is it to decree or determine something
within oneself except to purpose to do it? Some make the following distinction
between *thesis*, or purpose, and a decree or determination: the former is
properly an act of the intellect, or a judgment of the divine mind, joined
with divine foreknowledge, while the latter is an act of the will joined with
predetermination. I would certainly approve this very strongly if, because of
the dullness of my mind, I could sufficiently grasp and distinctly comprehend
it. But if they mean that we first judge something worth doing, or best and
most advisable to do, which is a function of the mind or judgment; and then
that we will that same thing, or decree and determine within ourselves—

[Marginalia: Explanation. Trelcatius. Note this carefully.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 104 | De Prædestinatione p. 62]

—that it is to be done, which is an act and function of our will or choice;
while the name *counsel* embraces both of these acts—then with God’s help we
certainly see something with them in this respect, and what we see we approve
with one accord.

Only let us always maintain that all these things are attributed to our God
solely according to our manner of understanding and conceiving. According to
that manner, for the sake of our clearer understanding, we distinguish these
two acts in the otherwise entirely simple counsel of God. We do this with
respect both to the purpose and to the decree itself, as others explain God’s
counsel by purpose and decree, in the way we have just set out. For they
themselves distinguish in each of these two acts or preordinations: one
concerning the end and the other concerning the means to the end. The former
is prior, not in temporal order but in the order of nature, and the latter is
subsequent. For God, who alone is supremely wise, the author of all reason and
order, and unable to act apart from order and reason, first purposed the end
of his counsel within himself and decreed it; he next ordained the means, both
those which immediately attain the end and those more remotely ordered to it.
In this respect we agree with them without any hesitation. As far as we have
advanced, we walk by the same rule, think the same thing, and do not disagree,
trusting that God will also supply and show in his own time what we lack, as
the Apostle teaches us in Philippians 3.

[Marginalia: The distinction of the divine counsel into two acts. Philippians
3:16.]

But lest we seem to have imagined and fabricated distinctions of this kind
from our own judgment, without any foundation in Holy Scripture, come then:
let us derive each of them from the testimonies of sacred Scripture. First,
the Apostle makes a clear distinction between the divine purpose and the
eternal decree of election or predestination itself in Romans 9, when he says,
“that the purpose which is according to election might stand,” and so forth.
That is, it is God’s purpose concerning people to be chosen. For, as will be
clear in its proper place, there is also another purpose of God concerning
some who are not chosen but passed by and reprobated. So too in Ephesians 1:
“predestined according to the purpose of him who works all things in all,
according to the counsel of his will.” There the decree of predestination is
evidently distinguished from the divine purpose as something prior by nature,
because the judgment of the mind is by nature prior to the decree of the
will. The purpose has already been said to be a judgment of the divine mind or
intellect, just as the determination or decree itself is an act of the will.
The former distinction, therefore, agrees very well with the words of
Scripture.

[Marginalia: Confirmation. First part. Romans 9:11. Ephesians 1:11.]

As for the latter distinction, by which that divine counsel, both in its
aspect as purpose and as decree, is distinguished into the preordination of
the end and of the means, the Apostle teaches it to us in that notable passage
of Romans 8. There he distinguishes not only the decree from its execution,
but also subdivides the decree itself into two acts. He places its execution
in these three things: calling, justification, and glorification. He places
the decree in these two: first, previous recognition or foreknowledge, by
which God recognizes some people before others as his own; and second,
predestination, by which he decreed from eternity to conform those same people
to Christ. In the same way Peter says in his first epistle that believers are
elect according to foreknowledge, unto sanctification of the Spirit. There,
foreknowledge contains both the purpose and the decree concerning the end,
that is, concerning a person simply taken up to life and glory. The words
*election* and *predestination*, however, contain the decree concerning the
means to the end, namely the restoration of a person fallen into sin and his
conformity to the image of Christ through effectual calling, adoption,
sanctification, and glorification. For the word *predestination* properly
embraces the whole order and plan of the grace and glory to be imparted by God
to the elect in Christ.

[Marginalia: Second part. Romans 8:29 and following. 1 Peter 1:2.]

Far be it from us in passages of this kind to admit the impious and foolish
invention of some who interpret the foreknowledge or previous recognition
asserted by the Apostle as the foresight of future faith. We will overturn it
in passing from the Apostle’s own words in this way, although, with God’s
help, we will refute this error more thoroughly below in its proper place when
we consider the causes of eternal election. Those who are predestined to be
conformed to Christ—that is, to be children by adoption and to be righteous
and holy, for so they will become conformed to Christ—are predestined to
believe, since we are justified through faith and likewise receive adoption
as children through faith. But those who are foreknown are predestined to be
conformed to the image of Christ. These are the Apostle’s own words.
Therefore, those who are foreknown by God are predestined to believe.

[Marginalia: A refutation in passing. Note the syllogism.]

God did not, then, first foresee that these would believe and afterward
predestine them to this conformity with Christ, as our opponents idly claim.
For whoever God foreknew, or foresaw would believe, he foresaw and foreknew for
the very reason that he himself decreed that they would believe when he
decreed to conform them to Christ’s image. Thus Justin Martyr correctly
describes the elect in this way: “those who are foreknown to believe.” Lombard
also correctly interprets the Apostle’s words, “Whom he foreknew, he
predestined,” that is, as he says, “by supplying grace he prepared them to
believe through the word preached.”

[Marginalia: Justin Martyr, *Against Trypho*. Lombard, commentary on Romans,
chapter 8.]

This is the familiar use of the verb *to know* in Scripture whenever it is
emphatically attributed, whether affirmatively or negatively, to God speaking
of creatures: it means to embrace and approve. Thus Psalm 1 says, “Jehovah
knows the way of the righteous, but the way of the ungodly will perish.”
Matthew 7 says, “Depart from me, you who practice iniquity; I never knew you.”
And 2 Timothy 2 says, “God knows those who are his,” not by bare knowledge but
also by the firm purpose and approval by which he recognizes them as his own.
For the Apostle there calls this knowledge of his “the firm foundation of
God,” on which the salvation of the elect is founded and supported. Finally,
in Romans 11, those whom “God foreknew” are afterward called “elect according
to the election of grace.” He therefore foreknew by graciously approving,
embracing with gratuitous love, and taking them up to eternal life, not
because he foresaw either merits, faith, or any other good that would be in
them before he destined them to life and grace.

[Marginalia: Psalm 1:6. Matthew 7:23. 2 Timothy 2:19. Romans 11:2 and 5.]

Thus Augustine himself, in book 2 of *On the Good of Perseverance*, takes
foreknowledge for predestination when he explains the Apostle’s statement,
“God has not rejected his people whom he foreknew.” Elsewhere he calls those
people children in foreknowledge who are inscribed with unshaken stability in
the Father’s memorial. Cyril likewise says on John that “Christ knows his
sheep, choosing and foreseeing them for eternal life.” As the Apostle says,
“The Lord has not rejected his people whom he foreknew.” Just as the Lord is
said not to know those whom he rejects, as with the foolish virgins—“Truly I
say to you, I do not know you”—so he is correctly said to know those whom he
predestines and preordains to salvation. Finally, Thomas explains the passage
in this way: those whom he foreknew by the knowledge of approval, these he
also predestined. He teaches that this knowledge of approval includes the
effectual will to confer grace.

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On Perseverance*, chapter 18. Cyril’s exposition on
John 7, chapter 6. Thomas on Romans 8.]

To return, then, to our subject: that former—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 105 | De Prædestinatione p. 63]

—act is the part and beginning of
the divine purpose and decree by which God claims certain people yet to be
created, passing by the rest, as his own peculiar people. As Peter says in his
first epistle, God takes them up into love and grace and, by taking them up,
makes them vessels of honor and mercy. This act is absolute in every respect,
whether you consider God who chooses, the person chosen, or the end to which
the person is taken up. God does no injury to anyone if, by that supreme right
and most absolute power which he has over every creature, he foreknows and
takes up from his mere will those whom he wills, but not everyone. We human
beings frequently use a similar freedom toward other human beings, although
we have no right or dominion over them. At our own choice we benefit one
rather than another, though both are equally unknown to us, since we either do
not wish or are unable to benefit everyone.

[Marginalia: Resumption of the distinction. 1 Peter 2:9.]

The latter act, which is by no means to be separated from the former but is
distinguished from it in this way only for the sake of order and our better
understanding, is the purpose and decree of saving or conferring glory. By it
God appoints and sets apart those same people, foreknown as his own in the way
just stated and destined to fall in Adam, to attain eternal life and salvation
in and through Christ; or by it he decreed to give those same people to Christ
to be saved and to unite them to himself through Christ.

[Marginalia: Second act.]

These two acts, thus distinguished from one another to make our knowledge
easier, agree first in the principal cause, namely God who chooses; then in
the antecedent cause, because each depends on God’s mere will and good
pleasure, or *eudokia*, beyond which it has no antecedent cause or inward
impelling cause; and finally in the object about which it is concerned,
because each concerns the same elect people.

[Marginalia: Agreements between the two.]

They differ from one another, first, in that the former is designated more by
the name *foreknowledge*, while the latter is designated more by *election*
and *predestination*, as is clear from what has been said.

2. By the former people are appointed to grace and glory, while by the latter
the means by which that grace is conferred and manifested are subordinated
and designated. Thus the former fixes the goal and terminus, while the latter
lays the road to its execution and completion.

3. The former regards people absolutely, as yet to be created, without any
regard to good or evil in the creature. The latter concerns the same people
not only as created but also as fallen into sin, and so regards the mass of
humankind as already corrupted by the stain of sin.

4. It follows that by the former, which regards a person considered simply
and without respect to any good or evil in that person, grace was shown to the
elect simply. Such was the grace obtained by the blessed angels who persevered
in good. By the latter, which regards a person as fallen and therefore
wretched, grace was shown to the elect mercifully, that is, joined with mercy,
by graciously raising the fallen person through Christ.

5. Finally, while the former does not actually include respect to Christ the
Mediator and does not descend that far—for Christ the Mediator is the first
and principal means of executing the decree, as well as the remedy and
restoration of humanity already fallen, as will soon be explained—the latter
necessarily includes respect to him. For all who are chosen are chosen in him
to grace and salvation. It is utterly impious and absurd to imagine or dream
of any election or predestination to glory outside him, since, as some say,
he is the foundation of election’s execution with respect to its beginning,
means, and end. Or, as others speak more suitably in my judgment, although the
matter comes to the same thing, he is the bond of election in whom and through
whom we are united and joined to God as children through election.

[Marginalia: Differences. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Perkins.]

From this we draw two corollaries. First, the object of the counsel of
election, which includes both the aforesaid acts, is a human being viewed
under every consideration, without excluding any time or condition. For
example, God purposed within himself a person capable of being created, to be
created, created, capable of falling, fallen, and restored. For with
God there is no difference of time, no “will be” or “was,” but a permanent
“is,” all together and boundless, without limits or the continual flow of
succession. The things, therefore, that we distinguish in his counsel for the
sake of order and clarity are in him one entirely simple act, which
consequently excludes no time, condition, or consideration of humanity.

[Marginalia: First corollary.]

Second, the whole nature of this decree can suitably be explained and
comprehended by these two things—the causes, objects, epithets, and other
things of this kind being set aside, for although these supply a clear
description suited to teaching, they by no means enter into the nature of the
thing itself—namely, by the action itself and by its mode. The action is that
by which God foreknows, decrees concerning, separates, and destines and
prepares his own for that glorious end which surpasses the powers of nature.
The mode is that by which he wills to save his own in Christ as their head and
through Christ as their Mediator, and to lead them to that destined end.

[Marginalia: Second corollary.]

Since the explanation of that mode is now the next matter required for a
fuller understanding of the nature of election, and since that mode is wholly
placed in Christ as the head and Mediator of the elect or predestined, it
follows that we should speak here of Christ considered in this way.

[Marginalia: Transition.]

Christ can be considered in two ways in this business of predestination or
election. First, he may be considered as the Logos, consubstantial and
coeternal with the eternal Father, the eternal Son of God, I say. Thus he,
together with the Father and the Holy Spirit, chose us from eternity, as we
will teach below. Second, he may be considered as the Mediator and head of the
elect, designated and foreknown from eternity, “before the foundations of the
world were laid,” as Peter says in his first epistle. Thus, both because of
his office and because of his human nature, he bears the character not so much
of one choosing and predestining as of one chosen and predestined.

[Marginalia: On Christ, the head of the elect. 1 Peter 1:20.]

Hence some orthodox teachers establish as the first division of election one
drawn from its subjects: first, the election of Christ; then, the election of
those united to Christ. The election of Christ is that predestination by which
God from eternity designated his only-begotten Son, even as the God-man, as
the head of angels and human beings and the Mediator between God and humanity.
This is shown both from the passage of Peter just cited and from other
testimonies of Holy Scripture. Thus in Isaiah 42 he is called, “Behold my
servant whom I sustain, my elect in whom my soul takes gracious pleasure.” The
Evangelist renders these words in Matthew 12: “Behold that servant of mine,
whom I chose, that beloved of mine, in whom my soul is well pleased.”
Likewise, in Peter’s second chapter of his first epistle, Christ is called “a
living stone, rejected indeed by human beings but chosen and precious before
God.” Three uses—

[Marginalia: So Polanus. Isaiah 42:1. Matthew 12:18. 1 Peter 2:5.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 106 | De Prædestinatione p. 64]

—arise for us from this consideration. First, since by God’s eternal decree
the Son of God himself was chosen and
designated to be the Son of God even with respect to his human nature and the
head of angels and human beings, it is lawful for no angel or person to reject
him. That would be insulting and sacrilegious toward God and would exclude us
forever from Christ’s grace and communion, since God from eternity appointed
no other way in which he would communicate himself to humanity unto eternal
life and glory than in Christ and through Christ the God-man as Mediator.
Hence the absolute necessity of Christ for salvation is evident, since he is
the head of the elect, himself the first chosen of all, in whom the others
would be chosen.

[Marginalia: First use. Necessity.]

Second, we see how stable a foundation of eternal salvation God has laid for
us in Christ the Mediator. God did not devise this remedy only recently, or
after some thousands of years, to relieve the misery of lost humanity.
Instead, in his eternal counsel, before he had either created humanity or
allowed it to fall into sin and misery, he appointed this head and this
Mediator for us. Our faith can therefore rest upon him with the firmest
confidence as one appointed from all eternity by the immutable decree of God
the Father for our salvation. For since God foresaw from eternity that
humanity would fall and would not long stand in that integrity in which he was
to create it—and, indeed, had himself decreed from eternity within himself to
permit humanity’s fall—by his marvelous wisdom he appointed his only-begotten
Son as humanity’s Liberator, who would rescue and redeem lost humankind, at
least so far as the elect are concerned, from eternal destruction. His
incomparable goodness certainly shines in this: he anticipated our evil with
the remedy of his grace and predetermined the means of restoring to humanity
an even better life, namely in and through Christ, before humanity had lost
that former life by its own fault and, enticed by Satan’s snares, fallen into
the pit of eternal death.

[Marginalia: Second use. Stability.]

Finally, the purely and entirely gratuitous election of Christ himself as our
head and Mediator with respect to his human nature must be set before us like
the clearest mirror, in which we may see plainly how gratuitous our election
is. Augustine certainly showed us this use of it splendidly in that notable
chapter 15 of his book *On the Predestination of the Saints*. He demonstrates
in the following way that the election of the man Christ is entirely
gratuitous:

“There is also a most illustrious light of predestination and grace: the
Savior himself, the Mediator between God and humanity, the man Christ Jesus.
By what preceding merits of his own, either of works or of faith, did the
human nature in him obtain that he should be this? Let an answer be given, I
ask: from what did that man merit that, being taken into personal unity with
the Word coeternal with the Father, he should be the only-begotten Son of God?
What good of any kind in him preceded this? What had he previously done,
believed, or asked in order to attain this ineffable excellence? Did not the
man himself, by the Word’s action and assumption, begin to be the unique Son
of God from the moment he began to exist? Did not that woman full of grace
conceive the unique Son of God? Was not God’s unique Son born of the Holy
Spirit and the Virgin Mary, not by the desire of the flesh but by the singular
gift of God? Was there any need to fear that, as he grew older, that man would
sin by free choice? Or did he therefore have no free will, and did he not have
it all the more insofar as he could not serve sin? Human—that is, our—nature
singularly received in him, without any preceding merits of its own, all these
singularly marvelous things and any others which can most truly be called
peculiar to him.”

Thus far Augustine. He afterward applies these things splendidly to us, his
members, in order to maintain the gratuity of God’s grace in our election
against its enemies and sacrilegious assailants, as we will see in the proper
place.

[Marginalia: Third use. Gratuitous, so to speak. Augustine, book 1, *On the
Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 15.]

The consideration of Christ as the head of the elect and predestined is very
important. Some call him the means and bond of election through whom we can be
united and adhere to God. Others call him the subject in whom we were chosen,
since in ourselves we were neither capable of such dignity and excellence nor
able to adhere immediately to God who chooses. Still others call him the
foundation of election’s execution. In order to see more distinctly in the
business of election this comparison with a head and foundation, let us dwell
on it a little longer. We presuppose as a foundation that the divine Majesty
decreed from eternity to execute everything in the same manner and order in
which he executes in time all things pertaining to human salvation, and that
the author designated within himself, before setting his hand to the work,
the same order which the work would have outside his mind.

With this presupposed, let us regard the whole work of our salvation as a
magnificent building in which God wills the glorious riches of his grace to
shine eternally and to be displayed before everyone’s eyes. Let us compare
that eternal decree with the counsel or purpose of the person who undertakes
the construction of a building. It is evident that the foundation of the
elect, that is, of the people themselves who are to be saved, is Christ the
God-man and Mediator. As many as are living stones in this house must be built
upon him as the cornerstone, so that from them, together with Christ the
foundation, only one spiritual building arises.

[Marginalia: A longer consideration and exposition. Comparison.]

Just as the foundation must be laid before a house is built upon it, the
author of the work likewise purposed the same thing within his mind, whether
the work is already built or is still to be built. It necessarily follows
that, in planning the execution of that eternal decree, first in the order of
nature and according to our manner of understanding, the thought and counsel
of glorious God—if it is lawful to speak this way—concerned the laying of that
foundation itself, namely the God-man Mediator. For what else was our being
chosen to life in him than God’s determining from eternity to found us upon
that chosen and precious foundation and, by the foundation’s power and divine
strength, to make us, founded in him, living and precious stones also?

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

Yet it does not follow that this foundation was a moving cause which impelled
the lord of the house to construct the building he had decreed to prepare for
the praise of his glorious grace and of his wise power or powerful wisdom.
Rather, his decree to fashion such a building depended on his mere good
pleasure and the pleasure of his most free will. He willed his Son to be the
foundation of that building, that is, the part which would support the whole
remaining work and supply strength, life, weight, and worth to the entire
building. He decreed to plant such a vine that he willed his Christ to be its
root and trunk. Finally, he decreed to substitute such a person in the
devil’s place that, before designating all the members, he designated Christ
the God-man as the person’s future head.

[Marginalia: Anticipation of an objection.]

From this it is evident, as regards the material or object about which
election is concerned, that our election is so far from being capable of
consideration anywhere—

[Marginalia: Corollary.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 107 | De Prædestinatione p. 65 (through Lecture XVIII close)]

—outside Christ that Christ himself seems rather to occur first of all in
that object as the head, root, and foundation in whom God chose the rest who
are to be saved. For the one in whom the others are chosen must precede all
the others in the divine mind. So far was it from being the case that God
regarded us first and Christ afterward in this counsel of election and
predestination that, when he viewed us as fallen, he first provided a Mediator
for us. He destined, chose,
appointed, and indeed placed Christ as the foundation; then he chose us in him
as members to be given to him, shoots to be grafted into him, and stones to be
built upon him.

Indeed, in order to express more effectively the very close bond between
Christ and the elect in this matter, nothing prevents us from saying, in a
correct and sound sense, that Christ the Mediator alone was principally
chosen and that all the rest were chosen in him as their head, root, and
foundation, since in some sense he contains all the rest within himself by
his power. God who chooses, as if in one simultaneous intuition of his mind,
beheld Christ the God-man as a kind of head pregnant with all the elect—that
is, with his mystical body—and in this way foreknew and preordained him. This
is what the Apostle means when he asserts that we were chosen in Christ:
Christ was chosen as the principal one and in some manner bore and contained
us in his person.

[Marginalia: Note the meaning.]

Yet we will not thereby be compelled necessarily to admit the opinion of
certain very godly men. They hold that God established the incarnation of his
Son and the office of the Mediator as a means more principal to his glory and
nearer to the end itself than the salvation of elect humanity. They say that
God willed the fall and deliverance of humanity to serve as a less principal
occasion for this principal means and subordinated them to it as the nearest
end. It is as if, in ordering and arranging within himself the sequence of his
decree and counsel, God first of all determined to reveal the riches of his
grace and the marvelous concurrence—indeed, harmony—of justice and mercy in
the person of his incarnate Son, and thus to glorify his name eternally in
Christ made man. Then, to this end, he determined to create people who would
fall. After they had fallen, he would designate his Son as Redeemer and
Mediator for those whom he had chosen from the rest for eternal salvation by
his most free will. Finally, he would give these people to his Son Christ to
be saved, redeemed by his death, and made alive, raised, and glorified by his
life.

They seem to have established this order to avoid the inconvenient and absurd
consequence that otherwise the person to be saved might appear in this
respect to possess some dignity and prerogative above Christ himself who
saves. For if Christ’s incarnation or coming in the flesh and mediation, and
thus Christ himself as Mediator, were directed toward that person’s salvation
as a means toward the nearest end, this consequence might seem to follow.

[Marginalia: Objection. The reason for this opinion.]

But following Holy Scripture, which teaches us this everywhere, we say that
Christ came into the world for us—that is, for all the elect to be saved and
redeemed—and that for this end he was sent by the Father and assumed our
flesh, so that he might atone for our sins on the cross and free us from
divine wrath and the curse. He was therefore destined and designated for us
from eternity by the Father for this end, according to the hypothesis laid
down above: God executes his decree in the way in which he foreknew and
predetermined from eternity that he would execute it.

[Marginalia: Response.]

Nor is this order and opinion contradicted by our statement that it may piously
be asserted that Christ himself was chosen first and principally, as the head
who contains and virtually includes us within himself, and that we were chosen
in him. There we were not speaking about the end itself which God purposed in
the decree of election, and therefore about a person considered absolutely,
but about the means which serve its execution. In the ordering of these means,
Christ the Mediator occurs first of all. I mean those things which are proper
to election. We were speaking of a person fallen into sin and wretched. In a
word, we were speaking not of the former act or beginning of the eternal
divine purpose and decree, but of the latter act according to our manner of
conceiving and understanding, in keeping with the distinction we made above
in the counsel of election.

[Marginalia: Note the solution.]

As for their argument, in my judgment, and without prejudice against a better
opinion, it can be answered as follows. God destined and appointed Christ as
Mediator for human salvation as its nearest end. Therefore, in the Prophet’s
words, “a child is born to us, and a Son is given to us.” This by no means
implies the greater dignity of humanity but instead displays God’s supreme
goodness, love, and gratuitous kindness toward humanity. It is not an argument
for our excellence but rather for our misery, which required so great an
atoning sacrifice, so great a Redeemer, and so immense and precious a ransom.
From it we must estimate the infinite mercy and kindness of God, who willed to
spend so great a price upon us and disburse it for us who were utterly
unworthy.

[Marginalia: Solution of the argument offered. Isaiah 9:5.]

The rule that ends are more excellent than the means destined to them is true
only of those things which by their nature are referred and destined to some
end. For we measure their entire goodness, use, worth, and excellence from the
end: as the worth of medicine from health, bread from nourishment, and a
sheepdog from the guarding of the sheep. The rule is by no means true of those
things which depend on mere good pleasure and the most free will of the one
who arranges and ordains them. In addition to the relation they bear to their
end, to which they were directed by the free will of the one who intends that
end, these things can possess in themselves a dignity and excellence far
greater than the end itself. They do not lose that excellence at all even if
they lower themselves in some respect to an end inferior to themselves.

For example, a steward can appoint one of his children to guard his geese or
pigs. He does not thereby prefer or equate the animals to his child but wills
to use the child’s work and service to increase or preserve the household’s
property. Furthermore, a king may send his dearest, only, and most excellent
son to deliver even the least of his servants from the hands of robbers, and
do so at the risk of the son’s own life, especially when he knows that he will
later compensate his son’s labor and danger with greater glory and dignity.

[Marginalia: Note the distinction. Examples.]

Yet it will not follow that the utterly insignificant person upon whom the
king took such great pity—because the king himself is good and merciful, not
because that person was worthy of such care and mercy—is to be compared in
any way in dignity with the king’s most noble son, much less preferred to him
in this respect. In a word, from the greatness of our Redeemer and redemption
we should estimate not the worth of the thing saved, but the gratuitous love
of the one who saves and the greatness of our death and misery. Let this
suffice, then, for what has been said about the gratuitous election of Christ
himself, the head and foundation of the elect.
