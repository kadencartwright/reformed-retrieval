VERSE 18. Therefore I hated this life, because this work that is done under the
sun seemed evil to me; for all these things are vanity and affliction of spirit.

He presents as the effect of this common

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA129 | printed pp. 129–130 | continues split-word join from p. 128; continues split word at foot of p. 130]

outcome of wise men and fools the following: weariness with his life, and
indeed hatred, began. Let it not seem strange if a poor man who is exhausted of
all goods grows weary of life; but that a man abounding in every good should be
driven by hatred for his life may seem altogether strange. For how many
myriads are found who, if they had even a hundredth part of the goods Solomon
used, would lead a joyful life and would prefer that their life here on earth
be prolonged for all eternity. But they are human reasoners and do not reckon
up both the frailty and the trouble of these transitory things; and if they are
wise, Solomon was foolish.

He presents the cause of this hatred: all things that are carried on under the
sun—not only those administered foolishly, but also those administered
wisely—seemed evil to him. He then adds the reason why they seemed evil: they
were all vain and an affliction of spirit, and there was nothing in this
wisdom, much less in folly, in which his mind could find rest. Here it must be
considered what in this verse is sound and consonant with piety, and what is at
least seemingly foreign to piety.

And, so that we may begin from the causes and come to the effect: the
pronouncement that all things are vain—both the things wise men do and those
fools do—is the judgment of the Holy Spirit, which he repeatedly presses
through the Prophet. That whatever is done under the sun also seemed evil to
him is consonant with truth, since “evil” is taken here not for what is morally
wicked but for what is troublesome. This is especially so because wisdom
itself is acquired not without great trouble; and once gained and apprehended,
it brings forth its fruits—that is, counsels and thoughts—not without exertion
of mind. To this is added what was said above: counsels wisely adopted
sometimes succeed no better than those seized upon rashly and inconsiderately;
indeed, they sometimes succeed worse. Therefore, the effect produced from all
these causes seems to have been rightly concluded: namely, that Solomon was
entirely justified in hating his life.

But a person who considers the matter more deeply and weighs it on the scales
of the divine Word, as on the scales of the sanctuary, will understand that a
fallacy lies beneath this and that the conclusion belongs to our depraved
flesh, not to the Holy Spirit—if, that is, this hatred were directed against
life absolutely and without qualification. For since life is an excellent gift
of God, this hatred ought to have been turned not against life, as though
against God its giver, but against sin. Sin produced this confusion of things:
that wisdom cannot be either acquired or exercised without trouble; that it
does not calm the mind; and, finally, that when it does not always succeed
well, it is sometimes a detriment to its master.

If these outcomes of wisdom consisted in sins, a person would be entirely
right to prefer death rather than provoke God with sins by prolonging life.
But to reject and hate life because of trouble and affliction of the flesh is
to choose sin over affliction, which is spoken of in Job with the greatest
reproach. Job 36.20–21.

This must certainly be guarded against and avoided all the more, inasmuch as
Scripture testifies that illustrious men distinguished in piety stumbled
against that stone through impatience: oppressed by evils, they desired that
life be exchanged immediately for death. Thus Job wished more than once that
the thread of his life would be cut short, something for which the Holy Spirit
reproaches him. Job 36.20–21. So too did Jonah; the same also befell Elijah.
Jonah 4.3, 8; 1 Kings 19.4.

They imitate those soldiers who, so long as they are in winter quarters or in
garrisons, delight in military service; but once they have been led into the
battle line, where battle must be joined with the enemy, they immediately seek
their discharge, when their service is most especially necessary. Yet there
are circumstances in which this hatred of life on Solomon's part has a place.
For we ought to hate our life when it must be exposed for the Gospel and for
Christ, and likewise to

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA131 | printed pp. 131–132 | continues split-word join from p. 130; ends on p. 131 before repeated Vers. 19]

desire death so that we may live with Christ, and to hate this life in
comparison with the heavenly life. But such is the perversity of human nature
that we are prepared to risk our life for things of almost no importance—for
an altered little word or for the slightest insult—yet we refuse to expose it
to danger for the kingdom of heaven. Just when life must be protected, we are
prepared to cast it away; on the other hand, when its loss must be incurred,
then we are seized by zeal to preserve it.
