The second question follows: what does it mean that Abraham's faith is here
said to be credited or reckoned as righteousness?

Socinus contends that there is a great difference between crediting and
reckoning. Sander likewise says that Paul's λογιζόμεθα in Romans 3:28—“we
reckon that a person is justified by faith”—may be rendered well by “we
reckon,” but very badly, though not absolutely wrongly, by “we credit.”

We reply as follows.

First, Sander's inference is foolish. Because λογιζόμεθα in Romans 3:28
means, according to the passage's reasoning and sense, to judge, reason, or
infer, he concludes that it should nowhere be rendered “to credit.” Yet even
his Vulgate sometimes renders it so.

Second, we do not deny that in authors' usage the words “credit” and
“reckon” sometimes differ in meaning. Crediting may follow from reckoning,
whose derivation signifies repeated thought; or, as among us English,
reckoning may follow from crediting. Because righteousness is credited to
someone, it follows that he is reckoned—that is, regarded—as righteous. So
in our justification before God, we are regarded and reckoned as righteous
from Christ's righteousness credited to us. Thus a glossary explains “I
reckon” by ἀναλογίζομαι, “I esteem.”

Third, Socinus or anyone else will never prove that the two are never used
for the same thing. The contrary is plain at least from the jurist
Modestinus: “There is nonetheless something which may be reckoned the
testator's fault, so that he is deservedly rejected.” Here “reckoned” plainly
stands for “credited.” Moreover, the same Greek word λογίζεσθαι occurs in
verses 4 and 5 of this chapter. The Vulgate renders it “credited” in verse 4
and “reckoned” in verse 5, treating them as interchangeable and of the same
meaning.

[1679 printed p. 209 | IA leaf 217]

Fourth, however the matter stands, it concerns us little. We are not
contending so much about the Latin words for crediting and reckoning; let
Latin grammarians and jurists judge their meaning and distinction. Our
controversy with the Roman party and others concerns the Hebrew חשב and the
Greek λογίζομαι, which the Holy Spirit uses on this subject in the Old and
New Testaments: in what sense something may be credited to someone as
righteousness; whether Christ's righteousness is credited to us for
justification, as the apostle appears to affirm in verse 6; and in what
sense faith is credited to us as righteousness, as the apostle repeatedly
says in verses 3, 5, 9, and 22 of this chapter.

This discussion of crediting touches the heart and soul of the controversy
over justification, although many Romanists and Romanizing writers hold it
up to ridicule. Since this is a suitable place to treat it, with God's help
we undertake to dispatch four matters before proceeding to the remaining
two clauses of this passage:

1. What is the genuine meaning of חשב and λογίζομαι, especially in these
passages?

2. What does the phrase mean when something is said to be credited to
someone as righteousness?

3. Is Christ's righteousness thus credited to us for our justification, and
in what sense?

4. In what sense does the apostle here and elsewhere say that faith is
credited as righteousness?

As to the first, the Greek noun λόγος signifies, first, speech; second,
thought, reason, and reasoning, because to think and reason is to speak
something in the mind; third, numbering, which is accomplished by thought;
and fourth, regard, which is directed by the mind's thought. Hence the verb
λογίζομαι derives not only its origin but also its meanings from λόγος. To
treat the matter generally, it is used in either a logical or an arithmetic
sense.

In the logical sense it embraces every act of reason in its meaning and
usage. It is sometimes explained as thinking or considering: love “does not
think evil,” 1 Corinthians 13:5; “we are not sufficient of ourselves to
think anything,” 2 Corinthians 3:5. Sometimes it means to determine, judge,
decide, or consider: “I consider that the sufferings of this present time
are not worthy,” Romans 8:18; “I do not consider myself to have attained,”
Philippians 3:13; and, “to him who judges something to be unclean, to him it
is unclean,” Romans 14:14. It may mean to judge with hope or opinion, as in
1 Peter 5:12, or to reason and draw an inference from reasoning: “We
therefore conclude”—that is, infer by reasoning—“that a person is justified
by faith apart from works of the law,” Romans 3:28.

[1679 printed p. 210 | IA leaf 218]

In the present passage, however, and in the others which treat
justification, the word is taken rather in an arithmetic sense. In
Eustathius, ἀριθμεῖσθαι is ἀναλέγεσθαι, to be numbered; according to
Hesychius, λογισθῆναι is μετρεῖσθαι and ἀριθμεῖσθαι, to be counted. Such
numbering and counting take place through thought, judgment, and reasoning.
As reasonings among the Hebrews take their name from thinking, so among the
Greeks λογίζομαι means that I carry something into an account.

It consequently stands for numbering or reckoning: “He was numbered or
reckoned with the lawless,” Mark 15:28; and “the children of the promise are
reckoned as offspring,” Romans 9:8. Most relevant to our present purpose, it
frequently means to credit: by reckoning or during calculation to ascribe
something, carry it into someone's account, assign it by numbering, allot it
in calculation, or enter it in accounts which are said to be calculated
when they are compared and settled. This is often expressed by the compound
verbs ἐλλογεῖν, ἐλλογᾶν, and ὑπολογίζειν, as in Romans 5:13 and Philemon 18
and in other authors. Yet the simple λογίζομαι frequently bears this sense
in Scripture, especially when joined to a dative—not only eleven times in
Romans 4, but often elsewhere in the New Testament, as in Galatians 3:6,
James 2:23, 2 Corinthians 5:19, and 2 Timothy 4:16.

As Flacius Illyricus rightly says, the metaphor is taken from merchants. To
credit a thing is to weigh it carefully, calculate it by reasoning, carry
it into an account, and so ascribe and credit it. Pareus says more plainly,
“To credit is to ascribe to someone a thing or the cause of a thing, good or
evil, which he either has or does not have.” Vasquez says, “To credit
something to someone is to number and count it among the things that are his
and belong to him.”

This is the general description of crediting, from which its various
divisions may be derived.

First, the thing credited is good or evil. A good thing is credited: wages
in Romans 4:4, faith in verses 3 and 5, and righteousness in verse 11. An
evil thing is also credited: guilt to the sinner and punishment to the
guilty, as in 2 Corinthians 5:19, “not crediting their trespasses to them,”
and 2 Timothy 4:16, “may it not be credited to them.”

Second, both good and evil may be credited rightly or wrongly. It is right,
if the rule of justice alone is considered, when righteousness and reward
are credited to the one who does well, and sin, guilt, and punishment to the
one who does evil. It is wrong when righteousness and reward are ascribed to
an evildoer, or guilt and punishment to an innocent person, as when adultery
was charged against Susanna, sedition against the apostles, and treason
against Christ. Thus Pliny said that people credit the causes of punishments
to the earth and the stars.

But good and right crediting is itself twofold, on the authority of the
apostle in Romans 4:4: either according to debt or according to grace.

Crediting according to debt occurs when something which truly belongs or is
owed to someone is credited to him. Some indeed maintain that crediting can
be spoken only of something which does not truly and actually belong or is
not owed, but is ascribed by thought alone. Aquinas says on this passage,
“Reckoning is customarily spoken of when what is deficient on someone's
part is freely reckoned to him as though he had done the whole.” Cornelius a
Lapide says, “Something is reckoned as what it is not in itself, as the
will

[1679 printed p. 211 | IA leaf 219]

is reckoned for the deed.” Erasmus likewise says, “To carry as received is
to regard as received what you did not receive; among the jurists, unless I
am mistaken, it is called acceptilation.” Some of our writers agree, since
this favors our doctrine of Christ's righteousness credited to us, a
righteousness which is not in us. Flacius Illyricus says, “The word ‘credit’
does not concern what is accomplished in reality, but what is ascribed in
thought.” Hyperius likewise says, “Λογίζεσθαι is for something to be
reckoned and received by someone as what it simply is not, or as the thing
whose worth it is judged to possess.”

Hence Piscator understands Paul's statement in Romans 4:4, “to the one who
works, wages are not credited according to grace but according to debt,” as
elliptical. He says its full form is, “to the one who works, wages are not
credited—that is, they are not given according to grace, but they are given
according to debt.” He accordingly renders it, “But to the one who works,
wages are not credited from grace but are given from debt.” In his notes he
adds that nothing is said to be credited from debt, since crediting concerns
a thing which is not owed. Thus in the latter clause, “but according to
debt,” he does not want “is credited” to be understood from the preceding
clause, which would yield “but is credited according to debt.” He considers
that expression improper and wants an ellipsis to be supplied: properly
speaking, “but it is given or repaid according to debt.”

In my opinion this is excessively scrupulous, and several of our writers
depart from him. Chamier asks, “What, indeed, is ‘it is credited not
according to debt but according to grace’? Is it not the same as if you
expressly read, ‘it will not be credited according to debt, but will be
credited according to righteousness’?” Forbes, Bucanus, and Tilenus agree.
Tilenus concedes that although something which a person did not do or have
is usually said to be credited to him, sometimes even what he did and has
may be said to be credited.

Beza once held that there was an ellipsis in these words and supplied it in
his earlier version by inserting “is.” In his final version, however, he
omitted that word. Moreover, in his notes he gathers two kinds of crediting
from Paul's words, by which a creditor releases his debtors in calculating
his accounts. In one he freely carries as received for them what they did
not pay; in the other he declares that he has received from his debtors what
was owed to him. Ludovicus de Dieu similarly says, “There is no doubt that
the apostle here establishes two opposed creditings: one has its foundation
in the person to whom the crediting is made, the other in the will of the
one who credits.” Since the apostle himself is our authority, it follows,
contrary to Piscator's supposition, that crediting may be according to debt,
and that what truly belongs and is owed may be said to be credited.

We must note, meanwhile, that the Roman writers err equally in the opposite
direction. Some on the one side claim that what truly belongs or is owed is
either not credited at all or only improperly. The Roman writers on the
other side maintain that no crediting is true, or at least properly so
called, unless truth in the thing itself corresponds to it. Bellarmine
speaks thus, and Estius expressly says on this passage that “the crediting
of a thing which does not truly belong or is not owed is a false reckoning
and cannot occur in God. In this way the person to whom faith is credited as

[1679 printed p. 212 | IA leaf 220]

righteousness would not be truly righteous, which favors the heretics'
dogma. According to Scripture, therefore, we say that something is reckoned
as something when it is regarded as such by a true estimate and judgment.
Thus Abraham's faith and Phinehas's deed were reckoned as righteousness
because they were remarkable works of righteousness, just as sins are
deservedly and truly credited.” So speaks Estius, in order to establish our
justification upon inherent righteousness. We shall treat that later; for
now we deal only with the notion and meaning of the word “crediting.”

Here we must walk a middle path. We say neither with the Roman writers that
only what truly belongs or is owed is credited, nor with Piscator and others
that only what is truly absent but deemed present by reckoning, thought, or
acceptilation is credited. Rather, both what truly belongs and what is
absent are quite truly and properly said to be credited, though in different
ways. Wages are credited to the worker according to debt, as the apostle
says in Romans 4:4, and sin to the sinner in verse 8, when wages worthy of
the work are repaid to the worker, and when the sinner is deservedly judged
for sin, condemned, and suffers punishment. Quintilian accordingly asks
whether a killing must be credited to the one who began the quarrel. In the
same way, righteousness from the first covenant of works would be credited
to us if we had fulfilled its condition.

“Crediting is according to debt when it has its foundation in the person to
whom something is credited, and according to grace when it has its
foundation in the person crediting.” Yet crediting according to grace is
equally true and, if we follow the word's ordinary conception and usage,
more proper. In this case, the thing credited does not truly—that is,
inherently—belong to the person, but is attributed and carried into his
account by an agreement, gracious remission and acceptilation, or a surety's
satisfaction. Thus, “to credit is to hold in an account what one could
rightfully have denied. God could rightfully have denied us righteousness,
because we have transgressed his law in a thousand ways, yet for Christ's
sake he credits righteousness to us.” Thus Christ's righteousness is
credited to us and our sins to Christ, according to 2 Corinthians 5:21,
where Christ, who knew no sin, is said to have been made sin for us “that we
might become the righteousness of God in him”; and Romans 4:5 says that to
the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly,
faith is credited as righteousness.

To bring all these points together, let us add this single, fuller division
of crediting.

Crediting is either essential or by thought.

First, essential crediting occurs when either an existing thing is ascribed
to someone, or a good or evil reward or value is allotted to a thing or
deed.

The thing itself is ascribed to someone and carried into his account, as
among merchants when the amount of gold or silver which someone received or
owes is entered in his account. Likewise, one who sins is regarded as a
sinner and guilty, while one who acted justly is judged righteous.
Phinehas's deed was thus credited to him as righteousness in Psalm 106:31:
it was divinely approved as having been justly done. Likewise Abimelech in 1
Samuel 22:15 begged Saul not to credit sin

[1679 printed p. 213 | IA leaf 221]

or guilt to him—that is, not to regard him as guilty. Conversely, sin is not
credited when there is neither law nor transgression of law, Romans 5:13.

Second, a good or evil reward is consequently allotted to a thing or deed.
A work is credited to the worker in Romans 4:4 when it is compensated with
wages. Sin is often said to be credited in Scripture when it is visited
with deserved punishments, and not credited when forgiven. Thus Leviticus
17:4 says, “Blood shall be credited to him; he has shed blood, and therefore
that man shall be cut off from among his people.” Shimei likewise begs David
not to credit iniquity to him in 2 Samuel 19:19—not to judge him as though
he were scarcely guilty, for he freely confesses that there, but not to put
him to death as confessedly guilty, which David could deservedly have done.
Conversely, in this sense sin is not credited when it is forgiven, Psalm
32:1–2, and its deserved punishment is accordingly not exacted, 2 Timothy
4:16. Thus non-crediting of trespasses and reconciliation are treated as the
same, or at least joined, in 2 Corinthians 5:19; and crediting faith as
righteousness is called “justification of life” in Romans 5:18, insofar as
it tends and leads to eternal life. This was the first kind of crediting,
the essential: what truly belongs or is owed to someone with respect either
to the thing itself or its cause is attributed to him as to the thing
itself, or allotted to him as to its effect or reward.

The second kind is called crediting by thought, not because it is always
merely imaginary in itself or its effect. Such crediting sometimes—indeed,
often—occurs among foolish or unjust human beings. An unjust judge acquits
the guilty and condemns the innocent, which Solomon says is an abomination
to the Lord. The pagans of old, out of hatred, customarily credited all
famines, plagues, wars, and misfortunes and their causes to Christians. The
worst people can dreamily credit and ascribe excellent works, supreme virtue
and piety, salvation, and eternal life to themselves out of self-love, or to
others through flattery, without any foundation whatever in the truth of the
matter, a covenant made, or satisfaction performed by a surety. Such
crediting is entirely imaginary with respect to the person crediting and the
one credited, and with respect to the thing and its effect.

No such crediting can occur in the infinitely wise and just God. He never
credits righteousness to anyone or pronounces anyone righteous who is not
truly righteous. He does indeed justify the ungodly, as the apostle affirms
in Romans 4:5: one who in himself was truly ungodly before this
justification, and who even after justification remains unrighteous to some
degree with respect to inward inherence. Yet this gracious justification is
not without its foundation. Antecedently, it rests on God's eternal decree,
his covenant made, and the full satisfaction of our surety Jesus Christ.
Consequently, it places the justified person in a state of inherent
righteousness, begun here and finally to be fully perfected in heaven. Thus
the apostle expressly says in Romans 5:19, “Through the obedience of the
one”—the one Christ—“the many will be constituted righteous,” not merely
imagined to be so. But this is an aside; we shall say more in the proper
place when we come to explain and assert the crediting of Christ's
righteousness. To return to our path, I have said these things in passing to
warn that this second species which I call crediting by thought does not
receive that name because it consists in bare thought

[1679 printed p. 214 | IA leaf 222]

while remaining vain, entirely imaginary, and destitute of every foundation
in reality. It is so named because it is opposed to the former kind, called
essential, which rested on the real existence and inherence of the thing
credited, or upon merit in the person to whom it was credited. This second
kind, called crediting by thought, neither finds, requires, nor indeed
admits such inherence or merit. Yet it takes account of a decree, covenant,
or promise freely made by the one who credits, or satisfaction made by a
surety as its foundation. By an act of the mind it bestows a reward, freely
forgives an offense, or carries and credits a surety's satisfaction into the
account of the person to whom the crediting is made.

This crediting also has two forms, personal and real.

First, it is personal when a thing or cause is transferred from one person
to another, as Onesimus's debt is transferred to Paul in Philemon 18.
Christ's righteousness is credited to us and our sins to Christ, when “he
was made sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in him,”
2 Corinthians 5:21. Under the law, the sacrificer's offense was likewise
placed upon the sacrifice in Leviticus 16:21, and the atonement accomplished
through the sacrifice was credited to the sacrificer.

Second, it is real when one thing enters the place of another and is
accepted or credited for it. It may be of equal value, as when a sum of
money is owed and payment is made with other things of the same amount and
value; of greater value, as when virtue is credited in place of noble birth;
or of lesser value, as when flattery is credited for friendship, a guilty
person's plea for innocence, or the modesty of the son Demetrius for the
innocence of his father Philip. Thus Numbers 18:27, 30 says that the
Levites' offering from the tithes was credited to them for all the grain
from the threshing floor and the fullness of the winepress. So
uncircumcision is said to be reckoned for circumcision in Romans 2:26. And
righteousness is said to be credited in Romans 4:6—that is, as our writers
rightly explain, Christ's righteousness in place of ours, so that through
this crediting it is reckoned ours. Faith is also said to be credited as
righteousness in Romans 4:5. Arminius and others explain this as though in
our justification faith succeeds to the place of the legal righteousness
owed by us and is accepted for it. That is not so, as we shall see later.

To conclude everything concerning all these species of divine and human
crediting, I add the words of a very learned man: “Divine crediting contains
nothing false. It is an act either of just severity in disapproving and
avenging; of just goodness in approving and rewarding; or of singular grace
in forgiving guilt and its deserved punishment, or giving an undeserved
reward. Some human creditings agree with truth and are just, in some
imitation of divine justice or mercy. Others arise from error, falsehood, or
even a lie, or are injurious in imitation of diabolical perversity and
slander.”

So much for the first question, the genuine meaning of λογίζεσθαι, or to
credit. We have lingered longer in explaining it because our justification
before God is from Christ's righteousness credited to us. Clarifying the
word will therefore greatly assist our subsequent explanation of the whole
doctrine of justification. It must be held all the more tenaciously because
our entire salvation and comfort depend upon this crediting. Yet very many
Romanists and Romanizing writers hiss it down, contemptuously call it a
merely putative righteousness, and

[1679 printed p. 215 | IA leaf 223]

hold it up to ridicule. This completes the first question concerning the
meaning of the word.
