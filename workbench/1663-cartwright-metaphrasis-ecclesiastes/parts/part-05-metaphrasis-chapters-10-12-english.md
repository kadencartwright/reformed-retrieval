CHAPTER X.

VERSE 1. When the spirit of the one who rules rises against you, do not leave
your place, for a healing man will quiet great sins.

Having explained the vanity that is perceived in human administration and
falsely attributed to God's government, I wish to apply remedies for healing
these evils. First, with respect to human administration, I give this counsel
to you who place yourself under my instruction. If—as happens not
infrequently—the spirit of a ruler boils against you, whether undeserving or
even well deserving, and harms you in your goods or reputation, do not
forsake your station and the due subjection of both mind and body. To
accomplish this more readily, I wish you to remember that a person endowed
with wisdom can heal the ruler's mind, even when deeply diseased by the
passion of disturbed anger, through his own humility of mind, gentleness of
speech, and calm and forbearing patience under injuries.

VERSE 2. There is an evil I saw under the Sun, according to the error that
proceeds from the face of the ruler.

Among other things in human life, I observed one that, unless great caution
is applied, can drive and cast you away from due observance and subjection to
a ruler and hurl you headlong into defection. This is especially so when it
proceeds either from a ruler's ignorance or from his mind being blinded by
agitation.

VERSE 3. Folly itself is set in high and ample dignities, while the rich sit
in abasement itself.

What I observed is this: the most foolish people are placed at the highest
summit of honor, as though upon the loftiest mountains, while those rich and
abundantly furnished with every virtue and branch of knowledge lie despised
and scorned in the lowest condition, as though in the deepest valleys.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA67 | printed pp. 67–68]

VERSE 4. I saw slaves sitting upon horses, but rulers walking like slaves
upon the earth.

What else is this but for rulers and the masters of such people to be made
slaves at their feet, while the basest bondservants, born through poverty of
mind to carry burdens, are borne on horses or in carriages?

VERSE 5. The person who digs a pit falls into it, and a serpent bites the
person who breaks through a wall.

Take care, however, that indignation at this matter does not move you to
contrive anything contrary to your duty. First, see that you devise nothing
secretly and treacherously against him. For if the person who digs a pit for
another rather often falls into it himself, having forgotten his own safety,
how much more will this happen when these traps are set for a king? Besides
the watchful eye of God intent upon preserving his safety, a king has no few
watchmen who will smell out and expose this fraud. If you attempt anything
against the ruler by open violence, many proverbs repeated on everyone's lips
will instruct you to use caution. Among them is this: the person who tears
down a hedge is sometimes bitten by a serpent concealed within it.

VERSE 6. The person who removes stones is hurt by them; the person who splits
wood will be endangered by it.

Likewise, the person who demolishes a wall by loosening its stones suffers
harm when those very stones fall back upon him unawares. It is therefore no
wonder if you suffer a severe punishment when you leap over the hedge and
wall of the obedience owed to a ruler. Finally, the person splitting wood
with an axe labors under the danger of receiving a wound.

VERSE 7. If, after the iron becomes blunt, he does not sharpen its edge but
exerts his strength, wisdom is profitable for directing it.

This is especially so if, without sharpening the blunted and turned-back
edge of an axe, he continues to strain every sinew of his strength, so that
the blunt axe rebounds upon the person splitting the wood and harms him.
Therefore, just as skill of mind applied suitably is more beneficial than
bodily power and strength in handling an axe and other iron tools, so there
is more help for calming a raging ruler in patience—which may rightly be
regarded as divine and excellent wisdom—than in either the cunning of a
little fox or the force and strength of a lion.

VERSE 8. If the serpent bites before the incantation has been applied, then
there is no profit for the one who possesses words of incantation.

One must withdraw all the farther from such things because a ruler who has
once been provoked by them is himself usually irreconcilable. For then all
the most ingenious devices for appeasing him will prove no more beneficial
than the most skillful incantations applied to a serpent after it has bitten
the enchanter.

VERSE 9. The words of a wise person's mouth are gracious, but a fool swallows
himself with his own lips.

Such is the power of wisdom that it can not only calm a ruler's anger but
also, by well-composed and properly ordered speech, return a person to his
favor. The fool, however, cuts his own throat as though with his own sword by
his disorderly speech.

VERSE 10. The beginning of the words of your mouth is folly, and the end of
the words of his mouth is the worst madness.

He scarcely begins his speech before immediately betraying his folly. The
longer he extends his speech, the more he displays his ignorance, until he
closes his discourse with sheer madness.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA69 | printed pp. 69–70]

VERSE 11. Though the fool himself uses many words, this person does not know
what he is saying; and who may tell him what will happen afterward?

Although he is profuse—or rather, endless—in speech with the purpose of
displaying his skill, the wretched person nevertheless does not know what he
is saying, much less what he is going to say. Yet he so trusts himself and
his own counsels that all labor undertaken to counsel him and recall him to
soundness is in vain.

VERSE 12. The labor of fools wearies each of them, as one who does not know
how to go into the city.

Fools bring trouble upon themselves in deeds just as they do in words. Just
as people who do not know the road to the city where they live wander here
and there until they wear themselves out, so fools, as though ignorant of the
road leading to the city where they live, contrive a grievous evil for
themselves. Those unable to associate and converse with their fellow citizens
are not undeservedly compared to people who do not know the road to the city
in which they dwell.

VERSE 13. Woe to you, land whose king is a child and whose rulers eat in the
morning.

I do not wish my earlier statement about the obedience and submission to be
rendered to unrighteous rulers whose anger oversteps its bounds to be
understood as though I were minimizing their injustice and perversity in
ruling even in the slightest. For in many respects I regard you, O nations,
as unhappy and miserable when a king who is a child in conduct and judgment
is set over you. And, as generally happens when a king is foolish, the rulers
and nobles of the kingdom take as their first and greatest concern the
sating of their pleasures. They consume in eating, drinking, feasting, and
reveling the morning time so well suited for taking counsel and administering
justice.

VERSE 14. O blessed land, whose king is born of the most illustrious and
whose rulers eat at the proper time, for strength and not for drinking
parties.

Conversely, O thrice and four times blessed people, whose king is renowned
not so much for his birth as for his virtue and is deemed worthy to walk in
splendor with the most precious adornment and the most exquisite bodily
clothing! With the king leading the way, his rulers also take food at
suitable and sufficient times so that, with bodily strength restored, the
mind may be eager and vigorous for conducting every affair—not so that they
may compete in drinking or indulge their appetites.

VERSE 15. Through the idleness of both hands the roof-beams grow weak, and
through the letting down of the hands the house drips.

How great and enormous a blow the commonwealth receives from vicious rulers
may be seen in the dissolute and idle management of a single household. The
idleness of each hand and, as it were, its repose from work causes a leak. If
this is not promptly repaired, the rotting roof-beams of the house threaten
the certain ruin of the entire building.

VERSE 16. They prepare a feast for gladness, wine makes the living glad, and
their money causes all these things to answer.

This generally happens most of all when those who preside over a household
cease from doing their work and instead apply their effort to furnishing
feasts and procuring wine, which cheers mortals. This is especially so when,
as commonly happens to the idle, they neglect agriculture and pastoral work
and are compelled to buy with money everything on which they spend. If
everything in a household goes to ruin when the master of the household is
constantly at feasts, how much more will a commonwealth go to ruin when its
rulers feast greedily and devote themselves to draining tankards?

VERSE 17. Do not curse the king even in your conscience, and do not curse the
rich person in the chambers of your bedroom, for even a bird of the heavens
would carry that voice and a winged creature would report the word.

Meanwhile, although they feast greedily and are devoted to luxury, your part
is neither to curse them in your conscience even in thought, much less to
revile the king and rulers with curses and insulting words. Do not do this
even when you speak about them in your most private chamber and suppose it
impossible that your words could get out. For while kings are very sharp of
hearing because of their eavesdroppers and spies, God, the most severe
avenger of insults against kings and rulers, has marvelous means ready at
hand by which what you muttered in the most private recesses may be made
public, so that just punishments may be exacted from you, as though a bird
flying overhead had disclosed the insulting words with its wings.

CHAPTER VI. [sic; Chapter XI]

VERSE 1. Cast your bread upon the waters, for after many days you will find
it.

Against the poison served by the Epicureans concerning indulgence and
gluttony, I urge and exhort you to distribute some of your goods and
resources even to those from whom you expect no repayment, so that they seem
to have perished as completely as if you had cast seed into the sea. For
although it rather often happens that God defers compensation for a long
time, he will nevertheless repay the reward eventually—in this life, but
certainly in the future life.

VERSE 2. Give a portion to seven, or even to eight, since you do not know
what evil will happen upon the earth.

Do not think it sufficient to give alms unless you also distribute them to
as many people as your resources allow. The changeableness and uncertainty
of all things in Europe, as it were, ought to stir you to this generosity,
for you do not know what will happen either to you or to the wealth you
possess. You should therefore rightly reckon as gain what you rescue, as it
were, from flame and fire by conferring it for the relief of the poor.

VERSE 3. When dense clouds filled with rain have let it fall drop by drop
upon the earth, whether a tree falls toward the south or toward the north, it
will remain in the place where it fell.

Therefore imitate clouds—not those carried about by a light breeze or wind,
but those pregnant, as it were, with water. Just as those clouds, full of
water, do not retain it enclosed within themselves but let it down upon the
thirsty and arid earth and discharge it, so you, furnished with wealth like
water suited to refreshing people of slender means, ought to relieve their
thirst and poverty. Do not be troubled that your beneficence rather often
happens to be placed upon unworthy people. For just as a felled tree,
whichever way it falls—south or right, north or left—undoubtedly yields to
the owner of the soil in which it grew, so, if you apply the caution in
giving that God commands in his Word, you will not be deprived of the fruit
of your generosity, whatever sort of person transfers it to another use.

VERSE 4. The person who observes the wind will not sow, and the person who
looks to the dense clouds will not reap.

I am not unaware that corrupted reason will dictate many things to break
your zeal for giving: that poverty may steal upon you yourself, that a large
family either already presses you or may press upon you hereafter, and very
many other matters of this kind. Yet just as the farmer who is deterred from
sowing by the wind and from reaping by a sky covered in clouds is
insufficiently prudent, being deprived of the desired fruit through empty
fear, so the person who listens to the reasonings of corrupted nature must
be regarded as an idle disciple of piety. By sowing no seed of beneficence
among the poor, he ought to expect no reward from divine grace and
generosity.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA73 | printed pp. 73–74]

VERSE 5. Just as you do not know the way of the wind or the way of the bones
in the pregnant womb, so you do not know the work of God himself, by what way
he makes all these things.

For just as you do not know in what region of the heavens the wind will
blow, for how long, and how gently or fiercely, nor the way an embryo is
formed and joined together in a pregnant womb—though these things appear
before your eyes every day—so you are still less able to know God's works
concerning you and all your affairs, which he governs entirely by his own
will and judgment and which remain hidden before they are accomplished.

VERSE 6. In the morning sow your seed, and in the evening do not let your
hand rest, for you do not know which will be right, this or that, or whether
both will be equally good.

Therefore, from the dawn, as it were, of early life to the evening, as it
were, of decrepit age, before death like night overwhelms you unexpectedly,
stand immovable in the course of your calling. Do not allow some misfortune
that confronts you to make you cast from your hands what you began well, for
you do not know whether this may turn out well for you. If it does not,
another undertaking that succeeds beautifully will compensate for the
earlier loss. There are also times when both this and that turn out according
to your desire.

VERSE 7. This light is indeed sweet, and it is good for these eyes to see
this Sun.

The thought that readily enters human minds should not delay this constancy
in doing one's work: that it is pleasant to enjoy present goods and pleasant
to receive through the bodily senses the benefits in which God shines
kindly, as though in the splendor of the Sun.

VERSE 8. But if a person lives many years and rejoices in them all, he must
nevertheless remember the days of darkness, that they are many and that
whatever happens is vanity.

Suppose, to be sure—though pleasure-seekers rarely obtain this—that you live
longer than any mortal. Suppose further—though I could hardly believe it
possible—that you pass that entire long span of life cheerfully and gladly,
without any injury of body or mind, loss of name or reputation, or, finally,
diminution of wealth. You must nevertheless remember that you will remain
longer in death and the grave than in this life, so that it would be foolish
to cast away all concern for a favorable departure from this life and a
dwelling in the grave to be followed by a joyful resurrection from the dead.
You must also remember that the entire fruit of an idle and pleasure-seeking
life not only vanishes but leaves behind a goad fixed in the conscience that
will create a torment that never dies. If you remember these things well and
meditate upon them, they will readily extinguish the passion burning for the
pleasures of this life and cause you to persevere to the end in the station
where God has placed you.

CHAPTER XII.

VERSE 1. Rejoice, young man, in your youth, and let your mind make you glad
in the days of your youth. Go on in the ways of your mind and in the sights
of your eyes, but know that because of all these things God himself will
bring you into judgment.

Yet I perceive that even by the arguments I have presented, however firm and
powerful they may be, people—especially the young—cannot be drawn away from
the allurements and enchantments of pleasures. Therefore go on, young man,
if you wish to rush headlong into these shipwrecks of pleasure. Go on, I say;
rejoice in your youth, stir up your mind, which of itself is already more
than sufficiently inclined to pleasure, to the enjoyment of pleasures and
delights, and pursue whatever pleases your mind and external bodily senses.
But hold this one thing as certain and beyond all doubt: God will bring you
into judgment for each of these things, often in this life and always in the
life to come, to exact punishment.

VERSE 2. Therefore remove indignation from your mind and turn evil away from
your flesh, for youth and early life itself are vanity.

Therefore take care and strive with all your power to drive as far away from
yourself as possible the viciousness that usually clings to this age. Besides
lust, these vices include anger, indignation, desire for revenge, and passions
of that kind. When you have cleansed your mind of them, you will readily
preserve your body and flesh uncorrupted by every defilement and stain of
graver sin. If it perhaps seems hard to pass the whole course of youth and
adolescence without this alluring pleasure, let it immediately occur to you
that the years of youth vanish as rapidly as the dawn.

VERSE 3. And remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days
of evil come and the years arrive of which you say, “I have no delight in
them.”

To preserve this holiness and innocence of life more surely and easily,
never allow this to fall from your mind: by the right of creation, God
requires you to revere him religiously, and to do so in this age of your
youth, which is best suited for exercising his worship, before old age
overtakes and reaches you. Then, because of the frequent infirmities and
troubles that age brings with it, the time will be less suitable either for
learning or practicing piety.

VERSE 4. Before the Sun, light, Moon, and stars are darkened, and the dense
clouds return after the rain.

Before the distressing time full of calamities arrives, when because of
mental anguish and bodily troubles, the Sun, Moon, and stars seem to you to
have fallen from heaven, since their joyful appearance refreshes you no more
than if they did not exist at all, and calamity continually follows calamity
in battle array, just as a cloudy and gloomy face of the heavens follows the
winter rain.

VERSE 5. At that time the keepers of the house will tremble, those strong in
power will bend themselves, the female grinders will cease because they grind
little, and those who look through the windows will grow dark.

Before the hands, suited for repelling injuries from the body and drawing
benefits toward it, begin to tremble and become unfit to guard the body like
a house; before the legs, like bases and columns, become unable to sustain
the body's mass and bend and sway beneath the burden; before the teeth,
appointed for grinding food, grind slowly and with difficulty the food to be
sent into the stomach because they have either fallen out, been worn down, or
remain loose in the gums; before the eyes, like little windows in the body's
dwelling through which you look out, begin to grow dim.

VERSE 6. The doors toward the street will be shut when the sound of grinding
is low; he will rise at the sound of a little bird, and all singing voices
will be brought low.

Before, because of the body's many maladies, the doors leading to the street
are shut and hold you confined in your house as in a prison, especially when
nearly all use of the teeth has perished, so that it is difficult for you to
eat at another person's table and possible at your own only when company
suited to your single tooth or weakened teeth is provided for you; before,
because your sleep is light and often interrupted, you awake at the crowing
of a rooster or another little bird; before the parts of the body suited
either for singing music yourself or receiving it from others through the
ears become so dull that no ability remains to take pleasure of mind from it.

VERSE 7. They will also fear a high place and terrors on the road; the
blossoming almond tree and the locust growing fat will render the desiring
appetite ineffective, when the person goes to his everlasting place and the
mourners surround him in the street.

Before you reach decrepit age, when the feet of the old, unable to lift
themselves, will fear even the smallest roughness of the road or a projecting
or prominent stone, lest they strike it, fall, and suffer some grave fracture
from the fall; when, even in spring as the almond tree begins to flower and
the locust grows fat upon the greenery of the grass—the far most pleasant
season of the year—every desire to walk into the fields and meadows and take
pleasure from the sight and scent of flowers and the chirping of birds will
completely vanish; when a person, with one foot already set, as it were, in
the grave, is ready to enter the way of all flesh and the common dwelling of
all people, where they will make a long stay, and the hired female mourners
already wait almost at the doors for the bier to be carried out of the house.

VERSE 8. Before the silver cord is moved far away, the golden bowl is shaken
out, the pitcher is broken at the spring, and the wheel is broken at the
cistern.

Before silver necklaces and golden rings are removed as things that will be
a burden rather than an honor and adornment; before you are deprived of all
the advantages of this life, both those nearby and readily obtainable, like
water drawn from a spring by a pitcher, and those procured only at great
labor and expense, like water brought up in a bucket by turning a wheel.

VERSE 9. When the dust itself returns to the earth as it was, but the spirit
returns to God, who gave it.

Finally, before the dusty corpse returns to the earth from which it was taken
and the soul returns to God, who placed it within the body at the beginning
of creation.

VERSE 20. [sic; Verse 10] Vanity of vanities, says Ecclesiastes; all these
things are vanity.

Therefore I not only remain in the judgment set forth at the beginning of
the book, but have been further confirmed by so many reasons gathered from
every side: namely, that all things under heaven are subject to vanity and
are themselves more vain than vanity.

VERSE 11. Moreover, because Ecclesiastes was wise, he also taught the people
knowledge, and by attending and searching he composed many sayings.

If these things perhaps appear insufficiently firm to anyone and he suspects
my trustworthiness and skill in the things I have written, I wish him to know,
so as to attach greater weight of authority to this book, that I was divinely
instructed in singular and incomparable wisdom. Furthermore, I instructed the
people entrusted to my faith in the knowledge of the greatest matters through
written books, and composed many and various proverbs or weighty sayings
after serious attention and careful investigation of the matters.

VERSE 12. Ecclesiastes sought to attain delightful words, and each thing was
written rightly—faithful words.

Moreover, I applied my effort to finding heavenly discourses full of divine
delight, and in doing so to drawing upon things most rightly handed down by
the other sacred writers—discourses, I say, that are most true and abhor even
the appearance of falsehood.

VERSE 13. The words of the wise are like goads and like firmly fixed nails,
the choicest words delivered by the same Shepherd.

These are the discourses of wise and divinely inspired people, whose use is
most excellent. First, by the goads, as it were, of promises and threats they
drive us, who are sluggish and furnished with an idle and almost asinine
disposition, toward piety and righteousness. Second, by the supports of the
same promises and threats—as nails driven into the ground to secure the
bars of a sheepfold—they keep us in the pursuit of piety and righteousness.
These are discourses collected and composed by God's Prophets in such a way
that by their excellence they readily surpass by immeasurable distances all
the collections and compilations of every human writer. Finally, what is
foremost and is the source and cause of all the praises of the divine Word
written above: these discourses were divinely given by the one Shepherd,
Jesus Christ, as a gift to his Church.

VERSE 14. Rather, I say, my son, be admonished by these things: there is no
end of making many books, and the reading of many books is wearisome to the
flesh.

Therefore I wish you to rest in these divinely inspired writings, which
render you abundantly cautious and admonished against every danger. For I
foresee as though in a mirror that an itch and diseased compulsion to write
will beset most people. If you apply your mind to them, you will always be
learning but never arrive at the knowledge of the truth; from reading them,
besides weariness and your own exhaustion, you will receive no fruit.

VERSE 15. The sum of the matter, when all these things have been heard: FEAR
GOD HIMSELF AND OBSERVE HIS COMMANDMENTS, FOR THIS IS THE WHOLE OF A PERSON.

And to put the final hand to this work, the sum of everything profitably said
in this work, elsewhere by me, or even by God's other Prophets—or of
everything that can be said at all—returns, I say, to this: we must remember
to revere God with a sincere fear arising from an undoubted assurance through
faith of the forgiveness of sins, and from the same utterly pure and clear
fountain of reverence to devote our whole selves to obedience to God's
commandments. The whole person, in all that he is, ought to apply and exert
himself toward this matter, since it contains a perfect happiness complete
in every respect. If this alone is present while all other things are absent,
they make a person completely blessed; if it is absent while all other things
are present, they are vain and futile.

VERSE 16. For God himself will bring every work into judgment with every
hidden thing, whether good or evil.

My statement that the whole course of our life must be measured by the
observance of the commandments was not made rashly. According to the law
either engraved on the mind or written upon tablets, God will pronounce
judgment at the Last Judgment upon all thoughts, words, and deeds. Then, when
the things now covered and hidden have been uncovered and brought to light,
each person will receive a reward from God, the most righteous judge,
according to the good or evil he did in this body.
