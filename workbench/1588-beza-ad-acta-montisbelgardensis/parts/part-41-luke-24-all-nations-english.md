## Part XLI: Luke 24—All Nations

### The Württemberg Doctors

Luke 24: “Thus it is written, and thus it was necessary for Christ to suffer and for repentance and forgiveness of sins to be preached in his name TO ALL NATIONS.”

### Theodore Beza’s Antithesis

Concerning Luke 24, “through all nations,” it is a marvelous gloss to understand every individual of every people. Even if that were true, it could not truly be said of every age, since only after sending the apostles did God reveal his saving will to all nations, while now by his just judgment—and not by a counsel newly adopted—he wills the greatest part of the world to remain in darkness and perish.

### Andreae’s Observations

1. It is more remarkable that you deny this, because the matter itself is manifest: under the universal term ALL, every individual in all nations is also understood.

2. From the world’s creation God revealed his will concerning human salvation and in every monarchy made the Israelite people illustrious, among whom these promises were preserved. Since people run across the sea for merchandise but were so negligent of God’s worship, they should accuse not God’s will or hidden decree concerning condemnation but their own impiety, because they could have become proselytes and been saved.

3. Paul plainly teaches in 2 Thessalonians 2 that the present darkness of the greatest part of the world cannot be referred to God’s hidden and absolute decree, but human malice is its cause: “Because they did not receive the love of the truth SO THAT THEY MIGHT BE SAVED, God therefore sent them a powerful delusion so that they believe the lie.” What could be said more plainly against the opinion of God’s absolute decree in condemning human beings?

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Observations

[Marginal note: To the replies.]

I censured as utterly foolish this interpretation: “All nations” means every individual of every nation. Andreae defends the contrary. By what argument? “The matter,” he says, “is manifest.” This, supposedly, is to refute Beza’s heresies, impieties, and blasphemies aptly and solidly. But how can “all nations” mean every person of the individual nations, when God himself—the surest interpreter of the promise made to Abraham that all nations would be blessed in him—excluded from that covenant of blessing not only Ishmael with his nation, since Isaac alone was the son of promise, but even Esau with the Edomites while still hidden in the womb, and indeed very many other nations brought forth from Abraham’s loins? God’s people later had no more bitter enemies than these. Moreover, the same God who had said all nations would be blessed in Abraham elsewhere interprets “all” as “many” (Genesis 17:4–5). Paul, so often citing and explaining Abraham’s blessing, nowhere remembers Andreae’s gloss, but everywhere distinguishes the church by the general division into Jews and Greeks or Gentiles, who were only then called to participation in the gospel. Nor is the use of the universal term thus removed, since the church has its own universality. Hence Augustine’s most useful division of the human race after the world’s creation into two human cities or societies: “One,” he says in book 15, chapter 1 of *The City of God*, “is predestined to reign eternally with God; the other to undergo eternal punishment with the devil.” Finally, how absurd and wholly irrelevant to the present issue is his citation of this passage! Christ speaks of the gospel to be preached not as though it had been proclaimed to all nations from the world’s creation, as Andreae maintains, but only after Christ’s departure and even after some intervening years.

By this observation Andreae finally undertakes to prove his supremely foolish and absurd fiction that no one has ever been condemned for any reason other than contempt for offered gospel grace or its rejection in unbelief, so that each should accuse nothing but his own impiety. Come, then: by what solid arguments? First, God revealed from the world’s creation his will concerning those to be saved. I grant this and confess that the former world, which possessed those long-lived patriarchs as teachers, cannot be absolved from contempt for the promised deliverer. But will Andreae dare affirm with his customary confidence that no infants then departed this life before they could hear anything of gospel grace, and that none perished in that group of evil people whom Moses calls sons of men? It is more probable than can be denied that gradually, through parental negligence and wickedness, all memory of that promise and true piety became obsolete among those profane people and most of their descendants, who never heard anything about it. I do not say this as though they deserve excuse, but to show how empty is the conclusion that gospel grace was offered to every individual of those ages.

Next, whom should we believe rather than God himself, who recalls as causes of the ensuing flood the life of those giants most polluted by every kind of vice? Although contempt for Noah the preacher of righteousness was added as a heap upon all preceding crimes, those words show that violation of every right and equity, rather than contempt for gospel grace supposedly offered to every individual, was the flood’s proper cause.

But Andreae says God made Israel, among whom those promises were preserved, illustrious in every monarchy, so people could seek from them knowledge of that truth and thus become proselytes and be saved, since they otherwise run across the sea for merchandise. Again, how foolishly and, for the most part, falsely are these things said! We are discussing the method God followed in condemning every individual he has condemned in every age from the world’s creation. When the Lord separated Abraham from all the families of all lands, renewed the gospel covenant with him and his descendants alone, and led him from Ur of the Chaldeans, presumably that mystery was known to all contemporary nations and peoples so that desire for its knowledge could touch them! When later Jacob’s one family, consisting of seventy-five souls, lived as exiles in Egypt, placed separately in Goshen’s territory although Joseph governed Egypt, whom will Andreae persuade that Pharaoh and every Egyptian were informed about that covenant of eternal life residing in this family? “They could have asked,” Andreae will say—if, that is, they had heard something about the Israelites capable of attracting them to its knowledge. Instead, what happens to everyone hearing the gospel unless inwardly drawn by God happened to them: whatever reached their ears, or whatever they learned more fully from Joseph or others, was through their Egyptian superstition either a stumbling block or a mockery.

The Israelite people were afterward made illustrious to the Canaanites and neighboring nations, but certainly not all; and not even to instruct the Canaanites, but to terrify and exterminate them. If the succeeding ages to Christ’s advent are considered, the Israelites were indeed illustrious far and wide, but through remarkable miseries and disasters—an excellent incentive, presumably, for profane nations to abandon their superstition and embrace the Israelites’ true religion. Why say more about these utterly foolish conclusions when the apostle in Ephesians 2 does not testify that the Gentiles were attracted by Israel’s peculiar calling to participate in the eternal covenant, but instead that this counsel and benefit of God did not pertain to them? They were outside Christ, had nothing in common with Israel’s commonwealth, were strangers to the covenants of promise, and existed without God in the world. Indeed, although the divinely appointed time of their calling was already approaching, Christ in the disciples’ first commission forbade them to proclaim his advent to the Gentiles and, when addressing the Canaanite woman whom he intended to bless singularly, denied that the children’s bread should be thrown to dogs.

Accordingly, when the apostle treats this very argument in Acts 14:17 and explains it carefully in Romans 1, he makes no mention of contempt for gospel grace. Rather, he demonstrates that God’s just wrath rested and rests upon the entire human race because natural knowledge of God was violated and nature’s law transgressed—the law which distinguishes shameful from honorable and dictates that no one be injured. He then particularly convicts the Jews from benefits bestowed peculiarly upon that nation until that time. Finally, what is the point of all this, since our question concerns not the known and plain causes of condemnation but the eternal decree itself, which precedes and orders all those intermediate causes? Andreae sins against common sense itself when he supposes that establishing those causes removes the decree. Let this also answer his third observation.
