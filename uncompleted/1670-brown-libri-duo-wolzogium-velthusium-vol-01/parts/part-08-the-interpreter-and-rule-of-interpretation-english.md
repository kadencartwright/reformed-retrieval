XVI. Having finished what he had to say about the first part of his proposed
treatment, namely those matters which pertained to Scripture, he has certain
things about the Interpreter on page 41 and following. In order to define
accurately for us what an Interpreter is, he proposes that three things must
be considered in every interpretation: namely, the mind of the one who
writes, the words with which he writes, and the conjunction of the words with
the mind, which is made by the person who is to interpret something. Yet in
this part of his treatment in which he discusses the Interpreter, he always
speaks of the Interpreter who interprets the Scriptures for himself. But all
our writers, in treating this question concerning the Interpreter of the
Scriptures, distinguish between the supreme, absolute, and infallible
Interpreter and the subordinate and fallible interpreter. They say that the
former is God, who alone is also the Supreme Judge of all controversies
concerning Religion, who judges, speaks, and persuades externally and
objectively through his word and internally and subjectively through his
Spirit,

[Page 88 | ONB canvas 142 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000142]

and so forth. Or, as the celebrated *Voetius* says accurately on page 421 of
*On the Judgment and Rule of Faith*, *Selected Disputations*, part 5: *The
Interpreter of Scripture is the Holy Spirit speaking to us in and through
Scripture, inwardly speaking and inscribing in us the meaning and truth of
Scripture, causing us to understand, assent, and believe, so that we may do
and understand, believe and act.* They further distinguish the subordinate
interpreter into public and private. They call public the one who judges with
an antecedent and directive judgment, but private the one who judges with a
judgment of discretion. Yet although *Wolzogen* boldly enough charges and
condemns everyone else for obscurity, he makes absolutely no mention of these
distinctions. To whom could it fail to be displeasing that one who objects to
ignorance or obscurity in others does not resolve the question, although he
presents himself as dispelling the remaining night? The *Paradoxical
Essayist* strives quite directly to overthrow this position which our writers
unanimously maintain. If *Wolzogen* had wished faithfully to defend the
orthodox cause, should he not therefore also have brought forward something
about this principal Interpreter consonant with the doctrine of the Orthodox?
The Pontificians ascribe to their Pontiff, or to the Church or Councils, the
self-authorizing right to interpret the Scriptures; and the *Essayist* whom
*Wolzogen* claims to refute ascribes this right exclusively to Philosophy.
Who will regard *Wolzogen* as a faithful defender of orthodox truth when, even
though the question raised by that Paradoxical writer turns upon this hinge,
at least in large part insofar as it concerns the Interpreter rather than the
rule—of which more later—he deserts all the Orthodox and even truth itself?
He no more mentions that Supreme Interpreter, namely the Holy Spirit, than if
we were all secure about this Interpreter and this supreme right had not
plainly been assailed and violated by this adversary. Alas! By what spirit was
*Wolzogen* moved or driven, that he wished to make absolutely no

[Page 89 | ONB canvas 143 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000143]

mention of this Spirit? He cannot say, as he replies on page 77 of the
*Censure of the Censure*, that he is not yet *treating those things which lead
and assist our mind in the Interpretation of the Scriptures; that it would be
wrong at that point to omit mention of the Spirit, but that he is defining
the Interpreter in general and showing what an Interpreter is, not how one
will interpret well.* For, 1., we know well enough that he is not yet treating
those things which lead minds in the interpretation of the Scriptures;
nevertheless, he is treating the Interpreter. And if he defines the
Interpreter in general here, why does he not propose to us such a definition
of the Interpreter as can apply to every Interpreter, especially the Supreme?
Or does he acknowledge no such Interpreter? 2. Precisely here, when the
Interpreter of the Scriptures is under discussion, it was most fitting and
indeed necessary to mention this Supreme Interpreter, namely the Spirit. 3.
The principal question does not concern those things which assist and lead
our mind in interpreting the Scriptures, but the primary Interpreter himself
and the infallible rule. It is the mark of a betrayer to pervert the state of
the question.

XVII. After certain statements about the author's mind which do not pertain
here, where the question concerns interpreting the Scriptures—because, as
*Wolzogen* himself admits on pages 42–43, *in God the signification of his mind
by which he exhibits to us the judgment he has concerning the things
themselves is never separated from that by which he willed something to be
disclosed to us in his word, since he can disclose nothing to us in his word
which he himself does not judge as true, nor does he judge anything as such
which is not capable of being revealed*—and after accumulating more than was
necessary about Discourse or signs, on page 46 he at last sets out to show
what it is *to interpret*. He says that it consists in the sentence of his
judgment by which, inwardly persuaded, he determines that this discourse
exhibits this meaning from the writer's will, which we know from the usage of
the discourse. A little afterward he says that *what principally remains to
be demonstrated is by what reason I may at last be moved to determine with
certainty in*

[Page 90 | ONB canvas 144 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000144]

*my mind that this meaning must be joined with this discourse.* How confusedly
this confused Doctor treats everything, though he is not afraid to accuse
everyone else of obscurity! A distinction must therefore be made between that
which, on the side of the efficient cause, persuades one that this is the
meaning of this passage, or that this meaning must be joined with this
discourse, and that which, on the side of the object or by way of an object,
moves the mind to this persuasion. He makes no mention of this distinction,
although he wishes to seem to surpass everyone else in distinctly resolving
this question. There is also another distinction which he should especially
have mentioned, namely that between the norm or rule of interpretation and
the means we employ in constructing an interpretation. Our entangled Dictator
repeatedly confuses these. Third, he should also have distinguished verbal
from real interpretation. By failing to distinguish them, he confuses
everything. He wills all interpretation to be verbal because he wills the
Interpreter to consider only the force of the words. Therefore he exalts the
force of the words so greatly in this case and assigns it the principal
place, although other things come under consideration when the question
concerns real interpretation. But verbal interpretation can properly be
called a *translation*, not an interpretation or exegesis of discourse,
because it considers only the force of individual words, terms, and phrases,
not that of the entire discourse insofar as it represents the author's mind;
nor does it draw out the genuine meaning of that sentence. Finally, there is
another useful distinction in this matter of which he makes no mention:
namely, between that which is necessarily presupposed in every language, and
consequently in every interpretation, that is, usage of speech and knowledge
of it, and that which is called the one infallible norm of interpretation.
Our writers say that this norm is Scripture itself, always presupposing the
usage of the language in which Scripture was written. Yet

[Page 91 | ONB canvas 145 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000145]

*Wolzogen* continually commits this error: he does not distinguish these
things, but takes this usage as the one norm. *Wolzogen* continues: *Nor would
there be any great difficulty in defining it if the signs of words were
certain and indubitable. It would suffice to have a known general account of
the institution of these signs, so that we would have clearly perceived what
their meaning was in each instance.* From these words it is clear, first,
that he seeks from the force of the words everything which so moves us that
we can determine with certainty in our mind that this meaning must be joined
with this discourse; for he says there would be no great difficulty in
defining this matter if the signs of words were certain and indubitable.
Second, it is clear that he regards only the force of the words, not as they
occur in this discourse, but as they are taken generally or individually.
Here, therefore, he makes it evident that he does not defend the cause of the
Orthodox but deserts all the Orthodox. For although they always presuppose
this usage of speech, they never regard it as the infallible norm of
interpretation; rather, they say that Scripture itself is this norm.

Further, a little later, on pages 49–50, he has the following in explaining
the question: *To be able to plant one's foot in so shifting a field and give
a certain judgment in an uncertain matter—this is the labor, this the
industry, and it contains the whole subject of our dispute, which we undertook
to explain in the third part of this book. In it this above all, and in these
words, is customarily asked: Who is the Interpreter of Scripture? I would not
wish captiously to censure this question itself, or to reprove the expressions
of those who call now the Holy Spirit, now Philosophy, and now usage of speech
the interpreter of Scripture, although this is said with less accuracy.*
Answer: I do not know who before *Wolzogen* said that Usage of speech is the
Interpreter of Scripture, for until now I have happened neither to see nor
hear even one. Therefore, if he wishes to reprove someone for this expression,
he ought to reprove himself alone; for I know no one else who says the same
thing as he

[Page 92 | ONB canvas 146 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000146]

does. 2. By this enumeration of various positions, he appears to concede that
he attributes as much to usage of speech in the interpretation of the
Scriptures as the Orthodox attribute to the Holy Spirit. 3. He also clearly
indicates that his position concerning usage of speech differs no less from
the position of the Orthodox, who assert that the Holy Spirit is the
Interpreter, than from the position of the Paradoxical writer, who ascribes
this prerogative to Philosophy. 4. What is there in the Orthodox expression
that *Wolzogen* can censure? Can the Holy Spirit not accurately enough be
called the Interpreter of the sacred Scriptures? Can one not with the best
right be called an Interpreter who interprets the Scriptures and teaches,
commends, and persuades what is the proper and genuine meaning of the
Scriptures and of the context of each of them? Thus the celebrated *Voetius*
speaks in the place cited above, page 423. Who would praise *Wolzogen* in this,
that he rejects or tacitly condemns the pattern of sound words which all the
Reformed have hitherto maintained, devises new terms, and brands all the
Reformed? But let us see how he makes it appear that this expression is less
accurate. *The one*, he says, *who reads or hears the Scriptures—whether he
is God, an Angel, whether the angel is black or white, it makes no difference,
or a human being—must be called an Interpreter by me if he reads with
understanding. For whoever by judgment joins the words he has grasped with
their meaning is an Interpreter. Provided he is endowed with reason so that
he can make that connection which is formed by reason alone, he already
deserves his designation.* Answer: We said above that there are private
Interpreters who, possessing a judgment of discretion, interpret the
Scriptures for themselves. But it appears entirely novel that he numbers
hearers also among the interpreters of Scripture. What kind of resolver of
controversy is this, I ask? The Question concerns the Interpreter; he gives us
no one besides a private reader or hearer, and thus confuses questions which
our writers treat separately and distinctly

[Page 93 | ONB canvas 147 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000147]

against the Pontificians, and thereby appears to desert the question being
debated here. 2. To whom ought it not to be displeasing that he wills God to
be no more the Interpreter of the Scriptures than the Devil, and the Devil no
less than God? On pages 80–81 of the *Censure of the Censure* he says that *he
is not here treating a good or bad Interpreter, but defining the Interpreter
in general.* Answer: Nevertheless, he speaks of the true Interpreter, namely
the one who joins the meaning with the words. He speaks of the Interpreter
who is the subject of this dispute and whom he undertook to explain in this
part of his first book. And he speaks of one who, endowed with reason, can
make this connection, which he asserts can apply even to the Devil. Indeed,
in the same place he says that the Devil was an interpreter when he cited
Scripture in Matthew 4. But what sort of interpreter was he? Did he join the
words he grasped with their true meaning? Must a bad and false Interpreter be
called an interpreter?

What else does *Wolzogen* have? *What is properly asked*, he says, *is to what
thing it must be attributed that it guides reason so that it does not err in
this judgment and places solid supports beneath it so that, when shaken, it
does not totter. Because that thing possesses all the truth of interpretation,
it was itself called the interpreter. Nor could we, led astray by the ordinary
usage of speakers, refrain from following the common error. Perhaps we will
follow it rather often hereafter too, because we will do so without danger if
the reader has been warned of the sense in which we do it. For we do not
impose this role of Interpreter upon a person, but upon a thing, whatever it
may finally be, which guides a person in interpreting the Scriptures.*
Answer: Here *Wolzogen*, that most accurate resolver of this question,
confuses distinct things. For there are many things which can guide reason in
making this judgment so that it does not err, concerning which the question
is not raised—namely, those which are called means. But here the question does
not concern the Interpreter himself, but that which, by way *of an ob-*

[Page 94 | ONB canvas 148 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000148]

*-ject*, renders us more certain of the true meaning. The question therefore
does not concern means, but the norm, rule, plumb line, principle by which,
from which, or through which interpretation must be made and examined. Our
writers say that this Rule and Norm is Scripture itself, and therefore by
metonymy Scripture is called the Interpreter by our writers. This can be said
no less fittingly than the law is said to *judge, accuse, and condemn* in John
5:45, 7:15, and 12:48, and Scripture itself is said to *speak* in John 7:38,
42 and 19:37, Romans 4:3 and 11:2, and Galatians 2:30, and to *shut up under
sin* in Galatians 3:22. Hence they also say that no real distinction is given
between this norm and the judge or supreme interpreter except insofar as we
can distinguish between Scripture itself and God speaking in the Scriptures.
Therefore this error is not so great that he should seek so anxiously to
excuse himself if he errs in it with others. But we must proceed.
