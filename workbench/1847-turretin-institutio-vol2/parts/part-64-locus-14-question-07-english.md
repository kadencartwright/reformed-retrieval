QUESTION VII.

CHRIST’S PROPHETIC OFFICE

*In what does the nature of Christ’s prophecy consist, or what are its parts,
and what was its mode?*

[Marginal heading: In how many senses is prophecy taken?]

I. The first part of Christ’s office is *prophecy*. Here it is not taken
broadly and improperly for any acknowledgment and celebration of God, in the
manner in which believers are said to prophesy under the New Testament, Joel
2:28; or for public instruction, which belongs to interpreters of sacred
things, or the church’s public teachers and ministers, in which sense “the
spirits of prophets are said to be subject to prophets,” 1 Corinthians 14:32;
or, more strictly, for those whom God called immediately and outside the
ordinary order, not only to teach the people, but also to restore God’s
corrupted worship and foretell many future things. This is the most usual
meaning of this word in the Old Testament, and is claimed for Moses in a
certain peculiar manner in Numbers 12, as will be seen afterward.

[Marginal heading: And in what sense is it attributed to Christ?]

Rather, it is taken *most strictly and most properly* for the fullest and most
majestic revelation of the mysteries of salvation which Christ, as the
church’s supreme teacher, exhibited to us.

[Marginal heading: It is not included under the priesthood or the kingdom.]

II. Some would have this prophetic office included under the kingly office,
to which legislation belongs, or contained under the priestly office, to which
the power of foretelling and the faculty of consulting God and giving responses
were attached. But they do not speak accurately enough, because this did not
prevent these three offices from being distinct in the past. Certainly, it is
one thing to be an ordinary pastor and another to be a prophet; one thing to
report responses and another to bring forth new things neither heard nor
sought. Prophets were raised up outside the ordinary order either to instruct
or to restore God’s worship, and godly kings and priests eagerly received
their words. They were also taken from a tribe other than Levi, to which the
priesthood belonged, and were consecrated chiefly for this end: to deposit the
sacred canon with the church. This is the prophetic word, not the word of
priests or kings, 2 Peter 1:19, upon which the church had to be built,
Ephesians 2:20. Thus the parts of prophet, priest, and king in Christ are
different, as said above.

[Marginal heading: Its necessity is proved;]

III. The necessity of this office is clear from three things: 1. *The
necessity of revelation*, because there can be no knowledge of God and divine
things without revelation. The natural person does not receive the things of
God, 1 Corinthians 2:14, and no saving revelation is given except through
Christ, John 1:18 and Matthew 11:27. Neither reason nor the law could disclose
the mystery of godliness to us, but only Christ in the gospel. 2. *The nature
of salvation*, because no means of salvation was given besides faith, but
“faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God,” Romans 10:17. 3. *The
oracles of the Old Testament*, which promise this prophecy and necessarily had
to be fulfilled.

[Marginal heading: And its truth.]

IV. Scripture frequently teaches the *truth* of Christ’s prophetic office: in
the oracle of Moses, Deuteronomy 18:15, “Jehovah will raise up a prophet for
you,” and so forth, which Peter teaches pertains to Christ, Acts 3:22; and in
Isaiah 42:1–3, 6, where the Spirit is said to have been bestowed upon Christ by
the Father so that he might “bring forth judgment to the Gentiles,” that is,
act as prophet among the Gentiles and lead them to true righteousness through
the just laws of the gospel and the just worship of God,

Hence he is said to have been given “as a covenant of the people” and “a light
of the Gentiles, to open blind eyes,” and so forth. This is confirmed in
chapter 49:12, but especially in chapter 61:1–2, where the speaker is
introduced: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me; therefore the Lord has
anointed me to evangelize the meek,” which he teaches was fulfilled in
himself, Luke 4. To this pertain Christ’s titles, when he is called the
*messenger of the covenant*, Malachi 3:1; *Counselor*, Isaiah 9:5; the eternal
Wisdom who calls human beings, Proverbs 9:1; the Word (Λόγος) through whom God
addresses us, Hebrews 1:1; and *Master and Teacher*, Matthew 23, who alone must
be heard, Matthew 17:5. This opinion had become so prevalent among the Jews
that it had even passed to the Samaritans: “The Messiah will come, and he will
teach us all things,” John 4:25. Nor, if Christ is distinguished from the
prophet in John 1:21, is it therefore denied that he is a prophet simply;
rather, he is shown to be more than a prophet. It is not denied absolutely
that he is a prophet, but that he is *the Prophet* (τὸν Προφήτην), namely, that
distinguished prophet whom the Jews thought would precede Christ, or one of
the prophets whom they believed would be raised at the Messiah’s advent.

[Marginal heading: Its nature is considered either in the matter of the
doctrine or in the mode of preaching.]

V. The *nature of this prophecy* must be considered chiefly in two things: 1.
The *matter* of the doctrine preached; 2. The *mode* of preaching. The matter
is referred to three heads: the exposition of the law, the promulgation of the
gospel, and the prediction of future things. For just as the prophets’ office
was chiefly comprised in these three parts—the explanation and application of
the law, the inculcation of the promises of grace, and the prediction of
future things revealed to them by God—so Christ most perfectly fulfilled these
three by interpreting the law most truly according to the lawgiver’s mind,
preaching the gospel, and predicting future things which contributed to the
church’s consolation.

[Marginal heading: 1. The first part of prophecy, in explaining the law;]

VI. The first part of the prophetic office concerns *the doctrine of the law*.
He devoted his effort to explaining and inculcating it, both so that a human
being, convicted of his own inability, might flee all the more ardently to
Christ as to a sacred anchor, where he could be safe from the law’s thunderbolts
and curse; and so that he might vindicate the law from the utterly false
interpretations and glosses of the Pharisees of his time and restore to it the
meaning intended by God the lawgiver. He did so by inculcating the spiritual
and inward obedience of the heart which the spiritual law required and
rejecting the external righteousness which the Pharisees considered
sufficient, Matthew 5–7; by subjecting the ceremonial law to the moral law and
its worship to the worship of the latter as more important, Matthew 12:1 and
following; and by rejecting the traditions of the elders as useless and
hateful to God, Matthew 15:3 and chapter 23.

VII. Whether in this preaching of the law Christ corrected the old law as
imperfect and augmented it with new precepts, as the Socinians and Roman
Catholics contend, was disputed above in Locus XI on the law, Questions IV and
V, which consult.

[Marginal heading: Can Christ be called a lawgiver?]

VIII. To the question customarily raised here, *Is Christ, or can he be called,
a lawgiver?* we answer briefly that Christ can be regarded in two ways: either
*absolutely and theologically*, insofar as he is the Word (Λόγος) and the same
God with the Father and the Holy Spirit; or *relatively and economically*,
insofar as he is the God-man (θεάνθρωπος) and mediator between God and human
beings. In the former respect, he is that one lawgiver who can save and
destroy, James 4:12. In the latter, he is not properly a lawgiver, as Moses
was, because he is opposed to Moses and his law as the author of grace and
truth, John 1:17; nor is he principally a lawgiver, because his principal
office was to reveal the mystery of the gospel to us. Nevertheless, nothing
prevents him from being called a lawgiver secondarily and less principally,
both because he was the restorer and vindicator of the law, and because he is
king and head of his church, while it belongs to kings to establish laws.
Hence he

[Printed p. 352 | Internet Archive PDF 380 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

is called the lawgiver (מְחֹקֵק), Isaiah 33:22. Christ’s doctrine also has its
own law, which prescribes faith and obedience of life to us. In this sense the
law is subordinated to the gospel, and the gospel itself is called the law of
faith, the royal law of liberty, and the law of the Spirit and of life.
Nevertheless, our theologians rightly deny against Roman Catholics and
Socinians that Christ is a lawgiver, insofar as *lawgiver* denotes one who
enacts new laws and new moral precepts which are not contained in the law
itself and do not have their foundation in it.

IX. Although faith in Christ, which is prescribed in the gospel, can be called
new with respect to its object, which is revealed by the gospel alone, it
nevertheless pertains to the law with respect to its act and obligation,
because we are bound to believe God and his entire word. Repentance also
pertains to the law—not as it was given to the first human being, but as it was
repeated to the sinner and illuminated by the gospel—and materially, if not
formally, because it teaches and prescribes the manner of repentance.

[Marginal heading: 2. The second, in preaching the gospel;]

X. The other and indeed principal part of the prophetic office is *the
preaching of the gospel*, or the announcement of grace brought by Christ.
Hence the gospel is called *the doctrine of Christ*, Acts 13:12, and *the
testimony of Christ*, 1 Corinthians 1:6 and Revelation 1:2. This is not only
because Christ is the proper object of this doctrine, 1 Corinthians 2:2, but
also because he is its principal cause and primary author. Here, however, we
do not understand the gospel broadly for every promise of grace, which also
had a place in the Old Testament, but strictly for the complete gospel which
contains Christ’s manifestation in the flesh. In it Christ taught, with
respect to his *person*, that he was not only the Son of Man, Matthew 8:20 and
9:6, but also the Son of God, John 3:16 and 5:19, and elsewhere; and, with
respect to his *office*, that he was that Christ promised under the Old
Testament, Matthew 16:16; the prophet, John 10:11, 14; the priest, verse 15 and
chapter 17; and the king of his church, John 18:36–37. He came to minister and
to give his life as a ransom (ἀντίλυτρον) for many, Matthew 20:28, to save the
people from their sins, Matthew 1:21, and to institute a new evangelical
worship different from the old legal worship: both with respect to *place*,
because God no longer chooses, as formerly, one particular place in which to
be worshiped; and with respect to the *mode of worshiping*, because he wills
to be worshiped “in Spirit,” without carnal ceremonies, “and in truth,”
without the shadows of the Mosaic law. This is because “God is Spirit,” and
therefore with a worship more accommodated to his nature.

[Marginal heading: 3. The third, in predicting future things.]

XI. The third part is *the prediction of future things*, either *general*,
such as the prediction of Jerusalem’s destruction and the end of the world,
Matthew 24; the calling of the Gentiles, John 4:21; the abrogation of
ceremonial worship so that it would give way to spiritual and evangelical
worship, verse 23 and John 10:16; and the future cross and nearly continual
persecutions of the faithful, Matthew 24, John 15:20 and 16:20, 22. Yet these
predictions are tempered by the sweetest promises concerning the gracious
presence of Christ and the Holy Spirit amid calamities, John 14:18 and 17:9,
and concerning the blessed outcome of afflictions, John 16:20, 22. Or they are
*special*: concerning his own sufferings, crucifixion, death, and resurrection,
Matthew 16:21 and 26:23; the disciples’ offense and flight, Peter’s denial and
martyrdom, Judas’s betrayal, and the advent of pseudo-Christs, impostors, and
false prophets. To this also pertain the prophecies of the Apocalypse, in
which Christ suggests to his servant the things that would occur until the
consummation of the age, both the church’s contests and calamities and its
victories and triumphs.

[Marginal heading: The mode of prophecy. 1. Immediate and mediate.]

XII. The *mode of Christ’s prophecy* is considered in various respects: In a
twofold *mode of teaching*: both *immediate*, by himself in the days of his
flesh; and *mediate*, through his ministers, both

[Printed p. 353 | Internet Archive PDF 381 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

before the incarnation through the prophets in the Old Testament, who spoke
as they were moved by Christ’s prophetic Spirit, 1 Peter 1:11 and 2 Peter
1:21—hence he is said to have preached by the Spirit in Noah, 1 Peter 3:19;
and if God is said to have spoken only in the Son in these last days, Hebrews
1:2, this must be understood not simply but comparatively, that is, in a
sensible manner and through him as incarnate (ἔνσαρκον)—and after his advent
through apostles and pastors. The former were called and inspired
extraordinarily, with the gift of infallibility for a time, but the latter are
called by the ordinary ministry of human beings and endowed only with common
inspiration until the end of the age.

[Marginal heading: 2. External and internal.]

2. *External and internal*, whereby he not only struck the body’s ears
outwardly through the word, but also inwardly bent and opened hearts through
the Spirit and led them into all truth, John 16:13. In this sense Christ’s
words are called “Spirit and life,” John 6:63, not only with respect to the
meaning of the words which he had spoken about eating his flesh—which were not
to be taken crudely and carnally, but spiritually and mystically—but also with
respect to the unconquerable efficacy he exerts in the conversion of hearts.
Hence the gospel is said to be written not on tablets of stone, but on “fleshy
tablets of the heart,” 2 Corinthians 3:3. This is the privilege of the new
covenant, in which God must write the law upon hearts and believers are to be
taught by God (θεοδίδακτοι), Isaiah 55:13.

[Marginal heading: 3. In various characteristics;]

XIII. 3. In various *characteristics* which accompanied his preaching: 1. In
teaching he everywhere exercised the highest *authority*, which befits the
supreme and infallible teacher and was unusual among other, inferior teachers.
For he taught “as one having authority” (ἐξουσίαν ἔχων), not like the scribes
and Pharisees, Matthew 7:28–29, so that no one could resist him and they were
compelled to admit that no one had ever spoken in this way, John 7:46. 2.
Admirable *wisdom*, by which he so tempered his doctrine that he vindicated it
from his adversaries’ cavils and often slew them with their own sword, Matthew
21. Add to this that divine knowledge by which he demonstrated himself to be
the knower of hearts (καρδιογνώστην) and foreknower of future things. 3.
Generous *freedom*, teaching God’s truth without partiality
(προσωποληψίαν) and admonishing everyone of his duty. 4. Distinguished
*eloquence*, through which words of grace (λόγοι τῆς χάριτος) flowed from his
mouth, Luke 4:22, and he surpassed all others in eloquence, so that majesty
vied with simplicity and elevated speech with sweetness. 5. *Unconquerable
constancy*, paying no regard to his adversaries’ authority and efforts and
rebuking their crimes the more sharply, the more they attempted to impose a
mask of holiness upon the simple, Matthew 23:5. 6. *Most fervent zeal*, by
which, when moved, he did not seek concealment but spoke openly, and often
passed over the time for taking food, Mark 3:20 and John 4:32. He gave an
example of it in John 2:15–16.

[Marginal heading: 4. In the efficacy of the doctrine;]

XIV. 4. In the plainly divine *efficacy of the doctrine*, by which Christ
outwardly taught with the greatest gravity and majesty, through which his
hearers were deeply moved and his preaching was plainly different from that
of the scribes, Matthew 7:29; while inwardly it powerfully insinuated itself
into minds and affected them in marvelous ways, converting some or convincing
others and exerting on all a force greater than human, John 7:46 and Luke 24.
Hence it is gathered that our prophet must be endowed with divine power, since
he not only floods us with light, but also furnishes eyes, opens hearts, and
irradiates minds with heavenly splendor, 2 Corinthians 4:6.

[Marginal heading: In the authenticity and perfection of the doctrine;]

XV. 5. In the *authenticity and perfection of the doctrine*, which was such
that after the revelation of the doctrine Christ brought, no other is to be
expected afterward, as the apostle shows in Hebrews 1–2. And if the Spirit is
said to teach

[Printed p. 354 | Internet Archive PDF 382 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the church after Christ, this must not be referred to a greater perfection
extensively and objectively, but intensively and subjectively. Hence the
Spirit is said to receive from Christ’s doctrine: “He will take from what is
mine,” says Christ, John 16:14, because he was to teach no other things than
those which had already been proposed by Christ and which pertain to his
person and office.

[Marginal heading: 6. In holiness of life and miracles.]

XVI. 6. In *holiness of life and the performance of miracles*. For he
sanctioned and confirmed his doctrine not only by the most perfect holiness of
life and admirable patience, but also by various stupendous miracles, both to
assert the divinity of his person and to seal the truth of his doctrine. For
the one who by his own power performs so many and such great works surpassing
the powers of all created nature must be furnished with divine power, since he
performs works which God alone can do. It is therefore evident that the same
one speaks God’s words, and he must be judged to bring self-authenticating
(αὐτόπιστον) and divine doctrine. For a lie does not befit supreme power, nor
should God be thought to lend his power to an impostor and false prophet. I
know that Christ’s adversaries allege many things to attack Christ’s miracles,
but it was shown above in Locus XIII, Question II, theses 18–19, that they are
empty and utterly false.

[Marginal heading: How Christ differs from the other prophets.]

XVII. And from this the manifold difference between Christ and the other
prophets is gathered not obscurely. For 1. They were only ministers in God’s
house, who spoke in Christ’s name and received commands from him. But Christ
is Son and Lord, Hebrews 3; the chief shepherd (Ἀρχιποίμην), 1 Peter 5:4; and
the supreme master, the teacher of teachers. By his Spirit all who speak are
moved, and he alone must be heard as the arbiter of faith, Matthew 17:5. 2.
They could only preach outwardly, and nothing further. John baptizes with
water, and Paul plants and waters, but he cannot add the increase; hence they
are said to be “nothing,” 1 Corinthians 3:7, with respect to inward power and
efficacy. But Christ works both outwardly and inwardly, inscribes the law itself
upon hearts, and “baptizes with Spirit and fire,” Matthew 3:11; that is, he
inspires inward efficacy and works within through his Spirit acting like fire,
whose property is not only to cleanse filth and wash the external surface like
water, but also to penetrate to the inward parts. 3. Others were illuminated
by divine light, but through a flashing and in the mode of a transitory act;
hence they could not reveal future or secret things whenever they pleased. But
Christ possessed it like a permanent habit which he could use whenever he
willed, although while he was on earth it did not extend simply to all future
things, but to those which the nature of his office and our salvation
required. Hence he is said not to have known the day of judgment, Mark 13:32.
4. The Spirit was given to others, but by measure; but he is given to Christ
without measure (ἀμέτρως), John 3:34. The Spirit is said to rest upon him with
all his gifts, Isaiah 11:1–2. The treasures of knowledge and understanding are
hidden in him, Colossians 2:3. 5. Others are called for a definite time, but
Christ for eternity.

XVIII. To others God revealed himself “in many portions and in many ways”
(πολυμερῶς and πολυτρόπως), through various degrees and modes: through visions
and dreams, immediate and secret inspirations, and various other modes by
which he presented himself to those either waking or sleeping; either to the
internal senses in imagination, to the external senses of eyes or ears, or to
the intellectual mind itself by representation, in rapture and ecstasy. But
Christ knew all things most perfectly—not things he received from elsewhere,
but things he himself saw and heard in the Father’s bosom, John 1:18. Hence the
revelation made by Christ in the last days, as the most perfect and ultimate,
is opposed to the various other revelations which had occurred through the
prophets, Hebrews 1:2.

[Marginal heading: And specifically from Moses.]

XIX. But Christ was not only superior to the other prophets; he also surpassed
Moses himself, although Moses was far more excellent than others and conversed
with God more familiarly. God did not speak to him as to others in a vision,
riddle, or dream, but “mouth to mouth,” that is, face-to-face and most
familiarly, as a friend speaks to a friend, Numbers 12:8. He is indeed said to
be one who would be similar to Moses, Deuteronomy 18:15, “like him,” and he was
such in various things. Moses and Christ were badly treated immediately from
birth; Pharaoh persecuted Moses and Herod Christ; Moses liberated the people
from Egypt and Christ from sin; Moses was mediator between God and the people,
and Christ between God and human beings; each confirmed his doctrine by
miracles; each fasted forty days; and each had his face illuminated with
divine splendor. Yet Christ was far greater than Moses. The latter was only a
servant in God’s house, but Christ was Son and Lord, Hebrews 3. Moses
promulgated the law, Christ the gospel. Moses was merely an intermediary, but
Christ was the true maker of peace (εἰρηνοποιός). Moses had glory in his
ministry, but a glory “being abolished” (καταργουμένην), such as the splendor
of his face; but Christ’s glory is constant and eternal, 2 Corinthians 3:11.

XX. The *object of the prophetic office*, insofar as Christ exercised it
immediately, was the Jews. For this reason he is called “a minister of the
circumcision,” Romans 15:8, and professes that he was sent to the lost sheep of
the house of Israel, Matthew 15:24. Insofar as he exercised it mediately in the
Old Testament, its object was the same Jews, Romans 9:4, except for the few
Gentiles who were called as the firstfruits of the Gentiles, such as
Melchizedek, Jethro, Rahab, Naaman, and others like them. But in the New
Testament its object is all nations indiscriminately, who are called to the
communion of salvation.
