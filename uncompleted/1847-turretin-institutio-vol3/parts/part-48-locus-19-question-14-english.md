QUESTION XIV.

ON THE LAWFUL MINISTRY OF BAPTISM.

Is baptism by laypeople or women lawful in any case? We deny against the
papists.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

I. The question is necessary because of the papists, who defend baptism by
laypeople and even women. They teach, indeed, that laypeople may not baptize
publicly and solemnly, or even privately when a priest is present, nor when
priests are absent except in a case of necessity. But in danger of death they
contend that any layperson, baptized or unbaptized, may baptize, provided that
the person knows the rite. They even grant this

[1847 vol. III printed p. 336 | IA leaf 370 | PDF p. 370]

power to women, an error that flows from the other error concerning the
absolute necessity of baptism.

II. We, however, contend that baptism by laypeople, of whichever sex, is null,
and we judge that it cannot be rightly administered by anyone except a
lawfully called pastor, whether in a case of necessity or apart from it.

[Marginalia: Baptism by laypeople and women is proved unlawful. 1. From
Christ’s order.]

III. The reasons are as follows. (1) The right to baptize belongs only to
those to whom Christ gave the right to preach the gospel, since these two
things accompany one another as parts of the public ministry and are joined by
an indivisible bond. But Christ gave this right only to the apostles and their
lawful successors (Matt. 28:19–20). It is clear from verse 16, where the eleven
apostles are said to have gone into Galilee, that it does not pertain to
others. Although several others besides the apostles were present when Christ
said these things, it does not follow that the words “Go, teach all nations
and baptize” do not pertain to the apostles alone. Nothing prevents Christ’s
command from having been given solely to the apostles in the presence of many
witnesses. It is certain that Christ addressed the sacred ministers whom he
was calling to proclaim the gospel. Therefore, just as no one was to preach
with public authority unless sent and particularly called to it, so no one was
to baptize without such a call. For this reason a woman must not teach in the
church (1 Tim. 2:12).

[Marginalia: 2. Because the sacraments are God’s seals.]

IV. Second, no one can handle a prince’s seals except one who holds this
office by a special appointment and performs the duties of a chancellor. But
the sacraments are the seals of the King of kings, and therefore their custody
and dispensation belong only to sacred ministers.

[Marginalia: 3. Because laypeople may not give the Eucharist.]

V. Third, a private person or woman may no more baptize than give the
Eucharist. It should not be said that the cases are different because baptism
is the sacrament of initiation through which we are incorporated into the
church, and is therefore more necessary than the sacrament of confirmation;
that adults, when they cannot use the Holy Supper, are indeed renewed by their
desire for it and by the gospel promise, which is the power of God for
salvation to every believer; but that no other means of regeneration and
salvation besides baptism has been left to infants. The same reasoning applies
in both cases: if we must enter the church, we must also be nourished in it.
And if in a case of necessity we can be nourished by the word alone, why can
we not be received by God’s grace into the internal communion of the invisible
church without baptism?

[Marginalia: 4. Because it rests on the absolute necessity of baptism.]

VI. Fourth, baptism by laypeople and women rests on the erroneous foundation
of the absolute necessity of baptism. Once that foundation has been
demolished, this practice must also be overthrown.

[Marginalia: 5. Because the ancients disapprove of this.]

VII. Fifth, the ancients agree that *baptism by women* (γυναικοβαπτισμὸν) is
unlawful. Epiphanius, *Heresy* 42, relates it among the errors of the
Marcionites. Tertullian, *On the Veiling of Virgins*, chapter 9: “A woman is
not permitted to speak in the church, nor to baptize, nor to offer.” The
*Apostolic Constitutions*, book 3, chapter 9, gives the chapter the title “That
women ought not to baptize, for it is impious and alien to Christ’s teaching.”
The one-hundredth canon of the fourth Council of Carthage is: “A woman must not
presume to baptize.” Lombard, book 4, distinction 7, and Gratian, canon *A
woman*, distinction 4, wrongly add “unless necessity compels,” because nothing
of this clause is found either in the canons of the African church or in
Balsamon.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. One office of teaching is public and from authority; another is private
and from charity. The latter can be performed by private persons, but not the
former. Now the sacraments, as the seals of the Prince, are acts of authority
that cannot be handled by private persons, and not merely acts of charity.
Thus instruction and teaching extend more broadly than baptism. For although
no one teaches unless baptized,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 337 | IA leaf 371 | PDF p. 371]

not everyone who teaches baptizes. Add that the necessity of doctrine is one
thing, for it is an absolute necessity of means for salvation; the necessity
of the sacraments is another, for it is hypothetical and a necessity of
command.

IX. The example of Zipporah circumcising her son cannot render baptism by
women lawful (Exod. 4). (1) We must live by laws, not examples. This example
cannot and should not be drawn into imitation, not only because it was
extraordinary and singular, with no other parallel occurring, but also because
the deed was undertaken by an unwilling and indignant woman, scarcely in
control of her mind because of anger (v. 25). (2) Circumcision and baptism have
different accounts. Circumcision could be administered by private persons,
and nothing peculiar is assigned to one who circumcises; but the right to
baptize is given only to those who have the right to teach. (3) An inference
from what was done before the constitution of the Jewish commonwealth to what
was done afterward is invalid. Otherwise it would have to be said that the
interruption of circumcision in the wilderness ought to have been tolerated in
Canaan, which is absurd. (4) She did this in the presence of her husband, a
prophet. But the papists do not allow women to baptize when priests are
present. (5) Nor, if God was thereby appeased toward Moses, does it immediately
follow that this deed pleased God in every respect or that there was no sin in
it. God is appeased from his mercy, not from the worth and merit of our works.
The thing could be approved by God but not its manner, just as Scripture
relates concerning Ahab’s humility (1 Kings 21:29), and how the gentiles
transported from Assyria into Samaria were delivered from the lions through
the worship they practiced by honoring the true God together with false gods
(2 Kings 17).

X. Those who are said in Acts to have baptized were not private persons or
mere laypeople. Philip was not only a deacon but also an evangelist (Acts
21:8). Ananias was one of the seventy disciples, a pastor of the church of
Damascus, who did this at the Lord’s command and to whom Paul was therefore
sent. It is not said that Cornelius and his household were ordered to be
baptized by the brothers who were present with Peter (Acts 10:48), but only
that they were ordered to be baptized, or that Peter commanded them to be
baptized. Hence it no more follows that they were baptized by Peter’s
companions than by Peter himself. Otherwise it would follow that laypeople can
baptize in the presence of ministers, which the papists deny.

XI. The three thousand people said to have been baptized by the apostles
could conveniently have been baptized by them through sprinkling, if not
through immersion. Add that various people from among the seventy disciples,
to whom this authority belonged, could have been present.

XII. Those who administered baptism by the apostles’ command and authority
are not to be regarded as private persons. A call could not have been lacking
to one who did something with the apostles as its authorizers, especially
since not so much they themselves as the apostles through them appear to
baptize, just as Christ baptized only through the apostles (John 4:12 [sic]).

XIII. Concerning baptism by laypeople, nothing certain can be gathered from
the ancients. Although it must not be denied that not a few admitted it—such as
Tertullian, *On Baptism*, chapter 17; Jerome, *Dialogue against the
Luciferians*; Augustine, *Against the Letter of Parmenian*, book 1, chapter 13;
and the Council of Elvira, canon 38—it is certain that others did not approve
it. Basil, in his epistle to Amphilochius, chapter 1, judges it “a sacrilege
for laypeople to baptize.” Ignatius, in his epistle to Hero, and Epiphanius,
*Heresy* 79, are so opposed to baptism conferred by laypeople that they do not
even permit deacons to baptize.

XIV. Since a sacrament is a public action, it must be observed publicly in the
church by public ministers. No necessity or suitable cause can be offered why
a sacred and public action should pass into a private one, because God’s
ordinance is for us the highest law and supreme necessity, which must be
obeyed rather than popular ignorance and weakness indulged.
