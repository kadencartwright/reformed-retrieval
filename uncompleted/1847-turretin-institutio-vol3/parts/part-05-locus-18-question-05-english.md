QUESTION FIVE

THE UNITY OF THE CHURCH

In what sense is the church said to be one?

I. Among the church’s attributes, the first is its unity, which flows from its
nature. For since it is a holy society and mystical body, embracing with a
bond all the elect who are united to one another and to Christ by the same
Spirit, faith, and love, it must have some unity through which all its members
are joined to one another. Nor is it enough to embrace the multitude of the
elect in thought and mind unless we conceive such a unity of the church as
one into which we are truly persuaded that we have been engrafted. For unless
we are united under Christ the head with all the other members, no hope of the
future inheritance remains for us. Hence the bridegroom says that his bride,
or dove, is one—her mother’s only one (Song of Songs 6:9)—and throughout
Scripture she is represented under one body and one flock.

II. Since the church can be considered either with respect to its outward
state or with respect to its inward state, its unity can accordingly be
discussed in relation to each. Here the subject is not its visible unity with
respect to its outward state, insofar as it is the dwelling place of the
faithful with respect to the profession of faith and the communion of sacred
things. Nor is the subject the schisms that can occur within it; that unity
will have to be discussed in what follows. Rather, the subject is the inward
and invisible unity that belongs to the church properly so called with
respect to its inward state.

III. Again, the subject is not an accidental unity of place, time, rites, or
age, since it is evident that the church possesses manifold diversity in
these. Just as it has its seat in various places and belongs to every time, so
it belongs not to one age but to many through which it passes successively,
experiencing now its infancy, now its youth, and now its maturity. Nor is it
uniform with respect to rites and government. Rather, the subject is the
essential unity that remains the same in both respects, amid diverse places
and times. Through it the faithful worship the same God, acknowledge the same
Savior, have the same faith, are animated by the same Spirit, and are members
of the same body. This unity can be referred to six heads: 1. unity of body;
2. of head; 3. of Spirit; 4. of faith; 5. of love; and 6. of hope.

[Marginalia: Unity of the body.]

IV. 1. Unity of the body. Although it is constituted from various parts, as
it were so many members living in diverse places and times, nevertheless the
body that arises from all of them is one. The apostle explains this
admirably: “As the body is one and has many members, but all the members,
though they are many, are one body, so also is Christ” (Romans 12:5; 1
Corinthians 12:12)—that is, the mystical Christ, namely, the church. Thus
neither the multitude of the faithful nor diversity of offices, age, and
character can prevent them from being one in Christ: “We have been baptized
into

[1847 vol. III printed p. 25 | IA leaf 59 | PDF p. 59]

the same Spirit, that we might be one body, whether Jews or Greeks.”

As Augustine explains this in relation to the faithful of the Old and New
Testaments, he uses the analogy of an infant being born whose hand emerged
before its head. “Just as in the case of one being born,” he says on Psalm 61,
“the hand came first while the head had not yet come forth; nevertheless both
the head and the hand were connected. Therefore, brothers, do not think that
all the righteous who suffered persecution from the wicked—even those who
came before the Lord’s advent to foretell the Lord’s advent—did not belong to
Christ’s members, and so forth.”

V. There is both a continuous body and a discrete body. The latter is such by
mere aggregation, as are the body of a city or family, a flock of sheep, or
the unity of a house; the former is such through the unity of one subject, as
is the human body. Accordingly, Scripture customarily describes the unity of
the church’s body in two ways. It does so at one time under the symbol of the
unity of a flock, in which both Jews and Gentiles are united into one: “I have
other sheep that are not of this fold; I must bring them also, so that there
may be one flock and one shepherd” (John 10:16). It also does so under the
unity of a city and people beneath the same king: “Therefore you are no
longer strangers, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God’s
household” (Ephesians 2:19). At another time it does so under the symbol of
the unity of a body, when the faithful are called members of one another and
Christ is called the head (1 Corinthians 12:12; Ephesians 1:22).

[Marginalia: Unity of the head.]

VI. 2. Unity of the head, which gives unity to the body. For from the fact
that there is one head of the church, the church must also constitute one
body, since neither a two-headed body nor a head belonging to two bodies can
exist. Paul discloses this mystery more than once, but especially when he says
that “this was God’s good pleasure: in the administration of the fullness of
the times to sum up all things under one head in Christ, both those in heaven
and those on earth” (Ephesians 1:10), and, “He gave him as head over the
church, which is his body,” and so forth (verse 22).

Nor was any other head required for the universal church than one who, as
omnipotent and omnipresent, can be present to his members everywhere and
always and can govern and protect them by his life-giving influence. Thus the
papists vainly contend here that the unity of a visible and subordinate head
is required to constitute a true church, as will be seen in its proper
place.

[Marginalia: Unity of the Spirit.]

VII. 3. Unity of the Spirit, which flows from the unity of the head to
constitute the unity of the body. The one head has one body joined to himself
because he animates it by one Spirit who pervades all its members. Hence the
apostle joins unity of the Spirit to unity of the body (1 Corinthians 12;
Ephesians 4:4; and elsewhere, 1 Corinthians 6:17): “He who is joined to the
Lord is one spirit with him.” Although this Spirit is presented under various
symbols and is described as sevenfold because of the diversity of gifts he
imparts to the faithful (Revelation 1:4; Isaiah 11:2), he is nevertheless no
less one in essence. “All these things are worked by one and the same Spirit,”
says Paul (1 Corinthians 12:11).

[Marginalia: Unity of faith.]

VIII. 4. Unity of faith, because there is one faith (Ephesians 4:5), both the
faith that is believed and the faith by which one believes. That is, there is
one doctrine of salvation set forth in the gospel that faith embraces. Both
subjectively, in relation to believers, and objectively, in relation to the
object toward which it is directed, it always has been and will be one, both
before and after Christ, for “Christ is the same yesterday, today, and
forever” (Hebrews 13:8).

Revelation could indeed grow successively in its manner and degrees of
manifestation, being at one time more obscure, as under the Old Testament; at
another time clearer, as under the New; and destined to be clearest when we
all attain “to the unity of the faith and of knowledge, to a perfect man”
(Ephesians 4:13), that is, when we are perfected in glory. Nevertheless, the
substance of the doctrine in matters necessary for salvation was always the
same and had to be believed by all. Irenaeus teaches this admirably in book 1
of *Against*

[1847 vol. III printed p. 26 | IA leaf 60 | PDF p. 60]

*Heresies*, chapter 2: “The church, sown throughout the whole world even to
the ends of the earth, received from the apostles and their disciples the
faith that is in one God, the almighty Father who made heaven and earth; in
one Jesus Christ, the Son of God incarnate for our salvation; and in the Holy
Spirit, and so forth.”

And in chapter 3: “Having received this preaching and this faith, as we have
said, the church carefully guards it as though inhabiting one house, and
believes these things alike as though having one soul and one heart. In
unison, it preaches, teaches, and hands down these things as though possessing
one mouth. For although the languages in the world are dissimilar,
nevertheless the force of the tradition”—that is, the doctrine of the creed
handed down in Scripture—“is one and the same. Neither do the churches
founded in Germany believe otherwise or hand down otherwise, nor those among
the Iberians, and so forth. Rather, just as the sun, a creature of God, is one
and the same throughout the whole world, so also the light of the preaching
of the truth shines everywhere and enlightens all people who wish to come to
the knowledge of the truth.”

[Marginalia: Unity of love.]

IX. 5. Unity of love, which follows the unity of faith. Through faith the
faithful are united to Christ; through love they ought to be bound to one
another, so that “the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace” may be
preserved (Ephesians 4:3) and the faithful may become one heart and one soul
(Acts 2). Love is therefore called “the bond of perfection” (Colossians 3:14),
not only in relation to the Christian virtues, because it binds them together,
so that whoever has love has and exercises all the others and without it they
are nothing, but also in relation to the church, which as God’s house is held
together by the bond of love (Ephesians 4:16), and in relation to the faithful
and members of the church, whom love unites to one another, so that each may
perform its own office and they may render mutual services to one another for
the benefit and preservation of the body.

[Marginalia: Unity of hope.]

X. 6. Unity of hope (Ephesians 4:4), that is, of the thing hoped for and the
heavenly inheritance to which we are all called on equal terms and in which
we will all share in due time (Colossians 1:5; Romans 8:17). One glory is
not stored up for the great and another for the small, one for Jews and
another for Gentiles. Rather, one and the same inheritance of the saints in
light is stored up for all.

[Marginalia: Unity of baptism.]

XI. Unity of baptism, of which the apostle speaks in the same place, belongs
to the visible church and its outward state, unless we understand inward
rather than outward baptism—that is, the thing signified rather than the
sign.

XII. Although there are many particular churches scattered throughout the
world, the church’s unity is not therefore broken, nor is its body torn
apart, because one catholic church always remains. It is composed of those
various parts, and since they are homogeneous, they receive the same name as
the whole.

XIII. The unity of God and the Father of all contributes greatly to
strengthening this unity. He is said to be “over all” by authority and
dominion, “through all” by his providence, by which he permeates, sustains,
and governs all things, and “in all” by his grace (Ephesians 4:6).

XIV. Moreover, these various kinds of unity that occur in the church are so
many most effective arguments for the faithful to foster love and concord
among themselves and to preserve the unity of the Spirit in the bond of
peace. They are brought forward by the apostle for this purpose in Romans 12,
Ephesians 4, and 1 Corinthians 12.
