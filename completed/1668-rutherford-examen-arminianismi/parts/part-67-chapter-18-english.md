CHAPTER XVIII

ON

Sacraments and Ecclesiastical Discipline

It is asked whether the Remonstrants are right in their *Apologia*, chapter 23,
folio 244, to say that the sacraments are signs that not only

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 717 | PDF p. 766]

represent and foreshadow things to us, but also in a certain manner—namely, one
that is only significative and commemorative—exhibit and seal them. For here
they agree with Socinus, who says in his book *On the Duty of a Christian Man*
(as the most learned Leiden professors observe in their *Censure of the
Remonstrants' Confession*, chapter 23): “When the Lord's Supper is celebrated,
the forgiveness of sins is not actually sealed. Rather, it is only commemorated
and proclaimed that forgiveness was once sealed in consequence of and together
with Christ's death, of which that sealing is an effect.” Hence the sacraments
are essentially only
declarative and commemorative signs, not seals that exhibit Christ and his
benefits. We therefore deny the Remonstrants' position in this sense and oppose
it as follows.

1. If the sacraments were only signs commemorating Christ and his benefits, the
bread in the Lord's Supper would no more be a seal of the Lord's body to the
one eating than to others who are not eating at that time. Baptism would no
more be the washing of regeneration to the person baptized than to the
witnesses who stand by and watch the administration of baptism. For the water
is a commemorative sign of Christ's blood to those standing by, and the bread
is a representative and declarative sign of Christ's body to those who are not
eating.

2. Paul says in Romans 4:11 that a sacrament is an exhibitive sign: “And he
received the sign of

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 718 | PDF p. 767 | split word joined: circumcisionis]

cision, a seal of the righteousness of faith received while uncircumcised, so
that he might be the father of all who believe while uncircumcised, with
righteousness imputed to them also.” Chapter 6:4 says, “We were therefore
buried with Christ through baptism into death, so that just as Christ was
raised from the dead unto the glory of the Father, so we too might walk in
newness of life.” But this burial is not shadowy and representative; it is the
true and real crucifixion of the old man. First Peter 3:21 says, “The
corresponding pattern, baptism, now also saves us—not by removing the filth of
the flesh, but by the pledge of a good conscience before God—through the
resurrection of Jesus Christ.” A mute and shadowy sign, however, will operate
only in the manner that the dawn is said to bring about the day: it is neither
the cause nor the instrument but only a sign of the day.

The Remonstrants do indeed say in the same place, folio 245: “God deigns to
grant those who use the signs with true faith a sense of his singular grace.
Hence in their minds they perceive a taste of divine grace, as though it were a
kind of seal, by which they are made readier and more cheerful to love in
return so benevolent and merciful a God and to celebrate his benefits and
praises in the church. How they may be called seals and be said to seal divine
grace in any other manner is incomprehensible.” But they make the sacraments
merely commemorative signs, and thus detract from their dignity, efficacy, and
proper use. For in this manner someone who keeps watch through the whole night
is made more cheerful at the sight of the morning star and rejoices greatly at
the rising of the sun, although the

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 719 | PDF p. 768 | split word joined: tamen]

sing of the sun acts upon his mind only as a bare sign, by a merely objective
action rather than a physical and real one.

3. Paul says in 1 Corinthians 10:16, “The cup of blessing that we bless, is it
not communion in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not
communion in the body of Christ?” If he means that the bread is communion in
Christ's body merely and solely by signification, the sacrament will be no more
effective as a means of salvation than the twelve stones erected in the middle
of the Jordan. By God's own institution those stones were commemorative signs
of the people's miraculous entrance into the holy land. Indeed, the bread we
eat in private houses can stir up a literal remembrance of the heavenly bread,
Christ's body. But in verse 21 Paul explains what it is to take the Lord's cup:
namely, to be a participant in the Lord's table, just as to drink the cup of
demons is truly and really to be a participant in the table of demons. Indeed,
on the believer's account of a sacrament it will be a sign only in the manner
that the bare letter of Scripture is called a sign by mere signification.

We are not detained by their claim that one cannot conceive an organic
exhibition of grace that is neither physical nor merely moral and objective,
nor both at once, but something more, something relative and sacramental. For
we grant a third kind, namely, a hyperphysical one that is neither physical nor
moral. Furthermore, even among human beings there are exhibitive signs of the
thing signified that have both a physical and a

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 720 | PDF p. 769 | split word joined: moralem]

ral character and possess more than bare signification or commemoration. Thus
the exhibition of the keys of a royal fortress to a duke is neither the
physical delivery into his hand of the stones and timber of the fortress nor a
merely commemorative and moral signification, which could be made by bare
words. Nevertheless, it is a real exhibition of the fortress to the duke for
him to guard. A seal attached to the document is not a physical exhibition of
the lands, nor is it a bare sign that only stirs the landowner's mind
objectively and literally. Rather, it is a real, exhibitive confirmation of
the lands. The same must be said about putting a stone and clod into the hand
of one whom the owner of an estate legally establishes as its new lawful
proprietor or possessor. It is neither the physical giving of every acre of the
land nor bare signification, but a real conveyance and alienation of the estate
into the possession or right of the new owner.

Nor are we detained by their claim that it cannot be understood how these
signs concur as instruments or exhibitive means of grace, since the faithful
who use them are already completely persuaded of the promised grace before
they use them and have already apprehended by firm faith the thing signified by
these signs; and since an immediate operation of grace upon the mind and will
and an instrumental operation are incompatible. For if this reasoning were
valid, a sacrament would hardly be even a bare commemorative sign, because
before eating the bread in the Supper the faithful have already apprehended

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 721 | PDF p. 770]

the grace of Christ by faith. But we say that a greater measure of grace is
given through the reception of the sacrament. And although the sacrament's
organic action is incomprehensible to us, and although the Arminians cannot
measure the matter by reason—the yardstick by which they always try to measure
God's mysteries—must the thing itself therefore be denied?

It is asked whether the sacraments may be administered by any layperson. The
Remonstrants, with the Socinians, teach that they may, *Apologia*, chapter 23,
folio 246.

With Scripture, we restrict the administration of the sacraments to ministers
alone.

1. Because in Matthew 28:19; Acts 2:42; 1 Corinthians 1:14–17; Acts 10:48; and
1 Corinthians 11:23, the administration of the sacraments is commanded and
assigned to ministers alone.

2. Because no necessity of the sacraments—since they are not absolutely
necessary for salvation—compels us to depart from the practice of Christ and
the apostles.

The Council of Carthage, canon 100, says, “A woman must not presume to baptize.”
Gratian, *On Consecration*, distinction 4, perversely and corruptly adds these
words: “Unless necessity compels it.” Tertullian says in *On the Veiling of
Women*: “A woman is not permitted to speak in the church, nor to teach, baptize,
or offer.” Augustine speaks here hesitantly and without settled assurance in
*Against the Letter of Parmenian*, book 2, chapter 13: “If a layman, compelled
by necessity, has given baptism to a dying person, I do not know whether it
would

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 722 | PDF p. 771]

be pious to say that it must be repeated.”

MATTHEW 28:19

“Go therefore, teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and
of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.”

It is asked whether the Remonstrants are right to teach in their *Apologia*,
chapter 23, folio 252, that infant baptism is practiced by them as an ancient
rite but has authority neither from Christ's command nor from apostolic
institution. We deny it.

1. Because here we have the Holy Spirit's argument. Acts 2:38 says, “Let every
one of you be baptized,” and so forth. Verse 39 says, “For the promise has been
made to you and to your children.” Hence Peter's argument is this: those to
whom the promise was made are to be baptized. But the promise was made to the
Jews and the children of the Jews. Therefore, and so forth.

2. This is God's argument in Genesis 17. Those to whom the covenant's promise
belongs also possess the covenant's seal, even if many of them, because of
their lack of age, do not understand the covenant's seal. But the covenant's
promise belongs to all the children of the faithful. Therefore, and so forth.
The major premise is in Genesis 17:7–13: “I will be your God and the God of your
offspring; therefore, let every male be circumcised on the eighth day.” But
this argument is valid among us because baptism, with respect to its substance,
is the same sacrament as circumcision and the seal of the same covenant, as is
clear from Romans 6:4–5 and Colossians 2:11–12.

3. It is no wonder that the Remonstrants call infant baptism into question,
since Scripture asserts that

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 723 | PDF p. 772 | split word joined: Baptismum]

tism is performed for the forgiveness of sins, while they deny that infants
have any sin properly so called. On that hypothesis, infant baptism would be a
custom and rite that is neither holy nor indifferent but plainly unlawful and
contrary to God's Word.

It is asked whether baptism is only a temporary rite, one that by Christ's
institution will not endure to the end of the world. The Remonstrants say it is,
Episcopius, private disputation 29, corollary 1. So also the Socinians.

We deny it. 1. Because it is a sacrament of the New Testament, annexed to the
preaching of the Word and the doctrine of the gospel, and it has the same
institution and duration as that doctrine. Therefore, by Christ's institution
and promise, it will endure to the end of the world, Matthew 28:19–20; Mark
16:15–16.

2. Because baptism is commanded to all adults who wish to be saved, just as
faith and repentance are, Mark 16:16; Acts 2:37–39.

3. Because it is commanded to all families professing the faith. Acts 10:47
says, “Can anyone forbid water, so that these people who have received the Holy
Spirit just as we have should not be baptized?” But the Holy Spirit is received
until the consummation of the age, and he remains with his people to the end of
the world, John 14:16.

4. Because baptism is a means of regeneration instituted by God and is said to
save us, Romans 6:2–4; Colossians 2:11–12; Titus 3:3–4; 1 Peter 3:21. And

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 724 | PDF p. 773]

its effect is also the inward washing away of sins, Acts 2:38; 1 Peter 3:21.
It must therefore continue in this world as long as regeneration and the
forgiveness of sins continue, that is, until the end of the world.

It is asked whether baptism is only a solemn rite by which we are distinguished
from others and incorporated into divine worship, but not one through which
grace is really conferred. The Remonstrants say it is, *Confession*, chapter
23, thesis 3; *Apologia*, chapter 23, folio 243; Episcopius, disputation 29,
thesis 8; Socinus, *On the Duty of a Christian Man*, chapter 4; Smalcius,
disputation 9 against Frantzius, page 299: “The claim that there is any inward
efficacy in baptism is a fable.”

Against this: 1. Mark 1:4 says, “John preached the baptism of repentance for
the forgiveness of sins.”

2. Romans 6:4 says, “We were therefore buried with him through baptism into his
death.” And 1 Peter 3:21 says, “The corresponding pattern, baptism, now also
saves us—not by removing the filth of the flesh, but by the pledge of a good
conscience before God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.” But to say
that the forgiveness of sins and regeneration are represented in baptism by a
bare signification, only literally, is to make baptism a bare sign.

3. Because Christ's end and intention is to cleanse the church by the washing
of water through the Word, Ephesians 5:25–26.

4. Because if infant baptism is a rite unnecessary by divine institution, it
cannot be a moral admonition to infants concerning the forgiveness of sins,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 725 | PDF p. 774]

since they teach that infants have no sin properly so called that needs
forgiveness, *Apologia*, folios 84–85.

It is asked whether in the Lord's Supper Christ's death is only announced and
commemorated, while no spiritual gifts are sealed. The Remonstrants say this,
*Confession*, chapter 23, thesis 4; *Apologia*, chapter 23, folio 249; Arminius,
*Articles to Be Examined*; likewise the Racovian *Catechism*, on the prophetic
office of Jesus Christ, chapter 3, page 186. Against this, we teach that Christ
and his benefits are sealed and really conferred upon us, but in a spiritual
manner.

1. First Corinthians 10:16 says, “The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not
communion in the blood of Christ? The bread that we break, is it not communion
in the body of Christ?” Matthew 26:28 says, “For this is my blood of the new
covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins.” Exodus
12:13 says, “The blood shall be a sign for you upon the houses where you are,
so that when I see that blood I will pass over you. Thus there shall be no
deadly plague among you when I strike the firstborn in the land of Egypt.” But
Israel's deliverance from slaughter and the killing then carried out was real
on that night when the firstborn of Egypt were struck down.

2. For although we deny communion with the physical substance of Christ's
body, nevertheless we must acknowledge a real but spiritual communion—one
opposed to an imaginary and merely significative communion. Otherwise, Christ
is not given to us as the true and real nourishment of the soul.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 726 | PDF p. 775 | split word joined: Objiciunt]

They object: “But in the sacrament we are participants in Christ's body only
in the manner that we are participants in it in the Word, that is, by faith
alone.” Response. Yet the Arminians will not even say that through the Word we
are participants in Christ's body in any way other than by bare signification
and moral action. 2. Calvin calls it “a deep and wonderful communion” in
*Institutes*, book 4, chapter 17, sections 7–9. And Beza, in his *Questions on
the Sacraments*, says it is an incomprehensible mystery. But although it is
communion through faith, by which we eat Christ's body as though with a
spiritual mouth, it is not through the faith alone that accompanies the
preaching of the Word. It is through faith insofar as faith receives the
gracious exhibition of the things contained in the sacrament, since God truly
exhibits grace at the presence and proper use of the elements and rites.

It is asked whether the Remonstrants are right to deny in their *Apologia*,
chapter 24, folio 256, that the church in the exercise of discipline exerts any
power at all—not only any carnal power of the kind the magistrate exercises,
but even any inward and ecclesiastical power that holds the offender's
conscience bound before God and excludes him from the kingdom of heaven for
the crime of heresy. We deny it.

1. Because the voice of the church proceeding against a heretic with an
unerring key is the voice of Christ, according to the saying, “The one who
rejects you rejects me; the one who hears you hears me.” And, “The sins you
forgive will be forgiven; the sins you retain will be retained.” Therefore, if
Christ has coercive power over someone who errs

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 727 | PDF p. 776]

concerning fundamentals and believes Christ to be an impostor, the church has
the same coercive power under Christ. And if Christ binds principally, the
church also binds ministerially.

2. Because this is the very thing they teach elsewhere, *Apologia*, chapter
24, folio 277: “In any sect you will be able to exercise discipline according
to the dictate of your conscience. What is permitted to us against others will
likewise be permitted to others against us, while each side preserves its
devotion to piety and the obedience owed to magistrates.” Hence the Arians
lawfully excommunicate us according to the rules prescribed by Christ the King.
And the Anabaptist, Socinian, and Arian sects will be permitted to excommunicate
and banish all the other sects from heaven—for that is what their conscience
dictates—while we will be permitted to excommunicate the Socinians and
Anabaptists and banish them from heaven. The banishment from heaven both of
those who believe Christ to be a “mere man” and of those who do not believe
Christ to be a “mere man” but a “God-man” makes the objects of two lawful acts
of discipline incompatible and contradictory, which is altogether absurd.

3. Because they suppose the private dictate of conscience to be a rule binding
the conscience in the exercise of discipline. Therefore, if the Roman Catholic
sect does not resist the dictate of its conscience in the exercise of its
discipline, it has rightly struck us with the thunderbolt of excommunication.
Why, then, do we renounce the pope's infallibility?

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 728 | PDF p. 777 | split word joined: Disciplinæ]

4. They deny or call into doubt the power of ecclesiastical discipline to
constrain consciences, not only with respect to those who err—that is, those
who err by a simple error of the mind—but also with respect to those who sin.
The Apologists say: “We see this done with sinners. How much more equitable is
it for this to be done with those who err? And perhaps it will be safer to
tolerate for a very long time someone who errs, provided he is upright and
pious, than ever to exclude him from our assemblies, if he delights in and
loves them.” But if error is not a fault, as they teach, it deserves pardon and
mercy. Indeed, it is innocence and piety—indeed, as they say in the same place,
“innocence properly so called.” Then one who errs ought not perhaps to be
excluded from our assemblies only at some later point but should never be
excluded, that is, excommunicated. For excommunication is a punishment
instituted by Christ, and the church ought not punish someone who does not sin;
much less can it punish a person for innocence properly so called. Therefore,
if some Jew, from a simple error of the mind, believed Christ to be a false
prophet, he should not be barred from our communion.
