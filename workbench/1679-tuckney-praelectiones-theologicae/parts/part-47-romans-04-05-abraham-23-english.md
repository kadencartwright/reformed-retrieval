Third, other passages speak not of what we possess here but of what we owe;
not of what we attain here but of what we are stirred up and duty-bound to
strive for. Thus Bellarmine himself, speaking there of hope, says that it
ought to be like a sure and steadfast anchor of the soul.

So too the passage he adduces from Matthew 5: “Be perfect, as your heavenly
Father is perfect.” But, good sir, this can be our duty, to which God's
command can summon us, although we fall far short of it. We can also owe
what we are unable to pay. It can therefore be required, or at least given
to us in the commandments, not because this implies that we now possess the
strength to render it, but rather our inability. Through these commands and
exhortations we are made more certain of our complete weakness and implore
an alien righteousness and grace, namely Christ's. Augustine puts it very
well: “Why, then, should this perfection not be commanded to man, although
no one possesses it in this life?” Finally, we ought earnestly to seek and
strive for what we cannot fully reach in this state. Thus Paul wishes that
the Corinthians should do no evil, 2 Corinthians 13:7, and prays for their
perfection in verse 9. We too can ask for the perfection of grace for
ourselves in order to attest the desire of our mind. Thus in Jeremiah 28:6
the prophet said, “Amen! May Jehovah do so,” in response to Hananiah's
prophecy about the Jews' return within two years. He knew well enough that
it would not happen so soon and had himself prophesied otherwise, but he
prayed in this way to declare his benevolent disposition toward his people
and his supreme desire for their welfare. In the same way we ask here for
perfection to show what we pant after and, furthermore, to spur ourselves
to greater efforts. By these, even if we do not attain absolute perfection
here, we may nevertheless make greater progress toward it, as the Apostle
does in Philippians 3:12–13.

Fourth, other passages Bellarmine adduces teach what faith, hope, and love
are in themselves and what they could accomplish if they were perfect, but
not that we attain them so while we live here. Thus, “whoever keeps the
word, in him the love of God is truly perfect,” 1 John 2:5—provided he keeps
it perfectly, which meanwhile he does not do. So Bellarmine says: “Faith,
more precious than gold tested by fire, is our victory by which we conquer
the world and extinguish Satan's flaming darts. Hope is a sure and steadfast
anchor of the soul, and love,” as he adduces from Augustine, “is the truest,
fullest, and most perfect righteousness.”

Perfect love is indeed perfect righteousness, as Bellarmine cites the same
Augustine saying—that is, if love were actually perfect here. Likewise the
Apostle James says of patience that, if it had its complete work, it would
make us perfect and complete, deficient in nothing. But this very point is
in question, indeed denied, and denied by this same Augustine, who “affirms
that the fullest love exists in no one as long as a man lives here.” “Faith
is indeed,” as Chamier rightly says, “a virtue perfect in itself and in its
own kind, but after

[1679 printed p. 261 | IA leaf 269]

the fall it is never perfectly sound here in anyone. The account of
abstract qualities and concrete subjects is very different indeed.”

Fifth, if grace is considered insofar as it exists concretely or in a
subject, its comparative perfection is one thing and its simple and
absolute perfection another. Some passages Bellarmine adduces speak of
comparative perfection, such as, “We speak wisdom among the perfect,” 1
Corinthians 2, and, “Let as many of us as are perfect be of this mind,”
Philippians 3:15—that is, perfect in comparison with the less perfect. As
for absolute perfection, in the immediately preceding words the Apostle
confesses that he himself was not yet perfect or had not yet attained, but
forgot what lay behind and pressed toward what lay ahead. He therefore
wants the perfect to think that they are not perfect. Indeed, our
perfection here consists in humbly acknowledging our imperfection,
according to Augustine: “There is a certain perfection suited to the
measure of this life, and it is counted as belonging to that perfection if
each person knows that he is not yet perfect.”

Thus Abraham's faith, as Bellarmine urges, could be strong when he believed
the promise of offspring and offered his son, insofar as it is compared
with the weak faith of others and even with his own faith when he sometimes
wavered. Yet the faith of Abraham and of everyone else is thin and feeble,
as Melanchthon said and Bellarmine mocks, if it is compared with the
complete perfection that ought to be present—even if it has risen to full
assurance, on which Bellarmine insists. For *full assurance* does not denote
absolute perfection in every respect, but its unshaken foundation placed in
God's omnipotence and truthfulness. Thus some of our theologians affirm that
all catholic faith exists “in full assurance,” even the faith of the man
who said in Mark 9, “Lord, I believe; help my unbelief.” But let this suffice
in passing.

Sixth, some passages Bellarmine adduces must be understood of evangelical,
not legal, perfection, as when some are said to have believed and to have
praised and loved God with the whole heart. This designates the evangelical
perfection of sincerity and completeness of parts, not the legal perfection
of consummation and degrees.

Seventh and finally, some passages he adduces speak of a thing insofar as it
belongs to its own kind. It can be called perfect in this respect without
including the perfection of its manner, in which it can be deficient. Such
is the statement, “No one has greater love than this, that someone lay down
his life for his friend.” This is indeed supreme in its kind, but it does
not follow that all the martyrs who did this with God's help labored under
no deficiency even in their martyrdom as regards the manner of doing it.
That belonged to Christ alone. When he was offered as a whole burnt
offering, he alone was the wholly spotless Lamb; when he laid down his life
for us as a propitiatory sacrifice, he alone performed the greatest act of
all in its kind and did so in a manner perfect in every respect.
