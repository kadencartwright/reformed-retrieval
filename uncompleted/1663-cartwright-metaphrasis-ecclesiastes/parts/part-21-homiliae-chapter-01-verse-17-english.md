VERSE 17. I also applied my mind to knowing wisdom and the knowledge of every
kind of madness and foolishness; I knew that this too was affliction of spirit.

From the study of natural Philosophy, by which what is true and what is false
is discerned, he turned to the study of moral and political Philosophy, or of
the divine Law, by which what is good, what is evil, what is just, and what is
unjust is distinguished. And in order that he might comprehend and perceive
this wisdom more accurately, it seemed good to him to inquire into folly and
foolishness—that is, into vices and corrupt morals—so that, by comparing
opposites with one another, what he sought might become more evident; for
opposites placed side by side are known more readily and clearly. He seems to
have fallen into the study of this wisdom with this purpose, because the study
of natural Philosophy contributed nothing to the correction of morals, either
by correcting corrupt things or by supplying what was lacking. How greatly he
profited in this study, the book of Proverbs is witness, and the book of the
Song of Songs is witness. Yet he assigns no other fruit to this study than to
the former one, namely torment of mind, even though there is as great a
distance between this one and that as there is between heaven and earth.

This opinion of Solomon, however, appears in a certain respect to be insulting
to the Word of God, and therefore to God himself. But it must be understood
that Solomon is speaking here about that knowledge of the divine Law which is
acquired by human powers, such as the study of the Law and the commandments.
Or, if it includes the Gospel and the gracious promise, which is understood
only through the teaching of the Holy Spirit, it nevertheless denotes that
knowledge of the gracious promise which is separated from regeneration. This
is so far from rendering us blessed that, without the true fear of God, it
summons a more severe judgment upon us. Therefore we must look carefully and
anxiously all around us, and the eyes of our mind must be directed in every
direction, so that under the preaching of the Word we may advance in godliness
and, for that purpose, examine ourselves, purge our minds of corrupt
affections, and implore God's aid for a fruitful hearing of the Word. But if
the study of Sacred Scripture does not bless a man without the true fear of
God, what will become of those who despise Sacred Scripture in favor of
letters, though they be liberal—that is, who court handmaids and attendants
before their Mistress? They read through the lives written by Plutarch, a
despiser of Christ, while meanwhile they
