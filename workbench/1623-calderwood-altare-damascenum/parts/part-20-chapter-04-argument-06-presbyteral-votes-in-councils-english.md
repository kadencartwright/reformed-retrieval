From presbyters’ votes in provincial councils we can reasonably infer, I
think, the power those same men possessed in presbyteries—namely, that in
them they were not only counselors but also judges. *In all Provincial and
National Synods,* Field says, *presbyters cast votes and subscribed in the
same manner as bishops, whether they had assembled to frame canons of
discipline, to hear cases, or to decide controversies in doctrine.* He proves
that they attended provincial councils from canon 7 of the Council of
Auxerre, canon 13 of Tarragona, Toledo q. c. 3, Toledo I, and Gregory, book 4,
epistle c. 88; and that they cast a deciding vote from the subscriptions to
the Council of Elvira and the synod under Gregory. Although certain papists
contend that votes in councils belong to bishops alone, Bilson does not say
that examples teaching the contrary are lacking from the furthest reaches of
antiquity. *Sixty bishops and several presbyters and deacons attended the
Roman synod convened by Cornelius against Novatus in the year of Christ 225,*
as Eusebius relates. *Not only a bishop but presbyters and deacons wrote from
the Synod of Antioch which deposed Paul of Samosata from the episcopate in the
year of the Lord 270, as is plain from their own letter. At the Council of
Elvira, near the time of the First Council of Nicaea, thirty-six presbyters
are recorded in addition to the bishops. At the Second Council of Arles,
about the same time, twelve presbyters subscribed together with deacons and
other clerics. The same thing can be seen in the councils held at Rome under
Hilary and under Gregory, where thirty-four presbyters subscribed after
twenty-two bishops, and in the first council under Symmachus, where
sixty-seven presbyters subscribed after seventy-two bishops. The same
occurred in the third, fifth, and sixth councils under that Symmachus. To
these may be added the council under Felix of Rome, with forty-three bishops
and seventy-four presbyters.* To confirm his position he also adduces canon 7
of the Council of Auxerre, canon 13 of Tarragona, and Toledo IV. In provincial
synods they framed canons and ecclesiastical laws. Could those who were able
to frame provincial laws not, of course, frame statutes in presbyteries? And
is it credible that those who could establish laws in provincial and
national councils could not, together with the bishops in consistories,
exercise censures against transgressors of those same laws? *This too can be
added,* says Dounamus, *to the aforesaid authority of ministers: in framing
ecclesiastical laws they themselves had a vote, either in their own persons,
if they were sent to the synod, or through those whom they themselves had
elected.* Likewise,

*The greatest ecclesiastical authority is exercised in synods, while the
greatest authority of synods consists in framing ecclesiastical laws.* If the
greatest authority is granted to them in synods, is a lesser authority to be
denied them in presbyteries? Moreover, in synods they not only decided
controversies in doctrine and condemned heretics, but also deposed bishops,
presbyters, and deacons by synodical sentence. Field says that provincial
synods dealt with the ordinations and depositions of bishops and with
lawsuits and cases brought by appeal from particular churches to provincial
councils. Those who exercised jurisdiction over bishops themselves in synods
and could judge cases brought by appeal must be thought to have judged in
presbyteries as well. Bishops and presbyters cast out the Samosatene in the
Council of Antioch. In the Council of Sinuessa presbyters together with
bishops condemned Marcellinus.

It is therefore plain from what has been said that the ancient bishops were
only presidents in the ecclesiastical senate and that the presbyters were
judges together with the bishop. *And therefore according to the truth and
Jerome’s intention a bishop is nothing other than an archpresbyter,* as
Marsilius of Padua says. *And what does a bishop do, ordination excepted, that
a presbyter does not do?* Jerome said. According to their own interpretation,
Chrysostom said the same thing, namely *that bishops would have been superior
to presbyters by ordination alone.* Jurisdiction was therefore common to
both, as Bilson saw when he wrote: *The things peculiar to bishops and not to
be entrusted to presbyters are of this kind: the privilege of succession and
the prerogative of ordaining.* And in his preface he says that ordination is
almost the sole distinction between a bishop and a presbyter. But we have
discussed ordination above and have proved that not even in that matter is
there any difference by divine law between bishop and presbyter.
