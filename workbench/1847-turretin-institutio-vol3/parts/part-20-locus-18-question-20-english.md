QUESTION XX.

Was the primacy which prevails in the Roman church established from the
beginning? Or was it introduced gradually and by stages over the course of the
centuries? We deny the former and affirm the latter.

I. Since it is evident from the foregoing that Christ did not institute any
monarchical government in the church, but only ministry, and that Peter never
usurped such government, the question is when and how such a primacy was
introduced into the church, and by what stages it raised itself to the height
at which it is now seen.

[Marginalia: The various stages of the papacy.]

II. Here it must be observed above all that the supreme power of the Roman
hierarchy—or rather, its tyranny and the primacy of the pope—was not
established all at once, but gradually and successively over the course of
many years and even centuries, as Satan worked this mystery of iniquity little
by little and imperceptibly. Although it is difficult to assign precisely the
points in time at which this primacy was either founded or confirmed, we can
nevertheless divide it into four stages according to its various increases:
(1) its beginning; (2) its increase;

[1847 vol. III printed p. 161 | IA leaf 195 | PDF p. 195]

(3) its flourishing and settled age; and (4) its decline and decrease. Its
beginning or birth is referred to the fourth century, or the time of the
Council of Nicaea, when it began to aspire to dominion in church government.
Its increase ran from the fourth century to the end of the sixth and beginning
of the seventh, when the pope first arrogated to himself the title of
ecumenical bishop, obtained dominion in spiritual matters by the grant of
Phocas, and departed in many ways from the former simplicity in doctrine and
rites; and from the sixth century to the tenth, from Boniface III to Benedict
IX and Gregory VII, who usurped both temporal and spiritual monarchy. Its
flourishing ran from the tenth century to the beginning of the sixteenth,
during which interval it wickedly exercised both kinds of dominion after the
purer doctrine had been driven out—from Gregory VII, or the year of Christ
1073, to Leo X, or the year 1517. Then it entered its decline, began to
diminish, and gradually lost strength through the Reformation, until it is
consumed by the Spirit of Christ’s mouth and destroyed at his glorious coming.

III. There is no doubt that in the time of the apostles the foundations of
this mystery of iniquity began to be laid, as the devil by his arts secretly
and craftily began to weave this web of his. He did this both through the
various disputes about primacy that arose among the apostles themselves and
through the dissensions born in the churches, in which Christians, divided
into parties, used the apostles’ names to foster their factions among
themselves, as if the apostles wished to lead households after themselves
rather than seek the glory of Christ the Lord alone. Paul speaks of these
things in 1 Corinthians 1:12 and 3:4: “When one says, I am of Paul, and
another, I am of Cephas, and so forth, are you not carnal?” He did this also
through the ambition of Diotrephes, of whom John says in verse 9 of his third
epistle that he “loves to have the primacy,” or aspires to it. Nevertheless,
it is certain that in those first centuries the primacy of which we speak was
not known even by name and had no place in the churches. They were so piously
governed by the bishops or pastors appointed by the apostles and apostolic men
that no church usurped rule or dominion over another, but each pastor cared
for his own flock. Before the destruction of the city, the church of Jerusalem
indeed held the first place among the Christian churches because Christ and
the apostles had their seat there and because from it the gospel was to go
forth into the whole world (Isa. 2:2). But after the death of the apostles and
the destruction of the city, the dignity of cities gave preeminence to their
churches, and the political order prevailing there gave rise to ecclesiastical
order and authority. Thus Jerusalem no longer held the first rank as the
mother of all the churches. But because the Romans had made Caesarea the
metropolis of Judea, the bishop of Caesarea became metropolitan of Judea. And
just as there were then in the Roman Empire three principal cities on which
others depended with respect to temporal jurisdiction—namely, Antioch,
Alexandria, and Rome—so the bishops of those three churches obtained authority
over the others subject to their jurisdiction, yet in such a way that there
was still no trace of ecumenical power.

IV. It must indeed be acknowledged that, just as all the best and most
wholesome human institutions customarily degenerate over time, so this also
happened here. The honor conferred upon the bishops of those cities gradually
altered their dispositions so that they began to arrogate somewhat more to
themselves and exalt themselves above others. It is evident that this happened
especially at Rome, the imperial city and mistress of the world. Hence
Socrates, in book 7, chapter 11 of his *History*, says that the Roman bishops
gradually degenerated so far that they “advanced beyond the bounds of the
priesthood to dominion.” Basil too, in Epistle 10, not obscurely strikes at
“that western arrogance,” as Baronius admits under the year 372, section 32:
“I hate the arrogance of that church.” Victor and Stephen furnish examples of
this ambition. Victor did so when, because of a slight disagreement about the
time for celebrating Easter, he rashly condemned and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 162 | IA leaf 196 | PDF p. 196]

excommunicated Polycrates and the Asian churches in A.D. 195. Stephen did so
when he received the disobedient Basilides, bishop of Astorga, and Martial,
bishop of Merida, both Spaniards, back into favor, rebuked the Spanish
churches, and sought a right of appeal to the Roman church in the year 250,
although Cyprian strongly protested in Epistle 18. Whatever the attempts of
the Roman bishops to obtain primacy over the other churches may have been, it
is certain that they still possessed no right or power over the others. Aeneas
Sylvius, later Pius II, could not conceal this when he said in Epistle 301 to
Martin and Major that “before the Council of Nicaea each lived for himself,
and little regard was paid to the Roman church.” I know that the papists
support their primacy from Canon 6 of the Council of Nicaea, by which they
allege that the first place among the patriarchs is assigned to the Roman
bishop, but the canon itself will make clear that no such thing can be
gathered from it.

V. The words of the canon are: “Let the ancient customs prevail: those in
Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis, so that the bishop of Alexandria has authority
over all of them, since the same is customary for the bishop in Rome; and
likewise with respect to Antioch and in the other provinces, let the
prerogatives of the churches be preserved.” That is, “Let the ancient customs
observed in Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis be retained, namely, that the bishop
of Alexandria has authority over them all, since the bishop of Rome has a
similar custom; let the same be established concerning Antioch, and let the
prerogatives of the churches be preserved in the other prefectures.” This
canon was made on account of Meletius, bishop of Lycopolis in the Thebaid of
Egypt, who wished to withdraw from the bishop of Alexandria so that he might
confer orders without the latter’s consent. But it confirms the custom already
received in the churches that the dignity of bishops followed the dignity of
the city whose bishops they were. Thus they were diocesan bishops, having care
of the city itself and of the region particularly subject to that city.
Therefore, after several provinces in the same prefecture had been converted
to the faith, the churches in most places thought that the pattern of the
civil polity should be imitated to this extent: provinces contained in the
same prefecture were in some way subjected to the bishop of the city that was
the seat of the praetorian prefect. Hence it happened that the provinces
subject to the Augustal prefect, whose seat was at Alexandria, were subjected
to the bishop of Alexandria; those subject to the prefect of the East, whose
seat was at Antioch, to the bishop of Antioch; and those subject to the
praetorian prefect of the city of Rome, to the bishop of Rome. The Nicene
Fathers therefore judged that those ancient customs should be retained and
that Meletius accordingly ought to be subject to the bishop of Alexandria.

VI. From this it is clear, first, that no prerogative over the other
patriarchs was conferred upon the Roman bishop. Rather, that patriarchal power
extended only over the provinces subject to the prefecture of the city, which
reached to the hundredth milestone from the city—that is, over the suburbican
churches, as Rufinus explains. These were not the churches of the entire Roman
Empire, as Perron supposes, for then they could not be distinguished from the
churches of Egypt and could only with the greatest absurdity be called
suburbican. They were only those under the jurisdiction of the praetorian
prefect, to whom, as is observed in chapter 4 of the *Commentary on the Notice
of the Empire*, “Augustus had granted all the power of the empire, not only
within the city but also outside it, within the hundredth milestone from the
city.” Hence in law 12 of the Theodosian Code “Tuscia and Picenum are called
suburbican regions,” and in the *Notice of the Empire* they are often
mentioned under the name “suburban provinces,” because the suburbs of Rome
extended that far. Perron therefore had no reason to censure Rufinus so
severely for mentioning the suburbican churches in his paraphrastic
exposition of the Nicene canon, since, as Sirmond observed, that very word is
found in the canon in an ancient copy in the Vatican Library. Second, this
prerogative, whatever its nature, is said to be founded not upon divine law
but upon mere custom. But if it rests upon divine law, as

[1847 vol. III printed p. 163 | IA leaf 197 | PDF p. 197]

the Romanists today allege, why did the Nicene Fathers mention only custom
and not divine law? Why are the Alexandrian and Antiochene sees made equal to
the Roman, so that one had no right over another, if Christ conferred upon
this see the ecumenical episcopate? These things teach more clearly than the
noonday light that the mind of the Council of Nicaea was far different and
that it acknowledged no primacy of the Roman bishop over the bishops of
Alexandria and Antioch. With this in view, Cusanus says in book 8, chapter 12,
*On Catholic Concord*: “We see how much the Roman pontiff has acquired beyond
the ancient observances through usage and the custom of submissive obedience.”

VII. The papists vainly try to corrupt this canon so as to make it their own
and draw it over to the support of their cause. 1. They do this at its
beginning, when they maintain that the beginning of the canon was, “The Roman
church has always held the primacy.” They gather this from Action 16 of the
Council of Chalcedon, where Paschasinus, vicar of the apostolic see, reports
it in this way: “Since the Roman church has always held the primacy, let Egypt
also retain it, and so forth.” I reply: (1) In the same council Constantine
the secretary reads it differently from the Codex of Canons given by Aetius,
archdeacon of the church of Constantinople. (2) No codices acknowledge this
beginning: neither Rufinus, Balsamon, Gratian’s *Decree*, Gelasius of Cyzicus,
nor the canons published at Paris in 1544; neither the Arabic edition in
Turrianus nor the copies formerly sent to the Africans from Constantinople
and Alexandria. Therefore, this recitation made by the Roman legate
“Palchasinus” is not from any authentic copy, but from his own paper or
memorandum. (3) The words in which it is framed—“Since the Roman church has
always held the primacy”—make clear that this beginning is not the text of the
canon but a copyist’s annotation or marginal title. Hence Dionysius Exiguus,
to give it a more suitable sense, removed the particle “since,” which marks
such marginal titles, inserted the word “but” into the text, and said, “The
Roman church has always held the primacy, but let the custom continue.” (4)
Even if this sentence were retained, nevertheless the papists would gain
nothing from it, because to possess primacy is one thing and to possess a
monarchy and the ecumenical pontificate is another. It is one thing for the
Roman church and its metropolitan bishop to possess primacy in his own
province—that is, for him to be first among the bishops of his province,
which is the proper sense of the canon because it dealt with the power that
belonged to metropolitans in their own provinces—or to have the primacy of
honor and order among the metropolitans themselves. In this respect also it
is evident that the Roman bishop possessed primacy, as was said, because of
the city’s dignity. But it is another thing to possess a primacy of authority
and jurisdiction over the metropolitans themselves, so that they had to be
subject to him and depend upon his commands. The papists allege this, and we
deny it. Indeed, not a few papists acknowledge that by this canon the bishops
of Alexandria and Antioch were made equal to the Roman, and that as much was
conferred upon them over their dioceses as the Roman possessed over the
suburbican churches. Foremost among them is the most learned Peter de Marca,
in book 1, chapter 3, *On the Concord of the Priesthood and the Empire*, where
he adopts the opinion of Zonaras and Balsamon concerning this canon.

VIII. 2. With respect to the end of the canon, they wrongly interpret its
words, “since the same is customary for the bishop in Rome,” or, “because the
Roman bishop has a similar custom.” Bellarmine, in book 2, chapter 13, wishes
the sense of these words to be: “Because the Roman bishop has customarily
acted in this way,” that is, “because before any definition of the councils
the Roman bishop customarily permitted the bishop of Alexandria to govern
Egypt, Libya, and Pentapolis, or customarily governed those provinces through
the bishop of Alexandria.” (1) But who could bear this hitherto unheard-of
gloss? It plainly conflicts with the very words of the canon. They give the
Roman bishop no authority over the bishop of Alexandria, but only take an
example from him for what

[1847 vol. III printed p. 164 | IA leaf 198 | PDF p. 198]

belonged to the Alexandrian bishop: just as the Roman bishop customarily
governed the churches subject to his jurisdiction, so the Alexandrian bishop
ought likewise to conform himself to that example—not by being subject to the
Roman, but by governing the churches subject to his own jurisdiction after
the Roman’s example. (2) This is confirmed from ecclesiastical history, in
which the bishop of Alexandria nowhere appears to have acted as the Roman’s
vicar. Rather, he governed his own churches or parishes by his own right, just
as the Roman did his, so that both were colleagues and used the same title of
patriarch. Indeed, it was decreed that the bishop of Constantinople should be
“equally great with the Roman in ecclesiastical affairs.” (3) The synod does
not ascribe this primacy of the bishop of Alexandria over his provinces to the
authority of the Roman bishop, but to ancient custom: “Let the ancient customs
prevail.” The sense is that these provinces had long been customarily
administered by the bishop of Alexandria, just as others were by the Roman,
and the synod judged and decreed that this custom should also be retained in
the future. From these things the absurdity of Bellarmine’s gloss is clear,
and no less that of Perron, who wishes these words to be added at the end of
the canon: “Since the Roman bishop in like manner governs all the churches of
the world.” This is contrary to the testimony of all the codices and to the
sense of the words themselves. As was just said, their purpose is not that the
authority of the bishop of Alexandria should be founded upon the Roman
bishop’s power, but that custom should be compared with custom and the one
should follow the other’s example.

IX. If it is further asked why the Roman bishop held the first place among
the patriarchs, various reasons can be given. (1) The dignity of the city.
Since Rome was the capital of the empire and the “reigning city,” it seemed
fitting that it should also have the primacy of order, not only among the
bishops of that prefecture but also among the patriarchs. Hence “the first
places” and “the prerogatives” mentioned by the ancients were assigned to
him. That this prerogative of honor is derived from the dignity of the cities
themselves is evident from various considerations. First, although the bishop
of the church of Jerusalem, which had been the mother of all the other
churches and for that reason seemed bound to occupy the first rank, is
nevertheless placed after the bishop of Caesarea in Canon 7 of Nicaea because
Caesarea was then the metropolis of Palestine. Second, Canon 17 of the Council
of Chalcedon decrees: “Let the order of the ecclesiastical parishes follow the
civil and political arrangements.” Thus the Roman bishop obtained the
prerogatives, or the primacy of order, not because he was Peter’s successor,
but because he was bishop of that city which held rule over the world, as the
Council of Chalcedon expressly says, “because that city reigned.” The same
could easily be shown concerning the others. Yet the dignity which the bishops
derived from the dignity of their cities gave them no power or rule either
over other patriarchs or over the bishops of that prefecture, and it did not
extend beyond a primacy of order and honor. Perron, in book 1, chapter 30 of
his *Reply*, wishes to maintain that Peter instituted this already in his own
time, so that he may infer from it that this authority rested on divine law
and not merely on human custom. This is a pure fiction, of which not a trace
or vestige exists in Scripture or in any monuments of the Fathers, and it is
rejected with the same ease with which it is proposed. Indeed, if this was
Peter’s design, why did he wish no account to be taken either of the church of
Jerusalem, which had been the mother of all, or of the church of Caesarea,
which he had so solemnly founded, so that they might also obtain their place
among the patriarchal churches? (2) An opinion that had gained strength among
many—that the Roman church was founded by Peter’s ministry—contributed much
to winning authority for it. Hence in the West it was honorably called the
apostolic see. (3) The division of the eastern churches. Since they were
troubled by many disagreements of opinion while the Roman church was more
peaceful, it happened that pious and holy bishops driven from their sees
resorted there as to a refuge and harbor.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 165 | IA leaf 199 | PDF p. 199]

Athanasius did so when driven from his see by the violence of the Arians, so
that by the authority of the Roman see he might both restrain the fury of his
enemies to some degree and strengthen the pious who were suffering. He was
honorably received by Julius, then bishop, and obtained the support of the
westerners in the defense of his cause. Later, evil and wicked men also
contributed not a little to this, for in order to escape lawful judgments they
fled to this refuge. If a presbyter had been condemned by his bishop, or a
bishop by the synod of his province, he immediately appealed to Rome. The
Roman bishops received such appeals more eagerly than was proper, seizing
every opportunity to enlarge their power. This was seen in the case of
Eutyches against Flavian and is known to have happened often in Africa, which
gave the African bishops occasion to condemn overseas appeals so severely
under penalty of excommunication.

X. In the course of time, two other primary sees were established in addition
to those three: Constantinople and Jerusalem. The Fathers of the First Council
of Constantinople assigned Constantinople the second place because it had
become the imperial seat and was like a new Rome. Thus they speak in Canon 3:
“Let the bishop of Constantinople have the prerogatives of honor after the
bishop of Rome, because it is New Rome.” The Council of Chalcedon confirms
this in Action 16 when it confers “equal prerogatives of honor” upon the
bishop of Constantinople and the Roman. “The Fathers gave the prerogative of
honor to the see of elder Rome, as was reasonable, because that city was queen
of the world; and aiming at the same end, the one hundred and fifty bishops
most dear to God at the First Council of Constantinople conferred equal
prerogatives of honor upon the most holy throne of New Rome.”

XI. Yet this equality, which prevailed among all the patriarchs and especially
between the Roman and the bishop of Constantinople on account of the dignity
of the two cities, underwent various changes. First, on the part of John,
bishop of Constantinople, who, raised up by ambition, aspired to the title of
ecumenical or universal bishop and obtained it from Emperor Maurice. Gregory
the Great, bishop of Rome, fervently opposed him, condemning this title as
wicked and antichristian. “I confidently say,” he writes in Epistle 30, “that
whoever calls himself universal bishop or desires to be called so is in his
exaltation the forerunner of Antichrist, because in his pride he sets himself
above the others.” He confirms this at greater length in Epistles 36, 38, and
39. From this it is invincibly established that at that time the Roman bishop
had not yet obtained spiritual monarchy or the ecumenical episcopate.
Perron’s evasion in book 1, chapter 34 is of no force: “Gregory condemned the
title of ecumenical bishop only according to its literal and grammatical
sense, insofar as it denotes the only bishop, but not according to its
metaphorical sense, by which it indicates one placed over the others.” For
Gregory condemns this title in no other sense than that in which John used it.
But John did not take it in the former sense, by which he would have abolished
the authority of all the eastern bishops—though they themselves did not
complain of that, and the reality itself showed otherwise—but only in the
latter, which is the proper meaning of the title. For the dignity of
ecumenical bishop does not aim to abolish the power of the other bishops, but
only to make it dependent upon the ecumenical bishop and subject to him.

XII. This ambitious title, which Gregory had so greatly abhorred, his successor
Boniface III not only did not reject, but sought with great zeal. Finally,
though with difficulty after many disputes, he obtained from Phocas, the
murderer of Maurice, who needed his favor in Italy to secure his own empire,
that Rome, his see, should be regarded as the head of all the churches and he
himself as the ecumenical pontiff. This occurred at the beginning of the
seventh century. From that point a remarkable change was made in the
government of the church. For whereas the five patriarchs had formerly

[1847 vol. III printed p. 166 | IA leaf 200 | PDF p. 200]

governed the churches by equal right and equal power, the Roman pontiff now
obtained precedence over the rest. Before this, all the more eminent bishops
were called popes; but from this time the name “pope,” as a term of supreme
and universal power, was taken from the other bishops and patriarchs and
reserved for the Roman pontiff as his proper and peculiar title. Before this,
there was one catholic church under one and the only head, Christ. But then it
began to be one under one head, the pope, and those who were not subject to
him and did not acknowledge him as the supreme head were regarded as
schismatics. Before this, there was a vast difference between the church of
the city and of the world and between the bishops of the city and the world.
Hence when the custom of the Roman church was raised against Jerome in the
letter to Evagrius, he said, “Why do you bring forward to me the custom of one
city? If authority is sought, the world is greater than a city.” From that
time the church, the authority, and the bishop of the city and the world began
to be the same, for the pope claimed both for himself. Before this, the
election of any bishop made by the clergy and people and confirmed by the
metropolitan was held valid without consulting the Roman pontiff. But at a
synod held at Rome Boniface decreed that the election of a bishop made by the
clergy and people and approved by the prince of the city would be valid only
if the Roman pontiff interposed his authority, in these words: “We will and
command, and so forth,” as Platina reports in his life of Boniface III.

XIII. The papists vainly reply here: (1) “Phocas did not give the pope the
title of universal, but called him head of the churches. Nor was Boniface’s
purpose that the title of ecumenical or universal patriarch be conferred upon
himself, but that it not be conferred upon the bishop of Constantinople.” For
this is to dispute wrongly about words when the thing itself is established.
It is evident that the dispute concerned primacy. The bishop of Constantinople
had usurped it, Maurice had granted it to him, and he had commanded the Roman
to yield to him. Boniface sought the same, and Phocas granted it. Nor does the
statement that Rome is called the head of the churches mean anything other
than that “all the churches of the world should obey the Roman church and the
Roman pontiff,” as Blondus says in decade 1, book 9. This is in reality to be
the universal bishop. Then the point at issue is not the simple title
“ecumenical,” which the patriarch of Constantinople retained, but the primacy
of power which the Roman pontiff arrogated to himself as peculiar to him. (2)
“The apostolic see obtained no new right from Phocas which all his
predecessors did not possess before, but only that the honor of this primacy,
which the bishop of Constantinople had usurped, should be reserved for the
Roman bishop alone.” But the reality itself cries out that something new was
obtained from Phocas, who is not said to have restored or preserved the
primacy, but to have given it. And Gregory’s complaint about John’s pride
sufficiently shows that no such thing had previously had a place in the Roman
church.

XIV. Thus in Boniface III the pope obtained ecclesiastical primacy or
spiritual monarchy, which the pontiffs thereafter strove in every way to
confirm and increase. But the final stage that was to constitute its
flourishing was still lacking, namely temporal monarchy. This was afterwards
added in the eleventh century through Gregory VII, called Hildebrand, an
Etruscan by nation and a Cluniac monk by profession. In the year 1073, he
despised the emperor’s authority and scorned the vote of the clergy and
people, forcibly removed those who seemed likely to hinder his wicked
attempts, and—as one highly skilled in pontifical arts and already accustomed
to them under four pontiffs—seized the papacy and held it with the greatest
pride and tyranny. Cardinal Benno and not a few papal writers testify that he
was imbued with diabolical arts by his predecessors Benedict IX and Gregory
VI, and was “a worse disciple of the worst masters.” He had occupied the
papacy by evil arts, retained it by worse, and advanced it to the summit of
tyranny, trampling even the imperial majesty underfoot. For although Emperor
Henry convened a council at Worms and caused him to be stripped of the papacy
and deposed, with a decree

[1847 vol. III printed p. 167 | IA leaf 201 | PDF p. 201]

made there which vividly describes his most grievous crimes, as may be read
in book 5 of Aventinus’s *Annals of Bavaria*, the same Aventinus testifies
there that not only the bishops of Germany and Gaul but also those of Italy,
in an assembly held at Pavia, subscribed to that decree and swore “that they
would never receive as supreme pontiff one who bore that name.” Nevertheless,
he proceeded to such a pitch of audacity that, at a Roman council assembled
in 1076, he did not shrink from excommunicating the emperor himself, stripping
him of the kingdoms of Germany and Italy, and releasing princes and peoples
from the oath of fidelity they had sworn to him. And thus he finally obtained
the monarchy of both powers, spiritual and temporal, which the popes had long
sought.

XV. To this pertain the *Dictates of the Pope* decreed by him, in which he
attributes unlimited and almost infinite power over all things to the pope.
Among others, they include these: “that the Roman pontiff alone can use the
imperial insignia; that all princes are to kiss only the pope’s feet; that his
name alone is to be recited in the churches; that he may depose emperors; that
his judgment ought to be revised by no one, while he alone may revise the
judgments of all; that he may release the subjects of wicked men from their
allegiance; and that the Roman pontiff alone is rightly called universal, and
so forth.” Thereafter Hildebrand’s successors, following in his footsteps,
placed the emperors under the yoke and regarded them as vassals. Aventinus
says most truly in the same book 5: “Hildebrand, who is also Gregory VII, was
the first to found the pontifical empire, which his successors continually
maintained for four hundred and fifty years against the will of the world and
the emperors. They brought both the lower and the higher realms into slavery
and placed them under the yoke, and terrified everyone with their thunderbolt,
and so forth. Now the Roman emperor is nothing more; it is merely a title
without body or appearance.” And Onuphrius says in his *Life of Gregory VII*:
“To him the Roman church must ascribe the fact that it is set over kings,
emperors, and all Christian princes. From him comes that greatest and most
excellent state in which the Roman church is the mistress of all Christians,
whereas before, like a lowly maidservant, it was oppressed not only by the
emperors but by any prince supported by the forces of the Roman Empire.” From
him, Onuphrius adds, flowed the right of that greatest Roman pontiff—dreaded
and venerated in every age—and of his almost infinite power. Previously they
were subject to the emperors; he was the first of all who dared not merely to
excommunicate Caesar himself, but even to deprive him of kingdom and empire.

XVI. Just as the papacy attained its flourishing under Gregory VII through
spiritual and temporal monarchy, so it was confirmed more and more in the
same by the wicked efforts of the pontiffs who succeeded one another, down to
Boniface VIII at the beginning of the fourteenth century. He abused the
simplicity of Pope Celestine through fraud, intruded himself into the see, and
acted there violently and tyrannically. Hence after his death the common
saying about him was, “He entered like a fox, reigned like a lion, and died
like a dog.” He exercised supreme power not only against Emperor Albert, whom
he alleged could not rule because he had been elected without consulting him,
but also against Philip the Fair, king of France. Because Philip refused to
do what he commanded, Boniface placed him under interdict, released his
subjects from their oath of fidelity, denied every pontifical indulgence,
grant, and favor to those who refused obedience, and displayed various signs
of a powerless rage. King Philip resisted him manfully, to such an extent
that Boniface was captured and mocked in prison and died from despair and
rage. Historians relate this at length: Paulus Aemilius in *Philip the Fair*;
Blondus, decade 2, book 9; Nauclerus, volume 2, generation 44; Bochellus,
book 2, chapter 32 of the *Decrees of the Gallican Church*; and others.

XVII. Moreover, it cannot be said how greatly the most cunning fictions
offered to the wretched credulity of men advanced and strengthened both the
temporal and the spiritual power of the pontiffs. For the confirmation of

XVIII. Nevertheless, the Roman pontiffs did not cease to follow the same path
and to establish and advance their monarchy at every opportunity, both in
spiritual and temporal matters, despite the courageous opposition of various
emperors and princes and the protests of faithful witnesses of the truth. At
length, when God brought about the great work of the Reformation, its decline
or decrease followed at the beginning of the sixteenth century under Leo X.
In his time that tyrannical and antichristian power suffered a most grievous
blow through the withdrawal of several kingdoms, principalities, and
commonwealths from the Roman church. The Council of Trent opposed it in vain.
Moved by the weightiest reasons, the Protestants rightly rejected that council
as “a petty council of profane men and an empty mockery of Christianity,”
which strengthened and increased papal errors and tyranny rather than
correcting and reforming them. By it, as by a kind of partition and
intervening wall, they separated themselves from the papacy, remain separate,
and will remain so forever, until Rome reforms its impious errors and
idolatries and its antichristian tyranny. Furthermore, whoever wishes to know
more deeply the various stages of the papacy’s increase and decrease should
consult Plessis in *The Mystery of Iniquity*; Flacius Illyricus in *The
Catalog of Witnesses to the Truth*; Du Moulin in *The Novelty of Popery*;
Salmasius in his most learned work *On the Primacy of the Pope*; and the
recently published *History of the Papacy* by the celebrated Heidegger, the
most worthy theologian of Zurich and our most intimate associate. In it that
excellent man treats this whole argument, as he does all others, with the
greatest accuracy and solidity over the intervals of seven periods.

XIX. Before concluding, I wish to append here the judgment concerning the
primacy of the pope by Francesco Guicciardini, a Florentine patrician and
privy counselor to Leo X. It was omitted in bad faith from various copies but
restored from the Florentine autograph and most recently published by the
celebrated Heidegger, as it stood in the Italian edition of 1636. There, after
describing the various stages and arts by which the Roman pontiffs were raised
from the deepest humility to that supreme height of majesty and power which
they now hold tyrannically, the author adds near the end of book 4: “Raised by
these foundations and methods to earthly power, they gradually forgot the
salvation of souls and the divine commandments and turned their every thought
to worldly empires. They began to abuse divine authority for nothing other
than a kind of weapon and instrument for transitory things, and to appear as
princes of nations rather than pontiffs of sacred things. Their concerns and
business were no longer holiness of life, the increase of religion, or love
toward God and men, but armies and wars against Christians, handling sacred
things with thought and hands spattered with blood, an immense desire for
money, new laws, new arts, and new snares to gather money from every quarter.
To this end they most audaciously brandished heavenly weapons and most
shamelessly practiced the sale of profane

out upon their whole court, from which came arrogance, luxury, the basest
morals, lusts, and unspeakable pleasures. There was no concern for a successor

destructive desire to raise sons, nephews, and likewise others connected with

If the author could say these things about the primacy of his own time, what
would he not say about that of today, which daily strives to strengthen and
increase the same tyranny by the same and even worse arts? Those who know
this mystery of iniquity more deeply cannot conceal this and, compelled by the
power of truth, are forced to confess it more than once.
