PROPOSITION SIXTEEN.

ARMINIUS. Third, because the abandonment of a creature in its own nature—I mean a creature upon
whom a law performable by grace alone has been imposed—is a cause of sin through the removal or
non-bestowal of that which alone can prevent sin. This, moreover, is grace. In this respect this
opinion falls into that former opinion which ascribes the ordering of sin to the decree, whence sin
necessarily comes to exist.

JUNIUS. The proposition cannot truly be predicated of a human being in integrity. For the law was
performable by Adam in integrity not by grace alone but by nature and grace together, since his
nature was rightly ordered to grace. Yet he failed in a natural matter and one performable by
nature, which did not belong to the common law under discussion here, but to a singular law having
regard to nature alone and performable by it alone, as God himself had foretold Adam, as we showed
earlier in Proposition 14 [printed: *ut propositione*].

But as regards us, as we now are, it can very well be declared that the law can be performed by
grace alone. Indeed, it cannot even be performed by us; rather, having been performed by Christ, it
is imputed through grace and apprehended by faith.

2. Further, it is also false that the abandonment of a creature in its own nature is a cause of sin
through the removal or non-bestowal of a preventive, if you understand this universally. For then
it is a partial cause of sin when the preventive is removed or not removed [printed: *removetur aut
non removetur*], if it is owed; but if it is not owed, it cannot justly be called a partial cause of
sin. If it ought to act, it is at fault; if it ought not, it is not at fault for the sin if it is
absent. Nature itself teaches this, and Clement of Alexandria expounds it most suitably in two
places in the *Stromata*. But in the law there is both something natural, which Adam could perform
by nature, and something ordered to grace, for which nature alone could not suffice. Therefore,
although Adam had sinned against the natural law, if he had nevertheless sinned in a natural matter
(where grace was not owed), his will alone would have been at fault, not the deprivation of grace,
as it is evident happened in that singular law given to Adam. The conclusion is therefore empty.

Concerning the ordering of sin and God’s decree, we said in Proposition Six what an ordering
properly understood is. That sin necessarily comes to exist from it is an inconsequent argument,
since God’s ordering does nothing that is not owed, whereas the one who commits sin does; and it
omits nothing that is owed, but provides and accomplishes all things most wisely. But we have said
enough about this whole matter in Proposition Six [printed: *in propositione sex in satis*].

ARMINIUS. When I say “grace,” I do not exclude nature, for the former presupposes the latter. The
phrase “performable by grace alone” amounts to the same as if I had said “not performable without
grace,” with the word “alone” operating not to exclude nature but to include grace necessarily.
But given those antecedents—a law given to a human being which he cannot perform without grace,
and grace not given to that human being—the conclusion follows that the cause of sin is not the
human being but the one who imposed such a law and did not bestow the faculty of performing it. Or,
to speak more correctly, the transgression of that law then cannot be called sin, since that law is
unjust, the law of a God as though reaping where he did not sow—which is far [printed: *ab est*]
from the good and just God—and its transgression is necessary, not voluntary, because of an
inability not to transgress.
Therefore it is altogether true: he who does not give that without which sin cannot be avoided, or
removes that without which the law cannot be performed, is truly the author of sin—or rather the
cause why that law is not observed, a non-observance which then cannot have the character of sin.

2. That condition, “if the preventive is owed,” is added here in vain. For God necessarily owes a
human being the

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 68 | BSB canvas 80]

power to perform that law which he imposes upon him, unless perhaps the human being has deprived
himself of the power by his own fault, in which case God is not bound to restore it. But that case
has no place in the first state before sin. In this sense I grant that one who is not bound to give
the power to perform the law and avoid sin is not the author of sin if he does not give it; but this
must be added: God is bound to give that power if he has given a law whose performance necessarily
requires it, etc. But the foregoing questions—whether a law imposed upon a human being is
performable without grace or not, and whether the human being received supernatural grace—were
sufficiently treated in the responses to Propositions 10 and 14. Nor is what is said relevant: “If
the human being had sinned in a natural matter (where grace was not owed), his will alone would have
been at fault, not the deprivation of grace.” For who denies this, if that law could have been
performed by the powers of nature? But I wholly deny that this happened in that singular law given
to Adam. And we have given the reasons for this denial in the response to Proposition 14.

3. The order of sin, and how according to the judgment of Calvin and Beza it is subordinated to
God’s decree, was discussed in Proposition 6. I grant that God’s ordering does nothing that is not
owed; but because an ordering of sin such as they attribute to God does not befit God, it is no
wonder that by it something not owed is attributed to God.

RESPONSE. Arminius himself has already confessed that the law imposed upon Adam was performable by
grace alone. Junius, on the contrary, said that that particular law commanding abstinence from the
fruit of a certain tree was performable even without grace. As for Arminius’s saying that in this
respect this opinion falls into the former one, Junius has declared more than once that these three
opinions are so related to one another that, as the saying goes, “One matter asks the aid of another
and conspires amicably” [printed introduction: *ut alterius sic*].

2. I do not see how Junius suitably frames his contradiction. For one who says that the law is
performable by grace alone should not on this account be judged to exclude nature. The meaning is
that the law is not performable by human nature unless it is supported by grace. Junius had already
affirmed that the particular law given to Adam was performable by the powers of nature, which is
also true as regards the substance of the act; and he repeats it later here when he says, “He failed
in a natural matter, one performable by nature and performable by it alone.” But what did he mean
when he added that Adam’s nature had been rightly ordered to grace? He recalls to mind the earlier
obscurity, which I am certainly not the one to explain. Meanwhile, I deny that the particular law
was performable by Adam in the manner required through the powers of nature alone. Nor can I approve
what he adds concerning the present state, that the law is performable by grace alone. For it seems
to me beyond controversy that it lies just as much within a human being’s natural powers to restrain
himself from the fruit of any tree as it lay within Adam’s while he was still in integrity; but we
cannot abstain in a holy manner today, nor could Adam ever do so, without grace. These statements
also proceed quite clumsily: “It cannot even be performed by us but is imputed through grace.” What
then? Is the law imputed to us? One may say that Junius means that the performance of the law
accomplished by Christ is imputed to us. That, I confess, is asserted more tolerably. But Junius
added that what he denied could be performed by us was “imputed by us” [printed: *à nobis
imputari*]. Yet the performance of the law is not what he denied could be performed by us, and the
law itself, which he denied could be performed by us, is undoubtedly not imputed to us.

2. To be a cause of sin through the removal or non-bestowal of a preventive is not to be a cause of
sin simply, but only to this extent: that one does not give what would have prevented it
efficaciously. But to be a cause of sin simply is to be its culpable cause. Yet Junius rightly
proves God to be a cause of sin in this manner, especially since by not giving grace he by no means
brings it about that sin is not committed freely, just as by granting the same grace he would not
bring it about that one did not freely abstain from sin. But I cannot approve Junius’s repeatedly
passing over in his discussion from the natural law to the particular law. For it betrays a
manifest distrust of his reasoning in his argument.

Certainly, from the ordering of sin made by God according to Junius’s meaning, the existence of sin
by no means follows; rather, it precedes that ordering, since Junius acknowledged no ordering of sin
made by God other than the establishment of order in a thing already done. Nor did Arminius infer
the necessary existence of sin from an ordering of this sort. Nor is the rationale Junius offers
timely—namely, that God’s ordering does nothing that is not owed. For it pertains rather to the
other ordering, which ordains that something should occur, not one which orders it after it has
occurred. Junius has not yet professed his approval of an ordering of that sort, although here he
makes a show of doing so. Indeed, on this point he appears to have equivocated above solely because
he lacked a sufficiently suitable response to the argument Arminius had raised. Yet even we who say
that God ordained sin so that it should occur would not support our cause by this rationale which
Junius supplies us—namely, that God’s ordering does nothing that is not owed. Rather, we would show
specifically that nothing follows from this by which God becomes guilty of any action that is not
owed when he wills sin to occur while he himself merely permits it.

3. Here at last Arminius not only complains that this opinion falls into the former one, but also
expressly contends that according to the consequence of this opinion it will follow either that the
cause of sin is not the human being but the one who imposed such a law and did not bestow the
faculty of performing it, or that the transgression of that law could not have been called sin.
Come now, let us examine those consequences which Arminius connects. Does Arminius really maintain
that the law given to Adam was performable without grace? Nothing could be further from his view,
as is clear from what he discussed at Proposition Fourteen and repeats briefly here. For he wholly
denies that the law given to Adam was performable without supernatural grace, or, what amounts to
the same thing, that it could have been performed by the powers of nature. It therefore remains
either that Arminius denies that grace of this sort was not given to Adam, or that he maintains Adam
was deprived of it by his own fault. But he rejects the latter here in number two with these words:
“That case has no place in the first state before sin”; and immediately before he had been speaking
of the case of deprivation of grace by one’s own fault. Therefore Arminius must maintain that the
grace by which the law imposed upon Adam could be performed was given to him and was by no means
withdrawn before his fall. But what was this grace? Was it sufficient or efficacious? In this part
Arminius is speaking entirely of sufficient grace, which he here calls the faculty of performing
the law given to Adam. Yet neither do we say that Adam was not given a faculty of this sort or that
it was withdrawn before his fall. Thus Arminius toys with his reader by mere equivocation. We say
that Adam possessed sufficient grace by which he could perform the law imposed upon him; we deny
that he possessed efficacious grace by which it would be brought about that he actually performed
the law. To have the faculty of performing the law and for it to be impossible that the law should
be performed are not contradictory—just as the soldiers did not lack the strength to break the
Savior’s bones, although, given God’s decree to the contrary, it was impossible for them to be
broken. By like reasoning, although Adam had the faculty of performing God’s law, given God’s decree
to deny efficacious grace, it was impossible for the law to be performed. Yet it will not follow
from this that Adam sinned necessarily and not freely, just as, on the other side, it by no means
follows [printed: *nequaqam*] from God’s decree that the soldiers necessarily and not freely
abstained from breaking the Savior’s bones. Certainly Adam would by no means have been the cause of
sin if God had not bestowed upon him the faculty of performing the law. But this does not follow
from our opinion, but from a corrupt

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 69 | BSB canvas 81]

interpretation of our opinion, which Arminius makes in his customary fashion. Arminius adds that it
is altogether true that “he who does not give that without which sin cannot be avoided is truly the
author of sin.” In these words he varies the phrase, putting “that without which sin cannot be
avoided” in place of what he had earlier called “the faculty of performing the law”; but he does not
change his intention to reason sophistically. For what else is this than the faculty of performing
the law? But we do not say either that this faculty was not given to Adam or that it was taken away,
just as God neither failed to give the soldiers the faculty of breaking Christ’s bones nor took it
away after giving it. Meanwhile, just as, given God’s decree that Christ’s bones should not be
broken, it was impossible for them to be broken, so also, given God’s decree not to concur
efficaciously by efficacious motion, both naturally efficacious and graciously efficacious, with
Adam in abstaining from the forbidden fruit—naturally, I say, as regards the substance of the act,
through general concurrence; graciously, as regards the holy manner of acting, through special
concurrence—it was impossible for him to abstain; that is, it was impossible [printed: *potuir*]
that he should not eat from it. He ate, I say, not necessarily but freely, just as it was impossible
that the soldiers should not abstain from breaking Christ’s bones; they abstained, I say, not
necessarily but freely.
Arminius knows very well that without God’s concurrence with abstaining it was impossible that Adam
should abstain, and that, given God’s concurrence with eating, it was impossible that he should not
eat. Yet God supplied Adam with that concurrence for eating and by no means supplied concurrence
for abstaining. Here Arminius is accustomed solemnly to reply that God supplied his concurrence for
eating only to Adam when he already willed to eat; even if Adam had willed to abstain, God would
have been ready to supply him with his concurrence for abstaining. My point is that Arminius and his
fellows have nothing to reply except that contrivance, and yet they are unwilling to permit
themselves to be driven to the point of openly professing what they think, lest the absurdity of
their opinion betray itself by its own evidence like a mouse. Observe now, I ask, how that
contrivance is devised beyond every restraint of intelligence and judgment. They themselves admit
that God’s concurrence is required just as much for willing to eat as for eating, and just as much
for willing to abstain as for abstaining. But let the learned and sober reader consider whether God
supplied his concurrence for willing to Adam when Adam already willed. For given that someone
wills, what need is there of God’s concurrence for willing? I grant that in commanded acts this
response has the appearance of a tolerable assertion; but as soon as it is applied to elicited acts,
even that appearance of probability immediately collapses and vanishes into nothing. Therefore
Arminius has nothing by which either to defend his own opinion or weaken ours except sophisms,
obscurities, and concealments of the true state of the controversy. As soon as these are ordered to
depart and the formulation of the question is properly framed, they immediately disappear, just as
the darkness of night retreats at the rising of the day. Therefore, when Arminius judged what he had
said earlier about the faculty of performing—as though it had not been granted to Adam—not suitably
enough adapted to his reasonings, which were devised solely to obscure the truth, he later recooked
that phrase and transformed it into “that without which sin cannot be avoided,” as something much
more useful to his cause. He observed, of course, that we affirm that unless not only sufficient
grace, which is habitual and consists in the faculty of performing the law, but also efficacious
grace, which is actual, is granted, it is impossible for sin to be avoided. We have now shown the
sense in which we affirm this, how this statement is freed from every absurdity when explained, how
feebly Arminius assails it while relying only upon the obscurity of the phrase, and how at the same
time he cherishes in his own bosom a most absurd opinion.

2. “That condition,” he says, “‘if the preventive is owed,’ is given here in vain, for God
necessarily owes the human being the power to perform that law which he imposes upon him.” But who
among us ever denied that this power was given to Adam? Arminius, however, judges it advisable to
hide behind a confusion of the matters. He is reluctant to distinguish sufficient and efficacious
grace; he can scarcely—or rather cannot at all—tolerate that to have the faculty of doing something
and yet for it to be impossible that the thing should be done are compossible. Yet we have already
shown this and have furnished an illustration from a very apt comparison [printed: *per, commoda*]
between God’s decree concerning the breaking of Christ’s bones and the soldiers’ natural faculty
considered in relation to breaking them.

Junius is so far from asserting that Adam lacked the faculty of performing the law imposed by God
that, on the contrary, he even professes that it was performable without grace. We have already
seen how unsuccessfully Arminius labored to extricate himself from that assertion. Here, however,
he takes his hand from the tablet and says only that this matter was sufficiently treated at
Propositions Ten and Fourteen.

3. We deny that the ordering of sin attributed to God by Calvin and Beza does not befit God. For
how could it not befit him, when nothing occurs throughout all Scripture which is either affirmed
more plainly or inculcated more frequently than that sins were ordained and decreed by God so that
they should occur? The sale of Joseph; the hardening of Pharaoh so that he would not let God’s
people go; the mutual and hostile fighting of Abimelech and the Shechemites; the violation of
David’s concubines; the arming of the sword against David’s house; the revolution of affairs and
the defection of the ten tribes from the royal tribe; Sennacherib’s deadly incursion into the holy
land and against God’s people, and at last his slaughter by the swords of his sons; the arming of
the Babylonians against the Israelites; and finally whatever Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles, and the
people of Israel perpetrated against the most holy Son of God—all these things God claims as
preordained by his decree so that they should occur.
