## CHAPTER IV

### Questions and difficulties concerning the preceding chapter

### QUESTION I

#### Whether the younger Cainan is spurious

First it is asked what should be determined concerning the younger Cainan,
whom Luke the Evangelist inserts into Christ’s genealogy in 3:36 between
Arphaxad and Salah, though Moses is silent about him. Answer: the words τὸ
Καινὰν in Luke appear to be the error of some copyist which long ago crept
into the text. Theodore Beza testifies that his very ancient manuscript was
free of it, and, relying on its authority, confidently expunges those two
words from his Testament as an intrusive error. We admit that this addition
still stands in most Greek codices, printed as well as manuscript, and now in
almost all versions—the Syriac, Latin, both English versions, and the new
Dutch. But Diodati’s Italian and Cartwright’s Latin in his harmony follow
Beza’s correction.

For besides Moses expressly asserting three times, Genesis 10:24 and 11:12,
that Arphaxad begot Salah, the author of the Septuagint likewise affirms it at
book 1, chapter 1, verse 18, with no mention of Cainan indicated. Here the
Chaldean paraphrast everywhere, Jerome’s Latin, and all the other Old
Testament versions follow the Hebrew text except the Septuagint alone. No one
should grant it any authority here, since in the very clause in which it
produces a fictitious Cainan it admits a gross chronological error confessed
by all: it says that Arphaxad begot Cainan in his 130th year and that Cainan
likewise begot Salah in his 130th year. Those who defend Cainan—and there are
many authors—add thirty years to Scripture’s true reckoning, but all are
forced to desert their authorities, the Septuagint translators, since the
latter add not merely thirty but a full two hundred years to the Hebrew truth.
Those who acknowledge such a foul chronological error by the Septuagint in
this very place should not use its authority to defend a blemish in the
history and a further anachronism of thirty years, although whatever error is
here is usually transferred from the translators themselves to their
copyists. Whoever wishes to see this whole question handled solidly and
clearly should consult the learned Spanheim’s *Evangelical Difficulty on
Cainan*. We too treat this celebrated question fully in the Additions.

### QUESTION II

#### Whether the Greek Fathers’ calculation after the Flood is correct

Second it is asked how, when only 292 years are now commonly counted from the
Flood to Abraham’s birth, the very learned Fathers Augustine and Eusebius
count respectively more than a thousand and as many as 1,720. Answer: either
an original error in the Septuagint translators—whom the Fathers held in the
highest veneration amid their profound ignorance of Eastern languages—or a
very early corruption from elsewhere led most of the Fathers into the
grossest errors about dates, errors now admitted by everyone. This occurred
not only in the centuries before the Flood but also in those afterward down
to Abraham. The Septuagint adds at least one hundred years to the life of each
patriarch from the Flood to Abraham, an anachronism and chronological error
which, within the bounds of the three centuries before Abraham’s birth,
amounts to far more than a thousand years. That we are now freed from such
hideous errors is due to the Lord’s kindness and the care and labors of
learned men in our age, who have advanced Hebrew learning and the study of
chronology far beyond the measure of several preceding centuries.

### Additions to Question II

#### Isaac Vossius’s and Brian Walton’s perverse correction of the Hebrew text
in the years of the patriarchs after the Flood

Against this the very learned Isaac Vossius and Brian Walton raise many
objections with sufficiently lofty disdain. On page 16 of his *Dissertation
on the Age of the World*, Vossius retorts that the Hebrew calculation assigns
too short a childhood to the patriarchs after the Flood, since puberty is
usually reached after a third or fourth part of a human life. Answer: as said
above, Walton himself pronounced this argument utterly worthless when it was
applied to the patriarchs before the Flood; applied to those after it, it is
much more worthless. Their lives were shortened by half, and most did not
reach a quarter of their predecessors’ lives. Peleg, Reu, Serug, Nahor, and
Terah passed two hundred only slightly. Their childhood and puberty should
therefore have occupied only a quarter of the childhood of Cainan, Mahalalel,
Jared, Methuselah, and the rest who passed nine hundred, whose prime even on
the Greek calculation is placed well before two hundred. The former men’s
prime, therefore,

[Printed p. 21 | IA leaf 51 | access PDF 51 | OCR object 50]

should not have reached fifty—an unfortunate argument for proving the Greek
calculation of 130 years.

Isaac’s second argument: if Arphaxad, Eber, and the others begot children as
early as the Hebrew codex maintains, they could have seen the fifteenth or
twentieth generation from themselves. But that is absurd. Answer: why ask
about a possibility never brought into actuality? It is evident that none of
them saw the tenth generation, and for an obvious reason. The argument
supposes that every descendant begot a child in his thirtieth year, whereas
Moses explicitly relates that Terah did not beget before his seventieth—or,
if Isaac is believed, his 130th. Abraham begot Isaac in his hundredth, Isaac
Jacob in his sixtieth, and Jacob Reuben after his eightieth.

His third argument: the patriarchs after the Flood did not beget at about
thirty, since in Isaac’s day, when human life was twice as short, the fortieth
year was regarded as youth. Answer: the learned man understands Isaac’s youth
to mean the year of his marriage. What force has that here? Jacob’s marriage
year was above eighty, but Judah and Benjamin must have married before thirty.
When Benjamin descended into Egypt before his twenty-third year, he was the
father of no fewer than ten legitimate sons, and Judah at forty-three had two
great-grandsons. What has any of that to do with the present matter?

The learned man’s fourth argument: it is utterly absurd, he says, and shows
unhappy judgment to think that what the Hebrew calculation nevertheless
states actually happened—Noah lived until Abraham’s fifty-eighth year, and
Shem saw Jacob, the thirteenth from himself. Answer: a learned man should
scarcely have opposed express Scripture with mere assertions and abuse. What
is absurd here? What unhappy judgment is there in our faith if we believe
Noah saw the tenth generation and Shem the thirteenth? Before the Flood did
not Adam live for sixty-six years with Lamech, Noah’s father, and for 253 with
Methuselah, although Methuselah was eighth and Lamech ninth from Adam? Indeed,
Ephraim, Jacob’s grandson, as will shortly appear, seems to have prolonged his
days far beyond his own tenth generation.

Fifth argument: various kingdoms were established before Abraham, and authors
record many Egyptian dynasties before that time; yet no Gentile mentions
Noah. Moreover, if Noah had lived until Abraham, Moses would not have passed
it over. Answer: Noah’s centuries fall within the bounds of the “obscure
time,” about which Gentile writers are silent. The “mythical time” began after
the Flood, and pagan writers possess nothing about it but fables. Those fables
are in fact full enough of stories about Janus, who saw both worlds with his
two faces; who, taught by Saturn, was the first to teach cultivation and vine
planting; who was the true Prometheus, Japheth’s father rather than his son;
and who first imparted to earthen men fire stolen from heaven—that is,
prudence—and taught astronomy. As for Moses, he declares plainly enough that
Noah lived in Abraham’s days for anyone willing to compare their years by the
most explicit calculation of the sacred text.

Sixth: the Hebrew calculation makes the building of Babel’s tower and the
dispersion of the nations occur scarcely a century after the Flood, when
perhaps Noah’s third heir was not yet born. To solve this difficulty, some
invent marvels: that after the Flood the fathers applied themselves to
begetting children in their twelfth year or earlier. Answer: Peleg was at
least fifth from Noah, or, if we believed the objector, sixth. I think no one
ever asserted that the patriarchs after the Flood began begetting in their
twelfth year, much less earlier, since Moses expressly places their begetting
above the twenty-eighth year. Nor, I think, has anyone placed the building of
Babel’s tower before the hundredth full year after the Flood, at Peleg’s
birth. Certainly Isaac’s very learned father, Gerardus Johannes Vossius, in
this very book which his no more learned son gave us, proves on page 53 that
the division of languages and nations occurred not at Peleg’s birth but at
the midpoint of his life, 128 years after the Flood.

Seventh: all who follow the Jewish codex assign the kingdoms of Semiramis and
Ninus to the fifty-eighth year after the Flood, which is absurd, foolish, and
impossible. Answer: the learned man should have named some of all these
authors. Those who follow the Hebrew calculation certainly do not begin the
kingdom of Nimrod, who undoubtedly preceded Ninus and Semiramis, before the
hundredth year after the Flood—or, if he believes his father, before the
218th. What he touches upon concerning the Egyptian dynasties and the Chinese
annals we shall weigh in its proper place.

Eighth: according to the Hebrew calculation the younger Cainan must be
omitted,

[Printed p. 22 | IA leaf 52 | access PDF 52 | OCR object 51]

yet learned men are utterly absurd in this; they act wickedly if they mean
what they say when they claim that the Septuagint added Cainan, and far more
wickedly when they affirm that Luke the Evangelist was deceived by their
fraud and erred. Answer: be more sparing of such words against these men. We
will soon address the younger Cainan in his own place. Meanwhile the learned
man should have been restrained from such atrocious abuse by respect either
for his father or for himself. The elder Vossius, edited here by Isaac, denies
on page 49 that the younger Cainan ever existed. The Samaritan text and
Josephus, whom Isaac will immediately allege as his witnesses, omit him.

The ninth argument is the venerable authority of the Samaritan codex, which
plainly agrees here with the Greek calculation. Answer: I shall add nothing
here about Cainan, but can the Samaritan version have any authority whatever
for Isaac when he wholly rejects its calculation for all the patriarchs
before the Flood? Why should it carry more weight for those after it? Further,
could the learned man so quickly forget what he himself printed in his
father’s name on pages 50 following of this very work: that the Samaritan
codex entirely disagrees with both the Greek and Latin in the lives of all the
patriarchs after the Flood? The matter is clear in seven—Salah, Eber, Peleg,
Reu, Serug, Nahor, and Terah—and not by a few years but by several hundreds.
If the son doubts his father’s reliability, he can now easily inspect the
Polyglot of his colleague Walton—his colleague in this cause, though not in
this argument.

The tenth is Josephus’s authority as a historian. Josephus counts the time
from the Flood to Abraham according to the Greek rather than the present
Hebrew calculation, which he would not have done unless the uncorrupted Hebrew
codices once agreed with today’s Greek. Therefore today’s Hebrew codices must
be corrected by the Greek version. Answer: 1. It is very hard to digest that
the Hebrew text should be corrected by Josephus’s history instead of his
history by the Hebrew text. 2. How little authority Josephus—or at least his
present manuscript and printed codices—has in chronological matters was said
above. 3. If Josephus’s authority were followed here, Isaac would lose his
case. In the cited place, *Antiquities* 1.6, both Greek and Latin codices
expressly affirm that 292 years elapsed from the Flood to Abraham’s birth,
which is the Hebrews’ precise calculation. Isaac trusts his eyes and does not
deny this, but says that the years assigned to the individual patriarchs from
the Flood to Abraham total 933. Answer: this sets Josephus against Josephus on
the same page. Why not say, then, that the copyists corrupted the calculation
of individual lives by adding a hundred years to each, a figure never present
in Josephus’s autograph? The Latin translator boldly expunges that hundred as
a manifest fault, contrary not only to the Hebrew truth but to Josephus
himself. 4. Granted that Josephus’s particular sums are true and sound, they
do not prove Isaac’s case. He contends for the number in the Greek codices,
which he says reaches 1,032, whereas Josephus’s number goes no higher than
933—a difference of a full 149 years. 5. Isaac admits that Josephus’s codices
disagree greatly among themselves about the years from the Flood to Abraham,
giving at various points 292, 343, and 383. Can anything certain—indeed,
anything true—be extracted from them unless they are conformed to the Hebrew
truth? 6. Isaac affirms that the Hebrew codices were uncorrupted in Josephus’s
day. From what version can the true reading of the Hebrew text in that day be
better inferred than from the Chaldean paraphrase, composed at the very same
or approximately the same time? Without any variant reading it follows to
this day the present Hebrew calculation precisely in the lives of all the
fathers after the Flood.

Finally, why does Isaac press others with Josephus’s authority when in the
same place he deserts Josephus on so many points? Besides what was said, he
holds that Josephus erred in omitting the younger Cainan and with him 130
years; erred about Abraham’s birth in his father’s seventieth year, since he
was not born until the 130th; and erred about Terah’s birth, which Josephus
places in Nahor’s 120th year, although both Hebrew and Greek codices give the
seventy-ninth.

Isaac’s eleventh argument comes from the antiquity of the Chaldean kingdom,
which cannot stand with the Hebrew calculation. Callisthenes, as Simplicius
reports in his commentary on book 2 of Aristotle’s *On the Heavens*, wrote to
Aristotle from Babylon in the year Alexander captured the city that the
Chaldeans produced no astronomical observations older than 1,903 years. On
the Hebrew calculation this number falls in the sixty-first year after the
Flood. The Chaldean kingdom could not have been established so early. Indeed,
the common calculation from Alexander to the first Belus, as may be seen in
the elder Vossius’s *Isagoge*, page 54, is 1,978 years, exceeding Callisthenes
by seventy-three years

[Printed p. 23 | IA leaf 53 | access PDF 53 | OCR object 52]

and thus exceeding the Flood by ten. Moreover Africanus, as Scaliger has it
in the fragments of Eusebius’s *Chronicle*, records two dynasties of Chaldean
kings who reigned 440 years before Belus, all of which must have passed before
the Flood if the Hebrew text is followed. Answer: the very learned man should
not have opposed the truth of sacred texts with such arguments. What is
reported outside Scripture about Chaldean antiquities is sheer fable and
madness. From the Chaldeans’ sacred books the Magi produced events not merely
before the Flood but more than four hundred thousand years before creation,
as Cicero relates in *On Divination* 1, page 88, and Lactantius from him at
7.14. Whatever Chaldean books prove Africanus’s dynasties also attest that
enormous Ciceronian number of 470,000 years. Cicero says they should be
ridiculed, scorned, condemned for stupidity, vanity, and imprudence—indeed,
judged to be lying and, as Lactantius puts it, raving.

Second, the report about Callisthenes rests on the sole testimony of Porphyry,
from whom Simplicius took it. The bare word of this apostate prejudices Holy
Scripture in no respect.

Third, Porphyry’s testimony about Callisthenes, as Simplicius reports it, says
nothing about a Babylonian kingdom, era, or city, but only about astronomical
observations. That nation, most familiar with the patriarchs from its very
origin, could have possessed an abundance of such observations long before
any kingdom or city was founded among them.

Fourth, Africanus presents those dynasties before Belus not as his own view
but as the Chaldeans’, who vainly contended with the Egyptians over antiquity.
All Hebrew as well as Greek writers make Belus, or Nimrod the father of Ninus,
the founder of the Chaldean empire, and he lived long after those dynasties.

Fifth, one must pity Petavius’s insolence in *On the Doctrine of Times* 9.11.
After rightly refuting the vanity of Chaldean and Egyptian antiquities, he
flies as usual at Scaliger as false, utterly absurd, and contrary to Scripture
because Scaliger places the Chaldean and Egyptian dynasties from Africanus in
his Julian period and censures Eusebius for departing from Africanus in those
dynasties, which place Chaldean and Egyptian kingdoms before the Flood. This
Jesuit’s extreme envy of Scaliger certainly spills out repeatedly and wholly
without cause. It is vain to object that these dynasties reach beyond the
Flood. Scaliger explicitly admits this and indeed teaches that many of them
surpass the world’s creation. But Petavius slanderously infers that Scaliger
or Africanus, as their own view, placed any king before creation or the Flood.
No impudence but a Jesuit’s could have fastened such an impious, atheistic
absurdity on these eminent men. Africanus did not present the dynasties as his
own view, but cited them without alteration as he found them in Manetho,
Ctesias, and other pagan books, for many uses on behalf of Christianity
against paganism. The same was Eusebius’s and Scaliger’s design, which Petavius
censures far too rashly.

The argument that Scaliger himself endorses everything he places in his
Julian period after his proleptic time is plainly ridiculous. In book 3 of
the *Isagogical Canons* he manifestly places in his hypothetical Julian period
before the true one six whole Egyptian dynasties containing 1,479 years. In
the true, non-hypothetical period he has five dynasties of more than seven
hundred years. Yet he begins his historical, non-proleptic epochs at the
world’s creation and thence to the Flood counts epochs containing Egyptian
dynasties eleven through seventeen. Has any sane person ever charged
Africanus, Eusebius, or Scaliger with embracing as true everything they placed
in their chronological tables, even things manifestly contrary to Scripture
and all reason? Such slanderers stray far from the very threshold of those
learned men’s purpose. They divided each page of their tables into columns,
assigning in one column the things Scripture records to their times and in
others the deeds of other nations—Assyrians, Egyptians, Greeks, Romans,
Africans, and the rest—according to those nations’ own writers. They did not
guarantee those writers’ reliability, much less embrace with settled
conviction everything they handed down. Whoever claims otherwise constructs
a most foul and witless slander. Petavius likewise unjustly censures
Scaliger’s correction of Eusebius, as could easily be demonstrated if we
thought it worthwhile to linger here.

Isaac’s twelfth argument is from Egyptian antiquities. Their years, he says,
cannot be preserved by the Hebrew calculation.

Answer: 1. Neither can the Greek calculation preserve Egyptian antiquities,

[Printed p. 24 | IA leaf 54 | access PDF 54 | OCR object 53]

for they extend at least two thousand years beyond creation. Menes, king of
the Egyptians, who ruled after heroes spanning many centuries, was—if Isaac
himself is believed and even the Greek calculation retained—contemporary with
Adam at the beginning of his creation.

2. The primary author of these antiquities is Manetho, from whom Africanus
and Eusebius profess to have taken their Egyptian dynasties. Isaac is forced
to admit that he was an exceedingly mendacious man. Yet following Petavius he
wrongly attacks Scaliger because that supreme scholar, after the example of
Africanus and Eusebius, placed Manetho’s utterly fabulous dynasties in his
Julian period and, to compare the true historical years of Holy Scripture
with those false years, established a hypothetical and proleptic period of
many centuries before the world’s creation.

3. Isaac gains nothing by rejecting Manetho and fleeing to Eratosthenes.
Eratosthenes’ credibility rests solely on the Egyptian priests’ reports,
which Manetho—the chief and quasi-pontiff of the Egyptian priests—knew much
more accurately than Eratosthenes or any foreigner when he described those
antiquities from the hieroglyphic archives to Ptolemy, king of Egypt.

4. Eratosthenes’ own dynasties far exceed the Greek calculation. What Isaac
wishes Africanus and Eusebius to attribute to Eratosthenes rests on Diodorus
Siculus’s testimony, and Isaac makes Eratosthenes’ and Diodorus’s calculation
one and the same. Yet by Diodorus’s numbers more than 1,400 years elapsed from
Menes, the Egyptians’ first mortal king, to Busiris, and from Busiris to
Pharaoh so many thousands that Eratosthenes’ and Diodorus’s calculation can no
more be contained within the Flood, or even creation, than Manetho’s.

5. In his first book Diodorus plainly affirms that in Egyptian matters he
finds nothing firmly credible—οὐδὲν παράπηγμα τοῖς εὐφρονοῦσιν—before the
Trojan age. He says he did learn from the Egyptian priests that heroes had
reigned from the Sun for 18,000 years and, after them, mortal men for 15,000
years before his journey to Egypt in the reign of the younger Ptolemy
Dionysius. Must Scripture’s calculation be corrected by a chronology to which
Diodorus himself gives no credit?

Finally, Isaac closes his argument with this exclamation: no one, I think,
will doubt the strength of our arguments unless he either does not understand
these things or hates the truth. Answer: this is far removed from his father’s
modesty. It is harsh to accuse any honest man of hatred for the truth; yet it
may well be many people’s lot not to comprehend much in Isaac’s reasoning. I
for one profess among them that, if I correctly understand the course of this
argument, most of it seems to rest on the lightest conjectures, which cannot
sustain any sensible person’s serious examination.

The very learned man’s last argument, which he calls invincible, is taken from
the utterly certain chronology of the Chinese. They possess, he says, a
continuous history of kings extending back 4,500 years, which on the Hebrew
calculation antedates the Flood by many years.

Answer: who will guarantee the certainty of Chinese chronology? Let that race
be the most learned of all who ever existed—something, however, the learned
man will in my judgment never persuade any sane person to grant. What prevents
their chronology from being as fabulous as that of the Chaldeans, Egyptians,
Greeks, and Latins, peoples no whit inferior to the Chinese in letters?

2. Who will guarantee that the Augustinian monks—for the Chinese dynasties
described by Scaliger in book 2 of the *Isagogical Canons* rest on their word
alone—had certain knowledge of writings preserved in the Chinese archives
older than Moses? Isaac does not deny that Ludovicus Vives, a most serious and
learned man, pronounced those monastic histories of Chinese antiquities to be
fabulous trifles.

3. We appeal to Isaac’s own candor: does he think it fair that the Hebrew text
be reformed according to the trifling Chinese dynasties, which according to
Scaliger rise a full five hundred years above the Flood, or that they be
corrected by Moses’ sacred text?

From this last argument the learned man derives two corollaries. The first is
that this assertion of his ruins the pre-Adamite writer’s effort more than all
his opponents’ arguments. Answer: on the contrary, Isaac’s assertion seems to
give life to the dying pre-Adamite sect. Its author changed his opinion,
convicted not—as the learned man claims—by the offer of many great rewards and
punishments, but by the clarity of the refutations. Isaac, however, by
asserting that nations existed many centuries before Adam’s creation, openly
professes that several thousand years passed, if not before Adam, nevertheless
before the time when almost all Protestant and Roman Catholic Christians,
following the Hebrew Bible, believe Adam and the world were created. He also
truly admits an excessive

[Printed p. 25 | IA leaf 55 | access PDF 55 | OCR object 54]

antiquity of nations, asserted not only by pre-Adamites but by ancient
philosophers and by many learned and serious men today as formerly, has
occasioned the teaching that the world is eternal contrary to Scripture, and
consequently the banishment of God from the world and from the very nature of
things. The madness of men more headstrong than learned finally breaks out
along this sinew.

The other corollary is that the pre-Adamite errs in denying that Noah’s Flood
was universal. Answer: what, then, came into Isaac’s mind that he should think
this gross error too, already recanted by the pre-Adamite himself, worthy of
the protection of his manifold learning? It is pointless to call the Flood
universal in words when by various arguments you try with all your strength
to prove it very narrow and particular—an inundation, as he says, of scarcely
one-hundredth of the lands—contrary to the most explicit Scriptures, Genesis
8:9, *“the waters were over the whole earth,”* and 7:19, *“all the high
mountains under the whole heaven were covered.”* The learned man’s contrary
arguments deserve the very censure he passed on Scaliger and Petavius at page
21: they are frigid and unworthy of refutation.

Let us now turn to Brian Walton’s aid. In Prolegomenon 9, section 63, he says
that the Hebrew calculation places the beginning of the Assyrian empire in
the 150th year after the Flood, which is incredible. Answer: learned men are
very uncertain about the Assyrian kingdom’s beginning. But suppose it began
with Nimrod or Belus in that year; why is this incredible? Walton offers
absolutely nothing against this common belief of the most learned Protestant
and even Roman Catholic writers. He is certainly too fair to think that the
learned world can abandon its faith, founded on Holy Scripture, at his bare
contradiction unsupported by argument.

Second, he says, the Hebrew calculation makes Noah and Shem live in the days
of Abraham and even Isaac and Jacob. This is incredible because neither
sacred nor secular writings then mention them, as though they had been
carried off to distant islands far from Abraham’s fellowship. There were also
great wars then among Noah’s descendants, which their common father could
easily have suppressed had he lived. Answer: to what was said against the
same argument of Vossius we add that profane authors mention very few events
in those obscure centuries, while Moses in the history of descendants does
not customarily mention their ancestors. He closes Adam’s history before Seth
and Seth’s before Enoch. Did Methuselah not live for several centuries with
Noah because Noah’s history makes no mention of him?

Further, why is paternal authority invoked as though Adam could have
restrained his descendants’ crimes for a thousand years? Shem, Arphaxad, and
the other fathers certainly could not restrain Nimrod and his companions from
rebelling against God by building Babel’s tower. Was it not the unhappiness
of those and all centuries that the best parents cannot and never could turn
wicked descendants from wars, murders, adulteries, and every crime?

Third, he says, in Abraham’s days there were many vast kingdoms. If the Hebrew
calculation stands, Ninus and Semiramis led almost innumerable armies about
three hundred years after the Flood, but mankind could not have multiplied so
greatly in so short a time. Answer: there were indeed many kingdoms in
Abraham’s day, for every city then had its own king, but nothing certain is
known about the population. Petavius rightly regards Diodorus’s and others’
accounts of the armies of Ninus, Semiramis, and Staurobates as utterly false
fables. If we believe them, why not give equal credit to Diodorus, Herodotus,
and Justin in the history of Sesostris? Why not believe that Sesostris, with a
far greater army, subdued all Asia and a great part of Europe and Africa more
than eight hundred years before the Flood, in Egypt’s twelfth dynasty?

Moreover, in an age of extraordinary human fertility under the divine
blessing and design to fill the earth, it can be shown in various ways that
Noah’s sons could very easily multiply almost without limit in two hundred
years. We will shortly discuss Capellus’s method and another of Calvisius.
Here Petavius also proposes his own, against which Walton’s objections do not
seem weighty. Petavius, says Walton, assumes without proof: 1. that all after
the Flood lived three hundred years. Answer: Petavius assumes no such thing.
Besides, it is not a hard postulate that ordinary human life from the Flood
to the Assyrian kingdom’s beginning did not differ greatly from the days of
the patriarchs then alive. All the patriarchs down to Peleg, who lived in
Nimrod’s day, passed not merely three but four hundred years.

Second, Walton objects that Petavius assumes everyone then begot children at
sixteen. Answer: had he assumed that people begot at full puberty, much
earlier than sixteen, there would have been nothing absurd in it;

[Printed p. 26 | IA leaf 56 | access PDF 56 | OCR object 55]

but Petavius explicitly rejects that supposition and lets no one beget before
seventeen. Indeed, begetting at twenty-four is enough for his purpose.

Third, Walton objects that Petavius supposes no one died in those three
hundred years, though the frequent wars of that time must have killed many.
Answer: Petavius does not suppose this at all. He only grants every man eight
male children from his seventeenth to twenty-fourth year. Their deaths detract
little from his purpose. Even if ten times one hundred thousand dead are
subtracted from his calculation, Noah’s three sons leave a multitude large
enough to assemble innumerable armies.

Walton’s fourth argument: the Hebrew calculation supposes that Abraham’s and
Sarah’s great-grandfathers and ancestors still lived in their day. Sarah
therefore had no reason to marvel that Abraham begot at one hundred or that
she conceived at ninety, for such births were frequent among the ancestors
living with them. Answer: by Abraham’s day human life had been shortened by
far more than half from that of their ancestors. Further, the Hebrew
calculation supposes many ancestors lived after Abraham’s birth, but never
supposes that any of them then begot children, much less that in Abraham’s day
a woman could ordinarily conceive in her ninetieth year.

I see no further arguments Walton adds to Isaac’s. Let Walton’s own candor
judge after review whether they have enough strength to support his very
harsh sarcasm against dissenters: they must all be the rabble of
chronologers, who take no care to free themselves from difficulties and
absurdities; for whom nothing within the olive and nothing outside the nut is
hard. Isaac and Brian willingly admit that this chronological “rabble” holds
great, if not the greatest, men—Joseph Scaliger, Petavius, Gerardus Vossius,
Louis Cappel, and many others. But before all of them we possess God’s Spirit
in the authentic Scriptures, which we wish such learned men had not attacked
with arguments that seem to us very unworthy of their names.

Long after dictating this, there came into my hands a treatise against Isaac
Vossius’s dissertation by the very learned professor of history at Leiden. I
was pleased that this learned man agreed with me on the substance throughout,
supporting it with various arguments I had omitted.

### On Question II of the second epoch

#### On the younger Cainan

This grave dispute over the younger Cainan had been quieted and almost buried
by the great labors of many very learned men, so that very few doubted the
fiction of an inserted Cainan, until Isaac Vossius and Brian Walton recently
drew it into the light again. Their express purpose was to use Luke’s divine
authority as an irrefragable argument to overthrow the authenticity of the
Hebrew codices and demonstrate their corruption, since in Christ’s genealogy
Luke 3 deserts the Hebrew codices and follows the Septuagint. The Hebrew
Bibles expunge the younger Cainan from David’s and therefore Christ’s
genealogy in Genesis 10:4; 11:13; and 1 Chronicles 1:18; the Greek translators
add him. Luke, by following the latter, demonstrates at least here the fault
of the former and the soundness of the latter.

Most Jesuits have long been so ashamed of this hideous argument and still
more hideous conclusion that they openly joined arms with us to crush the
futilities. Vossius’s and Walton’s weapons needed to be much stronger than
those of Salmeron, Torniellus, and Salian, which their own associates Cornelius
a Lapide, Petavius, Possinus, and others long ago broke. Let us nevertheless
test the confidence on which these men renew the war within our churches.

At page 20 Isaac begins with atrocious abuse: in expunging Cainan, he says,
learned men are utterly absurd, granting more to Jewish copyists than to
evangelical truth. Answer: respect for at least two men whom Isaac highly
commended in this very work should have restrained this Jesuitical invective:
Louis Cappel and Isaac’s own world-renowned and very learned father, Gerardus
Johannes. The latter at page 50 of the *Isagoge* edited by Isaac, and the
former at page 7 of his sacred chronology printed by Walton, expunge the
inserted Cainan with arguments neither Isaac nor Brian attempts to answer.

Isaac proceeds with abuse and bitter slander: they act wickedly, he says,
when they claim that the seventy holy elders added this Cainan at their own
pleasure, and far more wickedly when they say that Luke the Evangelist erred,
deceived by their fraud.

[Printed p. 27 | IA leaf 57 | access PDF 57 | OCR object 56]

Answer: why does the learned man charge his own father without cause with
such monstrous wickedness? Who in the Reformed Church has ever condemned any
God-inspired writer of error or affirmed that he was deceived by fraud? Yet
it is unsurprising that one tramples fathers and brothers with wholly
undeserved abuse and slander when in the same place he attacks the authentic
texts of Holy Scripture, blasphemously calling them—I shudder to repeat
it—Jewish copyists opposed to evangelical truth. The learned man could not
have been ignorant of the common Protestant view defended by Spanheim, Ussher,
Bochart, and many others: it exempts the sacred writers, both Moses and Luke,
and even the Septuagint translators themselves from every error here, and
assigns the entire fault in Cainan’s insertion solely to copyists, whether by
carelessness or design—a distinction of little relevance to the fact alone
in dispute.

Isaac is very laconic in his arguments and offers four in these words. First,
he says, the copies must be trusted. Answer: this is what our men insist: the
copies of Moses and the author of Chronicles, in which the younger Cainan
nowhere appears, must be trusted, not those of the Greek translators, who
were fallible men and whose copies in Genesis 11 before us already swarm with
many errors.

Isaac’s second argument: blessed Luke’s authority must be believed. Answer:
far be it from us to call it into doubt, although we correct an intrusive
error caused by copyists’ carelessness from many ancient manuscripts.

Third: the judgment of the entire ancient church must be trusted. Answer: we
will soon blunt this empty boasting when Walton repeats it.

Fourth: Berosus the Chaldean acknowledged Cainan, since he writes that
Abraham was the tenth of the patriarchs born after the Flood, as Josephus has
it in *Antiquities* 1.7. Answer: extraordinary thoughtlessness seems to have
seized Isaac here. If Josephus, or Berosus or Eupolemus as cited by Josephus,
has any authority, Isaac’s whole case falls; for if the younger Cainan is
counted, Abraham is not tenth but eleventh from the Flood.

So much for Isaac. Let us now hear Brian, Prolegomenon 3, section 64. He
decrees that Luke the Evangelist prefers the Greek computation to the Hebrew
and passes judgment for the Septuagint in this case, so that it can scarcely
be denied that the Septuagint reading is truer. Answer: the learned man knows
how many replies various scholars make to preserve the Hebrew calculation
even if Cainan’s name continued in both the Septuagint and Luke. They should
not have been scorned; neither the men asserting them nor their arguments are
so slight. They deny that Cainan originally stood either in the Septuagint or
Luke and affirm that copyists’ carelessness later caused the name to creep
in—whether into the Septuagint and thence Luke, or first Luke and thence the
Septuagint, matters little. Brian calls these “evasions,” a term scarcely
worthy of him; since Brian must prove the charge, we possess mere assertions
in place of arguments.

First: Cainan’s name is read in all codices, even the most ancient, both in
the Septuagint and in Luke. Answer: how rash this assertion is, when Spanheim,
Ussher, Grotius, and Bochart in the very places Brian cites demonstrate the
contrary with arguments, to most of which he answers nothing and to the few
he addresses nothing solid, as we shall see.

Second, he says, among ancient ecclesiastical writers no one read otherwise.
Answer: when the utterly shameless monk Antonius Contius asserted something
similar, Ussher—with Walton’s knowledge—produced in *On Cainan*, page 179,
more than twelve ancient writers who expunged Cainan and all his years from
sacred chronology.

Third, he says, Cainan is constantly retained by every church everywhere to
this day. Answer: until he props this sweeping assertion on some support, I
oppose it: Cainan has not been constantly retained in any church, Greek or
Latin. All who omitted Cainan’s years omitted Cainan himself, or certainly
should have. Brian will shortly admit this major premise, while every church
omitted Cainan’s years. See Baronius at December 25 in the *Roman Martyrology*,
where he recites from ecclesiastical tables the chronology from the world’s
beginning to Christ’s birth used by both Greek and Latin churches, as
Eusebius of Caesarea once arranged it. It is indeed poor in many respects,
but nevertheless plainly expunges all the younger Cainan’s years.

Fourth: from the apostles’ own times to this day, he says, no one except Beza
alone dared expunge Cainan from either the Septuagint or Luke. Answer: 1.
Walton retracts this in the next paragraph only a few lines later. 2. Besides
Beza, Cartwright in his Gospel harmony and Diodati in his Italian and French
Bibles expunged Cainan from Luke. Are Cartwright and Diodati mere shadows of
Theodore Beza and thus nobodies to Walton? 3. Scaliger at the beginning of his
prolegomena to Eusebius’s *Chronicle* testifies that the oldest copies of Luke
omit Cainan. 4. Irenaeus and the churches of his age seem to have preceded
them in boldness by expunging Cainan from both Testaments. Near the beginning
of *Against Heresies* 3.33, Irenaeus professes that he read only seventy-two
generations from Adam to Christ; with Cainan there are seventy-three. 5. In
heresy 55, on the Melchizedekians, Epiphanius specifically recites each
patriarch and his years while wholly passing over the younger Cainan and his
years. 6. Julius Africanus, the greatest chronologer of all the Fathers,
although he slipped in some things through his age’s fault, expunged the
younger Cainan. 7. Eusebius of Caesarea and others follow Africanus here. 8.
Walton admits that Cainan is omitted in Luke in the very ancient manuscript,
written in capitals twelve hundred years ago, found at Irenaeus’s monastery
in Lyons, and sent by Beza as a gift to Cambridge University. Walton refuses
it any authority because it often reads differently from common books and in
Luke 3 borrows a large passage from Matthew.

[Printed p. 28 | IA leaf 58 | access PDF 58 | OCR object 57]

Answer: if Walton strips all authority from the oldest manuscripts because
they differ at points from most later manuscripts, he will open a wide door
to entirely enervating various excellent witnesses. The Vatican manuscript
and even the Alexandrian in Genesis 11 itself, besides adding Cainan, contain
many numerical readings Walton will, I think, admit are spurious and to be
rejected; Salian, Torniellus, Henry Philippi, and many other learned defenders
of Cainan certainly admit it. Do those two excellent manuscripts therefore
lose all authority elsewhere?

Walton’s remaining labor here is not to establish his own case but overthrow
another’s. I wish he had spent more work even on this, for he entirely passes
over Spanheim, Bochart, Petavius, Cornelius, and many others who treat the
question seriously and fully, answering only Grotius and Ussher; even among
Grotius’s arguments he leaves various points untouched. Let us nevertheless
weigh what he offers.

Grotius objects, he says, that Africanus, Theophilus of Antioch, Eusebius in
his *Chronicles*, and Epiphanius omit Cainan’s name, while Josephus and before
him Berosus and Eupolemus acknowledge Abraham as tenth from Noah, although he
would be eleventh if Cainan were inserted. Reply: why omit Irenaeus, Origen,
Procopius of Gaza, and the best copies of the Septuagint which Africanus and
Eusebius received from Origen? Why omit the Chaldean paraphrase and Samaritan
texts Grotius cited? To what he does produce Walton makes three replies; let
anyone judge how much they bear on the matter.

First, he says, the oldest Greek copies, even before Christ, had Cainan’s
name, as Ussher proves from Demetrius’s *Chronicle*. Reply: 1. This is wholly
irrelevant to the present controversy. Grotius thinks Cainan’s name crept
into neither the Septuagint nor Luke before the fourth Christian century,
but that only then did copyists corrupt some copies of Luke and afterward the
Septuagint. Ussher thinks the common Septuagint version was corrupted long
before the Gospel age and the error crept from it into Luke. Walton knows
that Ussher does not disagree with Grotius on the fact: with Grotius he
regards Cainan’s name as a truly spurious intrusion into both the Septuagint
and Luke. Whether it first entered the Septuagint and later Luke, or flowed
from a corrupt Luke into the Septuagint, or rather from a corrupt Septuagint
into Luke, is entirely irrelevant. I note only that Walton proves the force
of Ussher’s argument from Demetrius, that one may infer Cainan from Cainan’s
years: although Demetrius never mentions him, he must be thought to defend
Cainan because he counts from the Flood to Abraham a sum that assumes the
anachronism of Cainan’s 130 years.

Walton’s second answer is that it is certain Eusebius and Epiphanius, though
omitting Cainan’s name somewhere, express it elsewhere and never deny it.
Reply: this is unsatisfactory. 1. Grotius had adduced many Fathers who omitted
Cainan, to whom Walton makes no answer. What of Origen, the most practiced of
all men in this matter, in his fifteenth tractate on John? He affirms that
Abraham was twentieth from Adam and tenth from Noah, statements true only if
the younger Cainan is expunged. What of Procopius of Gaza on Genesis 11, who
testifies that in a Greek manuscript he saw Origen’s obelus prefixed to
Cainan’s name and years? What of Irenaeus, who at *Against Heresies* 3.33 says
there are seventy-two generations from Adam to Christ? Without expunging the
younger Cainan there are, as said above, seventy-three. What of Julius
Africanus, whose calculation from the Flood to Abraham expunges Cainan with
his years? What of Theophilus of Antioch, whose calculation is the same? I
admit that the third edition of the *Library of the Fathers* names Cainan in
Theophilus, but in the more accurate editions used by Grotius and Ussher he
was expunged.

[Printed p. 29 | IA leaf 59 | access PDF 59 | OCR object 58]

What of Jerome, who in his *Questions on Genesis*, where he professes to
observe carefully every difference between the Hebrew and Septuagint, says
nothing about Cainan, openly indicating that in his day no more than in the
days of Africanus or Theophilus did the younger Cainan appear in the best
Septuagint copies? Walton answers none of them.

2. What he asserts as certain about Eusebius seems false. Where does Eusebius
mention Cainan? Ussher rightly warns that the first book of the *Chronicles*,
chapter 2, which Salian cites, is extremely corrupt and contains so many
insertions from various authors that Eusebius’s mind should not be sought
there. Eusebius needed no more explicit denial of Cainan than he gave in his
genuine Greek chronological canon, where near its beginning he three times
deletes both Cainan’s name and years while counting ten patriarchs and their
years: first according to the pure Septuagint from Origen’s Hexapla, then
according to Africanus, and third according to his own calculation. Every
trace of Cainan is erased throughout.

As for Epiphanius, in heresy 52 on the Melchizedekians, where he must
accurately count the patriarchs and their years to overthrow the heretics’
foundations, he says not a word about Cainan’s name or years. If in the
*Panarion* or elsewhere in passing he mentions Cainan from the common edition
rather than Origen’s complete one, that should not prejudice his formal
testimony.

3. Walton’s third answer is that Greek codices may have varied somewhat in
Eusebius’s day, with Cainan’s name inserted in some and not in others. Reply:
1. This retracts Walton’s recent harsh judgment against Beza as the first and
only man who dared expunge Cainan. He now acknowledges that in Eusebius’s own
day, long before Beza’s birth, various Greek copies lacked Cainan. Why not
interpret this confession as a concession of the whole question? We never
denied that some and perhaps many corrupt Greek copies of both Luke and the
Septuagint expressed Cainan. We only affirmed that other, better copies—those
of Origen, Africanus, Irenaeus, Theophilus, Eusebius, and similarly learned
Fathers—were free of this corruption and omitted him. If this is Brian’s
meaning, as his words here suggest, peace on this point is easily made.

Fourth, he replies that Hebrew codices varied in their numbers in Josephus’s
day, so it is unsurprising that Josephus, Berosus, and Eupolemus differ from
the Septuagint, since Josephus also differs from the Hebrew codices now in
use. Reply: 1. Such is my dullness that I cannot grasp Walton’s argument.
Allow the Hebrew codices in Josephus’s day to vary as much as one pleases;
what has that to do with Eupolemus and Berosus, who never saw Hebrew codices
but held Greek ones long before Josephus was born, in which no shadow of
Cainan then appeared if they are believed? 2. It is false that Hebrew codices
ever varied in the numbers before us, much less in Josephus’s day, when the
Chaldean paraphrases of Jonathan and Onkelos appear to have been composed;
both agree exactly in these numbers with today’s Hebrew text. Nor could the
Hebrew text easily fall into variant readings in numbers, for it writes them
not with individual letters or numeral signs, as Greek and Latin do, but with
complete words. In the Greek Bibles, which express all numbers with mere
letters, Genesis 11 alone has within a few verses such numerical variety,
disagreement, and contradiction—as Walton doubtless saw in Louis Cappel’s
*Chronology*, page 7—that no art can either conceal or reconcile it. 3.
Josephus does not differ here from today’s Hebrew text on the present
question, for he most clearly expunges the younger Cainan, the sole point now
at issue.

I add nothing to what was said above about Walton’s attack on Beza’s ancient
manuscript. His denial that another manuscript mentioned by Grotius exists in
England is of little relevance, since he just admitted the self-evident fact
that even in Eusebius’s day there were various manuscripts containing not a
word or trace of the younger Cainan.

So he answers Grotius. Against Ussher, whom he calls a man of great name and
whose posthumous treatise on this argument he held in his hands, he opposes
nothing but an answer to one alleged evasion: that Luke the Evangelist placed
Cainan in Christ’s genealogy not as his own opinion but the common one, not
according to the truth itself but because Hellenistic Christians so read in
the Septuagint. He rejects this view at length as opening a broad window to
every error, and finally pierces it with Bochart’s words. Reply: I appeal to
Brian’s candor whether he treats Ussher, his particular friend and a supreme
scholar, with the fairness due him. Ussher

[Printed p. 30 | IA leaf 60 | access PDF 60 | OCR object 59]

at pages 193 following, answering the hypothetical syllogism—*if Luke the
Evangelist placed the younger Cainan in Christ’s genealogy, then there is no
room to doubt him; but he placed him; therefore*—first distinguishes the
major premise according to the opinion of many great men, as Spanheim says,
and offers various arguments for that distinction. He then overturns with
new arguments the minor premise which his whole dissertation had denied.
Brian neglects the entire dissertation, which had overturned that minor with
many arguments, and seizes only the distinction of the major which Ussher
adduced as others’ view rather than his own. He lingers on this alone while
never touching the principal arguments asserted for it. As for Bochart,
Walton had no need to assert his words against this distinction drawn from
Cajetan, Genebrardus, Petavius, Junius, and others, since before him Spanheim,
Genebrardus, Vossius, and many others of our own had long since sufficiently
defeated it. Indeed, Ussher himself refuted it admirably throughout his
treatise. One could wish Walton had found leisure to answer at least
Bochart’s concise and forceful refutation of Walton’s own view in the same
place where he produced Bochart’s words for himself.

I observe nothing further in Walton’s arguments. What he presented certainly
does not have enough weight to make the Greek version preferable to the
Hebrew truth, especially where within a few verses it has, besides the
disputed point, more than seven manifest and confessed errors. In Walton’s
own Bible every other version follows the Hebrew exactly—Samaritan, Chaldean,
Syriac, Latin, and Arabic. And though nearly innumerable variant readings in
the Hebrew source are alleged from all those streams, even Walton’s extreme
diligence could scrape together not a shadow of a variant that lends the
slightest appearance of support to Cainan’s addition.

### QUESTION III

#### On reconciling Genesis 11:26 with Acts 7:4

Third it is asked how Abraham, born in his father Terah’s seventieth year,
could depart from Haran to Canaan in his own seventy-fifth year after his
father’s death, when Terah lived to 205. Answer: this knot, the most difficult
of all yet encountered, requires an Elias to untie it. We do not presently
promise an exact solution of the difficulty, since Joseph Scaliger himself,
in his *Notes on Eusebius*, folio 17, plainly sticks in the mud and surrenders
without attempting an answer, while all others I have seen leave readers far
more entangled than they found them while promising solutions.

The knot is this: Moses in Genesis 11:26 says Abraham was born in Terah’s
seventieth year, while Stephen in Acts 7:4 expressly asserts that Abraham was
moved from Haran into Canaan after Terah died. Yet it is certain and
undisputed that Abraham departed Haran at seventy-five and his father lived
to 205. If Abraham was born in Terah’s seventieth year and called from Haran
in his own seventy-fifth, that journey must fall in Terah’s 130th year, not
after his death but sixty years before it. Those whom we usually follow most
closely admit this: Joseph Scaliger, Seth Calvisius, Ubbo Emmius, Funccius,
Bucholcerius, and among Roman Catholics Tostatus of Ávila, Petavius,
Suarezianus, Pererius, and others.

But Henry Philippi, after Torniellus and Salian, and indeed our own Cappel,
Pareus, Junius, and Diodati, place Abraham’s Canaanite journey in his
seventy-fifth year and his father’s last, the 205th, and so say Abraham was
born not in Terah’s seventieth but 130th, sixty full years later than the
former writers. It seems hard to us, when Moses says Terah begot Abraham at
seventy, to interpret that—contrary to Moses’ manner in every other
patriarchal genealogy—as sixty years after seventy; besides, that calculation
greatly disturbs sacred chronology, especially the sabbatical and jubilee
years. It seems no less hard to place sixty years before the father’s death a
man whom Stephen says was moved to Canaan after it, or to say Stephen spoke
according to the people’s common computation, which then followed the
Septuagint and rabbinic Kabbalah in chronological matters, or that Abraham
departed Haran for Canaan at seventy-five but was not yet “transplanted” with
his whole household and estate until after his father’s death. These and
similar answers do not please. For the present, therefore, I am at a loss and
leave the solution to later consideration.

### Additions to Question III

#### On the year of Abraham’s birth

The very learned Voetius did not place this difficulty in his disputation on
insoluble questions, although various questions much easier than the present
one stand in his list of them.

[Printed p. 31 | IA leaf 61 | access PDF 61 | OCR object 60]

Jerome in his *Hebrew Questions* and Augustine at *City of God* 15.16
expressly profess that this difficulty is insoluble. Joseph Scaliger, as
said, went over entirely to their view. I would very gladly have rested in
their judgment, but some whom I revere have warned me that it is undesirable
to leave such difficulties undetermined, and I see that the deserved
expunction or necessary insertion of sixty whole years in the tables of
sacred chronology wholly depends on this question. Though with great
reluctance, I have therefore been led to abandon this equilibrium and incline
to one side.

There are two views, each supported by many great authorities and arguments.
Since the die must be cast, we embrace the first: Abraham was born in his
father Terah’s seventieth year, rejecting the latter view of the 130th.

According to Scaliger, all the ancients and all Protestants before Beroaldus,
a professor at Geneva, stand for the former view. Beroaldus drew to the
contrary view after himself—not Beza, but the great Calvin, whose lead was
followed by Martyr, Musculus, Junius, Pareus, Diodati, Rivet, Ussher, the
three Cappels, and others, all eminent men. Among Roman Catholics Lyra,
Cajetan, Salian, and others hold the same, especially Torniellus, who defends
it most fully and industriously. On our side stand all the Christian Fathers,
Latin, Greek, and Jewish, who take Genesis 11:26 of Abraham’s birth; among
more recent writers, Luther, Joseph Scaliger, and those cited above; and among
Roman Catholics, Tostatus, who handles it fully and accurately, and from him
Pererius, Petavius, Cornelius, and Lorinus. Setting authorities aside, let us
weigh each side’s reasons.

Our first argument is that Moses expressly teaches at Genesis 11:26 that
Terah begot Abraham in his seventieth year; this is a clear foundation of
faith from divine Scripture. Torniellus answers—and others borrow it from
him—that Augustine, *Questions on Genesis* 26, interprets the place like
Genesis 5:32: Noah at five hundred begot Shem, Ham, and Japheth. There Shem,
though named first, is not understood to be Noah’s eldest or born precisely
that year; rather Noah then began to beget his three sons, of whom Shem was
the worthiest and therefore named first, though born a year or two after the
elder Japheth. Similarly Terah at seventy began to beget three sons, of whom
Abraham, as the worthiest, is placed first though not born first. Haran,
Sarah’s father, must have been older, since Sarah his daughter was only ten
years younger than Abraham. If Haran were younger than Abraham, he must have
begotten Sarah or Iscah in his eighth or at most ninth year, too absurd for
anyone to admit.

Reply: 1. Torniellus and his followers misreport Augustine’s view. Augustine
never affirmed Abraham was born in his father’s 130th year, but repeatedly
says the seventieth—both in the cited place, throughout *City of God* book 16,
chapters 12–17 where he treats the question fully, and elsewhere. He does
offer various solutions by others to the present difficulty, of which
Torniellus’s is one; but it is no more Augustine’s own than the preceding
answer, the second one of the Jews and Jerome. Augustine nowhere embraces
it, though he calls it not contemptible. His own answer, in which he rests
and with which he begins and ends the cited place, is recapitulation: he
defends two departures by Abraham into Canaan, the first in his seventy-fifth
year before his father’s death, the second in his 135th after it.

Second, if Genesis 11:26 parallels 5:32, our case is made. For in 1659
Torniellus expressly declared, after Cajetan and others, that Augustine’s and
his own view is that Shem was Noah’s firstborn; by that parallel Abraham must
likewise be firstborn.

Third, the claim that Abraham was younger than Haran because Haran’s daughter
Iscah was Abraham’s wife Sarah and only ten years younger lacks foundation.
As the Jesuit Coquaeus shows on *City of God* 16.19, many ancient and recent
writers hold another view. However scholars vary here, Scripture is clear at
Genesis 20:12: Sarai was the daughter not of his brother Haran but of his
father Terah. Lest anyone escape by explaining “daughter” as a relative or
granddaughter, the clause “not the daughter of my mother” was added to rule
out every such exposition. The Roman Catholic objection that marriage with a
brother’s daughters was lawful but not with a father’s daughters or sisters
is made to excuse papal dispensations for princes’ incestuous marriages. In
Abraham’s day the law on marriage conditions was not yet given, but after
Moses marriages with both nieces and sisters

[Printed p. 32 | IA leaf 62 | access PDF 62 | OCR object 61]

were forbidden, and the popes dispense with the former no less than the
latter. By papal authority and solely for the Roman curia’s convenience, the
king of Spain recently married his sister’s daughter, as the queen of Poland
married two uterine brothers. Thus that “lawless man” dares in broad daylight
to trample every divine and human law.

Fourth, Genesis 5:32 and 11:26 differ greatly. Opponents say Noah begot his
three sons within two years, but teach that at least sixty years intervened
between Terah’s sons’ births—an enormous discrepancy.

Fifth, granted that Abraham was Terah’s youngest son, nothing excludes his
birth from the father’s seventieth year, for even on that assumption Abraham
could have been born then and his elder brothers much earlier.

My second argument: unless Moses’ words mean Abraham was born in his father’s
seventieth year, Abraham’s birth is left uncertain. Nahor or Haran will be
said to have been born in Terah’s seventieth; Moses will not say when Abraham
was born; sacred chronology will break off at Abraham and be corrupted by an
addition of sixty years to Moses. Torniellus—Pareus, Rivet, and others add
little or nothing—answers that nothing is added to chronological calculation,
but sixty years wrongly expunged are properly recovered. Moses is not silent
about Abraham’s birth year: though he does not speak of it here, he
sufficiently indicates it elsewhere through clear consequences.

Reply: 1. An admitted addition of sixty years to the received computation
makes a great chronological change. All that many learned men have adduced
from eclipses, ancient observations of celestial phenomena, or jubilee
reckoning to confirm or illustrate sacred chronology is fundamentally
overthrown. The addition should not be admitted unless we are compelled by
greater necessity than the arguments so far display.

Second, Moses’ exact genealogies from Adam to Abraham and for some time after
are made through the birth dates of the patriarchs in Christ’s direct line.
If Terah’s seventieth year refers not to Abraham’s generation but Nahor’s or
Haran’s, the whole method of Mosaic chronology changes in Abraham’s very
person. More than for any patriarch, Moses’ goal and design is to reveal his
generation and years so that the course of time and Christ’s line flow
clearly. It matters nothing to Christ or the church in what year of Terah
Haran, father of the Moabites, or Nahor, father of the Assyrian family, was
born.

Third, Moses relates every other patriarch’s birth in plain, express words,
not leaving it to be investigated through the winding paths of inference.
Abraham’s close and eminent relation to the covenant of grace, God’s people,
and Christ does not permit stripping him of this prerogative.

Their nauseating repeated reply about Shem, as just said, does not solve the
argument. 1. If Shem was Noah’s firstborn, as many maintain, the whole
question disappears. 2. Even if he was two years younger than Japheth, there
is a vast difference between Shem’s two years and Abraham’s sixty. 3. Those
who make Shem younger than Japheth profess to do so by express Scriptures,
not inferences. 4. The inferences that allegedly prove Abraham was born in
Terah’s 130th are remote and, as Torniellus admits, obscure; Pererius says
they are false and prove nothing.

Torniellus offers various arguments for the other view. 1. Terah lived 205
years and died at Haran; Abraham, after his father died, departed Haran for
Canaan in his seventy-fifth year. Abraham’s seventy-fifth therefore
corresponded to his father’s last, the 205th, and Abraham was born in his
father’s 130th, seventy-five years before the 205th when he died. Answer:
Torniellus and his followers triumph in this as an invincible demonstration,
but Pererius and all who follow Augustine answer readily enough. The whole
force of the argument rests on Abraham’s not departing for Canaan before his
father’s death. Moses indeed relates Terah’s death at the end of Genesis 11
and Abraham’s departure at the beginning of 12, but it does not follow that
the departure followed the death. Sacred history frequently relates later
events first, as these very passages show. Genesis 11 begins with Babel’s
tower, built in Peleg’s days long before Joktan’s second generation, though
the latter is described in chapter 10. Indeed, the divine command to Abraham
to leave his homeland Ur of the Chaldeans and his father’s house for Canaan,
though set out in chapter 12 after Terah’s death, long preceded the departure
from Chaldea recorded in chapter 11.

[Printed p. 33 | IA leaf 63 | access PDF 63 | OCR object 62]

What they urge beyond Moses’ history is Stephen’s explicit statement in Acts
7:4: *“After his father’s death God moved him into Canaan.”* No Christian was
ever so impious as to dispute Stephen’s words, but Augustine and many
excellent theologians, ancient and modern, denied there was only one
departure by Abraham from Haran to Canaan. By making several, they seem to
themselves to remove the whole otherwise enormous difficulty. According to
Moses, they say, Abraham left Haran for Canaan at seventy-five, sixty years
before his father’s death, but returned from time to time to visit him. When
his father died he left Haran, never to see it again, and departed for Canaan
to remain there, never again crossing the river to Mesopotamia.

Opponents answer that these are dreams supported by no Scripture. Reply: they
do not deny that, if true, they sufficiently solve the difficulty; nor do
they assert anything making them impossible. Whether they actually occurred
depends on the preceding hypothesis. Given that Abraham was born in Terah’s
seventieth, Terah lived 205, and Abraham went to Canaan at seventy-five, he
must have gone sixty full years before his father’s death. Since Stephen says
he departed thither after his father’s death, one must also hold that he
returned to Haran at least once to visit his father, and thence after his
death returned to Canaan. Nothing here is absurd; indeed, it is necessary for
a proper reconciliation of Stephen with Moses.

That Moses makes no express mention of these journeys accords with his manner
and custom. Although Noah, Shem, and all the other patriarchs after the Flood
lived many years with Abraham and, as seems most certain, sometimes paid one
another many visits, Moses mentions none. Isaac after his marriage lived
simultaneously with Abraham in different regions for more than thirty years,
and Isaac’s sons for many more; yet Moses nowhere mentions Abraham visiting
his children or his children their father before they came to Hebron for his
burial. The same applies to Jacob and Esau. They lived some years before
Abraham’s death and many before Isaac’s; did they never approach their
father’s or grandfather’s homes, or did the father and grandfather never
come to theirs? Moses certainly mentions no such journey except Jacob’s one
departure to visit Isaac shortly before his death.

Torniellus’s second argument is that Terah died before Abraham’s
seventy-fifth, because in Genesis 22, when Abraham returned from attempting
to sacrifice Isaac in his own 125th year, fifty years after first leaving
Haran, a messenger met him reporting as unheard news that eight sons had been
born to Nahor. Abraham therefore had not revisited Haran during those fifty
years, which would have been impossible in a pious son if his father Terah
still lived there. Answer: great change often comes to families in a few
years. Nahor’s eight sons could be born in eight years; Jacob certainly had
eleven children in almost six years in the same place. Abraham could have
revisited his father repeatedly within those fifty years before the eight
were born to Nahor. We have already noted how few visits among the patriarchs
are recorded in Scripture. One journey by Abraham from Canaan to Haran is
enough to preserve his opponents’ hypothesis.

We would be more pressed had Torniellus added—though he did not—that according
to Pererius Abraham must have been in Haran in Isaac’s thirty-fifth year to
bury his father. Nahor and his sons would then have learned sufficiently from
Abraham about his household affairs. Why only five years later did Abraham’s
servant Eliezer need so anxiously to instruct Bethuel and Laban about
Abraham’s riches and his son Isaac as unknown matters? Yet I answer: who
knows what Abraham discussed with Nahor when he last came to Haran to bury his
father? Whatever Abraham’s relatives at Haran knew of his condition, when
Eliezer sought a wife for Isaac there, it was useful for winning Rebekah to
so long a journey to recount Abraham’s and Isaac’s wealth fully. Bethuel,
Nahor’s eighth and youngest son, could have been sufficiently ignorant of it,
whatever Nahor himself and his elder sons knew.

Torniellus’s third argument is the testimony of Chrysostom and Theodoret.
Answer: 1. If Torniellus wished to stand by Greek or Latin patristic testimony,
his case would certainly fall. These two are far too few to sustain the whole
crowd who affirm Abraham was born in his father’s seventieth year. 2. In his
genuine writings Theodoret says nothing about this, although he raises many
less serious questions on Genesis in texts both preceding

[Printed p. 34 | IA leaf 64 | access PDF 64 | OCR object 63]

and following the present one. It is true that in a Latin chain of Greek
Fathers published at Cologne in 1572, under the name “Theod.,” the question
of Terah’s seventieth year is solved by making Abraham Terah’s last rather
than firstborn. How little this bears on the question was said above. For now
I answer that “Theod.” is by no means Theodoret but Theodore; who he was, what
authority he had, or when he lived, I do not know. The chain there appends a
very different solution by a certain Diodorus, making Abraham’s departure to
Canaan at seventy-five long precede Terah’s death. Whoever “Theod.” and
“Diod.” in the chain were, I think no weight should be given their testimony.

As for Chrysostom, he so neglects our question that in his homilies on Genesis
he wholly omits the text itself, all of 11:26, and though he writes on Acts
7:4, he does not touch the matter there by a word. I admit that on Genesis
11’s last verse he raises and solves the question by saying Abraham did not
come to Canaan *until after* his father died. But he adds “until after” to
Stephen’s words, which say nothing of the kind. If I said Chrysostom did this
carelessly and ἀμελῶς, I would use his own word there, though in a different
sense. Chrysostom certainly says there what opponents here would, I think,
unwillingly endorse: that Terah came to Haran with Abraham moved by no faith
in the divine promises—for he calls him ἄπιστον, unbelieving—but solely by
the charm of fatherly love for his son. This judgment against Terah is too
harsh.

Torniellus’s fourth argument: unless Abraham’s birth is moved sixty full years
after Terah’s seventieth and farther from the Flood than 492 years, mankind
could not have grown by the reign of Ninus into a multitude sufficient for
the armies historians say were led in that age. Answer: more than enough was
said above about this argument.

His fifth: it is incredible that Abraham would wish, from fatherly affection
or any other cause, to return to live in the region from which the Lord had
called him. Answer: no one says Abraham returned to reside in Mesopotamia,
but only that business took him there whenever he wished. God’s call to
Canaan did not prevent that.

Sixth: if for any reason Abraham ever traveled from Canaan to Mesopotamia, he
would have given his descendants a pernicious example of moving their homes
from Canaan, the promised land, to Mesopotamia, from which they had been
called by divine command. Answer: a business journey is not a change of
residence. Jacob, for whom Canaan was no less the promised land than for
Abraham, did not hesitate, with his father Isaac’s good grace, to journey to
Mesopotamia for business and remain there many years.

Seventh: had Abraham ever wished to return to Mesopotamia, he would have done
so during the famine, when he descended into Egypt at great risk to himself
and Sarah. Answer: in famines Egypt was always the usual granary for all its
neighbors, while Chaldea enjoyed no such privilege. But Torniellus’s
foolishness wearies me; nothing in it has the appearance of solid reason.

### QUESTION IV

#### How the Israelites sojourned in Egypt for 430 full years and yet for
only four generations

Fourth it is asked how in Genesis 17:12 the sojourn of Abraham and his seed is
foretold as four hundred years, when elsewhere it is usually called 430; and
how in the same place the return from Egypt is promised in the fourth
generation, when above we counted eight or ten generations in Joseph’s family
within those limits. Answer to the first: every calculation varies with its
endpoints. When 430 years are assigned to the Hebrews’ sojourn, they begin at
Abraham’s first departure from Ur of the Chaldeans, or at least from Haran in
Mesopotamia into Canaan. But Abraham’s deep sleep and ecstasy in Genesis 15
occurred after that journey. Moreover, Holy Scripture not infrequently
includes an excess or deficiency of some years, much less months, within a
round number. Thus a round calculation of four hundred years does not exclude
thirty more, or another larger or smaller excess or deficiency.

To the latter: a generation or age is commonly defined as a hundred years,
so what occurred after four hundred years is rightly said to have happened
in the fourth generation. Moreover, although eight or more degrees are
counted in some families during that span, in other no less illustrious
families scarcely more than four appear, at least from entrance to departure.
Hezron, son of Perez and grandson of Judah, begot Ram in Egypt; Ram begot
Amminadab, he Nahshon, and he Salmon, Rahab’s husband and David’s
great-grandfather.

[Printed p. 35 | IA leaf 65 | access PDF 65 | OCR object 64]

Levi likewise begot Kohath shortly before the entrance; Kohath begot Amram,
and Amram Moses. Here from entrance to exodus you have only two generations,
for only Amram and Moses were born in Egypt. Indeed, if you exclude the
endpoints and count the generations exclusively from Isaac himself to Moses,
you find only four names: Jacob, Levi, Kohath, and Amram.

### Additions to Question IV

#### Against Vossius: the Israelites did not dwell in Egypt more than 215
years

I would have added nothing here had Isaac Vossius not recently added this to
his other assaults on sacred chronology: under the name of his great father,
in a very lengthy and rather sharp disputation, he contended that two hundred
whole years must here be added to the common calculation. Not content openly
and deliberately to corrupt, contrary to his father’s express mind, Moses’
sacrosanct calculation both before and after the Flood by adding no fewer than
1,440 years to the common era before Abraham’s birth, he proceeds through his
father’s posthumous *Isagoge*, which he edited, to violate the most sacred
bounds of biblical chronology by adding several more centuries. He inserts
130 years for the inserted Cainan; by moving Abraham’s birth from his
father’s seventieth year to the 130th he seems to gain sixty; still unsated
with additions, from Jacob’s descent into Egypt to the exodus he counts no
fewer than 430 years—more than two hundred beyond the limit—against the view
of all ancient Jews and Christians, all Protestants, and almost every Roman
Catholic chronologer and interpreter of Scripture. In the following epoch,
against express Scripture at 1 Kings 6:1, which with boldness wholly unlike
the living Vossius’s modesty he therefore corrects as corrupt contrary to
every codex, he pronounces 580 years elapsed from the exodus to the temple’s
foundation.

We have weighed the first four paradoxes, and will soon address the sixth; we
now take up the fifth. Let us first examine the arguments for his new view,
then his answers to the foundations of the ancient and common view he
attacks. Nothing else in that long dissertation, expanded with many words,
bears on the case.

His first argument is from Genesis 15:13, Exodus 12:40, Acts 7:6, and 13:16.
These testimonies, he says, speak so plainly only of dwelling in Egypt, not
Canaan, that someone may perhaps evade but cannot overthrow them. Answer: it
is harsh to make every ancient and modern interpreter of these places an
evader of Holy Scripture so that Vossius’s novel exposition may stand. He
borrowed it from Genebrardus, a man more learned than either sound or upright,
and against the common view it has to this day appeared only in Steuchus,
Eugubinus, and some Thaddaeus Dunius I do not know. All other learned men
approved Augustine’s exposition. At *City of God* 16.16 and again more fully
at 16.24, and *Questions on Exodus* 3.47, Augustine refers the sojourn to both
Canaan and Egypt, but the slavery and affliction to Egypt alone; the number
of years to all three circumstances, beginning in sojourning and ending in
affliction. To restrict their beginning to the last circumstance, affliction,
or the second, slavery, excluding the first endpoint of sojourning, would be
to twist the words toward a false sense. Some interval undoubtedly passed
between sojourning and slavery, and between affliction and slavery; assigning
430 years equally to the middle and last as to the first does not agree with
the truth of the events.

Hear Augustine himself, *City of God* 16.24: *What was said to Abraham—“know
for certain that your seed will be a stranger in a land not its own, and they
will reduce them to slavery and afflict them four hundred years”—most plainly
prophesied about the people Israel who would serve in Egypt. This does not
mean that the people would spend four hundred years in that slavery under
afflicting Egyptians, but that within those four hundred years it was
foretold this would occur. For just as it is written of Terah, Abraham’s
father, “and his days in Haran were 205 years,” not because they were all
spent there but because they were completed there, so here the words “and
they will reduce them to slavery and afflict them four hundred years” were
inserted because this number was completed in that affliction, not because it
was entirely spent there. From Abraham’s seventieth year, when the first
promise was made to him, to Israel’s exodus from Egypt are counted 430 years,
which the Apostle recalls thus: “Now I say this: the covenant confirmed by God
is not invalidated, so as to make the promise void, by the law made 430 years
later.”*

Here Augustine expounds Scripture by other Scriptures of both Testaments. In
chapter 16 he professes to have learned this from Eusebius of Caesarea.
Jerome’s commentary on Galatians 3 certainly follows the same course; indeed,

[Printed p. 36 | IA leaf 66 | access PDF 66 | OCR object 65]

Vossius himself admits at page 95 that a vast host of Greek and Latin Fathers
follow this view. With George Syncellus in Scaliger’s notes on Eusebius, page
17, he could have said κατὰ πάντας τοὺς ἑρμηνεύσαντας, ἢ ἱστορήσαντας,
“according to all interpreters and chronologers or historians.”

Vossius wrongly ascribes this agreement of the Fathers to their reverence for
the Septuagint. The latter indeed illustrates Genesis with a right and clear
paraphrase: ἡ δὲ κατοίκησις τῶν υἱῶν Ἰσραὴλ ἣν κατῴκησαν αὐτοὶ, καὶ οἱ
πατέρες αὐτῶν ἐν γῇ Αἰγύπτῳ, καὶ ἐν γῇ Χαναὰν, αὐτοὶ καὶ οἱ πατέρες αὐτῶν
τετρακόσια τριάκοντα ἔτη—that is, *“the dwelling of the children of Israel,
which they and their fathers dwelt in the land of Egypt and in the land of
Canaan, they and their fathers, was 430 years.”* This authority confirmed the
Fathers in the true view, but, as we saw in Augustine, they placed its
foundation in the authentic Scriptures alone.

Against this universal, or at least common, exposition of the Fathers,
Vossius objects that Moses records a sojourn in Egypt alone, not Canaan,
because it was to be in a foreign land not their own, whereas Canaan was the
native homeland and possession of Abraham’s seed. Answer: this is said very
inconsiderately against express Scripture. Before the inheritance in
Joshua’s day, Canaan was more foreign than Egypt to Abraham and his
descendants. Of Canaan Paul says in Acts 7:5: *“He gave him no inheritance in
it, not even a foot’s breadth, and promised to give it to him and to his seed
after him, though he had no son.”* Hebrews 11: *“By faith he sojourned in the
land of promise as in a foreign country, dwelling in tents with Isaac and
Jacob”;* and verse 13, *“confessing that they were strangers and guests upon
the earth.”*

As for Egypt, although God never intended to settle the people there as their
inheritance, he obtained for them an excellent right to Goshen. A king’s
simple grant establishes good title to granted lands, especially if founded
on distinguished service. Such was Israel’s condition: for Joseph’s
extraordinary prudence in saving Egypt from ruin, Pharaoh gave him and his
people possession of Goshen, Genesis 47:11: *“Joseph gave his father and
brothers a possession in Egypt in the best part of the land, as Pharaoh had
commanded.”* Jacob rested there in perfect peace until death, professing that
all his previous days in Canaan or elsewhere had been mere and very painful
sojourning, verse 10: *“The days of the sojourning of my life are 130 years,
few and evil, and have not reached the days of my fathers in which they
sojourned.”*

Vossius’s second objection against the Fathers is that their interpretation
of the 430 years includes Abraham’s own sojourn, whereas Moses speaks of the
sojourn of Abraham’s seed and excludes him. Answer: 1. What is true of
Abraham’s seed is rightly attributed to Abraham himself. God promised to give
him Canaan in possession and to his seed after him, yet neither gave nor
promised it to Abraham in possession except in the persons of his descendants
four centuries later. 2. The Fathers rightly distinguish Abraham’s sojourn
from that of his descendants. Places giving four hundred years they explain
only of the descendants—Isaac, the promised seed, and those following—but
places giving 430 they understand of Abraham’s own sojourn from Ur, thirty
years before Isaac’s birth. 3. Vossius’s exposition excludes from the 430 not
only Abraham but Isaac’s whole life and Jacob’s sojourn until his 130th year;
from the slavery it excludes all Jacob’s descendants until Levi’s death,
more than ninety years after their entrance into Egypt.

Third, Vossius objects that the Fathers’ exposition contradicts the express
words of Exodus 12:40: *“The dwelling of the children of Israel who remained
in Egypt was 430 years.”* Answer: Holy Scripture should not be handled with
captious hands. Are those words more express about dwelling in Egypt than
Acts 7:6, *“and they will mistreat them for four hundred years”*? Vossius is
himself forced at page 79 to interpret the latter synecdochically, not of that
whole time but part of it, and to criticize those who take all those years as
slavery and extend the dwelling beyond five hundred.

Vossius’s second argument for his view comes from Judith 5, where Achior says
the Israelites dwelt in Egypt four hundred years. Answer: against
Genebrardus, Vossius admits this argument’s lightness, not only because Judith
is apocryphal and the speaker an Ammonite, Achior, ignorant of Jewish affairs,
whose false account cannot prejudice divine truth, but especially because
the Greek original says nothing about the time; the addition of four hundred
years comes from the Latin version alone. 3. Even if all this were passed
over, a suitable exposition would still be required, for the words speak

[Printed p. 37 | IA leaf 67 | access PDF 67 | OCR object 66]

of the dwelling of Israel’s children. If one distinguishes Israel from his
sons and excludes his own seventeen years in Egypt from their dwelling, the
words rigidly taken are false, for seventeen are missing from four hundred.

Vossius’s third argument is from Israel’s multiplication while in Egypt: they
could not grow to six hundred thousand men unless they remained there at
least four hundred years. Answer: Vossius admits this argument of
Genebrardus’s has no weight and rejects it.

The fourth is Josephus’s testimony, *Antiquities* 2.9, affirming that the
Israelites were afflicted in Egypt four hundred years. Answer: Vossius admits
Genebrardus labors here in vain, for here, as often elsewhere, Josephus’s
number is corrupted by copyists. This is clear from chapter 13, where he has
430 years from Abraham’s sojourn to the exodus, but only 215 from Jacob’s
descent into Egypt. As for other Jewish authors, from all their rabbinic
reading neither Genebrardus nor Vossius can produce one on their side, while
they grant us many.

### For our view

Our first argument is from Galatians 3:16–17: *“The promises were spoken to
Abraham and his seed; but the law made 430 years after the covenant confirmed
by God does not make it void.”* The beginning which the Apostle places in the
first promises made to Abraham about Christ the blessed seed cannot be set at
the Israelites’ descent into Egypt, for the first such promise long preceded
it. The first promises in Genesis 12 were spoken to Abraham when, aged
seventy, he left Ur for Haran in Mesopotamia, where he lived five years. He
left at seventy-five. From then to Isaac’s birth in Abraham’s hundredth year,
Genesis 21:5, are twenty-five years; to Jacob’s birth in Isaac’s sixtieth,
sixty; then to Jacob’s descent into Egypt and appearance before Pharaoh at
130, Genesis 47:9, another 130. These total 220, which subtracted from 490
leave 210 during which Jacob and his sons remained in Egypt.

No proper doubt can be raised against the major premise except about the
first five years. Since Augustine interprets Genesis 12:1 by *hysteron
proteron* of promises made before Abraham reached Haran, they must seemingly
have been made in his seventieth year, five years before he left Haran. I do
not wish to quarrel with great men over minutiae; the present question
concerns more than two hundred years, within which a dispute over five is
easily absorbed.

There should be still less controversy over the minor—that the Apostle began
the 430 in the time of the first promises to Abraham—for his words expressly
carry this meaning.

Vossius denies the minor. The promises the Apostle recalls, he says, are not
the first in Genesis 12 but the last made to Jacob at Beersheba as he traveled
toward Egypt, Genesis 46:30, from which he wishes to count 430 to the exodus.
Reply: 1. This invention belongs to Vossius alone against all mortals,
certainly against every Father and Protestant writer by his own admission,
and, I think, every Roman Catholic. Here Vossius produces from his own brain
various things which Genebrardus and the very few Roman Catholics who guided
him never seem to have dreamed. 2. It openly contradicts the Apostle, who
speaks expressly of promises spoken to Abraham, whereas Vossius denies his
words can be understood of the days of Abraham, Isaac, or Jacob before his
last journey to Egypt at 130. 3. The Apostle expressly speaks of new-covenant
promises which the law could not void and of the one seed who was Christ. But
the promises made to Jacob at Beersheba were not such; they said nothing of
the covenant or Christ, but were particular consolations to Jacob about God’s
presence with him in Egypt, Joseph’s care for his burial, and his
descendants’ return from Egypt to Canaan. 4. Vossius admits that only 407, not
430 years, passed from the promises at Beersheba. He therefore flees to
another beginning which he professes is his own invention, touched by no one
before: the 430 years Moses and the Apostle record must begin not with Jacob’s
descent into Egypt but Joseph’s, twenty-three years before. Once the gate of
invention opens to fertile and fluid minds, there is no end to novel fictions.

Fourth, this very recent invention of Vossius

[Printed p. 38 | IA leaf 68 | access PDF 68 | OCR object 67]

agrees with neither Scripture nor right reason. 1. The promises to Abraham
which the Apostle makes the beginning of the 430 years in Galatians 3:17 were
confirmed in Christ and made to the blessed seed; those made to Jacob at
Bethel, Genesis 35:9, say nothing of Christ. 2. Vossius assumes Joseph’s sale
into Egypt occurred in the same year Jacob moved from Shechem to Bethlehem;
he needed to prove, not assert, this. 3. The descent into Egypt of which the
Apostle speaks was not of one Israelite alone, whereas Joseph was only one.
Finally, Joseph’s descent cannot begin the 430, for by Vossius’s admission no
more than 423 years passed from it to the exodus. His appeal to round and
exact numbers is inopportune. By his own rule a smaller number added to a
larger is exact: thus four hundred alone might be explained as 430 or a few
more or fewer, but when thirty or any smaller number is added, the total is
not rounded but exact. Since by no effort can Vossius explain 430 as exact,
his calculation plainly cannot be right.

Vossius presents this second argument for our view: the generations Moses
records in various families from Israel’s entrance into Egypt to the exodus
are too few to exhaust 430 years. In Levi’s family only two generations were
born in Egypt: Kohath, born in Canaan, begot Amram in Egypt, and Amram Moses.
In Reuben’s tribe Phallu, born in Canaan, begot Eliab in Egypt, and he Dathan
and Abiram, Numbers 26:8. In Judah’s family Hezron son of Perez, born in
Canaan, begot Jephunneh in Egypt, and Jephunneh Caleb, who left Egypt aged
forty. In that age no two or even three generations could reach 430 years.

Vossius answers with three paradoxes, to call them nothing worse. 1. Kohath
begot Amram long after one hundred, and Amram likewise Moses long after his
hundredth. But suppose Kohath entered Egypt aged one, begot Amram at one
hundred, Amram begot Moses at one hundred, and the exodus occurred in Moses’
eightieth: together they do not approach 430 but only 280. Let us even grant
that Kohath begot Amram at the end of his life, 133, and Amram begot Moses at
his own extreme age of 137: these, 133, 137, and eighty, total only 350,
lacking seventy of the required 430. Some of Vossius’s postulates and our
concessions are quite gratuitous, if not absurd. It is incredible that both
Kohath and Amram begot at their life’s end above 130 when long before it was
regarded as a miracle that Abraham begot at one hundred. Vossius replies the
miracle lay not in Abraham’s age but Sarah’s age and barrenness, expressly
contrary to Romans 4:19: *“He did not consider his own body now dead, when he
was about a hundred years old.”* The Apostle places Abraham’s strong faith
and hope against hope in expecting a son from his dead body from the God who
raises the dead and calls things that are not as though they were.

Vossius’s second answer is no better: Moses omitted many generations in his
genealogies, so though Levi is said to beget Kohath, Kohath Amram, and Amram
Moses, several generations may intervene. He says the same of Judah’s,
Reuben’s, and other lines. Reply: this assertion is far too rash and greatly
enervates Scripture and all sacred chronology. What certainty could be drawn
from any scriptural genealogy if one may allege various degrees omitted even
in the principal genealogy of Christ himself in Judah’s tribe?

What he says of Matthew’s omissions is irrelevant, for other Scriptures fully
supply them. But to omit various degrees from principal genealogies which no
Scripture supplies is extremely harsh, especially when invented to save the
false hypothesis of Israel’s 430-year stay in Egypt. Embrace Scripture’s true
calculation, that Israel dwelt there only 210 years, and every such difficulty
and absurdity is avoided.

Vossius’s third answer is still more absurd: various persons said to descend
with Jacob into Egypt were not yet born, but born there much later. Thus
Manasseh and Ephraim were not only born in Egypt but

[Printed p. 39 | IA leaf 69 | access PDF 69 | OCR object 68]

born there after Jacob’s descent, expressly against Moses at Genesis 41:50.
He says the same of Perez’s sons Hezron and Hamul: although Moses says they
descended with Jacob, Vossius affirms they were born in Egypt much later. He
does not hesitate to say this of Benjamin’s ten sons, all of whom Moses names
as descending with Jacob; Vossius allows Benjamin no children then, when he
was only twenty-three, and says he did not marry until seventeen years after
the descent, at forty. I wonder what came into this great man’s mind that he
heaped so many open contradictions of Scripture into one place. I will gladly
excuse the father if his son Isaac accepts the blame, for the editor rather
than the celebrated author must answer for errors in a posthumous work.

As for Perez’s sons, I admit Vossius follows Pererius on Genesis 46, and the
very learned Rivet holds the same there; Augustine preceded all at *City of
God* 16.40. But Augustine’s hypothesis is surely too harsh: Jacob’s entrance
into Egypt must extend until Joseph’s death because Joseph caused Jacob’s
descent. On that principle not merely seventy souls but seventy thousand
could be said to have descended with Jacob, for more seem to have descended
from Jacob before Joseph’s death.

Augustine fell into this grave difficulty because he wished to defend the
Greek translator’s manifest error expressly against the Hebrew truth and
even the Latin translator. The Greek not only makes seventy-five descend with
Jacob, as Stephen also professes in Acts 7 and as can be reconciled in various
ways mentioned elsewhere, but in enumerating persons names Machir, Gilead,
Shuthelah, and other grandsons and great-grandsons of Joseph, which Augustine
should not have defended.

As for Perez’s sons, although it is very difficult to show how Judah, only
four years older than Joseph before the descent into Egypt, could have
great-grandsons, nothing absurd or seriously inconvenient lies in Pareus’s
chronology: Judah, Er, Onan, and Perez each married in his fourteenth year.
Those asking a much greater postulate—that Levi, Kohath, and Amram each begot
children after one hundred—should not deny it.

Finally, on Israel’s children multiplying until Egypt’s king feared they
might join his enemies, Vossius answers that Egypt then had many kings, not
one, and Moses speaks of only one of its many dynasts. This conjecture is not
only groundless but contrary to Moses’ entire history. Did famine not invade
all Egypt? Were granaries not placed throughout it? Was all Egypt not
acquired for Pharaoh, while all Egypt’s priests were freed from taxes? The
particular dynasties clearly coalesced into one kingdom long before Pharaoh’s
and Moses’ times.
