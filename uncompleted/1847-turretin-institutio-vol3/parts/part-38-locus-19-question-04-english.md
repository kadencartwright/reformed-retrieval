QUESTION IV.

ON THE SACRAMENTAL UNION.

Is the essential and internal form of the sacraments situated in the relation
of the sign to the thing signified, and in their merely relational union? We
affirm against the papists.

I. Just as the matter of the sacraments is twofold, one external, which has the
character of the sign, and the other internal, which is the thing signified,
so their form consists in the relation and disposition of the one toward the
other. The question is instituted concerning this form so that its true nature
may be known over against the errors of the papists.

[Marginalia: In what the form of the sacraments is situated.]

II. Before all else, however, it must be observed that the form in question is
not the external form, which belongs to the sacraments in an exercised act, or
in their very use, and which consists both in their lawful administration and
in participation in them according to God’s command. Rather, it is the
internal form, which belongs to them in a signified act and is situated in the
analogy, or relation, of the sign to the thing signified. In a word, this
relation is signification, since the whole character of a sign consists in
signifying.

[Marginalia: In practical signification.]

But this signification is not only theoretical, which is nothing other than a
representation or declaration of the promise of grace and the benefits of
Christ and is founded upon the analogy and likeness that exists between the
sign and the thing signified. It is also practical, by which the external
thing, dispensed with certain rites, functions as a seal of the divine
promises and therefore of the conferral and exhibition of the things promised.
In a word, it is the sealing of the covenant of grace and the gospel promises,
and of the things contained in them. From this use the sacraments are called
“seals” (Rom. 4:11).

[Marginalia: From this arises the sacramental union, which is relational and
moral.]

III. From this sealing arises the sacramental union of the sign with the thing
signified. It is not a physical union, such as that of matter and form or of a
subject and an accident; nor a local union through contact and the absence of
distance; nor a spiritual union in which the power of justifying and
regenerating is immediately instilled into the signs themselves. Rather, it is
entirely relational and relative, or moral, like the relation between the
rites of investiture and the fief with which someone is invested, or between
earnest money and the thing signified by the earnest. In the same way, things
signified are joined to the words by which they are signified not by natural
position and their very substance, but only by a relation and disposition,
insofar as those words, as signs of things, set the things themselves as though
present before the understanding of those who perceive the words. Thus also a
sacrament, which is therefore called a certain visible word because it sets
before the eyes what words impress upon the ears, requires no local presence
or inherence of the sign with the thing signified. It conveys only the relation
and disposition of one toward the other.

[Marginalia: But also real.]

IV. Yet that union is moral and relative in such a way that, in its own sense,
it is also real in lawful use—not by the sign and thing signified being without
distance, but with respect to the communicant, who becomes a partaker of both
at once. To this belong the phrases “to be planted together with Christ in
baptism” (Rom. 6), “to put him on” (Gal. 3:17), and “to eat Christ and drink
his blood.” To this also belong other expressions of theologians who say that
this union and participation in Christ in the Supper takes place substantially
and corporally. There those terms are partly opposed to what occurs

fantastically and imaginatively, and partly declare that believers become
partakers of the very substance of Christ, insofar as they must be truly and
really united with him.

[Marginalia: From this presence depends.]

V. Furthermore, from the reality of this union flows the presence by which,
together with the signs, the things signified are also made present to those
who use them lawfully. This presence is not corporal and local, as though the
things signified cohered with the signs without distance, for such a presence
is fictitious and has no foundation in Scripture. Rather, it is relative and
moral, insofar as the things signified are present through their signs, whose
nature is to cause something else to come to mind and therefore to set the
thing before the senses or the mind. And it is real, whereby the things
signified are made present to those who use them lawfully: namely, Christ
gives his whole self to believers to embrace. This is the presence of faith.

VI. Although this spiritual presence does not occur except through faith, it
does not follow, as our adversaries falsely object, that it is only imaginary
and fantastic, whereby not so much Christ as a phantom or image of him is made
present to us. For spiritual things are no less real and true than bodily
things; indeed, the more perfect they are, the more perfect a mode of presence
they ought to possess. I acknowledge that among those for whom faith is only
historical and consists in bare knowledge of the object, that presence can be
called less real. Yet not even in that case is presence denied by philosophers,
according to whom whatever is perceived by the intellect can be said to be
truly present to it. But Scripture describes faith and the presence it effects
far differently when it designates that presence by sight, hearing, and touch;
by approach, reception, and eating. All these argue not for a fantastic
presence that takes place only through phantasms or imaginary notions and
ideas, but for a true and real presence. Otherwise our union with Christ,
which takes place through faith (Eph. 3:17), would be merely fantastic, which
it is impious even to think.

[Marginalia: The union is proved to be merely relative.]

VII. Concerning this union it is asked whether it is purely and merely
relational and relative, or also physical and local. The papists and Lutherans
affirm the latter; we assert the former. (1) The sacramental union, which is
the form of the sacrament, ought to be of the same kind as the sacrament. But a
sacrament is merely relative, since its formal character consists in
signification. (2) This union ought to be of a kind that belongs to all the
sacraments. But no union other than a relative one can belong to the sacraments
of the Old Testament. Therefore no other can belong to the sacraments of the
New Testament. (3) If there were a union other than a relational one, ministers
would also exhibit the very thing signified, and those receiving the sign
would also receive the thing signified, which is absurd. (4) A physical union
can have no place here, both because it is impossible and because it is
useless.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. It is one thing only to signify and represent the things signified
theoretically; it is another to signify them practically. In the former sense,
the signs cannot be said to seal and exhibit the things signified. But in the
latter sense, which must be considered here, they actually seal and exhibit
them, not through coexistence and enclosure in one place, but through God’s
institution.

IX. A relational and relative union is sufficient to found the sacramental
phraseology in which names are interchanged between the sign and the thing
signified. This is because that relation does not merely signify but also
seals and applies. This occurs not only in the sacraments of the New Testament
but also in those of the Old, as when circumcision is called the covenant and
the rock is called Christ.

X. If the sacramental union is merely relational, it does not follow that the
hypostatic union ought to be of the same kind, because they differ greatly
from one another. Nor, if the Fathers sometimes compare them with one another
on account of some analogy, do they therefore intend to equate them.

XI. Although this union is not physical, it will not therefore be merely
verbal, but altogether real. This is both because it has a real foundation,
namely divine institution, and because *real* and *physical* are not
identical. There are many kinds of real union whose extremes are not together
through physical contact, such as the union of believers with Christ and with
one another.

XII. Although the sacramental form is situated in a relational analogy, it
does not follow that any water and any bread can be called a sacrament. We
must distinguish between an aptitude for signifying and actual signification.
Every bread has the former in relation to the body of Christ, but only bread
consecrated by God’s institution has the latter.
