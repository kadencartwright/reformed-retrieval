XLIII. In the same place, § 7, the *Exercitator* said that his Interpreter far
excelled all the rest and ought to be preferred to them by many parasangs,
because, while *[The others can neither certainly detect and expose the false
interpretations of others, nor investigate and demonstrate the truths of
passages of sacred Scripture, much less their true meanings, his Interpreter,
if he cannot draw out true meanings infallibly, will at least be able to draw
out truths and false expositions*

[Page 183 | ONB canvas 237 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000237]

*refute. In this way he will prevent us from assenting to erroneous
explanations as though they were genuine; and, if this has been denied with
respect to true meanings, he will at least lead us to truths—which has not
been denied—in which there is certainly the least danger, as Augustine teaches
in On Christian Doctrine, book 3, chapter 27, and The City of God, book 11,
chapter 19.]* *Wolzogen* does indeed censure him for having added that *this
Interpreter of his can draw out all the truths of Scripture*, although he had
said not that he could draw out *all* of them, but that he could draw out
*truths* indefinitely. But what is more significant, *Wolzogen* says on page
161: *No great dispute is raised about this first office of the Interpreter,
which is concerned with detecting error.* And on page 159: *[But what has the
Exercitator said here which others before him have not both said and thought?
Or what cause is there for this empty triumph? For this is the settled opinion
of all our Theologians: whatever can be proved to be error is utterly foreign
to Scripture.]* But do our Theologians say that there is no other way to
refute false expositions than by Reason or Philosophy? Cannot the circumstances
of the text itself, the purpose of the passage, and other passages of Scripture
accomplish this better? Why, then, does *Wolzogen* say that the Orthodox agree
here with *Paradox*? It is true that the Reformed say that everything proved to
be error is utterly foreign to Scripture. But they do not therefore say that
Reason alone refutes all false expositions, much less do they say that for this
reason Reason is to be preferred to all other Interpreters or placed many
parasangs ahead of them. What? Does *Wolzogen* concede that the other
Interpreters can neither certainly detect and expose the false interpretations
of others, nor investigate and demonstrate the truths of passages of
Scripture, much less their true meanings? Then it is necessary that he say
that up to this point false Interpretations have not been detected and the
true meanings of passages of Scripture have not been demonstrated or
investigated. It is also necessary

[Page 184 | ONB canvas 238 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000238]

that he maintain that the Holy Spirit not only has not yet exposed the false
interpretations of others, not yet declared the truths of passages of
Scripture, and not yet declared their true meanings, but also cannot expose,
declare, or demonstrate them. Finally, it is necessary that he say that
Philosophy can refute all false expositions of Scripture, if he does not wish
to raise a dispute with *Paradox* on this matter. But let someone show that any
of the Reformed speaks in this way, or else let him admit that he defends the
Orthodox cause badly and concedes far too much here to the adversary.

XLIV. In § 8 *Paradox* tries to prove that all the truths of a passage which
occur, or can occur, to a reader are also true meanings. He argues this from
God’s omniscience, then from his truthfulness, and then from the axiom, *It is
vain to do through more what can be done through fewer.* From this he concludes
that there are several collateral meanings of one and the same passage, and
thus that one who has drawn out even one truth has also drawn out a true
meaning. And the more truths someone draws out, the more meanings he will also
be said to draw out, and the more excellent an Interpreter he will be.
*Wolzogen* responds to these things at greater length from page 161 all the way
to page 188. It would certainly take a long time to subject everything said
here to a careful examination. It will suffice to note these few things.

1. When he said above on page 15 that *by applying all the aids to
interpretation, some meaning can also be drawn out from the mind of God*, and
thereby presupposed that there are several meanings of one and the same
passage, he rendered everything said here invalid. It is no answer for him to
say that page 161 makes it clear that he wishes to say nothing other than that
the word of God must have and admit some meaning. For no one denies this, not
even that despairing Interpreter whom *Wolzogen* wished to take heart for this
reason. Yet he cannot take heart if there is only one meaning of the passage
and this meaning cannot be drawn out even after all the aids to interpretation
have been applied. Therefore, if *Wolzogen* wishes to speak consistently, these
words must presuppose and imply that

[Page 185 | ONB canvas 239 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000239]

there are various collateral and distinct meanings of the same passage.

2. On page 166 he says: *[There is no reason for us to dispute much about the
ungodly. For it is certain that, even if they sometimes see any truth presented
in Scripture, they nevertheless often do not see a saving truth and are
similarly blind in perceiving other things.]* From this it is clear that,
according to *Wolzogen*, all the ungodly can perceive truths, for by *any
truth* he must mean this. But if by it he means only non-saving truths opposed
to saving truths, I ask what he understands by saving truths. Are they the
things necessary to know for salvation? I will then ask what he understands
by *seeing*. Does he mean seeing in a saving or salvific way, or merely in a
natural way? If the latter is said—namely, that they understand those things
only naturally and not spiritually—what reason is there why they should not
see other truths, since these, by *Wolzogen’s* own testimony, are clearly set
forth in the Scriptures and are perhaps very few? If he says that they ever
see any truth in a saving way, he is very greatly mistaken and is not
Orthodox, for before they are renewed they see nothing spiritually and can see
nothing in that way. And yet the following words prove this to be *Wolzogen’s*
meaning: *[Shall we say here that because God is omniscient, he provided for
true meanings to occur even to the ungodly? What, then, of that ignorance?
What of the darkness? What of the mind hardened in error?]* These things prove
that the ungodly cannot perceive anything spiritually and savingly. But they
do not prove that the ungodly cannot in their own way—that is, in a carnal and
natural way—perceive saving truths, since even the Devils perceive these
things in their own way. Therefore, when he said above that *they often do not
see a saving truth*, he presupposed that they can sometimes perceive truths
savingly and spiritually. Yet I do not see how these things prove *Wolzogen’s*
intended conclusion, for the question here is not about the manner of
perceiving, but about the objective truth itself, as will be apparent to
anyone who consults the passage. Let *Wolzogen* ex-*

[Page 186 | ONB canvas 240 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000240]

*tricate* himself and explain his meaning more clearly. He has similar words a
little later on page 167: *[They are blind by nature, so that they are often
unable to perceive heavenly truth thoroughly.]* What does he understand here
by heavenly truth? Does he mean the objective truth itself? But although they
are blind by nature, they often see it in their own way, as has been said—a
carnal, animal, and natural way. If he means a heavenly manner of perceiving,
then as long as they are blind they can never perceive it thoroughly.

3. On page 171 he has these words: *[I only distinguish between the truth of
God by which he always speaks true things, and the same truth by which he
speaks true things in such a way that he wishes the truth to be understood by
those to whom he speaks.]* Response: How insipid this distinction is! It is as
though God’s truth were something other than that by which he always speaks
true things, and as though God’s truth depended in any way, or could be said
to depend, upon the understanding of men, so that God’s truth would only then
be established—or God would only then be truthful—when men were able to grasp
the true meaning of God’s words, as he says afterward. What? Will God not be
called truthful if someone furnished with the necessary qualifications is
sometimes unable to grasp the meaning? Does he not himself say a little later,
on page 173, that the Discourse can be turned and turned over a hundred times
and nevertheless not be understood? Will God therefore be said to be by no
means truthful? Far be it!

4. In the same place he has these words: *[We also embrace God’s truthfulness
in the latter sense universally, but we do not think that it can extend so far
that no one is left doubtful about the meaning. I do not say that this is by
man’s fault, for we have already removed all such fault, but by a certain
peculiar constitution of Scripture.]* Response: Above he said: *[In the latter
sense, God is truthful only when his discourse has been so framed that a
person furnished with the necessary qualifications who comes to exa-*

[Page 187 | ONB canvas 241 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000241]

*mine it is able to grasp its true meaning.]* But now he says that God’s
discourse has not been so framed that a person furnished with the necessary
qualifications who comes to examine it is able to grasp its true meaning,
because even by a certain peculiar constitution of Scripture a person
furnished with the necessary qualifications remains doubtful about the
meaning. What, I ask, is this other than saying that God is not truthful?
Moreover, it is now plain that *Wolzogen* says that Scripture is not clear and
perspicuous in itself, and not only with respect to us; in this he gives no
little support to the Papists. Is it evident that what follows also places
this beyond all possibility of doubt? He says: *[Let us suppose a sinner
plunged even into the greatest ignorance. God could surely speak so that he
would be understood even by the most ignorant person. He did not wish to. He
composed the words and the Discourse in such a way that he knew that if a
sinner encountered it, he would be led into error.]* Who does not see that it
follows from this that God does not now speak as clearly as he could have
spoken, and accordingly that Scripture is obscure even in itself? But I
wonder with what confidence *Wolzogen* could adduce that proof, since the
blame would fall, or could fall, upon the sinful man—whom God afflicted with
that punishment, namely the immense efficacy of error—and not upon Scripture.
What? Are men now plunged into the very greatest ignorance? Why, then, does he
suppose this, as though it could not be understood otherwise than by means of
this supposition? Or does he imagine some degree above the superlative? Let him
take care lest he finally say that God can make contradictories true at the
same time.

5. On page 173 he says: *[Indeed, God sometimes speaks in such a way that he
leaves doubtful and undecided even those very persons whom he has endowed with
sufficient grace of the Spirit, so that they draw out whatever can then be
derived from that Discourse, and yet do not draw out every truth. For let a
person turn and turn the Discourse over a hundred times, let him be free from
preconceived opinions, and let him examine everything required by the usage of
the language, so that to one contemplating the text nothing appears to have
been neglected. Nevertheless, God did not wish to be understood at this time;
indeed, he wished to permit him to go some-*

[Page 188 | ONB canvas 242 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000242]

*what astray.]* Response: Here he appears to transfer all the blame back to
the Scriptures, but what follows says that the blame must be laid upon man. He
says: *[Nor is it difficult to conceive how he does this. All the words of the
text can possess as much clarity as any Discourse. But God has allowed some
history, some rite, or some region to perish to which an allusion is made.]*
Yet these things do not concern the Discourse itself, which, notwithstanding
them, can possess as much clarity as any Discourse. With what knot will this
*Proteus* be bound? What does he understand by *every truth*? Does he suppose
with *Paradox* that there are several collateral truths of one and the same
sentence? Then this whole disputation is in vain, for he shamefully yields and
raises vanquished hands to his adversary. If he does not suppose this, let him
say more clearly what he means.

6. On page 175 he says: *[Because we believe that the things our salvation
requires us to know are always readily accessible and so clear that they can
be understood by everyone.]* Response: Therefore even by infants, unlearned
persons, and the like? Beware saying it. Your own *Velthuysen* says this same
thing in the passage cited above, page 70. But he adds: *[provided that they
apply due care, attention, and meditation.]* From this it can be inferred that
none of the things in controversy is necessary to know for salvation, because
you say that all these things *are so readily accessible and clear that they
can be understood by everyone*, and above on page 73 that *God has so expressed
his will in them that we cannot doubt it*. See how well these things agree with
the statements of the *Socinians*, and of *Episcopius* and the other
*Remonstrants*!

7. On page 180 he says: *[Whatever truths someone draws out of the text from
the analogy of the text and according to all the laws of Interpretation, God
must be judged to have wished to place them in the Discourse, because he does
not wish to deceive and foresaw that explanation.]* Response: These things are
not set forth clearly enough, for they at least *se-*

[Page 189 | ONB canvas 243 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000243]

*em* to presuppose that there are several collateral and independent truths
of one and the same text. Because God foresaw the explanations of such men,
did he therefore wish to place such truths in the Discourse? These things seem
to savor of the Jesuits’ *middle knowledge* and to make God’s decrees depend
upon the deeds of men.

8. What he has a little later in the same place also sounds bad: *[The
Exercitator must therefore explain this: the reader encounters no truths which
God did not foresee that he would encounter.]* Does he think that anything can
happen or come to pass which God did not foresee?

9. What he has on page 174 sounds worst of all: *[Yet this dispensation of the
divine will, which is various and almost infinite and contains every cause of
obscurity that appears to arise not from us but from Scripture, by no means
proves God deceptive. For one is by no means to be judged deceptive who openly
displays what he is going to do and then carries into effect with the greatest
exactness what he displayed.]* For these words imply that God actually
deceives, but that he must not be said to deceive because he displays what he
is going to do.

10. On page 182 he says: *[Thus, if it should be asked concerning the creation
of the world whether God could have created it by a shorter way—no suitable
answer can be given here unless the word creation is freed from ambiguity. For
if we wish it to signify the simple production of things, there is no doubt
that God could have acted more briefly. But if by creation we understand that
production of things in which God wished not only his power but also his
wisdom to shine forth, so that it would be an example of order for us, then I
deny that creation could have been accomplished in fewer days.]* Response: I
judge *Wolzogen* rash for prescribing limits to God in this way. What? Would
God’s power and wisdom not have shone forth if he had created everything in
the space of four or five days?

[Page 190 | ONB canvas 244 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000244]

He spent six days upon it, I judge, because he wished to. And whatever he
wills, he accomplishes by supreme wisdom and power. If it had seemed good to
him to use a shorter way, infinite power and wisdom would likewise have shone
forth. Nor do I sufficiently grasp this distinction concerning creation, or
who takes the word creation in any way other than the former sense. Similar
are the things he has on pages 183 and 184 concerning the work of redemption;
nor do I dare to say with *Wolzogen*: *If Christ had been set forth more
simply, the matter would not have been set forth so perfectly.*
