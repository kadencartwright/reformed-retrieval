QUESTION XXIV.

ON THE BREAKING OF BREAD.

Is the rite of breaking bread necessary in the administration of the Supper?
We affirm.

[Marginalia: The papists’ opinion on the breaking of bread.]

I. The second question raised about the symbols pertains to the breaking of
bread, concerning which there is disagreement between us and the papists and
Lutherans. The papists indeed do not deny that Christ broke the bread and
commanded it to be broken. They also hold that the breaking of bread is
necessary, not indeed to the substance of the sacrament, but to the integrity
of the sacrament—or, as they like to speak, of the sacrifice—so that without
the breaking of bread the action and Mass are imperfect, as Bellarmine says in
book 1, chapter 27, *On the Mass*. But they retain that breaking for appearance
and name’s sake. For they customarily consecrate individual hosts to be
distributed one to each person, but they consecrate them whole, so that in this
respect no breaking is employed, but each person takes away his own bread for
the sake of communion. Nevertheless, for the sake of the sacrifice, the priest
selects one host from the whole number and breaks it into three parts. The
first, which he places in the chalice, they say signifies Christ’s body after
the resurrection. He eats the second dry, and it signifies Christ’s body that
walked upon the earth. The third,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 378 | IA leaf 412 | PDF p. 412]

which is left on the altar until the end of the Mass, represents Christ’s body
in the tomb. But none of these parts is distributed to the communicants, and
they acknowledge that the breaking they employ is not the one Christ employed.
Bellarmine says in book 1, chapter 27, *On the Mass*: “The breaking that is the
fourth part of the Mass does not have the Lord as its author,” etc.

[Marginalia: And the Lutherans’ opinion.]

II. As for the Lutherans, it is certain that Luther, in his book *On the Abuse
of the Mass*, acknowledges that the breaking rests upon Christ’s institution no
less than the distribution, and that without it the sacrament cannot be rightly
celebrated. But afterward, speaking differently in his book *Against the
Heavenly Prophets* and in the *Larger Confession*, he says that in Hebrew idiom
“to break” means the same thing as “to distribute,” and that it is not necessary
for the bread to be broken with the fingers in the distribution; it is enough
if many particles or hosts are distributed. Lutherans today follow this later
opinion, maintaining that *to frangere* (τὸ *frangere*) is nothing other than
to distribute bread and give it to another, although they do not deny that
Christ properly broke the bread. But they maintain that this ceremony is merely
free and indifferent, lacking a command, and that the church may observe or
omit it at its discretion. Indeed, others speak more harshly, calling it a
perversion of the sacrament and Satan’s mockery.

[Marginalia: What the orthodox think.]

III. The orthodox retain the breaking employed by the Lord, which divides as
many loaves as are sufficient for the people’s communion into various pieces
to be distributed among many. They judge it not to be an accidental and
indifferent ceremony, but to be from Christ’s institution and no less necessary
than taking into the hands, giving, and communicating. Yet they do not wish to
contend for it so rigidly that no fellowship can be retained with those who
omit it. They regard this defect as no slight matter and as something that
ought neither to be concealed nor tolerated if it can be removed. Accordingly,
they hold that those who establish this omission are to be reproved, though not
absolutely condemned.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

IV. From this the state of the question arises. (1.) The question is not
whether Christ broke bread at the Supper, for those with whom we are dealing do
not deny this. Rather, it is whether we ought to retain that breaking as a
ceremony necessary to the integrity of the sacrament, and whether Christ
sanctioned it by his example and word, or whether it is indifferent and free,
as our adversaries would have it. We assert the former. (2.) Again, the
question is not whether just any breaking of bread suffices here, for the
papists, as we have said, break a host into various parts. Rather, it is whether
the bread ought to be broken for distribution, so that pieces of that broken
bread may be distributed to the individual communicants. They deny this; we
affirm it.

[Marginalia: The breaking of bread is proved: 1. From Christ’s institution and
example.]

V. The reasons are as follows. 1. From Christ’s institution and example,
because Christ broke the bread, as all the evangelists and Paul unanimously
testify, each of them recounting it. Who would affirm that the Son of God did
something superfluous in so sacred an action, or that all the apostles related
this with such diligence, if it were a merely indifferent and free matter? Who,
on the contrary, does not see that Christ wished to prescribe the sacramental
rites and ceremonies by his example and command when he commanded the disciples
to “do this,” that is, with respect to those administering, to take the bread,
bless it, break it, and give it to the communicants? Again, the breaking must be
regarded in the same way as the thanksgiving and distribution, because it is
placed between them. But no one denies that these two ceremonies are necessary,
belonging to the instituted rite, and are not merely indifferent. It is vainly
replied, (1.) that many other circumstances and actions are carefully related
by the evangelists and were observed by Christ, which ought not to be drawn
into imitation, such as that he celebrated the Supper in the evening and in a
guest room, that he was reclining rather than standing, and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 379 | IA leaf 413 | PDF p. 413]

the like. For everyone sees the dissimilarity. The breaking of bread pertains
in itself to the Supper; the circumstances of time, place, and posture attend
it accidentally. The breaking was divinely commanded; those circumstances
were not. The breaking has a spiritual significance; they do not. (2.) It is
added with no better right: “The command ‘do this’ is to be referred principally
to the things the apostles themselves performed in that action—for example,
that at the Lord’s command they ate and drank. But if it is transferred to the
things Christ did, a distinction must be made between necessary and indifferent
things. The breaking, taken for distribution, is indeed necessary, but taken
for the act of breaking is merely indifferent. Thus, among the things Christ
did, the genus is indeed necessary, so that the Supper is celebrated in bread
and wine; but the form, manner, rite, ceremonies, and circumstances rest in the
evangelical freedom of the church, which may arrange them as occasion requires
and as edification demands.” For it is gratuitously assumed that Christ’s
command “do this” is to be referred only or principally to the things the
disciples had to perform as communicants, since it is evident that the
institution and celebration of the whole sacrament, both on the part of those
administering and of those communicating, rest upon this command. Indeed, since
the actions of those communicating necessarily presuppose the actions of those
administering, this command to act must regard both equally. We acknowledge
that the arrangement of external circumstances of place, time, and the like has
been left to the freedom of the church. But we deny that this is to be extended
to essential and necessary rites and ceremonies, such as we contend the
breaking of bread to be.

[Marginalia: 2. From the mystical significance of the rite.]

VI. 2. From the mystical significance of this rite. For a ceremony instituted
by Christ that has an evident significance of God’s grace and benefits expressed
in the Word of God cannot be considered indifferent, but must be called
necessary. It is evident that this applies to the breaking of bread. For Christ
commanded the disciples to eat, not just any bread, but the bread they had seen
him break, and to eat it in remembrance of his death as represented by the
breaking of bread. No one can deny that this breaking has an evident
significance of God’s grace and benefits, namely, the breaking of Christ’s body
on the cross and the saving fruits that flow from it to us. The apostle
testifies to this when, recounting the words of institution in 1 Corinthians
11:24, he says that Christ’s body is broken, *to hyper hymōn klōmenon* (τὸ ὑπὲρ
ὑμῶν κλώμενον), “which is broken for you,” to show more clearly the analogy of
the breaking of bread to the breaking of his body. Paul calls Christ’s passion
the breaking of his body, not because his body or bones were properly broken
on the cross as the robbers’ bones were, which the evangelists expressly deny
from the ancient oracle, “You shall not break a bone of him.” Rather, it is so
called because the bread that is the sacrament of Christ’s body is properly
broken, sacramentally attributing to the thing signified what properly belongs
to the sign. Nor should it be said that an analogy of breaking cannot apply
because Christ’s bones ought not to be broken according to the analogy of the
Passover lamb. For it is one thing for bones to be broken, and another for the
body to be torn by various wounds through a breach of its continuity and
through the separation of the soul from the body. Again, one breaking is real,
another mystical. Christ’s bones ought not to have been really broken, nor were
they in fact broken; nevertheless, his heart was no less mystically torn and
broken by the pains and torments he bore on the cross, and especially by his
sense of God’s wrath and curse lying upon him because of our sins. Hence in
Psalm 69:21 reproach is said to have torn his heart; his soul is said to have
been *perilypos* (περίλυπος), and agony and *ekthambēsis* (ἐκθάμβησις) are
attributed to him to designate the utmost anguish of soul.

[Marginalia: 3. From 1 Corinthians 10:16.]

VII. 3. From Paul’s words in 1 Corinthians 10:16: “The bread that we break, is
it not the communion of Christ’s body? For

[1847 vol. III printed p. 380 | IA leaf 414 | PDF p. 414]

we, being many, are one bread and one body, because we all partake of one
bread.” Here he clearly shows that he retains the breaking of bread in the use
of the Eucharist, and that it contains the mystery of the union of believers
with Christ and with one another, just as we all partake of one bread and one
bread is formed from many grains. And since Paul professes that in the
celebration of this mystery he delivered nothing that he had not received from
the Lord, who can doubt that the breaking of bread he retains is from Christ’s
prescription? How can those who remove this breaking say, “The bread that we
break—we are one bread and one body because we partake of one bread,” when
among them one bread is neither broken nor distributed among many? They also
lose the mystery, full of consolation, of our union with Christ and with one
another through participation in one bread. Nor can it be said that this
breaking is taken for distribution because breaking without distribution
cannot be regarded as communion in Christ’s body. For although breaking ought
not to take place without distribution and cannot be regarded as communion in
the body, it does not follow that they ought to be regarded as the same thing.
Things that are joined together so that one does not exist without the other
are not on that account immediately identical. If Paul had wished to intimate
nothing by breaking other than distribution, why did he not say, “The bread
that we distribute,” but, “that we break”? But Christ’s command and example and
the mystery of the believers’ unity required otherwise.

[Marginalia: 4. From the practice of the apostles.]

VIII. 4. From the practice of the apostolic church, which constantly retained
the rite of breaking. Hence the disciples are said in Acts 2:42 “to have
continued in the apostles’ doctrine and fellowship and in the breaking of
bread and prayers,” and in Acts 20:7 Christians are said “to gather on the
first day of the week to break bread,” that is, to celebrate the Holy Supper,
which was customarily done on the Lord’s Day when they gathered to hear a
sermon and perform the other public exercises of piety. Hence the entire
action is customarily described by the breaking of bread. To say that this rite
was indeed employed here, but not as necessary, is to beg the question and
assume what must be proved. On the contrary, we soundly gather the necessity of
this rite from this practice, because the church could do or prescribe nothing
here except what it had received from the Lord and to which it understood
itself to be bound by Christ’s command.

[Marginalia: 5. From the practice of the ancient church.]

IX. 5. From the practice of the ancient church. For it can easily be shown
from the monuments of the ancients, who everywhere mention this rite, that the
rite of breaking was constantly retained from the apostolic age until the
twelfth century. Ignatius says in *To the Philadelphians*: “There is one flesh
of the Lord Jesus and one blood of his that was poured out for us, and also one
bread broken for all,” etc. Irenæus says in book 5, chapter 2: “When the mixed
cup and broken bread receive the Word of God, they become the Eucharist of
Christ’s body and blood.” Theophylact says on Mark 14: “When he had blessed and
given thanks, he broke the bread, which we also do by adding prayers,” etc. See
more in Dallæus, book 3, chapter 25.

[Marginalia: 6. From the papists.]

X. 6. Not a few papists acknowledge this. Lorinus observes on Acts 2: “This
rite and custom belonged to the primitive church, which was accustomed to make
one loaf and break it after it had been consecrated into as many parts as there
were communicants, just as Christ also did at the Supper.” Justinianus confirms
the same on 1 Corinthians 10:16. Sirmondus, in chapter 4, *On Unleavened
Bread*, acknowledges that it was an ancient custom to break the bread into
various particles. Hence the sacred symbols went by the name *meridōn*
(μερίδων), “particles,” or *hagiōn meridōn* (ἁγίων μερίδων), “sacred
particles,” because they were made from broken bread. This expression was so
familiar to the ancient Latin church that, although the thing was abolished,
the name could not be abolished and still remains. For, as he himself notes,
“Although the offerings

[1847 vol. III printed p. 381 | IA leaf 415 | PDF p. 415]

are consecrated whole together, and whole offerings are dispensed to each
person, they nevertheless acquired the name ‘particles’ from the ancient
custom.”

[Marginalia: Sources of the solutions.]

XI. Although Christ took the breaking of bread from the custom practiced among
the Jews, just as he did the distribution, taking, and eating of bread, this
does not prevent it from having been ordained for a peculiar reason and having
its own significance in itself, for subordinate things do not conflict.

XII. Although “breaking bread” sometimes designates its distribution, as in
Isaiah 58:7 and elsewhere, it does not follow that the two are equivalent in
this sacrament. That would be to argue from one particular case to another.
(1.) They are clearly distinguished by the evangelists: “He broke the bread
and gave it.” Therefore breaking and distribution are distinct actions. These
two are likewise joined elsewhere more than once (Matt. 14:19; Mark 8:6).
(2.) Many partake of one bread at the Supper (1 Cor. 10:17). Therefore one
bread is necessarily broken so that it may be distributed among many. When
Paul speaks of the broken bread, he does not mention distribution, but this is
not because he takes these two as one and the same. Rather, it is because the
command to take follows immediately, and that presupposes giving.

XIII. Because they lack the breaking of bread, they cannot be said to have the
Supper complete in all its parts, but a defective and mutilated one—if not with
respect to the symbols, at least with respect to the rites instituted and
commanded by Christ.
