Lecture XXX.

The fifth and final argument advanced by Bellarmine is this. “This doctrine of
ours,” he says, “not only affords no certainty, but plainly induces despair.
Therefore it is opposed not only to Catholic truth but also to the undertaking
of our own opponents”—whom, with Jesuitical, that is, more than monastic,
haughtiness he calls heretics—“since they boast of nothing more frequently
than that they bring solid consolation to human consciences from the certainty
of justification.” He now proves the antecedent as follows. For someone to be
certain of his justification, he must be certain of his perseverance—namely,
he must know certainly that either he will never fall or he will return to
repentance. Yet no one of sound mind—these are his words—should dare to
promise himself this with certainty. The reason is that no one can promise
himself anything about future events except rashly, especially when he sees
both himself and others fall into various sins even against conscience. Thus
the wise man says in Proverbs 27, “Do not boast about tomorrow, because you do
not know what the day will bring forth.” Likewise Ecclesiasticus 11: “Before
death pronounce no one blessed; a man is known even from his children.”
Finally, James 4: “You do not know what will happen tomorrow,” and so forth.

[Marginalia: Lecture XXX. Reason 5. Prosyllogism. Proverbs 27:1. Ecclesiasticus 11:29. James 4:14.]

We answer first—passing over Bellarmine’s misuse of words, indeed that ignorant
abuse of language, when he says, “How can it happen that anyone of sound mind
should hope with certainty that he is truly righteous?” This statement is
hardly the product of a sound mind. For we do not hope for present things; we
hold and apprehend them by faith. We deny the antecedent of that first
enthymeme, namely, that this doctrine induces despair. As for the proof, we
concede the major premise to some extent: in order that someone may enjoy
solid consolation from certainty of his justification, he must be certain not
only of his eternal election but also of the means leading there—that is, to
salvation and eternal life, to which he certainly knows himself to be elect;
and in particular of final perseverance, so that he is certainly persuaded in
his mind that he cannot fall and depart totally and finally from faith, the
grace of adoption, and the righteousness received through faith. But we deny
the minor: that no one can be certain of this and that no one of sound mind
can promise himself anything about it. We say that no utterance more alien and
abhorrent to the faith of the truly elect could have been made.

[Marginalia: The first part of the answer.]

This is clear from the following twofold distinction. 1. We do not mention or
raise a question about just any future events—namely, not about those which
belong to the condition of this present and earthly life and which are not
contained in the gospel promises in themselves and directly. The testimonies
cited by Bellarmine speak only about these and therefore have nothing to do
with the point. We speak only about the things necessarily required for
eternal salvation. Next, it is one thing to be able to know or believe
something certainly by the powers of nature; it is another to do so from
ordinary divine revelation disclosed to us through a gospel promise, applied
through faith, and sealed upon our hearts by the power of the Holy Spirit.
Therefore we say that by the inward operation of the Holy Spirit and the full
assurance implanted in our hearts, we can certainly believe and be fully
persuaded of whatever God himself promises us in the gospel concerning eternal
salvation, that future and heavenly life, and the means necessary to it.

Now Christ promises that he will by no means cast out those who come to him,
that he will not extinguish even a smoking wick nor break a bruised reed; that
he will give his sheep eternal life, that no one will snatch them from his
hand, and that they will never perish. Likewise, whoever hears his word and
believes in him who sent him has eternal life, will never come into
condemnation, but has already passed from death to life. Likewise, this is the
Father’s will: that Christ lose nothing from all that the Father gave him, but
raise it up on the last day; and again, that the one who looks upon the Son and
believes in him should have eternal life and that he should raise him on the
last day. Likewise, when the truly righteous person falls, he will not be cast
down, because Jehovah sustains his hand. Likewise, God is faithful and does
not allow his own to be tempted beyond what they are able to bear, but with
the temptation will also provide an escape or outcome so that they may endure
it. There are innumerable other promises of this kind, on which the immovable
certainty of our faith and the stability of salvation, adoption, and divine
grace and favor rest and are supported. Therefore we must firmly hold and
apprehend these things by faith.

[Marginalia: Psalm 37:24. 1 Corinthians 10:13. Amplification.]

Why, then, should we embrace by faith only the part of the divine promise—that
is, of kindness and grace—which applies to the present hour, while rejecting
and renouncing the rest, which concerns future life itself? Would this be to
believe in Christ? Or what, I ask, is it to believe in Christ but, through the
preaching of the gospel and by the operation and testimony of God’s own
Spirit, to be certainly persuaded that you were eternally elect in Christ,
redeemed through Christ, reconciled to God, justified, adopted by him as a
son, and finally to be given eternal life, and consequently that you will
continue in God’s grace

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 167 | De Prædestinatione p. 123]

to the very end? Indeed, it is nothing other than this. Or will you be so
impious toward God, so foolish and insane, as to believe today that God has
adopted you as a son and loves you as a son with fatherly love, but that
tomorrow or the day after God will disown you and, in human fashion, change
his love into hatred—that is, revoke, alter, and make obsolete his counsel,
gifts, and calling—and that nothing certain or stable concerning you or anyone
else is established before him? Surely whoever once believes that he has truly
been adopted by God through Jesus Christ also necessarily believes that he
will never become otherwise, whatever all the gates of hell may roar, clamor,
and snarl against him.

[Marginalia: Proleptically.]

I acknowledge, indeed, that flesh and blood—that is, human sense, reason, and
prudence—utterly recoil from this persuasion, and that it is regarded as
nothing but the delirium of an insane mind by natural men, who consequently
greet the truly pious and faithful with jeers and turn up a hooked nose at
them. Do you want a weighty witness of this? Behold our Bellarmine, who in this
passage judges those who promise themselves anything certain about future
events to be unsound in mind. But what do you say, Bellarmine? Is it so even
when God reveals, promises, indeed solemnly declares and swears, applies his
seals, and gives his own Spirit as so precious an earnest, sealing his promise
upon our hearts—as we saw above in the proper place from Ephesians 1? Does it
seem the height of insanity to you to believe and determine with certainty
that, just as you have now obtained righteousness, remission of sins, and the
right of adoption as a son through faith in Christ Jesus, so also in him you
will finally obtain that eternal inheritance of the sons—when God and Christ
himself invite, urge, and exhort you to believe this and apply it to yourself
by living and true faith?

[Marginalia: Rebuke. Ephesians 1:14. A judgment to be noted.]

Indeed it is so: to this natural and carnal man and others like him, who feel
and savor nothing but flesh and blood, who follow only the direction of their
own judgment and human prudence and do not depend at all upon the divine
promise, the true faith of the elect seems sheer insanity, presumption, and
foolish and rash confidence. For they measure all their security not from God
who promises—to whom, even when he swears, you see that they in fact have no
faith—but from the merits of their works or their own firmness and constancy.
Because they are therefore compelled always to fluctuate miserably and remain
continually suspended and uncertain about the future, since they never
possessed that true present confidence and full assurance which exists in the
hearts of the truly faithful and elect from God’s promises apprehended by
faith and applied by name to themselves, it comes about that they judge that
same faith in others, estimating it from their own experience, to be insanity
and rashness.

[Marginalia: Note the corollary. Amplification from the contrary.]

Meanwhile, the faithful know that it is the very nature of true faith never
to rest so in the present sense of divine favor that it is not always carried
forward toward its future completion. Indeed, when a believer senses nothing
in the present except God’s wrath kindled and inflamed against his sins, only
an avenging and threatening face, only God’s stern and terrible countenance,
as it were thundering and hurling the sentence of death in his righteous
judgments, he still does not cease to struggle by faith and to stretch the
hand of faith farther, to apprehend the very throne of grace, even amid the
sharpest and most bitter sense of divine wrath. I will say even more—something
at which Bellarmine and Bellarmine’s companions, who measure all faith by the
span of their own feeling, may stand astonished, but which Jonah, James,
David, Paul, and their other companions will readily acknowledge and embrace.
Even if a faithful person could by God’s permission be thrust into hell itself,
even if he were handed over to Satan to be tormented and harassed in every
way, that torturer could still never shake out of him all faith in God—that
is, certainty concerning his adoption and eternal salvation. Even from the
depths themselves he would flee to God, cry out to God, depend upon God’s word,
and eagerly seize and apprehend God’s promise. And—to speak with the poet whose
single phrase Bellarmine sufficiently betrays and proves that he has more
knowledge of him, but no more faith—he would endure and preserve himself for
better things, relying on God’s word alone.

[Marginalia: From the lesser. Virgil, namely.]

From this word the faith of the weakest people draws such strength, although
it appears exceedingly weak and hangs as if from a slender thread, that all
the force, fraud, fury, and assault of hell cannot break it. For this, says the
apostle in Hebrews 6, is that firm—indeed secure—anchor of the soul on which we
may safely rely. It enters within the veil and extends to the place where
Christ himself, our forerunner, entered for us. In the same passage—something
especially to be noted against the most unbelieving of men, dressed only in a
mask of faith, or rather hiding beneath it—the apostle teaches why God, not
content with a bare promise, also gave his guarantee by an oath: “namely,” he
says, “so that through two immutable things, in which it is impossible for God
to lie, we might have strong consolation.” What can that strong consolation
be except certainty of eternal salvation? It can be no consolation at all if,
as this unbelieving sophist wishes, we are uncertain about the whole future.
What is the strong consolation of the faithful but Paul’s assurance that
nothing at all can be imagined or named which could separate us from God’s
love in Christ, change his will, make his counsel void, or frustrate or hinder
his effectual calling? Or, finally, what is it but David’s assurance: “Jehovah
is my light and my salvation; whom would I fear? Jehovah is the strength of my
life; before whom would I tremble? If camps were pitched against me, my heart
would not fear; if war rose against me, in this I would be secure.” Likewise,
“Goodness and kindness will surely follow me all the days of my life, and I
will rest in the house of Jehovah as long as the ages endure.” This consolation
can never have a place unless we hold with complete certainty that the truly
faithful can never fall wholly from God’s grace and can never become
unbelieving and wicked.

[Marginalia: Hebrews 6:19. Psalm 27:1. Psalm 23:6. Conclusion.]

Let Bellarmine go now and mock with a profane heart this confidence of the
pious, for whose strongest consolation God himself wished to provide so
carefully and kindly.

[Marginalia: Counter-objection.]

It is customarily objected that this doctrine gives an opportunity for
negligence and carnal security, indeed that it spreads beneath people a soft
and pleasant pillow on which they may sleep soundly on both ears. First, this
conflicts with our Bellarmine’s meaning and intention in this passage, for he
maintains on the contrary that it drives people to despair. Thus the unwary
man here betrayed his unbelieving mind. Next, we have answered the matter
itself so often elsewhere that it is tiresome to repeat anything here. We have
said, namely, that this doctrine is material for spiritual security—such as we
see in David and Paul—that is, for the firmest confidence, and hence for

[Marginalia: Refutation by rejection. Correction.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 168 | De Prædestinatione p. 124]

strong consolation in pious minds. The greater this spiritual security and confidence
is, the more necessarily it is joined with zeal for living piously, ardor,
filial fear, caution, prayers, watchfulness, and sobriety. It is so far from
leading any of the faithful to license for sin. If it gives a different
occasion to others—namely, to those who do not flee sin except from fear of
punishment, nor serve God except to earn a reward, thus betraying the mind of
a hireling rather than the affection of sons toward God as Father—Bellarmine
may settle the matter with them. Meanwhile, far be it from us treacherously to
reject and renounce the inexhaustible riches of divine grace and kindness;
that is, far be it from us to join them in refusing to believe God when he
promises, swears, and seals.

[Marginalia: From the concession.]

Thus we have examined all Bellarmine’s arguments. We shall add to them three
more of our own, neither inferior in rank nor less—indeed, more—worthy of
consideration than those Bellarminian arguments.

The sixth argument, then, is this. Adam, while still upright and free from
every stain of sin, nevertheless fell totally and lost that perfect, sincere,
and unspoiled grace. How much more after the fall can a person who knows,
believes, hopes, and loves only in part lose that imperfect grace which
continually struggles with the mire of sin? How much more can he now fall from
faith and holiness that have only begun and are imperfect than Adam then fell
from complete integrity and holiness?

[Marginalia: Reason 6.]

I answer that the argument is attractive at first sight, since it presents the
appearance of an argument from the lesser, *apo tou hētton*, as Aristotle
speaks—that is, from the less probable to the more probable. This kind of
argument is especially apt to dazzle the unwary. But it is in truth empty and
without force, and can readily be dissolved simply by denying the consequence,
or that connection which must be supplied to complete the argument. For
Adam’s original condition and the state of the faithful person are not alike.
The latter apprehends Christ’s righteousness by true faith, however imperfect,
and is justified by it before God under the gospel covenant.

[Marginalia: Answer. Confirmation.]

We know that a human being left to himself always remains human—that is,
fragile, falling, changeable, and liable to a fall day by day and hour by hour.
Accordingly, considered in himself, he is no less—indeed, more—capable now than
then of rushing into hell itself and eternal death, not to say being cast or
hurled down, if God were wholly to withdraw his kind hand for even the least
time and entirely deprive the faithful person of his helping and confirming
grace. Yet God promised something else to those whom he once founded upon
that Rock by true and living faith, as we taught abundantly above. He made no
such promise to Adam, whose fall he had decreed to permit, and consequently
did not add confirming grace which would support him in the good, fortify him
against the gates of hell, and render him invincible. Thus he opened the way
for the execution of his decree and illustrated the glory both of his justice
and of his mercy in a wholly divine manner.

[Marginalia: Note the reason for the difference.]

In brief, the account of original righteousness is entirely different from
that of the righteousness we have from faith in Jesus Christ. The all-wise God
willed, when the former was lost, to open the way to the latter; and when our
own righteousness was destroyed, he willed this still to remain as the sole
remaining way of salvation and the one gate of heaven. But when this is lost,
no other righteousness can remain, no hope, and no refuge of salvation for
those who have once fallen from the grace of Christ and the righteousness of
faith, or gospel righteousness. No sacrifice for sins remains for them.
Therefore God will never allow the truly faithful and elect, concerning whom
his eternal and immutable counsel cannot be made void, once endowed with this
righteousness and once built by faith upon this foundation, to be torn away
or fall from it.

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Hebrews 10:26.]

Indeed, to those who look closely at the matter and consider this disparity
between Adam’s state and the condition of the regenerate under the gracious
covenant, there appears to be so little room for inferring that conclusion
that an argument for the contrary stability and perseverance of the faithful
can be drawn from this very point. If a created person could fall, certainly
one who has once been restored—which happens through faith alone in Christ the
Mediator—will not fall again; that is, he will never again fall away entirely,
because there is now no third righteousness and no third way of salvation.
There is no regeneration other than the one, just as there is no baptism other
than the one, the unique symbol and seal of that rebirth. Adam could lose that
natural grace; whoever is called according to God’s purpose cannot lose this
supernatural grace. Then he stood or fell by the powers of nature, so it is no
wonder that he fell. Now the believer stands in faith by the power of Christ
who strengthens him, so it is no wonder that he continues. But it would be
utterly absurd for someone resting upon Christ and supported by so firm a
foundation to be able to fall. From this comes all his firmness and all his
confidence of persevering: he knows and senses that he is truly grafted into
Christ, lives in him, and indeed has already conquered in the one who conquered
for him by dying. Therefore those who assert that any member can either be
torn from Christ by the Devil or rot of its own accord are impious and
blasphemous against Christ himself. We must therefore not compare the
regenerate person with the newly created person apart from Christ himself,
whose part and living member he has now become. If we measure the faithful and
regenerate person together with this his base and foundation, and so compare
him with that first Adam, we will then see clearly how unequal that contest
was. It is therefore no wonder that the latter, founded upon nature alone, was
readily overturned and struck down, while no force and no fraud can overthrow
the former, who has grown together with the very Son of God into one mystical
body, has become bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh, and lives the same
spiritual life from his Spirit.

[Marginalia: Reversal of the argument.]

Let the seventh argument be this. Whoever can become a member of a prostitute
can lose Christ’s grace and justifying faith. But a truly faithful person, who
is now a member of Christ, can become a member of a prostitute. Therefore he
can lose them. The minor is evident from 1 Corinthians 6: “Shall I take the
members of Christ and make them members of a prostitute? Far be it. Do you not
know that the one who is joined to a prostitute is one body with the
prostitute?” From these words it is plain that one who commits fornication
becomes a member of a prostitute; but this can happen to someone truly
faithful, such as David. Therefore, and so forth. The major is proved as
follows. Whoever ceases to be a member of Christ has lost justifying faith,
since he has been separated from Christ and fallen from communion with him.
But one who becomes a member of a prostitute ceases to be a member of Christ.
Therefore. The reason for the minor is that we assume as a foundation that no
one can simultaneously be a member both of Christ and of a prostitute, or, as
the apostle speaks in the passage cited, that no one can simultaneously be one
body with a prostitute and one spirit with the Lord. This is the base and
foundation upon which, as you see, the whole objection rests.

[Marginalia: Reason 7. 1 Corinthians 6:15, 16.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 169 | De Prædestinatione p. 125]

Consequently, when it is distinguished and explained, the argument will readily be
dissolved. We answer, then, that “a member of Christ” can be understood in
various ways. 1. It is either true and living, or only apparent and presenting
the outward appearance. Members of the latter kind have no life derived into
them from Christ’s Spirit and communion and do not belong to God’s election,
but live only in the outward communion of the visible Church and profession of
gospel truth. That holy man of God Perkins most aptly compares such members to
a wooden leg, a glass eye, or any other member of this kind artificially
adapted to a human body.

[Marginalia: Answer. An elegant comparison.]

Again, however, a member is such either actually or at least by destination.
From God’s perspective, to whom nothing is future, the latter can be called a
member. But from the perspective of the thing, which does not yet exist, and
with respect to us, for whom many things are past and many future, it is not so
much a member of Christ as destined to become one in its time. But this is not
our present subject.

[Marginalia: A distinction to be observed.]

Finally, an actual member is either strong and vigorous or sick and diseased;
either sound and whole or injured and wounded; either lively and active or
withered and half-dead. The same doctor of most blessed memory no less aptly
compares a member of the latter kind to a leg which is broken, dislocated,
ulcerated, or wounded, or suffering from paralysis or atrophy. Although such a
leg, impeded by injury or disease, has for a time lost all use and power to
perform its function, it has not completely lost all life or union with the
rest of the body. Consequently, it still remains a true leg, whether or not it
is useful to its possessor.

[Marginalia: An apt comparison.]

We say the same about Christ’s true members. We say that some member, both
according to election and actually grafted into Christ through faith, can be
so dislocated and wounded by a more grievous fall that it loses spiritual
sense and motion for a time and is thus rendered half-dead. Accordingly, so
far as lies in itself, it extinguishes, casts off, and drives away the grace
and presence of the Holy Spirit. That is, it not only loses all sense of it,
but deserves to be wholly deprived of communion with Christ itself, and thus,
with respect to the desert of its sin, to become the Devil’s slave in place of
a member of Christ. This is what I meant by “so far as lies in itself,” namely,
if the account of its sin is considered.

[Marginalia: Application to the subject.]

Let us now adapt these senses to the present question. We pass over the
apparent member and the one which is not yet actually a member but is such only
by God’s counsel and destination. There is no controversy that members of
these kinds can be joined even to a prostitute. As for the true member of
Christ which is actually such, we say that while it remains sound and
vigorous, lively and active, it cannot become a member of a prostitute. But we
acknowledge that one which is paralyzed and half-dead, laboring under the
grievous disease of sin, can fall into this danger; or, if it was formerly
sound, that it can indeed be dislocated by such a fall and contract within
itself the most grievous stain through such a calamity. Unless God placed his
hand beneath it, it would wholly fall away and be torn off. But if it is a true
member, it will never be entirely separated from that saving communion, but
will remain grafted into Christ, who accepts it not for its merits but by his
grace.

[Marginalia: The solution of the argument itself.]

Therefore a member of this kind remains in Christ even then with respect to
the grafting of faith and the mystical union. Consequently it by no means
casts off or loses the righteousness once put on, since God constantly imputes
Christ’s merit and obedience. Yet it falls from Christ with respect to the
power, sense, and efficacy of his Spirit and with respect to that act of
justifying faith consisting in full assurance and complete confidence. By its
own fault it cannot perceive this for a time, until it repents seriously and
from the heart—indeed, vehemently and extraordinarily—as we warned above.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

If it seems to anyone impossible for that union with a prostitute to coexist
with communion with Christ, let him consider that the two are not of the same
kind: the latter is mystical and spiritual, while the former is bodily and
natural. Next, let him consider the solution of the final objection which I
now present, since the doubt itself is akin to this one and its resolution
helps to remove this scruple more fully.

[Marginalia: Counter-objection.]

The final objection, then, is from the very nature of the case, as they say.
Sin and the grace of the Holy Spirit cannot coexist, and to wish to teach this
is nothing other than teaching people to persist in sins and indulge the
desires of their flesh. If they are persuaded that, even if someone joins
himself to a prostitute, for example, he nevertheless does not thereby fall
from communion with Christ, then justifying faith and the grace of Christ are
wholly abolished through sin. The ears and minds of people already so inclined
to fulfill their sins and desires must not be imbued with the contrary
doctrine.

[Marginalia: The eighth and final argument.]

To this we answer first, because that antecedent has two parts. Its first part
is true of reigning sin, which has a place only in the unregenerate and is
committed without any struggle or conflict and with the full consent of the
will. But it is not true of sin which has been partly mastered and subdued, or
at least has begun to be mastered and subdued, such as exists in the
regenerate. They are not carried into sin with full sails and oars, but with
the regenerate part resisting more or less, as the temptation pressing and
weighing upon them is stronger or weaker, or as more or less strength is
supplied by God’s Spirit to resist it. This will appear more clearly both from
the various degrees of temptations and from consideration of the subject—that
is, the person who sins—if we consider how desire, *hē epithymia*, that is,
native corruption, exists in his mind, understanding, will, and affections.
James plainly teaches in chapter 1 of his epistle that this is the root,
tinder, and foundation of sin, and the inward and antecedent principle of the
temptation itself.

[Marginalia: The first part of the answer. James 1:14 and 15.]

As for the subject, this native vice, like a kind of elephantiasis of the whole
soul, reigns alone in the unregenerate person, who before his conversion to
God is entirely flesh, in his full extent. But in the same person once
regenerated, the flesh does not exist alone or separately, but mixed with the
Spirit, so that both Spirit and flesh are found throughout his whole soul.
This is not as though a single temperament were made from the two—that is, as
though they were reduced to harmony and agreement and by union constituted
some single form or tempered quality, like those contending qualities of the
elements in a mixture. On the contrary, the Spirit always opposes and resists
the flesh, and the flesh the Spirit, and there is never any agreement between
them. These two opposing qualities are as it were diffused through the whole
soul of the regenerate person and occupy the same subject and seat, so that he
is not flesh in one part, power, or faculty of himself and Spirit in another,
but is flesh everywhere and wholly, though not totally; and likewise Spirit
everywhere and wholly, though not altogether. It is as in twilight, where
darkness alone is not in one part or region of the air and light alone in
another, but throughout every

[Marginalia: Note concerning the subject of sin. Galatians 5:17. A clear comparison.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 170 | De Prædestinatione p. 126]

part of it, light is mingled with darkness and darkness with light. It is evident from the
theorems of natural things that contraries in their highest degrees cannot
occupy the same subject simultaneously, but only in remitted degrees. Thus
tepid water can be said to be wholly warm in a remitted degree or wholly cold
in a remitted degree. We must say the same about the grace of the Holy Spirit
in the soul of a regenerate person: it can coexist with sin which no longer
reigns, but has been broken and subdued.

Next, as for the degrees of sin and temptation themselves, James intimates
them to us in the passage cited. He teaches first that we are drawn and
enticed by sin—*exelkesthai kai deleazesthai*—when someone fosters a thought
about committing some sin or evil and does not immediately reject it, but
lingers upon it with some delight. Thus, enticed as if by some bait, he allows
himself to be drawn and led away from better thoughts. Next he also conceives
sin, when in addition the consent of the will and the purpose of perpetrating
it arise, so that, if an opportunity were given and fear, shame, and every
other outward hindrance removed, the act itself would immediately follow.
Finally, he gives birth to what was conceived—that is, brings it forth into an
outward work and act. James says that this birth also gives birth to death
along with it—eternal death, namely, considered according to its own desert
and God’s righteous judgment. Thus we acknowledge that not only the birth of
sin, although at a higher degree, but also its conception and indeed its seed
itself draw the guilt of eternal death upon the sinner, unless God’s mercy in
Christ intervenes to wash away and atone for the sin itself.

[Marginalia: Concerning its degrees. 1. 2. 3. Proleptically.]

In the regenerate, however, we deny that either conception or the birth itself
has the same character as in the unregenerate. As we noted above, the truly
faithful are neither carried to sin with the full consent of the will nor,
even if by God’s permission, the Devil’s instigation, and the provocation and
enticement of that inward principle of sinning together with the object itself
they proceed to the act and that deadly birth itself, do they ever sin
totally—that is, also with the part in which they are regenerate—but only
insofar as they are still flesh and blood. We readily acknowledge that in a
more grievous fall of this kind the Spirit is conquered by the flesh, faith is
grievously wounded, and conscience would be mortally injured unless the hand
of God who heals and shows mercy intervened. Thus faith, struck down for a
time, suffers a most serious fainting and swoon until repentance is renewed,
aroused, and restored; yet it is not utterly destroyed and extinguished, as we
taught in the preceding solution.

[Marginalia: Application.]

To put it in a word, an historian has recorded about the Romans, while their
ancient empire flourished, that they could often be defeated in a battle but
never in a war, because the outcome of the war itself was always favorable to
them. We say the same about this civil war which a Christian must wage with
the Devil and the flesh throughout his whole life. In some particular clash
and engagement, by God’s hidden judgment, a weak soldier of Christ can be
defeated by sin; but he cannot be killed or slain by it, nor can all spiritual
life be forcibly taken from him. The death of the soul is the total extinction
of faith and separation from spiritual communion with Christ.

[Marginalia: Comparison. The application is to be noted.]

We come next to the latter part of the same minor premise or antecedent, in
which it is said that people are fostered and retained in sins by this
doctrine. We answer that everything must be spoken and taught for edification
in Christ’s Church, not only according to the standard of truth but also
according to the rule of spiritual prudence. If that is done, we deny that any
harm comes from this to the faithful themselves. Rather, a great abundance of
consolation, and indeed of caution and watchfulness, flows from it. Their one
zeal and most ardent and earnest wish—indeed, the sum of all their wishes—is
by every means to foster, nourish, and preserve Christ’s gracious presence and
the efficacy of communion with him, and thus the power and life of Christ’s
Spirit, which is the life and soul of their soul; that peace of conscience, I
say, and joy in the Holy Spirit which arise from certainty of their election
and adoption and the sense of God’s fatherly love poured into their hearts,
and to hold them dearer than a thousand lives and worlds.

[Marginalia: The other part of the answer.]

They themselves know that this operation of faith cannot coexist even with a
purpose to sin, much less with the birth of sin itself. They know that they can
never apply to themselves in particular, with firm and solid confidence, that
doctrine which we delivered above from God’s word concerning the certain and
infallible perseverance of the elect unless they pursue newness of life and
devote themselves with a sincere heart to works of piety and holiness, always
keeping David’s words in mind: “Then I will not be confounded or ashamed when
I look upon all your commandments.” Therefore they will avoid with every
effort and caution the very occasions and enticements of sin, slippery places,
every boundary of a fall, and every turning from the right way. Following the
apostle Jude’s counsel, they will even flee the garment itself stained by the
contagion of the flesh more carefully than any death and plague, and will work
out their own salvation with fear and trembling.

[Marginalia: Note the axiom. Psalm 119:6. Jude 23.]

As for hypocrites, who love not God but themselves, serve not so much God as
themselves, and care not so much for conscience as reputation, it was said
frequently above that they abuse even the most wholesome things to their own
destruction. Let this judgment therefore remain invincible and unshaken for
us: the gift of saving faith and of Christ’s righteousness imputed through it,
once conferred upon the faithful, can never be entirely lost; and those once
grafted into Christ can never be utterly broken off through unbelief or
faithlessness.

[Marginalia: Conclusion.]

If it were necessary, we could now further establish this by many sayings of
the ancients consonant with this truth. But lest we appear to rely upon human
authority, we will be content to cite a few in passing. First, there is that
notable passage from Cyprian’s third epistle. Citing Peter’s answer from John
6, “Lord, to whom shall we go?” and so forth, he says that it “signifies and
shows that those who have withdrawn from Christ perish by their own fault, but
that the Church which believes in Christ and, once knowing what it has known,
holds it fast, never departs from him; and that those who remain in God’s house
are the Church. Those whom we see not strengthened with the stability of wheat
but tossed like chaff by the breath of the scattering enemy are not a planting
planted by God the Father. John also says of them in his epistle, ‘They went
out from us,’” and so forth.

[Marginalia: Cyprian, Letters, book 1, Epistle 3 to Cornelius.]

Similarly Augustine, in his book *On Rebuke and Grace*, compares the condition
of the faithful called to Christ with Adam’s original state: “But now,” he
says, “to the saints predestined by God’s grace for God’s kingdom there is
given not only such aid for perseverance”—namely, such as Adam possessed
before the fall, the ability to persevere if they willed—“but such aid that
perseverance itself is given to them.” Similarly Chrysostom, Homily 1 on
chapter 1 of the first epistle to Timothy: “This is proper to faith: however
much events contrary to the promises occur, it never entirely falls away or
is altogether disturbed.” Similarly Gregory on John 7: “Because the

hidden seed of faith. Although the spirit of the tempter has shaken off the

[Marginalia: Thomas Aquinas.]

If statements apparently contrary to these are sometimes found among those
ancient fathers—as I do not deny can happen because of the goal they set
before themselves, whether to console an afflicted mind, support one wavering
and falling, awaken one who is secure, recall one who is confident and swollen
with false carnal presumption to fear, humility, and saving caution, or also
because of the manifold variety of the argument they are treating—examples
are these words in Augustine, chapters 8 and 9 of the same book *On Rebuke and
Grace*: “God does not give perseverance to certain sons of his whom he has
regenerated in Christ and to whom he has given faith, hope, and love”; and
again in chapter 13: “It must be believed that some of the sons of perdition
begin to live in the faith which works through love, live faithfully and
righteously for some time, and afterward fall.”

[Marginalia: Anticipation.]

In passages of this kind, I say, we must say that they speak not about true
sons of God, but only about those who are such both by profession and in their
own and others’ opinion. Likewise they speak of faith, love, and outward
amendment of life which sprouted for a time only upon the surface, without
roots in the rocky and by no means sincere heart of those people, but were not
rooted and founded in its depths. It is therefore no wonder if they afterward
dry up and vanish, like those stalks or ears which we see grow on the roofs of
houses, especially roofs piled with turf: they are fleeting and strengthened
by no weight of grain within, but frustrate the hopes and expectations of
onlookers with empty husks and coverings.

[Marginalia: Answer. Psalm 129:6.]

objections which we proposed to survey at the beginning, and we shall at last
bring to an end this refutative part, in which we defended the truth of the
doctrine delivered above against the arguments of the heterodox.
