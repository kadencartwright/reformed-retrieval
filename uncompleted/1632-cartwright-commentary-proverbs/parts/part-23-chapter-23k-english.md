Verse 22. Listen to your father, to him who begot you; and do not despise your mother when she has grown old.

Verse 23. Acquire truth, and do not sell it; wisdom, and instruction, and understanding.

Verse 24. The father of a righteous man will exult with exultation; the parent of a wise man will certainly rejoice over him.

Verse 25. Your father and your mother will rejoice; indeed, she who bore you will exult.

Verse 26. Give me your heart, my son; and your eyes [let them attend] to my ways.

Verse 27. For a prostitute is a deep pit, and the strange woman is a narrow well.

Verse 28. She too lies in wait like a robber, and adds to the treacherous among men.

In קנה ואל תמכר, “acquire and do not sell,” there is an allegory drawn from merchants who conduct trade and buy precious merchandise at a high price. In עיניך, “your eyes,” there is a metaphor transferring bodily eyes to the mind's understanding. In באר, “well,” and שוחה, “pit,” there is an allegory to indicate the danger of sexual immorality.

The substance is that parents' wholesome admonitions must be absorbed in childhood, so that, by avoiding sexual immorality and other sins, you may at the same time escape the destruction that results from them.

He prepared the way for his previous warning against overindulgence and drunkenness with a single verse. Here, however, when he is about to warn against sexual immorality, a subject on which he has already spent entire chapters, he first sets out five verses to secure a hearing and attention for himself. Why is this? First, because this crime is more atrocious; next, because this sin causes greater harm to commonwealths, since not only are resources

[1632 witness | PDF p. 523 | printed columns 1030-1031]

drained, but inheritances are also transferred from their rightful heirs to outsiders. Moreover, we are naturally more inclined to this sin, since it clings more closely to our corrupted nature. Finally, we may reasonably suppose that Solomon labored more extensively on this subject because he was moved by awareness and experience of his own weakness.

Let parents and all who undertake the office of training and educating the young learn from this to examine and thoroughly understand the dispositions of those in their charge. Thus they may devote more effort and attention to restraining and subduing those sins toward which they perceive them to be more inclined, whether by nature, by their upbringing, or by the contagion of the place where they live, especially if these sins wound the conscience more grievously. This prudence is readily visible in the writings of the prophets and apostles. Isaiah, foreseeing that the faithful would be carried into captivity and there enticed to idolatry, labored very greatly to fortify the godly against it προφυλακτικῇ, by preventive instruction. Ezekiel too strove with all his strength to lead the Jews, among whom he prophesied when they were already devoted to idolatry, to hatred of that sin.

Next, awareness of the sins they themselves once committed through the weakness of their age ought to spur them to take diligent and timely precautions that those subject to them do not fall into the same sins. Thus Peter, who had fallen away from the faith, labored to keep others safe and sound from falling away. In particular, he wrote his second epistle to this end, as he himself expressly testifies, to confirm the Jews in the truth they had embraced, as Christ had admonished him.

So much, then, in general concerning this whole passage and some of the verses that follow. Let us now examine each point a little more carefully. In verse 22 he exhorts children, and sons under their father's authority, to obey their parents when they impart wholesome precepts by which they may be preserved from prostitutes' arts. From this we can settle with certainty a matter some have called into doubt: namely, that it belongs to Christian foresight and prudence to equip boys and adolescents, still ignorant of prostitutes' deceits, against them. The fear they devise is groundless: that in this way the minds of the young, being furnished with knowledge of sins previously unknown to them, will also be provoked and inflamed with desire for them. As though they did not carry enclosed within their minds the seeds of these corruptions, soon to sprout of their own accord! As though one could hope for an age so purified and cultivated that prostitutes would not offer themselves in it! For unprepared young men to encounter them is nothing else than for an unarmed, defenseless man to fall into robbers' hands. Not unlike this is the judgment of certain false evangelicals who want every mention of the pope, the mass, and the like banished from sermons in the church, on the sole pretext that by bringing these things up again, inexperienced people may be taught the religion of the papal party and embrace it.

Next, children are taught that it is their duty to set the highest value on their parents' instruction and to make the greatest progress in it, since they can readily understand that all the precepts with which they are imbued flow from exceptional love. For awareness of love in any teacher and instructor has great power to secure cheerful obedience to his precepts. Parents, then, must be obeyed most readily: why should it be surprising if they love their own children more than others do? Hence it is not without reason that the law commands that someone who has not listened to his parents' good admonitions should be carried off to death, Deuteronomy 21:18–19.

The prophet expressly warns against anyone's despising his mother because, of the parents, she is more exposed to contempt. We have previously given the reasons for this.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 524 | printed columns 1032-1033]

His addition, “when she has grown old,” plainly indicates that a mother's gray hair, which ought deservedly to gain her greater reverence and obedience, produces greater contempt through her children's perversity. First, because, always weak on account of her sex and least able to avenge herself, she becomes weaker as old age presses upon her; next, because she can no longer acquire resources to leave to her children; finally, because, as her mental powers fail, she almost loses her reason and becomes confused. Yet this confusion, however great it may be, ought to be borne patiently, especially by her children, whom she herself bore with most lovingly in infancy when they were devoid of all understanding.

Moreover, let parents also understand from this what is taught more fully elsewhere: that it is in their own interest to train their children in godliness and the fear of God, Genesis 18:19; Ephesians 6:4. For the greatest hope of an abundant harvest both in the commonwealth and in the church depends upon the right and proper instruction of the household. Nor are many godly people found who did not lay the foundations of their godliness in their parents' households.

We must not pass over the fact that it is not foreign to the duties of a godly mother of a household to instruct children, even male children, in godliness. Yet the papal party cries out that this is something monstrous. See 2 Timothy 3:15 and Acts 18:26, where Priscilla is praised for having explained the way of the Lord more perfectly to Apollos, a teacher of the church. Away, then, with the clamorous croaking of the pope's devotees against divine truth!

In verse 23 he exhorts godly youth to pursue wisdom and godliness by a comparison of equals. He wants them to devote no less labor to godliness and wisdom than merchants customarily spend on precious merchandise. These merchants, as is well known, undertake the longest and most dangerous journeys by land and sea to make their fortunes. Thus in chapter 2, verse 4, he shows that the godly must equal miners' industry in searching out wisdom. These people, indeed, cannot practice their trades and acquire what they seek unless equipped with a large sum of money. But wisdom, although it infinitely surpasses any merchandise and metals, is nevertheless easy to find for someone willing to understand, and is sold for nothing, Isaiah 55:1. Moreover, merchants' gain is uncertain, since, when they suffer shipwreck or pirates come upon them, they not infrequently lose their lives along with their goods. Those who work in mines often either fail to find the metal they seek or spend more in searching than its discovery will repay. But for those who seek wisdom and godliness in the way appointed by God, the gain is certain and beyond doubt.

Accordingly, we must hold fast to the saying in Proverbs 4:7: acquire wisdom, and above all your possessions acquire understanding. And in this passage, where we are forbidden to sell it, we are earnestly admonished to prefer nothing to it once acquired. For we value what we sell less than that for which we sell it.

But in the words קנה ואל תמכר, “buy and do not sell,” there is a dissimilarity, as though he were saying: merchants subsequently sell at a higher price the merchandise they buy dearly; but wisdom, once acquired, must not be sold for any gain in riches, glory, or pleasures. Much less must it be thrown away by forgetting or by turning aside from the right way, Proverbs 4:5.

We must also note that wisdom and understanding are always joined with truth, so that we may understand that fraud and cunning are unworthy of being honored with that name.

Next, by attaching מוסר, or discipline, to wisdom and understanding, he teaches most plainly that those who hate discipline, whether domestic discipline in the household or public discipline in the church or commonwealth, are not cultivators of these virtues.

For verses 24–25, see the observations made above on verses 15–16 of this chapter. Yet something further is added here concerning the

[1632 witness | PDF p. 525 | printed columns 1034-1035]

joy and exultation of the mother, for whom a son ought to provide all the more because she has endured great and bitter pains in conception, pregnancy, childbirth, and nursing. Although this burden rests upon her by God's judgment, that in no way prevents her children from being bound by a closer obligation on this account to comfort her and, as far as they can, cheer her in old age, so that, as it were, each torment may be repaid with a particular joy, and pains with exultation. But for aged mothers to be recalled, so to speak, to new births of pain by their children's dissipation and stubbornness is plainly savage and cruel.

It may seem somewhat strange that anyone is called the father of a righteous man and the parent of a wise man, since all are born children of wrath. The answer is that the son is called righteous and wise, not because he was born such, but because he has been made such by the supernatural power and operation of the Holy Spirit. By this manner of speaking, the prophet meant to teach that parents do not lose their right and authority over their children because these are heirs of the heavenly kingdom, as the Anabaptists nevertheless dream; rather, the children must still obey their parents and magistrates. Yet those who have so educated their children and trained them from their earliest years that they have been born again through the cooperating Spirit of God can deservedly be accounted and called the parents of the regenerate also. Conversely, those who do not endeavor, as far as lies in them, that the children born to them should be born again are altogether unworthy before God of the honorable name of parents.

Verse 26. So that we may assent more willingly to this request, it will not be out of place to consider whose it is. If we judge truly, these are the words of God himself. For the prophet spoke these things in the name of God, whose ambassador he was, rather than in his own name. In the same way the apostle also says in 2 Corinthians 5:20 that he and the other ministers discharged an embassy from God, through whom Christ himself in a manner became a suppliant to people, asking them to be reconciled to God. Since this is so, how monstrously ungrateful we shall be, indeed how foolish, or rather insane, if we deny him what is his by the right of creation, redemption, and preservation, and what it is supremely in our interest to render, when he in a manner asks it of us as a suppliant!

If it is asked why he does not himself take what is his, I answer that he does indeed take it, when he alone turns a person's heart to himself. But he does this through the ministry of the word. Far the most powerful part of that ministry for moving the hearts of his people is when he presents himself to them as though a suppliant and, setting aside the supreme authority to command with which he is endowed, resorts to entreaties. This ought also to admonish us that we should not try to obtain by harsher words what we can obtain by entreaties and kind words, Philemon 8–9.

That he asks for neither the head nor the foot nor the hand, but the heart, accords with his wisdom. For by possessing the heart he will have all the other members of the body bound and devoted to himself, while he counts their submission without the heart as nothing. Although he could readily obtain honor and glory for himself even from this, his love toward us is such that he sets little value on glory separated from our salvation. His requirement in the same verse of the eyes' attention also relates to this. For [he requires] not the sight of the body's eyes, but the mind's keen vision and the most intent application of the affections, through which, as through eyes, we can observe the Lord's way, lest we wander into bypaths.

Verse 27. At last he now comes to the reason why he has exhorted so earnestly to the pursuit of wisdom: namely, that, relying upon it, a person may guard himself against sexual immorality. Its atrocious and grave danger he describes by a comparison with a like thing. For the prostitute

[1632 witness | PDF p. 526 | printed columns 1036-1037]

is compared to a pit or a well, the comparison being amplified by the adjuncts of depth and narrowness. For just as rash and heedless people fall into wells and pits, so those destitute of heavenly wisdom and the fear of God smear themselves all over with the foulest mud of sexual immorality. Its stench causes nausea and disgust not only to God, angels, and godly people, but even to most of the ungodly, Proverbs 6:30 and 1 Corinthians 6:18. Hence, after the adultery committed with Bathsheba, David's sons Amnon and Absalom threw off the yoke of obedience and reverence owed to their father. For he seemed worthless and contemptible to them. Hence also come the foulest diseases, particularly the Neapolitan disease, which make those afflicted with them hateful to everyone.

The enormity of this crime is amplified by the difficulty of emerging from it, since it resembles a deep and narrow pit. Those who have once fallen into it sink further and further every day, as in a marsh, until they reach the bottom of the infernal abyss, Proverbs 2:18–19 and 5:5. How difficult it is for a person to free himself from this pit is clearly seen from Samson. Although he had repeatedly experienced a prostitute's laying snares for his life, he still could not disentangle himself until at last he fell into the hands of his most savage enemies. A prostitute, then, is not unlike the pit into which Jeremiah was thrust, since no one will ever emerge from it unless drawn out by the ropes of divine mercy. Magistrates therefore ought to stop up pits and foul-smelling wells of this kind by the punishments due to them.

Verse 28. By another argument from a like thing, he amplifies the danger arising from prostitutes. He compares a prostitute to a robber or brigand who lies in wait for travelers, to strip passersby of their goods and kill them. This argument is therefore prefixed by אף, a mark of amplification and of increasing force in the discourse. For by comparing her to a pit and well he touched upon people's heedlessness; but by comparing her to brigands, he teaches that to our ignorance and unwary disposition is added the exceptional deceit of prostitutes. Indeed, this is what happens: just as some fall into this vice through their own heedlessness and ignorance of prostitutes' arts, without any prompting or enticements from prostitutes, so there are also those who, though forewarned of these deceits and averse to prostitutes, nevertheless perish, entrapped by their craft and cunning. Accordingly, imitating travelers, as we truly are and are called, let us equip ourselves with arms and choose our companions carefully, so that we may finish the pilgrimage of this life more safely. Let us not, as we read that Judah once did in Genesis 38, adopt Adullamites as our friends who will make themselves slaves to our lusts. An excellent picture and representation of this kind of robbery, of which the prophet speaks, appears in Proverbs 7:10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15; an example is found in Genesis 39:7, 10, 11, 12.

He brings another argument from the effects of prostitutes: that they increase the number of transgressors in the commonwealth. For a single prostitute is sufficient to corrupt many young men, Proverbs 7:26. Nor do they endeavor to corrupt only young men, but also to draw honorable married women and virgins into the same destruction as themselves.

This passage can suitably be applied to the demolition of brothels. For if a single prostitute is so great a plague to young men, what will a large gathering of them accomplish? And if they do so much harm by secretly practicing their deceits, how much more if they are granted permission to ravage openly?

### Translator's notes

- The source presents verses 22–28 as a single group of seven lemmata, followed by their shared exposition. The verb supplied in brackets in verse 26 makes explicit the elliptical Latin, *& oculi tui ad vias meas*, “and your eyes to my ways.” The later explanation confirms the sense of attention.
- The small Greek type on PDF 523 is read as προφυλακτικῇ, with a dative ending, and rendered “by preventive instruction.” The ending is faint and warrants qualified human review; it has not been silently regularized into the more familiar adverb προφυλακτικῶς.
- *Scortatio* is rendered “sexual immorality,” and *scortum* “prostitute.” Cartwright's application includes adultery as well as prostitution. “Strange woman” renders the lemma's *aliena*.
- The anomalous Latin forms *Salomon* before *adductum* and *qui quid mirum* in the parenthesis about parental love are retained in the transcription; their evident contextual sense governs the English. The source's abbreviated *Apoll.* is expanded as Apollos. The printed reference Proverbs 6:30 is retained without silent correction.
- The statement about David's sons follows Cartwright's explanation of the consequences of David's adultery. The wording “after the adultery committed with Bathsheba” leaves David as the understood agent; it does not attribute that adultery to his sons. “Neapolitan disease” preserves the source's historical disease name.
- At the PDF 522–523 boundary, the source divides *exhauriat* after *ex-*; the entire word and its English equivalent “drained” are assigned to PDF 523. At the PDF 525–526 boundary it divides *comparatur* after *com-*; the entire word and “is compared” are assigned to PDF 526. Catchwords, running heads, and the signature are excluded. The unit stops before the verse-29 lemma on PDF 526.
