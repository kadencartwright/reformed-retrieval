God, Fountain of Lights and Father of Mercies, grant repentance, we beseech
you, to miserable sinners, so that you may grant pardon to the penitent.
Open our eyes that we may see the wonders of your law. Be pleased so to be
present with and preside over us in defending the truth of your Word by the

your auspices understand what is true, do what is right, and finally attain

THE MORAL LAW BINDS BELIEVERS UNDER THE GOSPEL.

“Let us go on to perfection,” said the apostle. He certainly did not wish
saints built up in a more mature faith to occupy themselves again in laying
foundations already laid once, but rather to climb to the summit of
perfection. But woe to us! As the psalmist said of old, “The foundations are
destroyed.” Because of our impious contempt for the divine Word, not only
have the foundations of the temple been struck from heaven by God's most
just wrath in the immense storm that now tosses us, but in this dreadful
earthquake overwhelming us the very foundations of the church lie
undermined and utterly overturned by the wicked treachery of Christ-haters:
the most holy deity of Christ and of the Holy Spirit, the proper and full
satisfaction of our Redeemer, the divine authority of Scripture, and
finally the most saving use of the sacraments—what firm and altogether
immovable foundations of our faith! Yet now at last they have been shifted
from their place by the sacrilegious hand of Socinians, Antiscripturists,
Anabaptists, and other idle troublemakers. Among the sanctuary's other
treasures which must by no means be violated is God's law. The holy apostle
calls it holy, just, good, and spiritual, and the prophet calls it eternal,
just as Demosthenes wished laws to be gifts of the gods. The Manichaeans of
old said that it was carnal and evil, proceeded from the devil, and was
given to the Jews for their destruction. Our Libertines and Antinomians,
while constantly proclaiming the Spirit and grace, strip it of every power
both to bind and even to direct, with ignorance no less than shamelessness
and ingratitude.

This leaven is old. We discover the first shoots of this unhappy harvest
sprouting already in the apostles' own times if we attend to what the
apostle wrote to the Romans and what Peter and Jude wrote in their letters
against the first authors of this conflagration. What else do “Let us do
evil that good may come,” Romans 3:8, and “Shall we remain in sin that grace
may abound?” Romans 6:1, sound and savor of but pure Libertinism? The words
of Saint Peter in 2 Peter 2:18–19—“Speaking swollen words of vanity, they
entice through fleshly desires and other lusts by promising liberty while
they themselves are slaves of corruption”—portray vividly not only the
peculiar speech and conduct of the Gnostic Borborites of that age, but also
those of our Antinomians,

[1679 new-series fol. 16 | IA leaf 336]

so that Calvin rightly said, “I certainly would never have understood fully
and clearly the things described there unless I had recognized them in
these people.” Although this heresy was splendidly triumphed over by the
apostles and apostolic men, it was not altogether defeated. Its poison still
crept through the ravings of the Valentinians, Cerdonians, and Manichaeans.
At length it lay dead and buried for several centuries, until, just as it
first arose with the apostles' original preaching of the gospel and
gratuitous justification, so at the renewed assertion of that article by
Luther and the other leaders of Reformed religion the soul of Mani, by a
most unhappy transmigration, descended into Quintin, Coppin, other men of
the same dregs, and above all Johann Agricola of Eisleben, the first to come
forward as the Antinomians' standard-bearer—or rather, it was recalled from
hell.

What dreadful darkness this plague brought into the church and what
slaughter into the commonwealth is witnessed by Germany, by far the most
wretched of all lands. Witness the many thousands miserably killed and the
many wicked plunderings and butcheries which these “Free Brethren,” as some
liked to be called, freely committed while claiming to vindicate liberty,
though they themselves were most worthy of God as avenger. May the good God
avert this evil omen from us, lest in this second reformation of the church
which we contemplate we become like them in punishment, having surpassed
them in guilt. Whatever blasphemies those wicked enemies of the divine law
in former ages vomited against it with unclean mouths, our present
Antinomians lick up again. They boldly rejected Moses and said that law and
works pertain to the Roman court or judgment, not to Christian consciences.
They therefore wished all their people—especially their hearers, their
elect, and most of all their “blessed”—to be exempt from the law and the
obedience due it. Our people sing the same tune. In God's kingdom, that is,
the church, they acknowledge no law except the law of the new creation, the
law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus, and the law of love. They assert
that the law, especially the Mosaic law, has been completely abrogated,
deprived of every power to direct, much less to bind, made dead through
Christ's death, and consequently no less deadly in itself than to us. They
cry that those who press its right and power to bind and our duty to obey it
act as legal preachers and, so far as they can, make people hypocrites and
even papists.

Among the papists, Suarez, Bellarmine, and Vázquez vigorously slander us.
Because we hold that justification rests on faith alone, to whom sins are
not imputed, they allege that we likewise hold the righteous bound by no
divine law but entirely freed from it. They gather various passages from
Luther and Calvin as though these supported the claim, but lie splendidly,
as will be clear to anyone who examines either the confessions of the
Reformed churches, the writings of our theologians, or the very passages
they cite from Luther and Calvin. We therefore have no reason to fear those
Tridentine thunderbolts: “If anyone says that God's commandments do not
pertain to Christians, let him be anathema. If anyone says that a justified
person is not bound to observe the commandments but only to believe, let him
be anathema. If anyone says that Jesus Christ was given to humanity as a
Redeemer in whom they should trust, but not as a Legislator whom they should
obey, let him be anathema.” These strike the Antinomians' throat; they pose
no danger to us.

I indeed acknowledge that Luther, in keeping with his temperament as in
other matters,

[1679 new-series fol. 17 | IA leaf 337]

spoke especially boldly and hyperbolically here. He uniquely cultivated
that sweetest article of gratuitous justification through faith without
works of the law, from which he had drawn so much comfort. Hence he said
several things more strongly and sometimes less cautiously about the law as
though abrogated, for two reasons:

1. The law contributes nothing to the justification of a sinner.

2. Its curse and threats customarily torment the soul of a penitent
believer.

The first sense applies when he calls the doctrine of works a doctrine of
demons, when he says, “The law has simply been abrogated,” and elsewhere,
looking to Christ, “I am entirely holy and pure, knowing absolutely nothing
about the law.” Elsewhere again: “Before Christ the law is holy; after Christ
it is death. Therefore, where Christ comes, we must know absolutely nothing
about the law. Without faith in Christ the law is deadly, not because the
law is evil but because it cannot justify.”

The following statements occur in the second sense: “Law, if you wish to
ascend into the kingdom of conscience, rule there, accuse it of sins, and
remove the heart's joy, you act beyond your office. Do not touch the
conscience, where there is no law. A person never sins more horribly than in
the article in which he begins to feel and understand by law. The chief art
and wisdom of Christians is not to know the law, to be ignorant of works
and all active righteousness, especially when conscience wrestles with
God's judgment. When conscience is terrified by law, consult neither reason
nor law. Outside conscience we ought to make the law a god; within
conscience it is truly the devil.”

These and many similar things occur throughout his writings, especially his
commentaries on Galatians, where he dealt with the papists concerning the
article of justification before the Antinomian plague was yet raging. Once
that plague raised its head more openly and spread its poison more widely,
he not only spoke more cautiously in his later writings, especially the
commentaries on Genesis, but defended the law's authority most fiercely and
restrained, to use his word, the Antinomians' insanity. The papists seize
those earlier passages to brand us with disgrace, and Antinomians to prop up
their opinions; both not only snatch them greedily but distort them
impiously.

Will Calvin ever pass over into the Libertines' camp, when, as Augustine
once wrote against the adversaries of the law, Calvin wrote against these
very people two whole books no less full of zeal than of piety and learning?
Should Luther, the hammer of this heresy, ever be reckoned among Antinomians,
even in a dream? He recited the Decalogue daily like a little child,
compelled Agricola of Eisleben to recant, held six public disputations
against his heresy, and wrote a fairly long letter in which, to show how
greatly he abhorred it, he appealed to his own and his followers'
expositions, catechisms, confessions, and apologies, and finally to their
regular custom of singing, painting, printing, and teaching children to
recite the Decalogue. Let the Antinomian therefore agree with Antichrist
only this far: the lies which papists forge against the Reformed are
confirmed by the Antinomians in themselves, to no less injury to the divine
law than supreme disgrace to the Reformed churches. To remove both, so far
as I can, so that the law's authority may stand firm and the church's
reputation and dignity remain uninjured, I have proposed this thesis—which I
would seal even with my blood—for explanation and defense:

[1679 new-series fol. 18 | IA leaf 338]

THE MORAL LAW BINDS BELIEVERS UNDER THE GOSPEL.

The psalmist's reasoning has always seemed divine to me: “They make your law
void; therefore I love your commandments more than gold, even the purest
gold.” The adversaries' fury ought to kindle the godly person's zeal. The
more they attack truth and endeavor to overthrow God's most holy law, the
more vigorously, as the apostle exhorts, we should contend for the faith
once delivered to the saints and devote ourselves not only to observing the
law but, as matters now require, to preserving it with all our power.

We do not have especially noble adversaries in this arena. Calvin describes
them in this way: “These men are uneducated and ignorant, not greatly
practiced in cultivating letters; one of their leaders would gladly have
endured becoming a doorkeeper.” Even now it is mostly little men of this
same bran who prosecute this cause, most hostile enemies of letters and law
alike. Perhaps there will be less glory in crushing them, but there ought to
be no less concern for defending truth, especially since the question is
noble and sufficiently difficult to explain. Just as Augustine found it
difficult to establish free will without meanwhile destroying grace, so in
this controversy there is great danger that, while we oppose the
Antinomians too fiercely concerning the law's obligation, we grant far too
much to the papists in gratuitous justification. To steer more safely past
both rocks, I judge that in explaining the thesis we should first discuss:

1. The law's obligation.

2. The persons who are bound.

3. Some matters concerning the obligation itself, as briefly as possible.

As to the first, the law called ancient is divided into commandments,
statutes, and judgments—that is, moral, ceremonial, and forensic law. The
first regarded Israelites as human beings; the second as members of that
church; the third as citizens of that commonwealth.

Although some forensic laws command concerning immutable matters common to
all nations and contain moral equity, and so are of perpetual right and bind
Christians by force of their subject matter, as some have thought, or by
that law's authority, as Piscator held, most of them concern mutable matters
proper to the Israelite people at the time when their polity still stood.
There is no doubt that when that polity ceased, they ceased with it. The
scepter has now been removed from Judah, and the ruler from between his
feet, so Christians are by no means bound to such a government. Rather, as
the apostle says, they are free for obedience “to every human institution.”
Since the dividing wall was dissolved by Christ's death, and the judicial
law was part of that wall insofar as it separated the Israelite people from
the rest as sacred to God, its obligation must likewise have been dissolved
for us to that extent. “There is neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor
free, but we are all one in Christ Jesus.”

But that intermediate material, like the temple veil at his death,

[1679 new-series fol. 19 | IA leaf 339]

principally concerned the ceremonial law, about which there is less
controversy. Although duties to be performed under the new covenant are
sometimes announced by the prophets through those ceremonial rites—as can
be seen in Isaiah 66:21, Zechariah 14:16–18, the final nine chapters of
Ezekiel, and almost all the closing passages of the other prophecies—this
was done according to that age's idiom. Gospel grace was expressed through
the typical ceremonies then in force to express the meaning of those types,
not to establish their continuation after Christ's coming and death. I do
not anxiously dispute the precise time when they ceased, since I acknowledge
that others have treated it more laboriously. For my purpose it is enough
that this heavy yoke has been shaken from our necks, Acts 15:20; these
shadows flew away at the coming of the body, Colossians 2:17, first becoming
dead and at length deadly. “If you are circumcised, Christ will profit you
nothing,” Galatians 5:2.

Those rites and sacrifices chiefly contained two things:

1. Confession of one's own guilt.

2. A typical prefiguration of the Messiah.

After his coming, the latter vanished completely. Consequently, if those
ceremonies still remained, only the former would remain: they would accuse
us of denying Christ and his advent. The ceremonial law would then deserve
to be called a yoke and a hostile handwriting against Christians much more
than against Jews of old. It therefore ceased altogether both because it
expired of itself when the time appointed for it had elapsed,

and because it was abrogated by numerous testimonies of the new covenant.

It was abrogated not only with regard to the opinion of righteousness—once
this was removed Luther held, less correctly, that it could continue—but
also with regard to use. Yet with regard to effect, as Calvin most correctly
says, it is never abrogated. We joyfully experience its effect in Christ,
while gratefully acknowledging that a great part of Christian liberty
consists in our liberation from its burden and use. Nothing is thereby
detracted from its holiness or dignity; rather, the splendor of both is more
clearly illustrated, as the flower ripens into fruit and the shadow, when
the body arrives, is not so much abolished as perfected.
