# Whether It Is Lawful to Marry a Deceased Brother's Widow

*A fresh translation of the complete Latin consilium in CO X/1 235–238.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 235]

What God has pronounced about this matter in his law is not obscure. Those
are bad interpreters who explain it to mean that the woman must not be taken
from the bed of her former husband, or that, if she has been repudiated, her
marriage is unlawful while her husband is still alive. For it is not fitting
to twist into different meanings statements pronounced in the same place
with the same words.

God forbids uncovering the nakedness of the wife of one's father, paternal
uncle, son, or nephew (Lev. 18:16). When he delivers the same sentence about
a brother's wife in precisely the same words, it is absurd to invent different
meanings. Thus, if it is unlawful to have the wife of one's father, son,
paternal uncle, or nephew in marriage, the same must be held about a
brother's wife, concerning whom an entirely similar law was delivered in one
context and tenor.

I am not unaware of the source from which those who hold this view took the
occasion for their error. In another place (Deut. 25:5), God commands that if
anyone dies without children, the brother who survives must take the widow so
as to raise up offspring from her for the dead man. They have wrongly and
ignorantly restricted this to full brothers, when God instead designates
other degrees of kinship. The idiom of the Hebrew language is well known: it
generally includes all blood relatives under the name *brothers*. Indeed, the
Latins of old also called different kinds of cousins by that name.

Therefore, the law now under discussion concerning marriage to a deceased
brother's wife is directed only to those relatives who are not otherwise
barred from such a marriage. In order that the dead man's name should not
perish, God did not intend to permit incestuous marriages which he had
condemned elsewhere. These two propositions will consequently agree well
together: a full brother is forbidden to marry his brother's widow, and yet
more distant relatives are bound by right of kinship to raise up offspring
for the dead where the marriage is otherwise free under the law's
prescription.

For this reason Boaz married Ruth, who had previously been married to his
kinsman. It is sufficiently clear from the history that the law applied to
all the relatives. But if anyone still contends that full brothers are
included in this number, by the same reasoning a daughter-in-law would have
had to be married to her father-in-law, a nephew's wife to his paternal
uncle, and even a stepmother to her stepson—which is shameful to say.

If someone objects that Judah's sons Er, Onan, and Shelah were full brothers,
and that Tamar nevertheless married two of them, the answer is

### [CO X/1, p. 236]

at hand: Judah, following the common and accepted custom of the nations, did
not rightly use this liberty. It is sufficiently clear from the histories of
all times that shameful mixtures existed in marriages among eastern peoples,
with modesty neglected. Judah was therefore drawn by bad custom, as commonly
happens, to give the same woman as wife to his second son who had previously
been the firstborn's wife. God indicates that this outrage also prevailed
among the nations when he condemns incests. I therefore retain this as
indubitable: marriage to the widow of a full brother was forbidden by the law
of Moses.

Those who wish Christians today to be released from this law object from Paul
(Gal. 5:1) that we must stand firm in the liberty to which we were called and
which Christ obtained for us. But they ignorantly apply to the present case
what Paul offered with a very different end and purpose. He is speaking there
about ceremonies, by the observance of which false teachers pressed
consciences into servitude as though they were necessary for the worship of
God. There is nothing similar in this question.

Their further objection that we are not subject to civil laws is easily
dissolved. The law now at issue must not simply be numbered among civil laws,
because it is drawn from the common law of nations and from the propriety of
nature, as is inferred from its preface. Since lust had swept all the
neighboring nations into incests, God prefaces his commendation of chastity to
his people in this way: “I am the Lord your God. You shall not walk according
to the custom of the land of Egypt or according to the practice of the land
of Canaan.” He next adds which and how many degrees of blood relationship or
relationship by marriage bar men and women from marriage.

If someone objects in turn that a law enacted particularly for the Israelites
should not be accounted part of the law of nations, especially since it was
despised among other peoples, I answer that the corruption which prevailed
widely in the East does not deserve the name *law of nations*, since it was
barbarism and forgetfulness of chastity and shame. What is natural cannot be
erased by any consensus or custom.

It must therefore be maintained that the prohibition now in question is not
among those which can be abrogated according to the circumstances of times
and places. Rather, it flows from the very fountain of nature and is founded
upon the general principle of all laws, which is perpetual and inviolable.
God indeed testifies that the custom which had become established among the
Egyptians and profane nations displeases him. Why is this, except that the
sense of nature itself rejects and spurns a foul practice even though it has
been approved by human votes?

Long ago, when the emperor Claudius had taken his niece Agrippina as his wife,
he arranged for a senatorial decree to be passed granting liberty for such
marriages, in order to remove the disgrace. Yet no one was found who wished
to imitate his example except one freedman alone. The case is different, I
admit. But I have related it to show that among profane nations

### [CO X/1, p. 237]

the law of nature was indissoluble. From this, fair and moderate people should
infer that a great distinction must be established between civil laws and the
law of nature, which is, as it were, innate in the human race and fixed in
everyone's heart.

The solemn admonition by which God separates his people from the profane
nations, namely the Egyptians and Canaanites (Lev. 18:3), bears upon this. In
the land of Canaan brothers joined themselves to sisters, disregarding what
is honorable. Ancient writings establish that there was no more modesty among
the Egyptians. Therefore, the law by which God clearly withdraws his people
from the pollutions of the nations ought not to be accounted a civil law.

To make the matter more clearly known, Paul sets the law of nature itself
before us in a very small matter. When he teaches that it is shameful and
unseemly for women to appear in public unless veiled, he commands us to
consult nature as to whether it is fitting for women to go about in public
with shorn heads. Finally he adds, “Nature does not permit it” (1 Cor. 11:14).
I therefore do not see why, under the pretext that it is a civil law,
something which belongs to the law of nature and therefore cannot be changed
ought to be abolished.

I come now to the Roman laws, which reject marriages of this kind. If their
rationale were founded upon the advantage of one people, the usage of a
particular time, a present necessity, or other circumstances, they could be
abrogated for new reasons, or their observance could be relaxed for particular
persons by an individual privilege. But because perpetual natural propriety
alone was considered in enacting them, not even a dispensation would be
tolerable. It can be decreed that something may be done with impunity, just as
it is in a prince's discretion to remit penalties. But no legislator will
bring it about that what nature declares vicious is not vicious.

If it is objected that we are not forbidden from such marriages in the New
Testament, I answer that marriage between a father and daughter is not
forbidden there either, nor is a mother prohibited from marrying her son. Will
it therefore be indiscriminately lawful for blood relatives to have sexual
relations with one another? But this objection is easily dissolved by Paul's
testimony. Reproaching the Corinthian who had polluted himself by marriage to
his stepmother, Paul says, “You tolerate a deed which is not even heard of
among the nations” (1 Cor. 5:1).

If someone replies that the present question is not about marriage between a
stepson and stepmother, the answer will be irrelevant. I intend nothing other
than to set before us disgrace in the example of the nations, so that we may
at least be ashamed if more shame and moderation are seen among them. And
certainly one admonition of Paul is sufficient for me in this matter. Writing
to the Philippians, he says, “Whatever things are true, whatever honorable,
whatever fitting, whatever of good name, if there is any virtue and if there
is any praise, think on these things” (Phil. 4:8).

Having discussed the question itself in general and simply, I shall now
direct my words to the Most Illustrious Prince. I shall both humbly ask and
solemnly beseech His Highness to dedicate himself and his affections

### [CO X/1, p. 238]

all completely to God's Word. I am indeed persuaded that this desire arose
from a plausible reason, whether the virtues of each party fostered mutual
love or public advantage was also considered. It remains, however, for the
Most Illustrious Prince to temper and moderate this affection—whatever its
origin, even if it arose from a good principle—so that it does not stumble in
its progress.

Paul reminds us that many things are lawful which are nevertheless not
expedient. But when everything has been weighed with a calm and composed mind,
it will be clear that in this case what is not expedient is also not lawful.
