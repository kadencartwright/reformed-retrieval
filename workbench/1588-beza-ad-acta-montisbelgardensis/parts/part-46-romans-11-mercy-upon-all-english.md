## Part XLVI: Romans 11—Mercy upon All

### The Württemberg Doctors

Romans 11: “God shut up all things under unbelief so that he might have mercy upon ALL.”

### Theodore Beza’s Antithesis

Romans 11: The context itself clearly shows that the apostle is not treating every human being individually but wished to divide the human race into two parts, Jews and Gentiles, from whom God gathers his church through mercy. Both were worthy to perish, so that no one who is saved is saved otherwise than through mercy. Indeed, the statement that God shut up all under unbelief proves our assertion: human beings fell into this misery neither contrary to God’s will nor by chance. Yet all the fault for this must be ascribed to humanity’s voluntary fall and its descendants’ consequent bondage to sin.

### Andreae’s Observations

1. Paul’s words are so manifest that they require no interpretation when he says, “God shut up all things under sin so that he might have mercy upon all.” As he does not say, “He shut up some under sin,” but “all things,” so likewise he does not say “that he might have mercy upon some” but “upon all,” showing that his mercy is sincerely offered to all and God wills all to believe and be saved, just as all sinned.

2. This assertion is simply impious, horrendous to hear, and contrary to God’s express and revealed word. Whatever God forbids, he does not will anyone to do. Our first parents therefore transgressed the command given in Paradise and fell into this misery while God did not will it and against his will. Thus teaches the Holy Spirit in Holy Scripture, and if even an angel from heaven teaches otherwise, let him be accursed.

3. If your doctrine is true, all fault cannot be transferred to humanity but to God the creator, who according to your opinion created and ordained them by his absolute decree for sin and damnation, as one who did not will them to act otherwise, be converted, or be saved. This is horrendous and abominable to hear.

### Theodore Beza’s Reply to Andreae’s Observations

[Marginal note: To 1.]

Andreae cannot bear that in Paul’s statement, “God shut up all in stubbornness so that he might have mercy upon all,” Paul should be said to speak of the whole body of the elect—as all are presumed to be in the ordinary ministry—distributed into two kinds of human beings, Jews and Gentiles. He instead wishes it understood of every individual from every people, with absolutely no one excluded. But I think it providential that he cited this passage, by which the falsity of his interpretation is most manifestly refuted. First, Paul’s whole disputation there so openly shows that he treats kinds comprising individuals, not every individual of those kinds, that wishing to raise controversy about it is sheer obstinacy. And why would the apostle overturn by this statement what throughout chapter 9 he denied pertained to every individual Jew and, by adducing Ishmael and Esau, proved pertained only to those whom he calls children of promise, that is, those destined for the promise? He does not stop at either the elect’s faith or the reprobate’s unbelief but ascends and leads the reader all the way to God’s supreme will. Yet there is certainly no depth regarding the reprobate if one establishes some cause inhering in them—whether corruption, unbelief, or something else—not only of damnation but of the decree of reprobation, whose foreknowledge in God is the initiating cause of his decree.

Then, in this passage the apostle establishes *apeitheia*—which Andreae would have translated more rightly as “stubbornness” than “unbelief,” since the former extends more widely—as the point of departure from which all these are called to mercy and consequently to faith. But stubbornness includes the preaching that is stubbornly resisted, which Andreae understands solely of the gospel’s grace. Thus his fiction that every individual is a despiser of the gospel will be true only when he proves the gospel was proclaimed to every individual among so many profane peoples then—indeed, from the world’s creation—when, as the same apostle writes to the Ephesians, they were aliens from Israel’s commonwealth, strangers to the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world. And will he dare say every individual Jew rebelled against the preaching of the gospel? Nothing was further from the apostle’s meaning, nor did this have anything to do with his proposed question concerning the Gentiles’ calling and the Jews’ cutting off. He signified that both the calling of the Gentiles, of which the apostle was the chief minister, and the restoration of the Jewish nation—which was to be cut off when the apostle wrote these things but afterward finally restored—proceed from God’s mere mercy. The Gentiles were called to the gospel’s grace when they were anything but obedient, holding God’s truth, of which Paul speaks in Romans 1:18, in unrighteousness—

—while God will restore the Jews in his time, however guilty of signal stubbornness. He will do both by an altogether admirable counsel: he used the Jews’ stubbornness to substitute the Gentiles in their place, and conversely will provoke the Jews through the Gentiles’ obedience to emulate them and receive the gospel in his time.

But how do all these things support Andreae’s dream? Even if his interpretation were true despite these considerations, every individual of every nation in every age would first have needed to be called to the gospel, which every individual then resisted, and afterward to be called from this unbelief to mercy—indeed, to have obtained mercy. For all whom Paul says were shut up in stubbornness so that God might have mercy upon them all, he earlier said partly “received mercy,” namely the Gentiles, and partly “will receive mercy” in their own time, namely the Jews. Who does not see how ridiculous it is to attribute this to every age and every individual? Andreae draws these things to God’s eternal decree, which must always have been one and the same. Furthermore, if rebellion against the offered gospel, the worst species of unbelief, is said to precede the call to mercy in every individual, at least two calls must be established for every individual, with *apeitheia* standing between them. This again is absurd and ridiculous, as is clear from the many examples of those granted immediately to follow God when he called.

Moreover, how will the distinction cohere with this position that Andreae established shortly before, in observation 5 on 1 John 2, between other sinners and those sinning through unbelief? He said the former sin in such a way that they can be helped somehow, namely through faith, while the latter are condemned because they cannot be helped otherwise, as those who reject the deliverer. But behold: according to Andreae’s present view, which conflicts with his previous one, the apostle will have said that every individual must first be stubborn before being effectually called to mercy.

Finally, so that all may observe how carelessly and negligently Andreae composed these observations: when in my reply I answered the passage from Romans 11:32 objected against me, he passes to the other passage, Galatians 3:22. He so mixes one with the other that, whether through drowsiness or knowingly and deliberately, he substitutes the final passage from Romans 11 for what is written in Galatians, an interpolation of the greatest importance. For when writing to the Romans he said, “so that he might have mercy upon all,” but to the Galatians, “so that the promise through faith in Jesus Christ might be given to believers.” By these words Paul restricts the whole body of those whom God shut up under stubbornness—not so that they perish in it but are called from it to mercy—to believers alone—

—Faith does not belong to all, but only to those whom God draws. It is not of the one willing and running but of God who has mercy, who has mercy upon whom he wills and hardens whom he wills. “But why,” Andreae says, “did the apostle not rather write, ‘He shut up some,’ and, ‘that he might have mercy upon some’?” Because he treats not individual persons but the elect universally, both from the Gentiles already called and from the Jews to be restored in their own time and united with the Gentiles into one body.
