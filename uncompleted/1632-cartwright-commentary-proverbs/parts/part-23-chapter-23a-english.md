CHAPTER TWENTY-THREE.

Verse 1. When you sit down to eat with a ruler, consider what is before you.

Verse 2. For you will have put knives to your throat, if you are a man of appetite.

Verse 3. Do not be possessed by desire for his delicacies, since it is food of deceit.

In שכין (*knives*) there is a metaphor transferred from knives, with which a person's throat is cut, to surfeiting and drunkenness, by which people are destroyed, or at least weakened. In לחם (*bread*) there is a synecdoche of the part. In לחם כזבים (*food of deceit*) there is a metaphor transferred from flattering speech, by which someone deceives another, to foods

[1632 witness | PDF p. 511 | printed columns 1006-1007]

whose pleasantness draws unwary people into surfeiting.

If anyone finds it surprising that Solomon lowers his precepts to matters so seemingly humble, let him see what was previously noted on chapter six, verse 1. Yet this matter cannot seem of little importance or unworthy of Solomon's giving precepts about it, if we either consider its fruits, which are as exceedingly harmful as they are abundant, or hear Christ himself so earnestly warning his Apostles that their hearts should not be weighed down with surfeiting and drunkenness.

Although in everything we readily fall into sin because of the depravity of our nature, in nothing do we do so more than in the desire for drink and food, especially when exquisite dishes are served in great variety. Hence Job offered sacrifices to God for his sons, fearing that God's anger might blaze against them because of some intemperance while feasting. Therefore the Prophet, well aware of our propensity to this vice, seasonably warns us not to be so captivated by the delicacies of a ruler's table, which is usually lavishly furnished, when admitted to it, that we commit anything unworthy of our most holy profession.

The exhortation, not merely stated but repeated several times, teaches that the concern here is not so much with food and drink, which descend into the stomach and are afterward expelled into the privy, and from which we gain nothing if we eat or lose nothing if we abstain, as with the corrupt desire which, proceeding from the heart, defiles us, as is clear from verse 30.

People commonly drive all cares and anxieties from their minds when they recline at table, so that they may enjoy themselves more fully and freely. I do not oppose their laying aside worldly care for a time, lest they eat the bread of sorrow, Psalm 127, which is a fault. Meanwhile, however, they must hold most closely to this care which Solomon commends to us, lest they either overwhelm the stomach with too plentiful a meal or, by tasting something of every dish amid a great variety of delicacies, so injure it that, because the qualities of the different foods conflict, they cannot digest them and reduce them to chyle. Hence he wishes the guest diligently to consider the powers and nature of foods and drinks.

He amplifies and strengthens this care from the subject: *if you are a man of appetite*. Those, therefore, who habitually prepare themselves for an approaching banquet by a prolonged abstinence from food and drink must use all the greater caution, lest there happen to them what is celebrated in a common proverb among us, that two scantier meals make the third a glutton, for thus we say: “Two hungry meals make, the third a glotton.” This happens to very many who stuff themselves so full while feasting that they cannot get rid of the effects of their surfeit even two days afterward. This must be observed by people of every age, but especially by the young, who, abounding in heat in the stomach, are endowed [with an especially strong appetite]; and next by those who have suddenly been raised from a humble condition to a royal table, of whom the preceding proverb speaks.

The reasons follow on which the admonition rests. The first is drawn from the attendant injury which the glutton inflicts upon himself. For since through luxury and intemperance he commonly loses his health, and even life itself, he in a manner lays violent hands upon himself, shameful to say! The table which was given him to sustain life becomes a snare in which he is entangled; wine and delicacies, which pass so pleasantly through the throat into the belly, are like a knife with which the throat is cut. Therefore, we who resent having injury inflicted on us by others should not allow ourselves to become, in a manner, our own killers, and strike at our own throats with the knives with which we cut our food.

The other reason is drawn from the attendant pleasantness

[1632 witness | PDF p. 512 | printed columns 1008-1009]

of foods, drinks, sweetmeats, and other such stimulants of the palate, which by their sweetness and agreeable taste slip so gradually into the stomach that they overwhelm it before one can feel satiety.

But if, when an earthly lord observes us, we must take care to eat moderately, how much more, since God's eye is always fixed upon us, ought we at every table to show ourselves worthy of such an observer? And if, when reclining at a human table, we must take such care to conduct ourselves honorably, when invited to the Lord's table we must take much greater care to approach in an orderly fashion, as befits Christians, lest instead of the life we seek we bring diseases and death upon ourselves. Yet the character of this table and that one is very different and unlike. There we sin by taking too much; here, however, we are found guilty if we do not receive everything offered to us. Sacred doctrine too is the Lord's table; when called to it, we must take diligent care not to abuse it to our own destruction.

### Translator's notes

The English proverb is already English in the Latin witness; its printed spelling is retained. The witness separates *appetenti aquā*; the context strongly suggests *appetentia quā maximè* (“with an especially strong appetite”), with the bar abbreviating the final nasal in *quam*. The English brackets the reconstructed sense while the Latin retains the printed spacing.

The printed reference *versu 30* is retained as “verse 30.” It may be an irregular rendering of “verse 3,” whose prohibition of desire fits the immediate argument; no silent correction is made. Printed forms including *proculentorumq́ue*, *irritetúr*, and *videndendum* remain in the Latin; their intended senses are rendered in English.
