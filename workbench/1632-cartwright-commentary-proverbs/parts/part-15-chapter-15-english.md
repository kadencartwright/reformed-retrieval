CHAPTER XV.

Verse 1. A gentle answer turns away a blaze of wrath; but a troublesome word stirs up anger.

In מענה רך (*a gentle answer*) there is a synecdoche of the part for every discourse employed to calm fury or a blaze of wrath. In דבר (*word*) there is likewise a synecdoche of the part, a word standing for an entire discourse.

The sum is to show the way by which headlong and rash anger may be restrained.

Since [the anger of man does not work the righteousness of God], James 1:20, it is worthwhile to devise a means by which we may put a bit upon so harmful and unbridled a beast, lest, rising up against us, it bite us with its tooth. How great its force is may be seen in Lamech alone, Genesis 4:23, but more so in Haman, who, angered by an offense from Mordecai alone, wished utterly to destroy the whole nation of the Jews, Esther 3:5–6. Although many things serve this purpose, there is nevertheless nothing whose use in this business is greater or more frequent than that of the tongue, which has the greatest power either to rouse anger or to tame it. Let proof of this be afforded by the fact

[1632 witness | PDF p. 274 | printed columns 522–523]

that anger, however kindled and smoking, is nevertheless extinguished by gentle and mild speech. The truth of this matter is first shadowed forth by a simile. For although it may appear strange that anger, which is hard, Genesis 49:7, should be unbarred by a gentle answer, nevertheless you may see balls, whether iron or stone, fired from engines, if they strike a hard object, either break it or themselves be broken; by contrast, if they are hurled into dust or wool, their blows are blunted and reflected without damage. An example of this matter appears in David, who, inflamed with anger against Nabal's whole household, was so melted by Abigail's calm and gentle speech that he became milder than a lamb itself. The force and power of this gentle answer shines more brightly in proud people, such as the Ephraimites in Judges 8:1–3. Indeed, even among the reprobate, as though among flints, it is of so great a weight that it has subdued them even while they raged with anger and has, as it were, compelled them into the circuit of reason.

There are indeed monsters and prodigies of humankind, rather than human beings, who do not grow tame through gentle speech, as is evident in the betrayer, Matthew 26:15. Their ruined malice is noted here by Solomon as though in passing.

But if anger grows mild through gentler words, how much more through benefits? First, indeed, you may see people raging with anger restored to favor with their most hostile enemies by this very thing: that they were not harmed by those in whose power it lay to injure them, 1 Samuel 24:17 and 26:21. How much more will they be reconciled if they are also treated with a benefit? Romans 12:10; Genesis 32, 33.

This is argued from the latter half-verse. Just as in the first part of the Proverb we are taught that anger already stirred and kindled is extinguished by sweetness of speech, so here we are taught that where there was no anger—indeed, where even a union of minds prevailed—anger is kindled by harsher and grief-bearing speech. Here it must be noted that there is so great an inclination and propensity toward anger in human minds that people are provoked by even one word, as though by a single syllable—I add, even by a mere nod and expression—in agreement with our proverb: *A word and a blow*. See an example of this matter in Judges 12:1, 4, 6, where because of one little word, or a taunt, 42,000 wanderers were slain; likewise 1 Samuel 1, 25, 10, 11, and so forth. But if this happens because of a little word, much more because of misdeeds and blows.

Verse 2. The tongue of the wise sets forth knowledge well; but the mouth of fools belches out folly.

In לשון (*tongue*) and פה (*mouth*) there is a metonymy of the cause. In יביע (*belches or bubbles out*) there is a metaphor from a spring, which bubbles out water in great quantity and with force. In אולת (*folly*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct for foolish words.

The sum is that by far the greatest difference between a wise person and a fool lies in the use of speech.

In a fool's silence—as Solomon says—the fool is not distinguished from a wise person, but in the use of speech they are found to differ wonderfully. Of the wise, however, it is affirmed that by speech they make good, or adorn, the matter about which they undertake to speak. For before the wise person opens his mouth, he first considers within himself and with his own mind what is to be said and how it is to be said. See Exodus 3:13. He sets a guard upon his mouth and establishes the fear of God as the gatekeeper of his lips, which does not grant permission for words to be brought into the open unless they have first proved themselves conformed to the rule of sound reason. For the wise weigh whether the place and time are suitable; they consider the character of the people before whom they speak; they weigh even their own words in the balance, or rather test them by the scale of the sanctuary; and they inquire whether these or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 275 | printed columns 524–525]

those are better fitted to the matter they are preparing. When about to praise a matter, they so arrange themselves and their speeches that nothing unworthy of the dignity of virtue is said and nothing is withdrawn from it that belongs to it by right. If someone should object that it sometimes falls to a wise person by vocation to deliver a discourse about a vicious matter, which no one could render good and praiseworthy by even the most artfully fashioned speech, I answer that the cause is made good when the matter's deformity is depicted in vivid colors so as to produce hatred and detestation of it. For just as a skillful painter merits no less praise for his art by portraying someone's deformity exactly and in vivid colors than by representing another person's beauty, so the speaker who casts down vices according to their deserts displays no less wisdom in speaking than one who extols virtue with just and due praise. And in treating evil deeds themselves, the wise know either how to magnify them so as to engender repentance, or how prudently to moderate them lest sinners be swallowed up. See an excellent example of this in Joseph, Genesis 50:20 and following. To this pertains what the Apostle teaches, Colossians 4:6.

*But the mouth of fools belches out*) It blurts out whatever comes into the cheek without any selection. For just as water flowing from a spring bursts forth with great force and the highest speed, so the fool, without selection of place, time, or persons, pours out whatever enters his mind without any meditation. By foolish treatment he makes a good cause in some manner bad, and a bad cause worse. If he brings forth anything that contains some goodness in itself, nevertheless, because the circumstances of persons, time, and place have been passed over and neglected, that good thing vanishes and becomes insipid.

Verse 3. In every place the eyes of Jehovah [are,] watching the evil and the good.

In עיני (*eyes*) there is a metaphor in which eyes are taken for God's knowledge or providence. In צפות (*watching*) there is likewise a metaphor drawn from those who are stationed in a watchtower to keep watch and look out for what is happening outside the city.

The sum is that nothing among all the things that happen in the world lies outside the embrace of divine providence.

Since God's creatures, the Angels, are described in Ezekiel as full of eyes both in front and behind, this much more befits God their creator, who is not so much full of eyes as wholly—as great as he is—an eye. Moreover, when his eyes are said to watch, this designates that God, just as he is most keen-eyed, applies himself to seeing through all things. For although God has no need to use any exertion of strength to know human affairs thoroughly, nor the aid of a higher place from which he might behold all things together and singly at one glance, nevertheless, to awaken our sluggishness and inertia, Scripture attributes to him the role of a watchman and places him in the highest heavens as though in a watchtower. From there he is even said to descend to learn of weightier cases, Genesis 11, 18.

Hence it is said in Psalm 121 that God does not even doze, much less sleep. For although watchmen, even while keeping watch, sometimes sleep or at least doze, this does not befall God. Nor is it without significance that he is said to keep watch in every place. Watchmen remain in only one place, from which they look out from their tower over a circuit of some miles; or, if they keep watch in different places, they nevertheless do it by turns of time. But God exercises the office of watchman in every place at once and at the same moment. Finally, watchmen perform their office and provide a report of things that lie some distance away from them, so that their eyesight

[1632 witness | PDF p. 276 | printed columns 526–527]

is sometimes deceived, especially if clouds or mist intervene. God is present in every place and is at hand to the things perceived by him, since all things are in him and he is in all things; for that reason he can in no way err, be misled, or be deceived. Furthermore, although God's providence enfolds all things within its embrace, the Prophet nevertheless, κατὰ περιοχήν (*by restriction*), in a certain manner confines God's watches to human beings because of the diligent and exact inspection he exercises over them. He looks upon the wicked no less in order to punish them than upon the good in order to reward them. See Psalm 139:7–14.

The use of this Proverb is, first, against those who say that all things happen and come to pass by fortune and chance, or by some instinct of nature placed and implanted in things.

Second, it especially answers the blasphemies of the wicked, who say that God does not see—that is, that he does not care greatly—but instead winks at their evil deeds and waits for repentance, and does not hurl his thunderbolts from heaven at each one of their crimes. See Psalm 10.

Third, it serves to aid the weaknesses of the godly. Because their minds lie amid evils for a long time, this thought comes upon them: that God does not exercise as much care for them as he professes in his freely given words and promises that he cares for them. Hence the frequent complaints that God acts deaf, hides his face, and has forgotten to show mercy.

Fourth, let this also teach us not to do anything wicked in corners or in the most inward and deeply hidden places that we would not do in the middle of public squares and before the sun.

Verse 4. The healing of the tongue [is] a tree of life; but perversity from it is like a breaking by the wind.

In לשון (*tongue*) there is a synecdoche of the part and a metonymy of the subject. In עץ חיים (*tree of life*) there is a metaphor drawn from the tree of life, which would have preserved perpetual life in Adam if he had remained in obedience to God. Likewise, in מרפא (*healing*) there is a metaphor from medicine. Likewise רוח (*wind*).

The sum is that, according to how the tongue is disposed, it has power to bring either a great benefit to one who is sick or harm to one who is well.

It is a great thing to restore a person grievously wounded in body to his former health, and surgery, by which this benefit is provided, is rightly valued very highly. Skilled physicians, who restore a body weakened by disease to its former condition, are valued still more, and not unjustly. How much more highly, then, should the tongue of a godly man be valued, by which a wounded and, as it were, transfixed soul is thoroughly healed; indeed, by which body and soul are together sustained when they are failing? For it often happens that when the soul is struck, the body's health, through sympathy, struggles with disease. Hence it also happens that when the soul is refreshed and strengthened, by that same work the body grows strong and recovers its health.

The sickness of the soul is contracted in two ways: either by the guilt of sin and the conscience, by which it is disturbed and remains restless, or by the punishment of sin and affliction that God sends. Whichever of these troubles a person, its remedy and medicine are sought from the tongue. See 2 Thessalonians 5:14.

Although this belongs to all the godly in common, it belongs to God's Ministers for a singular reason. For their tongue ought to be like a perfumer's workshop or apothecary, from which every kind of medicine is to be sought, both θεραπευτικὸν (*therapeutic*) and διεγερτικὸν (*stimulating*). See Isaiah, where it is said that the Prophet knows how to bring forth a word in its season by which he may refresh the weary and exhausted. See also chapter 57:22–23; likewise 61:1 and following; Luke 4:8; Job 33:22–25.

This is confirmed by the latter half-verse

[1632 witness | PDF p. 277 | printed columns 528–529]

from the contrary, because a wound inflicted by a venomous tongue is lethal and breaks a person, just as a violent wind, Job 8:2, casts down and destroys trees and even strong oaks by its force. It does this in a certain inescapable manner that no one may withstand. For who might guard against an enemy's sword and spear? But who can take precautions against the stroke of a tongue, by which a person is assailed both from afar and also secretly and in concealment? The sole defense against the tongue's stroke is to pray to God that he may render us sheltered from the scourge of the tongue, as in Psalm 120: *Lord, deliver my soul from a lying lip and a deceitful tongue*; but especially from the venomous tongue of false prophets.

And since by God's just judgment it happens that with the measure by which we measure others it shall be measured back to us, care must be taken that we do not wound others with the tongue.

Let there therefore be a contest between the sound and healing tongue and the poisonous tongue, lest the latter have more power toward death than the former toward life.

Verse 5. A fool despises the instruction of his father; but he who observes correction [is] very astute.

In אביו (*his father*) there is a synecdoche of the species for those who hold authority. Likewise, in ינאץ (*rejects*) the word stands for every kind of rejection, whether through contempt, neglect, or hatred. In יערים (*will be prudent*) there is a metaphor drawn from those who are naked and turn themselves in every direction; thus a prudent person accommodates himself according to what is suitable in time, place, and so forth.

The sum is that by embracing or rejecting instruction and chastisement, the upright person is distinguished from the wicked.

We are born foolish and like untamed colts, Job 11. For just as they are useless for the purposes of this life, so we are unfit for heavenly things. But just as colts handed over to breakers to be tamed grow gentle and are rendered useful for human purposes, so people are brought to good conduct by instruction and correction. Yet just as some colts are found so refractory and prone to kick that they bring destruction and death upon themselves sooner than permit themselves to be tamed, so some people are found who would prefer to suffer every extremity and cast themselves headlong into the abyss of hell rather than be compelled and forced into the circuit and rule of the divine will. Therefore one who is a fool by nature, and foolish already, becomes twice as foolish—or rather insane—by refusing instruction and correction.

When he is said to despise instruction and chastisement, by that he understands every rejection of instruction and so forth, whether through hatred, contempt, carelessness, or sluggishness; finally, it includes whoever does not love it. For only he is held worthy of the name of wise who loves the one who instructs and chastens him. Therefore, he who prefers silver or gold, much less he who values his own petty pleasure more highly, despises it, and so forth. For although correction and chastisement are not in themselves so much to be sought, nevertheless, because of the precious fruit of wisdom that they bring, they are to be valued most highly. See Proverbs 3:11, 12, 13, 14.

They further amplify this folly from the subject who instructs and chastens, namely, the father, whose instruction and chastisement ought to be pleasing and acceptable especially to a son, both because of his singular authority and because of the inward love from which it proceeds. And by this manner of speech he designates the deplorable condition of that fool. For he does not admit even the instruction and chastisement of his own father when he advises him well and chastens him moderately. He certainly will not bear any other person's instruction and correction with an even temper; he does not admit that of a companion and brother, much less of one who is inferior to him. See the example of Sodom, Genesis 19:9; of Nabal, 1 Samuel 25; and 2 Chronicles 24:20–21 and 2 Chronicles 25:14–16.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 278 | printed columns 530–531]

Yet this folly is greater still, because he spurns not only his father’s authority, but rejects the command of God himself. See Proverbs 3:11.

He who keeps reproof, both by retaining it in memory and by expressing it in deeds, is at last prudent and astute—or shows himself astute, or, from the astuteness and prudence with which he is endowed, makes others prudent—just as the fool by his folly renders others foolish.

It must be observed that prudence and good sense are defined, not by the fact that someone receives instruction, but by the fact that he admits reproof and chastisement. For you may see very many who at first eagerly seize the teaching of piety and virtue, but who afterward, when they are pricked more sharply because of the corruption of their life—and especially when they ought to undergo the severity of ecclesiastical discipline and its salutary censure—are immediately offended and shake off the Lord’s yoke. In this class are also those upon whom Christ in Matthew 13 brands the mark of impiety: they joyfully embrace the Gospel as it rises and shines, but afterward, when the sun of persecution grows hot, they immediately fall away. And these are not unlike the man who undertakes the construction of a large building, yet does not reckon the expense with himself and in his mind, and afterward, with shame, abandons the building not yet finished and completed—the work he began—and wastes both labor and oil. The prudent man, by contrast, will refuse neither the instruction nor the chastisement even of one inferior and more abject. See Psalm 41:5, where David promises that he will esteem highly anyone’s reproof, provided it is just. See the example of Naaman, who acquiesces in the reproof of his servant, 2 Kings 5:2, and Genesis 20:16. For whoever and of whatever sort the person is who reproves, the prudent man judges truly and wisely that the chastisement itself proceeds from God.

But that he who observes reproof and chastisement will likewise observe teaching and instruction ought certainly not to be doubted, although the same conclusion does not always follow conversely.

Verse 6. In the house of the righteous there is great strength; but the produce of the wicked is disturbed by itself.

There seems to be a transposition of the proposition because of the placement of the verse afterward: the word בית should apparently be placed at the beginning of the verse. Here there is also a synecdoche of the house for the household and all the furnishings of the house, or rather a metonymy of the subject, from the opposite member.

The sum is that only possessions justly acquired are stable.

Just as much care and labor are expended in acquiring riches, so very much toil is spent in preserving them. Hence the various use of bolts, locks, bars, walls, bridges, and so forth, by which a house is fortified against the fraud of thieves and the violence and assault of brigands. Bricks and stones are also procured against the danger of fire. But of all fortifications none greater or more stable can be found or devised than when riches justly acquired are possessed by a righteous master. For the master’s righteousness is in place of all these things, and surpasses them all in firmness and safe custody. Hence even the righteous man’s booth, hut, and tent have more strength to repel every hostile and harmful force than the wicked man’s most robust and firmly fortified palace, citadels, and ramparts: the paper walls of the righteous surpass the iron and bronze walls of the wicked; the straw coverings of the former’s roofs surpass the plates and tiles of the latter; the former’s fastenings surpass the latter’s locks and bolts. If you ask the cause of this, Satan himself will indicate it to you—begrudgingly indeed, but truly and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 279 | printed columns 532–533]

in agreement with sacred Scripture—Job 1:11. You may see an example of this matter in the Patriarchs, Abraham and the others, who, although they dwelt in tents and were hated by the whole world, nevertheless God did not permit any evil to befall them; and the Israelites, although they dwelt under tabernacles in the wilderness, nevertheless, and so forth. See Psalm 90. See also the passage where God promises that he will be a protection to his people from the enemies surrounding them, when all the males were bound by law to set out for Jerusalem.

The riches, or revenues, of the wicked, however, besides being spoil for brigands—on which matter see much in Job—melt away of their own accord, even though no one lays a hand upon them. See Haggai 1:6. See also Job, where their house is compared to a spider’s web. See also Proverbs 10:2.

Verse 7. The lips of the wise scatter knowledge; but the mind of fools, that which departs from what is right.

In שפתי (*lips*) there is a customary synecdoche. Likewise in לב (*heart*). In the latter part there is an ellipsis of the verb יזרו, which occurs in the former part. In יזרו (*they scatter*) there is a metaphor taken from sowers, who scatter seed into cultivated fields.

The sum is that from the godly come good things, but from the ungodly evil things, abundantly both inwardly and outwardly.

The world, or the people in the world, are like a field, Matthew 13. The principal and nearly sole instrument for cultivating, tilling, and sowing it is the tongues, or lips, of human beings. The husbandmen are God, who sends forth the godly to scatter the good seed of piety and righteousness, and the devil, who rouses up the ungodly to disseminate tares. Of the godly it is affirmed here that they scatter knowledge. By this it is indicated, first, that all things are full of ignorance and lack of knowledge of God; but the godly carry out their precious seed of knowledge and cast it with a full hand into the furrows and ridges of the human heart. For although they are said to store wisdom within themselves, and not to bring it forth except at an opportune place and time, nevertheless they must not be counted among those husbandmen who observe the winds and clouds and set before themselves unnecessary difficulties and dangers that need not be feared. Rather, once they obtain the slightest opportunity for speaking and teaching, they seize it, leaving the success and outcome of the sowing to God and sustaining themselves with the good hope that God will bless their labor. Indeed, confidently and with undoubting trust, they are persuaded that the good seed they have cast will not perish. For besides the very great difference between this sacred seed and the earthly kind, there is also this difference: although no harvest sometimes follows earthly sowing, a harvest always follows that heavenly sowing, and it returns both to God and to the sower. For it is a sweet fragrance to God in Christ—to these, indeed, unto death, but to those unto life. Therefore, if husbandmen cast their own seed into the earth very freely and eagerly, why should we not cast ours? Hence Elihu compares the words of his knowledge to new wine in skins, from which the skins must burst unless there is a vent, Job 32. See also Jeremiah 15:10 and elsewhere, where he teaches that the words of God were like fire and burning coals, so that he could not suppress them.

In the first part of the Proverb, however, there is an ellipsis of a complete sentence or proposition, namely, that the wise inwardly turn knowledge over within themselves and with their own mind, and that their mind in some manner scatters knowledge. For just as a husbandman, before carrying his seed out into the field, winnows and sifts it at home and within, and purges it of tares and other defects of grain, so the wise man first weighs within himself what he is to say, so that the words which confer favor upon the hearers may be pure and cleansed of dregs.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 280 | printed columns 534–535]

In the latter half-verse it can be asked why, in order to establish a full antithesis, he does not say instead that the lips of fools scatter what is not right, rather than the heart or mind. To this it may be answered that the wicked often simulate piety for their own advantage, and conceal their wickedness or ignorance, while their mind never ceases and, without any interruption, forges and turns over what is impious—especially when their words present an appearance of piety and righteousness. For the words are honeyed and oily while inwardly and in the mind there are war and spears. See Psalm 35:15–16 and Genesis 37:35.

Hence it is clear that the mind of the wicked is like a workshop and receptacle of all evils, like a chasm filled with snakes, vipers, and serpents of impious thoughts and deadly counsels. They do not, indeed, keep this pressed down inwardly and within themselves, for this is grievous and troublesome to them. Nor can the truth of the statement which Christ uttered fail: “The mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart.” See Matthew 12:34–35.

Verse 8. The sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination to Jehovah; but the prayer of the upright is acceptable to him.

In זבח (*sacrifice*) there is a synecdoche of the part for the worship of God. In תועבת (*abomination*) and רצון (*delight*) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The sum is that there is a very great difference between the worship of the ungodly and that of the godly.

In human judgment there is a great kinship and likeness between the godly and hypocrites. Each professes that he knows and worships God; each approaches the Church, casts himself upon his knees, hears the word of God, offers sacrifice, prays to God, receives the sacraments, and gives alms. Sometimes, however, it happens that, wherever they are unlike, the wicked are more constant and more generous in outward worship than the godly. For the more they are destitute of true and sincere piety, the more effort they make so that, in that respect in which they recede farther from piety, they may appear to approach it as nearly as possible by a certain outward form. But God observes the very greatest distinction within this likeness and kinship—as great as the difference between heaven and earth, or rather between heaven and hell. From this it is gathered that one must not rest in human judgment, however godly the judges may be, unless we also prove ourselves further to God, the knower of hearts.

Just as the judgment of others concerning the wicked is badly deceived and wanders far from the truth, so the wicked themselves deceive themselves in their worship of God. For they flatter themselves and persuade themselves that they merit God’s favor by their ceremonies and performance of outward worship. An example of this is set forth in Isaiah 1:11–12 and following. See 1 Samuel 15, and how Saul persuaded himself that he would appease God by sacrifices, although he had violated his command. See also the example of the harlot in Proverbs 7, verse 14. For foolish people think that beneath the shield of sacrifices and outward worship they will be thoroughly covered from the heat of divine judgment.

But now let us hear what God’s judgment is concerning the worship of the wicked, a judgment most strongly opposed to the opinion of other people and of the wicked themselves. It is grave if their worship is vain, Matthew 15, and if God disapproves of it; but graver if he hates it; and gravest if he not only hates it but also abominates it. Yet something graver and more dreadful than the gravest is that it is an abomination to him and that he abominates it supremely. When he says this, he has closed off every approach to saying anything graver and more bitter. Thus in Isaiah 1 their rites are said to stink, so that God cannot endure either their odor or their sight; they are a burden to him and in a certain manner make him weary.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 281 | printed columns 536–537]

If perhaps the cause of this abomination is sought, seek it from the preceding Proverb. For since the thoroughly corrupt heart of the wicked turns over nothing except what is evil and deadly—although God regards the heart for a certain principal reason—it follows that the person is perverse and corrupt. And since a gift is accepted because of the giver, while it is established that the giver is displeasing to God, it ought not to be doubted that the gift is hated. People, indeed, often find the gift of someone pleasing while they hold his person in hatred and contempt. But with God the matter stands far otherwise: he finds no one’s gift pleasing unless he has first found the giver pleasing and beloved.

In the latter part he teaches that the worship of the godly is a delight to God, and he appears to confirm the same thing by an argument from the lesser. It is as if he said: faithful and godly prayers, however separated from sacrifice, are accepted. For although there were certain cases in which offering sacrifice was appointed, nevertheless sacrifices were often voluntary and left to each person’s discretion; and it appears that the discourse here is properly about those of this kind. But if, in the time of the Law, when worship prescribed and commanded by God joined the slaughter of sacrifices with prayers, prayer alone—with sacrifice excluded—was of such moment and weight with God, much more will it be exceedingly acceptable in the time of the Gospel, when it is established that sacrifices are now even unlawful.

This should add a singular spur to our praying: we know that our prayers do not merely please God, but are acceptable to him for a singular reason. Scripture often employs this argument to correct our inertia in performing duty and sharpen our diligence to render obedience. Romans 12:2; 1 Thessalonians 4:1. People diligently observe the things in which the princes of this age take pleasure, so that they may conform themselves to their will. See the example of David’s two servants, who, when they understood that he was held by a longing for water from Bethlehem, cast themselves into certain danger of death. Likewise the example of Joab, who, when he had ascertained by certain indications David’s desire for Absalom’s return, immediately instituted his petition about that matter before David. If we render this to mortals, how much more to God? When we do this for them, relying only upon a conjecture that something will be pleasing, how much more should we render it to God, whom we know certainly to delight in our prayers?

Verse 9. The way of the wicked is an abomination to Jehovah; but he loves the one who pursues righteousness.

In דרך (*way*) there is a familiar metaphor drawn from travelers, whose sole purpose is to complete their appointed course; they neither do nor contemplate anything else. In מרדף (*pursues*) there is likewise a metaphor drawn from travelers or runners, who follow those who go before them on a journey or in a stadium with the utmost exertion of strength that they can summon, until they overtake them.

The sum is that there is a very great difference between the upright and the wicked in works of the second table.

Here someone may ask why Solomon sets forth in two Proverbs what he could have dispatched in one. For it seems that he could have said no less suitably—and yet more briefly—if he had declared that all the pursuits and works of the wicked are hateful to God, while those things done by the godly in agreement with God’s word are pleasing to him. I answer that by this twofold Proverb the different human opinions about the works of the wicked are met more effectively. For just as there are very many who condemn the worship of the wicked as illegitimate, so very few are found

[1632 witness | PDF p. 282 | printed columns 538–539]

who do not highly value the outward and merely apparent obedience of the wicked in matters of the second table.

Since by the name *wicked* is understood one who has not been justified by faith, as in Romans 4:5, it is evident that the genuine sense of this Proverb is this: every track and every step that the wicked man sets down, or every work that he performs—whether in thinking, speaking, or finally doing—in those things which relate to his neighbor, is execrable to God.

Just as in the preceding Proverb the Prophet compares the true worshiper, not with an atheist, an idolater, or one who spurns divine worship, but with one who performs outward worship no less diligently than the person who worships God truly and sincerely, so in this passage he does not compare the pursuits of the godly with the thefts, murders, adulteries, and treacheries of the wicked, but with the most glorious achievements and the most splendid and highly praised deeds of the wicked. Yet of all these things universally Solomon affirms that they are hateful and execrable to God. Therefore, the obedience of the wicked toward their parents, their most orderly exercise of authority, their zeal for preserving the human race, their chastity, almsgiving, and truth in speech—all of them without exception are hateful to God. For the tree must first be good before its fruits can be good; the fountain and spring must be pure before the streams led from it can become clear. And since the heart is the fountain of all actions and pursuits, and it cannot be purified except by faith in Christ, it happens that without faith all things stink and are putrid. To this also the latter half of verse 7 of this chapter is referred.

From these things it is clear at what price the structures of temples, hospitals for strangers, poorhouses, and colleges instituted by the Papists are to be valued.

This is not because there is no praise among people for such excellent and outstanding deeds, but because with God not only does no reward remain for them, but a grievous punishment also remains.

Nor are these matters to be judged as though there were no difference between these works of the Papists and acts of brigandage; there is a very great difference. But if they will be punished for these deeds because they were not done rightly and sincerely, how much more for robberies and murders.

In the latter half-verse it is taught, first, what the duty of a godly man is: namely, not only to seek what is just, but to pursue it and, with great exertion added, to strive after it. For what is said particularly about pursuing peace in Psalm 34 must be understood universally of every good work. See 1 Corinthians 16:13; Titus 3:8 and 14.

Next, this also is worthy of observation: the endeavors of the godly in good works are acceptable to God, although they are surpassed by others—and especially by Angels and heavenly spirits, toward whose innocence we ought to aim. Provided that they pursue what is right, they are pleasing to God even though they do not reach the goal. This, therefore, ought to sustain godly people who are dismayed by consciousness of their weakness. We see parents approve and praise the endeavors of their children even though they wander very far from the mark. How much more will the heavenly Father do this, from whom every fatherhood is named?

Finally, the fact that God loves those occupied in good works should add a spur to performing them; that is, he embraces them with constant and perpetual love. Previously he had said that God delights exceedingly in these people; now he says something fuller and greater. For there are those who delight in the deeds and conduct of others whom they nevertheless do not hold dear, namely, because they are ministers and providers of their pleasures. But God always embraces with love those in whose conduct and manner of life he delights. See Song of Songs 1:2: “Your love is better than wine,” and so forth, where he also notes

[1632 witness | PDF p. 283 | printed columns 540–541]

the reason why we do not love him, because we are as it were debauched by the allurements of this world.

But let Christians and Evangelicals be ashamed that, in those splendid structures which we have mentioned, they are inferior to the Papists, when they possess such exceptional and incomparable incentives for doing well. For if the least things that proceed from the Papists are gifts pleasing to God, then those monuments, built only with great expense and labor and destined to benefit all posterity, will be pleasing.

Verse 10. Instruction [seems] evil to one who forsakes the way; he who hates correction will die.

In ארח (*royal way*) there is a metaphor drawn from roads which are worn by the great frequency of travelers, or from royal roads.

The sum is that the unteachable and intractable wicked procure destruction for themselves.

It is a matter full of misery that people are prone to error and to wandering from the way and rush headlong into byways. It is sadder that, after being directed into the way, they turn away from it not only through imprudence but willingly and conscious of their error, or turn back altogether in the opposite direction. Saddest of all, however, is that, after being drawn and recalled into the way, they persist in their error and do not allow themselves to be led away from it. It may easily happen to travelers, especially strangers, that they turn aside from the way; even after being led into the way they may easily deviate from it through imprudence. But willingly and consciously to abandon the right road, or after warning of their error not to return to the way, occurs only in fools, or rather in the mad and furious. From this it is plainly evident how great is the depravity of our nature, especially of those who, after being made participants in divine grace, turn away and defect from it. We should lament it with streams, as it were, of tears, and, loathing ourselves because of such great perversity, show ourselves pleasing to God if we perceive that we have been exempted and delivered from so execrable and accursed a condition. At the same time, let us also take care to beg the Spirit of God, who may render doctrine fertile and fruitful for us.

It is expedient that we measure ourselves by this rule. Therefore, let us examine ourselves to see whether it is burdensome to us to be instructed in the right way and path of righteousness. For if it is, then beyond doubt what is said in this passage applies to us: that we have abandoned the royal way. Christ gives the reason for this in John 3:19–20.

In the latter half-verse an even more monstrous and prodigious perversity of the wicked is taught: correction and the refutation of error not only displease them but are also hated and hostile. There was madness enough in not acquiescing in sound counsel and correction and in rejecting it; but to go on to hate it belongs to lost and desperate perversity. An example of this is in Nabal, 1 Samuel 25, who could not bear even one little word of correction; but it is especially in Ahab, who professes that he hates Micaiah because he always foretold unpleasant things. Indeed, they cannot even bear that reproof which is made in silence, not by words but by a deed and by a manner of conduct and life entirely contrary to theirs. For the Apostle teaches this reproof in Ephesians 5:11–13. But even then they resent being reproved, when someone does not tread the same tracks in which they walk. See 1 John 3:12 and 1 Peter 4:4. They imitate and reproduce those who suffer from gout and cannot bear not only a touch, but cry out even if someone comes near.

The punishment of rejected correction is often the death of this life, as in Nabal struck by God and in Ahab pierced by men; it is always the death of the life to come, and sometimes of both, as in the Sodomites. For outside the royal way there are precipices, lions,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 284 | printed columns 542–543]

bears, serpents, and whatever else is destructive. For it is otherwise in these errors than in others, where the penalty of error is a fall and vain labor.

Verse 11. Hell and destruction are before Jehovah; how much more the minds of men?

The sum is that before God the most hidden things lie naked and exposed.

Jeremiah 17:9: *The heart itself is deceitful above all things and deadly. Who knows it?* God answers this question in verse 10: *I Jehovah search the heart and test the reins.* Acts 1:24, καρδιογνώστα (*knower of hearts*), and Psalm 7:10. Although it is readily known that he does this, since he knows all things, John 21:17, nevertheless, because of the perversity of people whereby they persuade themselves that they can exempt themselves from God's knowledge, he therefore proceeds to prove this matter. He proves it by a double comparison from the greater. For he knows the grave and the state of the dead, even hell and likewise the punishments of hell, however infinite they may be; much more, therefore, does he know the human mind.

Hell, or the place of damned spirits, is exceedingly deep. Hence it is also called the *abyss* in Luke 8:31 and Romans 10:7. See Isaiah 30:33, where it is called both exceedingly deep and exceedingly broad, and where he foretells that it will be broad and capacious enough to receive the king of the Assyrians with his entire retinue. This indeed refutes the vanity of those who, because they hear that by far the greatest part of humanity will perish, make sport of it and assert that no place will remain for themselves. See Job 26:6.

Afterward he institutes a comparison of the human heart with the punishment of hell, the magnitude of which cannot be comprehended in thought. For as to the lower regions, since they are a part of the world, they are certainly comprehended by measure; but as to the punishment of the reprobate, it can be bounded and defined neither by its magnitude nor by its duration.

For just as Isaiah 64 and 1 Corinthians 2:9 confirm that the joy of the godly in heaven is such that *eye has not seen, ear has not heard, nor has it entered into the heart of man*, so the same can be affirmed of the torments of hell. Hence it is compared to the bite and sting of vipers, to unquenchable fire, Mark 9, and in Isaiah 30:33 to fire heaped with wood, whose bellows, as it were, are the infinite breath of God.

Therefore, since God has these things known and ascertained, much more does he know the hearts of men with the utmost thoroughness. Their own mind is not fully known to people themselves because of its many hiding places and recesses and because of ignorance. Hence also in 1 John 5 God is said to be greater than our minds. How foolish, then, it is to suppose that anything escapes God when you yourself are conscious of it! Indeed, God knows our thoughts before they have been perceived in the mind. For just as it is said, [there is no word on the tongue which God does not know,] so it is also truly affirmed that there is no thought in the heart with which he is not acquainted. Indeed, before the people themselves existed, God knew well and perfectly what good or evil they would think and devise, as is plainly evident in Cyrus, Josiah, and others.

If it is asked what the use of this Proverb is, I answer that it is manifold. First, it is that which Jeremiah adds in the passage cited above: that God may repay each person according to his actions, whether good or evil. For some actions are held enclosed within the mind, and God could not reward them as justice requires unless he penetrated minds. Next, those which issue outwardly must be estimated from the affection and disposition of the mind; God would not be their just judge unless he were the knower of hearts.

Next, for examining and avoiding

[1632 witness | PDF p. 285 | printed columns 544–545]

hypocrisy, it is especially useful and advantageous. For if we reverence the eye of a mortal to such a degree that we are unwilling to admit anything shameful which might offend it, how much more ought we to reverence the flaming and all-pervading eye of God, which sees the hidden and secret things of the heart more clearly than any mortal sees those things done at midday in the sight of everyone. See Proverbs 5:20–21.

Verse 12. A scoffer does not love one who corrects him; he does not go to the wise.

The sum is that a scoffer who inwardly and in his mind hates his reprover betrays this very thing outwardly.

By the name of a scoffer Solomon describes one who so clings to his sins that, when amendment of life is proposed to him, whether by words, by the conduct of a life, or by both, he regards it as play and a joke. Or rather, from the first Psalm, a scoffer is one who has fully determined within himself and his mind to cling tenaciously to his beloved sins. For the Psalm seems to judge exactly this when it attributes to such people not walking or standing but sitting in sins.

Of this scoffer it is foretold that he will not love his reprover. In Proverbs 9 it is said that he will pursue his corrector with hatred. From this it follows that not to love is the same as to hate. And since all love is effective and shows itself in fruits of helping the person whom we love, this also follows: hatred of one's neighbor diffuses itself more widely and has a greater capacity than people suppose.

It will be useful for us to measure and examine ourselves by the standard and exact rule of this present Proverb, asking with what disposition we regard the person who convicts us of sin. For unless we embrace him with a benevolence which we declare by the fruits of speaking well of him and, if the matter requires it, of benefiting him, there is danger that we sit in the seat of scoffers, where human malice is seen in its greatest obstinacy. For messengers are immune among mortals endowed with some equity; in an embassy from God, however, not only is the messenger rejected, but sometimes he is killed. See Matthew 22. On the other hand, if we hold dear and welcome the person who convicts us of sin, it can be a most certain judgment and testimony for us that we are counted among the truly wise, as is expressly said in Proverbs 9 and is indicated not obscurely in this very Proverb.

In the latter half-verse he declares the effect of the hatred described above: the scoffer does not receive himself into the company of his reprovers, but withdraws from them as from a snake, or drives away and expels those who come to him. See a notable example of this in Pharaoh, who drove Moses away, adding a threat of death if he came again.

Hence it is also plain that the person who delights in the company and society of those who reprove and assail the corruptions of his life must be reckoned among the wise; and that his duty is not only to admit the wise when they come to him, but also to approach and seek them. See the example of Peter, who, though reproved by Paul before everyone, in his second Epistle calls him a beloved brother and reads the epistles written by him diligently; and the example of the Canaanite woman, Matthew 15.

Finally, it must be noted that one must go to a wise person and to one who convicts another of sin. From this it follows that those who undertake the duty of reproving must be instructed and endowed with wisdom.

Verse 13. A joyful mind makes the face well; but by sorrow of mind the spirit is broken.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 286 | printed columns 546–547]

In פנים (*face*) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole body.

The sum is that from the constitution and affection of the mind a person may lose a good or bad condition of the body.

Although mind and body differ very greatly in their substance and matter, God has nevertheless joined them by so close a bond that there is a mutual compassion, or συμπάθειαν (*fellow-feeling*), between them. For when the body is afflicted, pain returns to the mind; and conversely, when the mind is wounded, the body is shaken. Yet the shaking of the body is greater when the evil takes its beginning from the mind than when the reverse occurs. Although the evil spreads throughout the whole body, nevertheless, because the first indication of that evil is seen in the face, Solomon therefore makes principal mention of the face. He teaches that a joyful and cheerful mind not only makes the body healthy but also renders the face attractive and lovable, so that, just as the body is healthy, it also appears healthy and lively. Hence it happens that, when the heart is joyful, the eye is lively, mobile, and sharp in seeing; the cheeks are rosy, the blood pure and cleansed, and the skin beautiful and pleasing to behold. The opposite is true when the mind is afflicted.

How important this is to every part of life cannot easily be explained. For in divine worship it is worthwhile that it be done with a joyful and cheerful mind. Hence Isaac, when preparing himself for the prophetic blessing of his sons, first commands that game and wine be procured for him, so that, cheered by them, he might discharge the prophetic office more freely, Genesis 27. Elisha, saddened by the sight of the ungodly king Joram, before he attempted anything by which the three kings suffering from thirst might be refreshed, commanded that a skilled musician be brought to him, by whose art and skill in playing he might dispel grief. If Prophets, who spoke by inspiration, have need of this aid of a joyful mind in order to perform the worship of God worthily, 2 Kings 3:15, how much more do others. See also how the Lord complains in Malachi of the severity of husbands by which their wives were compelled to weep when they approached the altar. And in 2 Corinthians 8, *God loves a cheerful giver.*

Even among people, services rendered by the sad and sorrowful are unwelcome, as is plainly evident in Mordecai and Nehemiah, Esther 4:2 and Nehemiah 2:1–2.

That a joyful mind renders the face pleasant appears from the example of Esau, who, cheered by a gift from Jacob, showed himself so lovable and pleasant in countenance that Jacob seemed in him to behold the gracious face of God, Genesis 33:10. But this is more illustrious in the godly, who, refreshed by peace of conscience, have a lovable face and appearance. See the Nazirites, who, though they did not drink wine, nevertheless, and so forth. See further the example of Daniel and his companions, who, although they were fed on vegetables and water, nevertheless, and so forth. This gladness of mind, therefore, which consists in reconciliation with God and from which a good conscience arises, is better and more excellent than all the cosmetics and pigments which soft and delicate women zealously seek for painting and coloring themselves. It is more excellent than every regimen and selection of foods, upon which, nevertheless, very many place great importance, or rather place everything, which relates to preserving health and complexion.

In the latter half-verse there is an ἐναντίωσις (*opposition*). For the opposite of the former part must be understood, namely, that the face is deformed and grows pale. See the example of Cain, Genesis 4, of Ammon, 2 Samuel 13:2, and of Ahab, who, like Ammon, became sick from grief. Even Nehemiah did so, however godly he was. Grief not only deforms the face but also corrupts the lung, whence breathing is impaired more gravely. For just as the lungs cool the heat of the heart with air sent down and supplied,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 287 | printed columns 548–549]

so the heart supplies blood from itself to the lungs through the veins, as though through pipes and channels, and by it they are nourished. Hence, when the heart is saddened, the exhausted blood cannot be carried and conveyed to the lungs. The word *spirit* also, as often elsewhere, is referred to the mind itself. For by sadness of mind the faculty of understanding is blunted, the memory also is impaired, and all the affections of the mind are enervated. From this we are taught that every sorrow, except those which are according to God, must be expelled and removed by every means. For God is disposed toward us like kind and pious mothers, who bear their crying children with difficulty and dry their tears by telling them that children disfigure their faces by crying. But sorrow which is according to God does not harm health and life, since it has the promise of life and health joined to it. For just as vegetables and the drinking of water did not harm Daniel and his companions either by weakening their powers or by diminishing the beauty and dignity of their countenance, because that regimen had been undertaken from piety, so neither does sorrow which is taken up for just causes. Next, sorrow which is according to God is always compensated by joy so abundant and concentrated that no one receives any harm to health from it.

But if someone objects that the face is often fashioned and feigned by people, and that one appears sad when the mind is joyful, and conversely, I answer that it is not so easy to press and digest pain and joy inwardly without their breaking out in the face, if anyone considers attentively, as is evident from the example of Nehemiah, chapter 2. Nevertheless, to maintain the truth of the Proverb it is enough that the matter is so by its own nature, or at least κατὰ τὸ πολύ (*for the most part*).

Verse 14. The mind of the prudent seeks knowledge; but the mouth of fools feeds upon folly.

In ירעה (*feeds*) there is a metaphor drawn from the nourishment of the body to the food of the mind; and פי (*mouth*) is compared to the mind, because, just as food is received by the mouth, so folly is received by the mind as though by a mouth.

The sum is that one imbued with true wisdom pursues it, while one empty of it gapes after its errors and trifles and is satisfied with them as though full.

Here the prudent person is described, and he is described from the effects of investigating and seeking knowledge. The perverse nature and foolish disposition of man is so constituted that it never seeks or investigates God or knowledge, even in a dream. Hence the statement is universally true: *I was found by those who did not seek me.* But after God has found us, he immediately implants in us the disposition to seek him diligently. For when Christ has once been tasted, he is so sweet and pleasant to the prudent that we must be held still further by zeal for him. For just as an infant, after once tasting its mother's milk, is thereafter led by a great desire to suck, so that in the infant's judgment and taste nothing can be compared with its mother's milk, so the person regenerated by the immortal seed of God's word wonderfully desires the milk of God's discourse and can be appeased only by its nourishment, 1 Peter 2:2–3. Therefore it is also likened to honey and to the honeycomb because of the marvelous sweetness which is in it, Psalm 19. But there is this difference: one who eats very much honey is seized by nausea and disgust for honey; the contrary occurs in the use of the divine word, for by its liberal allowance it does not overwhelm or blunt the appetite of the mind but rather sharpens and stimulates it. See Psalm 42. Here, therefore, the folly and hypocrisy of many who profess the Gospel are evident: puffed up by a slight knowledge of the Gospel, they immediately swell with pride and seem to themselves sufficiently advanced in the knowledge of God; consequently, they allow themselves to be seized by disgust at hearing the word.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 288 | printed columns 550–551]

But if they have been imbued with solid and salutary knowledge of the word of God, so far are they from resting content in it that every fruit which is perceived by them and properly digested excites and sharpens, as it were, a new appetite. Hence also it is that those who now are called Christians were once called disciples.

Next, it is not enough to seek nourishment, but one must pursue it. See Prov. 8.17. Thus it is no wonder that Christ's sheep, if they have no nourishment at home, seek it abroad, much less that they should reject nourishment set before them.

Next, what is said must be noted: [the heart of the prudent pursues knowledge.] It is not enough if we speak about knowledge or are hearers of it, unless the mind is inwardly affected and suffused by it. For if there is anyone who loves knowledge and embraces it in the innermost entrails of his soul, he at last is prudent. See Prov. 8.17. For if anyone pursues knowledge with mind and affection, the ear, eye, tongue, feet, and the other members of the body will serve it obediently and follow the mind's nod and command.

In the latter hemistich he teaches that fools are led by no less delight in folly than the prudent man is by knowledge; and indeed that they are carried along by zeal for ignorance all the more violently because they run to it of their own accord with every impulse of nature. Therefore he compares folly to food or drink in which one delights, and the fool's mind to the mouth by which food is received and which lies wholly open for gulping down folly, or foolish things; so that fish do not swallow water so abundantly, nor locusts green grass, as the wicked swallow wickedness. From this we are also taught how we ought to be disposed in seeking knowledge: namely, that we should delight in it as in food and drink. The opening of the mouth is sometimes an indication of this appetite of the mind for imbibing folly, since whatever a man eagerly hears, he indicates his desire to receive it by opening his mouth and, as it were, gaping.

Verse 15. All the days of the poor man [are] evil; but those of the cheerful in mind [are] a continual feast.

In משתה תמיד (a continual feast) there is a metaphor from corporeal things to incorporeal things; here a good conscience is likened to a perpetual and continual feast, by which sorrow is driven away and joy introduced.

The sum is this: the sole consolation against adversities is a good conscience cleansed by faith in Christ.

By this Proverb we are first instructed that it is impossible for us not to be saddened by sorrowful and afflicting things. For since we are flesh and blood, not stones or iron, it is impossible that we should not be moved by calamities that befall us and, indeed, be pricked as though by the goads and spurs of divine wrath. See Heb. 12.11. They are called the arrows of God in Job 16; to be made to drink wormwood and gall in Jer. 9. For Stoic ἀπάθεια is to be rejected from the Christian commonwealth, though the Anabaptists and Libertines of our times have, as it were, summoned it up from hell.

Next is taught the remedy by which those sorrows are not merely soothed and softened, but furthermore joy and gladness are introduced in their place: namely, if anyone is of good courage and is sustained by the testimony of a good conscience. The poet expressed the force and power of a good conscience when he said:

Let this be a wall of bronze: to be conscious of nothing against oneself,
to grow pale from no guilt.

Although he did not know wherein the goodness of a pure conscience consists, the Apostle in 1 Tim. 5 teaches that it is placed in this: that being justified through faith, we have peace with God, nor are we

[1632 witness | PDF p. 289 | printed columns 552–553]

conscious of any evil, because our sins have been blotted out. See Acts 8, v. 8 and 39.

This joy, moreover, is depicted for us by the likeness of a feast, by which the sorrowful are customarily refreshed and restored, not only with food, but with the company of their equals and with musical instruments. Hence also it is a praiseworthy custom among God's people to offer the cup of consolation to one clothed in mourning because of the death of those who were dear to him. What else is this but to furnish a feast and a sumptuous table? See Jer. 21 and 2 Sam. 12.20.

Next, this teaches that the feast is perpetual and without interruption. [For I give my peace not as the world gives,] John 14.27. Incomparable is the feast that always endures. Sacred history relates that Ahasuerus instituted a feast which lasted 180 days; by so prolonged a feast he determined to make the greatness of his kingdom and its glory conspicuous, since he ruled over 127 provinces. How great, then, are God's glory and the blessedness of God's people, for whom a feast is furnished perpetually for eternity, not only for the nobles (as there), but even for all the very poorest. And indeed, as far as the feasts of princes are concerned, they are wholly interrupted at night, and sometimes for a period during the day; but this feast is never interrupted. And indeed, as regards their feasts, their provision depends upon something elsewhere, namely upon the market, and the market upon the fruitfulness of the year; but at this feast all the provision is at home, where no dearness of grain is suffered. Finally, at other feasts, especially when repeated and reiterated more often, nausea and disgust creep in, through which we come to desire a more meagre diet; but this does not occur in this spiritual feast.

Verse 16. Better [is] a little with reverence for Jehovah than a great treasure where [there is] vexation.

In יראת (fear) there is a synecdoche of the part for the true worship of God. An ellipsis seems to occur in each hemistich. In the former, there is supplied from the contrary of the latter, namely tumult or agitation of mind. In the latter, there is supplied from the contrary of the former, namely ignorance or contempt of divine worship.

The sum is this: the pleasant and desirable use of the advantages of the present life must be sought from the worship of God.

Piety toward God is a singular protection for obtaining those things which belong to the use of this life, Ps. 34.10, 11; Matt. 6.33; 1 Tim. 4.8; Ps. 37.25. Yet it will be a small thing to possess the goods of this life unless God surrounds them like a wall, Job 1; and then unless some pleasant and convenient use of them arises. That we obtain this by the benefit of piety is evident from this passage, in which it is taught that the fear of God has such force and vigour that the little which is supplied to us profits us more for passing this life conveniently and pleasantly than the great treasures and abundant household goods of those who lack the fear of God and true religion. See Ps. 37.16, where this passage is wonderfully amplified, since the little of one righteous man is worth more than the great riches of many wicked men; his poverty more than their opulence; his cottage more than their palaces; his destitution more than their numerous friends; finally, his nothing more than their abundance in all things. See 2 Cor. 6.10. The reasons for this are: first, nature is content with little; whence arose the proverb that it is easier to satisfy the belly than the eye. Next, the life of man does not consist in an abundance of possessions, Luke 12. Afterwards, the advantages of this life are lawful to them alone and sanctified through the word and thanksgiving. From this it also

[1632 witness | PDF p. 290 | printed columns 554–555]

follows that the pious use them with a freer and more untroubled mind, while the rest, like thieves and possessors in bad faith, are sometimes compelled to tremble and quake, and always have just cause for fear. Next, God's blessing, from which flows the fruit and efficacy of the advantages of this life, is poured out more abundantly upon the pious and those who fear God than upon the wicked. See Hag. 1.5, 6. Furthermore, since it is God who fills men's spirits with joy together with food and the other advantages of this life, Acts 14.17, he bestows that joy more fully upon his own than upon his enemies. And he indicates this reason not obscurely in the latter hemistich, where, by attributing vexation to the treasures of the wicked, he tacitly signifies that peace and tranquillity attend those who fear God.

The spirits of the wicked must indeed be tormented and tortured by turmoil and vexation, because, since they do not rest upon faith in God nor entrust their own affairs to him, it is necessary that they should be engaged in distresses and anxiety all the more, the more treasures and protections of this life with which they are fortified. Next, the wicked, who possess much, are for the most part tormented and tortured by a great desire to have more. Furthermore, he who does not fear God is compelled against his will (though he burst) to fear men and malignant spirits, thunderbolts, rapacious thieves, and indeed all creatures—even those who are bound by duty to fear him—as is clearly seen in Herod, Matt. 14, and in the Pharisees, Matt. 26 and John 7, and elsewhere; they are said to have feared the people for themselves, though the people ought to have feared them by reason of their calling. But if he be of the sort of whom Christ speaks in Luke 18, who reverences or fears neither God nor men, he is nevertheless struck by his own conscience and trembles with horror.

Verse 17. Better is a dish of vegetables where there is love than a fattened ox where [there is] hatred.

In ארחת ירק (a dish of vegetables) there is a synecdoche of the species for every common or coarse food, such as a dish of roots or legumes; just as, by contrast, שור אבוס (a fattened ox) stands for feasts of every kind.

The sum, then, is this: peace with all men (especially those with whom we feast and are joined in society) makes a table pleasant and agreeable; but hatred, by contrast, renders even the most delicate things tasteless.

But this is in some measure a hypothesis of the general proposition which preceded: namely, that a meagre meal is set beneath meagre household goods and comes into contention and comparison with a sumptuous feast, just as great treasures were compared with meagre household goods. In the other part of the Proverb is contained the ἀντιστοιχίας of those things which accompany a pacified and tranquil conscience and render the advantages of this life pleasant: namely, the second member. For the fear of God and true religion are the former thing, embraced in the immediately preceding Proverb; the latter is love of one's neighbour, especially of those with whom we are accustomed to live in the course of life.

The Prophet teaches that peace and concord have such force and power that they make a common and coarse meal (in which class is especially a salad of green herbs seasoned and prepared with no savoury sauce of spices) more excellent and preferable to a sumptuous feast with which quarrels and discord are mingled; and that they make a belly whose appetite is sharpened rather than satisfied by scarcity of food preferable to a full belly. I shall say more, yet nothing that departs from the truth: the food of the righteous and of those who pursue their neighbour with love, however unwholesome and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 291 | printed columns 556–557]

adverse to preserving health, conduces more to preserving life and increasing strength than the most wholesome table of the wicked and of those who blaze and seethe with hatred toward their neighbours. The water of those who love is better than the wine of those who hate; their dark bread made from acorns is more desirable than these men's bread made from the flour of the choicest wheat; their wooden dishes more than silver ones; their legumes more than cakes and other most exquisite delicacies. The first reason for this is that joy is joined with love, just as sorrow is joined with hatred; and also that by far the greatest pleasure of taking food lies in the fellow-diners in whom we delight. Therefore, when we cannot look with favourable eyes upon someone among the guests, nor raise our eye toward him without being seized by sorrow, this takes much away from the joy that is perceived from the food consumed.

An excellent proof of this matter is offered in Prov. 23.6, 7, 8. Next, since pleasant conversations are the best seasoning, among those who do not love there is either utter silence; or, if they hold conversations, these tend toward quarrelling and contention; or, if they contain anything delightful, the whole of it is feigned and treacherous and, for that very reason, not free from danger.

It is reckoned a singular benefit that the Israelites, immune and free from an incursion of enemies and even from fear, could fearlessly furnish a table, not only in their houses, but beneath the open sky in their gardens under fig tree and vine. How much greater is the benefit when we are free from inward hatreds! It is certainly so much more excellent, insofar as internal war and quarrels are more harmful and dangerous than hostile and external war. And indeed the true Solomon, Christ Jesus, came into the world to establish peace among men; and therefore in Isaiah 9 he is called Prince of Peace. And in Eph. 2 he is said not only to have reconciled us to God, but also men with men, verses 14, 15. Therefore, he who desires the testimony of God reconciled to himself should also cultivate peace with his neighbour, especially with his brethren. And all the more, insofar as quarrelling and enmity threaten him from the wicked and God's enemies. Hence that saying of Christ: [that he had come not to send peace upon the earth, but, etc.] Matt. 10.34, 35, 36.

But here someone may ask why the Prophet demands love more in the taking of food than in the other dealings of life. I answer: because eating together and a shared table are a profession and, as it were, a symbol and token of love and charity. Hence the Agapae formerly in the Churches in Apostolic times, which certainly, had they been used at their proper time, would have had a use not to be despised. To this belongs the solemn custom among men divided by quarrels and enmities of sealing their reconciliation, as it were, with a feast, as is evident from the example of Jacob and Laban, Gen. 31.45, and of Isaac and Abimelech, Gen. 26.30.

How much more malignant and malevolent, then, are those who dine at one table but are not held together in concord. Therefore, what the Apostle admonishes in Eph. 4.26—that we lay aside anger before sunset—may conveniently be transferred to the table, so that before we recline at table we cast away malice.

But if it is grievous for those who are discordant and practise enmity to eat common food together, much more will it be wicked to do this at the feast of the most sacred supper. See Matt. 5.23, 24.

Verse 18. A wrathful man stirs up contention, but the longsuffering man quiets strife.

There is a familiar Hebraism in איש חמה (a man of heat) and ארך אפים (long of anger), where there is a metonymy of the adjective for greater emphasis.

The sum is this: wrath is the fuel of strife, which patience quiets.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 292 | printed columns 558–559]

In the preceding Proverb, having set forth the benefit of love and the torment and malignity of hatred, he indicates in this Proverb the seed and source of each, so that by flight from the latter and embrace of the former we may render this life convenient and sweet. And first he establishes as the cause of strife and discord a man burning and seething with anger.

For first he marks the effect of anger in the body of the angry man himself, in which it kindles heat and burning, even to such a degree that angry men are little removed from fever when they grow hot with anger, not otherwise than, as men relate occurs in wounded lions, the more agitated they are, the more feverishly hot they grow.

What of the fact that men are sometimes cast by the inflammation of anger into paralysis and the loosening of their limbs? Should not wrath be avoided even on this account, because the wrathful man is not only the murderer of another, as he is, but also of himself, and either violently shakes or certainly diminishes his own health? Hence (unless I am mistaken) arose the distinction among jurists between hot and cold blood.

The reason for the truth of this Proverb is, first, that the wrathful man easily provides occasion for strife; and next, that he promptly receives and admits the occasion provided by others. For just as tinder, through its dryness and aptitude for receiving fire, catches flame when moved nearer a lamp (though it does not touch the lamp nor lie contiguous to it)—much more if it touches the lamp—so the wrathful man is not only provoked when assailed, but rushes into anger of his own accord when no offence has been offered. To this is added such perversity in men that, when they have discovered wrathful persons, they count it sport and diversion to provoke them. What of the fact that we are so submerged in the darkness of ignorance that we seize from there material and a crop for becoming angry, though from that very place the most just causes of love and goodwill are present?

From these things it is plain that Christ, in Matt. 5, not without cause represents and portrays anger to us with a bloodstained face, since, when a beginning is taken from anger, matters very often come to slaughter and killing. For anger excites quarrels, a quarrel insults, insults blows, blows wounds, and wounds are often followed by death. For this reason we must look about diligently and apply caution so that every entrance and passage to anger is closed, if we wish to cultivate a peaceful life; and accordingly we must refrain even from those games and jests by which anger is kindled. For an excellent example of this matter, see 2 Sam. 2.12, etc.

In the latter hemistich he confirms the former part of the Proverb from its contrary. For just as he who is quickly stirred to anger mixes quarrels and contentions, so the man slow to anger not only does not set quarrels in motion, but lulls to sleep those moved and stirred by others. For since the wrathful man is like fire, he who is slow to anger in some fashion represents and expresses cold water. From this it is understood how valuable and how highly to be esteemed is the longsuffering man, since he receives the same appellation that is attributed to God himself and is not undeservedly to be reckoned among those who among us are commonly called justices of the peace in this island.

Verse 19. The way of the sluggard [is] like a thorny hedge; but the path of the upright is raised up.

There is an allegory in each hemistich, but one obvious to everyone.

The sum is this: to the sluggard all things are hard and rough, even those which in themselves are easiest; but to the upright, who are diligent, all things, even the most difficult, are passable.

To teach this Solomon draws an argument from parallels: it befalls the idle just as it does those who walk upon a hedge twisted and constructed from thorns. For just as it is rough and hard to tread on thorn-bushes, so too the doing of work worth the effort, and of a task of honourable and praiseworthy use, is hard and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 293 | printed columns 560–561]

the other. Whence it also follows that the slothful man, pursuing everything that is pleasant to his mind and fleeing those things which contain any discomfort in themselves, nevertheless does not attain the pleasure which he seeks. For since he shrinks from labor, should there be even an effort and an application of the hand to the work, although it be very remiss and feeble, it nevertheless creates more discomfort for him than when those who come eagerly and willingly to the appointed work strain every sinew and all their strength. Hence also another evil arises. For because of a mind utterly averse to labor, what he undertakes is thorny and pricking, so that in doing the work he advances slowly and with a hesitant step, as though, stretched upon the tips of his toes, he were pressing his footprints (among the English, “he goeth gingerly”). For the sluggard’s step is a tortoise’s step. For since (as Solomon reports, Proverbs 6) the sluggard and idler is thoroughly addicted to sleep and indulges it, even when awakened and departing from his bed he is as though stupefied by sleep for the whole day. Not without cause have some called the idle man’s life death, and others perpetual and unbroken holidays. Although he is slow or unfit for every good work, yet for every kind of wicked deed he is energetic and swift. Proverbs 6.10 and verse 13, and 2 Thessalonians 3.11. These things may fittingly be applied to those for whom it is burdensome and rough to stand in the ways of the Lord. See Matthew 25.26.

In the latter hemistich the nature of the antithesis seems to require him to say that the path or track of the diligent and industrious man is like a raised road. He did not do so for two reasons. The first is that, by opposing the upright man to the sluggard and idler, he might convict the idle man of wickedness. The second is that diligence and industry are not sufficient for conducting affairs properly unless a man also devotes himself to uprightness.

From this it is first evident that every idle man is wicked, or rather a craftsman of wickedness, Proverbs 6.12. Therefore the idle man is to be driven out of every household, church, and commonwealth; not even food or the sustenance of this life is to be furnished and supplied to him: 2 Thessalonians 3.10, he who does not labor, let him not eat. To this also belongs the fact that in the same place they are likened by the Apostle to soldiers who desert their station in the battle line, 2 Thessalonians 3.6 and 11. Everyone who has even lightly tasted military affairs easily understands how harmful this is in war.

But to the diligent man walking in his calling, in which he has been placed by God, all things are passable, ready, and easy; as a traveler easily traverses a raised road and walks upon it with a quickened step.

Verse 20. A wise son gladdens his father, but a foolish man despises his mother.

There is a synecdoche of the part in בן (*son*) for children of both sexes; likewise in אב (*father*) and אם (*mother*), by which either parent is denoted. There is also in the first hemistich an ellipsis of the contrary, namely that which is in the latter, that is, *honors*; and in the latter there is an ellipsis of the contrary of that which is in the first, that is, *is saddened*, so that the complete Proverb is: children sadden their parents by contempt for them.

A Proverb very closely akin to this is found in chapter 14, verse 1, from which those things that concern this place are to be sought. This one thing is supplied by the present passage which was not contained in the former Proverb: namely, wherein consist the wisdom and folly by which parents are either cheered or saddened—in rendering the honor owed them, or in scorning them.

But this honor or contempt, taking its beginning in the very seat of the mind, afterwards bursts forth into external actions, not

[1632 witness | PDF p. 294 | printed columns 562–563]

only by bending the knees and uncovering the head, but especially by acquiescing in their counsels and even in their chastisements.

In this Proverb, however, it is distinctive that it signifies contempt to be directed at the mother rather than at the father. For very many children are found who outwardly show themselves obedient to their father, while those same children are found disobedient to their mothers: sometimes because in them, as women, they observe something worthy of censure; then because they are unwarlike and cannot prosecute the injuries committed against themselves. Moreover, the distribution and dispensation of patrimony and inheritance lies with the fathers, not the mothers. Sometimes it is because the mothers are sprung from a family not so noble. But whatever it may be that drives them away from obedience to their mothers, it is certainly wicked and contrary to duty; by that very thing they are also convicted of being disobedient to their fathers. For because the obedience which they show them does not proceed from the fear of God, it is to be reckoned rebellion rather than obedience.

Mothers therefore must walk circumspectly, lest they provide an occasion for contempt. They do this when they send their newborn children away from themselves and do not perform a mother’s duty by nursing their children, but merely bring them into the light, which they cannot avoid; and then when they rather often show themselves excessively indulgent to their children, and even obey their sons in matters in which it is least fitting: whence by God’s just judgment they are despised by their children.

But since this contempt against mothers generally occurs after the children are grown, hence we learn that at every time and age, in the unmarried condition as in the married, obedience is to be shown to either parent.

Verse 21. Folly is joy to the witless man; but a man of understanding makes his step straight [and, so to speak, rectifies it].

In the former part there is an ellipsis of the contrary, namely *a straight step*, and in the latter, *joy*, so that the full proposition is: the wicked, through their delight in sin, are further incited to wrongdoing; but the godly, affected by joy arising from virtue, proceed further in the pursuit of virtue.

The sum is: the wicked delight in sinning; but the godly, because of deeds well done and hatred of the sin which they commit, correct the course of their life and strive toward uprightness.

Since it is worthwhile for a Christian man to have laid up and sealed within himself a full and abundant testimony of his own salvation, just as knowledge of that matter is sought from all the books of sacred Scripture, so scarcely any book exists that is more fertile in this doctrine than the book of Proverbs. Indeed, this very Proverb which we have before us puts forth excellent proofs of it. For in this Proverb by far the greatest distinction is established between the godly and the wicked, by which anyone may easily discern whether he is the one or the other. First, it is proper and peculiar to the wicked man to delight himself in sin and hold it among his pleasures. To him sin is play and sport; although he be incited by no impulse of temptation or provocation, nevertheless of his own accord he rushes into sin. To this belongs what was set forth in this same chapter, verse 14: the wicked feed upon their wickedness. This likeness of feeding and food is graphically depicted in Job 20, where the Prophet likens the wicked to those who are captivated by delicate food and dainties, which, when they have put them in their mouth, they keep there a long time, roll them this way and that, and compress them beneath the palate, lest, if they quickly swallow them, they lose all the sweetness of their dainties—just like those gluttons and Epicureans who wished for themselves the neck of a crane, that they might perceive a long-lasting pleasure from their food. To this also belongs what was said above in this book: the wicked cannot enter into sleep unless they have perpetrated some evil. For just as those whose stomachs are empty and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 295 | printed columns 564–565]

vacant do not easily sleep, so the wicked cannot take sleep unless they have been fed and sated with sin as with food. As witnesses of this I summon those who, without thirst, gorge themselves with wine and strong drink to such a point that they even suffer loss of health and soundness. I summon also those who, although at home in their fathers’ houses they can honorably and comfortably satisfy themselves with food lawfully obtained, nevertheless prefer to procure outdoors and by stealth the very same food which they lawfully use at home, in agreement with Solomon’s saying, *stolen waters are sweet*.

Hence in the former hemistich the contrary of what is in the latter must be supplied: namely, that the fool or wicked man is strengthened day by day in the lust for sinning and rushes into worse things. For from this delight which he receives from sin, his mind is further kindled and inflamed to sin, so that in this respect there is a dissimilarity between the wicked man and those who are fed with food. For the latter are sated with food, especially when it is taken more liberally; but the former never satisfy themselves by sinning. Rather, they are to be compared with those who suffer from dropsy, whose thirst is never quenched and who, the more they drink, become the more eager to drink. The Apostle plainly confirms this in Ephesians 4.19, when he attributes to the wicked that they sin with a certain avidity. Let each man therefore examine himself, with what mind he sins: whether the sin which he has committed is pleasing and welcome to him.

In the latter hemistich it seems necessary to supply, first, the very thing expressed in the former hemistich, the joy of doing well, John 4.34, and consequently grief from wrongdoing: namely, that an understanding and godly man is exceedingly saddened by a sin committed, and that the sin is more bitter to him than gall itself and wormwood. The Apostle confirms this in Romans 7: *The sin which I hate, that I do*, etc.; 2 Corinthians 7.10–11.

Hence it follows—namely, from joy in doing well and grief in doing evil, which is placed in the latter part of the Proverb—that a good man makes straight his step, or the course of his life, or, so to speak, rectifies it. For it cannot happen that anyone who is affected by solid joy over a matter well done, or even is seized by serious grief over sin, should not pursue the amendment of his life.

Verse 22. There is a scattering of thoughts without counsel; but in a multitude of counselors [each thought] shall arise.

The metaphor in הפר (*scattering*) is taken from corporeal things—for example, chaff or husk, or other light things of that kind—and transferred to corporeal things.

The sum is: matters rashly undertaken are empty and ineffectual, but those fortified by counsel and premeditation attain their happy and desired outcome.

By this Proverb the Holy Spirit warns that in undertaking affairs, impulses and perturbations of the mind are to be sent and driven far away: nothing is to be undertaken through anger or fury; nothing through love or hatred; nothing through blind desire, and so on of that kind.

Solomon, Proverbs 29.20: [Have you seen, he says, a man headlong in his words? There is greater hope for a fool than for such a man.] If in words precipitancy is convicted of folly, much more in deeds. Then in Psalm 112.5 it is said of a man who fears God that he completes his affairs with judgment applied. Likewise in Psalm 119, section 1, verse 3, David professes that he has anxiously considered his ways. See an excellent example of this in Judges 19.30. What is more, God provides in a certain manner an example of this, who in Genesis 1 is presented by Moses—for the sake of our understanding—as deliberating and taking counsel about the creation of man, as about a great matter. But for an example of affairs undertaken without counsel and through perturbation of mind, see

[1632 witness | PDF p. 296 | printed columns 566–567]

1 Samuel 25 in David, who suddenly swore the death of Nabal, etc. See also Daniel 3.19–22; Herod, who, moved by a sudden perturbation of mind, made a promise under oath, of which he afterwards repented. This is to be observed by everyone, but especially by youth, which is borne headlong by a certain heat of the blood. See 2 Timothy 2.22. But counsels are especially neglected by those more powerful in strength, trusting in the powers which they possess; they do not consider that ingenuity overcomes strength, as craftsmen demonstrate in machines.

Then, when he says that in a multitude of counselors the matter is established, not of good men, he indicates that wicked counsels are not worthy of the name of counsels.

If it be asked how one should take counsel, I answer: from the great Counselor spoken of in Isaiah 9, who is to be approached through prayers; and then counsel is to be sought from the Law of God, which has been given to us in place of a more sacred Senate, Psalm 119. And in this way the will of God will be no less certainly known to us than formerly to the Israelites, who consulted the oracle through the high priest, or were taught by the cloud’s station or migration concerning their own rest or migration.

But above all, in matters that concern the kingdom of heaven, mature deliberation is to be applied both at the very approach and entrance into the profession of the Gospel, Luke 14.28, 29, 30, 1, 2, and throughout the entire exercise of divine worship. See Exodus 3.3, 4, 5, and Ecclesiastes 4, last verse, and 5.1.

Verse 23. Joy comes to each man from the speech of his mouth; and a word in its own time—O how good!

In מענה (*answer*) there is a synecdoche of the genus for good speech, as is evident from the latter part of the verse. There is also another synecdoche, of the species. For according to Hebrew usage the word *answer* is employed for every kind of speech, just as it is also employed in the Gospel both for him who begins a discourse and for him who is second in the discourse.

The sum is: pure speech always has this fruit, that it cheers the speaker and consoles him through the conscience of having spoken rightly; and it also often happens that it bears marvelous fruit for others.

Here it is asked first why he says there is joy from what is rightly spoken, when it ought rather to have been said that from all things rightly done—whether by thinking, speaking, or acting—joy returns to him by whom they are performed. I answer: he said nothing here about thoughts because good thoughts are free from every danger contrived by the wicked. Then he pronounces this about sayings rather than deeds because men claim for themselves great freedom in speaking—license, rather—although joy arises from all these things. In this the incredible bounty of God is shown, since he rewards even the least good things.

It is a notable fruit of things rightly spoken to receive from them the reward of eternal life, which God will render to each man by gratuitous promise, Matthew 12.37. And although there were no other fruit, it would nevertheless deservedly add a spur toward speaking well and uprightly. But now, since it is further apparent from Romans 6.22 that this is another fruit—that by things rightly said, or rather done, we are sanctified in this life and our mouth, by nature full of cursing, is cleansed—and since another exceedingly noble and excellent fruit is added, that it refreshes and revives us in this life full of miseries, both from the conscience of having spoken well and from the benefit which it brings to others, who does not see that it is worthwhile for us to devote effort to pure and uncorrupted speech, especially since no one can take this fruit from us? For although by speaking things true, godly, and honorable we stir up envy and ill will against ourselves among the wicked, nevertheless the joy,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 297 | printed columns 568–569]

which arises from the conscience of having spoken rightly always remains, and no man or demon will take it from us.

In the latter hemistich two things are proposed: one is a description of a profitable saying, the other concerns its excellence. In the former, the point is that what we say should not merely be good and expressed in fitting and suitable words, but should also be spoken at the proper time. For as the fruit of the noblest tree is tasteless or bitter unless it is ripe and timely, and as timely rain greatly refreshes while untimely rain even harms, so a discourse, although good, does not do so unless it also be applied at its proper time. Hence comes that saying of Ecclesiastes 8, where the heart of the wise is said to observe the time and the reason of the thing to be done. An example of this appears in Abigail, who kept silent concerning her husband’s drunkenness in this way, until he had slept off his surfeit. Thus David, at the time of the great feast which Nabal prepared for his shearers, sent his servants to obtain some provisions for the journey from Nabal. The same is to be understood of fasting and feasting, and of edification or building. The importance of time is greatest in the ministry: when God opens a door for bringing in, confirming, and establishing unto all eternity, let us not neglect it. Then also Ministers should know how to apply the word at the proper time. Isaiah 50.4.

The other point in this part of the Proverb concerns the singular excellence of a profitable saying, which is so great that it is easier to admire than to set forth in words. See 1 Samuel 25, how David celebrates the speech of Abigail because it was applied so seasonably. But the highest use of this is especially in the ministry. For just as the feet of those who proclaim peace are beautiful, so also are the times in which it has greater power for the conversion of souls—as when one observes the time of sickness or affliction, Job 33. Likewise, at a time when God has shown singular grace to men, doctrine may settle more deeply in their minds through the sense of divine kindness. See 2 Chronicles 15.1, 2, 3, 4, etc.

Moreover, the emphasis is to be noted: even one word is of great moment and excellence if it is employed truly and seasonably, such as merely *also*, or *not*.

Verse 24. The path of life for the instructed man tends upward, that he may depart downward from the grave, that is, [from the lower regions].

The sum is: the one way of departing from eternal death is to lift the mind and thoughts to heaven.

Two things are declared about the man instructed and excellently trained for the kingdom of God: first, what he does; second, to what end he does it.

In the former two things are noted: what it is that he seeks, and how he investigates it. He investigates the path of life. In this it is to be considered that no one unless regenerated from heaven pursues the things of eternal life. For in order to pursue those things, a person must be enlightened with knowledge of them and imbued with some perception and love of them. For how can anyone pursue what he does not know or is not held by desire for? Just as no one unless regenerated follows the path of life, so no unregenerate man performs this. From this we must see in what place we stand: whether we are children of wrath and darkness, or of light and grace. For unless we pursue eternal life, it is evident that we remain in death and have not yet been conveyed and transferred from the power of the devil and darkness into the kingdom of the Son of God and of light. For all must be like that merchant in Mark 13, who, as soon as he finds the precious stone, sells everything to acquire it.

How necessary this investigation of the path of life is may be perceived from this: that there is only one such path, while the paths which lead to death are infinite; and secondly because

[1632 witness | PDF p. 298 | printed columns 570–571]

that it is narrow and confined, not broad and splendid, as are the ways that tend toward death. And for that reason striving—or rather, struggling—must be employed (as Christ himself speaks, Luke 13), so that we may enter it and, once it has been entered, may hold to it steadfastly.

There follows the mode and manner in which we are to seek the path of life: namely, if we direct our spirit and our thoughts to the things that are above and do not permit ourselves to creep upon the ground and to keep our desires and affections fixed upon the earth. For although we conduct our lives on earth, our manner of life is nevertheless in heaven, Philippians 3:19. If we have risen with Christ, let us seek the things that are above, where Christ is at the right hand of the Father, Colossians 3:1.

Hence in Psalm 15 heaven is compared to a mountain which must be climbed and ascended by us every single day. And in Song of Songs 7:9 the Church ascends upward from the wilderness. There the passage seems to allude to the ascent of God's people from Egypt into the land of Canaan through the wilderness; and in chapter 3, verses 4, 5, and 6, it is taught that it is not right to remain here on earth with Christ. There the Church corrects herself for having said that she wished always to keep Christ in her mother's house.

This must be performed with all the more justice and courage because Christ descended from heaven in order to draw us to heaven, and did so with far greater labor, because of the steepness of the mountain, than we can undergo in ascending it. To this is added the statement that for our sake he leaped over and bounded across the very high mountains, Song of Songs 8, the final verse.

The end remains: namely, that we should withdraw from the grave, which is borne downward—that is, from the regions below. Its road is compared to a precipice and to the descent of a mountain, in which most travelers rejoice because a road of this sort is completed easily and with no trouble or exertion of strength. Hence it is not unfittingly compared to the descent into Egypt, which is forbidden to God's people. From this it is also clear that there are only two ways: one which leads to heaven and the other which leads to the regions below, contrary to the Pontifical purgatory. The pontifical limbo is also overthrown, in which they teach that the faithful fathers were shut up before Christ and that no one was received into heaven before Christ's ascension.

Verse 25. The Lord tears down the house of the proud, but he establishes the boundary of the widow.

In בית (*house*) and גבול (*boundary*) there is a synecdoche of the part for any possession; and in בית there is further a metonymy of the subject for those who are in the house. In אלמנה (*widow*) there is another synecdoche of the part for all who are cast down and destitute of aid.

The sum is that all the greatest things of the proud are scattered by God, while, on the contrary, all the smallest things, which are destitute of strength, are established by him.

That statement of James in chapter 5 is renowned: *God resists the proud*; indeed, it has passed into a Proverb in the Commonwealth of God: *the person who raises himself up must be cast down*. This alone ought of itself to deter people from pride, since they understand that by their pride they exercise enmity with God and, in a manner, declare war. Now, since the sword of divine wrath is directed not only against the persons of the proud, but its fury also blazes against all the possessions, revenues, and descendants of the proud, a kind of double bridle is imposed by which people may be held within modesty and sobriety. This severity of God toward the proud is proved just from the fact that their spirits grow and are raised up by possessions of every kind, whether estates or dwellings. What king would endure with equal patience that someone among his subjects should advance to such a degree of insolence that he rises against him? Or would he not strip of his possessions a man swollen by great estates with which he had been enriched by that king, and by which he attempts to obscure the King's glory and

[1632 witness | PDF p. 299 | printed columns 572–573]

splendor? Although earthly kings sometimes raise their subjects to that pinnacle of honor from which they cannot cast them down, God nevertheless effects this—both most justly and most easily. However high they raise themselves and their possessions—whether fortresses, towers, royal residences, or palaces—so that, if it could be done, they touch heaven, God is nevertheless higher and superior to them all. Thus they can neither strike him with any weapon, while they are exposed to all his engines of war, his hailstones, lightnings, and thunderbolts. Therefore, unless they are led by a vain hope of casting God down from his heavenly throne, let them take care not to assume courage and high spirits; and once these have been assumed, let them lay them aside as soon as possible, unless they wish to experience God as the avenger against themselves and their possessions. Frequent examples of this occur in Scripture: the monarchs of Assyria, Babylon, Persia, and Greece, Haman, and others. It is further illustrated by a comparison with traitors and those convicted of wounded majesty: not only do they themselves end their lives by the noose, but by our municipal law their houses must be demolished, their trees torn up by the roots, and their field laid waste. For since the proud are guilty before God of wounded majesty, and that in a particular manner and as though in the highest degree, it is not unjust for even their household to be overthrown by God. In this matter divine judgment, which is always just, surpasses human judgment and an earthly tribunal. For human judges ought not to rage against an innocent household, as is established by the law and the example of Amaziah; but so forth. Now, just as the proud raise their spirits beyond measure because of the greatness of their possessions, so those who possess narrow means cast down their spirits at the sight of their widowhood and poverty. The Holy Spirit wishes to raise and establish them. For that saying of James is well known: God renders grace to the humble and abject. He not only succors them, but preserves all their things under his protection, while he does the contrary to the proud. When strength and power fail in us, they ought not to cast down our spirits, but rather to arouse and sharpen them. The more completely we are exhausted in ourselves, the more ardently let us call upon God to supply our defects. Examples of both parts of the Proverb are found in Mary's song, Luke 1:51, 52, 53; they are begun in this life and will one day be fulfilled most fully and perfectly in the age to come.

Verse 26. The thoughts of the wicked man are an abomination to Jehovah, but the words of the pure are pleasant.

In each part of the Proverb there appears to be an ἀντίθεσις (*antithesis*), whose parts are mutually supplied from one another: the former from the opposite latter part, and the latter from the opposite first part. The complete proverb would thus be: the thoughts of the wicked man are an abomination to Jehovah, and consequently so are their words that flow from their thoughts; but the thoughts of the pure are pleasant, and by that very fact their words are pleasing to God.

The sum is that, according as thoughts are evil or good, so speech is displeasing or acceptable to God, in agreement with Christ's saying in Matthew 12, *from the treasure of the heart*, and so forth.

In verses 8 and 9 of this chapter the Prophet declared that the worship and indeed the whole outward conduct of the wicked are an abomination to God. It might have seemed that only this remained for him to declare: what God's judgment is concerning their thoughts. For a Proverb common in speech says that thoughts pay no taxes, as though it were free and granted to everyone to think in his spirit whatever he pleases, according to his desire. For it often happens that people thoughtlessly make the same judgment about the court and tribunal of God as about the court of mortals. Because people do not summon thoughts before the law, they imagine in their spirit that not even God ought to bring them into

[1632 witness | PDF p. 300 | printed columns 574–575]

judgment—but falsely. For since God made the whole person sound and incorrupt, he rightfully requires not only in words and deeds, but also in thoughts, all this innocence which he himself bestowed. Next, since thoughts are the parents and seed of all crimes, what justice would it be to punish the children and release the mothers unpunished? Or what skill in agriculture would labor to cut out noxious plants while allowing the seed or root from which they spring to put out shoots? And since the nest or cage is the place where all unclean and hateful birds are hatched, not only must the chicks be killed, but the nest and cage must be cast down. What sort of cleanliness would it be to clean the outside of the dish while allowing the inside to remain corrupted with putrefaction and rust? Furthermore, what prince or King among mortals, however august, will be found who, in reckoning and subtracting accounts, requires an account not only of pounds and crowns but even of obols and farthings? And since people punish the evil that falls under the perception of the eyes or ears, why should not God, who sees and hears what the heart considers and says, present himself as the avenger of its wickedness? God therefore holds the heart no less constrained by the obligation of his law than the hands or feet, especially since God requires it particularly for himself as a dwelling in which he may reside through his Holy Spirit.

When thoughts are evil, it follows that words, however golden or honeyed in appearance, are an abomination to God. From this it is clear how vain and futile is the defense of those who, when convicted of corrupt and impure speech, diminish their sin on the ground that they intended no evil, though vicious words can flow only from perverse thoughts. Therefore, just as in James 4:8 it is said, *Cleanse your hands, sinners*—and in order that this may be done, *sanctify your hearts*—so it may likewise be said: *Cleanse your mouths, sinners*—and in order that you may accomplish this, *sanctify your hearts, O double-minded people*.

In the latter part it must be noted that there is not a complete opposition and antithesis in every respect. For although the thoughts of the wicked are only evil—and that at all times, Genesis 6—and the words proceeding from them therefore continually savor of the same perversity, nevertheless neither the thoughts nor the words of the good are always found pure, because sin dwells in the godly, while the wicked, however great they may be, are nothing but wickedness.

How pleasant to God the words of the godly are we learned above from various testimonies in the Song of Songs. But if they are pleasant to God, they cannot be disagreeable or insipid to us who are truly godly. Earlier, in verse 23 of this chapter, speech seasoned with salt is commended from the singular benefit which it brings to others. Here, however, the Prophet uses a weightier and more illustrious argument by which to arouse us to useful and holy speech: namely, that it is pleasing to God. For what is acceptable to God ought to be done, however burdensome and troublesome it might be both to us and to others; see Romans 12. Therefore, let not the suspicion that our words will be unwelcome to people close our mouth against good speech. Rather, let this statement sustain and refresh us: that God holds those words among his delights.

Verse 27. The man devoted to gain troubles his own house, but the one who hates gifts will live.

There is an ellipsis in this Proverb which seems to require supplementation in this manner: the greedy man will both perish himself and trouble his household; but the one who hates gifts will both live himself and render his household peaceful and quiet.

The sum is that, through things wickedly acquired, both

[1632 witness | PDF p. 301 | printed columns 576–577]

the plunderer perishes and his household is troubled; on the contrary, with riches acquired moderately and lawfully, the head of the household both prolongs his own life and preserves his household safe and peaceful.

Avarice is a vicious affection of the spirit which desires the riches of this life beyond due measure. Here it is marked by the adjunct that the man is devoted to gain. Therefore, if he turns and applies his spirit to riches, and if he is a lover of riches, as the Apostle speaks, even if he acquires nothing, even if he squanders whatever he acquires, and even if he does this only by lawful and honorable means, he is nevertheless greedy and devoted to gain, and for this very reason he does not deal well with himself and his people but is plainly harmful.

Here see the truth of that saying which is in Psalm 33:10: [God renders the thoughts and counsels of people vain.] For greedy people apply themselves to accumulating riches for two reasons: both so that they themselves may live a life as long as possible and so that they may establish their households for all posterity. Neither of these things occurs. For, as Christ says, [a person's life does not consist in the fact that he possesses a great abundance of riches], Luke 12:15. God not only makes their counsels and thoughts vain, but further turns those very things to their own destruction. Riches not only fail to benefit them toward the end for which they are acquired, but further—as is established by this proverb—do them very great harm. For the things which are acquired as a shield by which they may protect themselves from the injuries of this life are turned into swords by which they are slaughtered. And the things they seek as props and supports for sustaining and establishing their households are like ἐμβολαὶ (*battering rams*) by which the house is stormed. What they thought would serve as a staff upon which they could lean and rest is turned into the reed of Egypt by which their hand is pricked and pierced. And what in their imagination they conceived would be a wall by which the house would be encircled and safe becomes an engine of war by which it is thrown down and destroyed. Thus fortifications and ingenious devices devised to preserve wealth—what else are they than so many whetstones for sharpening the ingenuity of thieves and robbers, by which they may break through all those defenses either by force or dissolve them by skill and cunning? Therefore, unless you cast avarice out of your spirit—avarice which is, as it were, the leader of plunderers and standard-bearer of thieves—you will in vain fortify everything with locks and bars.

Now, if it is asked how a man devoted to gain often procures untimely death for himself and destruction for his household, I answer: through his own agency and that of others. In the former kind it occurs: first, because he wears himself out and tortures himself with cares and anxieties; second, because in this manner he drives sleep from himself and from the members of his household by the excessive labors with which he wearies them; third, because, through excessive attention to gain, he supplies due and proper nourishment neither to himself nor to his people; fourth, because at the loss of acquired possessions he is tortured beyond measure, so that at times he contrives death for himself by the noose.

To this pertain the testimonies by which it is confirmed that the wicked man gathers treasures for the godly, Job 27:16 and Proverbs 28:8. An illustrious testimony to this matter appears in Jeremiah 17:11, where it is said both that the man himself perishes in middle age and that his riches are scattered; the passage illustrates this with the comparison of a partridge.

For just as a person who eats moderately is nourished by food and the food remains in his stomach, but, on the contrary, it is vomited out when too much is stuffed in, so a person who greedily accumulates riches will be compelled to disgorge them again. See Job 20:15.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 302 | printed columns 578–579]

An example of this matter is found in Achan, who by his avarice troubled himself and indeed the entire people of God and his whole household. Another is found in Ahab's numerous offspring, who all perished, among other reasons because of Naboth's vineyard, seized by tyranny. See also the example in Jehoiakim, Jeremiah 22.

In the latter part it must be noted that the praise of the righteous man consists not in his failing to accept gifts and bribes, but in his hating them. Thus it is said elsewhere in Psalm 97:10, *All you who love God, hate that which is evil*. For just as avarice consists in a person's love of riches, so true and Christian liberality consists in hatred of all the unlawful arts by which they are commonly acquired. Therefore, just as this pertains to everyone, for whom it ought to be more desirable to give than to receive, so it pertains especially to magistrates, who must take singular care to guard against the corruptions of gifts.

Yet gifts and honorary presents, by which we testify to gratitude and benevolence and either confirm a friendship already formed or recover one that has been lost, are not condemned by this saying.

But if it is not lawful to gape after presents and gifts, which they say can be nothing more liberal and free from injury, how much less lawful is it to gape after plunder, oppression, and exactions?

How true this proverb is, see in the example of Josiah, enlarged upon by Jeremiah in chapter 22.

Verse 28. The heart of the righteous man meditates how to answer, but the mouth of the wicked pours forth evil.

In מענה (*answer*) there is a synecdoche of species for every kind of speech. In יביע (*pours forth*) there is a metaphor drawn from a spring of waters which emits water without any interruption.

The sum is that the righteous man is distinguished from the wicked by precipitancy or deliberation in speech.

What was said above about deeds must now likewise be understood concerning words: that nothing should be said through blind love, hatred, wrath, lust, or any other disturbance of the spirit.

Here it can be asked why this duty necessarily rests upon us. For since [the mouth speaks from the abundance of the heart], and since the heart of the righteous person is righteous, it seems that nothing can flow from it except what is righteous. I answer that our righteousness is only begun, and that injustice which is innate and thoroughly implanted in us lies beside it. Therefore attention and caution are needed, lest that which remains unjust in us run ahead of the good which God has worked in us. This must be done all the more diligently and laboriously because the things we do by the instinct of nature—especially when practice and habit have strengthened and confirmed nature—proceed from us more easily and readily than things which are acquired from elsewhere and opposed to depraved nature. We see this in horses which buck by nature but are trained by art to walk in a particular gait: especially if they are pressed, they repeatedly return to that which nature dictates unless they are restrained by a tightened bridle. Next, the righteousness of a righteous person does not consist only in his saying what is good and righteous; rather, he also considers in his spirit how it may be expressed in the most fitting manner, time, company, and place, so that the things spoken may not only be golden sayings but also be interwoven with silver engravings for greater beauty and adornment.

Therefore we see that it rests upon all the godly to set meditation before their speech, and for that reason to speak nothing in a disturbed manner, nothing through blind love, hatred, wrath, or lust. With this excellently agrees what is commanded in James 1, that we should be slow to speak. And since the tongue is like

[1632 witness | PDF p. 303 | printed columns 580–581]

rudder in the ship of this our body, ought it not, like a rudder, to be moved deliberately? And since it is a bridle by which the untamed colt of our nature must be governed and turned, judgment and prudence must be applied in both tightening and loosening it, in turning and turning it back. To this pertains what is commanded in Colossians 4.6: that we not permit a word to be uttered before it is seasoned by grace as by salt. Two reasons for this saying, which is also found in Psalm 37.30, appear to be assigned in verses 31 and 32: one, that the teaching of God is in the heart of the righteous person; the other, that the wicked person continually watches and keeps guard in order to catch the righteous person.

Next, because of the gravity of the matter about which we speak, greater caution must be employed, especially when discourse is undertaken concerning the worship of God and matters pertaining to Christ's kingdom. Therefore, when Nehemiah approached the King in order to treat with him concerning matters pertaining to the Church, before he presented his petition to the King he silently prayed to God within himself with a sudden and darting prayer, Nehemiah 2.4. See further Ecclesiastes 5.1. And since this is required from everyone in prayer, how much more from Ministers, both in the preaching of the word and in composing prayers, or even in reciting them.

Let us examine ourselves as to how we may number our words and weigh them out, as it were, with just weights. For just as we give all precious merchandise by number and weight, so, since speech is a precious thing, let us examine it and bring it to the balance; let us set a guard, and as it were a doorkeeper, at our mouth.

But if this caution must be employed in giving speech, certainly no less care must be employed in receiving and admitting it, so that we likewise either approve or reject words by number and weight. Therefore Christ admonishes us what, and how, we hear, Mark 4.24; Luke 8.18.

In the latter hemistich it is taught that corrupt and depraved speech flows copiously and abundantly from the wicked. For just as a full river cannot but discharge abundant waters, so a wicked person full of malice is unable to contain it. For what can flow from a wicked person except what is wicked? “For how can you,” says Christ, “utter good things, when you are evil?” Matthew 12.

Therefore, the more talkative the wicked are, the greater and more grievous the judgment they heap up for themselves, so that their individual words will be as so many coals by which the fire of divine wrath will burn more greatly and fiercely.

But here it is asked whether the speech of the wicked is perpetually and constantly evil. For we know that Ahithophel's sayings were like a certain oracle. I answer that good sayings can benefit, not the speakers themselves, but others.

In this, therefore, lies the distinction between the righteous and the unrighteous: not that a righteous person does not sometimes speak foolishly and wickedly, as is evident in Peter, Matthew 16.22, and in David, 1 Samuel 25; but the distinction is established in this, that the godly, however much they may sometimes stumble, nevertheless do not do so perpetually.

Verse 29. The Lord is far from the wicked, but he hears the prayer of the righteous.

In רחוק (*far*) there is a metaphor transferred from people who are situated far away and cannot see or hear those calling to them. There is, moreover, an ellipsis in each part of the Proverb, which is supplied in this way: God is far from the wicked and does not hear their prayers; but he is near to the righteous and hears their prayers.

The sum is that God hears none but the godly who lawfully call upon him, lest either the wicked flatter themselves with a vain hope concerning their prayers, or the godly lose heart and distrust that they will be heard.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 304 | printed columns 582–583]

In the former part it is taught that, however much the wicked may cry to God, they are not heard by God. To teach this, he illustrates it by the likeness of a person situated far away, so that he cannot hear or see. For since God fills heaven and earth, he is not far removed, since in him we live, move, and have our being; nor in respect of his essence is he nearer to the godly than to the ungodly. But he is said to be situated far away because his gracious presence, by which he might succor or assist them, is not present to them; nor does he hear them when they call upon him. Rather, he turns or hides his face from them, as it were, while they receive none of the things they seek. See Proverbs 1.28, 29; Isaiah 1.15. The cause of this matter is assigned in 1 Timothy 2; Isaiah 1; Proverbs 1.29.

Although it is grievous for people not to be heard when, in danger, they cry to God, nevertheless something more grievous awaits them. For their prayers are not only ineffectual, but are further turned into sin, Psalm 109. For whatever is not from faith is sin. For just as it happens that people who hate a person cannot bear even for that person to address them, but the more he solicits them the more they are kindled to anger—as in Artaxerxes and Haman, and so forth—so it is here. From these things the common error of people is evident: they suppose that all who, when about to expire, cry, “God, have mercy upon me,” are seeking heaven, although the utterance of these words will not deliver them from hell.

In the latter part it is taught that God is near to the righteous and hears the prayers of the righteous, Psalm 145.18; as though he is always near before they call, and while they have not yet finished their prayers, Isaiah 62.25 and 65.24; Daniel 9.20; Acts 10.30. Whoever seeks finds; to the one knocking, and so forth; and, Whatever you ask of the Father in my name, he will give you.

But here it is asked how the truth of this Proverb stands, since the godly often complain that [God stands far away, hides his face, and plays the deaf man.] I answer that God is near, although we see him no more than Hagar saw the water which was before her feet and in her presence. Hence in the Song he is also said to stand behind the post or the door. Therefore, formulas of speech of this kind arise from temptation, because God does not immediately put into execution what they have obtained by prayers.

Verse 30. The light of the eyes cheers the spirit; a good report fattens the bone.

In לב (*heart*) there is a synecdoche of the part for the whole person; and in ודשן (*fattens*) there is likewise a synecdoche of fatness for a good condition of the whole body and spirit.

The sum is that by the honest and lawful use of the senses a person is preserved both in spirit and body; yet that greater power is placed in hearing than in sight. This is indicated by the word עצם (*bone*), by which a stronger benefit received by the ears is signified.

How wonderfully the sight of light and the sun refreshes the spirit is established in common sense and in everyone's understanding. This can also be understood more clearly from the fact that, when the sky grows dark with clouds or is darkened by night, much is lost and taken away from our delight. But it is shown most clearly by the Egyptian darkness, which affected the Egyptians with most grievous sorrow; likewise by the darkness sent out at the time of the Passion, at midday itself, to torment and torture Christ more grievously; finally, by the fact that darkness is numbered among the punishments inflicted upon the wicked. There are many other things by whose sight the eyes are refreshed and recreated, such as the greenness of trees, flowers of varied colors, and other things of that kind which God created to cheer mankind.

Here, however, diligent care must be taken

[1632 witness | PDF p. 305 | printed columns 584–585]

lest, through abuse of the eyes, those things which God bestows for our good become a fraud and harm to us. Hence the saying in Psalm 119: “Turn away my eyes, lest they see vanity”; as when someone looks upon another woman in order to lust after her, or upon wine when it glows in the cup and sparkles. For although a sight of this kind cheers the spirit for a time, its pleasure vanishes in a moment and afterward brings a piercing and stabbing of the spirit, and that a lasting one.

In the latter part of the Proverb Solomon appears to prefer the hearing of a good thing to the sight of good things, since hearing is a sense more suited to acquiring knowledge, but especially to learning the Gospel thoroughly, in which the sum of salvation is placed. Hence it also happens that the light of understanding and knowledge is carried and brought into the innermost recesses of spirits; without it, the outward light of the sun can supply no solid and lasting delight. Therefore care must be taken that we do not apply our ears to corrupt and depraved speech. For the more usefulness comes to people from its lawful use, the more harmful and dangerous is its abuse.

Verse 31. The ear that listens to the correction of life will lodge in the midst of the wise.

In אזן (*ear*) there is a synecdoche of the part; likewise, תלין (*will lodge*) is used for continual companionship and association.

The sum is: τεκμήριον (*it is an indication*) that people admit salutary corrections when they rejoice in the companionship of the wise, who know how to reprove them.

This Proverb has the same meaning and purpose as the one contained in the twelfth verse of this chapter, and from the opposite side corroborates the very same thing. Nevertheless, as often happens, there are certain things which enlarge and clarify the teaching.

Here he opposes to the scoffer a person who listens to correction, but in this passage he adds “correction of life,” to distinguish kinds of correction. For there are people who will persecute and harass us on account of good deeds and holy custom and conduct; nevertheless, it is not lawful to listen to them or lend them our ears, since this is a correction of death, not of life. And in this class many faultfinders are found, and also many who acquiesce in those rebukes. For they enter the way of salvation not unwillingly; but when they understand that they cannot persist in it without the mockery or contempt of their companions, they immediately turn aside from the way of life. They do not know how to bear even the taunts of the wicked for Christ's sake, much less to suffer the loss of their goods or their life. Christ openly declares to them that he will be covered with shame on account of them before the holy Angels, because they are ashamed of him and of his word before mortal and miserable people. So far are they from bearing the heat of the sun for Christ at shining midday that they do not bear even his slightest ἐκ τῆς πληγῆς (*from the blow*) stroke.

In the latter homistich he establishes the contrary of verse 12. For there it is affirmed of the scoffer that he will not go to the wise; here, on the contrary, it is said that the person who bears being justly corrected with equanimity seeks the companionship of those by whose correction he may become wiser. He also proves this by an argument of comparison from the greater. For he does not suffer himself to be drawn away from them even at night, the time least convenient for association, much less in the daytime; this is the same as dwelling and abiding with them. Hence comes what David declares he did in Psalm 141.5. See how humanely David received Abigail after her correction, and did not desist until he had joined her to himself as his wife. See the history of the Canaanite woman in Matthew 15, but especially of

[1632 witness | PDF p. 306 | printed columns 586–587]

that blind man of whom there is an account in Luke 18, who, the more he was rebuked because he cried out to Christ, called upon him all the more vehemently.

From this passage it can easily be established how few are genuine Christians and disciples of wisdom, since those who shrink from admonitions are no different from schoolboys whom, as soon as they have been punished for negligence and wantonness, a weariness of school seizes.

Verse 32. He who withdraws himself from instruction despises his soul; but he who listens to correction possesses his heart.

The sum is that people are preserved by instruction and correction, and, conversely, perish through contempt for them.

Since people by their nature, because of ignorance of God, are like brute beasts, Proverbs 30 and Psalm 92.7, and in Job are compared to an untamed colt which cannot keep to the road, it is evident that they need instruction, without which they do not even know how to enter the way of life. But just as we are by nature destitute of true knowledge of God, so by the depravity and perversity of that same nature we also bear a spirit that shrinks from instruction and knowledge of God. Thus no horse is more stubborn and recalcitrant toward receiving the saddle, nor any ox toward receiving the yoke, than a person is toward imbibing true knowledge of God. For beasts, however wild and unbridled, are nevertheless tamed by human labor and effort; but people are never restored to good conduct except by God's operation from heaven. Although this occurs by the impulse of the one Spirit of God sent down from heaven, nevertheless God uses human instruction and formation to accomplish it: the work of parents, teachers, masters, and ministers; without them he does not confer his Spirit. Therefore, in this Proverb the person who withdraws from instruction is said to despise his own soul.

First, a person withdraws himself from instruction when he withdraws from those places where knowledge of God is taught and flourishes, as did the prodigal son in Luke 15, who, unwilling to endure life under his father's rule, sought permission to depart abroad once he had received his patrimony. Many children of this kind are found who, weary of domestic discipline, are led by a great desire to travel. Likewise, there are many servants who bear a spirit averse to those households where they are not permitted on the Lord's day to wander, drink, play, and dance as they please.

Next, a person withdraws himself who, although bodily present through fear of those who preside or for the sake of his own advantage, is nevertheless as far absent in spirit and zeal as if he were removed by an interval of a thousand lands. Not a few people are found in this class. Those also withdraw themselves who approach the receiving of doctrine drowsily and without any powers of spirit, understanding, and affection, as is evident in Matthew 22. Finally, those withdraw themselves who, although attentive and diligent, nevertheless look to something other than they ought: those who hear in order to lay snares, or so that they may dispute—or rather wrangle—about religion.

When he says that people of this kind despise their souls, he thereby teaches that the outcome of this withdrawal is far different from what they imagine. For by withdrawing themselves in order to gratify their spirit, they suppose that they show honor to their souls, and, conversely, that to be enrolled and entered in Christ's school is something servile and abject; but they are greatly mistaken. For they plainly despise their souls, which in this manner lie in ignorance and mire and, as it were, in the blood of their native depravity. Nor do they merely lie there, but day by day they procure and draw upon themselves greater blindness, hardness, or perversity, until, laden with sins, they are plunged into the deepest abyss. What inconsiderateness is this, moreover, to serve bodily pleasures,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 307 | printed columns 588–589]

by which the soul of man, his noblest part, perishes; just as if someone, in order to remedy the pain of those restored to life, were to suffer the loss of his head? Yet in this manner not even the body is provided for, because it will share destruction with the soul.

In the latter hemistich he affirms that he who listens to correction possesses his own soul. Here, first, it is taught that, for acquiring piety and true wisdom, it is not sufficient that a person be imbued with salutary precepts and sound doctrine unless correction is added as well. For we ought to reckon that we are dealt with excellently amid this corruption and perversity of our nature if, by all the means jointly instituted by God, we make progress toward salvation. Remnants of an inert and asinine nature always remain in us and need the whip and scourge. For although the Spirit is ready, the flesh fails in strength, Matthew 26. This is clearly established by an enumeration of the parts.

First, in a household a rod is necessary for correcting children. For he who spares the rod hates his son. In David's household there existed abundant and copious knowledge of God, where the example of David's life—if you except the deed concerning Uriah—shone brightly and brilliantly in the eyes of the whole household. Nevertheless, both Absalom and Adonijah perished because the rod of correction had been withheld, as Scripture expressly notes. It is evident that the same is useful in the commonwealth, Ecclesiastes 8.11. In the Church of Christ there is all the greater need of correction because people are more unfit for performing their duty in the Church than either in a household or commonwealth. For maintaining and preserving those societies, we have drawn from nature certain seeds which are wholly absent for performing and perfecting the things required in God's Church, Matthew 18; 1 Corinthians 4.5. It will be especially profitable for correction to be established and exercised; indeed, its mere establishment in every society does the greatest good in restraining sin.

For just as in households and schools the mere handling of a rod introduces fear, and in a commonwealth the carrying and display before the Magistrate of rods and axes, or of the sword, strikes terror and constrains bold spirits so that they do not break forth as their nature impels them; so also in the Church the bare establishment of discipline and correction keeps many in their duty, Deuteronomy 13.12; 1 Timothy 5.28. Next, people are chastised not only when they themselves are corrected, but when they see others chastised and corrected. And certainly both the latter and the former correction are among the things contained in this Proverb, and all the godly are bound by duty to profit from them.

But when he says that the person who listens to correction possesses his soul, this is not unlike the never sufficiently praised saying which Christ uttered in Matthew 20.22 and Luke 21.19: “In patience you shall possess your souls.” For listening to correction is a certain kind of patience. Thus it can easily be established that those who reject correction suffer the loss of their spirit and consequently also of their soul.

From this it is also gathered how unjust is the opinion of those who take it ill that the doctrine of the Gospel, with exhortation applied, should be brought nearer by the goads of rebuke; and who say that it is sufficient for Ministers to present doctrine and permit each person to apply it for himself. This is just as if, at a spiritual feast, it were enough to furnish the table well and leave each person free to eat and feed himself from whatever dish he wishes, although Scripture commands Ministers to divide and distribute to each person his portion, and likens them to stewards who in a household supply each person food and clothing according to the condition of his age, strength, health, rank, and sex.

Verse 33. Before reverence comes

[1632 witness | PDF p. 308 | printed columns 590–591]

instruction in wisdom, and before honor humility.

The sum is: that the true and compendious way to all happiness—both piety and the honor of piety—is, first, through the admission of salutary correction, which flows from humility and abasement of mind.

Two things are taught in this Proverb, of which each exists as the prop and support of the other, so that the truth of the one illuminates the truth of the other.

And since two things are required for perfect happiness—the one, that we be true and genuine worshipers of God; the other, that we receive from God the reward of that piety—the road to the former is through that instruction which has discipline and correction joined to it; the road to the latter, through humility and abasement of mind.

And indeed, as to the former, it is manifest that no one has ever been brought to the true knowledge of God, or to reverence for God, unless he was first pricked by the goads and sharp points of the divine law. Testimony to this matter is Acts 2.37; Luke 3.10, etc.; and Acts 9.4, 5, etc. For just as neither any horse nor any ox is so docile and tractable that, before it grows tame for bearing either saddles or a yoke, it has not felt a spur or goad, so no mortal is found of so pliant and gentle a disposition that he can be brought to good conduct without threats and some sense of divine wrath. Next, because he calls this instruction “instruction in wisdom,” it is readily shown that men are brought to good conduct not by severity of any sort, but by that which the wisdom of God teaches and has delivered in the Scriptures; and, for that reason, that the severity employed by the Pontificians is vain: through self-flagellation, through pilgrimages undertaken with bare feet, through some abstinence or other from food and drink, before they have perceived what true fasting is. But the instruction of which the Prophet speaks is a graphic and true description, and as it were a picture, of sins; and then an express representation of divine judgment and of hell.

From this it is clear that he is devoid of true piety who has never been wounded or pricked by a sense of his sins, who has not mourned his sins separately and by themselves, as once the Jews mourned Josiah, as a parent mourns the death of a firstborn or only son, as a bride mourns the death of her bridegroom, newly joined to her in marriage. See Zechariah 11. Still farther removed from piety are those who cannot bear the rebuke and correction of their sins.

In the latter hemistich he teaches that the way to honor lies through humility and submission of mind. This indeed will be found true, whether concerning the obtaining of honor in this life or in the other. And indeed, as to the honor which in the age to come will be possessed by those humble in mind, it must be known that the way to climb into heaven is first to descend to the lower regions through a true and living sense of our sins and of the recompense which is owed us for our sins. This is illustrated by the parable of the Pharisee and the Publican in Luke 18, of the prodigal and dutiful son in Luke 15, and of the banquet guests in Luke 14; and it is confirmed and proved by a commonplace proverb corroborated by Christ’s authority and testimony: He who humbles himself will be exalted.

But as to the honor which is possessed in this age, although human prudence may declare that the man who does not thrust himself forward and advance himself will always be despised, and that he who is abject in his own judgment will be trampled down in the judgment of others, yet, since [advancement and primacy come neither from the east, nor from the west, nor from the desert; but God alone is he who raises this man and casts that man down,] it is certain that those are to be raised to the summit of honor who in their own
