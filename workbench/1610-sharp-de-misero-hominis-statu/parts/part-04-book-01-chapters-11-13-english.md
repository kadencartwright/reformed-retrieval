Chapter XI

What Original Sin Is and How Many Kinds There Are

The papists do not agree about the nature of original sin. Bellarmine
therefore refutes his fellow papists Pighius and Catharinus in book 5, chapter
10, *On the Loss of Grace*.

Original sin is that which a human being draws along with himself from his
origin, and it has two parts. The first is defection from God in Adam's loins.
The second is the depravity that followed that defection, which is the inborn
viciousness we bring from the womb.

Original sin is accordingly said to be twofold: imputed and inhering. The
imputed sin is that defection of Adam which was imputed to all his descendants
who were in his loins. That sin was in Adam by inherence, but is in us only by
imputation. It is imputed to us because we were in Adam as in our stock and
root, and just as we received God's image in him, so we lost it in him, Romans
5:12. Thus Bellarmine rightly says in his book *On the Loss of Grace*,

[1610 printed p. 67 | Google Books PA67]

chapter 17: “If sin is taken for an action that conflicts with the law,” he
says, “original sin is Adam's first disobedience, committed by Adam himself,
not as he was an individual person, but as he bore the person of the whole
human race.”

Calvin defines inhering original sin as follows in *Institutes*, book 2,
chapter 1, section 8: “It is the hereditary depravity and corruption of our
nature diffused through all parts of the soul, which first makes us liable to
God's wrath and then also brings forth in us the works which Scripture calls
works of the flesh.” This sin is called natural, not because it constitutes
the essence of a thing, nor because it always and necessarily flows from it as
a property of its subject, but because it exists together with the subject,
in the way that certain diseases are called natural.

The subject of this sin is the whole human being who has been infected with
this sin, Romans 7:18; 8:7; Ephesians 2:1; 4:17. This corruption has two parts.
I. The defect of original good, which is an inborn depravity by which our whole
nature has been deprived of the goodness or original grace in which the first
man had been created. It exists both in the soul, by which the mind has been
deprived of the light of understanding and reason and the will of the
rectitude of its liberty, as in Matthew 15:11, 16, 17; John 1:5; 6:44; 9:39;
Acts 26:18; Romans 1:21; 1 Corinthians 2:14; Genesis 6:5; 8:21; Romans 3:10;
7:23; 8:7; Ephesians 2:1; and in the flesh, by which the lower faculties, such
as the affections and appetites, have been stripped of conformity to God's
law.

II. The succession of evil into the place of original good, which is inborn
depravity, by which our whole nature has been rendered unfit for good and
inclined only to evil,

[1610 printed p. 68 | Google Books PA68]

both in the soul—whether you consider the mind, which has been darkened by
ignorance and is blind especially in divine matters, 2 Corinthians 4:4; 3:5;
1 Corinthians 2:14; Ephesians 4:17; 5:8, or the will, which rebels against the
will of God and is a slave of sin, Matthew 23:37; Romans 7:14—and in the flesh,
which is hostile to God, Romans 8:7. For in the sensitive appetite, which is
called the flesh, there is an inclination to resist right reason and to desire
objects that conflict with right reason. In Scripture this inhering sin is
called sin dwelling within, Romans 7:20; the evil that lies near at hand,
Romans 7:21; the law in the members rebelling against the law of the mind,
Romans 7:22; the body of death, Romans 7:24; concupiscence, James 1:14; and so
forth.

The answer to the question whether concupiscence is original sin is therefore
easy from what has been said: it is, but it is not the whole of original sin.
For besides concupiscence there is something imputed to us and there is the
absence of original righteousness, in whose place concupiscence succeeded.
Here “concupiscence” does not signify the soul's natural power or the act of
desiring, but an inhering vice which is the tinder and root of other sins.

The following therefore err:

1. Andradius, the interpreter of the Council of Trent, who denies that the
vice of our origin is sin.

2. Those who, like Anselm and others, say original sin is merely the bare
absence of original righteousness that ought to be present, since our nature
is not only empty of good but full of evil.

3. The scholastics, some of whom maintained that it is merely an inclination
to sin, while others maintained that it is not fault but punishment for the
first man's sin.

4. Pighius and Catharinus, who maintained that it is merely liability arising
from

[1610 printed p. 69 | Google Books PA69]

another's transgression, without any inclination inhering in us, contrary to
Scripture in Job 14:4; Psalm 51:7; John 3:6; Romans 5:19; Ephesians 2:1; and so
forth.

The papists affirm two things about original sin, or concupiscence—a term
sometimes taken for the whole of inhering original sin, and sometimes for the
part of it that succeeded into the place of original good. 1. Bellarmine says
in book 5, chapter 4, *On the Loss of Grace*, that ignorance, concupiscence,
and so forth are merely wounds of nature, weaknesses and diseases left by
original sin, and are not properly sin.

Stapleton, however, following Thomas, says that concupiscence is materially
original sin, as Bellarmine testifies in chapter 5 of the book cited.

Second, Pighius and others maintain that original sin and concupiscence are in
the lower part of the soul, which they call the flesh, but not in the intellect
and will.

We, however, say that “concupiscence” has two meanings. 1. It signifies simply
desiring something, with the mind's judgment and the will's consent going
before; in this sense it is a good work of God in nature. 2. It signifies the
inclination and propensity born from the first sin, which has so entangled all
parts of the soul that we cannot avoid sinning. It signifies not only the
animal or sensual appetite, by whose force we are incited to desire sensible
things, but also that of the mind itself—and especially of the will—by which we
think, meditate, undertake, and will things contrary to God's law and which
are prohibited by the last commandment.

[1610 printed p. 70 | Google Books PA70]

Hence there arises a twofold controversy between us and the papists:

1. Is concupiscence truly sin?

2. What is its subject?

First, then, that concupiscence truly is sin is proved by these arguments.

Argument 1. Concupiscence is prohibited by the tenth commandment. Therefore it
is sin.

Argument 2. Scripture testifies to the same thing in various places, such as
Matthew 5:28; John 8:44; Romans 7:7, 19; Galatians 5:20; Ephesians 4:22; 2
Peter 2:4; 1 John 2:16.

Argument 3. That which, after it has conceived, gives birth to sin is itself
sin. For fruits are of the same nature as their root, and a bad tree bears bad
fruit. But concupiscence is such a thing, James 1:15.

Argument 4. Augustine in book 3, chapter 21, *Against Julian the Pelagian*,
teaches that concupiscence is an evil thing and a vice of the whole nature.

Objections

Objection 1. That which is necessarily in us not from ourselves but from
another is not sin. But concupiscence, or original sin, is such a thing.
Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition, because it is true only in political
judgment, and even there only in part, but not in God's judgment.

Objection 2. Concupiscence is a work of God. Therefore it is not sin.

Response. This is a fallacy from equivocation. Insofar as concupiscence
signifies simply desiring, it is a work of God; but insofar as it signifies
disordered desire in fallen man, it is not a work of God.

[1610 printed p. 71 | Google Books PA71]

Objection 3. The tenth commandment prohibits the act of desiring itself, not
the disease.

Response I. That act is not prohibited insofar as it is a work of nature.

Response II. The disease is chiefly prohibited—that is, the very root of every
sin—along with every evil motion, not only in the lower part of the soul but
also in the higher.

Chapter XII

The Subject Who Has Original Sin

The second controversy on this matter between us and the papists concerns the
subject of original sin, or concupiscence. The subject of this sin is twofold.
I. The subject *who* has it, namely, every human being who comes into the light
under the common law of birth. Therefore Christ alone was free from this sin.

II. The subject *in which* it resides is the whole human being in soul and
body. We and the papists dispute about both. First, concerning the subject who
has it, Bellarmine says in book 4, chapter 15, *On the Loss of Grace*, that it
is a pious and probable opinion to believe that the Virgin Mary was conceived
without original sin, and he tries to confirm it with arguments. Indeed, some
affirm that she was free from every sin, both original and actual. Thomas said
she was free from every actual sin, while Scotus said she was free from
original sin, *Summa*, III, question 27, article 2; S., *Sentences*, III,
distinction 3, question 1. The Pelagians, Celestinus, the Armenians, and
Pighius maintained that infants are born without this sin. The Council of
Basel in session 36 asserts that Mary was conceived without original sin.
Their opinion is refuted by the following arguments.

[1610 printed p. 72 | Google Books PA72]

Argument I. Scripture exempts Christ alone from this sin, 2 Corinthians 5:21;
Hebrews 7:26; Romans 11:32; Galatians 3:22; Job 15:14; Psalm 143:1; 1 John 1:8;
Romans 3:19. Therefore.

Argument II. Christ is said to have died for all, with no exception added, 2
Corinthians 5:15; Romans 8:0; and so forth, as Bellarmine concedes in chapter
16 of the place cited. Therefore.

Argument III. All are said to have sinned in Adam, Romans 5:12, 18; 1
Corinthians 15:22, with no one excepted. Therefore.

Argument IV. If Mary or infants had been without original sin and other sins,
they would not have been or be liable to death, because death is the wages of
sin, Romans 6:23. But she died and infants die. Therefore.

Argument V. Bellarmine says in book 4, chapter 16, *On the Loss of Grace*, that
Pope Sixtus IV established the penalty of excommunication against those who
dare define either opinion as heretical; the Council of Trent does likewise in
session 5. Therefore either Bellarmine was excommunicated or he ought not to
have defined this question.

Argument VI. Whoever are baptized are baptized for the remission of sins,
Acts 2:38; 22:16; Ephesians 5:25. Bellarmine concedes this proposition and
proves it true in book 4, chapter 17, *On the Loss of Grace and the State of
Sin*. But Mary was baptized, according to Canisius the Jesuit in book 1 *On
the Virgin Mary*, who says that the learned do not doubt this. Albert the Great
also asserts in *On the Praise of the Virgin* that Mary participated in every
sacrament except that of orders, and so forth. Therefore. But those who are
baptized for the remission of sins have sin, or they are baptized in vain.
Therefore.

Argument VII. Whoever acknowledges that he has a Savior and that his mercy
extends to all who

[1610 printed p. 73 | Google Books PA73]

fear God—among whose number Mary was—acknowledges that he has sin. But Mary
acknowledged her Savior, Luke 1:47, and that God's mercy extended to her, Luke
1:50. For no one dares exclude her from the number of those who fear God;
indeed, Bellarmine calls Mary a daughter of mercy in book 4, chapter 16, *On
the Loss of Grace*. Therefore.

Argument VIII. Scripture teaches that there were sins in Mary, John 2:4
(Theophylact on John 2 says that Christ did not rebuke his mother in vain);
Mark 3:21, 31, 32; Luke 1:34; and so forth. Therefore.

Argument IX. If Mary conceived her prayers according to the form of prayer
Christ prescribed, then she confessed herself a sinner when she asked for the
remission of sins. But she did the former, even according to the testimony of
the adversaries themselves. Therefore. This is proved that she asked this for
herself and not merely for the Church: if she asked the other things for
herself, then she also asked for the remission of sins; but they do not deny
the former. Therefore.

Argument X. Augustine near the end of *On the Perfection of Righteousness*;
book 2 *On the Grace of Christ and Original Sin*; Ambrose, book 2 *On Luke*;
Anselm; Athanasius; and others—indeed, the papist doctors themselves—admit
this. Gregory of Rimini says as follows in distinction 30, article 1, on the
second book of the *Sentences*: “It seems to me that one should rather hold
what is more consonant with sacred Scripture and the sayings of the saints.
Therefore, without prejudice to a better opinion and always preserving
reverence for the mother of God, it seems to me that one must say that she was
conceived with original sin. I am led to this,” he says, “because wherever
Scripture speaks about this it pronounces a universal judgment concerning all
without exception, and all its interpreters understand it universally of all
who are born in a natural manner. From this it seems to follow that

[1610 printed p. 74 | Google Books PA74]

to except anyone from it is to contradict sacred Scripture.” Thomas, question
4, *On Original Sin*, article 6, says: “It is erroneous to say
that some are descended seminally from Adam without original sin, for then
there would be some human beings who did not need the redemption accomplished
through Christ. Hence it must be conceded without qualification that all who
are propagated seminally from Adam contract original sin.” But Mary was
propagated seminally from Adam. Therefore.

Bellarmine's Objections

Objection 1. The first man was a figure of Christ. But the first man was
created from earth that had not been cursed. Therefore the second was likewise
from a virgin untouched by every curse.

Response 1. The first man was a figure of Christ not with respect to the
matter of his body or his generation, but in that just as all die in Adam, so
all are made alive in Christ, as the text teaches and as Chrysostom and others
interpret the passage.

Response 2. Mary was not accursed but blessed, though not without sin. For all
the faithful are blessed, Matthew 5:3; Psalms 1:2; 32:1, yet they are liable to
sins.

Objection 2. Eve was a type and figure of Mary. But Eve was formed without
sin. Therefore Mary was also.

Response 1. Scripture says nothing about this type.

Response 2. Many things in figures do not agree with the things figured, and
conversely. Thus many things belonged to the priests of the Old Testament
which did not belong to Christ, and conversely. Finally, Mary, from the

[1610 printed p. 75 | Google Books PA75]

moment of conception, was sanctified in the Mediator, though she was not
without the inherence of sin.

Objection 3. The bride of Christ is called altogether beautiful, Song of Songs
2. Therefore.

Response 1. The bride of Christ is not Mary but the Church.

Response 2. The Church has the stains of both original and actual sin and yet
is called altogether beautiful because of pardon in Christ and
sanctification. The same applies to Mary.

Objection 4. It is certain that God could have preserved the virgin free from
this sin, and it is likely that he willed to do so.

Response. I deny the second part.

Insistence 1. Both a mother's honor and her disgrace redound to her son.
Therefore.

Response. No disgrace redounded to the Son. Rather, that Christ was conceived
from Mary's seed by its special sanctification was an honor to both. Further,
no disgrace is branded upon Mary by believing about her what is true, but the
contrary.

Insistence 2. What fellowship has Christ with Belial? But the fellowship of
Christ with his mother was great. Therefore.

Response. Christ's mother was not Belial, but justified and sanctified; for
not everyone in whom sin exists is Belial. Further, the seed from which Christ
was conceived was sanctified in a singular manner. Christ was therefore in no
way joined to his mother's corruption.

Insistence 3. Christ dwelt in his mother in a singular manner. But wisdom does
not enter a malicious soul, Wisdom 1. Therefore.

[1610 printed p. 76 | Google Books PA76]

Response. Mary's soul was not malicious, for that passage speaks about the
reprobate. Further, it does not follow that because Christ dwelt in his mother
in a singular manner, she was therefore without every sin.

Insistence IV. Mary was raised to a singular dignity, and many things were
revealed to her and about her. Therefore.

Response. I deny the consequence. Further, the things said about revelations
are ridiculous.

Insistence V. The feast of Mary's conception is celebrated in the Church. But
if she had been conceived in sin, the Church would not do this. Therefore.

Response 1. The Antichristian Church, instituted by Pope Sixtus IV, celebrates
the feast. Finally, Gregory of Rimini responds in the place cited that the
feast did not regard her conception but the sanctification that soon followed
it.

Objection V. In the angelic salutation Mary is called full of grace, Luke
1:28. But she is called full of grace in no different way than Christ, John
1:14. Christ is called full of grace because he was endowed with perfect
original holiness. Therefore.

Response. I deny the proposition, for she is not called *plērēs charitos* but
*kecharitōmenē*—therefore, freely beloved, one who has obtained grace, or one
favored with grace. The old interpreter therefore translated it badly as
“full of grace.” Of Christ, however, John 1 says that he was *plērēs
charitos*, because he is himself the fountain of grace; and Mary and others
have obtained, or do obtain, grace only in him.

[1610 printed p. 77 | Google Books PA77]

Chapter XIII

The Subject in Which Original Sin Resides

The pontifical opinion concerning the subject in which original sin resides
is as follows. The Master of the *Sentences*, book 2, distinction 31, said that
concupiscence is merely the tinder of sin inhering in the flesh, inclining the
sensitive appetite and bending the will. The scholastics and certain others
asserted this because they thought that original sin could be propagated only
through the body. Others held that the tinder of sin exists only in the
sensitive appetite, while the mind remains free from every stain. Many papists
subscribe to this opinion in order to construct from it free choice, the merit
of congruity, preparations for grace, and other heresies of that kind, by
which various articles of faith, and especially the article on justification,
have been corrupted.

Domingo de Soto in the writing he presented to the Council of Trent maintained
that only a lack of rectitude exists in the higher part of the soul, that is,
the mind and will.

We, however, assert that it exists in the whole human being as its subject,
relying on the following arguments.

Argument I. Scripture clearly testifies that the seat of original sin and
concupiscence is in the whole human being, Ephesians 4:17; Romans 8:7; 7:18,
23; 6:6, 12, 2; 1 Corinthians 2:14; 2 Corinthians 3:5; Genesis 6:5; 8:21; 2
Timothy 2:25.

Argument II. That which is restored and regenerated was corrupted through
sin. But the whole human being is restored in soul and body, Colossians 3:1,
5, 8, 12 and the places cited above. Therefore.

Argument III. Where the seat of righteousness and God's image was, there is
the seat of original sin and concupiscence. But the seat of God's image was
the whole human being,

[1610 printed p. 78 | Google Books PA78]

and especially the mind and will, Romans 12:1; Ephesians 4:17; 1 Thessalonians
5:23; and so forth. Therefore.

Argument IV. If the tenth commandment condemns not only the things of the
flesh but also those of the mind and will, then the seat of original sin is
not only the flesh but also the mind and will. But the former is true;
otherwise there would be no distinction between the tenth commandment and the
rest. Therefore.

Argument V. If among the concupiscences the apostle numbers not only
adulteries, drunkenness, and so forth, but also idolatry, heresies, and the
other evils of the mind, then not only the flesh but also the mind is the
subject of concupiscence. But the former is true, Galatians 5:20; Colossians
2:18. Therefore.

Argument VI. Sin is imputed to both body and soul, and because of original sin
both body and soul are punished, Romans 5:14. Therefore.

Argument VII. The members of the body are called weapons of unrighteousness,
Romans 6:12. Therefore unrighteousness itself does not have its seat chiefly
and entirely in the flesh, but in that which directs the flesh, namely, the
mind and will.

Argument VIII. Augustine says in his letter to Jerome that he firmly holds
that the soul draws the original stain and cannot be freed from original sin
except through Christ's grace. He says likewise in chapter 3 *On Nature and
Grace*: “Every good that a human being has in his formation, life, senses, and
mind he has from the supreme God; but the vice which darkens and weakens these
natural goods flows from original sin.” Therefore.

Objections

Objection 1. In Romans 7:18 and Galatians 5:19 the apostle uses

[1610 printed p. 79 | Google Books PA79]

the name “flesh” when he speaks about the subject of this sin. Therefore its
subject is only the flesh, that is, the sensible part of a human being.

Response. I deny the consequence, because in those passages “flesh” does not
signify one part of a human being but the whole human being insofar as he is
unregenerate. This is therefore a fallacy from the equivocation of “flesh,”
for the flesh is opposed to the Spirit—that is, to the regenerate person, not
to the soul—John 3:6. Further, *phronēma* is attributed to the flesh, Romans
8:6, 7.

Insistence 1. If this is true, then the same person will be entirely
unregenerate and regenerate.

Response. Let this be granted, but not in every respect, just as the same
person is rightly called healthy and sick: healthy insofar as the disease has
left the whole person; sick insofar as remnants of disease still exist in the
whole person. So it is also with us, because we are not perfectly regenerated
in this life.

Insistence 2. But what is born of the Spirit is spirit. Therefore in the
regenerate there is no longer flesh or concupiscence.

Response. Insofar as he is Spirit, concupiscence is removed. But he is not
Spirit in every respect; in part he is also flesh.

Insistence 3. Romans 6:12 says that sin reigns in the mortal body. Therefore
the soul is not its subject.

Response. In the word “body” there is a synecdoche for the whole human being;
this is therefore a fallacy from the figure of speech. Further, we obey the
concupiscences of the sin that dwells in the body not merely with the body but
especially with the faculties of the soul.

Question I. Do we draw original sin from Adam alone, or also from Eve?

Response. From Adam alone. I. Romans 5:12 says that sin entered through one
man. II. Only

[1610 printed p. 80 | Google Books PA80]

Adam is a type of the Christ to come. Therefore, just as sins are taken away
through Christ alone, so they were introduced through Adam alone. III. Death
came through one man, 1 Corinthians 15:21, and therefore so did sin.

Objection. In Psalm 51 David says he was conceived by his mother in sin.
Therefore.

Response. This is a fallacy from an accident. For he is conceived by a mother
in whom sin exists and into whom it is propagated, but he does not say that we
draw original sin itself from our mother.

Question II. If Eve alone had sinned while Adam remained in innocence, would
Eve's sin and death have been transmitted to their descendants?

Response. Thomas, *First Part of the Second Part*, question 81, article 5:
No. I. We contract original sin from Adam alone. When Adam sinned, we were in
him as in the active principle, but not in Eve in this manner. And if we do not
draw sin from Eve, neither do we draw death from her, because death is the
wages of sin, Romans 6:23. II. Although Eve sinned first, it is not said that
all sinned and die in Eve, but in Adam. Others, however, think differently,
namely, that from Eve's sin alone the necessity of dying and the other
disadvantages of mortality would have flowed down to the children.

Question III. If the first parents had not sinned but Cain or some other
descendant had later sinned, would that sin have flowed down to his children?

Response. Thomas, question 5 *On Evil*, article 4: Yes. The following argument
supports his opinion. Whoever first sinned would have transmitted a nature
lacking original righteousness, because he would have lost it through sin and
no one can transmit what he does not have. Therefore he would have transmitted
a nature

[1610 printed p. 81 | Google Books PA81]

turned away from God and sinful. For no one can give what is clean from what
is unclean. Therefore that sinner's descendants would have been propagated
stained with sin, while others would not.

Some, however, answer no, because the first man alone bore the person of the
whole nature, while every sin of all others is personal and particular and
therefore could not infect others.

Question 4. Does original sin apply as much to the children of believers as
to those of unbelievers?

Response. Yes. This is proved: 1. Psalm 51:7; Ephesians 2:3; Galatians 2:15.
2. Just as circumcision was formerly administered, so now baptism is to be
administered to the children of believers.

Objection. God is called the God of believers and of their offspring, Genesis
17; Acts 2:39. Therefore.

Response. That promise concerns spiritual offspring through faith, not carnal
offspring, as the apostle explains in Romans 4 and 9 and Galatians 3 and 4.
Therefore the children of believers are not in the covenant insofar as they
are carnally descended from believers, but because they are so spiritually.

II. This sin applies to the children of believers who are also themselves
believers, but in a very different way than it does to unbelievers. For in
believers it is present with respect to inherence, not with respect to
imputation; in unbelievers, in both ways.

Question 5. Is only Adam's first sin transmitted to his descendants, or are
Adam's other sins and the sins of other parents also transmitted?

Response. The first alone. 1. Scripture says this in Romans 5:12, for it says
“sin,” not “sins,” and “the sin of one,” verses 15, 17, 19; 1 Corinthians
15:22.

2. If the sins of others were propagated, then Adam alone would not rightly
be called a type of the Christ to come.

[1610 printed p. 82 | Google Books PA82]

3. If Adam had not sinned, he would have transmitted original righteousness,
which had been given not so much to him as to the whole nature in him. He would
not, however, have transmitted personal gifts such as wisdom, prudence,
acquired virtues, and so forth. Therefore neither would he transmit personal
sins, but only that sin of the whole nature on account of which he together
with his descendants was deprived of original righteousness.
