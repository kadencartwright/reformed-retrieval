> **Working-edition notice:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,

## Work and edition

- **Author:** Robert Boyd of Trochrig (Robertus Bodius a Trochoregia,
  1578–1627)
- **Title:** *In Epistolam Pauli Apostoli ad Ephesios Prælectiones supra CC*
- **Edition used:** London, at the expense of the Stationers' Company, 1652
- **Form:** more than two hundred lectures on Ephesians, with analysis,
  exposition, doctrinal observations, commonplaces, questions, controversies,
  and four indexes
- **Publication status:** posthumous

## Editorial method

Page images govern. BSB's layout-aware hOCR is used only as a finding and
collation aid. The Latin normalizes long `s`, ligatures, explicit
abbreviations, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v`; it retains
substantive spelling, Greek, headings, numbered structures, citations, and
marginal annotations. Running heads, ornaments, signatures, catchwords,
column letters, and bare printed foliation are omitted from the prose.

Source markers have this exact form:

`[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 39 | Præfatio p. 5]`

The first preface page has no visible number and is marked `Præfatio p. [1],
unnumbered`. At page-crossing word or sentence boundaries the Latin joins
printer divisions and places the new marker before the complete continuation;
the English marker is aligned to the corresponding phrase. Substantive
marginal notes are linearized after the relevant passage in both files.

The English is a fresh, full translation of the paired Latin, not a summary.
No modern English version served as a wording source. Greek quotations are
translated in context while the Greek remains visible in the Latin file.

Hinc jam secundo loco perpendendum venit Charitatis objectum, quod per
(*Sanctos omnes*) exprimit Apostolus, h. e. omnes Fideles, & quotquot sunt
Ecclesiæ Christianæ membra: quamvis enim proximi (quem ut nosmet ipsos diligere
lege Dei jubemur, in magno illo præcepto secundæ tabulæ summam & sensum
complectente) nomen ad omnes in universum homines extendendum sit, ut homines
sunt, ad eandem Dei imaginem nobiscum conditi; nec ejusdem tantum humanæ naturæ
vinculo, sed & ejusdem originis ex iisdem primis parentibus, ejusdemque
sanguinis cognatione devincti (Sicut nos monet ac docet Apostolus, Act. 17.
*Fecitque*, inquit, *ex uno sanguine totam gentem humanam, ut habitaret super
universâ terræ superficie*) ut homines sunt, inquam, non autem ut peccatores ac
Dei hostes ac adversarii: Sic enim verus ac perfectus Dei amor, per quem fit ut
omnia in Deo ac propter Deum amemus, eorum etiam omnium quæ Deus odit ac
detestatur odium secum necessariò importat; putà non peccati tantum ac malitiæ,
& iniquitatis omnis, quæ cum Dei summâ sanctitate, puritate, atque justitiâ
pugnat, sed & peccatorum qua tales sunt, eorum præsertim quos reprobos, & à Dei
communione æternum seclusos esse constat, ut diabolum inprimis cum suis Angelis,
& quotquot in ejus potestatem, immutabili Dei decreto judicioque traditi sunt,
juxta illud Psalmistæ Regii, Ps. 139. qui affectu vehementi sic in verba nobis
admodum notanda prorumpit: *An non osores tuos, O Jehovah odio habeo? Et de
insurgentibus in te, fastidio meipsum torqueo? Perfecto odio odi illos;
inimicorum loco sunt mihi.* Quod ut suæ sinceritatis argumentum, & non fucati in
Deum amoris, ac veræ pietatis indicium, Prophetam Deo afferre patet ex verbis
quæ proximè subjungit, *Pervestiga me Deus fortis*, inquit, *& cognosce animum
meum: Proba me, & cognosce cogitationes meas.* Quamvis inquam amor proximi, ad
homines omnes omnino, qua homines, ex Dei voluntate officioque nostro pertingat;
atque ita ad omnes in universum extendi debeat: ut nullo odio, nullis inimicitiis
privatis, nullius offensæ injuriæve prætextu, nullius denique noxæ quæ animos
nostros exacerbet obtentu, vinculum illud humanitatis, propinquitatis, & amoris
fraterni debeat abrumpi: Quia tamen, hic de Charitate tantum Christianâ propriè
mentionem facit Apostolus, quæ ex fide in Jesum Christum ortum habet, propterea
meritò ad Sanctos & Fideles, ut ad objectum suum proprium & peculiare eam
restringit. Siquidem amoris illius etiam sancti, & ex fideli corde, ex animo
puro, bonâque conscientiâ profecti, gradus quosdam esse convenit, secundum quos
fideles alios aliis arctius complectantur, alios aliis intentius, impensiusque
diligant, nec eodem planè charitatis affectu fidelem, v. g. & infidelem
prosequantur, Turcam & Christianum, Verè pium, & Atheum aliquem; Idololatram &
veræ Ecclesiæ hostem infensissimum. Absit ut hæc tam absona, tam absurda
cogitemus! cujus rei ratio rursus ex jam dictis manifesta. Quod scilicet vir
verè pius ac fidelis in Deo, ac propter Deum & Christum, proximos quoscunque
diligit.

From this, in the second place, we now come to consider the object of charity,
which the apostle expresses by “all the saints,” that is, all believers and
all who are members of the Christian Church. For although the name “neighbor”
—whom we are commanded by God’s law to love as ourselves in that great command
which embraces the sum and meaning of the second table—must be extended to all
human beings without exception insofar as they are human, created together
with us in the same image of God, bound not only by the bond of the same human
nature but also by the same origin from the same first parents and by kinship
of the same blood (as the apostle warns and teaches us in Acts 17: “He made the
whole human race from one blood, to dwell over the entire face of the earth”):
they are to be loved, I say, insofar as they are human, but not insofar as they
are sinners and enemies and adversaries of God. For the true and perfect love
of God, through which we love all things in God and for God’s sake, necessarily
carries with it hatred also of all the things that God hates and detests: not
only sin, wickedness, and every iniquity which conflicts with God’s supreme
holiness, purity, and justice, but also sinners insofar as they are such,
especially those who are known to be reprobate and eternally excluded from
communion with God—above all the devil with his angels and all who, by God’s
immutable decree and judgment, have been delivered into his power. This accords
with the royal psalmist’s words in Psalm 139, where he breaks out with intense
feeling into statements highly worthy of our attention: “Do I not hate those
who hate you, O Jehovah? And do I not torment myself with loathing over those
who rise against you? I hate them with perfect hatred; they are counted as
enemies to me.” It is clear from the words the prophet immediately adds that he
presents this to God as evidence of his own sincerity, his unfeigned love of
God, and true godliness: “Search me, mighty God,” he says, “and know my mind;
test me, and know my thoughts.” Although, I say, love of one’s neighbor extends
by God’s will and by our duty to absolutely all people insofar as they are
human, and must thus be extended to everyone without exception, so that the
bond of humanity, kinship, and brotherly love must not be broken by any hatred,
private enmities, pretext of any offense or injury, or allegation of any harm
that may embitter our minds; nevertheless, because the apostle here properly
mentions only Christian charity, which has its origin in faith in Jesus
Christ, he rightly restricts it to the saints and believers as its proper and
peculiar object. For even that holy love which proceeds from a faithful heart,
a pure mind, and a good conscience must have certain degrees. According to
these degrees believers embrace some more closely than others and love some
more earnestly and devotedly than others. They do not pursue a believer and an
unbeliever with exactly the same affection of charity—a Turk and a Christian,
a truly godly person and an atheist, an idolater and the most bitter enemy of
the true Church. Far be it from us to entertain thoughts so discordant and
absurd! The reason for this is again evident from what has already been said:
the truly godly and faithful person loves every neighbor in God and for the

It necessarily follows from this that each person is dearer to him in
proportion as that person is dearer to God and more closely joined to Christ
himself. He does not love indiscriminately and without distinction, as the
saying goes, “In the same basin he washes both the good man and the bad”—the
upright and the wicked are washed in the same tub. Rather, he loves all human
beings in God and for God’s sake as those near to him by the bond of nature and
kinship, indeed as brothers, if one considers the state of nature as created by
God. But with Christian charity he embraces only believers, who are Christ’s
members. He perceives that they are truly and properly bound to him in Christ
Jesus, their common head, redeemer, and mediator, through communion in grace,
faith, hope, baptism, calling, the Spirit, and the same spiritual life. He
therefore counts them as his brothers not so much by the condition of nature
—whose entire bond as regards spiritual communion has been abolished and made
void by sin unless it is repaired and restored by Christ’s grace—as by that
eternal and new covenant ratified by Christ’s blood. He counts them as
brothers not so much by the law of generation as by that life-giving law of
regeneration: from the same firstborn brother, they share with one another and
with him communion with the same heavenly Father.

[Marginalia: Proverbs. In a saying.]

The apostle himself indicates this distinction and these degrees of love
sanctified through the Spirit of Christ in Galatians 6: “Therefore, while we
have a suitable opportunity,” he says, “let us do good to all, but especially
to the household of faith.” He also teaches that we must in turn make a choice
even among these, assigning each person his own place and degree, so that in
helping and supporting them we preserve the order which the claims of nature
and kinship, as well as other rights of domestic or civil affinity,
friendship, and society, show and prescribe to us. Thus he says in 1 Timothy
5, “If anyone does not provide for his own, and especially for those of his
household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever”; and in the
first letter to the Thessalonians, chapter 5, “We ask you, brothers, to
acknowledge those who labor among you, preside over you in the Lord, and
admonish you, and to esteem them exceedingly dear for their work.” For just as
God’s Holy Spirit does not remove or eradicate from believers’ hearts these
natural affections of love and tender devotion toward parents, children,
kindred, and all others joined to us in any way, but only renews, sanctifies,
rightly directs, orders, and moderates them according to the prescription of
God’s word, so too he does not abolish the different degrees and every
distinction among the objects themselves. Instead, by the torch of the divine
word shining ahead he shows what must be followed in this matter: in what place,
degree, and estimation each is to be held, and in what manner, for what reason,
how far, and to what end each neighbor is to be loved. The general rule we
already touched upon above must always be observed: to us as Christians that
spiritual communion of the saints must always be more precious and dearer than
every natural kinship. This follows the most gracious example and affection of
the Lord and Savior Jesus Christ himself, an example full of all consolation
for you, which Matthew 12 indicates and attests. For when someone told him that
his mother and brothers were standing outside seeking to speak with him, he

Then, stretching out his hand toward his disciples, he said, “Behold my mother
and my brothers. For whoever does the will of my Father who is in heaven is”

“my brother and sister and mother.” Here he expressly shows that he values the
bond of the Holy Spirit, which indicates and attests itself through faith and
new obedience, above every nearness of blood and family. Had that natural
nearness existed in them alone, without faith and communion in the Spirit,
Christ Jesus would never have acknowledged even his own mother and brothers
according to the flesh among his own.

Thus far we have discussed the causes of this apostolic joy and thanksgiving,
or, if you prefer, its occasion or that twofold foundation. It now follows that
we examine and explain from the apostle’s own words the manner of that joy,
expressed and indicated by him in the following verse, which he adds: “I do not
cease to give thanks for you, making mention of you in my prayers.” As you see,
these words present no obscurity or difficulty. As for the matter itself, just
as the saving gifts divinely bestowed upon the believing Ephesians were a most
godly cause and holy foundation of his joy and thanksgiving, so he expresses
and attests it in an equally holy and godly manner: by thanksgiving and prayers
continually offered to God for their advancement and confirmation in the grace
they had received. Therefore his joy, conceived in God as the author of faith
and love, once again tends and rises toward God through prayer and is poured
back entirely upon him through thanksgiving. The apostle rightly joins these
two effects both of faith in God and of Christian charity—prayer and
thanksgiving—here and elsewhere, as may be seen particularly in Philippians 4:
“Be anxious about nothing, but in everything by prayer and supplication with
thanksgiving let your requests be made known before God”; and in 1
Thessalonians 5: “Rejoice always; pray continually, or without interruption;
give thanks in everything.” Both here and in the passage to the Philippians he
adds a third thing, joy, as the fountain and inseparable companion of both. For
in verse 4 he had prefaced his exhortation to prayer and thanksgiving with joy:
“Rejoice in the Lord always; again I say, rejoice.” A brief consideration,
then, will show that these three are joined rightly and with the best reason; I
will touch upon it in passing only so far as the present passage requires.

First, as for prayer, it is an eminent and excellent part of that spiritual
and inward worship which is given and owed immediately to God alone. It
proceeds from faith and trust in God and from a sense and acknowledgment of our
misery. We may sketch it in this somewhat rough description: it is the raising
of a believer’s heart and mind to God. Through humble and godly affection,
abasing ourselves, we approach that throne of the reverend and glorious
majesty of God, exalting him supremely above all we can conceive and
comprehend. From an apprehension of his fatherly love and mercy toward us in
Jesus Christ, from trust in his promises, and from a sense of our own misery,
frailty, and need, we flee to his grace and lay our wishes and prayers before
him. In accordance with his word and will, we ask and implore from him the
forgiveness of sins and the other things necessary for eternal life and
salvation, through and for the sake of Jesus Christ our mediator and redeemer.
We do this not only for ourselves but also for our brothers, whether generally,
specifically, or even expressly by name, and thus for the whole Christian
Church, relying both on his command and on his promise to hear.

[Marginalia: On prayers. What prayer is.]

From this sufficiently clear description, two things shine forth to us above
all in prayer, whether directed to God only mentally or also aloud: its matter
or subject and the manner of the act itself. The matter must be sought from
that rule of prayer which God has revealed to us in his word and according to
which all our prayers must be conceived, so that they may be directed and
regulated by God’s will and conformed to it in this respect. Thus we direct
and raise to him, either in mind and affection alone or also by uttering and
expressing them with the voice, those honorable and holy desires of the heart
which conform to God’s own will. “For Jehovah hears the desire of the poor or
meek,” says the psalmist in Psalm 10, and the apostle says in Romans 8, “The
Spirit himself intercedes for us with groanings that cannot be uttered.” We
desire that his name be glorified, his kingdom be raised up daily in our
hearts, and all obedience be rendered to his will; that we have faith to
depend upon his providence even in matters belonging to the present life; that
we have forgiveness of sins, his presence, and his grace strengthening us in
temptation; and finally that we obtain complete victory over all the enemies
of our salvation and that glorious consummation of his grace in us. The whole
subject of our prayers may be referred to these things.

[Marginalia: A twofold corollary. First, on the matter of prayers. Psalm 10:17.
Romans 8:26.]

As for the manner of acting or praying, in it we consider the preparation
preceding the act, which is a kind of remote cause, and then the act of praying
itself. The preparation occurs when, as we are about to approach God, we
seriously ponder who and what sort of being he is, who and what sort of people
we ourselves are, to what end we approach him, and what he requires from us in
this act. Thus, raising him to infinite sublimity, we humble and submit
ourselves correspondingly, and sincerely and without pretense compose our
hearts and affections for obedience to him.

[Marginalia: Second, on the manner of praying.]

In the act itself, again, three things may be considered: the foundation or
proximate cause of the prayers themselves; the one through whom and by whose
mediation they are made; and those for whom the prayers are conceived and
poured out. The foundation is twofold. First, it is a distinct and particular
acknowledgment and a living and serious sense of our misery, need, and special
deficiencies, necessarily joined with sorrow of heart and a certain
contrition. Second, it is faith: that is, an acknowledgment and apprehension
of God’s gratuitous mercy, favor, and benevolence in Christ Jesus, conceived
from his word and promises through the Holy Spirit of adoption; and the trust
born from it which rests upon God himself as benevolent and propitious,
truthful, constant, wise, and powerful to fulfill his promises toward us.

[Marginalia: Subdivision. Twofold foundation.]

Faithful prayer arises immediately from these two foundations. By their
benefit we are said to pour out our soul before God in prayer and to seek his
face; and the more lively and effectual the sense and apprehension of these two
things is in our hearts, the more fervently and ardently our prayer will rise
to God. This may be seen particularly in Psalms 77 and 88, Isaiah 38, and Jonah
2, among innumerable other passages of sacred Scripture selected for our use
in this matter.

[Marginalia: Note the corollary in passing.]

The one through whom we pray is Christ alone, the God-man and mediator. Far be
it from us to attempt to send our prayers and vows to God the Father without a
mediator and a propitiatory sacrifice, relying on the name and intercession of
anyone other than this his beloved Son, or resting and trusting in any other
sacrifice than that which he himself

once offered for us on the cross, never to be repeated. Finally, we pray not
only for ourselves but for all those for whom we have both God’s own command
that prayers be conceived and the example of the saints and apostles in sacred
Scripture—as we see in this very passage. We pray according as they are more
closely joined to us in God and Christ, we are more deeply obligated to them,
they have more earnestly commended themselves to our prayers, their particular
necessity and need has become known to us, or we have some other legitimate
cause and reason, under the rule of Christian charity and prudence, to be
specially concerned for the salvation of this or that person, or of these or
those people. But we do not pray for those who have departed this life. Such
prayers cannot arise from faith, since they rest upon no word of God, no
command, no promise, and indeed not even upon any approved example of canonical
Scripture. Consequently, besides being vain and ineffectual, as is abundantly
clear from orthodox teaching on the state of souls after their departure from
this body and life, they must be abominable and displeasing to God, since they
proceed from distrust and stubborn rebellion against God’s will, and so are
imputed by him as sin to their authors. We see that what the prophet prays
against his own and God’s enemies in Psalm 109 deservedly falls upon them in
this respect: “When he comes to judgment, let him go out condemned, and let his
prayer be counted as sin to him.” Let this suffice concerning prayer itself.

[Marginalia: Those for whom we pray, or the object of our prayers. A corollary

As for thanksgiving, it is usually joined with prayers also in actual use and
practice, just as its nature is akin to prayer. I touch briefly on only these
two things in it. First, it requires a faithful and firm remembrance and
serious meditation upon and acknowledgment of God’s blessings bestowed upon us
through Christ, especially our redemption. A grateful person must be mindful,
just as, conversely, one who forgets and is unmindful of benefits received
becomes ungrateful both toward God and toward other people. Second, in order to
attest this gratitude of ours, we must consecrate and dedicate ourselves and
all that is ours to God from the heart: that is, devote and pledge ourselves
wholly to his name, honor, and lawful worship, with the sincere zeal and
purpose of heart to live for and serve God. We are to consecrate body, soul,
affections, possessions, and actions to God throughout our life, so that in
everything we strive to glorify him according not to our own will but to his
will revealed to us in his word. We first undertook this solemn vow in baptism,
subsequently renewed it from time to time in the celebration of the Lord’s
Supper, and daily bound ourselves more closely to fulfill it. This part of
gratitude or thanksgiving, which must chiefly be observed, was formerly
foreshadowed in the legal sacrifices. First they were led to the altar and set
before God as though present; then they were slain for him and offered upon his
altar. So too, by this dedication and consecration of ourselves to God’s honor,
worship, and obedience, we ought to present and offer ourselves as eucharistic
sacrifices for benefits received, confessing, acknowledging, and celebrating
in voice and life that he alone is the author and giver of every grace, of
life, and of salvation.

[Marginalia: On thanksgiving. A maxim to be noted. Confirmation.]

From this explanation of both prayer and thanksgiving, their connection is
now evident. For since true prayers arise from faith and trust in God, as has
been said, no necessity that drives us to pray is ever so great that material
for thanksgiving is not meanwhile at hand, since at every moment we can
perceive and experience God’s grace and mercy toward us. Conversely, no
material for thanksgiving given in this life is so abundant that it releases
or exempts us from every need for prayer. For as long as we remain in this
frail condition, engaged in this Christian struggle and conflict, and still
stained with many remnants of sin, we always need daily pardon for offenses,
fresh accessions of strengthening grace and new powers from time to time, and
God’s hand continually supporting, directing, surrounding, fortifying, and
protecting us below and above until we are brought into the very harbor of
salvation. Hence in the examples of the godly and faithful of old set before us
in sacred Scripture, we see thanksgivings almost always intermingled with
prayers, and prayers in turn with thanksgivings and doxologies. This will be
clear to anyone who opens and attentively reads even the single volume of the
Psalms.

[Marginalia: Corollary 1, on the connection of prayer and thanksgiving.]

Again, as for joy, it is no wonder that the apostle almost always joins it with
these two parts of divine worship, because these three mutually foster,
increase, and preserve one another and follow one another as in a most
delightful circle. In the works of nature, the sun’s power and attraction draw
vapor from the moistened earth; the vapor gathers into a cloud and condenses
into rain; this, formed again into a shower, falls drop by drop upon the earth;
and the earth, watered once more, supplies material for the next vapor, cloud,
and rain—and so the same sequence continues. It is the same here in this
gracious work. By the power of the Holy Spirit, whom that Sun of righteousness
sends down into our hearts as a saving ray of his countenance, prayers rise
and are stirred up with unutterable groanings. By them our very hearts are
brought, as it were, nearer to God’s face and presence and perceive his
life-giving presence in Jesus Christ. Like wax melted and softened before his
face, they are gradually watered by that peace and joy which surpass all
understanding; amid tears and groans and in the midst of the fervor of their
desire, they are filled and occupied, indeed wholly absorbed and carried out of
themselves, by that most tranquil refreshment of peace and gladness and by
inexpressible sweetness. Then this saving shower of joy and peace must in turn
burst forth into thanksgiving, with the same Spirit of God acting within and
loosening our souls—and indeed our very mouths and tongues—for God’s praise and
glory. Thus, to state it briefly, we see that true and ardent prayers give rise
to a greater sense of the divine presence in our souls and unite us more
closely with God; from this come peace and joy in the Holy Spirit; and from
this, as from a fountain, thanksgiving springs forth. In the very act of
giving God thanks, that same joy from God’s presence is nourished, as is the
zeal and exercise of prayer through experience of that most desirable effect.
I know that the natural person can neither grasp nor perceive anything here.
But the person in whom that Holy Spirit of Christ dwells and who is led by him,
as the apostle says in Romans 8, knows not only that all these things are most
true, but that their truth rises above everything that can be spoken or
expressed by us or by anyone else.

from the constancy, or continuity, of both these actions. The apostle indicates

For how, someone may ask, can the apostle truthfully assert here that he does

We answer briefly as follows. First, passages of this kind must be adapted to
the lot and condition of humanity: we must devote ourselves to such pursuits
as far as the weakness of our nature allows. That continuity must be
understood not so much of an act which never ceases or is never interrupted

imagine—as of never abandoning the zeal and exercise of offering prayers
daily. This may be observed from the ordinary usage of speech among everyone.
For example, someone is said never to cease from gambling, fornication,
drinking, play, or other things of this kind. This does not mean that he always
perseveres in the act itself without interruption, but that he has contracted
such a habit that whenever opportunity is given, or every day, he repeatedly
performs the same acts, so that he is said to do nothing else. Conversely, a
person is said to pray without ceasing when he lets no day pass without
devoting some time to this holy exercise. He exercises himself daily in prayer
and offers this as a continual sacrifice to God as often each day as the
duties and cares of his particular calling allow—like the sacrifice formerly
customarily offered morning and evening. Nor does he neglect any opportunity
to call upon God through Jesus Christ in prosperity as well as adversity and
in every lot and condition of life. This is how we must understand what Luke 2
relates about the most holy widow Anna: that she did not depart from the temple
but worshiped God with fastings and supplications night and day. It refers to
this daily zeal and exercise of prayer in which she exercised herself by night
as well as by day. The same meaning of these sayings, and thus of this
“unceasingness,” so to speak, can be gathered from the beginning of chapter 18
of the same Gospel. There the Lord is said to have set before his disciples,
to the end that they should always pray, the parable of the widow who wearied
and battered an atheistic and unjust judge with continual demands. The words
immediately following explain for us what “always to pray” means. The Holy
Spirit says “not to lose heart”: that is, not to grow sluggish, fail, become
discouraged, or ever cast away the practice of prayer, even if one is not
heard at once, but to persist in it steadfastly throughout one’s whole life.
In a word, we are to reckon neither sleep nor daily food more necessary for
sustaining this frail bodily life than that continual fuel of prayer and
thanksgiving is for nurturing, advancing, and preserving the spiritual life
of the soul within us.

[Marginalia: First part of the answer. Confirmation of the answer. Luke 2:36.
Luke 18:1.]

Second, as may be gathered from the nature of that inward worship described
above, when we said that to pray is to raise the mind and affection to God,
this continuity of prayer must be understood not so much of the outward act as
of the inward affection. For in this sense it may be observed in every place
and time, even while performing the outward works belonging to each person’s
calling.

[Marginalia: Part 2 of the answer.]

Therefore the apostle does not want us to despise every other care, every
lawful calling, or the necessary duties and works of a useful and honorable
calling in order to devote ourselves to prayer alone. Rather, he wants us,
even while working, to remain with God in affection and soul; even when we put
our hands to our work, meanwhile to raise our minds to Christ. We should
remember that we are citizens and inhabitants of heaven and should not seek
and attend only to earthly things or remain wholly fixed and fastened upon
them. Instead, by the gaze of the mind and the affection of the heart we should
always advance farther, even to the place Christ has entered and to which he
has brought and raised our nature. Meanwhile we are to think about God,
Christ’s glory, and that life of ours hidden in Christ; in a word, as far as
possible we are continually to meditate upon those eternal things yet to come
and to be revealed at last in their own time. This cannot be done without that
inward prayer and thanksgiving of a mind turned toward God in this way. So far
is this from distracting us from the ordinary activities and duties of our
calling that, on the contrary, from it we obtain these two excellent benefits
or fruits, more precious than any price. First comes eagerness and readiness
of soul to fulfill our calling and both to endure and soothe its labors with
this oil, as it were, which relieves pain and gives comfort and is by far the
most precious of all. Then comes God’s blessing upon us and our labors, making
them prosper by his efficacy and sanctifying them by God’s own presence, so
that we may labor with a good conscience in that manner of life in which God
has willed to place us. For this presence of God, which is fostered and
increased in us by that secret meditation and inward prayer, alone sustains us
in our otherwise laborious work and under our otherwise burdensome load, and
also sanctifies both us and our works.

[Marginalia: Conclusion. Confirmation of this answer.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 187 | Caput I p. 143 (from Prælectio XXXV opening)]

And so we have explained at length both members of the first part of this
passage. It remains now for us, according to our custom and practice, to draw
some observations from the same doctrine contained in these two verses.

First, there is the distinction between faith and charity drawn from their
objects: faith is directed toward Jesus Christ proclaimed in the gospel,
whereas charity is directed toward the saints

[Marginalia: Lecture XXXV. Transition to doctrinal observations. Observation
1.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 188 | Caput I p. 144]

all of whom are members of Christ. This stands against the error of those who so
clumsily confuse the two that they maintain charity to be the form of faith;
we have clearly exposed and rejected their absurdity above.

Second, we may also observe the true nature of charity: we do not possess it
by nature, nor is the affection natural, since it flows from faith in Jesus
Christ. It follows that true charity exists only in those in whom faith is
also found. Consequently, however much unspiritual and unregenerate people
may boast of their friendship with one another, they are nevertheless
destitute of true charity. Indeed, even those celebrated pairs of friends sung
by poets and historians did not possess so much as a drop or spark of charity,
since they loved one another neither in God nor for his sake, but from the
impulse of corrupt, blind, and swollen flesh.

[Marginalia: Observation 2.]

3. Those two duties of a regenerate Christian must be sought from the two
chief parts of the divine word, namely the law and the gospel. Faith relates
to the gospel, while charity relates to the law. The gospel is rightly called
the word of faith, and the law the word of works, beneath which the apostle
places this form, covenant, and compact in Romans 10: “The one who performs
these things shall live by them.” Charity, however, is the fulfillment of the
law. Let no one be troubled that it may seem to follow from this that the law
is prior to the gospel. Faith is prior to charity, since charity flows from
faith, as we observed above from the apostle’s own order. Consequently, the
gospel, which is the word of faith, will precede the law, which is the word
that demands perfect charity. Yet this seems to conflict with the natural
order of these parts of Holy Scripture.

[Marginalia: Observation 3. Romans 10:5. First objection.]

We may easily remove this difficulty by making the following distinction,
based upon the law’s twofold use, which it exercises even upon the elect,
though upon them in different states and conditions. First, it has one use in
those not yet regenerate, preparing them to receive Christ’s grace. It does
this through a knowledge of their sin and misery arising from the law, which
rouses and terrifies the sinner’s conscience. Thus, after reducing the sinner
to the agonies of death, it impels and urges him to seek outside himself—that
is, in the gospel—the remedy for his evil. In this sense the law is prior to
the gospel. It then has another use in the person who has already embraced
the gospel through faith and from faith has received the Spirit of
sanctification. The law directs him in that new obedience as a rule, and the
regenerate person orders and fashions by it, as by a lamp, all the steps of
his life in the works of justice and charity to be performed by Christ’s
Spirit according to the law’s prescription. The psalmist especially urges
this use of the law in Psalm 119 when he declares God’s law to be a lamp to
his feet and a light to his steps. In this sense the law is posterior to the
gospel. In brief, then, the law precedes the gospel in the order of nature if
we consider a human being naturally holy and upright, because in that respect
the law concerns the covenant of works or natural covenant, which is prior to
the gospel’s gracious covenant. It also precedes in the order of our benefit
and of the better method of teaching if we consider a human being in the
state of sin, since it is an instrument preparing us to seek and embrace
Christ’s grace in the gospel. Yet in the actual practice and performance of
the things required by the law, insofar as it regards a person who is now
faithful and regenerate, the law is posterior to the gospel, just as
sanctification and new obedience are posterior to faith. Hence it follows
that the gospel is so far from abolishing the law that, on the contrary, it
especially establishes it; see Romans 3:31.

[Marginalia: Answer. Note the law’s twofold use. 1. 2. Psalm 119:105.
Conclusion of the distinction.]

But someone may still press the objection that those two members expressed
by the apostle do not adequately fill out and comprehend the Christian’s
duty, since a third is still required, distinct from both and directed toward
ourselves, just as faith regards God and charity our neighbor. Repentance has
therefore been omitted. By repentance we ourselves are converted to God and
renewed into Christ’s image through the communion we have both with his death
and cross and with his resurrection and life, crucifying and mortifying the
old man in us and bringing the new man to life.

[Marginalia: Second objection.]

We answer briefly that this third member may readily be brought under either
of the other two and included beneath either one, whether as the proximate
effect of faith or the foundation of brotherly charity. For, as we said above,
love of God, which arises from faith, is immediately followed by a desire to
please and obey God, then by an earnest flight from and loathing of what is
contrary to his will—that is, sin—and finally by sorrow over the defect which
we still feel in ourselves and the sins hitherto committed against God. This
is that very repentance and conversion to God of which we speak. Again, true
charity can have no place in our nature unless it has been renewed and
cleansed to some extent through repentance. By nature everyone is a lover of
self, not a lover of humanity or of the brethren; through sin people are not
only separated from God but also torn and wrenched away from one another.
Renewal is therefore necessary in order that this native and perverse
self-love may be transformed into a holy and spiritual love of the brethren.
That renewal is the work of repentance, and brotherly charity therefore
necessarily presupposes it.

[Marginalia: Confirmation. 1. 2.]

If someone asks to which of these members repentance should preferably be
referred, faith or charity, since, as we have said, it touches both through so
close an affinity, we answer that it matters little for removing this
difficulty under which member one includes it. Nevertheless, we prefer to
refer it to faith, with whose nature we find repentance so closely
interwoven that one can scarcely be considered or explained without the
other, and we can scarcely decide which to assign the prior place in order.
Both, however, can be distinguished much more clearly from charity, which
regards one’s neighbor. Therefore, whenever faith is distinguished by contrast
from charity, repentance is referred to faith and comprehended under its
name.

5. From that constancy in prayer and thanksgiving even for the good and
salvation of his brothers, let us observe how ardent a zeal for this matter
ought to exist in us even on our own behalf. For if Paul never ceased praying
to God and giving him thanks for other believers whom he had converted and
instructed in the Christian faith, what, I ask, will be fitting for us in the
work of advancing our own salvation and faith except an urgent and continual
practice of prayer and thanksgiving to God? We know that this practice
flourished more strongly in godly souls the greater the measure of the Holy
Spirit with which it is evident God had endowed them, and the more Christ’s
image had been restored in them. This is manifest from the single example of
David, who with precisely the same sense and disposition—though in words
different from our apostle’s—professes that at one time he called upon and
praised God’s name three times daily, and at another seven times. This was
certainly with good reason, if one weighs carefully what has already been
said above about so holy and saving a practice. In this way our communion with
God himself and Jesus Christ, and our sense of the divine presence in our
hearts, are preserved and fostered as though by the most effective fuel. In
this way new strength for faith, hope, and patience is drawn down and
attracted from heaven; new firmness for perseverance and new growth in every
spiritual gift and blessing are supplied; and above all there is growth in
that saving confidence, peace of conscience, and joy through the Holy Spirit,
to be recounted even amid tears and groanings. The apostle
explicitly teaches in Romans 14 that the kingdom of God in this life consists
in these things.

6. From this we also have an excellent mark of true zeal for God’s glory and
for the advancement of Christ’s kingdom, if we compare this holy affection
shining in our apostle with the condition in which he found himself when he
wrote this letter to the Ephesians. He was a prisoner of Jesus Christ,
detained in chains at Rome for the testimony of the gospel, as we noted above
in the preface. Yet when he heard of the believers’ constancy, indeed their
progress, in faith and charity, and heard of the growth of Jesus Christ’s
kingdom, he never lacked material for joy and congratulation, an occasion for
giving thanks to God and blessing his name—with which he began this
letter—and a reason for continually celebrating his praises.

[Marginalia: Observation 6.]

This is by no means the least, but rather the surest, testimony of true and
sincere zeal for God’s glory: though a person is himself afflicted, pressed,
and burdened by various crosses, he can nevertheless rejoice from the heart
over the advancement of the kingdom of Christ his Lord and the peace and
prosperous condition of the Church, and can sincerely congratulate his
brothers upon their happy lot. Conversely, though a person may himself
flourish and thrive in wealth, honor, favor, and outward peace, when he hears
of the affliction of his faithful brothers, the calamity, loss, or persecution
of God’s Church, some obstruction to the course of the gospel and growth of
God’s kingdom, or any danger threatening his brothers, he should grieve and
groan from the heart with Daniel, Mordecai, Esther, and other truly godly
people of this kind. With a humble and downcast heart he should pour out
ardent prayers before his God for the peace and protection of Christ’s Church,
expecting from this the matter and occasion of his own comfort and joy, with
the godly speakers introduced in Psalm 137: “If I forget you, O Jerusalem, let
my right hand forget itself; let my tongue cling to my palate if I do not
remember you, if I do not exalt Jerusalem above the crown of my joy.”

7. From this we may also see the character of true charity according to the
lucid description of it which we have from the apostle in 1 Corinthians 13.
So far is charity from envying anyone God’s gifts and benefits that, on the
contrary, it heartily congratulates everyone upon these gifts and their
increase. Indeed, it desires that the same grace which, by God’s will, it has
felt effectual for salvation in itself might be able to flow into all and
reach everyone.

[Marginalia: Observation 7.]

Therefore, from this example of our apostle, not only must we guard against
all envy, which everyone easily sees to be diametrically opposed to charity;
we must also rejoice sincerely over God’s gifts bestowed upon anyone, indeed
through anyone’s ministry, and congratulate our brothers. I think this must be
urged and remembered all the more because such is the self-love of our nature
that it intrudes even into duties which seem to proceed from charity and to
regard only the benefit of one’s neighbor—preaching the gospel, for example,
helping God’s Church by word, deed, or writing, and other things of this kind.
Many people would find it welcome and pleasant if something benefiting the
Church were done through their labor, if God employed them as servants of his
work, and if the task before them prospered through their ministry, care, and
labor. Yet they are ready to envy others an equal or greater success in the
same work when God has willed to prefer those others to them in this respect.
Why is this, unless from the remnants of that self-love which they have not
yet entirely denied? They still hunt after some petty glory, praise, and
reputation for themselves from God’s work rightly and successfully
administered. Consequently, their zeal or charity is not sincere on every
side.

[Marginalia: Corollary. Note the character of human self-love.]

Our apostle’s affection was certainly far different. He rejoiced exceedingly
that the kingdom of Jesus Christ was advanced, churches planted, built up,
and watered, and his God’s work increased, not only by his own labor and
ministry but also by the labors of any others called by God, even when they
were people otherwise hostile to him and afflicted by secret envy and rivalry
toward him. He left this clearly attested in Philippians 1: “Some indeed
proclaim Christ through envy and contention, not sincerely, because they
think they are adding affliction to my chains; but others preach Christ from
goodwill and charity, knowing that I have been appointed for the defense of
the gospel. What then? Nevertheless, in every way, whether in pretense or in
sincerity, Christ is proclaimed; and in this I rejoice, and I will continue
to rejoice.”

prosperous; if the year’s yield is welcome and abundant; if their enemies have
been supplanted, whether justly or unjustly; if their foes have been defeated
and cast down from their hope; if they possess favor with a prince, authority
among the people, and distinction of name and reputation among foreigners; if

strength, talent, beauty, and wealth they are vigorous and flourishing; or,

Second, as to the form and manner of congratulation, those whose minds are

are the work's exegetical method and preface, Paul's salutation, the form and
matter of the opening doxology, spiritual blessings in the heavenly places,
election in Christ, and the relation of predestination, adoption, Christ's
mediation, divine good pleasure, and the praise of glorious grace. Lecture VII
then derives six observations on assurance, foreseen faith and works, holiness
as the ordained means to salvation, the relation of faith and love, God's sole
causality in election, and the doxological end of the doctrine.
Lecture VIII then expounds redemption through Christ's blood, its price and
twofold liberation, union with Christ, forgiveness as redemption's effect,
the riches of divine grace, the typology of the two goats in Leviticus 16,
and the ethical force of having been bought with so great a price.
Lecture IX opens the application of these benefits through effectual calling,
distinguishes its external and internal aspects, and expounds the abundance of
spiritual wisdom and understanding in Ephesians 1:8. It distinguishes wisdom
from practical prudence by their objects, relates both to saving illumination,
and traces the gift through Christ to the riches of the Father's grace.
Lecture X then expounds the revealed mystery of God's will in Ephesians
1:9–10, the two senses of recapitulation, humanity's twofold dispersion from
God and neighbor, and three ancient readings of the restoration and gathering

the things in heaven and on earth, the debated relation of angels to that
gathering, Christ as its center, and the fullness and dispensation of the
times. Its concluding observations treat effectual calling, faith as a
spiritual gift, the sufficiency and depth of Scripture, wisdom joined to
Christian practice, the unity of the Church militant and triumphant under
Christ alone, and patient hope for the final consummation.
Lecture XII then begins the special application of Ephesians 1:11–12 to
believing Jews. It expounds their being allotted an inheritance in Christ,
the typology of Israel’s allotted land, the relation of inheritance to
adoption, and eternal predestination as the source of effectual calling. It
then examines God as the one who works all things according to the counsel of
his will, distinguishes divine counsel from creaturely deliberation, and
weighs restricted and providential readings of “all things” while denying
that divine providence makes God the author of sin.
Lecture XIII completes the exposition of Ephesians 1:12 by distinguishing the
inherent glory of God from creaturely praise and explaining the passive and
active senses in which believers exist for the praise of that glory. It then
interprets the Jews’ prior hope in Christ as a priority of time rather than
merit, grounds the gospel’s historical priority to Israel in God’s covenant
promises, and draws applications concerning personal appropriation of the
gospel, gratuitous election, submission to providence, and holy living as the

Lecture XIV then applies Ephesians 1:13–14 to believing Gentiles, especially
the Ephesians, as equal heirs with believing Jews. It expounds the word of
truth as the saving gospel, distinguishes outward hearing from the Spirit’s
inward opening of the heart, and argues for effectual calling from gospel
hearing. It then treats the Holy Spirit’s sealing as the confirmation of faith
through sanctification, develops the analogy of a sealed legal instrument,
and explains “the Spirit of promise” both as the promised Spirit and as the
one who seals the promise of adoption and inheritance in believers.
Lecture XV develops the Spirit as the earnest of the inheritance, distinguishes
an earnest from a returned pledge, and explains sanctification begun here as
the first portion of the life perfected in glory. It distinguishes redemption
already accomplished and applied from its final consummation, treats divine
glory as the rule of Christian desire, and argues that the Spirit’s sealing
and indwelling ground assurance without denying believers’ continuing
struggles with doubt. It concludes the chapter’s first major section with a
four-stage synopsis of election, redemption accomplished, saving application,
and the full possession of the eternal inheritance.
Lecture XVI begins the promised methodical treatment of eternal
predestination or election by asking whether the doctrine should be taught
publicly in churches as well as in schools. It answers five historical
objections associated with the controversy in Augustine’s age, argues that
God decrees both saving ends and their appointed means, and defends the
doctrine’s scriptural necessity and pastoral usefulness. Its stated fruits
include assurance, humility, defense of grace and Christ’s deity, love of God
and neighbor, holiness, and patience under the cross. It concludes that
reprobation cannot be separated from election, is itself taught in Scripture,
and magnifies the gratuitous mercy shown to the elect.
Lecture XVII then prefaces the treatment itself with six cautions governing
speech about God’s eternal counsel: God must be known from God, divine justice
and wisdom remain axiomatic when their reasons are hidden, all times are
present to God, providence orders contingent events without destroying their
native liberty, every good act requires divine efficacy, and God wisely uses
evil acts without sharing their fault. It distinguishes broad, narrow, and
middle senses of predestination, adopts the middle sense encompassing both
election and reprobation, and defines predestination as God’s eternal decree
concerning the final state of rational creatures. It closes by identifying
creation and the permitted fall as means common to the execution of both
parts, while denying that God is the culpable cause of Adam’s defection.
Lecture XVIII descends from predestination in general to election. It surveys
the scriptural senses of election, defines the eternal decree concerning the
elect, and distinguishes within the simple divine counsel a prior act
concerning the saving end from a subsequent act concerning its appointed
means. It rejects foreseen faith as the meaning of divine foreknowledge,
compares the two acts in their causes and objects, and then treats Christ both
as the coeternal Son who elects and as the elect God-man, Mediator, head, bond,
and foundation in whom the rest are chosen. Its concluding argument uses the
gratuitous election of Christ’s human nature as a mirror of believers’
gratuitous election and maintains that Christ’s being sent for the salvation
of the elect magnifies divine mercy without making those saved more excellent
than their Savior.
Lecture XIX then considers the elect as united to Christ, first treating the
election and confirmation of the blessed angels in Christ as their head and
then returning to the election of saved humanity. It identifies the Trinity
as the principal efficient cause and God’s free good pleasure as the sole
impelling cause, rejecting human free choice, foreseen works, foreseen faith,
and Christ’s mediatorial merit as antecedent causes of the decree. It uses the
gratuitous election of Christ’s humanity, with Augustine, as an argument for
the gratuitous election of his members. It concludes by distinguishing God’s
glory as election’s supreme final cause from salvation, holiness, adoption,
and glory as subordinate ends or stages in the decree’s execution.
Lecture XX then identifies election as eternal, free and gratuitous,
immutable, and knowable only from its effects. It treats the temporal promise,
exhibition, application, and consummation of the Mediator, distinguishes the
universal gospel command from the promise made to believers, and answers the
charge that the outward call conflicts with God’s decree. It then relates
election to faith, effectual calling, justification, adoption, sanctification,
perseverance, and glorification. Its closing corollary begins the treatment of
assurance from election’s effects, locating the Spirit’s testimony in the
gospel’s practical application and distinguishing it from carnal presumption
by prayer, filial reverence, obedience, and the accompanying graces.
Lecture XXI develops those accompanying graces by first distinguishing gifts
shared with the reprobate—learning, prophecy, civil virtue, restraint, and a
temporary joyful taste of the word—from the Spirit’s saving indwelling in the
elect. It unfolds that indwelling through assurance of reconciliation,
regeneration, spiritual government, consolation, and strength for difficult
duties and sufferings. It then reduces the signs of election to internal and
external forms: godly sorrow over past sin, conflict with present corruption,
care against future sin, hunger for Christ, love for his members and coming,
and whole-life obedience extending to every command. The lecture closes with
pastoral counsel for those who find only the smallest beginnings of grace or
no present assurance, directing them to persistent repentance, prayer, the
word, and hope rather than despair.
Lecture XXII establishes the fixed number of the elect, many when considered
in themselves but few in comparison with the reprobate, and concludes that
living faith, the saving promises, Christ’s benefits, membership in the
invisible Church, and final perseverance belong to the elect alone. It then
turns from election to reprobation, defends the term and doctrine from
Scripture, defines its human and angelic objects, and distinguishes passing
over from the decree to condemn for sin. The lecture denies that reprobation
is the proper cause of sin or condemnation and concludes by treating the
manifestation of divine justice as its supreme end and the admonition,
humility, gratitude, and consolation of the elect as its subordinate end.
Lecture XXIII distinguishes reprobation's effects from its wider consequences,
then divides divine desertion into an initial withholding of confirming grace
and a punitive deprivation of saving grace after sin. It treats the decree's
freedom, immutability, subjects, and unequal number; compares reprobation with
election in causes, forms, means, effects, and ends; and denies that God
produces the sins which the decree permits and orders. Its three corollaries
draw practical uses against fatalistic unbelief, warn hearers not to harden
under repeated gospel preaching, and prescribe restraint in judging anyone's
final state. The lecture closes by applying those rules to people outside the
visible Church, deaths clouded by illness, and ancestors who lived under the
papacy, affirming that Christ preserved living members even amid
Antichristian corruption.
Lecture XXIV opens the answer to objections against predestination by dividing
them into four classes. It treats the first three objections in the class
attacking the form of the decree: that an absolute decree makes destruction
the end of creation, that biblical statements about God's will to save all
people entail universal election, and that the gospel command to believe
entails an effectual redemption of every hearer. Its answers distinguish two
senses of “absolute,” several senses of “all,” God's revealed call from his
hidden counsel, and a promise from its particular application. The lecture
also develops its final answer through the conditional threat to Nineveh,
different kinds of divine command, and the responsibility of those who reject
the gospel, before closing the third objection immediately prior to Lecture
XXV's fourth objection.
Lecture XXV completes the first class of objections. It answers the claim that
a universal election would be a greater good by distinguishing simple and
comparative goods, the supreme end from subordinate means, and extension from
intension. It then reconciles the decree's immutability with creaturely
freedom, contingent secondary causes, and the necessary use of divinely
appointed means. Turning to the second class, the lecture rejects foreseen
faith, foreseen works, and Christ's merit as antecedent or impelling causes of
election. Its scriptural answers distinguish election in Christ from present
union by faith, eternal favor from actual acceptance in justification, the
signs and effects of election from its cause, and the meritorious cause of
justification from the antecedent cause of God's decree.
Lecture XXVI completes the second class of objections by defending the
personal and eternal sense of Romans 9 while distinguishing the typological
destinies of Jacob and Esau's peoples from the destinies of every individual
within them. It then rejects sin, unbelief, and divine hatred as antecedent or
impelling causes of reprobation. Its answers distinguish the evil of guilt
from penal evil, a producing cause from a permissive and ordering principle,
temporal destruction from the eternal decree, and the execution of
reprobation from its cause. An extended examination of Hosea 13:9 compares
the Vulgate, Hebrew, Targum, Septuagint, and early modern interpretations. The
lecture closes by explaining that condemnation necessarily presupposes sin
even though sin does not move God to make the decree, interpreting God's
hatred of Esau as rejection rather than passion, and briefly transitioning
to the third class of objections before Lecture XXVII.
Lecture XXVII answers the principal objection from the means appointed to
reprobation's execution: that God's willing the just condemnation of sinners
would make him will, decree, or author sin. It distinguishes three modes of
the divine will or providence—good pleasure, sustaining dispensation, and
permission—and subdivides the last into permission, the presentation of
occasions, and the ordering of evil works to good ends. It explains God's
ordinary concurrence with the creature's being and action while locating the
action's defect in the creature, then distinguishes God's approving will from
his permissive, directing, and ordering will. Its final analysis considers
sin concretely in its two relations, to God as permitting and ordering it and
to the creature as committing it, and materially, formally, and concretely,
ending before Lecture XXVIII turns to the immutable certainty of election and
reprobation.
Lecture XXVIII begins the fourth and final class of objections against
predestination, those directed against the immutable certainty of election
and reprobation. It answers arguments from renewed biblical “election,” the
book of life, Paul's wishes and self-discipline, Peter's command to confirm
one's calling, and Judas's apostolic election. It then begins the principal
objection that the elect can lose justifying faith and righteousness,
distinguishing true faith from outward profession, temporary belief,
historical knowledge, and a superficial taste of heavenly gifts. Its detailed
answers treat Ezekiel 18, the rocky soil, the vine and branches, love growing
cold, falling from grace in Galatians, shipwreck and departure from faith,
Hebrews 6, and 2 Peter 2, concluding that the elect may grievously wound faith
and conscience but neither wholly abandon faith nor finally fall from
Christ's saving grace.
Lecture XXIX completes the examples alleged for the loss of justifying faith.
It rejects Saul, Simon Magus, and Judas as possessors of saving faith,
distinguishes David's and Peter's grievous wounds from a total or final fall,
and compares obscured faith to living roots in winter and a stunned soldier
who retains his weapons. Its longest section argues cautiously from 2 Samuel
7, Solomon's names, prophetic office, typology, and Ecclesiastes that Solomon
repented, while explaining why the historical narrative leaves that repentance
unstated. The lecture then answers arguments from the alleged loss of every
habit, the later wickedness of baptized covenant children, the existence of
heretics, and the continued need for warnings and exhortations. It ends before
Lecture XXX introduces Bellarmine's fifth argument from uncertainty and
despair. It answers that assurance of final perseverance rests not on natural
prediction of contingent events but on gospel promises applied and sealed by
the Spirit, and argues that this assurance produces filial vigilance rather
than carnal security. Boyd then adds three further arguments: from Adam's loss
of original righteousness, from a believer becoming a member of a prostitute,
and from the alleged incompatibility of sin and grace. His replies distinguish
Adam's natural state from the regenerate person's union with Christ, apparent
members from living ones, healthy members from wounded ones, reigning sin from
resisted sin, and defeat in a particular battle from death in the whole war.

Lecture XXXI catalogues seven earlier errors and then examines at greater
length an Ubiquitarian synthesis of universal conditional election,
redemption, calling, and sufficient grace. Boyd argues that this synthesis
makes the divine will idle and dependent on creaturely choice, reverses the
order of causes and foreknowledge, confuses the sufficiency of Christ's merit

calling of the Gentiles and the experience of peoples without the gospel. He
answers appeals to natural revelation and patristic universal language,
rejects mutable election and a grace flexible toward either belief or
unbelief, and closes by distinguishing merely sufficient grace from the
effectual, renewing, cooperating, confirming, and persevering grace described
by Augustine. Lecture XXXII answers the three announced scriptural objections
from Isaiah 5, Matthew 23:37, and Revelation 3:20 by distinguishing external
means from inward effectual grace, Christ's ministerial will of the sign from
his divine will of good pleasure, and his effectual knocking in the elect from
his judicial testimony toward others. It then identifies two further errors,
compares the modern position with the Pelagians refuted by Augustine and
Prosper, recapitulates the entire predestination commonplace in eleven stages,
and closes that long digression with thanksgiving, prayer, and `AMEN.` Lecture
XXXIII resumes the apostolic context at Ephesians 1:15, recalls the two-part
division of the chapter, and analyzes Paul’s thanksgiving for the Ephesians’
faith in Christ and love toward all the saints. It explains faith as the
apprehension of God’s fatherly love in Christ, from which confidence in God
and reciprocal love for God arise; then it traces love of neighbor through
that reciprocal love and compares faith and love to the two hands of a person
climbing Jacob’s ladder. Lecture XXXIV then takes up the object and ordered
degrees of Christian love, distinguishing love owed to all human beings from
the special communion of the saints. It proceeds from the causes of Paul’s
thanksgiving to its manner, defines prayer and its matter, foundations,
mediator, and beneficiaries, explains thanksgiving as grateful remembrance
and self-dedication, relates prayer and thanksgiving to spiritual joy, and
closes by interpreting prayer without ceasing as daily constancy and an inward
orientation toward God that sanctifies rather than displaces one’s lawful
calling. Lecture XXXV draws eight observations from that exposition. It
distinguishes faith from charity by their objects, treats charity as a
supernatural fruit of faith, orders law and gospel according to the law’s two
uses, and places repentance under faith while showing why brotherly charity
presupposes it. It then applies Paul’s example to pastoral prayer, personal
devotion, zeal for the Church amid affliction, freedom from ministerial envy,
and the contrast between worldly congratulations and spiritual thanksgiving,
closing with a Trinitarian doxology and `AMEN.`
