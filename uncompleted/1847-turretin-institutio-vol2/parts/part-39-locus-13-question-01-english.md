LOCUS THIRTEEN

THE PERSON AND STATE OF CHRIST

QUESTION I.

*Has the promised Messiah already come? We affirm against the Jews.*

I. Since Christ is the Mediator of the New Testament and the cause and source
of all the benefits which flow to us from the covenant of grace, the doctrine
of the covenant can never be rightly understood unless Christ is known. Now
two things in him must especially be considered: his *Person* and his
*Office*. We must first speak of his Person before proceeding to his Office.

[Marginal heading: The first question concerns the Messiah’s advent.]

II. To make our inquiry into Christ’s Person easier, two questions must be
premised. The first concerns the *advent of the Messiah in the thesis*, from
which it will be established that the Messiah who was to come has already
come. The second concerns his *Person in the hypothesis*, or who he is, so
that we may know him to be none other than Jesus of Nazareth, Mary’s son.

[Marginal heading: He was promised from the beginning but was not sent
immediately, for two reasons:]

III. We presuppose here what is agreed among all. First, the Messiah was
promised immediately after the fall, from the very beginning of the world, as
the sole means of salvation. Second, although he was promised from the
beginning, he was not to be sent immediately but only after a long interval of
time. In the highest wisdom of this dispensation, many reasons can be given,
whether we consider Christ himself or human beings.

[Marginal heading: 1. with respect to Christ;]

*With respect to Christ*: first, this served his dignity, so that before he
came into the world various heralds would announce his advent as that of the
supreme Prince. “The greater the Judge who was coming, the longer the line of
heralds which preceded him,” says Augustine (*Tractate 31 on John*). Thus faith
in him would be prepared beforehand and the way prepared not only by various
types but also by the harmonious predictions of the prophets. Second, the
long expectation of so great a work had to make this benefit more highly
esteemed among human beings, so that the birth which was to surpass all
miracles and every measure of understanding might produce in us a faith all
the more steadfast as its proclamation had gone before it with greater
antiquity and fullness, as Leo says in *Sermon 3 on the Nativity of Christ*.

[Marginal heading: 2. with respect to human beings.]

IV. *With respect to human beings*, the magnitude of the evil required this:
consciences had to be convicted of the multitude and gravity of sins, the
impotence of nature, and the uselessness of every human means that could be
devised, so that the necessity of a remedy would appear and the sick person,
having recognized his infirmity, would cry out to the Physician and seek the
help of grace,

[Printed p. 242 | Internet Archive PDF 270 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

as Augustine says. God left man to a long experience of himself to see whether
he who had fallen by his own wickedness could raise himself by his own powers.
Therefore, during that whole time of his ἀνοχῆς, *forbearance*, God allowed sin
to grow and in a sense abound, so that the abundance of grace might thereby
become more illustrious. Yet he did not wish this advent to be deferred to the
end of the age, lest through so long a delay all knowledge and reverence of God
should disappear from human minds and despair should swallow up faith.
Moreover, the dispensations of grace and glory had to be distinguished, and
the work of restoration could not be contiguous with perfection. Its own age
and καιρός, *proper season*, had to be assigned to each work, and Christ’s
first advent could not be confused with his second.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

V. The inquiry here concerns the time of the first advent. It is not only
permissible to investigate it but also necessary for the ἀσφάλειαν,
*certainty*, of our faith. For this reason, the Holy Spirit wished to mark it
in the Old Testament by certain indubitable signs so that the faithful might
recognize him when he came and no unbeliever might have the pretext of
ignorance for rejecting him.

We must not listen to the present-day rabbis, who, to extricate themselves more
easily from the arguments by which we invincibly demonstrate from Scripture
that this time has long since passed, have sought this escape: no definite
time for the Messiah’s advent was fixed in Scripture, but God reserved this
secret to himself alone so that he might send the Messiah whenever he wished.
Hence they strictly forbid inquiry into the Messiah’s time and strike with an
anathema whoever wishes to calculate it, in *Talmud, Codex Sanhedrin*, chapter
11: “May the swollen bones of those who calculate the periods of times be
broken.” If anything is, this is surely the argument of a desperate cause. For
who can believe that persons could be found among them who would dare deny a
matter so clear, so constantly believed among the people from every past age,
and so greatly desired and expected, unless, bewitched by blind error and
prejudice of mind, they were so possessed by a spirit of contradiction that
they considered no fabrication so foreign that they would not cling to it
tenaciously, provided it departed as far as possible from a meaning that might
seem capable of favoring our Jesus?

VI. When we dispute concerning the time of the Messiah’s advent, three things
must be noted. 1. We are not treating such a *time*, or such *indications and
signs* of it, as fall upon our senses without our giving thought to them and
cannot be wholly unknown to anyone. The Jews would require signs of the kind
which are usually called *mathematical and physical*: for example, the Messiah
will come with the sun standing at midday, or during an eclipse of the sun at
the full moon, or with God’s mountain elevated above the other mountains, and
so forth. Rather, we treat signs of time which are called *historical*, which
can be known through deeds and events and are such that they must be observed
with attention and apprehended by faith. 2. We do not undertake to prove the
precise point of this time—say, the year, month, week, and so forth—which God
appointed or what that point was, for there is no need to reduce the inquiry
to this. We undertake only to prove ἐν πλάτει, *in breadth*, that a definite
time was appointed for the Messiah’s advent and that it was of such a kind as
could be recognized from the clearest deeds and events. This suffices now that
all those events have occurred. 3. We do not treat just any signs mentioned in
Scripture, but those which serve to convict the Jews and have an essential
connection with the Messiah’s advent, so that they would not exist unless the
Messiah had come, and because they do exist it must therefore be believed that
the Messiah has come.

VII. Thus the state of the question is reduced to these terms: Has the promised
Messiah already come, or is he still to come and to be expected by us? Has the
time of his advent fixed by God already passed and elapsed, or has it not yet
arrived? The Jews maintain the latter with great obstinacy. We, resting upon
invincible reasons, assert the former.

[Printed p. 243 | Internet Archive PDF 271 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: 1. A demonstration of the Messiah’s advent drawn from the
time of Shiloh.]

VIII. The *first demonstration* is drawn from the *determination of the time*
at which the Messiah’s advent had to occur. For if the time appointed for the
Messiah’s advent came long ago, the Messiah himself must also have already
come. That this time has come and passed is gathered most clearly from its
various characteristics.

The first is drawn from Jacob’s oracle in Gen. 49:10, where the Messiah’s
advent is joined with the removal of the Jewish scepter: “The scepter,” he
says, “shall not be removed from Judah, nor the Legislator from between his
feet, until Shiloh comes,” and so forth. Hence we argue thus: if the scepter
was not to be removed from Judah until Shiloh, who is the Messiah, came, it
follows most certainly that he has come, because that oracle was fulfilled
long ago, the Jewish scepter has been removed, and the Jews have no princes.
To confirm the argument against the cavils of the Jews, two things must be
proved: 1. that *Shiloh* here means the Messiah; and 2. that the passage treats
the removal of the Jews’ political dominion and authority.

IX. 1. First, *Shiloh* can be none other than the Messiah. Whatever the word’s
origin and meaning, it is certain that no one else is designated here. It may
be explained as ἀπεσταλμένον, *one to be sent*, as the Vulgate interprets it,
as though it read שלוח. Or it may be explained as *his son*, that is, as
Kimchi explains it, בנו, who will be born משלה from a woman’s *afterbirth*, in
the manner of all who are born. Or it may be derived from the Arabic language,
in which שיל means an *effusion of blood*: “until שילה, the effusion of his
blood, comes,” that is, the seed which flowed from him, as Louis de Dieu
maintains. Or the word may be derived from שלה, which means to act happily and
prosperously: “until the *Savior*, or the *Peaceful and tranquil One*, comes,”
as Oleaster, Avenarius, and others maintain. Or the word may be said to be
composed of שי, which means *a gift*, and the suffix לו, that is, *a gift for
him*, because gifts were to be brought to him, as R.S.J. insists. Finally, it
may be explained by אשר לו, *whose it is*, supplying *kingdom*. This opinion
appears the most suitable of all and the best accommodated to this passage, so
that the meaning is that the kingdom is not to be removed from Judah “until he
to whom it belongs comes.” Thus the paraphrases of Onkelos and Jerusalem
understood it: “until the Messiah, whose is the kingdom, comes.” The Septuagint
renders it τὰ ἀποκείμενα αὐτῷ, *the things reserved for him*, or ᾧ ἀπόκειται,
*for whom it is reserved*, as Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian have it.

Light can also be shed on this passage from Ezek. 21:27, where the crown of
Jeconiah is discussed: “This”—supplying *crown*—“will not be until אשר לו, the
one whose is the judgment, comes.” However it is explained, it can be most
properly applied to the Messiah, especially since the Messiah’s sign is
immediately added, namely, that *the obedience or gathering of the peoples
will be his*. This is everywhere attributed to the Messiah (Gen. 22; Isa.
11:10; Mic. 4:1). The sequence of the discourse also demands it. Just as Jacob
attributes to Judah preeminence and dominion over his brothers and his
father’s sons, so he says of the Messiah who would arise from him that not only
his brothers would worship him but all peoples would also obey him.

X. It can fit no one else. It does not fit *Moses*, because royal power had not
yet existed among Judah and so could not have been transferred from Judah to
Moses. It does not fit *Saul*, who was supposedly to be anointed in Shiloh and
through whom the scepter was removed from Judah and transferred to the tribe
of Benjamin. For 1. Saul was not anointed in Shiloh, but first at Ramah,
solemnly declared king at Mizpah, and confirmed again at Gilgal (1 Sam. 9, 10,
11). 2. Before Saul, the royal scepter did not yet exist in Judah; indeed, the
kingdom had not yet been constituted in a particular tribe among the people.
3. During Saul’s reign the scepter was transferred to Judah and David and thus
was not removed from him. 4. Neither the *obedience* nor the *expectation of
the peoples* could belong to Saul. It does not fit *David*, in whom the
preeminence of Judah’s tribe was translated into royal dignity, because the
scepter is not said to be transferred but *removed*, nor did the scepter cease
where

[Printed p. 244 | Internet Archive PDF 272 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

it first began. It does not fit *Jeroboam*, who was supposedly crowned in
Shiloh, because he was anointed in the city of Shechem, not in Shiloh (1 Kings
12); nor was the kingdom then removed from Judah. On the contrary, it
continued for a long time, and only Judah’s kings were even considered true
kings. It does not fit *Nebuchadnezzar*, who was supposedly to be sent because
of the people’s sins to remove the kingdom. For 1. all Jacob’s words in this
oracle refer to Judah’s blessing, but that deportation was a punishment, not a
blessing. 2. It is false that the scepter was then removed absolutely, because
it is certain that many of the leading men were deported to Babylon, the Jews
lived according to their own laws by the king’s consent, and they had their
own αἰχμαλωτάρχην, ריש גלותא, *head or prince of the captivity*, chosen from
Judah.

Finally, the ancient Jews acknowledge that the word must be understood of the
Messiah. 1. The Chaldean paraphrases of Onkelos, Jonathan, and Jerusalem say,
“Until מלכא משיחא, the King Messiah, comes.” 2. The Talmudists say in *Codex
Sanhedrin*, chapter 12: “What is the Messiah’s name? Let the house of R.
Shelah teach: Shiloh is his name, as it is written, ‘until Shiloh comes.’” 3.
The rabbis say in *Rabbah on Genesis*, “until Shiloh comes, that is, the King
Messiah.” Rashi and R. Bechai have the same.

XI. 2. Second, that the removal of the Jewish kingdom is meant is gathered
from the word שבט. Here it can refer to nothing other than the royal insignia,
namely, a *rod* or *scepter*, the symbol of royal majesty. Hence the Septuagint
renders it ἄρχων, *ruler*; Aquila, σκῆπτρον, *scepter*; and Symmachus,
ἐξουσία, *authority*. The Jews wrongly wish the *rod of chastisement* to be
denoted, as in Prov. 22:5—that is, *affliction and oppression*—so that the
meaning would be: “The taskmasters will not cease to afflict Israel until the
Messiah, who will liberate them, comes.”

But to the contrary: 1. Such a rod must be understood as belongs to the מחקק,
*Legislator*, who is joined with it here, for it belongs to a king to possess
a scepter and to enact laws, σκῆπτρόν τ’ ἠδὲ θέμιστας, as Homer says in
*Iliad* 2. But what does a legislator have to do with a rod of oppression? 2.
The patriarch would thus have prophesied Judah’s punishments, not his
blessings, the contrary of which is evident from the words, *Judah, your
brothers will praise you*. Victory is promised to him, preeminence of honor
among his brothers, a lion’s strength, and the peaceful possession of prey.
How can these things stand with a prediction of affliction? Moreover, since
the rod of chastisement would have to be common to all the tribes, there was
no reason to treat the tribe of Judah alone. 4. It is false that oppression
never ceased in that tribe, since it flourished through many generations from
David to Zedekiah. 5. The same words occur in Zech. 10:11, שבט מצרים, which
the Septuagint renders σκῆπτρον Αἰγύπτου περιαιρεθήσεται, *the scepter of Egypt
will be removed*. 6. The ancient Jews contradict this interpretation. Onkelos
says, “שבט, one exercising dominion, will not fail.” Jonathan says, “Kings and
princes will not cease.” The Jerusalem paraphrase says, “Kings will not
cease.” The *Talmud, Tractate Sanhedrin*, p. 5; *Bereshit Rabbah*, fol. 110;
Ramban, last book of *Yad*, chapter 4; and R.D.K. under the word שבט explain it
of principality and dominion.

XII. The removal of the Jewish kingdom is not without reason assigned as a
characteristic of the Messiah’s advent, because the ἀρχὴ, *rise*, of one is the
δύσις, *setting*, of the other: the ἀφαίρεσις, *removal*, of the Jewish kingdom
is the foundation of Christ’s kingdom. Since the Jewish commonwealth was
primarily instituted on account of Christ, to preserve the tribe from which
the Messiah was to arise, the commonwealth could not be overthrown nor the
tribes confounded until the Messiah’s advent. Moreover, since that polity was
shadowy and earthly, and therefore the promises were not made to it, it could
not endure forever. Its glory had to be καταργεῖσθαι, *abolished*, and give
place to Christ’s mystical and eternal kingdom.

It matters little whether the particle עד כי, *until*, is taken *exclusively*,
so as to intimate that the scepter had to cease before the Messiah’s advent,
or *inclusively*, so that the meaning is that the government would not cease
until the Messiah came and that he would come before the scepter’s removal.
The force of the argument always remains against the Jews. For if it is true
that

[Printed p. 245 | Internet Archive PDF 273 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the Messiah’s advent is joined with the removal of the Jewish scepter, it
cannot be doubted that he has come, since that scepter was taken away so long
ago, all judicial power was completely removed, the city was destroyed, and
the people were scattered. The Jews themselves are compelled to acknowledge
this. R.D.K. says on Hos. 3:4, “These are the days of exile in which we are
today. We have neither a king nor a prince from Israel, for we are under the
power of the nations,” and so forth. Menasseh says in his *Conciliator* on Gen.
49, “What punishment we now endure, living without king, prince, or
government.”

XIII. There is no need to weary oneself in seeking the precise point of time
at which this power was taken away from the Jewish people, provided it is
agreed—as is most certain—that it is taken away today and has been for many
centuries. Chronologists may dispute it sharply among themselves and differ
widely in their opinions, some insisting that the scepter was removed under
Pompey and others under Vespasian. Others, far more suitably, maintain that it
was not the work of a single moment: the commonwealth, shaken on various
occasions under Pompey and Herod, was finally overthrown under Vespasian.
Nevertheless, our argument against the Jews does not require a decision on
this intricate question. Its force always remains because it is placed beyond
every hazard of controversy that all government was removed from the Jews long
ago. It is now not days or years but centuries since this oracle received its
fulfillment. It necessarily follows either that the oracle was false and
deceitful—which it would be impious even to think—or that the Messiah has
already come.

XIV. Nor should we listen to the Jews who invent the story that the scepter
still remains in Babylon among those who preside over the people there.
Menasseh tries to confirm this from Benjamin, who reports that at Baghdad he
saw with his own eyes among the Israelites a certain prince born from David’s
line, riding in a chariot amid the acclamations of the people. These are the
utterly vain fabrications of their minds. They contradict the opinion of the
rabbis cited above and the testimony of all who have traveled throughout the
world or written about those people, among whom there is complete silence
about such princes. Even if there were some truth in them, is it credible that
the rest of the Jews, dispersed throughout the world under the miserable
servitude which oppresses them, would remain so for even an hour? Therefore,
Benjamin’s testimony should have no place among us, for he is a most vain
inventor of fables and was often convicted of falsehood by the very learned
L’Empereur. This can be said all the more certainly because the tribes are now
confused, making it impossible to know with certainty who are from Judah and
who are from another tribe.

XV. There remains the principal difficulty raised here by the Jews. They say
that it cannot be gathered from this passage that the Messiah has already
come, for otherwise he would have had to come before the Babylonian captivity,
because in it the scepter was removed from the Jews and was never afterward
returned to them. To answer it, we premise the following. 1. Definite and
indubitable points of time at which the flower and glory of kingdoms endure or
at which they fall and are overthrown cannot always be assigned. Just as the
constitution and dignity of kingdoms are not obtained all at once but
gradually and successively, so their decline and fall do not occur in one
moment or in an open and perceptible manner, but gradually, imperceptibly, and
without being noticed. They are sooner detected after they have occurred than
observed while occurring. Therefore, this must not be restricted precisely to
a particular moment but considered ἐν πλάτει, *in breadth*, within a certain
latitude of time. 2. It is one thing for the royal line possessing the right
to reign to endure; it is another for kings to be actually reigning. The
latter may be abolished while the former remains, as when someone tyrannically
seizes a kingdom and expels the lawful heirs: the κτῆσις, *possession*,
remains while the χρῆσις, *exercise*, is removed. 3. The oracle’s fulfillment
does not require the supreme dignity always to have been held in Judah’s
tribe. It is enough

[Printed p. 246 | Internet Archive PDF 274 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to preserve its truth if some government and principality flourished in it.
The words, *the scepter will not depart, nor the Legislator*, and so forth,
must be taken disjunctively, not conjunctively. The meaning is that the Jews
would not be deprived of both until Shiloh came, but that one or the other
would always be retained: if not the scepter, then the legislative and
judicial power and νομοθεσίαν, *lawgiving*. 4. The dominion need not always
have been held in a continuous and uninterrupted series. It is enough for the
designation always to have remained. Thus, although the Spanish kingdom
passed to the Austrian house, it did not cease to be called the Spanish crown.

XVI. Given these points, I say that the continuation of the scepter and power
in Judah can be demonstrated clearly. 1. It continued from David to the
Babylonian captivity, a period of 470 years. 2. During the captivity it
continued, first, in *Jeconiah*, whose “head Evil-merodach lifted up and whose
throne he set above the seats of the other kings” (2 Kings 25:27, 28); second,
in *Daniel*, who was of the tribe of Judah and royal blood (Dan. 2:25; 5:13);
and third, in the *aechmalotarchs*, or rulers of the captivity, who existed
during the captivity. Hence Ramban says in the last book of *Yad*, chapter 4,
“The aechmalotarchs hold the king’s place, because it is said, ‘The scepter
will not be removed,’” and so forth. R. Salomon says in *Sanhedrin*, fol. 5,
“The scepter will not be removed. These are the heads of the exile, who govern
the people by the indulgence of the Persian kings.” 3. After the Babylonian
captivity it continued in *Zerubbabel*, Jeconiah’s grandson, and *Nehemiah*,
whom Eusebius notes in his *Chronicle*, p. 156, to have been from the tribe of
Judah. Consequently, the Nehemiah numbered among the priests in Neh. 10:1 must
be different from this man, since it is evident from chapter 7:5, 7 that there
were two Nehemiahs.

4. From Nehemiah to Herod, over 335 years, the proof is more difficult because
the canon of Scripture fails, but it can be gathered in three ways. 1. With
respect to the *Maccabees*: although they were from the tribe of Levi, the
kingdom did not cease to be called Jewish. Only Judah returned from the
captivity together with Benjamin, which was its accession; hence the kingdom
and commonwealth belonged to Judah’s tribe. Those who ruled, although from
another tribe, had received the scepter from Judah and wielded it in Judah and
in its name. Therefore, the scepter could truly be said to be held by Judah,
because the majesty of the government always remained with it, just as it
remained with Rome when Spaniards and Thracians were elected to the empire. 2.
It continued in the *aechmalotarchs* discussed above. 3. It continued in the
*great Sanhedrin*, which always had great power, as Josephus notes in book 14,
chapter 17. Hence the royal throne is attributed to this senatorial college in
Ps. 122:5 and 2 Chron. 19:8. Its president was called נשיא and throughout the
books of the Jews אב״ד. He was always from Judah’s tribe, as the *Jewish
Chronology* testifies. And since only Judah returned with the accession of
Benjamin, it is not doubtful that this council was composed, at least for the
greater part, from Judah. The government remained with it until, when Judea
was subdued by the Romans, that power was gradually worn away and finally
entirely removed.

*Thus the fall of the Jewish commonwealth can be viewed under these three
stages*: first, *under Pompey*, beginning sixty years before Christ; second,
when it was advanced in the tenth year after Christ, when Archelaus was exiled
to Vienne and Judea was reduced to the form of a province; and third, when it
was consummated *under Titus*, with the temple destroyed and the city ruined.

[Marginal heading: The second demonstration, from Daniel’s weeks.]

XVII. The second demonstration is drawn from *Daniel’s weeks*. The passage
reads thus in Dan. 9:24–27: “Seventy weeks are determined upon your people and
upon the holy city, to restrain transgression, to seal sins, to expiate
iniquity, to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal vision and prophecy,
and to anoint the Holy of Holies. Therefore, know and understand that from the
declaration of the word concerning the return of the people and the rebuilding
of Jerusalem until Messiah the Leader there will be seven weeks and then
sixty-two weeks,” and so forth.

[Printed p. 247 | Internet Archive PDF 275 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

“And after sixty-two weeks Christ will be killed,” and so forth. Hence we
argue thus: if after seventy weeks of years—that is, after 490 years from the
permission of the people’s return—the Messiah had to be manifested and killed
to accomplish the work of redemption, he must have come long ago, because
since this oracle four times that period and more have elapsed. But the former
is true from the prophet’s words; therefore, so is the latter.

XVIII. To elucidate the argument, three things must be proved: 1. that the
passage treats the Messiah; 2. that the weeks here are not of days but of
years; and 3. that wherever their beginning is placed, this time passed long
ago. With these points established, it will be invincibly evident against the
Jews that the Messiah has already come.

As to the first, the passage’s reference to the Messiah is evident: 1. from
his open designation in verse 25, because in the case of Christ alone this
name is attributed absolutely and κατ’ ἐξοχήν, *preeminently*, as his proper
name. It is never found attributed to any prince or priest without some
addition, whether *of the Lord*, a suffix, or a proper name. 2. The Messiah is
called נגיד, that is, *Prince and Leader*, which belongs to Christ alone, as it
is attributed to him in Isa. 55:4 and Mic. 5:2 compared with Matt. 2:6. It
therefore calls him the leader by way of excellence because he would be
eminent above all other kings and priests. 3. The works said to be performed
here in the Messiah’s time can belong to no other than Christ alone: the
abolition of sin, the expiation of iniquity, the introduction of everlasting
righteousness, and the sealing of prophecy. 4. The Jews themselves explain
the passage of the Messiah, including R. Saadia Gaon, R. Nachman of Gerona,
and others, as Menasseh observes in his book *On the Term of Life*. 5. It is
said of the Messiah that he will confirm the covenant in the middle of the
week and be cut off, which fits no one but Christ.

XIX. Futile is what R.S.J. says on Daniel 9: that there are several messiahs
but only one son of David κατ’ ἐξοχήν, *preeminently*, of whom this passage
cannot be understood. He says verse 25 treats Cyrus, who is called *God’s
shepherd and messiah* in Isa. 44:28 and 45:1; verse 26 treats Herod Agrippa;
and verse 24 treats the ark and the other instruments of the Jerusalem temple.
For 1. since Scripture speaks three times of the Messiah in one and the same
passage without any indication of a distinction, no reason can be given why
several messiahs should be invented here and the only true one excluded, the
one of whom alone the Holy Spirit wished to speak for the consolation of
Daniel and the afflicted people. 2. Cyrus is indeed called *God’s anointed* in
Isa. 44:28 and 45:1 because of the singular benefit toward God’s people which
God’s providence appointed him to furnish. Yet no one would say he was the
Israelites’ principal Redeemer, of whom Daniel nevertheless speaks. Moreover,
Daniel speaks of a Messiah who would come after seven and sixty-two weeks, but
Cyrus died before the first week. Hence Aben Ezra and other rabbis reject this
fabrication. 3. What verse 26 says is no better referred to Herod Agrippa.
Apart from the absurd fiction that in the same passage Daniel produces a
second Messiah more than five hundred years after the former died, this last
Agrippa, who reigned in Trachonitis, never possessed any power in Jerusalem or
Judea. So far was any Jew from dreaming that he was the Messiah. It is also
false that he was killed before the Romans captured the city, since he lived
on the friendliest terms with them to the end of his life and assisted
Vespasian’s forces in subduing the Jews. 4. The Holy of Holies in verse 24
cannot be called the ark or altar, because it is a person who had to be
anointed. The ark was sprinkled with blood annually but is never said to have
been anointed with oil. Moreover, the Jews maintain that the ark and anointing
oil were absent from the second temple. Thus it can fit none other than our
Christ, who was not only the holiest of all but Israel’s very Holiness (Luke
1:35), anointed by the Holy Spirit with the oil of gladness above his fellows.

[Printed p. 248 | Internet Archive PDF 276 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XX. Second, the *weeks* in question are not of *days* but of *years*, such as
those treated in Lev. 25:8, so that seven years are counted in each week after
the prophetic manner of Ezek. 4:6. There are several reasons. 1. Seventy weeks
of days would be too narrow and would not even make a year and a half, which
scarcely agrees with so illustrious a prophecy, much less with its
fulfillment. 2. Nothing that the angel predicted occurred within this period,
nor could Jerusalem be said to have been built in seven weeks, since this
occurred only over the course of many years. 3. This style is prophetic, as in
Ezek. 4 and Rev. 12:6; 13:5. It is also taken in the same sense elsewhere, as
in Gen. 29:27. Where a week of days is intended, the word *days* is added, as
in Dan. 10:2 and Ezek. 45:23. No mention of years is made here because the
angel deliberately speaks more obscurely to preserve the prophets’ manner.

The Jews, pressed here by the force of truth, vainly flee to *weeks of decades
or jubilees*. For 1. no approved author and no part of Scripture mentions such
weeks, but only weeks of days or years; hence it is evident that they were
gratuitously invented. 2. The Holy Spirit’s purpose plainly opposes it. His
purpose is to console Daniel and the people, who were overcome with great
grief, by the Messiah’s approach after seventy weeks. But how cold this
consolation would have been if it was not to come for 3,500 years. 3. Daniel
places the Messiah’s advent before the destruction of the city and temple;
indeed, he subjoins that calamity as a consequence of the Messiah’s death and
the most deserved punishment for so great a crime. But these fabulists
postpone the Messiah’s advent until three thousand years after the city’s
destruction.

XXI. God’s purpose, therefore, was to console the people in their harsh
captivity and confirm their faith, not only by marking its end but also by
promising a far more excellent redemption. It would be revealed after a
longer interval, not seventy years but seventy weeks of years, so that God’s
grace might be compared with his judgment and seventy weeks of years with the
seventy years during which the Jews endured punishment. Thus the angel
responds to Daniel’s prayer, which had spoken of the end of seventy years, as
if to say: You spoke of the limit of captivity and the end of seventy years.
But I now announce to you not only this temporal liberation, which is near,
but promise a far more excellent one after seventy weeks of years. Thus God’s
mercy will exceed his judgment sevenfold and the magnitude of grace will
swallow up the magnitude of the calamity.

After the soon-coming completion of those seventy years, therefore, God fixed
seventy weeks—not of days but of years, namely, 490 years—for the duration of
the people and city. Within them the Lord Messiah will come, expiate and
abolish sins by his death, establish the evangelical righteousness which
abides forever, fulfill and finish the prophecies, seal the vision, and, as the
Most Holy One, be consecrated through the Holy Spirit’s anointing, whose χρίσμα,
*anointing*, he will receive as the eternal King and the Head of his church.

XXII. Third, interpreters contend among themselves with great agitation over
the beginning and end of the weeks, and a reconciliation is not easy. Yet we
need not torment ourselves in untangling those most serious difficulties. The
argument against the Jews always remains invincible without doing so. Wherever
the beginning of those weeks is placed—whether in Cyrus’s first year, when the
Jews received liberty to return and build the city, as Béroald and Calvin
maintain from Ezra 1:1; in the second year of Darius Nothus from Ezra 6, as
Scaliger, Junius, Calvisius, and others hold; in the seventh year of
Artaxerxes Longimanus, treated in Ezra 7:1, 13, 14, and so forth, as Capellus,
Bullinger, Helvicus, and others insist; in his twentieth year from Neh. 2:1, as
Julius Africanus, Torniellus, and Ussher maintain; or somewhere else—we do not
now make this dispute our own, but

[Printed p. 249 | Internet Archive PDF 277 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

freely leave the Jews to follow whichever opinion they wish. Wherever the
beginning of these weeks is placed, we insist upon what is clearer than the
midday sun: they passed long ago. The difference here is not a few years or
one century, but many centuries. Once the chronological question is removed
from the center of our controversy, therefore, this must be retained as
certain and indubitable: the beginning is to be placed in the liberty to
return and build the city which was promulgated to the people through the
edicts of the Persian kings, and the end in the city’s destruction and the
people’s utter desolation. Everything predicted here concerning the Messiah’s
advent and the city’s restoration and final fall had to occur within these
limits. The facts themselves proclaim that these events occurred long ago.

XXIII. Some rightly judge here that the beginning and end of these weeks
should not scrupulously be restricted to a definite point or instant of time,
but understood ἐν πλάτει, *in breadth*, and with some latitude. The four
edicts, successively given for the people’s return from captivity and the
city’s restoration, are to be taken collectively and regarded as one, since
the later were, so to speak, interpretations and, as commonly said,
enlargements of the first. Our own age has experienced the same in the various
edicts made for religion’s sake. Minds are not to be tormented with useless
labor in seeking an indivisible point at which these things were fulfilled.
God does not wish us to cling to a point but to regard the substantial events
and bodies of the deeds themselves.

Thus he wished to comprehend various connected things here together, setting
them as it were before our minds in a single view: the Messiah’s advent and
death, the city’s siege and destruction, the destruction of the temple, the
abrogation of Jewish worship, and the people’s distress and desolation. They
necessarily require a certain latitude of time within which they would be
fulfilled. Therefore, everything predicted here, from the release from
captivity and restoration of the city to its ruin and destruction, had to
occur within the limit of these seventy weeks.

XXIV. The Jews, who torment themselves miserably in attempting to evade this
argument, flee to a distinction between an absolute and a hypothetical
prediction. They claim that *Daniel’s prediction of the Messiah’s advent was
hypothetical, provided that the people did not delay his advent by their sins,
but that the people’s sins prevented him from coming at the time Daniel
announced*. But 1. this is gratuitously invented. There is not the slightest
indication of any condition in this oracle, whether in Daniel or anywhere
else. 2. The Messiah’s advent flowed from the mere grace and mercy of God, and
its primary purpose was the abolition of the people’s sins. Hence nothing on
the part of human beings could delay it, least of all sin, since God appointed
that advent for the sole purpose of removing and destroying sin. 3. If any sin
delayed the Messiah’s advent, it had to be graver than all those previously
committed. But we read of no peculiar crime committed by them which exceeded
their earlier crimes, unless one excepts the guilt of crucifying the Messiah,
which they deny was a sin. Under the first temple, idolatry, murders,
adulteries, and every kind of most grievous sin raged among the people, as the
prophets testify. Notwithstanding these, the promise of the Messiah’s advent
was made. But after the time fixed for the Messiah’s advent, idolatry ceased
among them, and apart from obstinate unbelief, which they do not acknowledge,
no one imputes to them anything among their other crimes beyond their previous
sins. Therefore, no reason for this delay can be drawn from them.

XXV. Nor can the cavil of the Jews have a place here, *that they truly suffer a
harsh and lengthy exile on account of their sins, but that it will

[Printed p. 250 | Internet Archive PDF 278 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

finally have an end just as the Babylonian captivity did*. For what comparison can
there be between the Babylonian captivity and the Jews’ most sorrowful present
dispersion? What does an exile of seventy years have in common with an exile
of sixteen centuries? In the former there was the hope of restoration and a
designated end, but here there is nothing of the kind. Then the body of the
nation still stood and used its own laws; now they live under another’s power
and laws. Then the tribes were still distinct and the priestly and royal
families were differentiated; now everything among them is confused and
disordered, so that no appearance of either a polity or a church remains, but
only a horrible example of the divine curse upon these remnants who wander so
miserably.

[Marginal heading: The third demonstration, from the glory of the second
temple.]

XXVI. The third demonstration is drawn from the *glory of the second temple*.
The Messiah had to come while it still stood. The second temple’s prerogative
is placed in this: the Messiah, the Prince of Peace, would honor it with his
presence. Since the second temple was destroyed long ago, it is a most certain
argument that the Messiah came long ago.

This characteristic is gathered from two oracles in Haggai and Malachi, where
it is clearly asserted. Haggai 2:6, 7 says, “Yet a little while, and I will
make the heavens and the earth, the sea and the dry land, tremble, and I will
make all nations tremble, and the desire of all nations will come, and I will
fill this house with glory, says the Lord.” Verse 9 adds, “The glory of this
last house will be greater than the first.” Malachi 3:1 confirms the same:
“Behold, I send my angel, and he will prepare the way before my face, and the
Ruler whom you seek, the Angel of the Covenant whom you desire, will suddenly
come to his temple.” Hence we argue thus: the second temple will not be
destroyed until the Messiah comes; therefore, the destruction of that temple
is a certain sign that the Messiah has come. In the former passage he is said
to be coming soon after the temple whose building began while Haggai
prophesied was constructed, and that temple would then be filled with greater
glory than before. In the second he is said to be coming suddenly to his
temple, namely, the one which had been constructed when Malachi prophesied.
How could he be said to come suddenly if he would not come for several
thousand years?

XXVII. It is clear that both passages treat the Messiah. 1. In Malachi, it
treats one who was to be sent and come to his temple as Lord and Angel of the
Covenant; who would have Elijah as his forerunner; and who was sought and
expected. To whom but the Messiah can these things belong? Nor do the more
learned Jews themselves deny it, including R.D.K., Aben Ezra, and Maimonides,
who refer it to the Messiah. 2. Likewise in Haggai, what is said of the shaking
of heaven and earth and all nations so that they come to their desire cannot
be referred to another. Either the *desire of the nations* designates *those
desired from the nations*, that is, all from the nations who were to be chosen
and called to Christ, corresponding to Jacob’s oracle, which foretold the
gathering of the peoples to him; or, as seems more suitable, it means the
Messiah himself, who far more truly than Daniel is the *Man of desires*,
ἐπιθύμητος, *wholly desirable*, whom the faithful desired with an ardent
desire.

Moreover, what he adds concerning the second temple’s greater glory belongs
here, because it could arise from nothing other than the presence of the
house’s Lord, who was to bring peace—not corporeal peace, in which the Jews are
not found to have greatly rejoiced under the second temple, as they themselves
admit, but spiritual and heavenly peace. The Messiah, the *Prince of Peace*,
had to bring it, and the angels announce it in Luke 2:14.

XXVIII. The Jews would vainly refer this second house’s greater glory to the
external things which made it conspicuous: either to its duration, because the
second supposedly stood 420 years, ten years longer than the first; to the
splendid gifts which Darius, Artaxerxes, and other princes contributed to the
temple’s construction and sacrifices; or to

[Printed p. 251 | Internet Archive PDF 279 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Herod’s additions, because he constructed a more glorious temple, as Josephus
maintains, or at least adorned it, according to Hegesippus. I say these things
are offered in vain. 1. Such external and trivial things do not establish the
glory of God’s house, nor would God speak of such trivial matters so grandly
and magnificently that he would say he would shake heaven and earth and
perform wonders on their account. 2. God expressly declares that he does not
wish to place that glory in these perishable things when he says, *The silver
is mine; the gold is mine*, as if to say: I could adorn this temple with gold
and silver if I wished, as Solomon’s temple was adorned, for all gold is in my
possession, and so forth, as in Ps. 50:10–12. But I will adorn it with
something far more precious: the presence of my Son, who is Israel’s glory. 3.
Whatever additions or ornaments Herod made in the temple, they could never
equal, much less exceed, the glory of Solomon’s. Above the rest, five chief
adornments of the temple rendered that glory conspicuous, and the Jews confess
that they were lacking from the second: the ark of the covenant, the continual
fire, the holy oil, the Spirit of prophecy, and the Shekinah, or God’s presence
at the mercy seat.

XXIX. What they add about *a third temple to be built, to which these things
belong*, is no better. The passage treats the temple which the Jews saw, which
is called הבית הזה, *this house*. Otherwise, no consolation could thereby
have come to Zerubbabel and his companions who were building the temple, whom
the Lord nevertheless wished to encourage by this argument: this house would
be filled with greater glory. Although Herod is said to have restored this
house, he must not therefore be thought to have built a wholly new one. It was
always considered the same as Zerubbabel’s temple. Moreover, a temple to be
built anew is vainly invented when the former was so completely destroyed
that no remaining hope of its restoration exists. Those who attempted it
under Hadrian or Julian undertook it in vain and were prevented from heaven.

[Marginal heading: The fourth demonstration, from the signs.]

XXX. The fourth demonstration is drawn from the *signs* which had to accompany
the Messiah’s advent. With respect to the *Jews*, these were the fall of the
Jewish commonwealth and polity and the people’s rejection. With respect to the
*world*, it was the end of false oracles. With respect to the *church*, it was
the calling of the nations. Since all these things were fulfilled long ago,
the Messiah must also have come.

[Marginal heading: 1. With respect to the Jews, the fall of the commonwealth.]

1. *With respect to the Jews*, the sign was the *fall of the commonwealth*.
Since the Messiah was to arise from the Jews, the commonwealth could not be
destroyed before his advent. It is agreed that the Messiah had to come to that
*people*, distinguished into its *tribes* and families; to that *land*; and to
the *temple* in which he was expected in the daily worship. But the
commonwealth has now been scattered for many centuries, and the people are
miserably dispersed through every part of the world, expelled from their
country and land, their entire polity overthrown, their city leveled to the
ground, their sacred rites abolished, their temple destroyed, all their tribes
confounded, and no hope of return remaining. This has continued not merely for
the seventy years which were the limit of the Babylonian captivity, nor for
the two hundred during which the Egyptian servitude lasted, but for sixteen
hundred and more. What do all these things proclaim except that the Messiah
has already come and is no longer to be expected?

If that miserable nation considered these matters more attentively, they would
recognize their own error and faithlessness sufficiently from themselves and
their own state. What other cause could they offer? Why, for so many centuries,
should they be so neglected, exercised by such hard fortunes, scattered so
widely as exiles, and made so sad an object of mockery for the entire human
race, although they anxiously expect their Messiah and weary him with daily
vows and prayers, and are not so defiled by the worship of idols or other sins
of the nations as their ancestors were? Another cause must certainly underlie
the miserable wandering of this people once so dearly loved by God. It can be
none other than the impious rejection and

[Printed p. 252 | Internet Archive PDF 280 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

unspeakable murder of the true Messiah and apostasy from the true faith, in
which it perseveres to the present day.

We must not listen here to the Jews’ common objection that this advent is
deferred because of the sins of themselves and their fathers committed under
both temples. This concerns not one or another oracle but the constant
trustworthiness of the prophets and prophecies. No certainty would remain in
it if it were not fulfilled in this chief point upon which all the rest
depend. Moreover, if God ever promised anything to his people, it is never
said that he omitted it on account of their sins. This is evident from their
liberation from Egypt and Babylon, which God fulfilled precisely at the
appointed time although the people were no less burdened by sins than now. Why
then should it be said that he did not do the same in this chief promise, in
which the good of the whole nation was situated, although the fulfillment of
so great a promise has now been delayed more than sixteen centuries and no
hope of its fulfillment remains? Third, if the Messiah’s advent is deferred
because of the people’s sins, he will never come, because the people will
never be free from every crime. So far are its sins from being able to delay
and prevent his advent that they should rather hasten it, since he comes to
remove them.

[Marginal heading: 2. With respect to the world, the destruction of idolatry.]

XXXI. 2. *With respect to the world*, the sign was the destruction of false
oracles and *pagan idolatry*, which the prophets more than once foretold would
be destroyed in the Messiah’s time (Isa. 2:18, 20; Zech. 13:2). Profane
histories do not conceal that oracles ceased at Christ’s advent. Hence in
Tiberius’s time the questions arose: Why had the oracles become silent? Why
did demons not operate as before? So sudden and wonderful was this change that
it was formidable and astonishing even to the enemies of the Christian name.
Porphyry complains that since Jesus came, the gods, being angry, have given no
answers. Cicero testifies in book 2 of *On Divination* that the oracles had
failed in his times. He absurdly refers the cause to the age of the place from
which the exhalation proceeded by which the Pythia, her mind inspired,
delivered oracles. Plutarch, who lived in Trajan’s time, that is, a hundred
years after Christ, published a treatise *On the Failure of Oracles*. He
reports that Epitherses, sailing past the Echinades islands, heard a voice
announcing that *the great Pan was dead*, namely, in the time of Tiberius, when
Christ was crucified. And under Augustus the oracle of Apollo, when asked who
would reign after him, is said to have answered: “A Hebrew boy, ruling over
the blessed gods, commands me to leave this house and depart immediately into
hell.”

[Marginal heading: The fifth demonstration, from the adversaries’ confessions.]

XXXII. The fifth argument is furnished by the Jews’ own *confessions*. Among
them, 1. there is the celebrated *tradition of the house of Elijah* in *Codex
Sanhedrin*, division *Chelek*. It distributes the times of the world into
three periods and concludes its whole duration in six thousand years,
attributing the final two thousand years to the Messiah’s reign: *two thousand
years of emptiness, two thousand of the law, and two thousand of the Messiah*.
It does not help them to reply either that the time of his advent has elapsed
but is deferred because of the people’s sins—for since the promise is not
conditional, as already said, but absolute, so far is he from having to be
deferred because of the people’s sins that he should instead be hastened all
the more to remove them—or that he would indeed come during the δισχιλίαν,
*two-thousand-year period*, but it is not said whether he would come at its
beginning or end. Those two thousand years are assigned to him in the same way
that the other two thousand are assigned to emptiness and to the law: the
duration of the law would follow emptiness and had to complete two thousand
years. Thus the Messiah’s advent had to follow the end of the law’s two
thousand years so as to endure for the next two thousand.

2. A settled opinion had prevailed among the Jews that the Messiah would come
in Christ’s time, and the minds of the faithful were raised in expectation of
him. Hence Simeon and Anna are said to have expected the consolation of Israel
in Luke 2; the Samaritan woman testifies that she believes the Messiah will
come soon in John 4:25; and various pseudo-Christs arose at that time.

[Printed p. 253 | Internet Archive PDF 281 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

To this is added the fame of pagan predictions by which the Gentiles
themselves were moved. To this belong the words of Josephus, who says that the
χρησμόν, *oracle*, concerning a king arising from their borders who would rule
the world was then fulfilled; and of Suetonius in *Vespasian*, chapter 4:
“Throughout the East an ancient and constant opinion had become widespread:
it was in the fates that at that time men going forth from Judea would obtain
the rule.” Whatever Suetonius says, the fact itself declares that this did not
pertain to Vespasian but to Christ, who was born and suffered shortly before
Vespasian’s time. Anyone who considers all the wonders which occurred around
Christ’s birth, related by Orosius and others, will easily recognize that
nature was then in labor to bring forth the King who would come from the East,
to whom all who wished to be saved had to submit. Anyone desiring more should
consult Raymond’s *Dagger of Faith*, part 2, chapter 10, which collects the
testimonies of the Jews which pertain here.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXXIII. The Elijah who had to precede the Messiah is not the Tishbite
literally, but John the Baptist analogically and figuratively, because of his
similarity to Elijah in zeal and fervor. He would come in Elijah’s spirit and
power (Luke 1:17; Matt. 11:14), just as the Messiah is often called David (Jer.
30:9; Hos. 3:5).

XXXIV. The *end of days*, or the *last days* (Isa. 2:2), in which the Messiah
would come, does not signify the end of the world, but the πλήρωμα καιρῶν,
*fullness of the times*, or the last dispensation, after which no other is to
be expected but only eternity. It is therefore called the end of days not
absolutely, but comparatively in relation to the preceding times, because
those days had to bring the Old Testament to an end. Until that time
everything was, so to speak, suspended; the people were commanded always to
look to the Messiah, to defer their hope, and not to fix their eyes upon the
present shadows. But through Christ’s advent came the fullness of days, in
which the church’s integrity was to be restored.

XXXV. When *the mountain of the Lord* is said to be *exalted above the other
mountains* in the Messiah’s time (Isa. 2:2), this oracle is not to be
understood properly and literally, which could not be said without absurdity,
but mystically of the church’s exaltation. It is everywhere designated by the
mountain of the Lord and Mount Zion because God’s majesty shone forth in it,
for he wished to place his sanctuary there. Hence the gospel’s voice was heard
throughout the world to carry us even to heaven, and a banner was raised to
all the nations. Thus *Bereshit Rabbah* says, “The great mountain is the
Messiah.” Likewise Aben Ezra says, “This mountain is the Messiah, to be
exalted most highly in the midst of the nations.” What is added in verse 3
concerning the gathering of all nations does not prove that only one religion
had to exist in the world in Christ’s time. Rather, the meaning is that a
catholic church would be gathered indiscriminately from all peoples and would
profess one religion and one faith in Christ. This was also fulfilled under
the New Testament.

XXXVI. The *peace* which the Messiah would bring was not to be worldly and
corporeal. Christ did not conceal that his followers should not expect such
peace (Luke 12:51; John 14). Rather, it was spiritual peace, which Christ
acquired for us by his death and proclaimed through the gospel. And when “the
wolf will dwell with the lamb” is said in Isa. 11:6, it is to be understood
figuratively of the calling and conversion of the nations. Through it, nations
once utterly alien to Christ and like wild beasts were to be numbered among
Christ’s sheep. Fierce and cruel people like beasts were to be tamed and
endowed with such gentleness that they would cultivate communion with each
other. They would live so that those whom a desire to harm once drove here and
there would instead vie with one another in good offices and devote their work
to the common good. This is designated by the *transformation of swords and
spears into plowshares and pruning hooks*, implements of agriculture intended
for human use in time of peace (chapter 2:4). This did not escape Maimonides,
who in the

[Printed p. 254 | Internet Archive PDF 282 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*Summary of the Talmud, On Kings*, chapter 12, teaches that “these and other
similar things written concerning matters which pertain to the Messiah are
allegories.”

XXXVII. If the *light of the sun* had to be *sevenfold greater* in the
Messiah’s time, and the light of the moon like the light of the sun (Isa.
30:26), this cannot be understood literally. Apart from the fact that no one
could bear the sun’s brightness, no night would remain because of the moon’s
brightness. The Lord illuminates the good and the wicked with his sun, but the
passage treats a happiness which cannot be common to the impious. These things
must therefore be explained figuratively, either *historically* of the
magnitude of the joy which the Jewish church would experience when liberated
from the Babylonian captivity, or *mystically* of the magnitude of glory,
knowledge, and joy which the Messiah, who would come to heal the church’s
wounds, would bring it when the law’s dark shadows had to recede and the light
of the gospel shine forth, surpassing by far the Old Testament’s faint light.

XXXVIII. Ezekiel 41 and the following chapters do not mean that the Jerusalem
temple is to be restored again, but portray the church’s mystical temple under
the symbol of the old Solomonic temple. Its fulfillment is not to be sought in
a carnal and earthly Jerusalem but in a spiritual and heavenly one: the one
John describes in Rev. 21:22 with words taken from this representation in
Ezekiel. In this life it is begun through the temple of grace, but in the next
it is perfected through the temple of glory in heaven.

XXXIX. When it is said that in the Messiah’s days Judah will be saved and
Israel will dwell securely (Jer. 23:6), it does not mean Israel according to
the flesh, for the Jews themselves do not deny from Jer. 3:14 and Isa. 8:14,
in *Sanhedrin*, division *Chelek*, that only its remnant is to be saved. It
means Israel according to the Spirit, to whom alone the promises are made
(Rom. 9:4), under which is understood the whole church of the elect and
faithful.

XL. The Messiah’s advent cannot be called into doubt because the *Gog and
Magog* whom he will defeat have not yet appeared. It is gratuitously invented
that the Messiah must conquer them. What is said concerning Gog and [sic]
Magog in Ezek. 38:2, 3, and so forth, is wrongly drawn to him, since it is
agreed that the discourse there does not concern him. It is quite clear that
this must be referred to the cruel oppressors of the Jewish church after the
Babylonian captivity, such as the kings of Syria and the peoples of Asia
Minor. They were so called either from Gyges, king of the Lydians, or from the
Scythians and Tartars who were called this in Gen. 10:2 and had long before
occupied Asia Minor and Syria. Hence in Asia, Hierapolis near the Euphrates
was called *Magog*, as Pliny testifies in book 5, chapter 28. And in Syria,
Scythopolis, east of Lake Tiberias, was formerly called Beth-shan. Eusebius
notes this in the *Greek Chronicle*, book 1, q. 40: “The Scythians traversed
Palestine and occupied Baisan,” that is, Beth-shan, “which they named
Scythopolis after themselves.”
