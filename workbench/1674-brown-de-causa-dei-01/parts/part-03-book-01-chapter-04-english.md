## CHAPTER IV

### On God's natural and positive right

Having explained, so far as the plan of our undertaking allows, the nature of
law in general and of divine law in particular, order requires us to descend
to the consideration of certain particular divine laws—namely, those which
will frequently be mentioned in the course of this treatise, and a distinct
knowledge of which will be useful to us.

Just as theologians and others enumerate various divisions of laws in general,
so divine law also is distributed in different ways. It is not necessary,
however, that we touch upon all these distributions, much less examine them.
But the division of divine laws into positive and natural, and into moral,
ceremonial, and judicial, is celebrated and commonly received. A brief
treatment of these will sufficiently serve our purpose.

For a distinct perception of these divine laws, an explanation of *natural
and positive right* must first be given. This is all the more necessary because
occasion will be given rather often in this treatise to discuss this subject.

This *natural right* is twofold. One is called *a right natural to God
himself*; the other, *a right natural to us*. We must say certain things about
each of these rights. First, here, we shall briefly explain the nature of that
right which is called natural to God, intending to treat the other in the next
chapter.

*Ulpian* holds that *jus* [right] was so called from *justitia* [justice].
Others, however, dislike this because *justum* [the just] and *justitia* more
properly derive from *jus*, which is the primary and simple term. *Cicero* is
said to have reproved those who thought *Venus* was named from *venustas*
[loveliness], since *venustas* was named from *Venus*, as though Venus stood
in it. They therefore maintain that *justitia* is derived from *jus* because
*jus* stands in it. Hence *Isidore* calls *justitia*, as it were, the state of
*jus*. Some say that *jus* is what either law or reason dictates must be done
by us. Hence among the Greeks justice is called δικαιοσύνη, as though δικαίου
σύνεσις—knowledge or understanding of right. They therefore hold that
*justice* is necessarily later than *right*, since every knowledge is later
than that of which it is knowledge, and justice is, as it were, the servant of
right. See *Connanus's Commentary on the Law*, book 1, chapter 2. *Grotius*, in
the prolegomena to his little book *On the Law of War and Peace*, thinks it
can not improbably be said that the very name *juris* derives from *Jove*,
because every right draws its origin—

[1674 logical printed p. 40 | IA leaf 58 | PDF p. 58]

—from him. Others judge more probably that *jus* derives from the word
*jussum* [a command] by cutting off the last syllable, just as from what was
once *ossum* came *os* [bone]. And *Festus* says that the ancients called
*commands* (*jussa*) *rights* (*jura*). *Connanus*, however, thinks *jus* is
the same as *juxta* [beside]. For just as *juxta* means the same as *equally
and in like manner*, so what is equal and even is *jus*. And just as *juxta*
also denotes what is near, so *jus* neither exceeds nor falls short, but is
near and beside the thing to which it is fitted, so far that it is altogether
equal. This occurs when nothing in it is less or greater; for one thing which
exceeds another in magnitude seems to recede from it by as great an interval
as that by which it surpasses it.

But leaving these things aside, from whatever source the word is derived the
matter comes to the same thing. Let us see what it denotes. It is taken in
various ways: for *relationship*, as when we say, “I have the right of
kinship,” and so forth; or for *the place itself in which justice is
administered*. Thus Digest, book 1, title 1, and Terence, “Walk into court.” It
designates the place of the senate, and ἀγορά among the Greeks is the judicial
forum itself. Or it is taken for the *art of law*, or a collection of legal
precepts, in which sense we say that law is *the art of the equitable and the
good*. Or it is taken for the *state or condition of people*, as in the title
of the Institutes *On the Law of Persons*. These meanings, however, are
farther removed from our purpose.

There are other meanings of the word more important for us to note, as when it
is taken for *law*, taking the word “law” (as Grotius says in *On the Law of
War and Peace*, book 1, chapter 1, section 9) in its widest sense, as it denotes
a rule of moral actions obliging one to what is right. Thus *jus* is the same
as a rule of what is lawful and honorable, or a norm of things to be done. Or
it is taken for the *effect of justice*, or the action itself which conforms
to the law or which the law requires and prescribes, as Ames speaks in *Cases
of Conscience*, book 5, chapter 1—or, rather, for *what is owed to anyone by
reason of the laws*. In this sense we say, “to render each person his right.”
Or it is taken for an *attribute of an action*, denoting the same as *just* or
*what is not unjust*, as Grotius holds in the place cited, section 3, where he
divides right so understood into *rectorial*, because it is exercised in an
unequal society such as that consisting of parents and children, masters and
servants, kings and subjects; and *equalizing*, because it is exercised in an
equal society, or among brothers, friends, citizens, and confederates. Finally,
it is taken for *a legitimate power to do or omit something, to have and
possess it, or to dispose as one wills of that of which one is the owner*. In
this sense someone is said *to use his own right*.

Since we are going to speak both of that right which belongs to God and of
that which concerns human beings and has its subject in them, it will suffice
for us to distinguish *right* into *divine and human*.

*Divine right*, as also *human right*, is spoken of in two ways. In one way it
denotes almost the same thing as divine law, as when we say, “This is lawful,
but that is unlawful, by divine right”; that is, divine law approves, commands,
and sanctions or permits the one action, but disapproves, prohibits, and
condemns the other. In the same way something is said to be lawful or unlawful
*by human right* insofar as it is prohibited or not prohibited, commanded or
permitted, by human laws. Thus this distinction is taken from the diversity of
the efficient cause. But we are not speaking here of *divine right* in this
sense, because what is said to be lawful *by divine right* is rather the right
of human beings according to the meaning of right under discussion here.

In another way, therefore, *God's right* is understood here as something
within God or, as it were, formally in God; that is, as it denotes a right and
legitimate power adhering to God—

[1674 logical printed p. 41 | IA leaf 59 | PDF p. 59]

—to do, omit, or permit something, or to dispose of his own as he wills, and
so forth. Consequently, *the right of human beings* will denote that right and
legitimate power adhering to a person to do, omit, or manage something, and so
forth.

*God's right* taken in this way differs greatly from divine law. The latter
speaks of something outside God and efficiently from God which constitutes the
duty of rational creatures, as we showed above. It is a practical rule (as the
celebrated Voetius speaks in *Selected Disputations*, part 1, page 340) which
God judges must be observed by the creature; he therefore wills that the
creature be obligated by it, and by actual revelation and command he obligates
the creature according to that will. Moreover, the law at most connotes or
presupposes *that right* which is in God insofar as he is considered as
legislator. We shall show immediately, however, that *right* should not be
restricted so narrowly.

We do not here touch upon *that right of God which is internal*—that is, which
terminates in God alone, by which God is conceived as though obligating
himself concerning himself, or owing himself to himself (as Dr. *Voetius*
speaks in the place cited, *On the Right and Justice of God*, page 341). This
is so whether that termination in God is considered *essentially*, by which
right God can and is bound to love himself, or *personally*, in which way the
Father is bound to love the Son and conversely, and each to love the Holy
Spirit and conversely. An inquiry into this contributes nothing to our
purpose. Nor do we touch upon God's not dissimilar right by which he is bound,
in whatever he does, establishes, commands, prohibits, or permits in or about
creatures, to do, establish, command, and so forth wisely and agreeably to his
nature, since he is most wise and absolutely perfect.

We must therefore speak of *God's right* insofar as it implies a relation to
creatures and terminates indeed in God, but only in order to the creature. We
shall speak afterward of that right which terminates in the creature when the
discussion turns to *human right*. We do not treat it here, though it too can
be called *God's right* because it is caused by him.

Now this *right of God* which regards creatures regards them either as
*possible* or as *created*. That right which regards them as *creatable or
possible* can be called God's eminent dominion, by which, out of the infinite
possibilities (syncategorematically, that is), by reason of his omnipotence, he
transferred these definite creatures—both as to number or individuals and as
to species, and no others—by his merely free and most wise will from the state
of mere possibility to the state of futurity. He fixed and ordained that they
should actually exist at the predetermined, appointed time. For he founded all
things, and by his will they exist and were created, Revelation 4:11. So too
Psalm 33:9: by his speaking it exists (namely, whatever exists), and by his
commanding it stands. Hence by his word he produced all things, saying, *Let
there be light, and it was made*, and so forth, Genesis 1. This right or
dominion of God is regarded not only in the simple futurity of those creatures
and their actual existence together with all their circumstances, but also in
their ordination to their ends. Hence he destined some rational creatures to
cultivate eternal communion with himself, but others to eternal destruction,
for the glory of his name. *For Jehovah has wrought all things for himself,
even the wicked for the day of evil*, Proverbs 16:4. He has the same power over
all creatures, even the best, as a potter has over clay—and far greater—as the
apostle argues in Romans 9:21–24. This right also regards the disposition or
ordination, in the divine will, of all the means tending toward these various
ends, as could be shown by many things.

[1674 logical printed p. 42 | IA leaf 60 | PDF p. 60]

*That right* with respect to creatures actually existing can be called either
*dominion*, properly and distinctly so called, which includes the right to
*possess, dispose, and use*; or *government*. For with respect to the creatures
produced by him, God can be considered either as *Lord*, properly so called,
or proprietor—that is, one who rightfully possesses what he possesses, can
dispose of it as he pleases, and uses and enjoys it at his own discretion—or
as *Rector and Governor*, who has legislative power as well as the power to
judge and execute the sentence rendered.

This dominion regards all creatures, for by right of creation he can and does
exercise this dominion over all. First Chronicles 29:11, 12, 16: *Yours, O
Jehovah, is greatness—indeed, all things in heaven and on earth; yours is the
kingdom, O Jehovah—for riches and glory are from your presence, and you have
dominion over all, and in your hand are strength and power—Jehovah our God,
this entire abundance of things which we have prepared—is from your hand, and
all these things are yours.* Psalm 24:1: *The earth is Jehovah's, and what
fills it, the habitable world and those who dwell in it.* And Psalm 50:10: *For
every beast of the forest is mine, the cattle upon a thousand mountains.*
Verse 11: *I know every bird of the mountains; indeed, the wild animals are
with me.* Verse 12: *For the habitable earth and its fullness are mine.* Acts
17:24: *That God who made the world and all things in it, since he is Lord of
heaven and earth.* Psalm 115:16: *The heavens themselves are Jehovah's, but
the earth he gave to the sons of men.*

Therefore, just as he is Lord of all, so he disposes of all at his pleasure.
Psalm 115:3: *But our God is in the heavens; he does whatever pleases him.* And
135:6: *Whatever pleases him Jehovah does, in the heavens and on earth,
through the seas and all the depths.* Daniel 4:34: *All the inhabitants of the
earth are regarded as nothing, and according to his will he acts in the host
of heaven and among the inhabitants of the earth; nor is there anyone who can
restrain his hand or say to him, “What are you doing?”* Hence, although he has
established an order in the universe according to which he disposes, orders,
and preserves all things and uses them in an ordinary way, he can nevertheless
at his discretion invert this order, dispose of all these things otherwise,
and act beyond and above this natural order—and thus perform miracles.

By reason of this right and dominion, strictly considered in itself, God can
do whatever he wills to his creatures without even the least appearance of
injury. For injury presupposes some right or debt in the one to whom injury is
done. But in a creature considered in relation to God as Creator and absolute
Lord, there is no right and no debt; therefore no injury can be done here.
Hence, even if he should torment an undeserving creature in any manner, he
would do it no injury; there would be no injustice at all here, because there
is no debt, and therefore no right; and where there is no right, injury can
have no place. Justice, as is known, is concerned with *rendering each one his
own*. But in this state, what can a creature claim by any right as its own, so
that if it were taken away the creature could say injury was being done to it?
Who will deny that by this absolute dominion God can annihilate all creatures?
But if he can annihilate all, can he not also kill the living? If he can kill,
can he not do this either in an instant or over some longer period? And if the
latter, why not with some pain? And if with some degree of pain—say, four—why
not also six or ten, and so forth? Indeed, since it is lawful for us to
dispose at our own discretion of creatures placed under our power and to
torment them, no reason can establish why this should not also be lawful for
God as absolute Lord. Those fellow creatures which we torment at our own
discretion, without doing them any injury, have nothing from us—neither their
*being* nor their *existence*. But whatever we possess, both our being—

[1674 logical printed p. 43 | IA leaf 61 | PDF p. 61]

—and our existence, we ought to acknowledge as received from God alone. God,
according to his good pleasure, gave us being and such a being; he can in turn
take this away however it seems good to him and dispose of our being according
to his absolute good pleasure. See the very celebrated *Twisse's Vindication*,
book 2, part 1, charge 2, section 5, digression 1.

Although it is true that God borrows no right from creatures, since he is a
most perfect being in himself and independent, and therefore his right ought
not to be measured by anything in the creature except insofar as it has seemed
good to him to obligate and bind himself in such a manner, nevertheless I do
not see why this *right of absolute dominion* of which we are now speaking—
which differs from the *right of governing*, or from that dominion which
appears in government and is exercised by a rector as such—cannot be said to
be proximately founded in creation. For the question here concerns only the
proximate title of ownership or absolute dominion, not the right of governing.
And creation, which includes the total conservation of all things, which is
nothing other than continued creation, is the best title of this absolute
dominion. For in this work God has no partner and no helper, but he himself is
the first, total, and absolutely independent cause. Therefore, because he is
the Creator of all, he can rightly be said deservedly to claim for himself the
absolute power to do whatever he pleases to his creatures. For since he
created them, they can by the best right be said to be God's, and that most
properly. The Arminians, however, argue badly that God's right flows from
creation as a benefit, since by that right they understand not this absolute
dominion distinctly so called, but the power of governing. For a benefit as
such, whatever it requires by way of gratitude from the one to whom it is
given, does not on that account permit the powerful person who bestows the
benefit to claim public authority or a right of governing over the one upon
whom the benefit is conferred, as is well known.

To this also belongs that *right and dominion* which God has and exercises
even over rational creatures in the category of being: over their souls,
faculties, acts of will, and operations, namely, by freely moving, directing,
disposing, and ordering them at his own discretion; and that most wisely, most
powerfully, and most holily, as our theologians prove against the Pelagians and
the like, the most senseless enemies both of grace and of God's dominion. See
the Subtle *Bradwardine*, who in book 1 of *The Cause of God*, chapter 2,
proves *that God is the necessary preserver of all other things*; and that
immediately, in corollary 2 of the same chapter; indeed, *more immediately than
any created cause*, in corollary 3 of the same. In chapter 3 he proves that
God is the necessary efficient cause of every thing made. And in corollary 2,
that nothing can do anything unless God by himself and immediately does that
same thing; and in corollary 3, that he does so more immediately than any other
agent. So too in chapter 4 he proves that whenever any creature moves, God
necessarily co-moves: and that by himself and properly, in corollary 1;
immediately, in corollary 2; and more immediately than any other mover, in
corollary 3. Similarly, in chapter 27 he proves that all things occur by divine
providence. In chapter 31 he proves that even voluntary actions are subject to
divine providence. In chapter 32 he proves that all things come from God's
providence actually disposing, not merely permitting. Scripture also
demonstrates this clearly in Genesis 50:20; 2 Samuel 12:11–12 and 16:10; Psalm
127:1–2; Proverbs 16:1, 9; 19:21; and 21:1; Isaiah 10:5–7, 15; 45:6–7; and
47:11; Jeremiah 10:23; Amos 4:6; Acts 2:23; 4:28; and 17:28; Romans 9:17 and
11:36. Very many other testimonies of Scripture confirming this same thing
can be added. Hence it is evident that by the best right God can dispose,
direct, and order human wills and their actions, incline them this way—

[1674 logical printed p. 44 | IA leaf 62 | PDF p. 62]

—or that, and move them efficaciously, omnipotently, and irresistibly wherever
and whenever he wills.

These statements about the *right of dominion* will suffice. It follows that
we should say something about the *right of government*.

This *right of God's government* properly regards rational creatures (for we
are not speaking here of that metaphorical government by God of irrational
and inanimate creatures), and that in the moral category. It supposes that
they are free agents and social, yet capable of defect, and thus governable by
laws properly so called.

No sane person will deny that this right belongs to God alone. For it is he
who founded them and gave them such a being; he alone can also search hearts
and therefore govern them. Whatever government over other people he has
granted to human beings, therefore, he reserved the supreme government to
himself. He is thus the supreme Rector and Governor of all. Hence he is called
our one Legislator, James 4:12.

That he actually performs the office of Rector and governs rational creatures
by his laws is proclaimed by all reason, attested by everyone's conscience,
confirmed by the whole course of Scripture, proved by the established series
and order of things, demonstrated by the government exercised in some manner
among almost all nations, shown by the difference between human beings and
beasts, and partly demonstrated by us above in chapter 1.

Since it is certain that God alone can exercise this government—since he is
supreme and independent, omniscient, omnipresent, omnipotent, all-sufficient,
and best, and thus fit in every way to enact laws and put them into execution—
and that God is the supreme and absolute Lord of all rational beings, even
considered as such, there is no reason for us anxiously to inquire what the
immediate foundation of this right of God is. For it seems to result naturally
and necessarily from the fact that God, to whom supreme power belongs by
right, is the absolute Lord of the human being, or rational creature, that is
capable of being governed.

To this *government* by God belong *legislation, judgment, and execution*.

Here also shines forth *God's justice*: not that *universal* justice which is
the same as God's holiness and rectitude and is the perfection of God's wisdom
and goodness, but that special justice by which he enacts good and equitable
laws, justly governs and judges rational creatures according to those laws,
rewards those who are good and compliant, and punishes those who are
unmanageable and rebellious. This is not the place to inquire what God also
does here, or could do by his absolute dominion.

It must be investigated further what this *right of God* is with respect to
rational creatures. The very celebrated *Voetius* describes it for us in this
way in *On the Right and Justice of God*, page 340: *It is the idea of God's
rectitude and goodness concerning the government of rational creatures, with
the power of diffusing itself into law or precepts and through them directing
and obligating creatures.* *Paul Voetius*, son of Gisbert, in his commentary
on the Institutes, book 1, title 2, *On Justice and Right*, page 50, says that
*God's right is the rule of what is lawful and honorable in the divine mind,
according to which God obligates, or as it were obligates, himself with a view
to the direction of creatures or of himself*.

But insofar as *right* denotes the legitimate power to do or omit something
(in which sense it can not inconveniently be taken here), it can be defined
not badly in this way:

*Divine right is the supreme and independent power in God by which, by reason
of his supereminence and supreme authority and of his absolute dominion over
rational creatures created and preserved by him—*

[1674 logical printed p. 45 | IA leaf 63 | PDF p. 63]

*—both in their being and in such a being, he is, as it were, bound to direct
them and obligate them by laws necessary both absolutely and by hypothesis.*

Here *God's right* is limited to that right which concerns rational creatures
actually existing, and that in the moral category.

I say that *divine right is a power* because every right in general, taken in
this sense, is a right power adhering to the person to whom it belongs to do
or omit something, and so forth, as said above.

I say *a supreme and independent power* in order that it may be distinguished
from the power granted to human beings, which is dependent, subordinate, and
freely granted by God.

I say that God *can and actually is (as it were) bound*, and so forth, because,
just as every right grants to those who are the lawful subjects of that right
the ability to use it for that to which they have a right, so in certain cases
those to whom such a right belongs are bound to use that right, because in
that case the right implies an obligation, not mere permission. Given a
rational creature, therefore, and the other things to be considered having
been duly considered, we judge it necessary that God actually exercise this
right. Nevertheless, because I judge that he can only improperly be said to
be bound, I therefore said, *as it were, bound*.

I say *by reason of his supereminence*, and so forth, because I do not judge it
necessary to settle the controversy concerning what the immediate foundation
of this right is, since I take it as certain that all these things are required
and present here. For by reason of his supereminence and supreme authority,
God is fit actually to exercise government; because those creatures are
rational, they can be governed; and because God created them and preserves
them in their being and in such a being, he has absolute dominion over them,
which also is required to found this right.

I say *by laws necessary both absolutely and by hypothesis* because, as will
immediately appear, some of those laws are absolutely necessary—that is, so
necessary that, things standing thus, he could not have failed to enact them,
cannot even now entirely remove or abrogate them, and indeed cannot dispense
with them. But others flow freely from his mere good pleasure, with freedom
both of contrariety and of contradiction. He can change or entirely remove
these or dispense with them (taking the word broadly), at least with respect
to time.

*This divine right is commonly distinguished into a right natural to God and
a positive right.* The *former* is that according to which, from the necessity
of his nature, God cannot fail to prescribe and require from rational
creatures such duties as have opposites which would imply a contradiction,
because they are founded not in the divine will but in the perfection,
eminence, holiness, and rectitude of the divine nature. The *latter* is that
according to which, freely and from his mere good pleasure, God has prescribed
such duties as he either could have refrained from prescribing, or whose
opposites he could have willed and enjoined—antecedently, in the order of
reason, to the divine will—without any prejudice to his perfection and
holiness and without any implication of contradiction.

In this all agree: some precepts have so close a connection with God's nature
that, once a rational and governable creature is posited, God cannot fail to
be obligated to prescribe them. Such are that the creature *subject itself to
God, comply with God in all things, regard him alone as its God*, and the like.
But some precepts flow from God's free good pleasure. Yet in the particular
enumeration of both the former and the latter, all are not unanimous. For some
refer to this natural right things which others judge should rather be referred
to positive right. It would be far too long and foreign to our purpose—

[1674 logical printed p. 46 | IA leaf 64 | PDF p. 64]

—to involve ourselves in these controversies now; and occasion will be given
later to say something about some of them.

It will suffice now to propose certain general rules by which both those
things that are of *natural right* and those that are only of *positive right*
can be recognized. And so:

*First*, whatever things have so close a connection with God's nature,
perfection, and eminence that God cannot command their opposites without
prejudice to his nature, we can say are of divine natural right. For it is
certain that God cannot deny himself or his own nature, nor can he will that
it should be diminished in any manner. Hence God cannot command angels or
human beings to believe *that God is not one, is not infinite or omnipotent*,
and the like. For in commanding such things he would in some manner deny
himself and his nature, infinity, unity, omnipotence, and so forth. And since
he is the first truth, it is repugnant to the divine nature to command someone
to believe as true what is manifestly false. So too he cannot free a human
being from dependence, whether physical or moral, because this cannot be done
without prejudice to the divine nature. For this would be the same as saying
that God is not the one God, is not the first cause or the absolute Lord of
all, but that the creature is God. Hence:

*Second*, we can say that those things are of *divine natural right* whose
opposites are repugnant to the divine nature. Thus, because it is repugnant to
the divine nature that God should not be the absolute Lord of his creatures,
not be the supreme Commander, and not be the one and most simple essence, he
cannot command that another be regarded as God or that the creature not obey
its commanding Creator. Similarly, because it is repugnant to say that the
first identity is not identical, God cannot command us to believe that
contradictories are not simultaneously true—namely, that the same is not the
same. For to say *the same is not the same* will finally resolve into this:
*the first identity is not identical*, which is blasphemous.

*Third*, whenever morality, whether complex or incomplex, flows from God's
nature—as does the moral truth of the propositions *God is one*, *God is
essentially good*, and the like—*God's natural right* has place in these
things. For it is not from God's will that these propositions are true, but
from God's nature itself. *Nor is God's free will* (says the very celebrated
*Rutherford* in *On Providence*, chapter 22, page 338) *properly the cause why
God commands that such things be believed. Therefore the moral rectitude in
these things believed by us is originally in God. For we believe that God is
infinitely good because he is so rather than because God freely commands us
to believe it, or at least because God commands us to believe that God is so,
and so forth. For God really is such in himself, and we ought not to believe
that God is such in himself because God has so commanded, as though God's
command were the original cause of that moral rectitude which is in the act
of believing. Rather, it is because there really is conformity between the
object or thing believed and faith. For God cannot command us to believe the
opposite, or that the matter is so when it is not so.*

*Fourth*, whatever things imply a contradiction if commanded belong here.
Such are these: *A rational creature is not bound to tend toward God as the
best good*, and the like. For it is repugnant that this should be so; it is
repugnant for a rational creature to be a creature and not subject to God, or
to be such a rational creature and not be bound to seek God as the highest
good. So too it involves a contradiction for God to command hatred of himself
as a morally good thing, since it is foundationally evil. Therefore he cannot
command anyone to believe that the devil is God, because this is essentially—

[1674 logical printed p. 47 | IA leaf 65 | PDF p. 65]

—evil, since it is essentially false and intrinsically involves a
contradiction—namely, that there are several gods, that the devil is God, or
that God proposes as true what is essentially and intrinsically false. So too
it is repugnant that a rational creature should not be bound to comply in all
things with God and its Creator. For it is a contradiction that a created
being should exist and not be subject to its Creator, because of God's
primality and independence and the creature's inferiority and dependence. Such
a being would be both created and uncreated, dependent and independent, which
is repugnant.

*Fifth*, moreover, there are certain common precepts which cannot be changed,
such as these: *One must not act unjustly*; *one must not sin against God*; and
the like, which are said to be of eternal truth. These indeed pertain to God's
natural right, so that God can no more command the contrary than command or
bring it about that a human being is not a rational animal or that each thing
is not what it is.

*Sixth*, there are certain *rationally prior things*, as Bradwardine speaks in
*The Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 21, which are such antecedently to the
divine will and can move it, such as *God exists*, *God is eternal*, and the
like. These can well be reduced to *divine natural right*.

*Seventh*, furthermore, those things in which there is an intrinsic and
essential connection can also be referred here, as when it is said, *Hatred
of God in a rational and intelligent creature is sin*, and, *It is equitable
that a creature subject itself to its Creator*, and the like. Here the
morality resulting from this intrinsic connection cannot be changed.

*Eighth*, some distinguish between the things God commands as God and the
things he commands as Legislator. They hold that the former are of natural
right but the latter of legal right, as Bradwardine speaks in the place cited,
page 231.

*Ninth*, those things which they say have an essential connection with the
very creation of a rational human being, and thus are co-created, can also be
referred here. The very celebrated *Rutherford* explains this in the place
cited, page 570: *When God created the angelic or human nature, in that same
act he co-created the intellect's tendency toward the first and most
delightful fountal truth, and the will's and affections' tendency toward the
purest ocean of the first lovable one.* And a little afterward: *Nor does it
follow that, once God founds a rational creature, it is properly free for God,
or that God can will, to command or not command that a human being should not
know God, should possess or retain no theology, natural synteresis and
conscience, or should suppress or condemn God's truth or cause in
unrighteousness. For creation essentially includes the co-engraving of natural
law. In the same act in which a human being is created, a creature possessing
synteresis and natural conscience is created precisely because it is rational.
And thus, given the supposed existence of a human being, it would follow that
God could will to command that a human being not retain natural conscience
after, in the act of creating, he had commanded the person to retain
synteresis and natural conscience. This must not be said. Indeed, from God's
natural dominion which he possesses over existing rational creatures, there
results the creature's natural subjection to the Creator, because servants are
related to a lord and creatures to the Creator.*

*Tenth*, those things which are good antecedently to God's free good pleasure
because of a fittingness to God which follows his nature and natural mode of
acting also pertain here. He therefore cannot command hatred of himself, or
that a creature believe that God does not exist, or similar horrible acts, as
the very celebrated Rutherford speaks.

*Eleventh*, similar are those things which God cannot approve without a
contradiction, for whatever he commands he also approves. See Rutherford in
the place cited, page 582.

On the other hand, some rules are laid down by certain authors which do not
appear so clear. One is when they say, *That is natural to God which human
beings naturally perceive in God or recognize by the power of natural
conscience.* This assertion, I say, is not so evident as to compel our assent,
because human beings can naturally perceive and by the power of their natural
conscience recognize God as beneficent, while God bestows those external good
things upon his creatures not naturally but freely; he freely causes the sun
to rise upon the just and unjust.

Nor is what some bring forward valid when they say that our rational nature
is the rule of this immutable morality. For this nature is at most only a
regulated rule, and whatever it has, it has from God. What, was it unfitting
to Abraham's rational nature to slay his own son? Yet we know that this was
commanded him, and therefore it was good and just at that time.

Nor does it hold that those things which reproduce God's image in a human
being pertain here, for the foregoing reason. For it was part of God's image
in *Abraham* to cherish and preserve his son's life, yet he was commanded to
kill him.

Nor does it hold that natural right is that whose obligation arises from the
nature of the thing. For sometimes that nature of the thing resolves into
God's will itself, and thus the first foundation of this obligation will be
the divine will. See *Voetius* in the place cited, page 346.

From what has been said we can easily recognize those things which pertain to
*divine positive right*: namely, all those of which it cannot be said that God
was constrained by the necessity of his nature to one side of the
contradiction, or in which we know that God can make or has made some change
in the obligation, whether this was a dispensation—that is, the revocation of
a precept in some case—the abrogation or derogation of a law, an
interpretation, an equitable relaxation, a variation of the matter or
circumstances, or anything of that kind.

*To this belong* (says *Voetius* in the place cited, page 343) *the permission
of sin, and so forth. To these add the symbolic law concerning the tree of the
knowledge of good and evil laid down for Adam in Paradise, as well as the
whole ceremonial law and the Mosaic forensic law, and, furthermore, a great
part of the Decalogue. In the first table this includes the positively
instituted mode of worship—for example, through the external preaching of the
word, and so forth. In the second, it includes monogamy, prohibited degrees,
distinctions of ownership, the magistrate's power and homicide, and so forth.*
To these can be added the institution of the Sabbath, or of the seventh day
in number, whose observance is commanded in the fourth precept of the
Decalogue, as we shall make plain afterward.
