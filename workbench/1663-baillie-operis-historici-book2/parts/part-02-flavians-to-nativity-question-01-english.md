This most grievous hand of the Lord upon the Jews was the just vengeance for the crucifixion of Christ and the despising of the Gospel, according to Christ’s manifest prediction in Matthew 24. Yet the Romans’ excessive cruelty toward them did not please God; hence very great disasters were inflicted upon the Romans in those times. Indeed, Vespasian and Titus themselves (although otherwise excellent emperors) did not escape God’s scourge. The father’s rule lasted only a few years, scarcely twelve, and the son’s far fewer. Titus, in the flower of youth and the second year of his reign, was removed either by fever or by the plots of his brother Domitian; and soon the whole stock of Vespasian was rooted out in Domitian’s wretched and violent death.

While divine fury pursued the unbelieving Jews everywhere, the Lord’s fatherly mercy embraced the Christians. Warned by that divine oracle (as Eusebius relates), they all left Jerusalem shortly before the siege and withdrew to Pella beyond the Jordan, where by the Lord’s right hand they were preserved in peace amid that most savage storm.

Vespasian, the tenth of the Caesars, was staying at Alexandria when he learned that Vitellius had been killed at Rome. When he entered the city, the Senate, people, and praetorians received him with open arms. His first concern was to restore the commonwealth, which had collapsed through the vices of his predecessors. Much money was needed to accomplish this. He gathered it from every quarter, even to the disgrace of avarice and sordidness, but spent what he acquired exceedingly well: most sparing in private expenditure, most liberal in public buildings and in relieving the poverty of people and senators whose fortunes had fallen. He abstained from every war so far as he could. He built a splendid temple to Peace and placed in it the law, the sacred vessels of the Jerusalem temple, and the other spoils of the Jews; he engraved their forms on the triumphal arch at the foot of the Palatine, which remains at Rome to this day. He was also exceedingly averse to shedding even the blood of those who provoked him; but he indulged in jokes and taunts to the point of buffoonery. What the Magdeburg Centuriators, Baronius, and other recent writers have concerning him is all taken from Suetonius; Dio—at least his epitomator Xiphilinus—adds scarcely anything. In the sixty-ninth year of his age and the twelfth of his reign he died of dysentery at the villa where he was born. At the end he scarcely lay in bed, but attended to public business in his accustomed manner with this saying: *An emperor ought to die standing.* Although here and there some ministers of the church suffered martyrdom under him at the hands of a raging mob, he himself instituted no persecution of Christians; he did, however, expel the pagan philosophers from Rome as corrupters of the people.

Titus, the eleventh of the Caesars, had all but ruled jointly with his father under the title of consul. Before his own reign many strongly suspected him of avarice, lust, cruelty, and other vices; but as soon as he alone obtained the empire, he at once appeared utterly foreign to vice. He regarded Berenice, sister of King Agrippa and a most excellent woman, almost as a lawful wife; but when he learned that the people disliked a marriage with a Jewess, though unwilling himself he dismissed her unwilling. So far was he from cruelty that he wished to become chief pontiff precisely so that he might stain his hands with no one’s blood. Such was his inclination to generosity that, if he had passed a day without doing good to anyone, he used to declare at supper, *My friends, we have lost a day.*

The notable public disasters of his two years supplied ample occasion to display his ready kindness toward all the afflicted. Mount Vesuvius in Campania suddenly vomited so many flames, rocks, and ashes that it buried several neighboring cities and many people, and soon stirred up so great a pestilence that it destroyed very many families in Rome and throughout Italy. Fire from heaven also kindled that blaze in the center of Rome which for three full days reduced several districts of the city and many public buildings to ashes. This, I think, was divine vengeance for the burning of Jerusalem. At any rate, Titus applied all his strength to repairing these public losses. While occupied in these things, fate crushed him—to the people’s great relief and consolation—in the forty-first year of his life and second of his reign, whether by poison from his brother Domitian or by fever from God.

Domitian, the twelfth of the Caesars and a young man of thoroughly depraved character, had repeatedly plotted against both father and brother, impatiently gaping after empire. When he had sat upon the throne for a year or two, he inspired great hope of every virtue. He punished judicial corruption so severely that magistrates both in the city and in the provinces were fairer and more restrained under him than usual. For a time he showed no sign of avarice but great signs of generosity. He was a stern avenger of adultery and every impurity, especially among the Vestals. He dedicated an hour each day to solitary meditation, but spent it all—

—killing flies. His skill in archery was such that from some distance he could shoot between the spread fingers of a hand without injury. But soon his concealed depravity broke out.

He killed many of the highest nobility so that their property might serve his extravagant expenditure, and wished to be addressed not only as Lord but even as God. He poured out his wrath upon philosophers and Jews, and instituted the second persecution of Christians after Nero. He killed his kinsman, the consul Clement, as though guilty of impiety, and also the former consul Acilius Glabrio on the same charge. Baronius would have both men be Christians, but, as I think, without any competent witness. It is certain that at Domitian’s command John the Evangelist was sent to Patmos and was released from there by a decree of Nerva. But Baronius proves no more that John was thrown into a vat of boiling oil at Rome than he proves that Peter was crucified by Nero because by his prayers he cast Simon Magus, flying through the air, down to earth—first breaking his legs, then causing his death—or that Paul was struck with the axe because he had turned one of Nero’s concubines from his company. Recent authors of legendary tales are not competent witnesses to any truth; neither Roman martyrologies nor Greek menologies carry much credibility. In any event, Domitian at last became a victim of public hatred through his crimes.

One of the naked little boys with whom he used to associate took from his bed a paper listing the names of those marked for death and ran playfully here and there through the palace holding it. Domitian’s wife met him and, astonished on reading the paper on which her own and many others’ names were written, immediately summoned those condemned to die. A conspiracy was formed. They determined to anticipate the tyrant and, after many had refused, scarcely persuaded Nerva to accept the empire once the tyrant was killed. When he had at last consented, the tribune Stephanus was admitted very early by the chamberlain Terennius with a written accusation of conspiracy. As Domitian read it with closer attention, Stephanus drove a dagger into his groin; and while Domitian wrestled with Stephanus, Terennius and others finished him with seven wounds in the forty-fifth year of his age and fifteenth of his reign. His nurse Phyllis stole away the corpse and buried it in an ordinary bier and tomb; by decree of the Senate his images and inscriptions were broken everywhere.

Although mathematical studies were banished from all Italy together with the philosophers, they flourished at court. Vespasian mocked the young Domitian for abstaining from the mushrooms that had been fatal to Claudius and warned him that he had to fear iron, not mushrooms. Not only the day but the hour of his fate was announced to him; with great fear and every precaution he tried in vain to avoid it. When Ascletarion admitted that he had predicted Domitian’s fate and was asked what fate the stars foretold for himself, he replied that he would be torn apart by dogs. Domitian immediately ordered Ascletarion strangled and carefully buried. But while the body was being burned, a tremendous storm arose and scattered those attending the burial; meanwhile dogs tore apart the half-burned body. Apollonius of Tyana, a most loathsome magician, was declaiming among his followers at Ephesus at the very instant of the emperor’s death. Exulting like a madman, he cried, *Strike the tyrant, strike!* Then, suddenly quiet as though his wish had been fulfilled, he announced that the tyrant had been killed.

Cocceius Nerva entered upon the empire at sixty years of age and diligently applied himself to repairing its ruins. He recalled those whom Domitian had driven into exile and forbade prosecution for the charge of impiety against either Jews or Christians. The praetorians despised his age, and he could not prevent them from killing Domitian’s assassins. To restrain their insolence he adopted Trajan, then governor of Belgium, who had been born at Italica, a city of Baetican Spain sixteen miles from Seville. While inveighing too bitterly against an abusive senator, the very violence of his anger killed the old man in the second year of his reign.

Trajan began to reign in the forty-second year of his life and, according to Baronius, the hundredth year of Christ. He administered the empire with great praise for nineteen years, except that he stirred up the third persecution of Christians, in which very many received the crown of martyrdom. Among them Ignatius was led in chains from Antioch to Rome and thrown to lions in the theater. John the Evangelist died peacefully at Ephesus in Trajan’s second year. Baronius himself rejects what the writers of legends tell from apocryphal books concerning John’s grave and death.

Domitian’s inertia had raised Decebalus, prince of modern Transylvania and Wallachia, to great boldness against Rome. When he grew insolent toward his neighbors, Trajan crushed him with great slaughter and imposed hard terms of peace upon him in subjection. The proud man could not bear them, and war broke out again. Trajan went a second time into Dacia and in a few days laid an astonishing bridge of squared stone across the Danube, with twenty arches, each 120 feet high, sixty broad, and 160 distant from its neighbor. After defeating Decebalus in several battles, he finally killed him, reduced Dacia to a province, fished the king’s treasures from their hiding-place on the bed of the Danube, and displayed them in triumph.

When the Parthians disturbed the East he turned his arms there. He reduced Armenia to a province, crossed the Euphrates and Tigris, captured Nisibis, Ctesiphon, and Seleucia, and everywhere routed and put the Parthians to flight. In the north he subjected Colchians, Alans, Iberians, Sarmatians, and others; he even extended his boundless ambition to the conquest of India—

—but when he had experienced the harshness of climate and soil at the mouths of the Euphrates and Tigris and in Arabia, and age was growing heavy upon him, he recalled his mind from that vanity.

The Roman dominion attained its widest extent under him. His successor Hadrian, a prudent man, contracted it to Augustus’ earlier boundaries—the Euphrates, Danube, and Rhine—and abandoned the provinces Trajan had subdued beyond them. Hadrian also demolished the Danube bridge lest it give the barbarians entry into the empire, leaving only a few columns as a monument to its astonishing power.

While Trajan wintered at Antioch during the Parthian War, God took stern vengeance for the killing of the martyr Ignatius and his other servants. For very many days a terrible earthquake leveled most of the city with an innumerable slaughter of people. Trajan barely escaped by throwing himself from the window of a collapsing building into the fields, where he spent several days in tents.

In the last years of his reign the Jews displayed their customary madness, especially in Egypt, Cyrene, and Cyprus. In a most cruel fury they attacked Romans and Greeks together and, as Dio reckons, killed more than 460,000; but Trajan soon restrained them with a still greater slaughter. He moderated the persecution of Christians when letters from Pliny the Younger and other provincial governors informed him not only of their great numbers everywhere but also of their great innocence and holiness, if their single flight from the worship of idols were excepted. While returning from the East to celebrate a triumph at Rome, he died at Selinus in Cilicia in the sixty-third year of his life and nineteenth of his reign. By a will substituted by his wife Plotina, Hadrian—son of Aelius Hadrianus Afer, Trajan’s cousin, born at Rome but sprung from Italica in Baetican Spain, Trajan’s own city—was named successor.

Hadrian was a thoroughly distinguished man, had he not stirred up the fourth persecution of Christians in which many martyrs were killed. Yet he desisted after hearing the apology of Quadratus and others. At the beginning of his reign he also ordered several very noble men killed as conspirators against him, and at its end Severianus, his ninety-year-old brother-in-law, together with his grandson Fuscus, because they resented being neglected in the adoption of Antoninus. For these killings he was less pleasing to the people than Trajan, though otherwise averse to the blood of citizens. He was distinguished equally for liberality, prudence, gravity, learning, and courage. He had no need of war, so greatly did subjects and neighbors alike revere him. He exercised the troops vigorously in the ancient discipline and spent most of his years traversing the Roman world, correcting and restoring roads, harbors, and public buildings in Spain, Gaul, Germany, Pannonia, Greece, Asia, and Egypt. In Britain, with great labor and expense, he constructed that long wall of 200 miles from the mouth of the Tyne to the British Ocean to restrain the Scots and Picts from incursions into the province.

Among other cities he rebuilt Carthage and Jerusalem. Baronius would have Jerusalem rebuilt after the Jews had been defeated, but Dio—whom Baronius and others commonly follow in most matters—expressly says that the Jews were provoked to war because Hadrian had planted a colony of pagans at Jerusalem and built temples to Roman idols in their sacred city. This Jewish war against the Romans lasted several years with great slaughter on both sides. It was not ended until Julius Severus, summoned from Britain, broke that most obstinate people more by skill than by force. Fifty strongly fortified places were taken and overthrown, nearly a thousand important villages destroyed, and 580,000 fighting men killed, apart from the innumerable multitude that perished by famine, fire, and disease. The Jewish leader Bar Kokhba, “son of the star,” wished to be regarded as the true Messiah—that star, of course, which arose from Jacob. Rabbi Akiba, the most celebrated teacher of that age, abandoned his school and offered himself as armor-bearer to that false Christ, until both met a bloody death. This immense disaster did not open the madmen’s eyes so that they might acknowledge their sins against the true Christ; they got only so far as to call Bar Kokhba *Ben Kozba*, “son of the lie.” Certainly, because of their most stubborn rebellion against God and men, Hadrian forbade the Jews, under severe penalties, every entry into Judea and every sight of Jerusalem, by a law that continued for several centuries after his death. To frighten them still more from the city he stamped upon its gates a pig, an animal detestable to Jews. To exclude Christians as well from those formerly holy places, he built a temple to Jupiter on the site of the Passion and one to Adonis on the site of the Nativity.

When Severianus was being killed, he called the gods to witness his innocence and prayed that Hadrian, when he most wished it, might not be allowed to die. Hadrian certainly wished for death many times before he could die. After adopting Aurelius Antoninus and making him adopt Lucius Commodus and Marcus Verus, he suffered from a most painful dropsy and longed for death, repeatedly asking for poison and a sword. But Antoninus preserved him from death so far as he could, thereby acquiring the surname Pius. At last, however, on his physicians’ advice Hadrian killed himself by fasting and by deliberately choosing unsuitable foods, in the sixty-second year of his life, twentieth of his reign, and 140th of Christ, according to the common computation which we follow in the lives of the emperors.

Antoninus Pius, the sixteenth emperor, was a man of the greatest virtues, surpassing even Trajan and Hadrian. Under him there were no wars, robberies, or killings. Neighboring barbarians submitted their disputes with one another to his justice. Throughout the Roman world one could travel as in one’s own country, without fear of bandits or pirates, with the same language and the same laws everywhere.

When Christians here and there were put to death by pagans without an imperial edict, the emperor heard Justin’s Apology for the Christians and forbade further persecution. Unlike Hadrian, he always remained at Rome, thinking that the movement of the court burdened the provinces and that the people revolted more easily when the emperor was far away. In accordance with Hadrian’s arrangement he adopted Marcus Aurelius Antoninus, the true philosopher, and Lucius Verus. He died at seventy, in the twenty-fifth year of his reign.

Marcus Aurelius, the seventeenth emperor, a man of every kind of virtue and a Stoic philosopher in both life and profession, upheld the Roman world in the most difficult times. In most matters he administered the commonwealth excellently at home and at war, but the character of his colleague Lucius was very different. He gave him his daughter Lucilla in marriage. When the Parthian Vologeses pressed him with arms, he sent Lucius, a sufficiently vigorous man, to the East. Lucius prepared at Antioch everything needed for war and sent Avidius Cassius against the enemy. Cassius broke the Parthians in many battles, burned the royal cities Seleucia and Ctesiphon, and compelled the Parthians to supplicate the emperors for peace upon whatever terms they pleased.

While Lucius was in the East, he severely persecuted Christians, and Christ’s wrath consequently blazed against the Romans. When soldiers at Persepolis opened a golden box dug from a tomb, such pestilential air breathed from it that the whole army was infected with a terrible plague. It spread throughout the Roman empire, especially Rome and Italy, with an infinite slaughter. When the plague had weakened Roman strength, the Marcomanni, Quadi, Sarmatians, and a vast northern rabble of barbarians crossed the Danube and Rhine and flooded the empire. Marcus had become sickly from excessive literary study, yet dared neither entrust this war to Lucius nor leave so dissolute a man at Rome, and therefore took him to war. Before they left Italy, while traveling together in one carriage through the Venetian territory, Lucius fell dead of apoplexy. Some attributed this to Marcus’ treachery, which Julius Capitolinus declares utterly foreign to his integrity.

Proceeding against the barbarians, Marcus struggled for several years against both a hard enemy and hard conditions. At last a numerous army of Quadi enclosed him in most unfavorable ground, bringing him close to utter destruction; heat and thirst in particular were killing the legions, while the terrain allowed neither battle nor flight. When commander and soldiers were losing heart, the Melitene legion from a city of Armenia, entirely Christian, knelt and raised its hands to heaven, imploring Christ for help. At once a great rain refreshed the Romans, while lightning and a mighty blast of wind driven into the faces of the Quadi brought them complete victory. Marcus therefore wrote the Senate a most friendly letter on behalf of the Christians. Dio is rightly censured by his epitomator Xiphilinus for transferring this miracle from the Thundering Legion to an Egyptian magician. Despite the emperor’s letter, persecution continued through the malice of governors and the severity of ancient laws against new religions; besides Polycarp and Justin, very many other martyrs received the ultimate punishment.

Vengeance for this cruelty soon shone forth against the emperor himself, his family, and the whole empire. Although he cultivated every virtue as much as any pagan, as his Greek memoir of his own life testifies, his wife Faustina, daughter of Antoninus Pius, became with his connivance the foulest of all women since Messalina. He discovered that his son Commodus, upon whose education he had spent the utmost care, was a monster of the most depraved disposition; grief of mind over this wasted him away.

While Marcus was occupied in the Marcomannic War, Faustina roused Cassius in the East to seize the empire. She feared that, if the sickly Marcus died, her son’s character would destroy him, her, and the whole family. She therefore contrived that Cassius should gain the throne and that through marriage to him she might retain her former dignity. Marcus bore even this from Faustina, but flew against Cassius into Syria, merely celebrating along the road at Rome a triumph over the Germans. Before they met in battle, Cassius’ own men cut off his head, against Marcus’ wishes, for Marcus intended to preserve the defeated enemy’s life and rank. Faustina died on the journey as he returned with the army to Rome.

He was not permitted repose even in old age. Rebellious Germans compelled him to take up arms again; after struggling with them for three years he died in camp, either of fever or through fraud of the physicians on Commodus’ behalf, in the fifty-eighth year of his life, eighteenth of his reign, and 181st of Christ.

Commodus, the eighteenth Caesar, was immediately acclaimed emperor in camp and made peace with the Germans. A handsome young man descended from the excellent emperors Hadrian, Antoninus, and Aurelius, he was received everywhere along the road to Rome—toward which he hurried in his impatience with war—almost as one of the gods. —casting himself openly and headlong into every vice, he incurred such hatred that his own sister Lucilla, her husband Pompeianus, and most of the nobility conspired against him. Quadratus, who had undertaken to strike the emperor, brandished his dagger from a distance and was run through by the soldiers before he could stab him. This opened to Commodus a broad gate for killing senators.

Perennis, Commodus’ eye and praetorian prefect, had grown immensely rich from the goods of the proscribed and conceived designs upon the empire; he was removed. The same fortune met others who later held his place in the emperor’s favor. Cleander was torn apart by a rioting crowd before Commodus’ eyes.

At last Marcia, his favorite mistress, saw her own name in the list of those to be killed. After consulting the praetorian prefect Laetus she gave the emperor poison. When it struggled against the vigor of his youth and she feared he would survive, Marcia sent in the athlete Narcissus, who strangled the wretch. It was given out among the people that he had died of apoplexy from gluttony.

At times he wished to be called Hercules and walked the streets with a club and lion skin; at others, because of his love for Marcia, he preferred the dress of an Amazon. He ruled twelve years and lived thirty-two. His skill and power with the bow were such that in the theater he killed lions and every kind of beast with the shots he intended, never needing a second.

Herodian and Dio, excellent writers contemporary with Commodus, relate many things differently in his history. Baronius rightly observes—and this especially deserves notice—that Christians suffered very many things during the life of the excellent Marcus Aurelius but nothing at all in the days of the most cruel tyrant Commodus. Divine mercy brought this about, whether through the favor shown the church by his concubine Marcia or rather because the tyrant daily inspired his prospective assassins with fear for themselves, leaving them no leisure to trouble others.

Pertinax, son of a freedman, learned arithmetic as a boy for his father’s trade, then taught grammar, pleaded cases, and entered the army. He rose through every rank in Syria, Britain, Dacia, and Lower Germany. Aurelius appointed him governor of Syria and Asia, then the greatest military office. He spent some time in exile under Commodus, but was recalled and sent to Britain to suppress mutinies. His companions were killed there, and he hid among the corpses as though dead; soon, however, after killing the rebels he left the army peaceful and went to govern Africa. Returning to Rome he became prefect of the city. One midnight, while daily expecting death from Commodus, he was told that Laetus was at hand. Certain that he would die, he lay fearless and calmly ordered the approaching Laetus to execute his lord’s commands, which he had long expected. Instead he heard that Commodus was dead and, scarcely persuaded by Marcia’s and Laetus’ entreaties to accept the empire, was received in the Senate by everyone’s acclaim, a seventy-year-old man and the nineteenth emperor.

His first care was to restrain praetorian insolence; in all else he was so kind that everyone loved him. When the barbarians heard that Pertinax ruled, fear of the man made them everywhere lay down their arms. But praetorian treachery did not allow the excellent man to reign four full months. When some mutineers were punished, others at once rushed armed into the palace. Pertinax thought it dishonorable to flee into the city or hide, came out unarmed, and subdued the first by the majesty of his speech; those pressing behind stabbed him. Thus a wise, brave, beloved emperor was deprived of life and power at midday in Rome by about three hundred soldiers, and that unavenged.

The praetorians at first kept within their camp for fear of the people, but soon shouted from the towers that the empire was for sale. Sulpitianus, prefect of the city and Pertinax’s father-in-law, was present. News of the bidding was brought to the immensely rich senator Didius Julianus while he dined, and his guests drove him to the auction. At the camp he offered more than Sulpitianus, was heard, drawn up a ladder into the camp, and adored as emperor. Armed men led him to the Senate and then the palace. Wherever he appeared the people cursed the merchant; he bore the insults without anger. When he failed to pay the soldiers what he had promised, they soon hated him. The Pannonian legions declared Septimius Severus emperor, and the Syrian legions Pescennius Niger. Severus hurried into Italy. Julianus prepared to meet him, but the praetorians, fitter for mutiny than battle and softened by long peace, lost heart. Severus refused Julianus as colleague. At last the Senate, seeing Julianus helpless, deposed him as unworthy and proclaimed Severus emperor. Assassins entered the palace, found Julianus weeping with his friends, and beheaded him in his seventh month.

Severus, the twenty-first emperor, was born at Lepcis in Africa, a learned and eloquent man who had passed through the whole course of both military and civil offices. He hurried to Rome with utmost speed. Before entering he ordered the praetorians to meet him unarmed, surrounded them with his legions, and commanded them to lay down daggers and military cloaks. He then pronounced sentence: they were to remove their belts and go into exile one hundred miles from Rome.

His next concern was to hasten against Niger. But lest Albinus occupy Rome with the powerful British legions during his absence, by the Senate’s advice he made him Caesar and colleague. After many battles with Niger’s commanders in Asia Minor, at last in a dreadful engagement near Issus on the Cilician coast he routed all Niger’s forces—

—and, once Niger was removed so that he might rule alone, turned to overthrow Albinus. They met at Lyons in Gaul and fought most stubbornly for the whole day. Severus long had the worse; his horse was killed under him, he fell, and was regarded as dead. But Laetus charged with fresh troops, mounted Severus, and routed Albinus’ men with immense slaughter. Albinus was captured at Lyons and stabbed. His head was fixed on a spear in the forum, and Severus, mounted on a fierce horse, trampled his corpse. He exercised his victory most cruelly. After his triumph at Rome he killed and confiscated the property of many noble men and women as though they had favored Niger or Albinus. Yet he strove to be most generous to friends and people and provided costly shows, feasts, and buildings. Though troubled by age and arthritis he again crossed to the East with the utmost speed, seeking full vengeance on Niger’s adherents. Armenia admitted whatever terms he chose. The Parthian Artabanus, after a long siege in Ctesiphon, fled when the Romans broke in by force, leaving his children and treasure as spoil. Severus subdued rebellious Arabia and visited the pyramids of Egypt on his return. Here he instituted open persecution of Christians. Very many throughout the empire were crowned by martyrdom, although in that age many learned men defended Christian innocence in excellent writings, among them Pantaenus, Clement of Alexandria, Tertullian, Irenaeus, Minucius Felix, and others.

Divine vengeance upon Severus did not long delay. His young sons Bassianus and Geta, both of whom he had named emperors with himself, burned with irreconcilable mutual hatred. Plautianus enjoyed Severus’ favor; Severus made him praetorian prefect and at last colleague in the empire, and gave Plautianus’ daughter Plautilla to Bassianus. Because Bassianus despised her, Plautianus resolved to kill father and son and entrusted the deed to a praetorian tribune. The tribune disclosed the plot to the incredulous Severus. But when in the dead of night he brought an armored Plautianus into the emperor’s chamber as though to witness the completed deed, the emperor believed and ordered Plautianus removed.

To draw his sons from Rome and occasions of strife, the moment he heard of danger to the British legions he flew there. Though old and sickly, after a bloody and dangerous war made harder by the terrain, and the loss of 50,000 soldiers, he restrained Scots and Picts, excluding them from the province by a wall of thirty-two miles from sea to sea, from Abercorn to Patrick’s Isle. With the work scarcely complete he died at York, aged sixty-six, in the eighteenth year of his reign and the 203rd of Christ. He left at Rome enough wine, oil, and grain for five years, besides immense money, although throughout his life he had spent greatly upon arms and public works. He had a huge body worthy of empire, ate scarcely any meat, and drank deeply without drunkenness.

By Severus’ will Bassianus (called Caracalla from a kind of garment he gave the people), Marcia’s son, and Geta, Julia’s son, ruled together. On the road to Rome their discord advanced so far that they used separate tables, lodgings, and retinues. The same hatred continued at Rome, and they met only in the Senate. Julia, as the common mother, made every effort at reconciliation. Literary men surrounded Geta, soldiers Bassianus; each openly had his party and favored his own in every crime. At last, when secret plans failed, Bassianus unexpectedly burst in at midday as Geta dined safely in the palace and stabbed his brother in Julia’s embrace. He then flew from the city to the camp like a madman, pretending victory over a plotting Geta, and accused his brother in the Senate. Geta was condemned as a traitor. Everyone in Senate, army, or people who had favored him was removed. Hence arose immense hatred of Bassianus; only the praetorians, won by gifts and indulgence, favored him. Consciousness of crime drove him from the city through the provinces: in Germany he wore German dress, in Thrace was a second Alexander surrounded by a phalanx, and at Troy a new Achilles. Visiting Alexandria and hearing that he was mocked in jokes, he ordered his soldiers to massacre countless people. Pretending a marriage with Artabanus’ daughter, he inflicted a great defeat upon the Parthians.

Amid these things he wrote to the city prefect Maternianus at Rome to summon astrologers and inquire into his fate. Maternianus replied that Macrinus was plotting. As Bassianus hastened to a chariot race, the letter was given to Macrinus, prefect of the legions, to read. Seeing inevitable destruction prepared for him, Macrinus resolved to strike first. He ordered the praetorian centurion Martialis to execute the deed, knowing the hatred he bore the emperor for an injury. When the emperor withdrew beside the road to relieve nature, Martialis ran up as though summoned and stabbed him from behind. The fleeing assassin was cut down by the soldiers without revealing Macrinus’ plot.

Bassianus was killed in his sixth year of rule, forty-third of life, and 219th of Christ. While this most cruel beast reigned, the churches enjoyed great peace—not so much because his nurse was a Christian as because God saw fit to give his people calm after the great storm under Severus, turning the emperor’s fury upon the Romans, Alexandrians, and other persecutors of the godly.

After his death the army debated a successor. Everyone’s votes demanded Adventius, but the prudent man obstinately refused. The empire was therefore offered to Macrinus, who miserably embraced it with both arms. He fought Artabanus for two consecutive days with uncertain result; but when Artabanus learned that Bassianus was dead and Macrinus offered to release the prisoners without ransom, peace was easily made.

While Macrinus lingered at Antioch intent upon pleasure, the homesick army grew weary of him and impatient with delay. Moesa, sister of the empress Julia, had long lived in the Roman palace and had two daughters, Sohaemia and Mammaea. Mammaea’s son was Alexander and Sohaemia’s Heliogabalus, both of Syrian fathers; the latter was born at Emesa near Lebanon, the former at nearby Arca. After acquiring immense wealth at Rome, Moesa built at Emesa a splendid temple to the Sun, called Elagabal—not from a Hebrew word for sun, but from the Syriac *Ela*, God, and *Gabal*, the Phoenician city. She made her grandsons priests of the Sun, Sohaemia’s elder boy aged fourteen, Mammaea’s younger boy twelve. When several legions camped in that region and frequently visited the temple and boy-priests, Moesa, long acquainted with Roman ways, pretended both boys were Bassianus’ sons and gave the troops much money. Filled with hope of more, they immediately seized the elder, Heliogabalus, and proclaimed him emperor.

Macrinus at first despised the matter and sent Julianus to kill him. But while Julianus besieged the camp, his own men beheaded him and joined the rebels. Roused by this blow, Macrinus attacked with all his strength, but was defeated in open battle and fled toward Rome. Illness forced him to bed at Chalcedon, where pursuers from Heliogabalus killed father and son, though the son too was Caesar.

Heliogabalus, the twenty-fourth emperor, was a most beautiful youth of fourteen. When he reached Rome he became a dreadful monster of every luxury. In the palace he established a senate of prostitutes and addressed them in female dress, beginning, “Fellow soldiers.” He wore a golden, jeweled robe only once; his fingers were full of rings which, once removed, he never resumed; he never drank twice from the same golden cup. His beds were covered in gold and strewn with flowers, amber, musk, even gems. The meanest vessels were pure gold, the chamber pots onyx, and his lights lamps of balsam. He gave pheasants to lions, marrow of geese to dogs, brains of partridges and peacocks or handfuls of gold to courtiers. He turned day into night and night into day, and appointed the companions of his lusts as provincial and legionary commanders.

The people endured this Sardanapalus for a time because he enriched the commons with huge gifts, while his grandmother Moesa governed public affairs by prudent counsel so far as she could. To restrain the people she persuaded Heliogabalus to adopt the excellent young Alexander. These things did not long protect the monster. Continuing in luxury and disdaining to touch ground not spread with gold dust, he was at last dragged by the praetorians from a latrine where he hid and thrust into a narrow sewer. They hauled him through the streets, sank him in the Tiber, and attached weights lest he rise for burial. He was twenty years old and in the sixth year of his reign.

Alexander Severus, the twenty-fifth emperor, born at Arca in Syria, entered upon the empire at sixteen with universal applause. He excelled in letters and every liberal art, though after becoming emperor he was never heard singing or playing an instrument outside his most private rooms. He expelled every disreputable man from Senate, palace, and provincial office; abhorred the sale of public posts; and took upright judges with him in his carriage. The sight of wicked men moved his bile almost to vomiting. Both he and his court used ordinary food and no gems. He believed public money should be spent only on public works, which he built most magnificently. He was very sparing of money with his intimates, merciless to thieves, most generous to the poor, and visited sick nobles familiarly. He venerated an image of Christ and one of Abraham among the other gods, professing to have learned much from Christians, especially, “What you do not wish done to yourself, do not do to another.” His mother Mammaea is said often to have gladly heard Origen discuss religion.

Maximinus, whose father was a Goth and mother an Alan, stood eight geometric feet tall. As a young Thracian shepherd he came to Severus in Syria, where he threw sixteen of the strongest camp followers in wrestling. He deserted Macrinus, joined Heliogabalus, and could bear to look at that effeminate man only once. He gladly served Alexander until lust for rule drove him mad. As emperor he killed all Alexander’s friends in the army and Senate, stirred up the sixth persecution of Christians, and afflicted the Germans in a most cruel war.

Meanwhile the Africans killed their procurator for excessive cruelty and greed and compelled the proconsul Gordian—Severus’ son-in-law, a modest, learned, brave man of eighty—to assume the imperial insignia together with his son Gordian. The Senate also added the boy Gordian, his grandson. Carthage was occupied at once, messengers went to Rome, the praetorian and city prefects were killed, and throughout Italy Maximinus’ agents were punished with death. In Africa, however, Capellianus, proconsul of Mauretania and an old enemy of Gordian, assembled the legions near Carthage, routed and killed the younger Gordian, and filled captured Carthage with slaughter. His army, distressed by hunger, began to mutiny; when Maximinus, repulsed from Aquileia, sat in his tent, the soldiers rushed upon him and beheaded him with his son. He was sixty, in the third year of his reign and the 240th of Christ. His head was viewed at Rome with great joy.

Pupienus, hurrying to Aquileia, pacified Maximinus’ legions with gifts and sent them back to their stations. The praetorians and Senate praised the emperors at Rome. But in the Senate they were told to rule wisely because wise men had elected them, for those whom fools elected would rule foolishly. This saying so stung the praetorians that, while the city and most of the emperors’ household attended games in the theater, they ran armed to the palace. Hearing this, Pupienus immediately summoned the German guard. Balbinus forbade it, fearing that Pupienus had invented the rumor in order to bring the Germans against him. Meanwhile the praetorians arrived, dragged the bound emperors through the streets toward the camp, and killed them when word came that the avenging Germans were near. They happened upon the fourteen-year-old Gordian and carried him off to the camp to assume the imperial insignia.

Herodian ends with Pupienus, as Dio does with Alexander. Other excellent historians of the Caesars continue, however: Lampridius, Capitolinus, Spartianus, Eutropius, Aurelius Victor, Eusebius, and others.

Gordian’s reign reconciled the Senate and praetorians. His father-in-law Misitheus was an excellent man by whose counsel everything was governed with perfect justice. Gordian departed for the Persian War, since Sapor, brother of Artaxerxes, had even occupied Antioch in Syria. Broken in one or two battles, Sapor fled into the interior of Persia. Misitheus was removed by poison through Philip the Arab, whom Eusebius, Jerome, and other ancients call a Christian. Baronius, however, denies that he was Christian at the beginning of his reign, though he says Philip became one after reforming his very wicked conduct. Scaliger angrily denies in his note on Eusebius that Philip was ever a Christian and declares the letters of Origen cited by Eusebius to prove it spurious. At any rate, appointed by Gordian to his father-in-law’s prefecture, Philip managed affairs so that the army would blame its want of provisions upon the emperor. Philip was therefore named colleague; now a Caesar, he commanded Gordian in everything. The high-spirited youth summoned the army and complained of Philip’s insolence. Yet the crafty Arab brought Gordian so low that after laying down the title of Caesar and being denied even the praetorian prefecture, he begged in vain for the rank of commander. Philip’s men finally killed him in the sixth year of his reign and eighteenth of his life.

Philip, the twenty-ninth emperor, in his eagerness to see his own Rome, ceded Mesopotamia and Syria to the Persians. Decius, the thirtieth emperor, was born of noble family in Lower Pannonia. The Senate received him with joy and counted him among the prudent emperors, although he stirred up a most cruel persecution of Christians, which Baronius treats at length from Origen, Cyprian, and other writers of the age. But behold God’s vengeance! After defeating the Goths in Thrace, their king fled to the mountains and offered to return home if granted a free road. Decius refused and surrounded every exit, entrusting one to Trebonianus Gallus. Desiring the empire, Gallus secretly agreed with the Goths to attack the imperial camp. As the Goths withdrew toward an ambush, the younger Decius fell in battle. Seeing himself defeated, his father drove his horse into a deep marsh and, weighed down by his armor, never emerged for burial. This was in the second year of his reign.

Gallus, the thirty-first emperor, added to his infamous treason a disgraceful peace with the Goths purchased by tribute. Nevertheless, the Goths devastated the empire as far as Thessaly, and by their example the Persians invaded Syria and Armenia. Gallus increased Decius’ cruelty against Christians. Hence came a loathsome ten-year plague upon every city, and almost every house, of the empire. Aemilianus gained a notable victory over the Goths, but, elated by it, proudly wished to be called emperor. Gallus fought him and was killed with his sons in the second year of his reign.

Valerian refused to recognize Aemilianus as the thirty-second emperor. As Valerian advanced against him, he was relieved of the journey’s burden, for in his third month Aemilianus was stabbed by his own men.

Valerian, the thirty-third emperor, assumed the empire at age seventy with his son Gallienus amid universal joy and hope. Until then he had passed his whole life in great virtue and happiness. With Decius’ permission the Senate had made him censor. At first his house was not merely an asylum but a home for Christians, but an Egyptian magician seduced him and he became a savage persecutor; hence disaster from God. Marching against the Persian Sapor, either through negligence or treachery he entered such narrow terrain that he fell into Persian hands without a battle. Sapor treated him most wretchedly: whenever the Persian mounted, he used Valerian’s neck as a mounting block, and at last blinded, flayed, and killed him. Under Valerian the persecution of Christians was severe everywhere. Baronius relates the martyrdoms of Cyprian and others fully and elegantly. A prudent reader will readily notice and avoid what he everywhere inserts to support his theory of the Roman pontiff’s power and the Roman church’s prerogative.

Gallienus’ reign was most unhappy. He had no concern to ransom his father, and so emperors sprang up everywhere. More than thirty tyrants in Greece, Asia, Slavonia, Gaul, and Africa—all prefects appointed by Valerian—thought themselves worthy of empire when they saw that Gallienus cared for nothing but the pleasures of Rome.

Odenatus, prince of the Palmyrenes in Syria, acted as emperor and won splendid victories against Sapor. He captured Nisibis, Carrhae, and even Ctesiphon; when Sapor was routed, the royal treasure and wives fell into his hands. At last his cousin Maeonius treacherously removed the valiant Odenatus and was soon himself removed by the same plotters. The command was left to Zenobia, Odenatus’ wife, who defended all the eastern provinces most bravely against both Persians and Romans.

While Gallienus besieged Milan, which Aureolus had occupied, conspirators killed him. During his reign Germans broke into Italy as far as Ravenna and Spain as far as Tarragona; Sarmatians ravaged Pannonia, and Goths Greece and Asia. Almost Italy alone remained intact, yet plague raged so fiercely at Rome that 5,000 died in one day. Gallienus ruled seven years with his father captive and eight alone.

None of the conspirators could seize the empire. By universal consent Claudius the Dalmatian was therefore chosen. Large and handsome, immensely strong, chaste, just, and prudent, he governed everything by the Senate’s judgment. He captured Milan after Aureolus was killed and recovered the provinces. When men debated whom to attack next, he said, “Tyrants are my enemies, but barbarians are enemies of the commonwealth; they must be defeated first.” Great forces of Goths, Heruli, and others stood on the Danube—320,000 fighting men besides camp followers, with 2,000 ships. He destroyed them in an immense slaughter and also killed 200,000 Germans at Lake Benacus.

As the unconquered emperor, having expelled the barbarians, prepared arms against the tyrants, fever killed him in his second year, the 273rd of Christ.

Quintilius, Claudius’ brother, had been left with several legions in Italy and after his brother’s death they declared him the thirty-sixth emperor. Hearing that Claudius’ army had chosen Aurelian, and conscious of his own weakness although approved by the Senate, he opened his veins.

The Palmyrenes later rebelled, but the emperor hurried from Rome, easily suppressed them, and razed Palmyra.

Tetricus remained master of Gaul and Spain. As Aurelian prepared to march against him, letters came from that prudent man saying that, weary of military insolence, he was ready to surrender the provinces. Aurelian celebrated a magnificent triumph with Zenobia and Tetricus in the procession. On his way to Persia to avenge Valerian’s captivity, Aurelian prepared decrees for persecuting Christians, whom he had previously tolerated. As he was about to sign he was nearly struck by lightning. He persisted, but was deprived of life. Mnesteus, his secretary, fearing for his life because of Aurelian’s threats, drew up a supposed list of men marked for death. Once he showed it, a band of conspirators readily united and killed the emperor in Thrace in the sixth year of his reign.

After Aurelian the empire, astonishingly, stood vacant for six months. The murders of tyrants under Gallienus deterred everyone from seizing it; repeatedly the army referred the nomination to the Senate and the Senate to the army. At last the Senate named the upright Tacitus the thirty-eighth emperor. While he advanced against the Persians, fever extinguished him at Tarsus in his sixth month.

Florian, Tacitus’ brother, then seized the empire, but the eastern legions named Probus. On hearing this Florian opened a vein.

Probus, born at Sirmium in Lower Pannonia, was the fortieth emperor. Had he found a Livy or Polybius in his age, he would have been proclaimed superior to Hannibal and even Caesar. After Aurelian’s death the Germans occupied Gaul. Probus broke them in three days of continuous fighting, interrupted only by the darkness of night, and killed more than 400,000. He also drove the Sarmatians from Slavonia in a dreadful slaughter. As he passed through Thrace to destroy the Goths in their own homes, they voluntarily submitted to the emperor’s judgment. His next march was against Persia. On the road he subdued the Isaurians, proud of their terrain; the Ethiopian Blemmyes were expelled from Syria, almost all of which they had occupied. Fearing to meet him in battle, the Persian accepted Probus’ prescribed peace terms, and the empire thus gained complete repose.

Saturninus, a man of great virtue, was in Syria. When he came to Alexandria the people hailed him emperor. Though he escaped the crowd’s attack, his soldiers forced him, despite many tears, to accept the name of Caesar. Probus’ war with him was difficult; once victorious, Probus grieved that Saturninus had been killed, for he had resolved to pardon a man compelled to offend.

At the same time Bonosus rebelled in Britain and Proculus in Germany. Defeated, Bonosus hanged himself. He could drink as much as ten of the heaviest drinkers without intoxication, for at will he vomited to the last drop. The Germans killed Proculus.

By very bad counsel Probus had granted the barbarians’ request to settle devastated Thrace. Impatient of peace, they overflowed into the other provinces, but with dreadful slaughter of the Vandals Probus forced the barbarians back into their old channel and then celebrated a most splendid triumph. Not even a mouse gnawed anywhere in the empire for fear of Probus. At last he intended to destroy the Persian Empire, but on the march soldiers who resented universal peace and could not bear the emperor’s excellence killed this marvelous man. This was in his sixth year, the 285th of Christ.

Carus, a Slav and praetorian prefect, was elected by the army as the forty-first emperor. He immediately punished Probus’ murderers, marched against the Sarmatians who threatened Italy itself, and routed them with great slaughter. Victorious against the Persians in several battles, he was preparing to uproot Persian power when severe sickness attacked him on the bank of the Tigris, and lightning killed him as he lay in his tent.

He had left his elder son Carinus, a dissolute youth, as Caesar in Gaul, but kept with him in Persia the younger Numerian, a learned and upright young man. Numerian was immediately hailed emperor, but as he returned to Italy in a closed litter because of pain in his eyes, his father-in-law Arius Aper arranged for him to be stabbed. On the third day the litter’s smell disclosed the crime before Aper’s canvassing secured the empire.

Diocletian was treated as father by his colleague Augustus and as lord by the Caesars Galerius and Constantius. All four simultaneously waged enormous wars. With great labor Diocletian finally subdued Achilleus, who had rebelled in Egypt, and took Alexandria by force. Maximian suppressed the African regions with greater difficulty.

The Germans pressed Constantius so hard that he had to make peace with Carausius, the tyrant of Britain. After seven years of tyranny Carausius was stabbed by Allectus, and Allectus after three years by Asclepiodotus. Thus after ten years Britain returned to Constantius.

Galerius, defeated by the Persian Narses in many battles, at last won a great engagement. Narses himself fled; his wives, sisters, and treasure were captured. Diocletian, who had previously despised Galerius, now embraced him. The four Caesars then moved with united arms against the northern barbarians, broke them in various battles, and celebrated a triumph when every war was ended.

Diocletian then issued an edict requiring men of every station to adore him in Persian fashion and adorned with gems the shoes they were to kiss. More ruinously, he commanded a most loathsome slaughter of Christians. This eleventh persecution lasted ten years. At last he voluntarily renounced the empire and peacefully cultivated gardens at Salona in his homeland. God often permits one whom he has resolved to torment eternally to enjoy a certain happiness on earth for a time. On the day of his own abdication he either persuaded or compelled his colleague Maximian to renounce the world, leaving the empire to the Caesars Constantius and Galerius. Fearing the discord that Diocletian’s authority had suppressed, they immediately divided the empire. Indeed, after the division Constantius voluntarily yielded Africa and Italy to Galerius, content with Spain, Gaul, Germany, and Britain. To govern his large share better, Galerius named two Caesars: his sister’s son Maximinus, whom he placed over Asia and Egypt, and Severus, to whom he entrusted Italy and Africa, while he governed Slavonia, Illyricum, and Greece.

The praetorians at Rome, weary of Severus’ conduct, created Maxentius, Maximian’s son, emperor; when Severus approached with an army his own men betrayed him. Constantius had already died, leaving Constantine, his son by his former wife Helena, as heir. Fausta, Maximian’s daughter, was given to him in marriage. When Maximian, from love of rule, heard that his son Maxentius was reigning, he flew to Rome. He was honorably received until his plots against his son appeared. The praetorians treated him so badly that he fled to his son-in-law in Gaul. There he was received with great friendship, but when his treachery against Constantine was discovered and he tried to escape, he was captured at Marseilles and ordered by his son-in-law’s command to die.

Leaving Licinius as Caesar in Slavonia, Galerius himself marched on Rome against Maxentius. Sensing his men’s treachery on the road, he hurried back. When an excruciating disease arose in his side, he begged Christians to pray for him and revoked every edict against their religion. His repentance was late and feigned, however, and he died amid dreadful torments of the disease of lice in his sixth year.

Licinius immediately called himself Augustus. As Maxentius’ tyranny increased, the Senate secretly implored Constantine to come to Italy. He obeyed and met Licinius at Milan, giving him his sister Constantia in marriage. Invited from retirement to the wedding, Diocletian refused to come. Both Caesars therefore wrote threatening letters to the decrepit old man as though he favored Maxentius. In fear Diocletian took poison and, as some relate, died insane.

Constantine defeated Maxentius once. When they were about to meet again with all their strength, Constantine, fearing Maxentius’ magic more than his arms, humbly sought Christ’s aid. In prayer he saw a huge cross in heaven and heard, “Conquer in this sign,” which came to pass. At their first collision Maxentius retreated across a bridge he had laid over the Tiber, fell with his horse, and drowned. Constantine and Licinius issued very broad edicts in Christ’s honor, which Caesar Maximinus was compelled to obey. Later, however, pretending repentance, Maximinus continued the persecution of Christians and prepared war against the Augusti. He was routed in the first battle; as he tried to restore his forces, a plague of such savage pain seized him that he rolled on the ground and gnawed his fingers, until in madness he breathed out his soul. His death established complete peace for Christians.

Licinius, however, unable to bear Constantine’s glory, began to harass Christians and, after twelve years of friendship, contrived everything for Constantine’s ruin. They met in Pannonia and Licinius was routed. Later there was a very fierce battle in Bithynia, where Licinius was completely defeated. CHAPTER II.

Questions and doubts concerning the first epoch of the New Testament.

QUESTION I.

On the year of the Savior’s birth.

All recent chronologists usually place great importance upon this question, and most of them no less difficulty. The Savior’s nativity is easily chief among all chronological epochs and markers. The beginning of the world and every time following creation look toward it as their blessed and fortunate center; everything later flows from it as from a spring toward the end of the age and the great sea and abyss of the eternity to come. However many divisions of time chronologists employ, Christians are never satisfied unless all are explicitly referred to Christ’s birth: prior ages numbered so that one knows how many years preceded it, and later ages so that it is clear how many followed. Thus every boundary of time wavers, trembles, and fluctuates unless this center is fixed immovably and this guiding star set in meridian light.

I said recent chronologists placed so much importance on this question, for older theologians and chronologists—astonishingly—so neglected this famous epoch that very few even mentioned it in passing. Certainly no one before the seventh Christian century applied the Lord’s birth to date any civil or ecclesiastical matter. More than five hundred years after Christ, Justinian in his Authentics, Novella 47, ordered a fourth marker, apparently not used before—the reigning Augustus’ regnal year—to be added to the old designations from the city’s foundation, consuls’ names, and tax indiction; he says nothing of Christ’s nativity. In his gloss Accursius claims the law commands that Christ’s birth be prefixed as a fifth temporal marker. That gloss is vain, like another there in which, through singular chronological ignorance, Accursius holds not only that indictions—first instituted by Constantine—were used in Christ’s time, but that Justinian, author of the Novellae, lived in Christ’s days. Godefroy or Cujas rightly condemns this as false in a marginal correction.

Certainly the Fathers and church councils, greater and lesser, nowhere mention years of Christ in their dates but use only the civil markers from the city’s foundation, consuls, indictions, and the emperor. Dionysius Exiguus, a Scythian by nation and a Roman monk or abbot, was the first mortal to object that his own and earlier centuries designated years in Paschal cycles chiefly from Diocletian’s persecution. Expunging the marker of this pagan and savage persecutor of the church, in the days of Justinian, A.D. 532, he substituted Christ’s nativity in his private calculation. The venerable Bede followed Dionysius’ invention in the seventh century after Christ, as did many others, but privately. The Christian era did not begin to be used in public ecclesiastical or civil acts before Charlemagne’s day, about the 800th year after the Lord’s birth. The great superstition that has flourished for several centuries in the Roman church concerning observance of the month and day of the Nativity cannot be ancient, since observation even of the nativity year entered the church so late.

No more on the question’s importance. Many complain greatly of its difficulty, none more than our learned Spanheim at the beginning of the second part of his Gospel Doubts. I concede that the points many adduce to show its difficulty are not slight. Yet I think Petavius and other Jesuits exaggerate it beyond measure to soften and excuse their church’s manifest and foul errors, as though the subject were so difficult that clear truth could not be found in it. Spanheim, though unwittingly, seems far too much to fill the sails of their craftiness. For although some knots in this question are quite hard to untie, many matters are so clear and certain that most errors, especially those of the Roman church, can be demonstrated and refuted very easily and solidly.

The whole question can be one and simple: the true boundary of the nativity year—whether the beginning of the common era is correct, and whether 1,656 years, neither more nor fewer, have passed since Christ’s birth. To resolve this great chronological difficulty concerning the eras’ connection, Joseph Scaliger, the greatest reformer of chronology—greatly praised even by his opponents and by Petavius himself—invented the Julian Period. It contains 7,980 years, the product of three cycles multiplied together: the sun, moon, and indictions.

The solar cycle has twenty-eight years. In it the leap year, produced every four years by intercalation on the sixth day before the Kalends of March—that is, by repeating or doubling February 23—is marked by one of the seven dominical letters G, F, E, D, C, B, A, indicated in this technical verse: “The Frenchman delights in a horse, the Dacian in a dog, the barbarian in a chest.” Seven multiplied by four gives twenty-eight; when this cycle is completed the solar motion becomes equal and the sun returns to the same appearance it had at the cycle’s beginning. The sun completes its annual course in 365 days and six hours. Every fourth year the hours run to a full day, inserted into February, the shortest month, between its twenty-third and twenty-fourth days.

The lunar cycle is a nineteen-year period invented by Meton. At its completion the same order of new moons and the same relation of moon to sun return; it is marked in the calendar by the golden number.

The cycle of indiction was a fifteen-year period in which the same order of provincial taxes returned. In its first five years the provinces paid bronze and iron for arms, in the second silver for soldiers’ pay, and in the third gold for the treasury, in a variable proportion, to the Roman emperor. The solar and lunar cycles multiplied together produce the Dionysian period of 532 years, invented by Dionysius Exiguus. Multiplied by fifteen, the cycle of indictions, these produce Scaliger’s Julian Period of 7,980 years, so called from the form of the year notably reformed by Julius Caesar and used in it. Its beginning is fixed in the 764th year before creation, when the first solar, lunar, and indictional cycles coincide. Those three markers do not meet again before the end of this great 7,980-year cycle. Its table describes every chronological marker, so that when one is given all the others are immediately placed before the eyes. Once the year of the Lord in which an event occurred is known, the period readily reveals its year of the world, of the city’s foundation, of the Olympiad, of Nabonassar, of Julius, and so forth. It is universally conceded that this Scaligerian instrument is of immense use in chronology and cannot be praised enough, as perhaps we shall show more fully in its place.

Almost all count nine years from Cyrus’ twenty-first, when he captured Babylon, to Cambyses’ first—one more, at least, if eight years are assigned to each; thirty-six to Darius Hystaspes; twenty-one to Xerxes. Add seven of Artaxerxes Longimanus and, including endpoints, you have eighty-one, or excluding them, seventy-nine. Joined to the prior fifty-two, these make 131 or 133.

This extra-scriptural part of seventy-nine or eighty-one years is placed beyond doubt by Herodotus, Ctesias, Ptolemy, and other ancients, if any faith is to be given human testimony. To make such testimony more credible, Ptolemy long ago and recently Scaliger, Lansbergius, and Petavius elegantly demonstrate it from celestial motions. Comparing solar and lunar eclipses observed in the archives of Chaldean and Egyptian priests with history, they find the ancient calculations agree well with their own. In many cases this argument is firm, and in all it would be irrefragable if mathematicians had given a perfect calculation of ancient eclipses and historians had marked events by numerous eclipses. I acknowledge, however, that contrary opinions, and even palpable errors, among the excellent mathematicians Scaliger, Petavius, Calvisius, and others often make this argument doubtful and sometimes false precisely where they profess to prove and demonstrate by exact eclipse calculation. Lansbergius confidently proclaims that by the most certain celestial motions and clearest demonstrations he proved precisely seventy years passed from the burning of the temple to Cyrus’ capture of Babylon. We showed above, however, that this is false and conflicts with Scripture and the soundest chronologists and interpreters.

The final article’s 453 years, from Longimanus’ seventh to Christ’s birth, are demonstrated with sufficient certainty as follows. Given what was proved above, that Daniel’s weeks began in Longimanus’ seventh, the seventy weeks contain 490 years. The Messiah died in the middle of the last week (Daniel 9:27). From his baptism to his death were 3½ years, as will appear later. Christ was therefore baptized at the beginning of the last week; his birth preceded his baptism by thirty years, as Luke 3:23 says. Thus Christ was born thirty-seven years before the end of the seventy weeks. Subtracting thirty-seven from the total 490 leaves 453, the required number.

Thus we have briefly proved all seven articles from creation to Christ’s birth, all from open Scripture except one—or rather only part of one—for which abundant human testimony and firm arguments are supplied.
