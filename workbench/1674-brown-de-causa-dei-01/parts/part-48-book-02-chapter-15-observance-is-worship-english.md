CHAPTER XV

The Observance of Principal and Solemn Time Is a Part of Worship

To explain more fully this *solemn, necessary, and sufficient time*, I wish to
inquire whether its observance ought to be regarded as a part of worship.

Not only do our adversaries deny this, but so also do other orthodox men as
regards our principal question. It will therefore not be useless to inquire
what ought to be said here, not only to explain more fully this solemn time
which we are now treating, but also to prepare the way for the holy and diligent
observance of determined solemn time. For people who are not openly profane
will be urged and pressed all the more in conscience to spend piously this time
determined by God when they hear that not only those pious exercises, both
public and private, which are then to be performed are parts of divine worship,
but that the observance itself of the determined day or time is also a part of
worship.

To make clear that this *principal and solemn, necessary and sufficient time*
determined by God, together with the day itself, or the beginning of the time,
likewise determined by God, is a part of worship, certain things said above in
the first chapter of this same book concerning the worship of God must be
recalled, so that it may be better known what is a part of worship and what is
a mere adjunct of worship.

*To worship God in general is, as we said, to exhibit immediate honor to him,
or to declare and attest our homage and subjection.*

A part of worship, therefore, is that which is exhibited to God as a sign and
testimony of our homage, as immediate honor; or that thing, whether it is an
object or some action, by which we honor God immediately and celebrate and
declare his absolute dominion and his other excellences.

An adjunct of worship, however, is only a mere circumstance which is attached,
naturally or occasionally, to the thing or action that is a part of worship. It
is not exhibited, nor ought it to be exhibited, to God as some special and
immediate honor, but surrounds and in some way serves the thing or action by
which we exhibit honor to God.

Although a part and an adjunct, or circumstance, of worship differ in their
formal concepts, so that a part of worship as such is not a mere circumstance,
nor is a mere circumstance as such a part of worship, nevertheless the same
thing which in one respect is a part of worship can in another respect be a
mere circumstance of worship. And conversely, that which here and now is only
a mere circumstance of worship can in another respect be a part of worship and
can be offered immediately to God with the special intention of glorifying him
and celebrating his honor. For example, among the young cows, sheep, lambs, and
so forth which once had to be offered to God under the law,
age, color, and sex were very often mere accidents and adjuncts, of no
importance, so that it made no difference of what age, color, or sex they were.
Sometimes, however, even these circumstances assumed the character of worship,
as when it was specially commanded that

[1674 logical printed p. 335 | IA leaf 353 | PDF p. 353]

at minimum an ox, lamb, or goat to be
offered should remain seven days under its mother (Leviticus 22:27), or that an
ox or animal should be offered to God precisely on the eighth day, neither
before the eighth day nor afterward (Exodus 22:30). So also with respect to
color: when the water cleansing from impurity was to be prepared, the young cow
had to be red (Numbers 19:2). Likewise with respect to sex: the animal to be
offered for Passover had to be male (Exodus 12:5; see also Leviticus 1:3, 10;
4:23; 22:19). Sometimes the offering had to be a female goat (Leviticus 4:28).

For legitimate worship there is required not only the intention of the
worshiper—which is necessarily required in all worship, whether legitimate or
illegitimate, for without it an action cannot be regarded as worship—but also
God's command. For whatever he commands to be offered to himself for special
honor, whether it is a substance, an accident, an action, or a circumstance,
assumes the character of worship, honor, or homage to be exhibited to him. And
whenever it is exhibited to him, it ought to be exhibited with the intention
of glorifying him. Whenever it is so exhibited, the worship is pleasing and
acceptable to God, and this acceptance by God ought to be expected by the pious
worshiper.

This command of God is either special or general. A special command enjoins the
thing itself that is to be offered, such as the command concerning daily
sacrifices and offerings. A general command enjoins the affections signified by
the offering of things which were indeed commanded as to their kind but not
determined as to their number, being left to the worshiper's free discretion.
Such were the voluntary gifts and also vows, increased and multiplied for a
particular occasion.

God reveals his will concerning a thing or action to be offered immediately
for his honor either through the law of nature, which points out natural
worship to us, or through some positive institution. Thus the use of water in
baptism and of bread and wine in the Eucharist is a part of worship.

From this it is clear what our question means when it is asked whether
*principal and solemn, necessary and sufficient time*—that is, time determined
with respect to *how long*, *how often*, and also *when*—is a part of worship,
or instead a mere adjunct of worship or pure circumstance. The question is
this: when that time so determined by God is observed by us, do we exhibit
immediate honor to God, attest our subjection by that observance, and render
homage to God? And can or ought we to observe that time for this purpose as
something commanded by God and therefore pleasing and acceptable to him,
expecting his blessing and acceptance in Christ and through Christ? We affirm
it; others deny it. The affirmative can be confirmed as follows.

*First*, it is that time which God claims for himself as something special and
his own, as we abundantly confirmed above. Hence in the fourth precept of the
Decalogue itself it is called *the Sabbath of the Lord*, and in Isaiah 58:13,
*God's holy day*, or the day of God's holiness, *the delight and holy thing of
Jehovah*. In the New Testament it is *the Lord's Day*. By these phrases it is
abundantly declared that God, the supreme and absolute Lord of all time,
reserved this span of time for himself alone so that it might be spent
immediately for his honor. That whatever God thus claims as his own, commands
to be offered as an expression of immediate honor, and promises to accept is
truly a part of divine worship—even if

[1674 logical printed p. 336 | IA leaf 354 | PDF p. 354]

otherwise it could be a mere circumstance of a religious action—is
sufficiently clear from similar examples. Namely: 1. From the *tithes* which
God claimed for himself in Leviticus 27:30, 32. Thus to offer them properly was
a part of religious worship, rendering homage to God as Lord of all and
exhibiting immediate honor to him. No one should say that they were instituted
to support the *Levites and priests*, for they had not yet been applied to this
use, as is evident from Numbers 18, where we see them at last applied to this
use some years later. Hence God is frequently called *Levi's portion*, because
Levi was to possess as his inheritance that portion of goods which God had
reserved for himself. Therefore, although this payment of tithes, insofar as it
was made immediately to the Levites and only mediately to God through them as
his ministers, was not a part of worship, nevertheless insofar as God claimed
for himself that portion of the Israelites' goods and called it his own, it
was to be regarded as a part of worship. Hence they are said to have robbed God
himself when they did not pay them (Malachi 3:9). It was therefore undoubtedly
a part of worship. 2. From that portion of the spoil which the Israelites
possessed after the war with the Midianites in Numbers 31, which is called *an
offering to Jehovah* in verses 29 and 41, as though a tribute which they had to
pay to God. It is so called before it had been applied to any particular use;
thus this portion had to be offered to God as tribute or a part of the homage
owed. And so it was a part of worship. 3. The same is clear from another
example, namely the *temple*, which is frequently called *God's temple* and
*God's house*, because it was the place God had specially selected for himself
from all the tribes so that his name might be celebrated there in a special
manner. It was therefore a special part of divine worship that sacrifices be
offered to God in that place, whereby immediate honor was exhibited to him; and
he so accepted this worship that he utterly rejected sacrifices offered
elsewhere, except in some extraordinary case such as that in 1 Kings 18. But if
the matter stands this way with place and things of this kind, why not also
with time, since the rationale is everywhere the same? Namely, God claims this
for himself as something special and requires it to be offered to him as a sign
and special testimony of immediate honor, even before it is applied to any
particular use or special religious exercise. This time, therefore, is God's
time even before it is actually spent in religious exercises. And because it
belongs specially to God, it must be spent in pious exercises, whether many or
all, both private and public, so that it may actually be spent in a special
manner upon God and his glory. For although it cannot properly be spent and
employed for God's honor without some exercise of piety, it is nevertheless
distinguished from the exercises of piety, is considered distinctly, and
requires divine duties to be performed within it. Therefore, although it is
not actually separated from all religious duties, yet insofar as it is
distinguished from them and considered in itself, it too is a part of divine
worship, or a part of the immediate honor to be exhibited to God. Hence
religious acts performed at that time become acts of worship in a twofold way,
and pleasing and acceptable to God: both in themselves, and because they are
performed in time specially appointed, and through them that entire continuous
time which God specially reserved for himself is spent whenever it recurs.

*Next*, that to observe this principal and solemn time properly is to worship
God is clear from the fact that it is to obey a moral precept of the first
table of the Decalogue. Everyone concedes that the precepts of the first table
of the Decalogue concern the immediate worship and honor of God, and that
everything commanded there, whether actions or other things, whether external
or internal, are parts of divine worship, because they

[1674 logical printed p. 337 | IA leaf 355 | PDF p. 355]

regard God immediately and are directed to his immediate honor; for they are
required parts of homage. This is further confirmed, by way of superabundance,
from the fact that a positive ceremonial precept of God can make a thing or
action a part of divine worship—for example, cedar wood, hyssop, twice-dyed
scarlet, the washing of garments, the sprinkling of blood, and so forth. A
moral precept of God, such as the fourth precept of the Decalogue, can do this
much more, as even those who contradict us here acknowledge. And it is well
known that no moral and rational cause can be given for those and similar
ceremonial injunctions except this one: the supreme good pleasure of God. But a
clear and distinct moral reason can be assigned for this determination of time
and moral precept; and, as is clear from what was said before, it can easily be
shown how much it contributes to promoting God's glory and our own eternal
salvation.

*Third*, that the observance of the determined day is a part of worship is
clear from the fact that it is specially commanded for the worship of God. It
was explained above, and will be proved more fully later when the Lord's Day is
discussed, that the day itself is specially commanded by God. This appears
certain, and it is conceded by some who oppose us here, namely, that by a moral
precept God commanded one day in the weekly cycle to be dedicated to God and
spent religiously for his honor. When the seventh day was abolished, the first
day rightly succeeded it, resting on the same foundation as the former seventh
and last day. Therefore, since the first day of the week rests no less on
divine authority than the precise seventh day formerly did, it is evident that
the observance of this day is a part of divine worship. For without God's
precept nothing, however necessary, can be a part of worship; and whatever he
commands to be offered to himself as a sign of honor, however little necessary
it is in itself, is a part of worship because of the command of him who alone
is the author of worship.

*Fourth*, what is not a mere circumstance of worship is a part of worship—we
are speaking of a thing which must necessarily be observed in connection with
worship. But this principal and solemn time is not a mere circumstance.
Therefore, and so forth. The adversaries will not deny the *major* premise, for
they refuse to make this day a part precisely because they judge it to be a
mere circumstance of worship. The *minor* premise can be established from all
the arguments by which we will prove later, in book 3, that the precept
concerning the Sabbath, or the observance of principal and solemn time, is
moral; and from the reasons by which we will make clear, in book 4, that the
fourth precept concerning the Sabbath, or the sanctification of this solemn
time, is moral. Moreover, it is established from the fact that this time is the
substance of a divine precept, indeed of a precept of the Decalogue. What is
expressly commanded by God in this way is not a mere, mere circumstance. That
the observance of solemn time is the substance or essence of the fourth precept
of the Decalogue will be clear from what will be said in book 4, especially
chapter 42. Finally, if it were a mere circumstance, it could easily be
changed; but this must not be admitted.

*Fifth*, the observance of the Sabbath, or of solemn time, is a virtue.
Therefore it is not a mere circumstance, but a part of worship. The adversaries
will not deny the *consequence*, since they treat these as synonyms. The
*antecedent* is confirmed as follows. 1. What is obedience rendered to God's
precept is a virtue. But the observance of the Sabbath, or of solemn time, is
obedience rendered to the fourth precept. Therefore. The *minor* premise is
established from the fact

[1674 logical printed p. 338 | IA leaf 356 | PDF p. 356]

that the fourth precept of the Decalogue enjoins the observance of solemn time,
as we will prove later. The *major* cannot be denied, since virtue is to obey
God. Let Nathanael Jonson be heard. On p. 87 he abundantly proves that the
observance of the Sabbath is a virtue from the fact that it is such an aid to
worship as removes impediments to worship. He speaks as follows. *1. What is a
good work and obedience to God's command is a virtue, for to theologians these
are nearly synonymous: good, good work, and virtue, and so forth. But to remove
impediments to piety is a good work and obedience to God's command, such as
abstaining from food on a day of supplication, and so forth. 2. When the formal
object of a virtue is posited, an act of virtue is posited—that is, when there
is a special command or express institution of God. But resting on the Sabbath
has God's command as its formal object, and so forth. Therefore it is an act of
virtue. 3. What is in itself the effect of virtue is an act of virtue. But
resting on the Sabbath and abstaining from food on a day of supplication is an
effect of virtue performed for the sake of obeying. Therefore. 4. That whose
intrinsic, proper, and adequate end is virtue, a good work, indeed exercises of
every kind of piety, is a work of virtue; for when the intrinsic, proper, and
adequate end is posited, the thing ordered to the end is posited. Therefore. 5.
That whose opposite—for example, not eating from the tree of knowledge before
the fall, or working at the labors of one's calling—is vice or sin, is virtue
or piety. The author's colleague on p. 74, where holy cessation is declared to
be a work that is not unholy. But, and so forth. Therefore. 6. To abstain from
evil is a virtue, for this is the description of virtue καθ’ ἀφαίρεσιν τῷ
ἐναντίῳ, “by negation in relation to the opposite,” as logicians teach from
Boethius: it belongs to virtue to flee vice, and so forth. See Psalm 34.
Objection: therefore
not to do any work will be a virtue. I answer from the same place: unless not
doing any work on the Sabbath day were a virtue, observance of the Sabbath
under the Old Testament would not have been a virtue, the worship of God, or a
good work. 2. Abstaining from an evil work is undoubtedly a virtue. But working
on the Sabbath is evil because of God's prohibition. 3. Even abstaining from a
lawful work is a good work when done for such an end—namely, to obey God, to
perform the exercises of religion, and to worship God solemnly. Objection 2: a
virtue has as the object to be avoided a thing or action evil and base in
itself and by its own nature. I answer from the same place. 1. It can also have
an indifferent thing as an object to be avoided, as in a case of scandal. 2. It
is sufficient if the action or thing is evil because of God's prohibition;
otherwise it was not a virtue in Adam to abstain from the tree of the knowledge
of good and evil, nor would it have been a virtue in Abraham to slaughter his
son when God commanded it (Genesis 21).*

*Sixth*, every special manner or special means of worshiping God which is
instituted by God is a part of worship. For these things pertain to the first
table of the Decalogue, which concerns the worship of God. But the observance
of the Sabbath, or of principal and solemn time, is a special manner or special
means of worshiping God, as is established; and it was instituted by God, as
will be proved later and is clear from what has been said. Therefore, and so
forth.

*Seventh*, since so many exercises of piety, both public and private, are
required for the sanctification of the Sabbath, or of this solemn time, as we
will explain when we treat the sanctification of the Lord's Day in book 6, the
sanctification or observance of that time itself cannot fail to be a part of
divine worship. But the former is true. Therefore, and so forth. The
consequence is firm.

*Eighth*, the adversaries themselves sometimes acknowledge as much. For they
sometimes say that what is commanded is worship if it is done because of the
command. But we infer that the observance of the Sabbath, or of solemn time, is
commanded, and that in the first table of the Decalogue. Therefore it is a part
of worship if it is done because of the command. Next, they say that

[1674 logical printed p. 339 | IA leaf 357 | PDF p. 357]

violation of the Sabbath is impiety and profaneness. Therefore its observance
and sanctification will be a part of worship.

There is no need for me to add more arguments, since the very acute Nathanael
Jonson proves this more fully and with many arguments on pp. 97–99. I will not
shrink from transcribing his words, so as to assist those who do not have that
book at hand. He proves it by the following arguments. *1. What is a good work
of the first table, obedience to an express and special command of God, indeed
what is called by the Holy Spirit obedience to God's commands, is the worship
of God. For to theologians, and also to the author, these are nearly
equivalent, as we proved elsewhere. The minor premise is established from
Nehemiah 9:13–14 and Exodus 16. There a holy thing is called a holy day—that is,
a holy observance objectively and subjectively (Isaiah 56:2, 5, 7). Obedience
is likewise spoken of in Isaiah 56:2, and so forth, and the observance of God's
commands in Exodus 16:28. Nor does the author's colleague dissent on p. 154:
the Sabbath was a holy cessation at God's command, and the day was proclaimed
holy. In Leviticus 23 they were to hold an assembly εἰς πανήγυριν τῆς ἀγάπης,
“into the festal assembly of love.” 2. What is a special manner and means of
worshiping God according to God's institution is the worship of God. But the
observance and sanctification of the Sabbath is a special manner and means of
worshiping God according to the institution of God himself, both under the Old
and under the New Testament, as we proved elsewhere. The major premise is
established, because means and manners of worshiping God are obedience of the
first table. 3. The sanctification of a holy thing according to a precept of
the first table is the worship of God, as the author's colleague says in *On
the Sabbath*, p. 94: what is commanded is worship if it is done because of the
command. But the sanctification of rest under both the Old and the New
Testament is commanded, as the same man acknowledges in *On the Sabbath*, p.
22. Therefore. Proof: otherwise both the sanctification of a fast and the
celebration of a Eucharistic day would not be worthy of the designation “the
worship of God.” Yet ceasing because of God's command is the worship of God,
as the author's colleague taught again on pp. 125 and 94. 4. What is συνδρομὴ,
“a concourse and confluence,” a compendium and synopsis of all the exercises of
piety, is the worship of God. But the sanctification of the Sabbath is the
concourse of all the exercises of piety. Proof: the Sabbath is to be converted
into delights of the Spirit (Isaiah 58:13). But what are the delights of the
pious? Are they not the whole practice of piety (Proverbs 21:15; Psalms 119;
1:2), extending even to tears, grief over sins, and fasts (Jeremiah 8:18;
Zechariah 8:19)? Hence, since the sanctification of the Sabbath requires sacred
meditations of every kind, public and private prayers, the dispensation of the
divine word, and under the Old Testament the doubled daily sacrifices, and so
forth, it follows that the sanctification of the Sabbath is the confluence and
joining of all the exercises of piety. 5. What synecdochically designates the
whole worship of God, or the whole practice of piety, is itself the worship of
God or the practice of piety. But the observance of the Sabbath
synecdochically designates the whole worship of God (Isaiah 56:2, 4–7; 66:23).
Rivet and Spanheim agree. 6. What is done from faith, according to a command of
the first table, for God's glory, is the worship of God. But the observance of
the Sabbath is performed from faith by every believer, according to a command
of the first table confirmed under the New Testament by the institution and
practice of the apostles, and so forth. Therefore. 7. That by which God is
worshiped in spirit and truth, according to John 4:24, is the worship of God.
But through the sanctification of the Sabbath God is worshiped by believers in
spirit and truth—and this equally under the Old and under the New Testament.
Therefore. 8. An act whose formal object is a command of the first table of the
Moral Law is the worship of God. But as regards true believers, the formal
object of the sanctification of the Sabbath is a command of the first table,
confirmed under the New Testament by the apostles' teaching and example.
Therefore. The minor premise is proved from the author's colleague on p. 94:
what is commanded is the worship of God if it is done because of the command.
I add Psalm 119:4, 9. How shall a young man keep his ways pure? asks the
prophet. If he directs them according to*

[1674 logical printed p. 340 | IA leaf 358 | PDF p. 358]

*your word, he answers himself. 9. That whose proper or appropriated means and
instruments are exercises of piety—prayers, meditations, reading the word of
God, and so forth—is the worship of God. But the appropriated means and
instruments necessarily required for the sanctification of the Sabbath are all
kinds of exercises of piety (Isaiah 58:13; Psalm 92; Exodus 16:28). 10. That
whose adequate intrinsic end is the exercise and increase of faith, hope, and
love toward God and neighbor is the worship of God. Therefore. Toward God: for
we are bound to love God with the whole heart, all our strength, and every
thought (Matthew 22:38), and to devote the whole day to prayers, meditations,
and so forth for that end. Toward our neighbor: because we are bound to consider
one another εἰς παροξυσμὸν τῆς ἀγάπης, “unto a provocation of love” (Hebrews
10), as the author's colleague acknowledges, and to bestow alms on the poor.
11. Every means enjoined in the first table whose proper effect is the
promotion of salvation (Philippians 2:12) is the worship of God. But the effect
of the spiritual practice of the Sabbath is the promotion of salvation (Isaiah
55:2, 5, 6). The intent of catechesis is the same. 12. What pertains to the
first table of the Moral Law as a part is the worship of God. But the
sanctification of the Sabbath pertains to the first table of the Moral Law as
a part of it. Therefore. The consequence is certain, because what is said of a
homogeneous whole is also said of its parts, according to the logicians, as was
said above. The minor premise is proved where we taught that the fourth precept
is of perpetual right as to its substance, and so forth. 13. A practice which
is an inward sign of the state of grace or blessedness to everyone who
exercises himself in spirit and truth, and indeed of eternal adoption, is the
worship of God. The major premise is proved from Matthew 5:4, and so forth:
blessed are those who mourn, and so forth. Whatever practice Matthew 5
pronounces blessedness upon is the worship of God or a good work. The minor
premise is proved from Isaiah 56:2, 5, 7. 14. That whose acts have as their
occupying object exercises of every kind of piety is the worship of God. The
minor premise is established from argument 4. 15. Titus acts either well or
badly when sanctifying the Sabbath, or the Lord's Day, through exercises of
every kind of piety. There is no middle, since every individual human act is
good or bad. If well, therefore, he worships God, because he consecrates the day
to God (Romans 14). If badly, therefore, he sins. 16. That whose opposite is
profaneness and impiety is the worship of God. But the opposite of the
sanctification of the Sabbath, or the violation of the Lord's Day, is
profaneness and impiety, according to the author on p. 28 of his little book.
Therefore. 17. Absurdities will follow. 1. The practice of the Lord's Day will
be ἐθελοθρησκείαν, “self-imposed worship,” and superstition. For every worship,
manner, or means of divine worship which has not been instituted by God is
ἐθελοθρησκεία, self-imposed worship. Nor does the author resolve this argument
on p. 54 of his little book, where he raises it against himself. 2. Therefore
the opposite cannot be proved by any argument to be impiety. 3. If anyone does
not wish to sanctify the Sabbath, he will not sin properly and directly against
God. 4. Nor is there reason for the pious to grieve—in the manner in which the
prophet says in Psalm 51, “Against you only have I sinned”—if at any time they
have not observed the Sabbath and infer this in their confessions. 5. Nor is
there reason for them to implore the aid of divine grace so that they may
sanctify the Sabbath in the proper manner, since according to the author's
opinion it is an easy and light burden. 6. It will therefore be lawful in
conscience to work on the Sabbath, as the author's colleague taught on Hebrews
4 and again in his book on the Sabbath. Indeed, it will be lawful to race ships
and carts, to fish, to hunt—and what not? 7. Pastors labor in vain in synods and
classes and wear down the estates so that profanations of the Sabbath may be
removed and punished, since properly speaking there are no profanations of the
Sabbath. 8. There is a danger that our authors relapse into Judaism or remain
in the sin of ἐθελοθρησκείας, self-imposed worship, since every year we explain
the fourth precept publicly and privately in catechetical sermons and
catechizations and inculcate it in children from infancy. Thus he speaks.* And
so the truth is abundantly confirmed.
