On Romans 5:18: “Therefore, as through one trespass judgment came to all men
unto condemnation, so also through one righteous act the gift came to all
men unto justification of life.” Many similar statements occur repeatedly
in the same chapter. There the first and second Adams are set forth as
counterparts. It is therefore asserted that we are justified in the second
Adam in precisely the same manner as we are condemned in the first. But we
are condemned through the crediting of Adam's sin. Therefore we are
justified through the crediting of Christ's righteousness.

There was, indeed, a most beautiful twofold analogy. We become God's
righteousness in Christ in the same way that Christ was made sin for us:
namely, by imputation. The preceding passage gave us that analogy.

This passage supplies another equally beautiful one. We are accounted
righteous through Christ in the same manner as we are accounted guilty
through Adam. The latter occurs by imputation. Therefore so does the former.

The passage is especially noteworthy and highly suited to this subject.

The Apostle's extraordinarily prolonged attention to the matter proves
this. From verse 12 to the very end of the chapter he holds his ground here,
states and presses the same matter so often, and abounds in so many
transpositions, extended treatments, and repetitions that they might almost
be regarded as so many tautologies. They certainly make the course and order
of the entire argument so intricate and involved that its analysis becomes
more difficult—an analysis which, in my judgment, Piscator has disentangled
more successfully than the other interpreters I have happened to read.
Unless we would charge the most holy Apostle, even while he was writing Holy
Scripture under divine inspiration, with empty talk, this at least plainly
teaches that the matter which he repeats so often and upon which he dwells
so long is most worthy of attention.

Indeed, Socinus himself sufficiently testifies that (as I said) the passage
is especially suited to the subject before us. He speaks thus: “I judge
that nothing written in the sacred records has given you a greater occasion
for error than Paul's comparison of Christ with Adam, which he establishes
and pursues at length in chapter 5 of the Epistle to the Romans.” Whether it
has given us an occasion for error, however, or instead the strongest
argument for this truth concerning the crediting of Christ's righteousness
for our justification, remains now to be demonstrated.

According to Socinus, the summary of this passage is: “Just as Adam's
trespass and disobedience caused men to be condemned and to die, so Christ's
righteousness and obedience caused them to be absolved and to live.” With
his leave, this does not exhaust the full sense of the Apostle's argument.
Here the Apostle further expounds and confirms what he had said in the
preceding fourth chapter about righteousness credited to us. Therefore,
according to our position—which Socinus himself correctly states in the
same place—its summary is rather this: “Just as men were condemned and died
through Adam's trespass and disobedience because that trespass and disobedi-

ence were credited to them, so they are absolved and live through Christ's
righteousness and obedience because that righteousness and obedience are
credited to them.”

This argument is properly ours and, as we are persuaded, the Apostle's own
in this passage. What, then, does Socinus finally offer against it? This:
“It is false that Adam's trespass and disobedience were credited to men, and
this is nowhere written—not even in the words which the Vulgate edition
reads, ‘in whom all sinned,’ namely Romans 5:12, for those words cannot
contain this meaning.” Why not? Because, he says, the Greek words “upon
which all sinned” were wrongly rendered “in whom all sinned” by the ancient
translator. Although Socinus concedes that *epi* is sometimes used for *en*,
he nevertheless insists that the New Testament never uses “upon which” when
it expresses something's being or coming to be in something in some
respect. Therefore, in this passage “upon which” means *because*, *on
account of which*, or rather *insofar as*; this last can also be expressed
by “in which.” Accordingly, Socinus says that the Apostle's meaning in this
comparison of Christ with Adam was this: just as through one man, Adam, sin
entered the world, and death through sin, and passed to all men because or
insofar as they all personally sinned, so through the obedience of one man,
namely Christ, righteousness entered, and life through righteousness, and
thus came to all men, in whom, or because, or insofar as all obeyed. Thus
there is no mention or intention here of imputing either Adam's sin or
Christ's obedience. Rather, Adam's sin merely caused sin first to enter the
world; prompted by it, his descendants followed him and likewise sinned in
their own persons. They were accordingly made liable to death because of
their own personal sins insofar as they became such actual sinners.
Likewise, through Christ's obedience life first entered because men were in
turn made obedient from him, and through this or to this extent became
participants in life.

I reply as follows.

First, the assertion that “it is nowhere written that Adam's trespass and
disobedience were credited to men” is far too bold. For:

1. In this very chapter, Romans 5:13 (as also in 4:8), we read explicitly
about the non-imputation of sin: “Sin is not credited when there is no law.”

The Socinians will ask what this has to do with the imputation of Adam's
sin. It has everything to do with it. Although the statement is general and
holds universally in every case and concerning every person's every sin—no
one's sin is ever credited to him as guilt where no law forbidding it
exists—we must nevertheless remember the place and occasion upon which the
Apostle said it. He was there engaged in comparing the first and second
Adams and the derivation of righteousness and life from the latter with the
derivation of sin and death from the former. Therefore the imputation of
which he speaks must be understood not only of later, personal, actual sins
but also of that first, original sin of Adam. For in verse 14 he says it is
credited to infants unto death, although they are guilty not of actual sins
but only of original sin, since they sinned in Adam. Should you say that the
Apostle speaks negatively here of non-imputation—“sin is not credited where
there is no law”—and does not affirmatively say that sin is cred-

[1679 printed p. 236 | IA leaf 244]

ited, I answer that this negation includes the contrary affirmation.

The Apostle's reasoning runs as though he had said: sin is not credited
where there is no law; but it was credited before the law promulgated by
Moses. Therefore a law had previously been given to Adam and his
descendants. Adam violated it, all men violated it in him, and thereafter
each also violated it in his own person. They were thereby made guilty of
sin and liable to death.

2. Suppose it were conceded that Scripture nowhere states in express terms
that Adam's sin is credited to his descendants. We formerly answered
Bellarmine's denial that the crediting of Christ's righteousness to us
occurs word for word in Scripture. The same answer must be given here to
Socinus when he likewise denies that Adam's disobedience and unrighteousness
are credited to us: it is enough for us, as it must be enough for everyone,
if Scripture contains statements equivalent in force and the proposition
itself can be deduced by necessary consequence from what the sacred text
contains. This can be done as follows.

1. Unrighteousness is derived from Adam in the same manner as righteousness
is derived from Christ, and conversely. The Apostle's words and the course
of his argument in verses 12, 13, 14, and following plainly declare this.

But the righteousness derived to us from Christ was by imputation. We
established this from the preceding chapter, where it says explicitly twice
that God credits righteousness apart from works, in verses 6 and 11, and
affirms six times that faith is credited unto righteousness, in verses 3,
5, 9, 22, 23, and 24. The conclusion therefore necessarily follows that
unrighteousness is derived to us from Adam through imputation.

2. In addition, verse 17 reads: “For if by the trespass of the one death
reigned through the one.” Why does the Apostle appear to repeat himself in
the phrases “of the one” and “through the one”? He had already said that
death reigned through one man's trespass; why add “through one” afterward
as though redundantly? Should we follow Beza and read the earlier words from
his Codex Claromontanus as “by one trespass” rather than “by the trespass of
the one”? That is far too bold, since it is contrary to the testimony of all
the other manuscripts, to the readings of Chrysostom, the Vulgate, the
Syriac, and the Arabic, and also to the Apostle's own usage in this chapter.
For in his established comparison between the first and second Adams, the
word *one* occurs eleven times, and it always appears to be spoken not of a
thing but of the person of Adam or Christ. Let the true reading therefore
stand: “by the trespass of the one.”

Then, in order to avoid tautology, should we omit the later phrase “through
the one” with the Syriac and Arabic translators? That is not safe either.
We should firmly retain it, for, as the distinguished Louis de Dieu rightly
observed on the passage, it contains a most useful doctrine especially well
suited to our present purpose. It shows how it came about that by the
trespass of one death reigned over all: this occurred not through the
descendants' imitation, but through that same one man who committed the
trespass. The guilt of Adam's trespass was poured out and propagated through
that same Adam to his descendants, to such a degree that death also reigned
over the rest through him.

This is the by-no-means pleonastic reason for repeating “of the one” and
“through the one,” as though the Apostle had said: through one man's
trespass death reigned over all—through, I say, that one man's trespass
credited to us.
