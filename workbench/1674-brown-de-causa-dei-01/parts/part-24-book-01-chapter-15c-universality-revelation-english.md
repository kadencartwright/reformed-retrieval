He continues on page 10, section 13: “Since this is the difference between
moral and positive precepts, so that the two never coincide, it is evident that
no precept which has been given to human beings by God’s positive will alone
can be called moral, even if it were given to all, but must always be called
positive, because it cannot be deduced or known from natural law or right
reason—that is, from the nature and attributes of God or the human being.”

Reply: It is perfectly true that there is a difference between precepts which
flow from God’s positive will alone and those which are deduced from the nature
and attributes of God and the human being, or which command things having the
closest connection with them. But he has not yet given us any certain
demonstration that only the latter are to be called moral and all the rest
positive. Indeed, as will be evident from what follows, if he adheres to this
explanation, many things which are admittedly moral will not be moral but
purely positive. 2. He speaks here of right reason. But where is that right
reason? The reason by which we know anything is corrupted. Adam could deduce
or know more from natural law or right reason than we can. Things can therefore
be moral because they once possessed that character even though we are unable
to deduce or know them from natural law or right reason. Indeed, even to this
day, after the experience and industry of so many centuries and the
investigation and earnest study of so many learned people, there remain no few
things even in human affairs, in which our reason is more lynx-eyed than it is
in divine matters, concerning which our reason can render no certain judgment.
3. He has not yet proved, nor can he prove, that there is not and cannot be
some composite precept formed from both kinds: that is, a precept which is
moral with respect to its foundation, ground, and similar matters, but which
has something positive annexed with respect to easier practice, clearer
explanation, and certain necessary adjuncts. Notwithstanding that annexation,
the command considered as a whole will be moral and not positive. See what was
said in the preceding chapter.

Let us hear the argument: “Whether such a precept is given to a few, many, or
even all

[1674 logical printed p. 201 | IA leaf 219 | PDF p. 219]

human beings, whether for a time or even in perpetuity, this does not change
its nature, since it is assumed to flow not from the nature of God or the human
being but from God’s positive will. In the same way, it makes no difference
whether a king has given some positive law to his people only with respect to
a certain part, place, or condition of them, or universally and perpetually.
Provided that the law is such that the subjects cannot recognize it from the
natural duty owed to the magistrate but must learn it solely from the ruler’s
positive will, it will always be only a positive law.”

Reply: No reason drawn from the nature of the thing appears here why such laws
cannot be called moral. Are we compelled by any native force of the word to
take the term *moral* in this way and no other, just as he wishes? What proves
this? Why does he not labor to demonstrate it? We know that when something is
given to many or all in perpetuity, what flows from God’s purely positive will
cannot be said to flow from God’s nature. But he will never prove that a
precept which is given to all human beings in perpetuity should not be called
moral, although it concerns human conduct and for this reason differs greatly
from purely positive precepts which are given to this or that person, or to a
few, only for a certain time and for a special end which will shortly cease.
2. I desire to know which laws, properly enacted by kings, can be recognized
from the natural duty owed to the magistrate without any promulgation or
special legislation. He must show us such laws if he wishes to offer a
parallel. 3. The precepts which a king gives to all his subjects in perpetuity
differ greatly from those which he frames here and now and which will soon
cease and lose all force, because the former, provided they are given with
reason, are usually more necessary to the common good and can be recognized
from that fact, though not from the natural duty owed to the magistrate.

“And here,” he continues, “is that *very great demand* (μέγα λίαν αἴτημα),
which ought at least once to have been proved: that God has prescribed, or even
could prescribe, moral laws to the whole human race which are not known from
nature but depend upon revelation alone.”

Reply: 1. He himself, no less than we, is bound to prove this very great demand
if he does not wish to deny what he has conceded elsewhere and himself
asserted. For, as we have heard and will hear again, he acknowledges—indeed,
affirms and asserts—that faith in Christ is a moral duty. What then? Will he
say that this law concerning faith is known from nature and does not depend
upon revelation alone? If he does not dare assert this, he will undoubtedly
see that God has prescribed moral laws—not merely that he could prescribe
them—which are not known by nature but depend upon revelation alone. 2. The
demand which he proposes for us to prove here is greatly distorted. For first,
above he said
that moral laws flow from the nature and attributes of God and human beings.
Here, however, he requires that they be known by nature. Now let him tell me:
Are these one and the same thing? Can nothing be known from nature which does
not flow from God’s nature? If he affirms this, he must concede that God could
have implanted nothing in the human mind which depended upon his free good
pleasure. It would follow that God could not have created the human being
differently, that everything which was in the human being flowed necessarily
from God’s nature and attributes, and that absolutely nothing flowed from
God’s free choice or good pleasure. Who does not recognize the falsity and
absurdity of this? Next, can that not be said to be known from nature which is
made known through creation, created works, the entire order of things, the
manner which God followed in creating all things, and the rest after his works
were completed which he declared, signified, and proposed for imitation—and
which is thus known through the law of creation? If he

[1674 logical printed p. 202 | IA leaf 220 | PDF p. 220 | ends before Quid porrò? Si quicquam enim]

concedes this, as cannot reasonably be denied and as he himself later appears
to concede, he must also concede that some things which depend upon God’s free
good pleasure and do not flow from his nature and attributes can be known by
nature. Second, earlier he said that positive things are those which flow from
God’s free good pleasure, but here he implies that they depend upon revelation
alone, as though these were the same. Yet, as has been said, it cannot be
proved that God is unable to engrave some things upon the human soul, so that
they cannot be said to depend upon revelation alone, although they depend upon
God’s free good pleasure. What then? Did God not freely form the human being as
he wished? And if he freely formed the human being in the manner which seemed
good to him, could he not implant in the human soul knowledge of some precept
which flowed not from his nature but from his free good pleasure? Let the
adversary prove that this involves a contradiction or repugnance, and he will
say something relevant. 3. When he says “which are not known from nature,” I
would like to know whether he speaks of nature as originally constituted or
of nature now corrupted and vitiated. For if he speaks here of pure and
uncorrupted nature, the reply will be easy: we can concede the question as thus
proposed. But if he understands corrupted nature here, the reply will be
equally easy, for we deny the proposition. We can prove without any difficulty
that God has prescribed—not merely that he could prescribe—at least some moral
laws to the whole human race which are not known from this corrupted nature.
It was not known to Aristotle from nature that vengeance is a moral evil, yet
it is prohibited to the whole human race by moral law. Covetousness was not known to
Paul himself from nature (Romans 7), yet it was prohibited to the whole human
race by moral law. 4. When he adds “but depend upon revelation alone,” does he
take these words precisely and strictly, so that the meaning is whether God
has prescribed a moral law which, considered in every respect and in its
totality, depends upon revelation? If this is the meaning, we can readily
concede that God has not prescribed such a law to all, for that falls outside
our principal question. We will later show that the precept concerning the
Sabbath is not such a law. But if the words are taken more broadly, so that he
means every law which depends upon revelation in any manner or in any respect,
he does not dare assert that all such laws are not moral or that God could not
have prescribed them to the whole human race, since it is evident that no few
laws of this kind have been prescribed to all. 5. If the meaning of this
question is whether God has prescribed, or could prescribe, any moral law to
the whole human race and has implanted, or could implant, knowledge of it in
the human soul, even though it did not flow necessarily from his nature and
attributes but from his free good pleasure, we will immediately prove that God
not only could prescribe such laws but has in fact prescribed them.
