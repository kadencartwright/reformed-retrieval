## CHAPTER VIII

### On the Ceremonial Law

ALTHOUGH it is not our purpose to treat these kinds of Divine Laws more fully,
we nevertheless judge it not useless, but serviceable to our purpose, to set
down some explanation of them beforehand. We shall therefore treat the
Ceremonial Law first.

Some derive this word *ceremony* from *Caere*, a town near Rome, in which the
sacred objects of the Romans were deposited and held in the greatest reverence
when Rome itself had been captured by the Gauls, as Thomas, part 1 of the
second part, question 99, article 3, in the body, reports from Valerius Maximus.
Or it derives from Ceres, who was called the goddess of crops, because offerings
from crops were first offered to God. And thus *ceremony* is the same as *the
duties of Ceres*, or gifts. See Thomas in the same place. Others derive it from
*carendo*, as Massurius Scabinus does in Gellius, book 4, chapter 9, according to
the very celebrated Voetius, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 2, treatise
1, chapter 3, and Matthias Martinius in his *Lexicon*. Others derive it from
*caritas*. Still others derive it from the adjective *carus*, because among the
ancients it was synonymous with τῷ *sanctus*; and thus *ceremony* comes from
*cerus*, just as *sanctimonia* comes from *sanctus*. Voetius, with Scaliger,
judges this more probable in the same place. From this he thinks someone might
not ineptly conjecture that the Etruscan discipline derived this from the
Chaldeans' קרא, that is, “he read, prayed, invoked,” because hearing God speak
in the word and speaking to God through prayers are proper acts of a holy and
religious man. See Martinius's *Lexicon* under the word *cerus*.

The Greek words corresponding to it in the sacred writings are ἔθος, “custom,
rite,” Acts 21; δόγμα, “decree,” Colossians 2; and δικαίωμα, “ordinance,” the
word by which the Septuagint renders the Hebrew word חק. This is not because
the Israelites' observance of these ceremonies was righteousness before God,
but because they
were types and shadows of Christ and his benefits, as Apollonius speaks in *On
the Law of God*, page 371. He also holds that these ceremonies are designated
by the term λατρείας, “acts of worship,” in Romans 9:4 and Hebrews 9:1. The
Hebrew words corresponding to this are משפטים and חקים, but the latter is more
usual, especially whenever these two are found together. For then political
constitutions are meant by משפטים.

Some ceremonies are *Divine*, others *Human*. Both kinds are either *Political*
or *Ecclesiastical*. A *human political* or civil ceremony, as the very
celebrated Voetius explains in the place cited above, page 376, is a certain
external formality or manner of acting among—

[1674 logical printed p. 97 | IA leaf 115 | PDF p. 115]

—human beings that has become customary either by law or by custom. It is
twofold: either grounded upon and deduced from natural reason and necessity,
whether for being or for well-being, as natural decorum; or received by the
mere choice and institution of a human being—in the forum, court, palace,
school, camps, colleges, crossroads, banquets, weddings, games, funerals, and
so forth. See its subdivision in the same author and place. A *human
Ecclesiastical ceremony* is one observed in the church and about exercises of
ecclesiastical religion; it is also called an *adiaphoron*, decorum, or rite.
But these ceremonies, which have a human being as their author, do not pertain
here.

A *divine ceremony* is one which God instituted and which is therefore
necessary to observe. It is either *Political* or *Ecclesiastical*. A political
ceremony in turn is either *General and perpetual*, as are all observances of
natural decorum—for example, those concerning garments and hair, by which a
man is distinguished from a woman, Deuteronomy 25:5 and 1 Corinthians
11:13–14—or *Particular*, as were those ceremonies of the Mosaic forensic law
in Exodus 21:6 and Deuteronomy 25:9. We also pass over these political
ceremonies, for.

Here we speak only about *divine Ecclesiastical Ceremonies*, which are also
sacred, mystical, and religious, and which pertained particularly to the
economy of the Old Testament.

The celebrated Voetius, in the place cited above, page 377, testifies that
authors take an *Ecclesiastical ceremony* in various ways and senses. “At times,”
he says, “it is taken broadly for all instituted worship, or for the whole of
religion, which embraces all divine duties and their manner, order, and
formality. At times it is taken strictly for certain observances about those
duties, and at the same time for all the proximate adjuncts and requisites of
divine duties, such as persons, utensils or instruments, places, times, the
order of acting, and so forth. At times it is taken most strictly, and this in
three ways: either for a circumstantial action about those actions which are
integral parts of instituted divine worship and are specifically called divine
duties and sacred things to be done; or for the order in executing divine
duties and in applying and using their adjunct circumstantial actions; or for
the observance of the circumstances of place and time.” Here we take it neither
in the broadest nor in the most strict sense. Nor do we take it generally in
the stricter sense, but restrict it to Jewish worship, or the economy of the
Old Testament.

Therefore, here we do not take *ceremony* for any ecclesiastical and external
rite pertaining to external worship, because such rites have also been
instituted in the New Testament, but for those rites which had been instituted
by God in the Jewish Church.

Some hold that the ceremonial law is the same as a mutable and temporary law,
as if a ceremony were χρῆμα τῆς εἰς καιρὸν χρείας, “a thing for temporary use.”
But although it is true that the ceremonial law was given only for a time, if
*ceremony* is the same as something mutable, the Ceremonial law will be
confounded with the Judicial or Forensic law, for the latter too, insofar as it
regarded the Jewish commonwealth, is also mutable. It is indeed true that many
Judicial provisions were joined and intertwined with Ceremonial ones, yet no
sane person will deny that they were distinct. For Moral provisions too
sometimes had Ceremonial ones joined to them, although they are nevertheless
entirely distinct in themselves.

There are some who understand by *Ceremonial things* only *figurative things*,
which were of three kinds: either *Memorials* of things past, *Signs* of things
present, or *Types* of things future. The Passover and certain other ceremonies
were indeed both *Memorial*, *Significative, and Typical*. But it cannot be
proved that all—

[1674 logical printed p. 98 | IA leaf 116 | PDF p. 116]

—Memorials of things past or Signs of things present are properly Ceremonial,
as Ceremonial things are distinguished from Moral ones, or that they are
therefore Mutable or temporary.

We refer to the ceremonial law those typical ordinances in worship which
prefigured Christ, his χαρίσματα (gifts), actions, sufferings, and benefits;
which were lessons in various spiritual and moral duties; and which were prescribed to
the Jewish Church, as such, in order to exercise it rather severely in that
infantile state. See Hebrews 9 throughout and 10:1; Galatians 4:1–4; and
Colossians 2:16–17. Thus the laws concerning Sacrifices, Washings, the
Sacraments peculiar to them, Distinctions of Persons, Places, Times, and Foods,
Leprosy, and uncleanness of that kind pertained here. To enumerate every one of
these things here does not much pertain to our present purpose.

Thomas says in part 1 of the second part, question 101, article 1, that the
rationale of ceremonial precepts consists in this, that they pertain to the
worship of God. And indeed, if *worship* is taken broadly and those things are
also said to pertain to worship which, although they do not appear to be
immediately ordered toward God, are nevertheless preparatory and are required
beforehand in those who worship God, this can be admitted. For in this way the
precepts concerning abstinence from certain foods, garments, and similar
things can be said to pertain to worship, because by them the Israelites were
rendered fit to worship God externally. Yet it cannot be denied that there
were certain ceremonies which did not immediately pertain to the worship of
God but were lessons in certain moral duties, as appears in 1 Corinthians 5:7,
2 Corinthians 6:17, and Jude 23.

When we say that this Ceremonial Law was given to the Israelites, or to the
Jewish Nation insofar as they constituted such a Church, we mean that
illustrious dispensation which had been established through the ministerial
work of Moses, in which all things had been ordered more fully and plainly. We
do not mean that God had no Church before that time, or that no Ceremonies had
been instituted before then. For just as God always had a Church, so from the
beginning of the Church God communicated the promises of the Gospel and other
means of salvation; and before the time of reformation, it pleased God to teach
his people through shadowy ceremonies. Thus in that earlier period, namely,
from Adam to Moses, various ceremonial precepts were given: concerning
sacrifices, Genesis 4:3–5; Hebrews 11:4; Genesis 8:20–21; 12:7–8; and 13:4, 18;
concerning the distinction between clean and unclean animals, Genesis 7:2;
concerning not eating blood, Genesis 9:4–5; and concerning circumcision,
Genesis 17:10.

Thomas must not be heard when he says in part 1 of the second part, question
103, article 1, that those sacrifices were offered from a certain devotion of
their own will, for this would be the sin of ἐθελοθρησκείας, self-imposed
worship. But the Apostle expressly says in Hebrews 11:4 that “Abel sacrificed
by faith.” Faith, however, presupposes the word of institution and rests upon
the word; it does not rest upon voluntary devotion or the commandments of
human beings. Colossians 2:22–23; Matthew 15:3–4, 6. Furthermore, Abel was
rewarded by some visible testimony, as is clear from Cain's indignation. But
who will say that God was willing to accept in this way worship not instituted
by himself?

Nor should the other papists be heard any more when, in order to defend the
sacrifice of the mass, they refer the institution of sacrifices to the Light
of Nature. For it follows from this that Adam, even in the state of innocence,
willed or ought to have offered sacrifices to God. But—

[1674 logical printed p. 99 | IA leaf 117 | PDF p. 117]

—this cannot be proved from the Scriptures, in which we first read about the
sacrifices of Abel. Furthermore, sacrifices bear a relation to the Mediator
and consequently to the covenant of grace, Hebrews 9:18, 22.

The Scholastics, with Thomas in part 1 of the second part, question 101,
article 4, divide these ceremonies into Sacrifices, Sacraments, Sacred Things,
and Observances. Thomas illustrates this division as follows in the same
place: “In worship there can be considered, first, the worship itself, which
consists especially in sacrifices. Next there are the worshipers, whose
institution must be considered; this is effected by a certain consecration of
either the people or the ministers, and the Sacraments pertain to this. Then
there is their particular way of life, by which they are distinguished from
those who do not worship God; and,” he says, “observances in foods, garments,
and other such things pertain to this. Finally, there are the instruments of
worship, to which Sacred Things pertain, that is, the tabernacle, vessels, and
things of that kind.”

Others consider the object of ceremonies in the *Sacred Things to be
performed*, in *Persons*, and in *Times*. It is not part of our purpose,
however, to treat these ceremonies. Let the reader consult others: Tilenus's
*Syntagma*, part 1, disputation 44; Dudley Fenner's *Theology*, chapter 8; and
especially the very celebrated Essenius's *Larger System*, disputation 6 under
title 11, *On the Gospel Covenant*.

These ceremonies are indeed called “ordinances” by the Apostle in Ephesians
2:15 and Colossians 2:14. This is not because they rested upon the mere choice
of the legislator and proceeded from no underlying rationale, because, as
Apollonius says in the place cited above, pages 371–372, “those laws were so
placed in ordinances that there was undoubtedly a mystical and spiritual
explanation and signification of them, which aimed either at pressing the
coercive enforcement (ἐκβιοδίωξιν) of the moral law, at shadowing forth and
sealing the future redemption in Christ, or at instilling a tropological
formation of morals.”
They were indeed matters of Positive and Arbitrary Right, not simply of
Natural and Necessary Right perpetually binding; yet these precepts were not
ἄλογα, irrational, or unsupported by just reasons. For they were all
instituted from divine wisdom and therefore ordered toward some end, from
which their rational causes can be assigned, as Thomas says in the place cited
above, question 102, article 1, in the body. He should be consulted in the
following articles, where he proves that an appropriate rationale can be
assigned for the ceremonies which pertained to sacrifices in article 3, for
the ceremonies which pertained to sacred things in article 4, for the
sacraments in article 5, and finally for the observances in article 6.

The aforesaid authors—namely, Tilenus, Fenner, and most lucidly the very
celebrated Essenius—sufficiently show what those particular ends were, and what
their significations and moral equities were. Apollonius, however, in the
place cited above, chapter 12, thesis 6, reduces them to a few heads. The first
and chief is the *signification and foreshadowing* of Christ and his benefits,
Colossians 2:17; Hebrews 8:4–5; 9:9; and 10:1, and their application to the
faithful through sacraments and sacrifices. For although those ceremonies,
considered in themselves and abstractly from their σχέσει, relation, and
Gospel signification—that is, materially, as acts necessarily to be observed
by reason of God's precept—are referred to the law, insofar as they were
instituted to signify spiritual things and to instruct the faithful in the
way of their eternal salvation promised through Christ, they must be referred
to the Gospel. For they announced Christ beforehand, signified him, and
shadowed him forth in various types, so that by this aid the faith and hope of
the devout might be raised toward him and might, in waiting for him and
expecting redemption and remission of sins through him—

[1674 logical printed p. 100 | IA leaf 118 | PDF p. 118]

—be exercised and confirmed. And after certain other things, on page 392, he
has this: “And surely God willed to be worshiped through ceremonies in such a
way that he chiefly willed attention to be paid in them not so much to what
they were as to what they signified. It is very easy to prove this. For if the
performance of these things in itself, and, as is commonly said, *ex opere
operato*, had constituted worship pleasing and legitimate to God, he would not
so often have protested in the Scriptures that he abhorred and detested all
these rites considered only in themselves. To this point pertain Psalm
50:9–15; 51:18–19; Isaiah 1:10–14; 66:3; and Micah 6:6–8. Far be it from us to
believe that God was delighted by the odor of incense or the smell of victims;
even very many pagans acknowledged that this is remote from his nature and
perfection. Therefore, those ceremonies regarded something else through which
their observance was so pleasing to God. John the Baptist also teaches that
Christ was that Lamb of God who takes away the sins of the world, John 1:29,
showing that the other lambs offered according to the ceremonial law were not
in reality such as could take away sins, but were only types or figures; and
that this Jesus was that true victim whose offering is true expiation before
God. All the other sacrifices looked to this victim as shadows to the body.
We conclude, therefore, that the principal end of the Ceremonial law was to
signify and exhibit Christ and his benefits.”

He further enumerates some other less principal and secondary ends, such as:
1. *a reminder of sin*—of its uncleanness, gravity, and desert, Hebrews 10:3;
2. *a mark of profession and a sign and distinction of the people of God* from
all other nations, for the ceremonies were as it were a hedge and wall,
Ephesians 2:14; Deuteronomy 4:8; 12:30; 3. *an exercise of worship and
obedience toward God*; 4. “they were *the sinews of public assemblies, bonds
uniting the people, and supports for preserving the ecclesiastical ministry*”;
and 5. finally, “they *provided many useful reminders*” concerning the worship
of God and the exercises and conduct of holiness. Hence came the prohibition
of leaven at Passover, 1 Corinthians 5:7, and the use of salt in every
sacrifice, Leviticus 2:13 compared with Mark 9:50.

There are some who hold that these ceremonies are meant by “those precepts of
human beings” about which the Apostle speaks in Colossians 2:22. Yet although
it is true that the Apostle also speaks there, namely in verses 14 and 17,
about the Mosaic ceremonies, it nevertheless does not appear that “those
precepts of human beings” are Mosaic ceremonies. For among them he counts the
“worship of angels” in verse 18, which God never prescribed to the Jews through
Moses. This is also clearer from Christ's words in Matthew 15:9: “In vain they
worship me, teaching doctrines which are commandments of human beings.” Those
doctrines, however, were not the legal ceremonies, which were still in force.
It is therefore better to understand Jewish traditions by those “precepts of
human beings.” Beza says in the margin that these words contain the Apostle's
third argument: namely, “Because God is not the author of those traditions,
and therefore they do not bind consciences.” It can indeed be that certain
ceremonies prescribed by Moses are also meant here, but with respect to the
superstitions attached to them through Jewish traditions. For they
superstitiously added many things beyond God's prescription. Furthermore, Dr.
Beza notes on verse 20 that “in this last passage the Apostle fights against
those corruptions which were mere superstitions and were devised by human
beings—partly by those who in their cunning deceived the simplicity of some,
and partly by those who were plainly ridiculous—as when piety, remission of—”

[1674 logical printed p. 101 | IA leaf 119 | PDF p. 119]

“—sins, and finally some efficacy is placed in a certain kind of foods and in
other similar things, although not even the authors of rites of that sort
understand it, since it exists nowhere.”

These ceremonies are also called by the Apostle in Galatians 4:9 “the meager
and weak elements of the world”—namely, in opposition to Christ, but not
insofar as they led to Christ as exhibited and promised while they remained in
force. For in this respect they were not empty shadows, but led to the Christ
who was to come; in their own less clear way they set him forth as present and
exhibited him to those exercising faith. But they are called “elements of the
world,” as Apollonius notes, both “because they consisted in the observance of
many earthly and worldly things”—insofar as they are called carnal rites in
Hebrews 9:10 and “the sanctuary consecrated to their use” is called worldly in
Hebrews 9:1—and “because, just as all worldly things pass away, they had to
last only until the appointed time of reformation,” Hebrews 9:11.

They are also said to have been “the dividing wall,” Ephesians 2:14, because
the Jews were separated from the Gentiles by this ceremonial worship, so that
they could not unite with them; and while that law remained in force, the
Gentiles could not be admitted into the people of God. Hence also they are
called “hostilities” in verse 15, and in Colossians 2:14 they are called “the
handwriting which was against us.” So too they are called a “yoke” which could
not be borne in Acts 15:10, and a “yoke of slavery” in Galatians 5:1.

From the very nature and institution of these ceremonies it is sufficiently
clear that they could be abrogated. For they were instituted until “the
appointed time of reformation,” Hebrews 9:10. They “were a shadow of good
things to come,” Hebrews 10:1; Colossians 2:17. This is also clear from the
prophetic oracles in Isaiah 11 and 60; Jeremiah 31:31–34; Daniel 9:15; and
Malachi 1, 2, and 3:3–4. Hence some observe that when Moses speaks about the
Decalogue, he often speaks absolutely and calls it a *covenant*, without a
determination of time and place, to indicate that the moral precepts were not
bound to some fixed place or time. But to the other Forensic and Ceremonial
precepts he adds a determination to a fixed place, namely the land of Canaan
and its possession, and consequently also to the time for which that
possession would last. Deuteronomy 4:5, 14, 40; 6:1; 11:32; 12:1. Furthermore,
very many of those ceremonies were bound to the place which God had chosen.
They were also adapted to the infantile state of that Church so long as it was
to be under a pedagogue, and they were a “partition wall.” They are also
called “elements of the world” and “carnal rites” in Hebrews 9:10, and a “yoke
of slavery” in Galatians 5:1 and Acts 15:10. All these and similar titles
sufficiently indicate both the use and nature of those Ceremonies and prove
their mutability. Nor is it necessary for us to pursue this more fully, since
all reason recoils from calling that law eternal and immutable whose causes,
foundation, and principle are not eternal and immutable. This is more than
sufficiently proved everywhere by the orthodox. See the celebrated Essenius's
*System of Theology*, disputation 14 under title 11, *On the Gospel Covenant*,
thesis 26 and following, and Apollonius in the place cited above, pages 399–400
and following.

In this question concerning the abrogation of ceremonies, theologians commonly
distinguish three times with Augustine. The first was before Christ's death,
when they were salutary, having been instituted to nourish, foster, and confirm
the faith of the devout. The second was after the promulgation of the Gospel,
when they were not only unwholesome but deadly and lethal to those using them,
Galatians 4:1–4, 9. The third was the intermediate time, from Christ's death to
the promulgation of the Gospel, when they—

[1674 logical printed p. 102 | IA leaf 120 | PDF p. 120 | ends before CAP. IX]

—were indeed dead in themselves but not immediately deadly to those, namely,
who did not fix their hope upon them or attach to their use the opinion that
they were necessary, as the orthodox Tilenus speaks in his *Syntagma*.
“Therefore, during that intermediate
time,” as Apollonius says in the place cited above, page 412, “the Synagogue's
funeral ought to have been attended to and carried out honorably. Thus those
who try to dig up its corpse, once buried and covered with earth, and to bring
it back into the church, both sacrilegiously violate its grave and ruin the
church as it collapses and groans miserably under the mass of Jewish
ceremonies.” Hence comes that very true axiom of the theologians: The
ceremonial law is dead, and if it is recalled by a restoration to its former
right, it becomes deadly. This is very true.

The Socinians devise these causes for the abrogation of the ceremonial law:
namely, because the ceremonies were plainly contrary to the religion of
Christ; rested upon the legislator's choice; were mere shadows which could
neither strengthen their followers in piety nor raise them to hope for more
excellent goods; and because by this means he willed to testify that Christ
exists as the true king of that people. But these causes must certainly be
rejected, because the religion of Christ also prevailed under the Old
Testament according to that economy. Those ceremonies were not contrary or
poorly suited to that religion, for they all led to Christ. Nor does it follow
that every positive thing, or what depends upon the legislator's choice, has
been abrogated. Furthermore, in opposition to Christ, they were indeed
shadows, but such shadows as, while they remained in force, exhibited Christ
to the faithful, and therefore were spiritual means according to that economy,
so that those who used them rightly were strengthened and confirmed in piety
and could also be raised to the best hope. As for the last reason, it is clear
that Christ did not abrogate them while he was on earth, or before he removed
on the cross the handwriting which was contrary to us, Colossians 2:14;
Ephesians 2:15.

The causes which Apollonius assigns in *On the Law of God*, page 409, are of a
better kind. Namely, the now more mature age of the Church required that it be
freed from this pedagogy. Because Jews and Gentiles united into one through
Christ's benefit, that partition wall had to be removed. Once Christ, whose
coming they signified, had been exhibited, they could no longer serve that
purpose. They had been appointed only for a fixed time, namely until the time
of διορθώσεως, reformation, Hebrews 10:18. Once Christ had been exhibited,
that time arrived. They were therefore null by right itself.
