QUESTION XIX.

CHRIST’S SESSION AT GOD’S RIGHT HAND.

*What is Christ’s session at God’s right hand, according to which nature does
it belong to Christ, and does it pertain to the notion of bodily position? We
deny the last.*

I. This question is necessary because of the Lutherans, who contend that
Christ sits at God’s right hand according to his human nature in order to
prove the omnipresence and omnipotence of that nature. But so that the matter
may be more easily understood, a few things must first be said about the
session itself. From these it will readily be gathered how it belongs to
Christ.

[Marginal heading: The session at God’s right hand must be understood not
literally but figuratively.]

II. The session at God’s right hand cannot be understood properly and
literally, since God has neither a right nor a left hand. It must be understood
figuratively and metaphorically to designate Christ’s supreme dignity and
sovereignty. The metaphor is drawn from the custom of kings and nobles, who
are accustomed to place at their right hand those to whom they grant the
degree nearest themselves in both honor and power of government. This phrase
is understood of the nearest degree of honor in 1 Kings 2:19, where Solomon,
intending to honor his mother singularly, places her at his right hand. And in
Psalm 45:9 the king’s bride, that is, the church, is said to stand at the
Messiah’s right hand, that is, in the degree of dignity nearest him. It is used
of sovereignty, or the administration of a kingdom, in Matthew 20:21, where
the mother of Zebedee’s sons asks that her sons sit at Christ’s right and left
hands in his kingdom, that is, that they discharge its principal offices.

III. From this the nature of Christ’s session at the Father’s right hand is
easily understood. Two things are principally designated here: 1. Supreme
majesty and glory, by which he was supremely exalted by God and received the
name above every name, Philippians 2:9–10; Hebrews 1:3. 2. Supreme sovereignty,
which he powerfully exercises over all creatures and displays especially in
the government and defense of the church. Thus Paul explains it in 1
Corinthians 15:25, where he expounds τὸ *sedere ad dextram* (“to sit at the
right hand”), Psalm 110:1, by βασιλεύειν *regnare* (“to reign”): “He must reign
until he has placed his
enemies,” and so forth. Likewise in Ephesians 1:22 this session is expounded by
the subjection of all things beneath his feet, and in 1 Peter 3:22: “Having
gone into heaven, he is at God’s right hand, with angels, authorities, and
powers subjected to him.”

[Marginal heading: The session belongs properly not to the natures but to the
person.]

IV. From this explanation of Christ’s session, it is easy to gather how it
belongs to Christ, or what is the subject of this session. Is it simply the
human nature, as the Lutherans would wish in order to support their hypothesis
about the ubiquity (πανταχουσίᾳ) of Christ’s flesh? Or the divine nature, as
others hold? Or the person? The last is the position received among the
orthodox which we follow: the session properly belongs to Christ’s person
according to both natures, but with a certain distinction.

V. The reason is that the session at the right hand is not a property of
either the divine or the human nature, but a dignity of the person—namely, the
glorious state of Christ’s person and the administration of the mediatorial
office, whose effects (ἀποτελέσματα) are common to the whole person with
respect to both natures. Thus the Word (Λόγος) does not say to the flesh, “Sit
at my right hand,” but the Father says it to the Son who was called to the
mediatorial office. Here power must also be distinguished from its exercise.
The subject of the power is the person, or Christ the God-man (θεάνθρωπος)
manifested in the flesh, and not simply the natures, because that power is not
an absolute accident which necessarily inheres in the natures, but a simple
relation, just as

royal power belongs neither to the king’s soul nor to his body but to the
suppositum. The subject of its exercise, however, is each nature: the divine
through its omnipotence and omnipresence, and the human through those excellent
gifts and the glory by which it excels all creatures.

VI. With respect to the person, therefore, this session consists in that
supreme glory and power granted to him through exaltation, by which he was
installed as king and head of the church. With respect to the human nature, it
consists in the glory and honor with which it was adorned above all creatures
and which it previously lacked. With respect to the divine nature, it consists
in the manifestation of the power and majesty which had previously lain hidden
beneath the veil of weak flesh and a servant’s condition.

VII. Just as the passion, death, and crucifixion belong subjectively to the
human nature in such a way that they are also predicated denominatively of the
person—in which sense actions and passions belong to supposita—so the session,
which entails a real communication of honor and glory with respect to the
human nature, also denotes a relation of dominion and power which is proper to
the person.

VIII. When Christ is viewed theologically simply as the Word (Λόγος), he is
rightly called God’s own right hand, insofar as he shares the same omnipotent
essence of God with the Father. But when considered economically as mediator,
he is rightly said to sit at God’s right hand. In the former sense, God’s right
hand refers to divine omnipotence; in the latter it refers metaphorically to
the sovereignty and dominion which properly belong to the person. It can also
be attributed to the divine nature in time, if not with respect to the grant
of a new thing not yet possessed, then with respect to the manifestation of a
thing previously possessed but still hidden.

IX. Although omnipotence and omnipresence are required for the exercise of the
power granted to Christ, it does not follow that they were given to Christ’s
human nature. It is enough that they belong to Christ’s suppositum by reason of
the divine nature. Thus the human nature was made a participant in this
sovereignty, but in the manner of a creature, which it always remains. The
power and operations of the deity by which he gloriously administers his
kingdom are divine and equal to God, because as God he is equal to the Father.
But the actions of the humanity, although more perfect than the actions of all
other creatures, are nevertheless not equal to the divine actions, because he
indeed sits at God’s right hand according to both natures, but with the truth
of each nature preserved.

[Marginal heading: When did Christ begin to sit at God’s right hand?]

X. From this it is easy to answer the question commonly raised here about the
time of Christ’s session, or when he began to sit. Was it from eternity, as
Thomas, Maldonatus, and others hold, who interpret the session as the Son’s
equality with the Father? Or from the very moment of the incarnation, as
Brentius and his followers hold, who explain this session as the personal
union of the flesh with the Word (λόγῳ) and the communication of properties to
the flesh? Or only after the resurrection and ascension, as we maintain with
the orthodox?

XI. First, because Scripture expressly places Christ’s session after his
resurrection and ascension, Ephesians 1:20; Mark 16:19; Hebrews 10:12; 1 Peter
3:21–22. Second, because it is the fruit of his emptying and death, Hebrews
1:3; 12:2. Third, because he could not gloriously administer the mediatorial
kingdom until his enemies had been conquered on the cross and in the
ascension.

XII. Christ as the Word (Λόγος) was indeed with the Father from eternity, but
he cannot on that account be said to have sat at God’s right hand in the sense
in which the session is attributed to him after the resurrection as the
mediator’s glorious state. He reigned as Son in the kingdom of nature, but not
as God-man (θεάνθρωπος) in the economic kingdom. Christ as mediator can be
said in a certain sense to have sat at God’s right hand as *without flesh*
(ἀσάρκος), even before the incarnation and from the beginning of the world,
because he was always the head and king of the church who governed and
defended it, Psalm 2:6; Hebrews 13:8. But

as *in the flesh* (ἔνσαρκος), he sat only after the passion and resurrection,
Luke 24:26. He indeed possessed the right to the kingdom from the hypostatic
union, but he did not obtain its actual possession until after the ascension,
Psalm 110:1; Acts 2:34–36.

XIII. No limit is set to the subsequent duration of the session, because it
must be “in perpetuity” (εἰς τὸ διηνεκές), according to the apostle, Hebrews
10:12. Nor do the words of Psalm 110:1 oppose this, where he is said to sit
“until” (עד כי) his enemies are placed as his footstool. For it is well known
that this particle asserts the definite period under discussion and about
which there can be doubt without denying its continuation in the future, as
is gathered from Genesis 28:15; Matthew 28:20; 1 Timothy 4:13. Thus Christ’s
dominion is asserted here in the midst of his enemies, that is, in the most
difficult time, but it is not denied for the future, because his kingdom must
be eternal. There must indeed be a change in the manner of reigning, but not
in the kingdom itself, as will be seen in its proper place.

XIV. Furthermore, this session of Christ at the Father’s right hand does not
denote the position or location of Christ’s body, since it is a state of
Christ’s person. Nor can this be imputed to us except through slander, as if
God’s right hand were a place for us and the session at his right hand were a
particular local position. Heaven, in which Christ is said to sit at God’s
right hand, is indeed for us a place in which he sits at God’s right hand as
in a royal palace and a sanctuary not made by hands. But none of us says that
God’s right hand is a place.

XV. Yet the error which the ubiquists falsely fasten upon us we rightly
attribute to them according to their own hypothesis. For they infer the
ubiquity of Christ’s body from his session at God’s right hand on the ground
that God’s right hand is everywhere. They cannot argue in this way without
supposing and conceiving of God’s right hand as a kind of place and the
session as a local position.

XVI. As for the argument drawn from Christ’s session at God’s right hand in
favor of ubiquity, besides what has already been said above in Question 8,
section 29—and to say nothing of the form of the syllogism, which is plainly
faulty because it has four terms, since from the fact that God’s right hand is
everywhere it concludes that the one who sits at God’s right hand must be
everywhere, although God’s right hand and sitting at God’s right hand are far
different, nor must one who sits at God’s right hand immediately be wherever
that right hand is, especially since that session does not designate a local
position but the conferral of sovereignty and glory—it is certain: 1. The
state and office of the person are here confused with a property of the
nature, because ubiquity is a property of the nature, whereas the session at
the right hand is a state and office of the person. 2. Figurative and
metaphorical speech is confused with proper speech. For τὸ *sedere ad dextram*
(“to sit at the right hand”) is said figuratively, whereas being everywhere is
understood
properly. 3. Glory and dignity are confused with omnipresence. For something
glorious need not therefore be omnipresent if omnipresence destroys its proper
nature, as occurs here. To posit an omnipresent body is to call a body not a
body. 4. Christ’s flesh is confused with his sovereignty, as if the essence of
the flesh extended as widely as his kingdom and sovereignty. This is indeed
true in God, but not in the flesh, however glorified it may be. Nor must the
flesh be present wherever he reigns, any more than a king must be bodily
present in all the provinces of his kingdom, especially since Christ is
everywhere present with respect to the person by reason of the divine nature.

XVII. Although receiving the name above every name also belongs in its own way
to the assumed nature, it must nevertheless be understood so that the
properties of the natures are preserved according to Scripture. Thus the Son
is also glorified according to the flesh, but not so that the flesh is made
equal to the Word (Λόγῳ). Nor do we thereby divide Christ any more or attribute
two glories and powers to him, any more than, when we say that man was created
in God’s image

[Printed p. 327 | Internet Archive PDF 355 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and afterward made corruptible, we therefore either make the body equal to
the soul or cast the soul down to the body’s degradation.

XVIII. It is one thing to say that Christ is present everywhere, endowed with
glory equal to the Father, and fills all things; it is another to attribute
this to Christ’s flesh. For what is said concretely of the person cannot or
should not immediately be drawn to the natures in the abstract. Although it is
true that Christ the man is God, one would nevertheless speak wrongly by
saying that this flesh is divinity, because the Son of God became man not by a
mingling or confusion but by the unconfused and unmixed union of the two
natures.

XIX. It is one thing for a glorified body to be made spiritual with respect to
its qualities by reason of splendor, agility, subtlety, and the like; it is
another for it to become spirit, or to be changed into the nature of a spirit,
and indeed of an immeasurable and infinite spirit. Through exaltation and the
session at God’s right hand, Christ’s body was indeed made spiritual with
respect to its qualities, but it was not changed into spirit with respect to
its substance or essential properties, much less into an omnipresent spirit.

XX. Finally, if there appears to be some diversity among our theologians in
explaining Christ’s session at God’s right hand, some place it in four things:
1. The equality of the Word (τοῦ Λόγου), or the mediator’s person, with the
Father, which he did not receive in the session but manifested after it had
been hidden during the time of humiliation. 2. The perfection of the
mediatorial office, through which he was constituted king and head of the
church. 3. The perfection of Christ’s humanity, which was carried to supreme
glory. 4. The perfection of the honor and worship by which Christ is worshiped
and adored as Lord and king of the church. Others place it solely in the
communication of honor and sovereignty conferred upon him after the ascension.
The disagreement is not so much in the thing itself as in the manner of
teaching it. The former consider this session more broadly and describe it not
only from its proper form but also from its principle or foundation—namely,
equality with the Father, without which he would not have been capable of such
great honor and sovereignty—and from its antecedents and consequences, which
are necessarily joined to it. The latter view it more strictly with respect to
the form of the session alone, which properly consists in the perfection of
the mediatorial office by which Christ, crowned with glory and honor, rules in
the midst of his enemies.
