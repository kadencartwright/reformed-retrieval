# On Various Articles

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin presentation at CO
X/1 258–262. CO reports that Theodore Beza translated the work from French.
The unlocated French wording has not been reconstructed: the translation below
is accountable to Beza's printed Latin witness and does not present that Latin
as Calvin's own wording. Biblical language is translated directly from the
Latin. No wording from Beaty and Farley's modern translation or a modern
English Bible supplied the English.

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 258]

Dearest brothers, because this writing concerns for the most part each of the
churches among you, we have thought it most convenient to send you our common
letter. We do so not so much to spare ourselves labor as to avoid spending
many words to no purpose in separate letters to each church, and especially
so that you may be persuaded that in different letters we give no different
answer, since we wish our agreement to be equally evident to everyone.

First, we have learned that some people disapprove of the practice that, when
they are questioned about the faith and found to be less well instructed, they
are not admitted to the Supper for a time, until they have made better
progress. In the Lord's name, we ask those who dislike this strictness not to
indulge themselves in their own harm, nor to seek an indulgence which would
contribute to their destruction. That statement of the apostle ought to be
well known among Christians: whoever eats the Lord's bread unworthily is held
guilty of sacrilege (1 Corinthians 11:27). For anyone to use it worthily, he
requires each person to examine himself. There he teaches clearly enough that
those who are not fit to examine themselves ought not to approach this table.

Whoever, then, rashly approaches the Supper without first being instructed in
the faith provokes God's wrath. Those who are accordingly barred from
approaching as unworthy have no cause for complaint, since their salvation is
instead being sought in this way. If each person had enough wisdom, each would
be his own judge. But because many pay too little attention to themselves, it
is expedient for a fixed order to exist in the church, as has always been the
case, so that the sacraments are not profaned. For where everyone is admitted
indiscriminately and without selection, there is a contempt which the Lord
cannot tolerate.

The Lord himself distributed the Supper only to disciples. Therefore, whoever
has not been instructed in the doctrine of the Gospel ought not to approach
the Lord's institution. So let no one take offense when at least the barest
test is made of his Christianity as he is to be admitted to the Supper. The
same judgment must be made concerning the whole state and order of the
discipline which ought to flourish in the church: those judged unworthy are
not to be admitted. We know this seems novel to people who do not grasp the
difference between the yoke of our Lord Jesus Christ and the tyranny of the
papacy. But it is your part to look instead to what the Lord's

### [CO X/1, p. 259]

will is, so that you submit yourselves to him, rather than loosen the reins on
your own desires.

Indeed, the ancient example of unbelievers ought here to strike us with shame.
Although they did not show in their superstitions the reverence which we must
show in the sacraments, they nevertheless held it as an acknowledged principle
that anyone considered polluted by some crime was to be barred from them.

When the Lord's Supper is forbidden to someone, let him not think that he is
being excluded perpetually or cast into despair. Rather, let him understand
that this is done so that he may humble himself and others may learn through
him. Since all this is prescribed by God's Word, we ask you by the Lord not to
be ashamed to submit yourselves to a practice which you know by experience to
be both right and holy.

We also do not doubt that among you there are men selected and appointed to
correct scandals, admonish those who have sinned, and recognize those who
conduct themselves honorably. Those called to these offices must display such
humanity and moderation that no one, under the pretext of censure, is defamed
or oppressed by slanders, false accusations, or suspicions.

We mention this expressly so that admonitions which are made are admitted in
such a way that the person whom they concern is also heard, if he has any
defense, lest a window be opened for disparaging and harassing the innocent.
It will therefore be expedient for someone to be reported to the elders as
duty requires, but also for the person reported to be allowed to clear
himself. If he is found innocent, the accuser should be corrected separately
for having been insufficiently circumspect. But if an evil disposition drove
him to a slanderous accusation, that would be plainly intolerable.

It is your part to pray that the Lord may govern by his Spirit those whom you
have chosen for this office, with true prudence, gentleness, zeal, and
uprightness. For your part, allow yourselves to be restrained by those reins
which we all need. Those whose office it is to inspect others should themselves
be the first to be corrected and chastised.

Among the other faults which many would like to pardon in themselves is this:
that they should be allowed to retain what are called benefices of the papacy,
or to trade in them. This must be condemned entirely and without debate. For
it is a crime expressly contrary to Christianity for anyone to contaminate
himself with such sacrileges.

Another matter appears not to be of such great importance: presenting infants
for papal baptism or attending the funerals of the dead. Yet when those who
wish these acts to be permitted or not charged against them have searched
themselves seriously and set God as judge before their eyes, they will readily
pronounce sentence against themselves. For you know well with what foul and
detestable superstitions—indeed, with what sheer mockeries of Satan—baptism has
been defiled in the papacy. Anyone who presents an infant there pollutes the
child as far as lies in him, rather than obtaining the Lord's blessing for the
child. To take part also in prayers made for the dead

### [CO X/1, p. 260]

can be nothing other than detracting to the same extent from the unique
sacrifice of Christ our Lord, since purgatory rests solely upon human
satisfactions. Consider, then, whether that is excusable and whether, when
someone has scandalized the church in this way, the matter should be ignored
or tolerated as a slight fault. Consider whether he does not instead deserve
the correction which warns a person to whom such a thing has happened to be
more cautious in the future. We know the straits in which you live, and in the
duty of love we grieve with you. But because it would do no good to be
absolved by us, allow judgment from God's Word instead to be passed upon you,
so that the whole matter may contribute to your good and salvation.

We hear that another question is debated among you: whether anyone may thrust
himself into preaching in a place where a Reformed church exists, even if the
church has not ratified it; and, if someone has conducted himself in this way,
whether people may also come to hear him.

Here we ask you to attend carefully. Although the Lord has constituted some
body of a church among you, it does not follow that many things may not be
tolerated amid the public disorder which still prevails there, things which
would not be bearable in a well-constituted church. We are not speaking of
actions in which people sin against God and which conflict with his Word.

But since the pitiable papists are like scattered sheep, when the Lord raises
up certain people to gather them from their dispersion, we are so far from
being obliged to obstruct such help offered to them that in this case we
should instead apply the Lord's saying: let us then regard as ours those who
are not against us (Luke 9:50). For the question here is not the office of a
pastor, but only the work of a person who teaches in such a way as gradually
to lead to Christ those who were previously scattered. Nor is the question a
permanent order, but a certain extraordinary means which the Lord uses amid
the confusion of the papacy.

Remember also the apostle's statement that those who are not members of the
church's household are not subject to its rule and statutes (1 Corinthians
5:12). Finally, one must observe what the same apostle teaches elsewhere:
matter for rejoicing is offered wherever the Gospel is advanced, even if this
happens under a pretext (Philippians 1:18).

We therefore do not think the faithful should be barred from the preaching of
such people, provided sound and pure doctrine is present there. But every
superstitious prayer or any other admixture must be absent. This does not mean
that they should seize from it an occasion to withdraw from the regular
congregation, or even to gather less diligently to hear their regular pastor.
They should instead be encouraged and confirmed in maintaining the order into
which the Lord once introduced them.

Accordingly, watch yourselves lest you be deceived by those who try by an
indirect path to lead sheep away from the flock and estrange them from their
regular pasture. Meanwhile, if some people approach those preachers without
being drawn by curiosity, and not

### [CO X/1, p. 261]

in order to deviate from the right path, they do indeed seem tolerable.

More serious is the fact that some people also attend the sermons of monks,
who sprinkle some word of truth into their lies so that they may win favor for
themselves. As though we were not already far too prone to vanity and lies, so
that new teachers had to be sought to lead us into them! The Lord does not
punish without cause those who delight in such mixtures. The facts themselves
establish that very many of them both turn from the truth and are infected by
Satan's impostures. This comes from nothing other than their ingratitude: they
do not rest in pure doctrine but desire to feed on wind. By a just judgment,
the Lord allows these people to become drunk.

In general you must take care not to be among those who are always learning,
as Paul says, and never attain knowledge of the truth (2 Timothy 3:7). We also
wish you to remember the Lord's warning that you guard yourselves against the
leaven of the scribes and Pharisees. Since we ourselves were once steeped in
it, it is so difficult for us to be truly purged of it and for none of the
dregs of that corruption to remain in us. What, then, will happen to those who
desire to live among them as well and thus willingly put God to the test?

The same judgment must be made about the whole class of little devotions which
they call Hours and Paternosters, and the other instruments of idolatry of this
kind. Idols must be so utterly abominable to us that not even their names are
in our mouths, as it stands in Psalm 16:4. Corresponding to this is Ezekiel
36:32: if we truly repent of our past life and of our ways in which we went
astray, we cannot help but also be ashamed of them.

There are other corruptions which are added to these. Dice and what they call
cards, even if they do not belong to superstitions but have been instruments
of other vices, have likewise brought much harm and evil everywhere. As for
games of this kind, we would not condemn them so completely if lawful use had
any place in them. But where is that sobriety found?

First, nothing anywhere ensnares people like birdlime as these do, so far as
to occupy all their senses, just as if they had been seized by a spell. Just
as those who have become accustomed to them cannot so readily bid them
farewell, so a person who has drawn his foot back even a little will easily
come to despise all of them. Those who give themselves to these things and
continue to act madly fall into many troubles. It is well known that games
scarcely ever pass without blasphemies, frauds, and outbursts of anger. How
many families have also been ruined by them! No one keeps within bounds
without exhausting himself or consuming himself little by little. Especially
where profit is at issue, it can scarcely happen without God himself being
offended in some way. It is therefore exceedingly expedient to remain as
distant from them as possible. The best course is to abstain entirely.

### [CO X/1, p. 262]

We also ask you not to be troubled to hear this: in the churches among you
whose pastors are burdened by larger families, take care that provision is
made for those men and their need. A person oppressed by poverty cannot have a
free and quiet mind so as to devote himself entirely to his office while his
family is in need and he has nothing at hand with which to assist it.

Moreover, when we hear from God's Word that those who serve the church are to
be supported by us, this must not be restricted merely to their own persons.
They themselves would be inhumane if they were not equally concerned for their
families and for themselves. We provide here what we can, but the poverty is
such that we are unequal to relieving it completely. Because we do not doubt
that you will sufficiently perform your duty, so that the spur of our
exhortation need not be added, it will be enough to have indicated this to you
in a word.

Finally, to bring our letter to a close, because we do not doubt that you are
attacked daily by enemies of the faith, we ask you to contend steadfastly and
yield nothing until you have overcome every assault of Satan and reached the
blessed finish by a resolute course. Recognize the weapons supplied to us from
heaven: namely, that we take refuge in him alone who deigns to grant us the
grace of receiving us into his protection, and that in this way we possess our
souls in patience.

It is not lawful for us to prevail by force, and you know well how badly
whatever we rashly undertake without the Master's command turns out. It is
altogether a hard temptation, and difficult to overcome, when we see the
ungodly raging in this way against the innocent. Yet we must consider what we
have been called to. Since the Lord's will is that we be afflicted, and for a
cause of such importance, let this not seem foreign to us.

Let us strive instead to learn by this means how God's glory, the obedience we
owe him, Christ's own kingdom, and that hope of eternal life are to be
preferred to this fleeting life. Here, as in general, put into practice what
Scripture teaches: by renouncing yourselves, become a sweet-smelling sacrifice
to the Lord.

Having first greeted you most warmly, we pray the Lord to look upon you and
vindicate you from all adversaries, protect you by his defense, govern you by
his Spirit, and cause you to be filled with every good.

## Translator's notes

1. CO's source note makes this Latin a translation by Beza from French. The
   English is therefore a translation of a derivative language witness, not a
   reconstruction of Calvin's French wording.
2. The letter consistently speaks as “we,” calls itself a common letter, and
   addresses “dearest brothers” across several churches. CO places it among
   Calvin's consilia but preserves no subscription identifying the other
   people, if any, included in that collective voice.
3. “The barest test ... of his Christianity” renders *christianismus* in the
   context of examining a person's elementary instruction before admission to
   the Supper. It does not mean a test of nationality or of every aspect of
   Christian character.
4. CO prints the Greek *ἀταξίᾳ*, “disorder,” within the Latin sentence.
   The transcription retains it; the English uses “public disorder.”
5. The qualified permission to hear an extraordinary preacher explicitly
   denies that the person thereby holds the permanent office of pastor. It is
   also conditioned on pure doctrine, the absence of superstitious prayer,
   and continued attachment to the ordinary congregation and pastor.
6. *Preculae*, here represented by the genitive *precularum*, are small
   prayers or devotions. “Hours and Paternosters” retains the names Calvin's
   Latin presentation gives to the criticized devotional instruments.
7. The discussion of dice and cards distinguishes an imaginable lawful use
   from the habitual practice the letter describes. The final recommendation
   of complete abstinence rests on its claims about addiction, anger, fraud,
   household ruin, and profit-seeking.
8. Biblical propositions and allusions are translated from the printed Latin,
   not copied from a modern English Bible. The closing gives no date, place,
   personal names, or subscription.
