The celebrated Hoornbeeck, in his vernacular book *The Sunday, a Day of Rest*,
page 39, says that this distinction of moral precepts into natural and
positive is confirmed by all who write about laws. He cites Grotius, *On the
Supreme Powers’ Authority*, and so forth, chapter 2, where, in discussing
moral matters, he has the following: “To answer this question properly, one
must distinguish altogether between natural right and positive divine right.
What goes beyond this, once a positive law and the obstacles which arise
extrinsically are posited,

[1674 logical printed p. 228 | IA leaf 246 | PDF p. 246]

is natural in a certain respect—for example, that the king and the priest
should be the same person. This is not, indeed, from that class of natural
things which cannot be otherwise, but from the other class, consisting of
things which sufficiently agree with nature and right reason.” And in chapter
3: “It therefore follows that only those things are determined which are
either owed or unlawful by their own nature, or which have been made such by
divine command. Those which belong to the former class are referred to
natural right.—— Those which belong to the latter class are called matters of
positive divine right. Some such things are prescribed to individuals, some
to a people, some to a community, and some to every member of the human race.
Some have been commanded to the whole human race for a time, such as the law
of the Sabbath in creation itself, as some think, and the law against eating
blood or what was strangled after the flood. Others were to remain in
perpetuity, which Christ instituted, such as the law of excommunication, the
law of baptism and the Lord’s Supper, and any similar laws.”

Nathanael Johnson, in *The Socinian Opinion concerning the Sabbath*, and so
forth, page 71 and following, proves that those who maintain that matters of
positive right are not matters of moral right overthrow the true practice of
piety. [“For if,” he says, “they do not pertain to morals or the moral law,
then those things which depend upon God’s positive right and bind only by the
force of God’s precept likewise have no character of vice or virtue.
Therefore our obedience with respect to God’s precepts which are matters of
positive right is not a practice of piety. Consequently, the practice of the
Lord’s Holy Supper and baptism will not be true worship of God. Yet since the
Supper cannot be practiced without renewed repentance; examination of
conscience (2 Corinthians 13:5); investigation of sins (Lamentations 3:40);
humiliation of heart (1 Peter 5:5–6); desire for grace (Psalm 42:1–2; Matthew
5:6); a purpose of gratitude (Psalm 116:12); ejaculatory prayers (Mark 9:24);
a formal act of faith in Christ; meditations in which we compare the sign with
the thing signified; joy in God (Psalm 4:7–8, compared with Psalm 51); and
thanksgiving (Psalm 103:1–2), what follows except that the very marrow of the
practice of piety is judged unworthy of the name of piety? For the application,
use, and exercise of all these spiritual gifts—or this entire manner and means
of worshiping God—binds only by the force of God’s precept and is a matter of
positive rather than natural and immutable right. This is so even according
to the author’s hypotheses, for whom only that which is known by nature to
everyone and common to us and the nations is a matter of natural or moral
right, which the practice of the Holy Supper is not. Therefore. 2. I say the
same about reading the word of God. For it to become a spiritual work, it
requires an inward sense of the majesty of God speaking to us through his
word; the subjection of the intellect and will with respect to revealed truth
and God’s commands (James 4:7; 2 Corinthians 10:5), indeed, the denial of both
(Matthew 16); a desire to learn that heavenly art of worshiping God which is
called ‘citizenship in heaven’ in Philippians 3:20, which the prophet sought
with continual vows throughout Psalm 119; likewise ejaculatory prayers (Psalm
119:4–5); attentive meditations; an intention to attain the end of God’s word,
namely the advancement of piety, faith, and salvation (1 Peter 1:9; John 20,
last verse); and an effort of mind to turn to use before God and our neighbor
what we have observed by attentive reading. But this practice, this manner or
means of worshiping God, is a matter of positive right, unknown to the nations
and to nature, and binds us only by the force of God’s precept. Therefore it
has no character of virtue, nor does its omission have the character of vice,
if the author’s dictum must be followed. 3. No one calls into question whether
the practice of fasting, days of thanksgiving, and supplications is a matter
of positive right. Yet the spiritual practice of days of this kind, or this
manner of worshiping God, through

[1674 logical printed p. 229 | IA leaf 247 | PDF p. 247]

renewed repentance, examination of conscience, confession of sins, public
and private prayers, meditations upon God’s benefits and our sins, the
practice of humiliation, and everything else, will have no character of
virtue, nor will its omission have the character of vice. Where are we
sliding? 4. The practice required for the edification of the regenerate
spirit in the public dispensation of the divine word is beyond doubt a matter
of positive divine right and binds us only by the force of a divine precept.
Therefore, from the author’s hypothesis, let someone conclude that hearing the
word of God is not a matter of moral right, and accordingly has no character
of virtue, nor does the omission of its practice have the character of vice.
It would follow from this that meditations, prayers, and so forth are not
matters of moral right, and so forth. 5. Indeed, the daily practice with its
subordinate exercises, as such, or insofar as it is a peculiar manner of
worshiping God morning and evening, is a matter of positive divine right
(Deuteronomy 6 and 11), although the acts of which this practice consists are
matters of natural right. Monasticism, for example, if it had a foundation in
God’s word—which it does not—would, as a peculiar manner of worshiping God, be
a matter of positive right and would have to be referred to the second
precept, to which it is now opposed as a human invention. 6. Believing in
Christ is a matter of positive divine right. At least the author cannot deny
it, since he teaches that the Gospel is a matter of positive right in his
*Apology*, page 24. What, then? Do believing in Christ and the unbelief opposed
to it not have the character of virtue and sin? What? Will errors, heresies,
and apostasy against the teaching of the Gospel, which oppose God’s positive
right, not have the character of vice? 7. Countless abuses of the divine name
and superstitions of the nations oppose God’s positive right. Is there no sin
here, then? 8. Marriage is a matter of God’s positive right; the distinction
of ownership, various modes of acquisition, and this or that particular form
of political and ecclesiastical order are matters of positive right.
Therefore fornication, adultery, polygamy, marriages with unbelievers,
schisms, rebellion, and everything else are not sins and do not fall within
morals? 9. Special commands can be added: ‘Sacrifice your son’ (Genesis 22),
and ‘Sell all your substance’ (Matthew 19), which must be referred to positive
right. Yet Abraham’s obedience is no less commended and praised as a work of
faith and love *par excellence*. 10. Let us conclude in the Apostle’s words
from Philippians 4:8: ‘Whatever accords with truth, justice, honor, and purity,
whatever does not sound evil—if there is any virtue, if any praise—meditate
upon these things.’ Yet among these are many matters of positive right, such
as whatever is becoming, whatever does not sound evil, and abstaining from
every appearance of evil (1 Thessalonians 5).” Thus far he.]

And on page 81 and following, he proves with many arguments that certain
positive precepts are moral, as follows: [“1. Those precepts which, according
to God’s judgment, are equally instituted as holy—objectively in themselves
and subjectively with respect to believers—are equally moral with others. But
the precepts which are matters of positive right and bind us only by the force
of God’s command or will, together with their spiritual practice, are equally
moral with God’s other precepts. Therefore. The minor premise is proved
because the practice of fasting, the practice of the Holy Supper and baptism,
the reading and hearing of the divine word, and even the Lord’s Day are beyond
controversy matters of positive right and nevertheless moral.—— The major
premise is proved because ‘a holy work,’ ‘a good work,’ ‘a work pleasing to
God,’ and ‘a moral work which has the character of virtue according to God’s
judgment’ are synonyms. 2. Those things which God calls his counsels, or means
necessary for human salvation, contain something moral. But precepts of
positive right, and those which bind only by the force of God’s command or
will, are called counsels of God by the Holy Spirit just as much as the others,

[1674 logical printed p. 230 | IA leaf 248 | PDF p. 248]

instituted for human salvation—that is, means necessary for the possession
of eternal life. This is proved because precepts of both kinds of right, that
is, God’s natural right and positive right, are marked by this designation:
the practice and institution of baptism, the practice of repentance and
faith, and so forth (Luke 7:30; Revelation 3:16–17); the dispensation of the
divine word (Jeremiah 23:22; Isaiah 5; Psalm 107:11, compared with Acts 20).
The Apostle had delivered God’s entire saving counsel to the churches, and
among the things he had delivered to the churches were also precepts of
positive right, such as concerning the use of the Holy Supper (1 Corinthians
11:23). 3. Precepts whose obedience is worship of God and the practice of
piety are matters of moral right. But the practice of precepts which are
matters of positive right, and so forth, is also true worship of God and the
practice of piety.—— Whatever is done from faith, for God’s glory, according
to God’s precepts—whether they are distinctly matters of natural or positive
right—is a good, pious, and holy work; and if it regards God immediately, it
is worship of God. But the practice of the Holy Supper, fasting, baptism, and
so forth, which are matters of positive right, is such a work.—— Therefore. 4.
Once the foundation of morality, or the formal object of moral actions, is
posited, morality or a moral act is posited. (That which is the foundation of
morality considered objectively in the idea is the formal object of a moral
act, namely, a special and express command of God.) But the formal object is
the same for acts which are natural and for those which are matters of
positive divine right. 5. Those precepts by whose observance God is glorified
are moral precepts. But God is also glorified by the observance of those which
are matters of positive right. For the same thing is declared about Paul’s
ministry in the last verse of Galatians 1 as about all good works in Matthew
5:19–20. That which the acts of religion serve—or rather, which consists
of such acts, both simpler and more composite, of faith, hope, love, prayer,
meditation, reading, and hearing the divine word—is a matter of moral right
insofar as it is such, or with that practice taken formally. Examples are the
practice of the Holy Supper and the Lord’s Day, fasting, and so forth. But the
practice of the Holy Supper, and so forth—which is a matter of positive right
and binds us only by the force of God’s command, not by the force of natural
law—is served by, or consists of, simpler and composite acts of religion.
Therefore. 7. What is performed by believers in spirit and truth is something
moral. But precepts which are matters of positive right and bind only by the
force of God’s will, not natural right, are performed by believers in spirit
and truth. Therefore, whatever practice is truly spiritual and the work of a
sincere heart before God, according to John 4:24, is genuinely moral. But the
practice of the godly with respect to precepts of positive right, and so
forth, is such a practice. Therefore. The author cannot deny the minor
premise, because he maintains that the phrase ‘in spirit and truth’ in John 4
is commanded in the second precept—although not accurately enough, since it
is not commanded there more directly than in the fourth precept. Yet the
practice of the Sacraments and of the other means of salvation pertains to
the second precept, even though these are matters of positive rather than
natural right, and so forth. 8. Precepts which are parts of the Decalogue or
the moral law are moral, for what is said of a homogeneous whole is also said
of its parts. But precepts of positive right—for example, at least the second
and fourth—are parts of the moral law. Therefore they are moral. That the
whole Decalogue is homogeneous is evident because it contains nothing that is
strictly called ceremonial and nothing peculiar to the Mosaic polity.—— 9.
The practice which under the New Testament is a spiritual sign of blessedness,
of the state of grace and glory, a sign of eternal adoption, and a sign of the
Gospel covenant, from which one may conclude that the promises concerning

[1674 logical printed p. 231 | IA leaf 249 | PDF p. 249]

the remission of sins and salvation are ours, is a matter of moral right. But
these things
are also declared of precepts which are matters of positive right and bind
only by the force of God’s institution, not by the force of natural law
(Isaiah 56:2, 3, 5, 7). Therefore. 10. From an adequate meaning or description
of the term: what pertains to the proper institution and ordering
of morals before God and human beings is moral. But the practice of precepts,
even those which are matters of positive right and bind us only by the force
of God’s command, is such. Therefore. Many absurdities will follow. 1. That
precepts of positive right, which bind only by the force of God’s command and
not by the force of natural law, contain works that are merely external, not
internal and spiritual. 2. Consequently, that their genuine practice is not
true worship of God or the practice of true piety. 3. That they are useless
works and not means to the possession of salvation or assurance of it in this
life. 4. That precepts which are matters of positive right can be observed by
unbelievers and the unregenerate. 5. That the moral law of God contains some
commands which are not moral.”]

The End of Book I.
