# CHAPTER XII

## The various degrees or kinds of necessities according to Jackson, *On
Providence*, chapter 23; his arguments are examined.

Jackson, chapter 21.

“The person who denies that God's law or covenant is stricter and more
peremptory in some effects than in others supposes that the prophet presents
God's covenant concerning day and night as more certain and immutable than the
covenant concerning the early and latter rains. But God's law for the heavenly
creatures is not like the law of the Medes and Persians. When Joshua prayed, a
dispensation from that law occurred; the motion of the sun and moon is not
perpetually necessary with respect to the decree itself. The seasons of sowing
and harvest can become certain and immutable for those who walk in God's
commandments. Leviticus 26:4. Such was the wisdom of the laws given to God's
people that, by observing them, the disposition of the air and sky could be
changed.”

[Marginal summary: Jackson falsely states that, with respect to God's decree,
some things are more necessary and some less necessary.]

I answer: Jackson does not establish that some of God's decrees are more
peremptory and immuta-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 127; PDF page 178]

*ble than others unless he posits God's decrees as changed. For the inference
from the comparative degree to the positive is valid. Nor is it far from
blasphemy to say that God's law or decree (for he is speaking of such a law)
concerning the most glorious creatures in the firmament is not like the law of
the Medes and Persians—as though God's decree concerning the motion of the sun
and moon were changed at Joshua's prayers, and thus the Medes were more
immutable in their decrees than Jehovah in his. The Medes were variable and
inconstant people who retracted their decrees by the new light of second
thoughts. This has never been done by God, upon whom no shadow of change falls;
indeed, it is impious even to think it. But Jackson's argument is not worth a
straw.

What follows? The decreed things were changed: namely, the sun stood still and
did not move at Joshua's prayers, which is no slight change in the sun and
moon. Therefore God's decree concerning the sun's perpetual and natural motion
is not as immovable and invariable as the law of the Medes. This by no means
follows. By the same reasoning, because God decreed various changes in the moon
and in human beings, God's decree would be mobile and changeable like the moon
and human beings. Is this Jackson's logic? He assumes that God decreed the sun
and moon's standing still at Joshua's prayers less immutably than the sun's
first and subsequent natural motions. That is atheistic, for then the sun's
miraculous standing still would have occurred without God's decree, with
Fortune or Chance ruling. And all miracles, in which God passes beyond nature's
barriers and customary channel, would be so many changes and determinations
less certain and peremptory in God's decrees than the laws of the Medes.

2. Jackson reasons that this seems to be the least necessity, or a necessity
in the lowest degree, because it admits of dispensation. In reality, however,
Jackson is mistaken when he thinks that the necessity of a decree admits of
more and less. Whatever God has decreed will happen—whether necessary things,
such as the rising and setting of the sun and moon, or contingent things, such
as Peter's disputation—is equally necessary with respect to the decree,
because they are equally founded upon God's infinite immutability. It is
therefore false that some things are not as necessary as others with respect
to God's decree. For those things which are less necessary—such as the early
and latter rains, a clear sky, an abundant harvest, and fertility—either were
decreed by God and preordained from eternity by wise counsel, or they were not.
If the latter, many benefits which flow from God's kindness and mercy come to
pass in time but were not-*

[Marginal summary: All things are equally necessary with respect to the divine
decree.]

[Chapter XII; printed page 128; PDF page 179]

*willed by God from eternity to happen. This is atheistic, for things would
occur by fortune and chance which happen by no eternal counsel and no
providence of God. What could the Epicureans dream that is more foolish? But if
they were decreed by God from eternity, then, first, they are necessary with
respect to God's decree and are just as necessary as all other things decreed
by God. If they are less necessary than other things with respect to the
decree, they are less necessary because, although decreed, they can fail to
exist. God's decrees are therefore mutable, and thus God himself is mutable,
contrary to Scripture: Romans 1:23; 1 Timothy 1:17; James 1:17; Malachi 3:6;
Psalm 102:27. That is, he would be mutable because he changes his decrees and
counsels daily, as we human beings do, in whom second thoughts are more mature
than the first and who learn something today which we did not know yesterday.
But it is blasphemous to think this of God. If, however, these evils—an
intemperate sky, storms, floods, sterility, famines, an iron earth, and a bronze
sky—were not decreed, the just Judge of the world would inflict punishments
without any counsel. This is not true of earthly judges unless they are foolish
and unjust. And thus God would threaten in time an evil which he did not decree
from eternity would happen.

But suppose the good or evil things which Jackson calls possible were decreed
conditionally and are “consequent upon our duty, either performed or
neglected.” These very conditions (at least the doing of what belongs to our
duty), and our free obedience, either fall under God's decree or they do not.
If the latter, all acts of obedience, even those performed through God's grace
and through the grace given us before times eternal (2 Timothy 1:9), are
outside God's eternal counsel, contrary to Scripture: Revelation 4:11; Romans
11:36–37; Ephesians 1:11. If the conditions were not decreed (as follows from
Jackson, they were in no way decreed precisely to this side rather than that,
for then they would not be free acts according to Jackson and the Jesuits),
then all the good works which God *proētoimasen*—ordained and prepared for us to
walk in as God's new creation, created in Christ for these works (Ephesians
2:10)—are second thoughts which arose in God in time and were not willed and
decreed from eternity. If these good or evil things were decreed not
conditionally but absolutely, then they are immutably necessary with respect
to the divine decree, contrary to Jackson.

Finally, Jackson quite plainly raves when he says that observing the laws God
gave his people could change the disposition of the air and alter the
influences of the heavens. The heavens and-*

[Marginal summary: Our actions do not change God's eternal decree; on the
contrary, God's decrees draw forth our actions.]

[Chapter XII; printed page 129; PDF page 180]

*air were decreed by God only with less necessity—that is, were so fixed that
they were not as immovable as “the laws of the Medes, which were unalterable.”
Indeed, he raves that God's decree concerning fair weather or storms was
mutable and was actually changed, and that in Joshua's days God's eternal
decree and law concerning the perpetual motion of the sun and moon was
retracted and revoked. It is as though, with a new miracle, God entered today
upon a new counsel which had not come into his mind yesterday, or indeed from
eternity. But then God would have to yield to the Medes and Persians in the
stability of laws and the immutability of decrees.

Jackson writes: “Notable and remarkable events which at this moment are merely
contingent and easily preventable through various practices ordained by God
can, with the revolution of a succession of times, become more necessary, and
at length truly fatal and altogether inevitable. Nothing can be judged
absolutely necessary from all eternity except the deity and the internal
operations of the eternally blessed Trinity. Many things which at the
beginning of time possessed the first degrees of possibility (when their
contraries or incompatible opposites were in the highest degree of
probability) were drawn by the continuous sinister course and progress of
human thoughts and evil actions from mere possibilities to things more than
probable, and then to the first degree of necessity—although at the beginning
they were not absolutely and irresistibly necessary. The pagans customarily
called these the lesser fates. Those same events, through the omission of the
practices which God's decree had established to anticipate and prevent the
necessity, become altogether irresistible and absolutely necessary, though not
absolutely necessary from eternity. Events in this manner entirely stripped
of every contingency and possibility by which they could be avoided were
customarily attributed by the pagans to the greater fate. I discuss these in
the treatise on prodigies.”

I answer: 1. By “events” Jackson here means judgments and punishments divinely
threatened and to be inflicted because of sin: namely, the Babylonian
captivity and the destruction of Jerusalem. Although God threatened these
conditionally unless the people repented, they were always necessary and
inevitable with respect to the decree. It is true that at first God threatened
that captivity only conditionally and said that it was to be averted and
prevented “if the Jews repent.” But that threat did not reveal God's secret
and hidden counsel concerning the captivity. The captivity was always and from
eternity equally necessary with respect to the decree; it was always
inevita-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 130; PDF page 181]

*ble with respect to the decree, even when God threatened it conditionally.
With respect to the means and second causes—namely, the Jews' repentance—it
was avoidable and could be turned aside through a serious conversion to God.
But Jackson vigorously asserts that at the beginning some punishments are
avoidable and merely contingent with respect to the divine decree, and that
this necessity of the decree, or hypothetical necessity, grows from fewer or
lesser degrees of necessity to more degrees of necessity. At last the event
becomes absolutely necessary with respect to the decree, although before it
had been necessary with respect to the decree only in a qualified sense, in
such a way that it was avoidable—not necessary with respect to the decree but
only possible. In this manner he treacherously and cleverly insinuates that
God's decrees, like the moon, wax and advance from mutable and retractable
decrees to ones more stable, a little more deeply rooted, and firmer. At
length, as the Jews' sins increase, God's decrees concerning famine, plague,
and captivity become fatal, simply immutable, peremptory, and irrevocable,
although before they had been less stable and far more mutable than the
decrees of the Medes and Persians, and could have been entirely cut off and
broken. To prove this he supplies not one word from God's Word and not even the
faintest shadow of reason—only a Jesuit analogy which wanders outside the olive
groves.

[Marginal summary: Jackson imagines God's decrees as less fixed and mutable,
then growing in time to fixed, simply peremptory, and irrevocable decrees as
the Jews' sins or evils accumulate.]

But such an accretion and cutting off of decrees posits that some of God's
decrees are mutable and revocable while others are irrevocable and immutable.
This distinction is blasphemous.

2. It follows from this that God's decrees are not causes of things; on the
contrary, things outside God are causes which give stability, firmness, and
immutability to God's decrees. Jackson makes God hang in suspense, as though he
neither could nor dared decree the captivity completely, peremptorily, and
irresistibly until he saw the Jews' cup of iniquity filled, and were prepared
to advance in decreeing at the same pace at which the Jews continued sinning.
God's decree could be neither completed nor grow to maturity as the firmest
and immutable decree—which Jackson calls the “greater fate”—except through the
succession of time. So let God's eternal decrees gain strength and grow! Nor
can a decree be made whole and completed until the things decreed actually
exist in time.

3. It follows that God is prepared to revoke and cut off a decree if people
repent, and that God's decrees begin and end: God decreed something yesterday,
decrees no such thing today, but tomorrow will take up the same decree anew if
the-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 131; PDF page 182]

*Jews return with the dog to its vomit.” This is what the atheist Conrad
Vorstius teaches. For it is certain that God commanded ceremonies which he
does not now command but forbids; and it is certain that from eternity he
decreed to command those things to the Jews for a time, and decreed afterward
to forbid the same things. Here there is a change in things, but none in God's
decree. Indeed, the same will remains in God eternally.

[Marginal reference: Vorstius, disputation 6, *On God's Will*, thesis 35. God
does not will to do certain things which he had formerly undertaken that he
would do, indeed which he would annul, because things outside God have
changed.]

We acknowledge that the necessity of the captivity, with respect to the Jews'
sinning or repenting, grows and finally becomes supreme in this respect, but it
never becomes absolutely necessary as Jackson asserts. The necessity of the
decree, however, is equal and the same always—unless Jackson proves that God
once decreed from eternity to punish the Jews with the Babylonian captivity
because of their first defection (which he will never prove), and afterward
was prepared to retract that partial decree. In fact, God decreed from eternity
to inflict that captivity because of all those sins accumulated one upon
another and their continued final rebellion (notwithstanding that the prophets
tried through continual sermons to lead them to repentance), because of which
they were actually carried into captivity.

I concede that God did not inflict upon Ahab in his own time, or upon the
Ninevites, the destruction he had threatened. But it does not follow that there
was in God an eternal decree to punish them at that time (for Scripture knows
no temporal decrees of God) which was retracted. For when a conditional decree
is not put into execution because some people repent, such a decree is not
mutable and revocable; indeed, it is fulfilled. It is conditional only on the
side of its object. God decreed both to punish the Ninevites if they did not
repent and, at the same time, decreed from eternity to give the grace of
repentance by which they would escape the destruction God had threatened. The
eternal decree remains firmer and more immutable than the decrees of the Medes,
although it is conditional. For all decrees are equally immutable and
irrevocable. Therefore, all things decreed are equally necessary with respect
to the decree.

No events, because of other accumulated sins, become more irresistible and
necessary with respect to God's decree than they were from eternity. Nor are
contingent events ever stripped of the contingency and possibility by which
they could be avoided through second causes if the person who sinned repents,
except after the events are past; for then certainly nothing past is either
possible or contingent. Nor are contingent events ever clothed with contin-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 132; PDF page 183]

*gency and a possibility by which they could be avoided with respect to the
first cause and the divine decree. For with respect to God's decree they
possess a necessity which is incapable of increase or the addition of degrees,
but which, once the things have been decreed, is as great and maximal as it can
be. Thus they cannot fail to happen at their appointed time if God has decreed
that they happen—just as God has certainly decreed everything which exists and
does not exist to exist and not to exist.

2. Although Jackson mentions only evil actions which render contingent events
absolutely necessary, he says nothing relevant unless he means that God also
has a decree to save the elect, and that this decree grows as their obedience
is accumulated upon itself, until at last it becomes so fatal and so
absolutely necessary that they be saved that their salvation is stripped of
every contingency and possibility by which they could fail to be saved. Thus
the elect owe it to themselves that they reach the state of an indefectible
salvation. When they are elect at the beginning of their course of obedience,
God decrees to save them, but the necessity of saving them is minimal in
degree: it is only a probability and possibility. At that time God's decree to
save them is unstable and mutable, not like the laws and decrees of the Medes.
From this it follows that all mortals can actually be saved or actually be
damned if they wish, and that God has a decree—though a mutable one—to save
each and every person. If they wish, through free actions (as they can), they
can render this decree firm and immutable and advance it to the state of the
greatest and fatal necessity. God also has a parallel decree to damn each and
every person. Golden dreams!

[Marginal summary: Jackson necessarily has to posit a decree of election which
waxes and wanes as the moon is capable of doing.]

Jackson writes: “Some things are called absolutely necessary and entirely
inevitable, although that necessity arose from temporal occasions—just as some
diseases become incurable through diet and neglect. Thus the Savior's death
was absolutely necessary from the beginning of the world. But whether it was
absolutely necessary from eternity apart from the supposition of Adam's fall
(which was not necessary) will not be disputed here. It is certain that nothing
decreed by God is as absolutely necessary as God's nature and the most holy
Trinity. It is certain that some things which are not necessary today can be
absolutely necessary in their own kind tomorrow.”

[Marginal reference: Jackson, pp. 186–187, section 2. According to Jackson,
Christ's death was absolutely necessary and was also free—something
contradictory according to Jackson and the Arminians.]

I answer: It is inept to say that some things are absolutely necessary. God
alone is absolutely necessary. No created things possess so much necessity.

2. The necessity of the eternal decree does not arise from temporal occa-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 133; PDF page 184]

*sions, just as nothing eternal can arise from a temporal thing without a
contradiction.

3. “Christ's death was necessary from the beginning of the world.” We owe
Jackson no thanks for this theology, for according to him it was not necessary
from eternity. According to Jackson, the necessity of Christ's death did not
arise from God's decree but from Adam's fall, as he says on p. 182. Yet on p.
187 this fall has no necessity from God's eternal permissive decree. How, then,
is Christ's death necessary? It must be from creatures, for the Jews freely
crucified the Savior and therefore could have refrained from crucifying him.
Christ died by the most free obedience rendered to God: John 10:18; Psalm 40:7;
Hebrews 10:7. What kind of absolute necessity is this, Jackson? Is it a natural
necessity? You will not say so. I refute that elsewhere. Is it the necessity of
a decree? According to Jackson that is chimerical. Is it a free necessity?
According to Arminius that is a contradiction in terms.

[Marginal reference: Thus Corvinus in the *Anatomy of Molinaeus's Sin*, chapter
on providence.]

Jackson writes: “By divine providence certain sinister events become
irresistibly necessary long before they are fulfilled or inflicted. Such were
the destruction of Pharaoh and Sennacherib and the destruction of the kingdom
of Judah and Jerusalem by Titus. Some other events become so a few days or
hours before they happen, while still others become irresistibly necessary
only at the very moment when they happen.”

[Marginal reference: Chapter 23, section 4.]

I answer: 1. The destruction of Pharaoh, Sennacherib, and the others became
necessary long before it was necessary. What? Was the destruction of all the
wicked not necessary from eternity by the necessity of the decree? 1 Peter
2:8; Jude 6. Does a new attribute of justice sprout in God, the Ancient of
Days, which was not in him from eternity? Indeed, on that account God would
never have been just and never exercised a just decree until Pharaoh and the
other wicked people were created in time.

2. But Pharaoh sinned when he actually pursued God's people into the Red Sea.
After Christ, shedding abundant tears, had peremptorily foretold the decreed
destruction of the Jews and that the things which pertained to their peace and
salvation were hidden from their eyes (Luke 19:42), they sinned most gravely by
crucifying God's Son. Therefore they did not perish by an absolute and
irresistible necessity, since no one sins by an irresistible motion of the
will. Hence the irresistible and absolutely necessary necessity of this event
either did not exist, or came entirely from God's decree. See what was said
above.

3. “From eternity a grim lot of eternal punishment was carved for every sin,”
Jackson says later. Therefore, the punishment of sin was preordained for
Pharaoh from eternity.

[Marginal reference: Jackson, *On Providence*, chapter 24, section 12, p. 213.]

Jackson writes: “It must always be remembered that God did not decree all
things before they happen, or the manner in which they happen, in such a way
that he does not still decree the same things; for he continually decrees both
necessity and contingency and produces effects both contingent and necessary
from this present hour, although each was possible at some earlier time. — The
parts of God's disjunctive decree, and the degrees both of things decreed by him
and of times in which they could happen, can exceed number with respect to us.
— And by failing to seize the opportunities through which he could reduce to
act his powers of performing God's antecedent will, a person can lose those
very powers of acting, eternally or for a long time.”

[Marginal reference: Thus Gabriel Pennottus, *Bulwark of Human Liberty*, book
4, chapter 4; how he is thrashed by Dr. Twisse, *On Middle Knowledge*, chapter
30, digression 5.]

I answer: This is what I expected: God's decrees are temporal, coeval and
coexistent with our temporal actions. Thus God decrees today what happens
today, decreed yesterday what happened yesterday, and will decree tomorrow
what will happen tomorrow. He did not so decree all things before they happened
that he does not continue decreeing that they happen. I do not think he decrees
today the creation of the world or the incarnation of Christ his Son. But if
he decreed some things before they happened—namely, Josiah's name, birth, and
deeds; Cyrus's name and deeds; and the nativity, humiliation, suffering,
miracles, death, resurrection, and glory of Emmanuel Christ from the seed of
Abraham and David and from Mary—why not all things?

[Marginal summary: He imagines all God's decrees to be temporal and subject to
arising and perishing.]

“Because some things happen necessarily and some freely and contingently. But
it was not fitting for God to determine things which would happen freely and
contingently precisely to one side; he therefore left them to possibility.”
Yet by his decree God determined some things which happen freely and
contingently precisely to one side even before they happen. Why, then, did he
not also determine all free things? The antecedent is clear in many ways.

1. Jackson concedes that God determined the captivity of Judah, Jerusalem's
destruction, and Babylon's devastation through the Chaldeans, through Titus
Vespasian, and through the Medes and Persians—agents possessing the greatest
liberty—precisely to one side.

2. Because, as the Jesuits concede, God determined marriages contracted with
the greatest liberty, the consent given within them, and the free generations
from the seed of the Jews from which Christ is descended according to the
flesh precisely—indeed, absolutely—to one side.

3. God determined Christ's free will precisely to a free, obedient, and
volun-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 135; PDF page 186]

*tary death to be undergone. But if God determined these and many other things,
although they would happen contingently, two years before they occurred, there
would be no reason arising from a fear of necessitating or inflicting violence
upon the free will why he did not determine the free will to those same acts
from eternity.”

2. Let the reader see what I have written elsewhere on this matter.

3. We see three exceedingly foul errors in Jackson's decree which he
attributes to God.

1. God's decree is temporal, not eternal, but coeval with the creature's
temporal acts. God's counsels, on the contrary, are eternal. What Scripture
says that God now begins to will and decree what the creature does, and does so
in time, so that while the creature acts and continually until the action is
completed, God's decree is in the process of becoming? For if God foreknows all
future things before they exist—as is certain from Scripture, Psalm 139:2–5;
Psalm 94:9–11; Isaiah 41:22–23; Acts 15:18—he either wills the things which
happen in time to happen, and wills and decrees them from eternity, or he does
not will and decree them from eternity. God's will cannot have been suspended
from eternity, possessing only the potentiality to will and then finally
actually willing only in time. For then in time he would do many things which
he had not decreed from eternity and before time to do. Even prudent people do
nothing which they do not resolve and determine to do before they do it. It is
the greatest imperfection and poverty of counsel, and conflicts with wisdom, to
do something which one can neither know nor determine whether it is right or
not and consonant with reason or not. God would therefore not be an
intellectual agent if he did what he had never decreed to do, nor would he be
an eternal intellectual agent if today or in time he begins to will and decree.
Therefore, he willed and decreed from eternity.

[Marginal summary: Jackson imagines three heterodox and exceedingly foul
absurdities in God's decree.]

2. What God inscribed in his book before it happened in time, he decreed from
eternity would happen. To inscribe in a book from eternity is to decree that
which will be. The metaphor is taken from a king or commander who inscribes the
names of senators or soldiers in a book. But God has all things inscribed in
his book, even all the bones and members of David's body before they were
formed in time. Psalm 139:16: “Your eyes see my unformed mass, and in your book
all these things were written which were formed over the days, when not one of
them yet existed.” The one who inscribes-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 136; PDF page 187]

*all things in a book when they do not yet exist ordained and decreed from
eternity that they would happen. Calvin says that “the entire order of creation
was known to God.” But God inscribes all things in his book from eternity and,
as it were, enters them into records. The Hebrew כֹּל signifies everything—both
things and people—when no member or day yet existed from eternity. Thus
eternity is explained negatively: “before any creature existed,” as in Psalm
90: “You are our God and our dwelling place throughout the age.” “Eternally”
does not indicate the actual existence of the people created in time, but the
eternity of the decree. Verse 2: “Before the mountains were brought forth.” In
Proverbs 8, when the Son's eternity is described, verse 13 adds uncreated
wisdom to him. Verse 24: “When there were no depths, I was brought forth. When
the mountains and hills had not yet been established, I was brought forth.
When he had not yet made the earth,” and so forth. Thus God's throne is from
eternity. Psalm 93: מֵעוֹלָם, “from eternity.” Therefore, the King's decrees are
from eternity.

[Marginal reference: All things determined by God's eternal decree are
inscribed in his book. Calvin, commentary on Psalm 139:16.]

Psalm 78:69: “He built his sanctuary like the heights, like the earth which he
established and founded. He founded it לְעוֹלָם, forever.” The sanctuary was
founded only in time and for a short time. Therefore it was founded in the
eternal decree. Psalm 103:17: “God's kindness from the age, from eternity to
eternity, is upon those who fear him.” This cannot refer to anterior eternity
in any other way than to mercy founded upon God's gracious eternal decree.
Isaiah 22:11: “You made a pool between the two walls. And you did not look to
its maker and its fashioner מֵרָחוֹק, from afar; you did not see him from far
off.” This word signifies God's eternity. But God did not form the pool from
eternity; therefore he decreed from eternity to form it. Isaiah 25:1: “Your
counsels מֵרָחוֹק, from far off, from eternity, are faithfulness.” Likewise
Isaiah 37:26: “Have you not heard that I made it from afar, from eternity?” God
formed nothing from eternity except in his decree. Acts 15:18: “All God's
works are known to him ἀπ’ αἰῶνος, from eternity.”

4. God's foreknowledge never began to exist, for then at some time he would
know what he had not known before and would formally learn—which is absurd.
Yet all God's works are known to him from eternity, although none of his works
existed from eternity, only in his free decree. This decree must therefore have
been from eternity. But if he were newly to will and decree what he had not
decreed from eternity, and decrees it today from this hour (as Jackson says),
he truly becomes willing and decreeing from being unwilling and not decreeing.
A new knowledge and consideration of the newly willed thing which was not
previously in him begins in God. And if he there-*

[Chapter XII; printed page 137; PDF page 188 — the CHAPTER catchword for the
next chapter is excluded]

*fore decrees because the thing arises in time—as a free act newly sprouting
and subsisting antecedently to the free decree—and therefore does not will or
decree the free act because it pleases the created will not to will it, the
temporal and coeval coexistence of God's decree would thus become
contemporaneous with it. God wills what he did not will before because the
creature wills and decrees; and he does not will or decree because the creature
does not will or decree. But because in this manner a human being has an act of
repentance and an act of not willing and not decreeing what he once willed and
decreed, and in turn an act of willing what he had not willed—for the human
will, like the Euripus, flows backward and wearies itself with an alternation
of contrary dispositions—God's decree proceeds at the same pace and along an
equal course through all things. Until Pennottus, Jackson, and people of that
kind arose, everyone had hitherto believed this decree to be eternal. But in
this way repentance and the retraction of a decree must be ascribed to God,
contrary to Scripture: Numbers 23:19; 1 Samuel 15:29; Psalm 33:11; Psalm
102:23–25; Malachi 3:6; Proverbs 19:21; Isaiah 46:10; Isaiah 14:27. In all these
passages God's decree is the cause of all the creature's free decrees, not the
reverse.

2. Jackson attributes to God decrees whose object is not one determinate thing
but something merely possible. No person possessing even a crumb of wisdom
would do this.

3. He invents a disjunctive decree in God: namely, God decrees to create or not
create the world, and that Peter τὸ credere, believe, or τὸ non credere, not
believe. He decrees that everything which happens in the nature of things
either happen or not happen, according as it smiles upon the course and genius
