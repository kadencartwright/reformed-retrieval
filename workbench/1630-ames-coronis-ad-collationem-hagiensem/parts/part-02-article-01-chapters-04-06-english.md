### Chapter IV

#### Second argument

Pages 92–93.

The force of the reasoning is as follows.

Those who were given to Christ before faith were also elected before faith.
The reason is that this giving flows from election. But some were given to
Christ before faith (John 6:37, 39; 10:29; 17:6). Therefore some were elected
before faith.

The Remonstrants reply to each of the passages that confirm the minor premise.
But how sophistically!

John 6:37: “Everything the Father gives me comes to me.” If “to be given” is
taken as being given in order to be saved, those who are given to Christ are
believers; if it means being given in order that they may believe in Christ,
that does not flow from election to salvation. I answer: 1. This is a
distinction without a difference: to give in order to save, and to give in
order to believe. The proper work and use of faith is nothing other than to
save: “Your faith has saved you.”

It also involves an altogether obvious contradiction that someone should be
given to Christ Jesus—or to the Savior as such—so that he has Christ as his
own Savior (for this is what it means to believe in him), and yet should not be
given to him in order to be saved. 2. In this passage “coming” must be
understood not as a bodily but as a spiritual coming, which is nothing other
than believing, verse 35: “He who comes to me; he who believes in me.” Faith,
therefore, is a fruit,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 29 | printed p. 17]

or at least some consequence, of that giving: “Everything he gives will come.”
These words therefore cannot be applied to that giving which follows from
faith and is unto salvation. Of those given in that manner it is not rightly
said, “They will come; they will believe,” for they have long since believed
and persevered in faith all the way to the end. 3. Nor do the words denote the
giving which the Remonstrants suppose to be unto faith. On their view not some,
but each and every person, is given to Christ—namely, by the right of salvation
for all and the good of salvation acquired for each one (to use Nicolaas’s
words, p. 49), and also by the right of calling and the administration of all
sufficient and necessary means (which is the common opinion of the
Remonstrants). They therefore teach that all are given to Christ in order to
believe. Consequently, either each and every person must be said to come to
Christ, believe, and be restored to the state of salvation, or a giving of
another kind must be conceded. For “everything the Father gives me will come
to me.”

John 6:39: “This is the will of him who sent me, that I should lose nothing of
what the Father gave me.” The Remonstrants reply: This concerns believers, who
are given to Christ to be saved, as is clear from verse 40. Very well; but if
the giving in verse 39 is understood as a giving unto salvation, it must
necessarily be understood in the same way in verse 37 as well. For the later
statement supplies the reason and proof for the preceding one: namely, that
whatever has been given will come to Christ and remain in him, because this is
the Father’s will, to execute which Christ came down from heaven—that whatever
has been given should not be lost but, by coming to Christ, should be made
safe. This analysis of the unbroken context (plain and evident enough to the
reader in itself) openly shows that the same giving is denoted in both places,
unless someone should dare to attribute a faulty homonymy, or an equivocation
of four terms, to Christ’s most perfect reasoning.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 30 | printed p. 18]

But if it is the same giving, then it precedes a person’s faith, as I proved
above: one who has been given comes and believes; it is not one who is yet to
be given. And surely nothing is clearer in Scripture than that people (whether
all or some) were given to Christ to be saved before their faith, for it was
for this reason that the Son of Man came: that he might save them (Matt. 18:11;
Luke 19:10; 1 Tim. 1:15). And this was according to the will of our God and
Father (Gal. 1:4).

John 10:29: “The Father, who gave them to me.” The Remonstrants reply: He gave
believers, verse 27. It is beyond dispute that those who were given to Christ
from eternity are believers. But whether they first were believing sheep and
heard Christ’s voice, and afterward were given to Christ—this is the very
question at issue. The Remonstrants affirm it; we believe it is false. 1. The
logic of the similitude itself urges the contrary. For sheep must exist both
in the nature of sheep and also in the shepherd’s possession (whether by gift
or by purchase)—indeed, they must grow somewhat accustomed to his leading—
before they can adequately recognize his voice. 2. In verses 26–27 the state
of being sheep is made not a consequence but the antecedent cause of hearing
and faith. 3. In verse 16 he expressly calls those “sheep” who had not yet
believed.

John 17:6: “I made your name known to the people whom you gave me”; and verse
9: “I pray for those whom you gave me out of the world.” The Remonstrants
reply: This concerns a giving to the office of apostleship; the whole chapter
is directed to that. And, however the matter stands, these apostles were not
given to Christ except because they were believers—because they were upright
and sincere Jews who believed in the Christ to come.

This is a new and wholly inept escape. I call it new because I believe they
cannot produce any interpreter (unless they wish to honor either Huber or
Socinus with that title) who teaches that this whole chapter, or even the
verses just cited, must be restricted to the apostles. In fact, on this point
they have Arminius himself as an

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 31 | printed p. 19]

opponent expressly contradicting them. For he writes as follows (in his
oration on the
priesthood of Christ, p. 18): “The prayer Christ offers for himself and the
intercession he offers for believers are both very clearly described for us
by John in chapter 17. For that chapter contains a perpetual rule of the
prayers and intercession which Christ offers to the Father in heaven.”

This whole invention is no less inept than it is new. For 1. if those words
are taken of election to the apostleship, they pertain to Judas just as much
as to the other apostles, since he too is said to have been chosen among the
rest (Luke 6:13, 16). And so it would also have been said of Judas that the
world hated him because he was not of the world, just as Christ himself was
not of the world (verse 14), although elsewhere he is called a thief and a
devil, and in this very passage the son of perdition, by Christ himself. 2. If
only the apostles are understood by “the elect,” then all the rest belonged
to that world which hated Christ and his apostles, for this is Christ’s own
division. But blessed Mary and many others were then living who were neither
apostles nor, nevertheless, of that world. 3. The election of which Christ
speaks here is said to have been made out of the world—and out of that unclean
world which pursued Christ with hatred. It was therefore not made out of
upright and sincere Jews believing in the Christ to come, as these men
describe the disciples to us. Nor had all the apostles been so upright before
their calling, as is evident in Judas and appears more than probable in Matthew
the tax collector—unless perhaps they mean that uprightness which Socinus so
anxiously presses as an antecedent condition of election and calling
(*Theological Lectures*, ch. 12). 4. The parallel passage, John 15:16, 18–19,
where the same words occur, makes it perfectly clear that this election must
be extended to all those branches which are in Christ (verse 1), and to all
the friends

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 32 | printed p. 20]

—all those for whom he laid down his life (verse 13) and to whom he made known the
Father’s will, although the apostles are principally in view in both forms of
speech. 5. Christ himself openly testifies in verse 20 that he prays not only
for the apostles, but also for others who would believe, whom he denotes by
the same title in verse 24: “Father, those whom you gave me—I desire that
where I am, they also may be with me.” Therefore let the carnal thought be
crushed here (to use Augustine’s words), and let it perish. The Son says that
people were given to him by the Father out of the world; to them it is said in
another place, “I chose you out of the world.”

### Chapter V

#### Third argument

Pages 93–94 and following.

Scripture teaches that God displays his mercy in some and his justice in
others (Rom. 9:22–23), and that he does so according to his predetermined
purpose (Eph. 1:5), antecedently, that is, to human faith. The Remonstrants
reply: 1. God does not display his mercy unto salvation except in believers,
or in those who fear him (Luke 1:50). This is neither true nor relevant. It is
not true, because the greatest and saving mercy is displayed in the giving or
sending of Christ (Titus 2:11; Eph. 1:7–9). Mercy is exercised and displayed
unto salvation in conversion itself and in the gift of faith. Thus Paul says
of himself, “God had mercy on me” (1 Tim. 1:13, 16), speaking of his
conversion. Nor is the point relevant, because the force of the argument lies
in the fact that God displays mercy according to his predetermined purpose.
But he could determine mercy beforehand, before he considered faith in a
person, even if he displayed or exercised that mercy only in believers. Even
a blind man can somehow perceive that there are many

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 33 | printed p. 21]

effects of saving mercy before its vessels have completed the course of this
life. Yet, according to
the Remonstrants’ opinion, those who are to be called believers in the matter
of election, or are regarded as capable of its act, must have completed that
entire course, as was demonstrated above.

The Remonstrants add that the context itself in Romans 9:22–23 clearly shows
that the vessels of mercy are children of the promise, verse 8. Therefore, I
reply, they did not exist as believers before the source of the promise—that
is, before mercy was applied—but by the force and efficacy of that merciful
promise. Otherwise I do not see why they should not be called fathers of the
promise just as much as children of the promise, insofar as it is applied to
them. They are “from him who calls” (verse 11), and therefore not from the one
who is called, nor from any work or action that properly belongs to the one
called. But in truth they themselves are not said to be “from him who calls,”
much less to have been believers before they obtained mercy. Rather, God’s
purpose is said to have remained firm from him who calls, and that from love
or mercy shown toward Isaac before he had done anything good, and consequently
before he had believed. Verse 11 therefore seems not to help the Remonstrants’
cause but utterly to cut its throat, as do verses 16 and 18: “It is not of him
who runs nor of him who wills, but of God who shows mercy”; “He has mercy on
whom he wills.” Accordingly, when they ventured to bring forward passages of
this kind, I think they wished to show what they dared to assert rather than
what they could prove. I still do not grasp how verses 30 and 33 pertain to the
matter, for they will not say, I hope, that anyone attained righteousness and
confidence without preceding mercy.

They try to infer from the Apostle’s words a certain parallel between election
and reprobation, but without reason. For the privilege of exemption and
deliverance from deserved punishment can rightly be granted to some without
any moving or antecedent condition, while punishment is nevertheless inflicted
on the rest on account of their own

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 34 | printed p. 22]

desert. The exposition which they bring forward to obscure the
Apostle’s words in Ephesians 1:5 was discussed above under the first argument.

### Chapter VI

#### Fourth argument

Page 95 and following.

Whomever God elected to salvation, he also decreed to bring to salvation by
certain means tending toward it: namely, to call them through the preaching
of the holy gospel and to draw them through his Spirit, so that they come to
Christ by true faith (Rom. 8:30; Acts 13:44; Titus 1:1). But God does not
communicate these means to all. He does not grant the hearing of the Word to
all, nor does he draw all who hear the Word by his Spirit in such a way that
they come. Therefore he elected not all, but certain particular people, to
salvation. That is, he did not will the salvation of all equally, but willed
that of certain persons in a special manner, and did so before he could
consider them as believers.

Here the Remonstrants, after a short speech about the nature of this argument,
reply first to the proposition by frankly denying that God decreed to draw
those whom he elected to salvation by the Word and Spirit in such a way that
they come to Christ, if this is understood absolutely. Yet the truth of what
they so frankly deny is evident from the following. 1. All spiritual blessings
flow from election (Eph. 1:3–4). Here it must also be observed that the
Remonstrants (p. 114) interpret the words in verse 4, “that we should be holy
and blameless,” of election to salvation and glory. Once that is granted, it
seems necessarily to follow that every spiritual blessing, and therefore
calling and conversion themselves, depend upon election to salvation. 2. The
revelation of the mystery of the will in Ephesians 1:9, and the κλήρωσις,
allotment or calling, in verse 11, are also indicated to flow from

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 35 | printed p. 23]

that predestination which concerns particular persons (those who had first hoped
in Christ) and is completed unto salvation or glory—to the praise of God’s
glory—in the obtaining of the inheritance and redemption (verses 1, 14). 3.
Calling is nothing other than the execution of election; hence it is that the
called are termed elect, and calling election. First Corinthians 1:26–27 says,
“Consider your calling: he chose the foolish; he chose the weak; he chose the
lowborn.” Here it must also be noted that the Apostle denotes the election of
particular persons, and indeed a comparative election: “not many wise, not
many powerful, not many noble, but,” and so forth. Thus the Apostle refers the
proper cause of effectual calling to that election which Christ calls the
Father’s εὐδοκία, or good pleasure, when he gives the same resolution of the
same matter in Matthew 11:26. 4. Calling belongs to those whom God
προητοίμασεν εἰς δόξαν, “prepared beforehand for glory” (Rom. 9:23–24). 5.
People are chosen from the beginning (or from the first) unto salvation,
through sanctification and faith in the truth, to which he afterward calls
them through the gospel (2 Thess. 2:13). 6. What we affirm can be confirmed
from the nature and use of the Word and Spirit themselves. For the Word is
that seed of regeneration by which God regenerates his own unto eternal life,
and for this reason: because he willed it (James 1:18; 1 Pet. 1:23). By the
Spirit the same people are sealed and receive the pledge of that life (Eph.
1:13–14). We therefore may not imagine that God works effectually through
that seed of life where he has not determined to communicate that life, or
that he impresses that seal and hands over the pledge where he has not yet
determined with certainty whether he will give life or not. 7. On the
Remonstrants’ opinion it is permissible to argue as follows: commands,
promises, threats, exhortations, and the like, which belong to external
calling, always signify God’s true will concerning

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 36 | printed p. 24]

the conversion and salvation of those toward whom they are employed. This
they insist on
everywhere. Therefore, I reply, it is necessary that God does not equally will
the conversion and salvation of those toward whom he does not employ these
means and of those whom he presses by all these means so that they may be
willing to come to him. 8. To conclude with the Prophet’s clearest words:
“Blessed is the one whom you choose and cause to draw near” (whom you bring
near or join to yourself and to the church), “that he may dwell in your
courts” (Ps. 65:5). The source of every saving good is God’s election to
blessedness; the stream which most certainly flows from it is effectual
calling, by which God causes the elect to come to his courts—that is, to the
church.

Thus far we have established the proposition which the Remonstrants deny. It
will now be worthwhile to hear the reasons on which that denial rests. 1. They
say that the calling which takes place through the preaching of the gospel is
also granted to the non-elect. It is granted in the same manner as sunlight is
granted in the case of those who are blind. That light is not properly
communicated for their sake, but for the sake of those who see; nevertheless,
it beats upon them externally and in some manner shines upon them. Exactly the
same thing happens in this spiritual illumination: it reaches many reprobates
in some manner, although it both properly belongs to the elect and is directed
by God to them. Resurrection from the dead is also granted to the reprobate;
yet in the elect their being raised by Christ to eternal life flows from
election (John 6:39). “But,” they say, “the means precede faith in Christ in
order; therefore they have another cause.” In the same temporal order,
Abraham’s faith preceded Christ, as did the grace by which both he and the
others among the ancients once believed in the coming Messiah. Does it
therefore necessarily follow that this saving grace had a cause and source
other than our Lord Jesus Christ? That most ancient and most true maxim holds
here: the means precede the end in execution, but follow the end in

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 37 | printed p. 25]

designation and intention. Thus the Jesuit Suárez rightly says
(*On Predestination*, book
1, ch. 8, p. 22), “An argument from an effect to the act of predestination is
sophistical.” 2. They say: if the means flow from election with respect to the
elect, then it must necessarily be said that the same means flow from
reprobation with respect to the reprobate. This is an ungenerous argument,
which passes from election to reprobation. Resurrection flows from election
with respect to the elect; nevertheless, properly speaking, it does not flow
from reprobation with respect to the reprobate. External illumination by the
sun flows from the intention to elicit sight with respect to those who see;
yet with respect to the blind it does not flow from an intention to blind.
Since, therefore, this consequence contains no force of inference, it would
be superfluous to examine the subordinate consequences which they try to draw
from so rotten a root in order to generate odium. One point, however, must be
disposed of in a word: if only the salvation of the elect is intended in
calling, why are the reprobate addressed, admonished, invited, illuminated,
and so forth along with them? 1. Let them tell me why God causes the sun to
shine upon blind animals, and why he causes rain to fall upon rocks. Whatever
they think should be answered here, let them regard it as also said about the
calling of those who will not come. 2. I say that they are addressed along
with the elect because they live and act together with them. For it seemed
good to God to deal with people in a human manner, not distinguishing
completely in calling between those who cannot be discerned by human eyes.
The external means of salvation are dispensed to the elect in such a way that
they also become externally common to those who are externally the same as
the elect. 3. This is also done in part so that the elect, the true believers,
may thereby be instructed when they see others who share the same external
calling nevertheless left in sin. For they are taught by this example that
faith does not belong to human powers or to their common nature, but to divine
and particular grace. They are also warned what would have happened to them
apart from God’s special mercy. 4. Those who are called but not elect also
often become more useful through calling of

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 38 | printed p. 26]

that kind, both to human societies and to the church itself, in external
duties. 5. The manifestation both of divine grace and of human guilt is also
among the ends proposed.

Such were the reasons by which the Remonstrants say they are moved to deny
that proposition. The replies by which they try to evade its proofs are of
exactly the same quality. Here the first place for itself is claimed by that
noble chain of our salvation, Romans 8:29: “Those whom he predestined to be
conformed to the image of his Son, he also called,” and so forth. This golden
passage is like a fatal rock for all those who refuse to admit the grace of
election; they kick against the goads when they try to blunt its edge. Those
who have fallen upon this stone have always so far been broken to pieces, and
the one upon whom it falls it utterly shatters. Let us see, then, what new
method of evading its force and impact these men think must be chosen above all
others. They say that the passage concerns neither this predestination to
salvation nor the means now under discussion, but the predestination and
calling of believers to the cross and afflictions, and through them to glory.
This interpretation receives an enlargement from the gloss of Johannes
Arnoldus in his *Defense of Arminius*, p. 284: “The sense of the passage is
that those whom God acknowledges as his own—namely, believers and those who
love God—are predestined to conformity to the image of the Son of God in the
cross and, through the cross, in glory; that the same people are called to
that conformity in the cross and are justified contrary to the judgment of
the world which curses them,” and so forth. Socinus’s *Catechism*, chapter 10,
holds the same opinion. But how monstrously this sense degenerates from the
golden truth must be considered more carefully, point by point.

1. Two things are distinguished from each other in verse 28: afflictions and
similar things, and the persons around whom they are so ordered as to turn out
for good. “All things work together for good to those who love God.” But it is
plain that the words which follow—“to those who are called according to his

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 39 | printed p. 27]

purpose,” and so forth—belong to the description of the persons, not to the
afflictions and their origin, as these men would have it.

2. The first word found in the description of the persons, κλητοί, “called,”
is nowhere read in all of Scripture in the sense of directly denoting those
who are afflicted as the terminus of the act signified. There is no mention in
all of Scripture of κλητοῖς θλιβομένοις, “called to be afflicted,” but
everywhere of κλητοῖς ἁγίοις, “called to be saints” (Rom. 1:7; 1 Cor. 1:2,
etc.). In those places the object to which they are called is directly nothing
other than grace (1 Thess. 4:7) and glory (Eph. 1:18); affliction is merely an
accidental condition, not that which has the character of an aim. First Peter
5:10 says, “He called you to eternal glory, after you have suffered a little
while.”

3. The second adjunct, “according to purpose” or predetermined purpose, κατὰ
πρόθεσιν, demonstrates clearly enough both the kind and the nature of that
calling. For what Paul customarily denotes by this expression appears from
other passages. Ephesians 1:11 says, “In him we were allotted an inheritance,
having been predestined according to his purpose, that we should be to the
praise of his glory.” Second Timothy 1:9 says, “He saved us and called us with
a holy calling, according to his own purpose and grace.” Romans 9:11 says,
“That God’s purpose according to election might remain, from him who calls.”
Is anyone so blind that he does not see this purpose constantly referred by
the Apostle to glory and grace?

4. At the beginning of the chain, its first link or first element—“for those
whom he foreknew”—shows not obscurely where those steps tend. For it contains
the force of a proof of this kind: all things work together for good to those
called according to purpose, for God foreknew these people before they
existed, and so forth. This reasoning would have been entirely invalid if both
that calling and this foreknowledge regarded not salvation and grace but
afflictions alone. For afflictions and persecutions turn into a

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 40 | printed p. 28]

deadly stumbling block for very many who, on the Remonstrants’ opinion, are true
believers (Matt. 13:21). That invention about affliction is also foreign to the
meaning and sense which the Holy Spirit denotes by this word, as many other
passages show, but especially this one: “Elect according to the foreknowledge
of the Father, unto sanctification of the Spirit,” and so forth (1 Pet. 1:2),
as also the other one, Romans 11:2.

5. The later element of the same link, “he also predestined them,” both
explains the earlier one and confirms that prior explanation. For προόρισμος,
or predestination, properly signifies a prior ordination to an end, or to
something else considered as an end and a good, since end and good are
convertible. The meaning by which it is applied to the cross and afflictions,
considered in themselves, is very improper. We are indeed said to be appointed
to suffer afflictions (1 Thess. 3:3). But this should be referred not so much
to those afflictions as to the glory to which they lead, so that through many
afflictions we may at last enter the kingdom of God (Acts 14:22). Since,
therefore, this understanding of predestination is improper and unusual, and
there is no compelling reason here to depart from the customary sense, they
think far too poorly of their readers when they press so diluted an
interpretation upon their credulity.

6. The end of that act leaves no uncertainty: “that they should be conformed
to the image of his Son, that he might be the firstborn among many brothers.”
That this conformity is not to the image of the Son’s afflictions (which the
Remonstrants nevertheless take for granted), but to the image of glory, is
proved by various evidences. 1. Predestination to conformity to the image of
Christ the Son is one and the same thing as predestination to the adoption of
the sons of God through Christ (Eph. 1:5). The affinity—or rather the
identity—of the description and the words indicates this abundantly. For this
reason this conformity is said to be made to the image of the Son rather than
simply to the Son: so that the respect in which this likeness is located may
appear more clearly,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 41 | printed p. 26 (sic; intended p. 29)]

namely, in sonship. But this adoption regards glory and dignity; therefore that
conformity does also. 2. Conformity to the Son has, as it were, conformity of
inheritance attached to it, verse 17: “If sons, then heirs—heirs indeed of God
and fellow heirs with Christ.” But the inheritance pertains to glory;
therefore conformity to the Son does also. 3. We have the same Apostle’s
express exposition of the image of the Son in terms of glory in 2 Corinthians
3:18: “We are transformed into the same image, from glory to glory.” So also
John, 1 John 3:2: “When he is revealed, we shall be like him,” or “in his
likeness.” 4. The appended declaration, “that he might be the firstborn among
many brothers,” seems to breathe nothing other than glory. For it signifies
that Christ’s primogeniture both shines forth and is made illustrious through
this conformity. But in Holy Scripture this is constantly referred not to
afflictions and the cross, but to glory. Thus Colossians 1:18 says, “And he is
the head of the body, the church; he is the beginning and the firstborn from
the dead, that he might be first in all things.” Primogeniture does not pertain
to the state of the dead or afflicted, but to those who are *from* the dead,
just as in Philippians 2:9 it is openly mentioned as a consequence, not a part,
of afflictions and humiliation: “Therefore God also exalted him to the highest
place and gave him the name which is above every name.”

7. The next link also has the same force: “Those whom he predestined, he also
called.” For 1. even if it is granted, as the Remonstrants assume, that
afflictions are regarded here both in predestination and in calling, it will
nevertheless follow by force of the analogy that, just as people are
predestined to the cross before they are called, so the same people are also
predestined to salvation before they are called. This utterly destroys the
Remonstrants’ entire defense, in which they contend that calling precedes in
order that predestination

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 42 | printed p. 30]

which is unto salvation. 2. The passage teaches that this calling is of such
a kind that it causes all things to work together for good, something that can
in no way be affirmed of afflictions.

8. What follows has the same value: “Those whom he called, he also justified.”
For 1. he called many to the cross whom he nevertheless did not justify. Both
common experience and daily observation confirm this, as does the example of
the rich man in Mark 10:31, whom Christ our Savior called to take up his cross
and follow him, yet did not justify. For he went away sorrowful because he had
many possessions. 2. Nowhere in the New Testament is the term
“justification,” when it is taken absolutely and denotes an act of God, used
in such a way that it is referred only to the judgments of worldly people,
without any regard for that divine sentence by which God frees people from
his wrath to be revealed against sinners. Since, therefore, neither the usage
of the word permits nor any reason compels us to regard the judgment of the
cursing world either solely or even principally in this act of justification,
the Remonstrants cannot be excused from reckless audacity when they try to
twist an open statement to their private opinion by so crude an invention.

9. The statement which occupies the last place is no less forceful: “Those
whom he justified, he also glorified.” For 1. in this clause the Apostle
assures us that this whole series regards nothing other than proving the
glorification of all those who are called according to purpose. 2. He plainly
establishes that all the justified attain glory, and consequently that
justification of a cause against the slanders of worldly people cannot alone
be understood here. For many are justified by God with respect to their cause
and the sufferings dependent upon it who nevertheless never ascend to glory.

10. By those foreknown, predestined, called, and justified in this passage,
only those must be understood for

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 43 | printed p. 31]

whom Christ was delivered in such a way that, together with him, all things were
also given to them, verse 32. But not all those who are called to the cross are
of this kind. Therefore not all of them are predestined, called, justified, and
glorified. Here it must also be observed that this argument is constructed
affirmatively *a minori*: that is, if what seemed the lesser thing to do was
done for us, then what seems the greater thing to do will also be done, the
former being presupposed. But it seems altogether unworthy that Christ’s death
should be brought into such a comparison with calling to the cross and to
afflictions.

11. What occurs in verses 33–34 must also be referred to this point: it
concerns God’s elect, those justified by God, and those saved by Christ’s
intercession. Since these things belong only to true heirs of salvation and to
those elected to salvation (as Nicolaas himself seems to admit on p. 137), and
yet are all attributed to the very same persons to whom the preceding calling,
predestination, and justification are attributed, no reason permits those
preceding titles to be restricted to the cross and afflictions.

12. Finally, to put an end to this boundless material: 1. when they say this
predestination is to afflictions, they either understand bare and naked
sufferings, or at the same time include the manner of bearing them, the
certainty of victory, and the crown which follows. If they mean the bare cross,
then they locate conformity to Christ’s image in something that happens to
many apostates who perish. For Christ himself teaches that persecution often
arises for them because of the Word, or because of Christ (Matt. 13:21). But if
they think that grace, victory, and the crown are also to be understood
together, then they concede the very position they are trying to avoid:
election to salvation and the means connected with it. 2. When the Remonstrants
themselves concede that those who are called are predestined to glory through
afflictions, they understand this as occurring either absolutely or upon a
condition. If absolutely, then they surrender the case; but if, upon a
condition, they

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 44 | printed p. 32]

suspend it, then 1. they seem to correct the Apostle, who makes all these connections
necessary. 2. They attribute nothing greater to the afflicted than to the
unafflicted. For anyone, even with affliction removed, has an open entrance to
salvation and glory if he can furnish the condition of faith and perseverance.
What need, then, was there that so accepted an opinion should be proved with
such great effort and through so many dangers? And Arminius himself expressly
says that this passage must be understood in this way (*Private Disputations*
40, final thesis).

By God’s grace, I think I have now sufficiently reinforced that principal and
powerful passage. Another wholly similar and almost equal passage is Acts
13:48: “As many as were appointed to salvation believed.” They say that Luke
has in view here not any prior ordination or predestination of God to
salvation, but the present condition, disposition, fitness, or aptitude of the
Gentiles of whom he speaks, with which they had been endowed by God’s grace so
that they might attain eternal life. Thus, on hearing God’s Word, they rejoiced
in it and extolled the Word of God with praises; hence they are contrasted
with those disorderly Jews who rejected the Word of God and judged themselves
unworthy of eternal life, verse 46. We owe this wicked corruption of so clear
a passage to the ill-fated Socinus, whose opinion and almost whose very words
the Remonstrants follow, as can be seen in *Christ the Savior*, book 4,
chapter 13, and in the *Catechism*, chapter 13. But it will be easy to scatter
these clouds of opinion.

1. The word which the Evangelist uses here, τεταγμένοι, is nowhere read in
this sense throughout Holy Scripture, or even in any Greek author, so as to
denote an internal quality. Those who have so much as greeted the Greek
language from its threshold understand that there is a great difference
between τάσσεσθαι and διατίθεσθαι. 2. Both Junius’s Arabic version and
Tremellius’s Syriac agree upon a divine ordination and placement. 3. The

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 45 | printed p. 33]

customary sense of this word in the New Testament denotes an external
appointment and
election: “The powers that exist are ordained by God” (Rom. 13:1); “They
appointed him a day” (Acts 28:23); and so forth. I will not describe every
example, but this one very notable passage will stand in place of them all:
Acts 22:10, 14, which clearly explains the meaning of this word for us. There
God promises that Paul will be told at Damascus what τέτακται, “has been
appointed,” for him to do; and in verse 14 he is told by Ananias what was
προεχειρίσατο, “appointed” or “designated,” for him. Here we are, as it were,
divinely ordered to take ordination in the same sense as divine designation or
election. 4. Chrysostom himself, who understood the Greek word extremely well,
interprets these “appointed” as ἀφωρισμένους τῷ θεῷ, that is, as Erasmus
translates it, “foreappointed by God.” Erasmus himself also seems to have
borrowed from him the genuine paraphrase of this passage, which he frames as
follows: “Not all the Gentiles believed, but as many as divine mercy had
destined to eternal life, which no one attains unless called and elected by
God.” 5. The theologians of the Augsburg Confession (with whom the
Remonstrants boast of intimate agreement in these articles) regularly
interpret this passage in no other way than of divine predestination. Indeed,
in the *Book of Concord* itself these very words are applied to eternal
election: “Our salvation is so founded upon eternal predestination that even
the gates of hell cannot prevail against it. For it is written, ‘No one shall
snatch my sheep from my hand.’ And elsewhere, ‘As many as were appointed to
eternal life believed.’” Thus the *Formula of Concord*. 6. A disposition which
is an internal quality is not customarily referred to a reward (nor can it be
without a most hyperbolical misuse, ὑπερβολικωτάτῃ καταχρήσει). People, and
other subjects of qualities of this kind, are said to be disposed to a habit,
to a form, or to some action—not to a recompense or reward. It therefore seems
less

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 46 | printed p. 34]

fitting that a disposition of this kind should be referred to eternal life,
especially by those who contend that the character of a reward is principally
found in that life. 7. It likewise seems foreign to reason that they should be
said first to have been disposed to life and afterward to faith, since faith
itself (if this designation is appropriate to any endowment) is the primary
disposition to eternal life. Thus they would have been disposed to the reward
before they were disposed to the disposition which leads to it. “How absurd,
how incongruous, how incomprehensible!” the Remonstrants regularly cry when we
say that people are chosen and disposed by God to life before they are chosen
and disposed to faith. Do they take this away from God in order to attribute
it to man? 8. An inward disposition to life is a condition for life. If,
therefore, such a disposition exists before faith, then a certain condition of
the New Testament or new covenant is given both apart from and before faith.
9. When they place a disposition to life in a person who does not yet believe,
they necessarily say that it inheres in those who desire and seek life and who
walk in the divine commandments, insofar as they can, in order to obtain it.
But not all such people believe—which the Remonstrants must nevertheless
maintain from this passage. This is evident from that young man who approached
Christ on bended knee, seeking nothing other than life and also observing
God’s precepts in such a manner that on that account he is said, in a certain
way, to be dear to Christ (Mark 10:17, 21). He certainly seems to have been as
well disposed to life as almost any nonbeliever can be; nevertheless, he did
not believe but departed sorrowful. 10. The word employed here shows that it
denotes not a present disposition but a prior ordination. For τεταγμένοι ἦσαν,
“they had been appointed,” belongs not merely to what grammarians call the
past tense but to the pluperfect. The expression should properly be translated
in this way, although “they were” is often used with the same sense and tense.
It therefore denotes an ordination which had long preceded the present
disposition: namely, the eternal

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 47 | printed p. 35]

predestination of God. 11. Rejoicing in the Word and extolling it with praises
does not always have true faith as its consequence, which nevertheless this
text affirms concerning those appointed to life. For the whole people is said
to have hung upon Christ when they heard him (Luke 19:48), and to have given
praise to God for his deeds and words (Luke 8:43); yet not all believed.
Indeed, sincere joy arises out of faith as its fruit; it does not precede
faith. Joy therefore cannot be understood as that disposition to eternal life.
12. These words contain a restriction and a causal distinction. For the reason
why they believed, and the primary cause, is denoted in the same way in which
Christ himself said, “All whom the Father gave me will come to me.” Nor is it
consistent with the tenor of Scripture that the adequate reason for faith
should be sought from a person’s prior disposition, as in these passages it is
sought from divine ordination. Let me therefore conclude with the weighty
words of Dr. Calvin: “It is a ridiculous quibble to refer this to the
disposition of those who believed, as if those whose minds were rightly
disposed received the gospel. For this ordination can be understood only of
God’s eternal counsel. Luke does not say that they were appointed to faith,
but to life, because God predestined his own to the inheritance of eternal
life. This passage therefore teaches that faith depends upon God’s election.”

The third passage (Titus 1:1), which seems to agree very well with the former
ones, says that faith belongs to God’s elect. For when Paul had assumed for
himself the title “Apostle,” that is, “one sent,” he also wished to show for
what he had been sent, or with reference to what he had been appointed an
apostle. He therefore adds that he is an “apostle according to the faith of
God’s elect,” or “concerning the faith of the elect” (for κατὰ can be
explained in this way, as in οἱ καθ᾽ ὑπεροχὴν νόμοι, “laws concerning
excess”); that is, in order both to engender and to confirm that faith to which
the elect are called by God, and so forth. That this is the Apostle’s true
meaning appears from comparison with similar passages. When the same Paul
wished to explain to us the aim and

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 48 | printed p. 36]

end of the whole apostolate in Ephesians 4, he teaches that it regards nothing
other than the building up of Christ’s body, “until we all attain to the unity
of the faith and of the knowledge of the Son of God.” This is exactly the same
manner of speaking as that used here to Titus: “according to the faith of
God’s elect and the knowledge of the truth.” What in the former passage was
“the body of Christ” is here “God’s elect”; what there was “unity of faith” is
here “faith”; what there was “knowledge of the Son of God” is here called
“knowledge of the truth”; and what there was “to” is here denoted by
“according to.” When he likewise had to make a declaration about himself in
Romans 1:5, he presented the same form of expression: “Through whom we have
received grace and apostleship unto the obedience of faith among all nations,
for his name.” Nor is what he says in 2 Timothy 1:1 much different: “An apostle
of Jesus Christ through the will of God, according to the promise of life
which is in Christ Jesus”; that is, in order to reveal and fulfill the promise
of God, who promised us life in Christ Jesus, in whom he elected us before the
foundation of the world. This faith, then, is that gracious gift which he
communicates through the gospel. This treasure is not given to all: “Faith
does not belong to all” (2 Thess. 3:2), but is peculiar to certain chosen
people—“the faith of the elect.” Therefore the following can with very good
reason be deduced from this testimony. 1. God’s proper aim in sending apostles
and the means of salvation is to engender and promote true faith. 2. This faith
is given through such means to certain people from special love—that is, by
virtue of election.

Here the Remonstrants make two exceptions. 1. By “faith” is here understood the
doctrine of faith, which the Apostle in the same place calls “knowledge of the
truth.” Paul was the apostle of this doctrine in such a way that he announced
it to the elect, that is, to saints and believers, just as in Jude verse 3
faith is said to have been delivered to the saints or elect and to have been
received by them. 2. “The faith of the elect” can also be understood as the
faith which the elect possess, without regard to which no one is elect.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 49 | printed p. 37]

But 1. this faith is not called *cognition* in the text, but *recognition* of
the truth. The word is ἐπίγνωσις, whose proper significance must not be
neglected here, where it has emphasis and force. 2. If this recognition of the
truth is one and the same thing as that faith, then “faith” in this passage
does not signify doctrine. For there is no small difference between recognition
of the truth and the truth itself. The truth is the doctrine of faith, but
recognition of the truth is the reception of that doctrine—that is, the gift
of faith in a person. 3. I do not adequately understand what they mean when
they say that the Apostle’s office was to announce that doctrine of faith to
believers. For 1. the apostles’ principal duty and concern was “to evangelize
not where Christ was named, lest he should build upon another’s foundation,
but to those to whom he had not been announced.” The Apostle therefore
preached neither only nor principally to believers. But if they think this is
said because he also preached to believers, then they leave no reason why
something should be attributed to believers as their peculiar possession when
it is at least equally common to others, if not more so. 2. When they say that
election concerns believers, they mean that believers exist as such before
they are elect. If they follow the same reasoning here, then they teach that
people were believers before the doctrine of faith was announced to them. 4.
They confuse things which must be distinguished when they make the elect and
the saints the same not only in reality but also in logical account. People
are elect in order that they may become saints (Eph. 1:5; 1 Pet. 1:2). It is
therefore necessary that they be elected before they can be called saints. 5.
This faith is the obedience of faith, as Paul himself explains it (Rom. 1:5).
“According to the faith of the elect” therefore means according to that
obedience of faith by which the elect are subjected to God. 6. This cannot be
called the faith “of the elect” because no one is elect without regard to it
(which is the Remonstrants’ other escape), because then 1. it would not so
properly be called “the faith of the elect.” Election would follow faith,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 50 | printed p. 38]

not faith following election; it would be election of believers, or rather
election from faith, not faith of the elect. The elect would not be the
subject of faith and prior to it by nature, but the reverse. 2. Only that faith which is
the prerequisite condition of election would be understood—that is,
consummated obedience of faith, which is attained only at the end of life. But
this faith is not so restricted. It denotes a present state, as is clear from
the words attached to it—recognition, godliness, unto the hope of life—and
especially from the fact that Paul says Titus was already at that time his son
in that faith.

Thus far I have confirmed the first part of the third argument. They reply to
the minor premise by means of a twofold distinction. 1. The decree not to give
the means does not flow from God’s absolute will, but from his just judgment
against those unworthy of such a benefit. 2. God draws all sufficiently,
although not effectually with respect to the outcome. The subject of divine
drawing and the mode of conversion must be investigated under the fourth
article. The cause of the decree must be treated not here, but in the other
part of this first question. For now it will be enough to have noted that all
are unworthy of these benefits, and yet the means of salvation are granted to
some (often those more unworthy) and indeed are rendered effectual, while the
rest are left in sin; and that this occurs by a firm decree and before the end
of life. From this it necessarily follows that the first distinction of those
who are to be saved from the rest has its origin in election and special
grace.
