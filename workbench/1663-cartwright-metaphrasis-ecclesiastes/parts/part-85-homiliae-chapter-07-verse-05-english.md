VERSE 5. It is better to hear the rebuke of a wise person than for someone to
hear the song of fools.

Solomon further confirms what he said about preferring sorrow to laughter from
the efficient cause of each, just as if he were to say: that whose cause is
more excellent is itself more excellent. The cause of sorrow is more excellent
than the efficient cause of joy and laughter; indignation and sorrow must
therefore be preferred to gladness. He takes the proposition as manifest and
granted. He proves the assumption because reproof and rebuke, which produce
sorrow, must be preferred to the flattering speeches of fools, which bring
gladness. For a just reproof, such as the one applied by the wise, leads those
reproved to acknowledge their fault and to exercise greater caution
thereafter, and it produces a repentance not to be regretted. Flatteries and
the enticements of words, however, corrupt the mind and render it both proud
and secure. The Prophet David knew this

thoroughly when he desired to be treated and chastised more harshly by a righteous person
rather than taste the delicacies of the wicked. Psalm 141:4–5. Conversely, the
calamities of God's people are shown to have arisen from their desire that the
Prophets preach things pleasing to them. An outstanding example is found in
Ahab, who is said to have perished for listening to false prophets who
announced everything to please him and in accordance with the judgment of his
own mind, while refusing to lend an ear to Micaiah when he foretold things
disagreeable to him. It must nevertheless be observed that the sadness
arising from reproof does not mean the sadness that is felt on account of the
reproof itself, but that which arises from the sin committed and reproved.

It should further be noted that flattering words are compared to songs,
because they are no less pleasing to our corrupt and depraved nature than the
sweetest music. We must therefore imitate the serpent's prudence and stop our
ears against these enchantments. The recently cited Psalm 141 agrees with this
most aptly, for there the flatteries of words are compared to exquisite
delicacies that are exceedingly treacherous and alluring to people. And in
Proverbs 13 the lips of a prostitute are said to drip honey, while her words
are said to be smoother than oil; see their effect in Proverbs 7.

Finally, it must not be passed over that Solomon regards as fools those who
employ flattering adulation and speak to gain favor, however prudent they may
consider themselves and be considered by others. Conversely, those who rebuke
us deservedly are accounted wise.
