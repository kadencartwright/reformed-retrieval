[Book I, printed p. 87; BSB canvas 00113]

PENOTTUS.

Secondly, in St. Thomas's opinion eternity is the measure of things subject to time insofar as it
contains time, which is properly their measure, because whoever contains a containing whole also
contains everything contained in it. Therefore the distinction between the existence of things in
eternity as an alien and inadequate measure and in time as their proper measure is empty and
contrary to St. Thomas's mind.

RESPONSE.

Without doubt all things, insofar as they exist in time, also coexist with eternity and can in this
respect be said to exist in eternity. But we deny that contingents exist in eternity from eternity,
or that Aquinas teaches this anywhere. Yet I would not dare affirm that Thomas's opinion is so clear
as to be evident to everyone reading his words. Not only have the Dominicans interpreted Aquinas as
the author of the opinion that all things exist in eternity from eternity; Scotus, Durandus, and the
Nominalists understand his mind in no other sense. I think no sober person before this man of Novara
failed to notice that Thomas's words strongly incline this way. But the Novarian fiction that things
are really, not intentionally, present to God from eternity yet not in eternity appears devised
beyond all sobriety. He must either introduce the eternity, simply stated, of things subsisting not
only in eternity but also in time, or obtain no foundation for so monstrous a fiction.

Nevertheless, the Novarian's argument here is defective on two grounds. First, if according to
Thomas eternity measures things subject to time only insofar as it contains time, how will it measure
time itself? Time itself, not merely temporal things, is posited as coexisting with God in eternity
from eternity, and for a plausible reason: while time exists it undoubtedly coexists with eternity;
and since eternity is indivisible, it supposedly must coexist with all eternity and consequently
from eternity, since the instant with which other things coexist is both indivisible and eternal.

Secondly, if eternity were a common measure entirely like other common measures, nothing would exist
in this common measure prior to existing in its proper measure, just as nothing exists in other
common measures prior to its proper measure. But eternity has a very different character from other
common measures. Other common measures are of the same kind as things' proper measures: as a proper
measure is divisible, so are the common measures. Eternity, however, is indivisible, a single instant
abiding from eternity to eternity. Thus whatever coexists with it must coexist with its whole.
