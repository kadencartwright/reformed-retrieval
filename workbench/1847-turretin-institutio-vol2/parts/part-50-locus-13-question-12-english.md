QUESTION XII.

THE GRACES AND GIFTS CONFERRED UPON CHRIST.

*What graces were conferred upon Christ’s human nature? And did he have faith
and hope? We affirm.*

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. The question does not concern the eternal grace which is in God, namely the
love and benevolence with which he has embraced Christ and will embrace him
forever. In this sense Christ is called

[Printed p. 305 | Internet Archive PDF 333 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the ἀγαπητὸς, *beloved,* Son in whom God was well pleased (Matt. 3:17). Nor
does it concern the grace of union, by which the humanity was assumed by the
Λόγῳ, *Word,* into the unity of the Person. Rather, it concerns habitual
graces, or gifts and perfections, conferred upon the human nature by virtue of
the hypostatic union.

[Marginal heading: It is proved that graces were conferred upon Christ both
truly and fully.]

II. Scripture teaches in more than one place that such graces were conferred
upon Christ truly and most fully. Isaiah 11:2 says that the Spirit of wisdom,
knowledge, and so forth would rest upon Christ. For this reason he is so often
called righteous, holy, and innocent (Isa. 53:11; Luke 1:35; Acts 3:14; Heb.
7:26). To this also pertains his being said to have been anointed with the
Spirit and power (Acts 10:38) and anointed with the oil of gladness (Ps. 45:7),
which can refer to nothing other than the gifts of the Holy Spirit which
wondrously gladden the soul. Their fullness is also designated when Christ is
called “full of grace and truth” (John 1:14) and is said to have received the
Spirit ἀμέτρως, *without measure* (John 3:34).

III. Yet this fullness must not be thought absolutely infinite, both because
the humanity itself is finite in itself and cannot be capable of the infinite,
and because this grace is something created, to which it is therefore
repugnant to be infinite. Rather, this must be understood in a qualified
sense: 1. With respect to the graces communicated either to angels or to men,
than which Christ’s graces are far greater. In others there is a fullness of
*sufficiency,* insofar as they obtain from God as much grace as suffices for
their salvation. But in Christ there is a fullness of *abundance,* which
suffices not only for him but also for others, so that we all draw from his
fullness (John 1:16). 2. With respect to degrees, because all Christ’s graces
possessed every degree which can be possessed under God’s law, beyond which
nothing can be given and to which nothing equal or comparable can be found
among men. It is no objection that there is no finite thing which cannot be
said to be equaled by another. For this indeed holds among things of the same
kind and account. But Christ’s grace is not such with respect to the graces of
other creatures, because his was universal while theirs are particular.

IV. These gifts of the Holy Spirit in their fullness, whether extensive with
respect to their parts and species or intensive with respect to their degrees,
are enumerated in Isaiah 11:1–2: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude,
knowledge, and fear. The scholastics add piety from V.V., but without basis or
necessity, since it is already included in the fear of God. This does not mean
that all the gifts are contained in that number, since mention of other gifts
is made elsewhere. Rather, the principal gifts are indicated which were
especially required for the administration of the office imposed upon Christ.
Nor is it only said that this Spirit with his gifts would be conferred upon
Christ, but that he would rest upon him. This denotes not only a true and real
communication, but also the duration and perpetuity of the gifts. For Christ
possessed them not as an act and as a certain transitory or unstable motion,
but as something permanent and fixed, which he used most freely however much
and as often as he pleased.

[Marginal heading: Did Christ have faith and hope?]

V. The scholastics raise the question here whether Christ had faith and hope.
Not a few of them bring an accusation against Calvin, especially Feuardentius
in book VIII of the Θεομαχίας, *Theomachia,* because Calvin attributes faith
to Christ. He calls this error impious and contrary to Scripture. Yet that
faith must not be denied to Christ absolutely is shown by Scripture in more
than one place. It calls Christ faithful (Heb. 2:17; 3:2). Peter in Acts 2
refers the words of Psalm 16 to Christ: “My flesh will rest in hope.” And in
Matthew 27:46, when Christ calls the Father his God, he attests his faith in
him. But faith cannot belong to him under every formal aspect under which it
belongs to men, since in that manner it involves imperfection.

[Marginal heading: And in what manner?]

VI. Faith, therefore, is attributed to Christ, not insofar as it is a trusting
apprehension of God’s mercy, because in that sense it belongs only to sinners;
nor with respect to the mode of knowledge, as regards the obscurity of a
riddle and insofar as it is opposed to εἴδει, *sight* (2 Cor. 5:7), which
cannot occur without an imperfection that does not befall Christ. Rather, it
is attributed with respect to the substance of knowledge and assent to the
thing known, that is, to the doctrine revealed by God, and with respect to the
confidence which rests upon God’s goodness as he provides us all necessary
things.

VII. The same must be said of hope. Whatever perfection there is in it with
respect to certainty, by which we rest firmly upon the divine promise about a
future thing, is rightly attributed to Christ. But what involves defect and
imperfection, insofar as it is a still-obscure expectation of a thing not yet
possessed (Rom. 8:24), must not be ascribed to him.

VIII. Although Christ’s soul already enjoyed blessedness from the beginning
and enjoyed God himself by virtue of the hypostatic union, it did not yet have
the fullness of blessedness because of the necessity of the passion. It was to
attain that fullness at last after the resurrection and ascension, when, having
become one who had attained the goal, Christ was fully glorified with respect
to both soul and body. Therefore, nothing prevented him from believing in God
and hoping for the glory promised to him (Heb. 12:2).
