QUESTION X.

ON THE CHARACTER.

In the three sacraments of baptism, confirmation, and orders, is a character
imprinted on the soul, that is, a spiritual and indelible mark? We deny against
the papists.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

I. The papists teach that the character is a mark impressed either upon the
substance of the soul or upon its powers, by the power and efficacy of the
three sacraments of baptism, confirmation, and orders. They maintain that the
reason why these three sacraments must not be repeated is that they imprint an
indelible character.

II. Canon 9, Session 7, of the Council of Trent states: “If anyone says that in
the three sacraments—namely, baptism, confirmation, and orders—a character is
not imprinted on the soul, that is, a certain spiritual and indelible mark by
which they cannot be repeated, let him be anathema.” Here they craftily conceal
and refuse to define the numerous questions about this character debated by
the scholastics, thus hiding their domestic disorders and their theologians’
conflicting opinions. For they dispute sharply among themselves whether this
character pertains to grace; whether it is an absolute or a relative entity;
with respect to its subject, whether it is in the soul’s substance or in its
powers; and if in its powers, whether it is in the intellect or the will. They
also dispute to which species of quality it can be referred.

III. Setting aside the scholastics’ vain disputes, we declare that this whole
invention, whatever it is, must be eliminated from the church and the school
of sounder theology, since no such character is taught in Scripture or can be
supported by any suitable reason. We say that believers have a twofold seal.
One is internal, namely the Holy Spirit, by whom they are sealed for the day of
redemption (Eph. 4:30). The other is external, namely baptism, by which we are
distinguished from Turks, Jews, and other unbelievers. But we acknowledge no
other character.

[Marginalia: It is proved that no indelible character is given in the
sacraments.]

IV. First, all the efficacy of the sacraments comes from their institution.
But that indelible character does not come from the institution of the
sacrament. For since the institution comprises only the external sign and the
thing signified, while this character belongs to neither, it follows that the
character does not depend upon the institution. Now it is evident that the
character belongs neither to the external sign nor to the thing signified. It
does not belong to the sign because the sacramental sign is external and falls
under the senses, while that character is invisible and is imagined to be
imprinted upon the soul. It does not belong to the thing signified because the
thing signified is grace. The papists deny that this character pertains to
grace because, being indelible, it remains in the damned, while there is no
grace in the damned.

V. Second, this character can distinguish us neither before God nor before
human beings. It cannot distinguish us before God because, as the knower of
hearts, he has no need of such a character. It cannot distinguish us before
human beings because it does not do so in this life, since it is said to be
spiritual and not to fall under the senses; nor does it do so in the future
life, since neither the damned nor the glorified will need this distinction
there.

VI. Third, it is falsely assumed that confirmation and orders are sacraments,
as will be proved later. And although baptism is a sacrament, God has not
appointed it for the operation that the adversaries ascribe to it.

VII. Fourth, the sacraments have the same operation and at least the same kind
of efficacy. Yet according to them the Eucharist imprints or leaves no
character, nor did the sacraments of the Old Testament. Add that there is no
reason why sanctifying grace should be said to be effected by all the
sacraments, but not the character, which is a secondary effect that falls even
upon the wicked.

VIII. Fifth, such a character has no use. For whatever is attributed to it
belongs either to sanctifying grace, as when it is said to render us fit for
the worship of God and conform us to Christ, or to the external sacrament
itself, as when it is said to distinguish us from others who do not receive
the sacrament. Add that this latter function belongs no less to the other
sacraments. Finally, since that character is found in the wicked, it cannot be
said to conform us to Christ unless we wish the wicked to be conformed to
Christ.

IX. Sixth, this character was introduced in later times after Gratian and
Lombard for no other purpose than to build the sacrament’s certainty upon
shifting sand and to take away the true consolation that ought to be drawn
from the sacraments.

X. Seventh, not a few at the council called such a character into question,
both because neither Gratian nor Lombard had mentioned it, and because Scotus
even said, “Neither Scripture nor the words of the fathers make it necessary
to posit it.” And after a long disputation Biel adds: “The things said about
the character are for the most part arbitrary and supported by little reason;
neither the authority of Scripture nor that of the holy fathers compels us to
posit a character understood in this manner.”

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XI. The anointing and sealing by the Holy Spirit of which Paul speaks (2 Cor.
1:21–22; Eph. 4:30) has nothing in common with the sacramental character,
either in name or in reality. It has nothing in common in name because the
name *character* does not occur there, nor are the sacraments under discussion.
It has nothing in common in reality because that anointing and sealing
metaphorically declares the communication of spiritual grace and the
confirmation of our hearts concerning our filial adoption and eternal
salvation. Both are proper to believers and pertain no less to the Old than to
the New Testament, which cannot be said of the papists’ invented character.
Various adversaries do not deny this, such as Cajetan, Dionysius the
Carthusian, and Thomas, who understand anything but that character.

XII. Although circumcision left some scar from the excised foreskin, it does
not follow that in baptism some character is imprinted on the soul. It is not
necessary for the latter to correspond to the former in every respect; it is
enough for some likeness to exist. Nor does baptism correspond to circumcision
as the thing signified corresponds to the sign, but as a new type corresponds
to the old. Moreover, Scripture does not teach that the scar had any
signification. If it signified anything, it signified not the papists’
character, but rather the seal of the Spirit by which believers are
distinguished from unbelievers, or simply the external rite of baptism by
which Christians are distinguished from non-Christians.

XIII. The seal with which God’s servants are said to be marked on their
foreheads (Rev. 7:3) cannot be the sacramental character, which is common to
the godly and the wicked alike. Rather, it is in part the internal seal, namely
the Spirit of adoption, who is given only to the faithful servants of Christ
to seal them for the day of redemption, so that their salvation may be placed
beyond all danger. Or it is the external seal, namely the profession of God’s
name by which they are distinguished from unbelievers. The Holy Spirit means
nothing other than to signify their certain preservation through the
providence of their God, alluding to the human custom of impressing a
conspicuous mark on things that people wish to preserve and separate from
common things.

XIV. When Isaiah says that “the nations will come and see

his glory, and God will place a sign among them” (Isa. 66:19), this cannot mean
a character hidden within the soul. Rather, it means the
profession and preaching of the Christian faith, which is like a banner to
which people must gather so that they may be assembled under Christ. Nor, even
in Bellarmine’s own judgment, does the mark placed upon the foreheads of the
faithful in Ezekiel 9:4 belong here. They receive it so that they may be
exempted from the judgment threatening Jerusalem, because that mark is proper
to believers and not common to the wicked.

XV. The reason why baptism is not repeated while the sacred Supper is repeated
often is not that baptism imprints a character while the Eucharist does not.
Rather, it is because baptism is entrance into the church and the sign of
admission into the covenant, which must not be repeated, while the Eucharist
is the sacrament of spiritual nourishment, to which one must often return. Nor
is a second baptism withheld from those who return to the Christian faith
after falling away from it because baptism imprints an indelible character. It
is because the power of the baptism once conferred and God’s promise in it
remain for the person whom God has decreed to recall to repentance.
