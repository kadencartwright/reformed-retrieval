#### Some of our arguments against the real presence of Christ's body and blood in the Eucharist, under the species of bread and wine, drawn from the Scriptures

Because those arguments which Bellarmine, book 1, chapter 14, proposes for
solution are for the most part extrinsic and remote from the cause of the
Eucharist, and do not of themselves bind the adversary unless other things
which come to be discussed afterward are presupposed, we therefore will not
linger over them in this passage. Instead, we will briefly vindicate only two
or three of them which can be treated by themselves. For in this cause we
labor not from a scarcity but from an abundance of arguments.

[1632 witness | printed p. 92]

53. Protestant argument. Matthew 15 says, “Everything that enters into the
mouth goes into the belly and is cast out into the privy.” But it is impious
to think such a thing about Christ's body. Therefore it does not enter into
the mouth.

Bellarmine. The Lord speaks of foods which are taken to nourish the body.

Protestant reply. The truth of the statement does not depend upon the
intention of the one taking something but upon the nature of the thing.
Bellarmine therefore concedes that all those things which nourish the body go
into the belly and are cast out into the privy. But we assume that what is
received by the bodily mouth in the Eucharist nourishes the body, for upon
this depends the analogy which this sacrament has to our spiritual food.
Hence the scholastics also maintain that those things which remain in the
Eucharist after consecration, which they call the sacramental species, refresh
and nourish, and that the strength of wine can be taken in such quantity that
it would naturally intoxicate a person. Alexander of Hales, Part 4, question
10, member 7, article 2; Bonaventure and others, on Book 4, distinction 12.

Bellarmine. After the resurrection the Lord ate and drank, and yet did not
cast the foods out into the privy.

Protestant reply. He did not eat or drink so as to be nourished from it, but
for the sake of familiar intercourse. Therefore the counterexample does not
apply.

54. Protestant argument. Luke 24 says, “Touch and see,” and so forth. The
truth of Christ's flesh is proved by sight and touch, but it cannot be so
proved in the Eucharist. Therefore it is not there.

Bellarmine 1. The inference follows affirmatively: “This is touched and seen;
therefore it is a body.” But it does not follow negatively: “This is not
touched or seen; therefore it is not a body.”

Protestant reply. 1. If the inference follows affirmatively concerning this
or that body, then since bread is seen and touched in the Eucharist, it is
also bread. 2. It also follows negatively concerning a human body: “It is not
palpable or visible; therefore it is not a human body.” For although it may
happen that a human body is not actually seen or touched, it cannot happen
that it is not palpable.

Bellarmine 2. The Lord wished only to prove that he was not an empty specter
or shadow but a true body, and he proved that very well from the testimony of
the sense of touch. But by this argument alone the Lord did not prove that it
was a human body, living and the same as it had been before, for that argument
was without doubt insufficient.

Protestant reply. The Lord himself shows what he proved by this argument in
Luke 24:39: “For it is I myself.” Yet in this Bellarmine is more unbelieving
than Thomas Didymus himself, because he pronounces this argument insufficient
when all the circumstances are considered. For Thomas, whom the Lord rebukes
for singular unbelief, nevertheless believed because he saw, John 20:29.

55. Protestant argument. John 14 says, “I leave the world and go to the
Father.” There the Holy Spirit is also promised to supply the lack arising
from Christ's absence.

Bellarmine. He left the world with respect to human intercourse, and the Holy
Spirit was promised so that he might supply the presence of Christ conversing
among human beings in a human manner.

Protestant reply. 1. If “to leave the world” signifies nothing other than to
abandon human intercourse, then Christ's ascension into heaven was not a
change of place but only of intercourse. 2. If the Holy Spirit supplies human
intercourse, then the pope invades the place and office of the Spirit when he
boasts that he supplies that presence. 3. If what the papists teach about the
real presence of Christ's human nature wherever he saw fit is true, and thus
that he could be with all the faithful if he wished, then this vicarious work
of the Spirit would not be needed. For Christ himself, even insofar as he is
man, could furnish all things in the same manner immediately by himself as
they are now procured through the Spirit.

56. Protestant argument. In 1 Corinthians 10 and 11 the Eucharist is called
“bread” at least six times, even after consecration.

Bellarmine 1. It can be said that Scripture ordinarily calls things as they
appear outwardly. Thus it calls the bronze serpent a serpent, and so forth.

Protestant reply. 1. This is a tropic designation contracted by similarity
into a single metaphorical word. On this rationale, therefore, a trope would
be of frequent use in the doctrine of the Eucharist. 2. If Christ's body is
rightly called bread because of the similarity which it has to bread, then
bread can also rightly be called Christ's body because of its similarity and,
moreover, the singular relation which it has to Christ's body.

Bellarmine 2. It can very well be said that it is called bread because every
food is understood generically by the name “bread.”

Protestant reply. 1. Thus a trope is also admitted, namely a synecdoche of the
species for the genus. 2. It is not rightly said of every food, “which we
break.” 3. If this were the whole rationale of this designation, then in it
no account would be taken either of the bread before consecration or of the
species of bread afterward. Consequently, nothing would prevent the phrase
from having been equally apt if the apostle had employed the name “food.”
Why, then, is this tropic mode of speaking repeated so often?

Bellarmine 3. It is called bread because it is not only food but solid,
principal, substantial food, such as wheat bread is among bodily foods.

Protestant reply. 1. If it is food and a body, bodily present and eaten, then
it is wrongly distinguished here from

[1632 witness | printed p. 94]

bodily foods. 2. If it is such food for the soul as bread is for the body,
then it is received by the bodily mouth no more than bread is received
spiritually.

Bellarmine 4. In John 6 the heavenly food is frequently called bread.

Protestant reply. It is called bread by a trope which the adversaries do not
admit in the Supper. 2. There is no place for such a trope in the Supper,
where wheat bread is received, blessed, and, appearing the same, is thereafter
called by the same name.
