QUESTION VI

THE GENUS OF THEOLOGY

What Is the Genus of Theology?

[Marginal heading: Systematically, the genus of THEOLOGY is doctrine.]

I. Theology is considered either systematically and objectively, in the
manner of a discipline, or habitually and subjectively, in the manner of a
habit residing in the intellect. In the first sense no genus can be assigned
to it more precisely than *doctrine*, because it is taught by God and learned
in the church. But it is doctrine preeminently so called, far surpassing all
human doctrine by reason of its origin, matter, form, and end. Thus it is
commonly described in Scripture as “teaching,” John 7:17; 1 Timothy 4:6 and
6:3, and as a “pattern of teaching,” Romans 6:17, just as in the Old Testament
*Torah* denotes the whole doctrine of salvation. In the latter sense it is
properly described as a certain habit of the mind.

[Marginal heading: What habit is to be assigned to it.]

II. To discern more clearly what habit of the mind should be assigned to it,
we must observe that every habit of the mind is either a habit of knowing, a
habit of believing, or a habit of opining, just as there are three assents of
the mind to which the acts of knowing, believing, and opining are ordered. For
every assent of the mind rests either upon testimony or upon reason. If it
rests upon testimony, it is faith; if upon certain and solid reason, it is
knowledge; if only upon probable reason, it is opinion.

[Marginal heading: The source of the fivefold distinction of habits.]

III. The habit of knowing, insofar as it is opposed to the habit of believing
and opining, embraces under itself the five intellectual habits enumerated by
Aristotle in *Ethics* 6: understanding, knowledge, wisdom, prudence, and art.
For it is then taken broadly for any certain and evident habitual cognition,
whether it concerns necessary and contemplable things or contingent and
practical things. This fivefold distinction arises from the fivefold variety
of objects with which the mind is occupied. For it is occupied either with
necessary things, which are principles to which we assent spontaneously
without demonstration—the cognition of these is called *understanding*;
conclusions to which we assent by demonstration—which constitute
*knowledge*; or conclusions joined with principles—which express *wisdom*.
Or it is occupied with contingent things, which in turn are either things to
be done, whose end is nothing other than acting well—with these *prudence* is
occupied—or things to be made, which have production as their end—with these
*art* is occupied.

[Marginal heading: It is properly referred to none of the habits.]

IV. None of the intellectual habits, insofar as they are treated in the
*Ethics* and distinguished from one another, can constitute the true and
proper genus of theology, because all those habits are habits of knowing, but
theology is not a habit of knowing but of believing. 2. They are natural
habits, discovered and cultivated by human skill; but theology is
supernatural and given by God, and its principle is not human reason but
divine revelation. 3. They are all simply either theoretical or practical;
but theology is of a mixed genus, partly theoretical and partly practical.

V. In particular, theology cannot be *understanding*, because understanding
is only the cognition of principles, not conclusions, but theology is the
cognition of both principles and conclusions. Furthermore, understanding is
occupied with principles known by nature and clear by their own light, but
theology with principles revealed by God's Word. It is not *knowledge*,
because it rests not upon the evidence of reason but upon testimony alone,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 19 | IA leaf 73 | PDF p. 73]

and it does not rest in cognition but directs and orders cognition toward
action. It is not *wisdom*, because all the parts of wisdom are denied of it,
namely, the understanding of principles known through themselves and the
cognition of conclusions. It is not *prudence*, because it concerns not only
things to be done but also things to be believed, and prudence is a virtue
that directs civil actions, not spiritual ones. Finally, it is not *art*,
because it is not a productive habit concerning things that terminate outside
the producer in some work.

[Marginal heading: Yet it contains them all eminently.]

VI. Although theology cannot properly and strictly be called any one of these
habits, it is rightly said to include them all eminently within itself. For
insofar as it treats God as the supreme cause, it resembles wisdom; insofar as
it contains principles, understanding; insofar as it demonstrates
conclusions, knowledge; insofar as it directs actions, prudence; and insofar
as it builds the church, art. Hence Scripture indiscriminately attributes to
it the names of understanding, Psalm 119:22, 34; knowledge, Psalm 119:66;
Isaiah 5:13; wisdom, 1 Corinthians 2:6–7; prudence, Psalm 119:110 and
throughout Proverbs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5; and art, when the doctrine of faith is
called a work and a building which we ought to complete, 1 Corinthians 3:11;
2 Corinthians 6:1.

[Marginal heading: Wisdom is the most fitting of all for theology.]

VII. If some genus from among these habits is to be ascribed to theology,
wisdom is most analogous to it and comes nearest to its nature. This is not
precisely in the Aristotelian sense, for so it does not differ greatly from
understanding and knowledge, but rather in the Stoic sense, in which it is the
collection of all habits, both speculative and moral, according to Stobaeus,
*Discourse on Virtue* 1. Thus the Suda says that wisdom is “learning of all
things, and art, prudence, knowledge, and understanding.” The doctrine of
faith is likewise often designated by the name *wisdom* in Scripture, as in
Proverbs and 1 Corinthians 2:6–7, and so forth. Reason also proves this. 1.
Wisdom is the knowledge of the most excellent things. But theology treats
God, his works, and our eternal happiness, which are altogether the most
excellent things, and it treats them in the most excellent manner—not from
reason, but from divine revelation. 2. Wisdom is an architectonic discipline
that commands and directs all the others. But this is especially proper to
theology: it is the judge and mistress of them all, so that it judges them and
is itself judged by no other science. For all other disciplines must be
measured by its rule, so that whatever they contain which does not accord with
theology is rejected. Thus, although it does not prescribe principles and
objects to other disciplines, it nevertheless rules them to this extent: it
sets limits for them, so that they dare neither establish any object against
theology nor use their principles against it. It also rules them by reason of
the ultimate end, namely, the glory of God. Although they do not reach it
immediately, they must nevertheless direct themselves toward it.

VIII. If theology takes certain things from other disciplines, it does not
seek them as an inferior from superiors, but as a superior from inferiors,
like a mistress who freely uses her servants. It does not so much take things
from others as presuppose certain things known beforehand upon which it builds
revelation.
