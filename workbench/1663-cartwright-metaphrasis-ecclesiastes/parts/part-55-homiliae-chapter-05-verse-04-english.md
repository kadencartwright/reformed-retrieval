VERSE 4. When you have vowed a vow to God, do not delay to pay it, for he does
not delight in fools. Pay what you have vowed.

From the genus of words or speech directed to God, he descends to the species
of vows. Here it should be observed that he does not command a person to vow,
but commands him not to delay a vow, much less to make it void and fail to
perform what he has vowed. When we promise something to men, they resent it
unless what was said is done. They will aggravate the charge of bad faith even
further if people promise of their own accord without being asked. How much
more severely will God avenge this bad faith when we do not perform what we
vowed voluntarily? If it is a serious thing to mock men, much more is it
serious to make sport of God. If one is not permitted to break a promise given
to God, much less may one break a promise confirmed by an oath. Leviticus 27
shows that there are two kinds of voluntary and discretionary duties performed
for God: one consists of those made by a bare promise alone, and the other is
made with an oath added.

Whichever is performed for God, care must be taken that what is uttered by the
lips is accomplished in reality and deed. In this matter God so strictly
requires people who vow to stand by words once uttered—Leviticus 17:10—that if
something better than what was vowed is substituted in its place, he does not
accept it. What the person gives in substitution passes to God in such a way
that he remains a debtor who must pay in addition what he vowed.

Yet it is not only dangerous to break a pledge. Caution must also be exercised
so that it is fulfilled at the due and appointed time, without delay or
postponement. The day appointed for the performance of a vow must be observed
exactly. Those who have bound themselves by a written bond to pay money on an
appointed day suffer serious loss if they deviate from the prescribed and
appointed time by even a single hour. How much more serious is it to delay
with God, to whom we are bound and obligated at the peril of our lives? He
observes exactly the pledge he gave us. He is not even one moment later or
slower than he promised, but discharges what he promised and releases his
pledge. In the same way, he resents a pledge to him that is not performed at
the proper time.

In Saul—1 Samuel 15—God severely condemned the forestalling and anticipation
of the time he had appointed. Just as he hates premature haste, so he rejects
tardy delay and procrastination. Genesis 34 and 35:1–2. It is indicated plainly
enough that Jacob fell into misfortunes because he delayed paying his vow.

He gives the reason for this precept from God's accompanying displeasure with
vows of this kind, which either are not paid or are not performed at the right
time. Therefore, in making vows, as in the whole worship of God, we must look
not to what seems good and acceptable to us, but to what seems so to God. It
often happens that things which appear glorious and splendid to men are foul
and hateful before God, as in the case of those who slaughtered their children
to God. Whenever we present gifts to mortals, our chief care is to inquire and
search out what will be most pleasing and acceptable to them. How much more
must that be done for God, who always desires and delights in gifts which are
both lawful and conducive to the advantage and benefit of the givers? Men, by
contrast, often crave gifts which bring loss upon the givers.

In their entire worship the papists offer God what is most pleasant to
themselves: incense, broken caution, the sound of bells, idleness and release
from labor, and so forth. They are just like boys or fools who are going to
present a gift: neglecting everything else, the former offer a many-colored
garment or a fan, and the latter an apple or children's toys.

He confirms this point about God's displeasure from the character of those who
vow: they are fools. Those who vow what they either do not pay, or pay later
than is right, are plainly fools. What could be more foolish than wishing to
deceive one who both can and will avenge this vanity and bad faith, especially
when the choice was yours and it lay within your power to keep yourself free
from every danger by not vowing?

Thus it also happens that what men attribute to prudence and shrewdness God
attributes to folly. By keeping for themselves what they vowed to God, or
retaining it longer so that they may receive some profit from it, men refer
the whole business to prudence. God, however, reckons either such
misappropriation, or delay and procrastination in payment, to folly.
