### Chapter VI

#### On the Necessity of Works for Salvation

Bellarmine. 1. *The adversaries agree in this: that good works are not
necessary for salvation except by a necessity of presence, so that they have
no relation to salvation as if they were merits, causes, conditions, and so
forth. We, on the contrary, say that good works are necessary for a righteous
man’s salvation even by reason of efficiency, since they effect salvation.*

Protestant reply. We do not deny that good works have any relation to
salvation. For, as they say, good works are related to salvation already
obtained as a consequent adjunct and effect; to salvation to be attained as an
antecedent adjunct and disposition; and also as an argument confirming
confidence and hope of salvation. But we deny that any works of ours can be a
meritorious cause of justification and salvation.

#### Bellarmine’s Arguments

2. Bellarmine. *If the promise of eternal life is conditional, it is certainly
necessary to fulfill the condition if anyone wishes to be saved. But the
former is true. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant the conclusion in a sound sense, but this has
nothing to do with a necessary efficiency of works with respect to salvation.
For there are conditions other than efficient causes, and there is a necessity
other than that of an efficient cause. II. The promise of eternal life in
itself does not depend upon works as upon a condition effecting life, but as
one marking those who will live.

3. Bellarmine. *If the righteous man is not free from obligation to the divine
law, certainly he will not be saved unless he fulfills it. But the former is
true. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. I. The consequence is null. For by the same reasoning one
could conclude that no one can be saved who commits even the smallest sin
which the Pontificians call venial, since such a person does not fulfill the
law. II. Although the righteous man is not free from every obligation to the
divine law, he is nevertheless free from obligation to that obedience to which
righteousness would be imputed and life would be owed.

4. Bellarmine. Hebrews 10:36. *Patience is said to be necessary in order that
we may receive the promise, that is, in relation to salvation. But patience is
a certain work and an act of virtue distinct from faith.*

Protestant reply. I. We grant a relation of works to salvation, but not one of
meritorious efficiency. II. In that passage patience is nothing other
than the fortitude and constancy of faith and confidence, as plainly appears
from the context.

5. Bellarmine. 1 Timothy 2:14–15. *Perseverance in love and sanctification is
set down as necessary for salvation, without which a woman cannot be saved.*

Protestant reply. What does the fact that we are bound to enter upon the way of
salvation have to do with efficiency?

6. Bellarmine. Philippians 2:12. *Work out your salvation. Here efficiency is
mentioned.*

Protestant reply. There is no mention of the efficiency of works, but of those
who work. Their efficiency is directed and referred to salvation as a serving
means, not as a meritorious cause.

7. Bellarmine. 2 Corinthians 7:10. *Sorrow works repentance unto enduring
salvation. Repentance therefore effects enduring salvation.*

Protestant reply. The consequence is null, unless all things which are *unto
salvation* effect it as meritorious causes.

8. Bellarmine. 2 Corinthians 4:17. *Patience in tribulation works an eternal
weight of glory.*

Protestant reply. It works as a serving means tending toward it, but not as a
meritorious cause.

3. Bellarmine. Romans 8:13. *If you mortify the deeds of the flesh, you will
live.*

Protestant reply. Mortification, then, is a condition for life; who denies it?

13. Bellarmine. Romans 8:17. *For I judge that the sufferings of this present
time are not commensurate with the glory to come. This comparison plainly
declares that there is some power in the work of patience for acquiring that
glory.*

Protestant reply. I. This denial of parity plainly declares that no power of a
meritorious cause inheres in such a work. II. That comparison declares no
other power than that which every way has with respect to its end.

11. Bellarmine. Romans 10:10. *With the mouth confession is made unto
salvation. We see in this passage that faith does not suffice for salvation,
since it cannot be true and entire in the heart unless outward confession is
also added.*

Protestant reply. I. This is precisely what we maintain against the
Pontificians: true and entire faith cannot be separated from works. Bellarmine
labored strenuously to prove the contrary in *On Justification,* book 1,
chapter 15. II. Since the righteousness which confers a right to salvation is
attributed to faith in this very passage, and since confession, by
Bellarmine’s own admission, necessarily follows faith and for that reason is
said to be made unto salvation, it necessarily follows that salvation depends
wholly upon faith.

12. Bellarmine. Matthew 25:35. *For I was hungry, and you gave me food, and so
forth. This reason given plainly shows that good works are in some manner the
cause of eternal salvation.*

Protestant reply. I. It is not enough to establish meritorious efficiency if
works are *in some manner* a cause of salvation. II. Giving a reason does not
always show a cause. A certain reason is given without showing the cause of
mercy in 1 Timothy 1:13: *Mercy was shown to me, for I acted ignorantly.* III.
The cause of salvation is plainly indicated by the preceding words: *You who
are blessed, possess by hereditary right the kingdom prepared for you.* Only
fitness and congruity between the works and rewards of believers is noted in
the reason which follows. For it accords with Christ’s mercy and kindness that
he should do good to those who, in his name, did good to others.

13. Bellarmine. *If faith alone saved, it would follow that faith could save
even if it lacked every good work and were joined with every vice and sin. But
this consequent is plainly false. Therefore.*

Protestant reply. Not only the consequent but also the consequence is plainly
false, because to suppose a living faith joined with every vice and sin is to
imagine a faith living and dead at the same time; and to suppose a man saved
with every vice and sin is to imagine a man saved along with perdition.
Didymus of Alexandria, Jerome’s teacher, answered long ago: *It should be
noted, namely, that since faith is dead apart from works, it is now no longer
faith; for neither is a dead man a man. On James, chapter 2.*
