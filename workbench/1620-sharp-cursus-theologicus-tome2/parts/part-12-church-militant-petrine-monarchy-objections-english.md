#### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. If Peter is the rock upon which Christ builds the Church, then
Peter was constituted head and monarch of the Church, because what the
foundation is in a building, the head is in a body and a king in a kingdom,
and so forth. But the former is true in the cited passage. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied. Even the Fathers who interpret Peter as
the rock give other reasons why Peter is called a rock. Nazianzen says:
*Peter is called a rock because he has the foundations of the Church
entrusted to his faith.* And Theophylact: *because he was the first to issue
that confession upon which the Church was to be founded.* Furthermore, if he
were monarch of the entire Church because he is called a rock, then Peter
would have to be the only rock. But this is false, for Origen, *Tractate 1 on
Matthew*, says that every disciple of Christ is a rock, and Cyprian, epistle
27, says that this pertains to all bishops. It is therefore the fallacy of
putting what is not a cause in place of a cause.

2. The assumption is denied, because by “the rock” Christ does not mean Peter
the apostle, but himself, whom Peter

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 56 | printed col. 111 | bsb10175568]

confessed, as Augustine and even the Papists interpret this passage.

Instance. The pronoun “this” indicates some rock about which Christ spoke a
little before. But immediately before he had called Peter a rock. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because it is not necessary that this
rock had previously been named by Christ; it is enough if it was mentioned
by Peter, whom he is addressing. Furthermore, if in the proposition “a little
before” means “immediately before,” the proposition is false; if it does not,
then there are more than three terms.

3. It is not necessary for a relative always to refer to the nearest
antecedent, because in Scripture it is often referred to something more
remote, as in Acts 1:11; 2:23; 3:16.

4. The assumption is denied. Although the word *Cephas* signifies Peter and
a rock, it must nevertheless be explained according to the circumstances of
the passage, and here it no more signifies a rock than it does in Galatians
2:9.

Instance 2. Christ spoke in Syriac. But in the Syriac language Peter is
called *Cephas*. Therefore in Galatians he said “Cephas” and “upon this
Cephas,” a rock; and consequently “this” can refer only to Peter, who in that
passage was called a rock.

Response. Although in Syriac Peter and rock are expressed by one word,
Matthew, who understood Christ’s meaning under the Holy Spirit’s dictation,
nevertheless distinguished them both in person and in gender, just as the
Vulgate translation also distinguishes them. The meaning of the passage is:
you are rightly named Cephas by me, because, as is clear from your confession,
you are founded upon me, upon the faith as upon a rock, upon which I shall
henceforth build my Church, and this by the ministry of the word which I
commit to you and your companions.

Instance 3. The Greek translator whom

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 56 | printed col. 112 | bsb10175568]

the Latin followed did not render *σὺ εἶ πέτρα* but *Πέτρος*, because, since
among the Greeks *Πέτρος* and *πέτρα* mean the same thing, it seemed fitting
to give a man a masculine name; then, to explain the metaphor, he used the
word *rock* so as to remove the ambiguity. Therefore.

Response. It is false that those two words mean the same thing among the
Greeks, because they differ as a proper and a common noun, and as the name
itself and a derivative. Augustine, tractate 134 on John, testifies: *The
Church is founded upon the rock, from which Peter also received his name,
just as Christian comes from Christ.* Furthermore, Bellarmine’s reasoning
must be turned back against him: Matthew changed the gender because the
things are different. Indeed, if he had said, *σὺ εἶ Πέτρος κἀπὶ τούτῳ τῷ
Πέτρῳ*, he would have removed all ambiguity.

Instance 4. Christ said, “I say to you.” Therefore what follows refers to
Peter.

Response. The inference is denied, because he addresses Peter about other
things rather than about Peter himself, just as two things are contained in
those words.

Instance 5. If those words refer to Christ, he would say “I build,” because
while he was establishing it he had built many upon himself. But he says “I
shall build,” because he had not yet constituted Peter the foundation but was
going to do so after the resurrection. Therefore.

Response. This puts what is not a cause in place of a cause. He speaks in the
future because he looks toward the Church that was to be gathered chiefly
from the Gentiles, of which Peter was not even a minister because of the
compact made with Paul. Furthermore, it is denied that Peter was constituted
the foundation after the resurrection. Finally, if Bellarmine’s reasoning
were sound, he would have said “I built” in the past tense.

Instance 6. For the Church to be built upon Peter signifies metaphorically
that the government of that

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 57 | printed col. 113 | bsb10175568]

Church was entrusted to Peter. Proof: it belongs to a foundation-stone to
govern and support the entire building.

Response. The antecedent is denied. It is indeed the function of a foundation
to support, but not to govern. The elect are built upon this foundation by
faith, because he is speaking only of the Church of the elect, against which
the gates of hell will not prevail. From this we argue against the
Pontificians as follows: Peter and rock differ as a proper and a common noun,
and as a primary and a derivative term; but these cannot be one thing.

2. Christ speaks of the foundation of the entire Church, therefore of the
Church Triumphant as well as the Church Militant. But Peter cannot be head of
the Church Triumphant as well as the Church Militant.

3. If Peter is constituted head and foundation of the entire Church, then
when Peter fell the whole Church fell, because when the foundation falls the
building cannot stand. But the Church which is built upon the rock cannot
fall, because the gates of hell will not prevail against it.

Objection 2, from Matthew 16:19: *I shall give you the keys of the kingdom of
heaven.* The one to whom Christ promised the keys of the kingdom of heaven
was constituted head of the Church. But Christ promised the keys to Peter.
Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because to receive the keys signifies
the power of administering the Church, not monarchy.

2. He did indeed give the keys, but not to Peter alone. Thus he promises them
to Peter in the name of the others, because when all were asked, “Whom do you
say that I am?” Peter answered in the name of all, and thus the promise was
made to all in his person, as is clear from Matthew 18:18 and John 20:22.

3. Granted that this promise was made particularly to Peter, the thing
promised was not yet conferred, as is clear from the cited passages. Thus
Augustine, tractate 50 on John, says that Peter

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 57 | printed col. 114 | bsb10175568]

bore in this the figure of the Church; Jerome, book 1 *Against Jovinian*, says
that all the apostles received the very same thing; and Lyranus, *On the
Simplicity of Prelates*, says that the Lord gave the keys in the person of
one in order to indicate the unity of all. So too Augustine, tractate 124 on
John. It is therefore not a cause in place of a cause.

Instance 1. At that time Peter was not the Church’s legate or vicar. Therefore
he received the keys in his own name, not in the Church’s.

Response. The inference is denied, because he received them for another
reason, namely, because he answered in the name of the others. Furthermore,
Bellarmine himself admits that Peter did not receive the keys at this time,
because he was not constituted the Church’s foundation until after Christ’s
resurrection.

Instance 2. The keys are properly promised to the one who issued the
confession, and therefore he says, “I say to you.” But Peter issued the
confession in his own person. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is distinguished. “The keys are promised to the
one who issued the confession” is true insofar as he issued the confession,
but otherwise false. Yet he issued it in the name of the others, because they
were all being questioned; and thus the assumption is denied.

Instance 3. “I shall give you the keys” was said to the one to whom “Get
behind me, Satan” was said. But the latter was said to Peter alone. Therefore.

Response. If “alone” is added to the proposition, as it must be, the
proposition is denied, because Peter alone was the one who dissuaded Christ
from suffering for the Church, and thus he alone had given him occasion to
say, “Get behind me, Satan.” But in the name of the others, who no less than
he were questioned, he answered by the confession; and because it was made
in the name of the others, it gave Christ the occasion to make the promise.

Instance 4. He answered in the name of the others, not as a herald but as
prince, head, and mouth, since the others did not know what to answer.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 58 | printed col. 115 | bsb10175568]

Response. It is false that he answered as prince and head; he did indeed
answer as their mouth. Furthermore, it is false that the other apostles did
not know what to answer, as is clear from the outcome, for he questioned them
all and accepted Peter’s answer as the answer of all; otherwise he would
afterward have questioned the others.

3. Bellarmine contradicts himself here, for if Peter answered as their mouth,
he answered from their judgment—or else he lied.

Instance 5. If he answered in the name of all, he did this either because the
others had entrusted this charge to him or because he knew what they would
answer. But neither can be said: not the first, because he learned this by
the Father’s revelation, not by human consultation, for it is said, “Flesh
and blood did not reveal this to you”; not the second, because the revelation
was made to him alone. Likewise, if he had known their mind he would have
indicated it in some way, as in John 6:68.

Response. The assumption is denied, for neither branch proves the point. Not
the first, because this does not follow: he learned it by divine revelation;
therefore he did not answer in their name—since both could have occurred.
Furthermore, the reasoning of the second branch is false, for Christ approves
Peter’s judgment as that of all, while no one knows the Son except through
the Father, Matthew 11:27. Finally, it does not follow: he used words in the
plural number in John 6; therefore he had to use them in this way, or he did
not answer in the name of the others.

Instance 6, from the word *keys*. The keys signify supreme power, Isaiah
22:22 and 9:6; Revelation 3:7, where the metaphor of the keys does not signify
the remission of sins or the ministry of the Gospel but ecclesiastical
principality. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because the same phrase can signify
different things in different passages

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 58 | printed col. 116 | bsb10175568]

according to their circumstances. Furthermore, in Isaiah 22 the keys do not
signify supreme or ecclesiastical power, because the passage speaks of Shebna
the scribe, whom Eliakim succeeded in the house of David, politically, in a
political office. Bellarmine was deceived because Eliakim is called the son
of Hilkiah: he falsely thinks this was Hilkiah the priest mentioned in 2
Kings 22:4, because the latter lived in the time of Josiah, namely fifty years
after Hezekiah’s death. 3. We concede that supreme authority was granted to
Peter equally with the other apostles, but not to Peter alone above the
others. Finally, Isaiah 9 does not speak of the keys, and both in that passage
and Revelation 3 the subject is Christ’s kingdom as it is administered by
Christ in his own person.

Instance 7. *And whatever you bind.* In Scripture the person who commands and
punishes is said to bind. But Peter is given the power of binding—that is, of
punishing, commanding, and dispensing—and of loosing—that is, of remitting
sins and dispensing concerning the law, vows, oaths, and so forth. Therefore
he is made judge and prince of all who are in the Church.

Response. The inference is denied, because there is more in the conclusion
than in the premises. 2. The verb *to bind* does not signify supreme right
and power, because it is attributed to attendants and lictors, Matthew 22:13.
3. It is false that the verb *to bind* is used for punishing as a prince does,
because in Matthew 18 it is used for excommunication by an ecclesiastical
senate. Finally, only ministerial power was given to Peter as well as the
others, and to the others as well as Peter.

Objection 3, from John 21:15–17: *Feed my sheep.* The man whom Christ ordered
to feed his sheep he constituted head of the Church, because to feed is to
preside. But in the cited passage he ordered Peter alone to feed the sheep.
Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because to feed is not to preside over
the flock with dominion, 1 Peter

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 59 | printed col. 117 | bsb10175568]

5:3, much less over everyone, sheep as well as shepherds.

2. The person who feeds another’s sheep is a servant, not a lord. But Peter
is ordered to feed another’s sheep.

3. Peter alone did not have this charge; all the apostles had it equally with
him, Matthew 18:19; John 20:21–23, and according to Augustine, tractate 123 on
John and sermon 62 *On the Words of the Lord*, and Chrysostom, *On the
Priesthood*, book 2, section 4. Peter never fed all the sheep, or even the
greater part, because the gospel of the Gentiles was entrusted to Paul,
Galatians 2:1, 5. He did not feed the apostles: not by teaching or sending,
because they had been taught and sent directly by the Holy Spirit; not by
correcting, because we read only that Peter was corrected by others; and not
by commanding, because he was sent by others, but by himself sent no one.
Finally, Christ questions Peter three times and three times orders him to feed
the sheep, so that a threefold confession and restoration to the office from
which he had fallen by his threefold denial would answer the threefold
denial, as Augustine, tractate 133 on John, section 2, and Cyril, book 12 on
John, sections 5, 6, and 4, say. It is therefore a fallacy of what is not a
cause.

Instance 1, from the wording. Peter alone was Simon Bar-Jonah; he alone was
asked, “Do you love me?”; he alone is grieved; his crucifixion is foretold to
him alone; and to him alone it is answered, “What is that to you?” Therefore
“Feed the sheep” was said to Peter alone.

Response. It does not conclude the point in question. The question is whether
Christ constituted him head of the Church because he said “Feed” to him
alone. We deny the inference, because there are other reasons why he said
“Feed,” namely, the repair of the denial and restoration to apostolic
dignity.

Instance 2. Christ asks Peter, “Do you love me more than these?” and adds,
“Feed the sheep.” Therefore he handed over the care of the sheep because of
love; and because

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 59 | printed col. 118 | bsb10175568]

love was greater in Peter, a greater charge was entrusted to him.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because the charge is not said to have
been entrusted to him because of love, nor was Peter’s love greater than that
of the others. Christ questions him only to recall to his memory that
remarkable confidence with which he had professed that he would never abandon
Christ even if all the others abandoned him; indeed, Peter himself answers
only that he loves him.

2. The inference “he loved more, therefore he received more authority” is
denied. It no more follows than if someone concluded: Christ loved John more
than the others, therefore he gave him more authority; or John loved Christ
more than Judas did, therefore he was Judas’s head and prince. Finally, the
Fathers testify that these words were spoken to all in Peter’s person, as
Augustine, *On the Christian Struggle*, chapter 30, and Chrysostom, *On the
Priesthood*, teach. Bellarmine is therefore compelled to admit that in their
own manner these words apply to all bishops.

Instance 3, from the word “feed.” “Feed” shows that supreme power is
attributed to the person to whom “Feed the sheep” is said. But it is said to
Peter. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because in the person who feeds
another’s sheep it signifies a subordinate ministry. Furthermore, at the
beginning those properly called pastors were only in the fourth rank of
church officers, Ephesians 4:11. Therefore to feed does not signify supreme
authority.

Instance 4. The person who supplies another with food in any manner is not
properly said to feed him, but the person who attends to and provides food
for another, which belongs to a superintendent and governor, Luke 12.
Therefore it belongs to the person placed over a household to feed it.

Response 1. The antecedent is denied, because the servant who distributes
food feeds just as does the lord who provides it.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 60 | printed col. 119 | bsb10175568]

2. Not everyone who provides another with food rules over him; nor does this
passage teach that he does. Indeed, on the contrary, it distinguishes the
lord from the steward. The steward is called superintendent of the household,
not because he commands but because he distributes food.

Instance 5. By the word “feed,” according to the common usage of speakers,
every pastoral act is understood. But a pastor’s act is not only to teach but
to lead, bring back, protect, preside, govern, and chastise. Hence in
Scripture “to feed” is used for “to shatter,” Psalm 2:9; and in Isaiah 44
Cyrus the king is called a shepherd. Therefore.

Response. Bellarmine falsely says that the word in Psalm 2 signifies “you
shall feed,” because תְּרֹעֵם is not from the root רעה but, according to Arias
Montanus and Pagninus, from רוע, meaning to be evil or to break, as Montanus
also translates it. Even if it be granted that this verb signifies every
pastoral act, when it is attributed to kings and bishops it is used
metaphorically, and every metaphor must be determined according to the
subject under discussion. Thus a king feeds in one manner and a bishop his
flock in another; otherwise Cyrus, who is called the people’s shepherd,
taught the people. It therefore belongs to bishops by reason of episcopal
office; but episcopal office does not admit the dominion of one, as was
proved above. Finally, it would follow that all bishops govern with supreme
power because all feed.

Instance 6. In Matthew 2:6 the verb is ποιμαίνειν, and in Micah 5:2, from
which he cites the passage, it is not רעה but משל, “to rule.” Therefore.

Response. The cited passage speaks of Christ’s kingdom in his own person.
Furthermore, it does not follow: Peter feeds and governs the Church;
therefore he does so as head and monarch not only of the sheep but especially
of the shepherds. Moreover, this proves nothing for the pope, because he does
not feed. Bellarmine admits near the end of section 5 that for a long time the
popes have preached only through others. But

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 60 | printed col. 120 | bsb10175568]

the one who does not feed in person does not possess dominion in person,
because to feed is to rule, as Bellarmine wishes; yet the pope does not feed
in person, because the principal part of a pastor’s work is preaching.

Instance 7. The sheep signify all Christians, even the apostles themselves,
as entrusted to Peter. This is proved because twice he said, “Feed the
lambs,” and once, “Feed the sheep,” although the Greek text is corrupt, for
πρόβατα should be read προβάτια, as Ambrose notes on the last chapter of
Luke. He therefore entrusted lambs, little sheep, and sheep. Or, if we say
that “lambs” is repeated twice, it signifies the two peoples, Jewish and
Gentile, while the sheep are the bishops who are the mothers of the lambs.
But if by little sheep we understand sheep that are small but larger than
lambs, we say that to Peter were entrusted lambs, that is, beginners; little
sheep, that is, those making progress; and sheep, that is, the perfect. Or
finally, by lambs we understand the peoples who are children; by little sheep,
priests and parish ministers; and by sheep, the greater bishops who are
subject to Peter.

Response. Bellarmine falsely asserts that the Greek text is corrupt, for in
all copies ἀρνία occurs once and προβάτια twice. Furthermore, Scripture uses
these words interchangeably in Matthew 19:16; Luke 10:3; Acts 8:32.

3. This explanation is allegorical, and therefore no article of faith can be
concluded from it.

4. The distinction between sheep and lambs is ridiculous.

5. The word *sheep* is never used for bishops as such. 6. He does Peter an
injury, because if the name *sheep* signifies those who are subject to Christ
the shepherd, as in John 10, and Peter is not a sheep, he will not be in the
fold; for just as there is one shepherd, so there is one fold.

Instance 8. Without any restriction the pronoun is added to the noun “sheep,”

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 61 | printed col. 121 | bsb10175568]

“my”; therefore all the sheep to which the pronoun “my” extends are entrusted to
Peter. But “my” extends absolutely to all, because everyone in the Church is
one of Christ’s sheep. Therefore all Christians without exception were
entrusted to Peter.

Response. The inference is denied, because he speaks indefinitely, but not
every indefinite proposition is universal, Acts 2:17; 4:26; Luke 1:52. Nor
were all the sheep entrusted to Peter, Galatians 2, but also to the other
apostles, Matthew 28. 2. Sheep are spoken of in two ways. First, in relation
to Christ, and thus Peter was a sheep, John 10:11. Then in relation to other
pastors, and thus all bishops are pastors and the people alone are sheep,
although in a certain respect the prophets, evangelists, and pastors can also
be called sheep in relation to the apostles.

Instance 9. “Feed my sheep” means nothing other than “Have charge of my
fold.” But there is only one fold. Therefore he entrusted the entire flock to
Peter.

Response. The inference is denied, because there is more in the conclusion
than in the premises, if he speaks of Peter alone, since Christ entrusted all
the sheep to all the apostles.

2. “Fold” is taken differently in the proposition, namely, for those who are
fed by Christ, in which sense Peter was a sheep. There are therefore four
terms.

Instance 10. Either he entrusted all his sheep to Peter, or none, or some that
were certain and defined, or some that were indefinite. But no one says none,
or some certain ones; nor does anyone say indefinite ones, because it does not
belong to a wise provider to leave the charge indefinite when he can define
it, since confusion can arise from this.

Response 1. He entrusted all the sheep to Peter, but not to him alone, because

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 61 | printed col. 122 | bsb10175568]

he entrusted all to all, Matthew 28:19. The care of all the sheep was entrusted to him with the others;
therefore he was not head and monarch of all, including the apostles.

2. It is not absurd for him to have entrusted the sheep to Peter indefinitely,
because it was said to James together with the others, “Teach all nations,”
yet he did not leave Judea. Christ indefinitely entrusted all to each; it was
not required that each teach every individual, but any persons whatever. Nor
does it follow that because he entrusted them indefinitely confusion could
arise, since the apostles were going to receive the Holy Spirit. Nor does it
follow: no one feeds a flock he does not know, therefore Christ defined it;
for they came to know the flocks definitely only when they fed them.

Objection 4, from the prerogatives. The man who possessed prerogatives above
the other apostles was head and monarch of the rest. But Peter was such a
man. Therefore.

Response. If by *prerogatives* he means things which happen to only one
person, the proposition is denied, because in this sense each apostle had his
own prerogatives, and therefore they were heads of the Church. But if
*prerogative* is taken for a privilege by which someone is preferred to
others, the proposition is also denied, because many are preferred to others
who are not their heads and monarchs.

3. The assumption is denied, because Peter alone did not possess his own
prerogatives: the other apostles possessed theirs, yet were not heads of
Peter and the others.

4. Prerogatives belong either to the person or to the office. If to the
person, they prefer only Peter’s person to the others, and thus the middle
term is irrelevant, because the office is under discussion. If to the office,
then it must first be proved

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 62 | printed col. 123 | bsb10175568]

that Peter possessed that office. Furthermore, if the prerogatives belong to
the office, they must be found in everyone who holds the office. Yet the
privileges he enumerates are found in no one besides Peter.

Objection. The first prerogative was the change of name, which occurs for
weighty reasons to signify great privileges, as can be seen in Abraham,
Genesis 17. But among the apostles Peter’s name alone was changed, John 1:43.
Therefore.

Response. This does not conclude what is in question, for it does not follow:
a change of name signifies privileges; therefore it signifies ecclesiastical
monarchy.

2. The assumption is denied, because in Mark 3 the names of John and James
were changed, for they were called Boanerges; Saul’s name was likewise
changed.

Instance. In Syriac *Cephas* signifies a rock, and in Greek, a head, and
Christ is often called a rock. Therefore, when Christ communicated his own
name to Peter, this signifies that he made him the head and foundation of the
Church.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because although *Cephas* and κεφαλή have
a certain similarity, they no more mean the same thing than μαθητής and μάταιος,
that is, foolish.

2. A word’s etymology must be sought only in the language from which it was
taken.

Finally, it is false that *Rock* is a name of Christ; it is a property of
Christ, as are shepherd, vine, and so forth. Nor does it follow: Peter received
the name Peter from Christ, therefore he was made the same thing as Christ,
any more than it follows: Christians have their name from Christ, therefore
they were made what Christ is.

Objection 2, the prerogative: When the evangelists or apostles name either
all or some of the apostles,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 62 | printed col. 124 | bsb10175568]

Peter is always placed first, as in Matthew 11:12 and elsewhere. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied: therefore he was monarch of the Church.

2. The antecedent is denied, because in various passages he is not named
first, Mark 16:7; John 1:45; 1 Corinthians 3:5 and 9:5; Galatians 2. The
apostle spoke in this way, although Bellarmine argues that the text is corrupt
against every Greek and Latin copy. 3. Peter is called first either in age,
gifts, order, or even dignity, although not in office.

Objection 3, the prerogative. Peter alone walks on the waters with Christ,
Matthew 14:29, and something similar occurs in John 21:7. But the sea
signifies the world, Revelation 17, and the ship the Church. Therefore his
walking on the waters signifies the power by which he was to preside over
each church.

Response. The inference is denied, because even if this allegory were granted
as sound, it would not follow that he was monarch of the Church, because he
did not walk over all waters but over part of the Sea of Tiberias.

2. An article of faith is not to be concluded from allegories but illustrated
by them.

3. Chrysostom, homily 51 on Matthew, concludes that Peter’s deed displayed
ardor and impatience; indeed, in the cited passage Peter’s unbelief and fear
are reproved. Finally, it does not follow: the waters in Revelation 17
signify nations, therefore wherever waters are mentioned nations are meant.

Objection from Matthew 16:16, prerogative 4. Peter, first of all and taught by
God, knew the highest mysteries of the faith, the distinctions of persons in
God, and the incarnation. Although Christ had previously been called the Son
of God by others, he was called the Son of God by them in the way all the
saints are, Matthew

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 63 | printed col. 125 | bsb10175568]

14; John 11:1; and elsewhere. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because Andrew was called to the
apostolate before Peter and therefore knew the mysteries of faith before
Peter.

2. In the cited passages others made the same confession before this
confession of Peter. The Jews who were in the boat also confessed Christ to
be the true Son of God, Matthew 14:33, as did the two demoniacs, Matthew 8:29.
Finally, the inference is denied: he was the first to issue the confession
after being taught; therefore he was constituted monarch of the Church.

Objection from Matthew 16:18, prerogative 5. Stability is promised to the
rock upon which the Church is founded. Therefore it was promised to Peter
that his see would never fall. If the other apostles had possessed this
promise, the see of James at Jerusalem, of John at Ephesus, of Matthew in
Ethiopia, and of Andrew in Scythia would still stand. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because Peter is not that rock, as was
proved above. Furthermore, stability is not promised to Peter but to the
Church built upon Christ the rock. Finally, it is false that the apostles
possessed episcopal sees simply because they remained for some time in
certain places. For then it would follow that Paul was bishop of Rome because
he preached at Rome for two years, and likewise bishop of Ephesus because he
preached at Ephesus for a whole year.

Objection from Matthew 17, prerogative 6. Christ orders the tribute to be paid
for himself and Peter. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because payment of tribute is a sign of
subjection, and Peter paid at Capernaum as head of the household. Furthermore,
Bellarmine’s explanation is allegorical, and therefore by itself does not
prove an article of faith.

Objection from Luke 5:8 and John 21:6, prerogative 7. Christ performed two
miracles in Peter’s fishing. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied.

Instance from Luke 5. In the first fishing the nets are not cast only on the
right, lest we believe that only the good or only the evil come into the
Church, but the nets are let down without distinction. In the second,
however, the nets are cast only on the right, because only the good will be
gathered to eternal life. Therefore.

Response. It concludes nothing; furthermore, the explanation is allegorical.

3. Peter was governor of only one little boat.

Instance. In the first fishing the nets were breaking and the boat was almost
sinking, which signifies schisms, heresies, and scandals that cause the
Church to be tossed about. In the second the net is not broken. In the first
the fish are comprehended without number; in the second there was a fixed
number. In the first the fish are brought into a tossing boat; in the second
they are drawn to shore—that is, to life. But in each fishing, which signifies
the state of the Church, Peter is found as prince. Therefore.

Response. The allegory is still being continued. Furthermore, not everyone
who presides over one little boat and one or another fishing is prince of all
boats and fishing expeditions. Finally, if allegories were to be fabricated
from all the circumstances, conclusions contrary to all these could be drawn.

Objection from Luke 22:31, prerogative 8. *When you have turned, strengthen
your brothers.* Therefore this shows that Peter would be prince and head of
his brothers.

Response. The inference is denied, because Christ restrains Peter in his
pride and reminds him of his fall

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 64 | printed col. 127 | bsb10175568]

and office.

2. Strengthening the brothers was the office of all the apostles, but Christ
prayed for Peter in particular because of the denial that would follow.

Objection, prerogative 9. When Christ rose, he showed himself to Peter first
of all the apostles, Luke 24:34. Therefore.

Response. The inference, and then the antecedent, are denied, because he first
appeared to Mary Magdalene. If therefore the first vision of Christ after the
resurrection makes someone head of the Church, Mary was head of the Church.

Objection from John 13:5, prerogative 10. Christ first washed Peter’s feet.
Therefore.

Response. The inference “therefore head of the Church” is denied. The
antecedent is also denied, for verse 6 of the same passage shows that Christ
washed either several or all the disciples’ feet before Peter’s. Chrysostom,
Theophylact, and others on this passage think that Christ first washed Judas’s
feet.

Objection from John 21:18, prerogative 11. To Peter alone Christ foretold
death on the cross, so that just as he had given him his name and office, he
would also have him as his companion in death; and he adds, “Follow me” in
pastoral office and death. Therefore.

Response. The inference “therefore head of the Church” is denied.

2. The prediction of death was not a privilege, because the same happened to
others, Matthew 24:9; Acts 21:1. Finally, the exposition is false, and the
passage does not teach that Peter was crucified, because that stretching out
of the hands was so that he would be bound with straps, not fastened to a
cross with nails.

Objection from Acts 1, prerogative 12. Peter, as head of the household,
gathers the assembly of disciples into one gathering and teaches that one
person must be chosen in Judas’s place. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied. 2. Luke does not say that Peter convened
that assembly; nor does it follow that because he first advises the choice of
one man, he was head of the rest.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 64 | printed col. 128 | bsb10175568]

Objection from Acts 2, prerogative 13. After receiving the Holy Spirit Peter
is the first to proclaim the Gospel and converts three thousand people.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because the others could have yielded
Peter this honor either because of age or eloquence, but not because he was
universal monarch.

2. The antecedent is denied, because Acts 2 says that each and all of the
apostles spoke the magnificent works of God before Peter’s sermon was
delivered. Finally, it does not follow that because Peter was then, and often
elsewhere, the mouth of the apostles, he was their head. Likewise, Aaron was
the mouth of Moses; therefore he was superior in authority.

Objection from Acts 3, prerogative 14. The first miracle in testimony to the
faith was performed by Peter. Therefore.

Response. The inference and then the antecedent are denied, because this was
not the first miracle: all the apostles are said to have performed various
miracles, Acts 2:43. Nor does it follow: he healed the lame man’s feet,
therefore he was the Church’s foundation, because Paul performed the same
thing, Acts 14:10, yet was not head of the Church.

Objection from Acts 5, prerogative 15. Peter as a divine and supreme judge
recognized and condemned the hypocrisy and deceit of Ananias and Sapphira
and killed them by his word. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied. Furthermore, if the antecedent speaks of
Peter as the sole supreme judge, it is false, because he had this in common
with the other apostles. Thus Paul alone blinded Elymas the magician, Acts
13.

Objection from Acts 12, prerogative 16. Peter passed through all the churches.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because planting and visiting churches was
an office common to all the apostles and is attributed to Paul, Acts 18:23
and elsewhere.

Objection from Acts 10, prerogative 17. Peter was

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 65 | printed col. 129 | bsb10175568]

the first of all to begin preaching to the Gentiles, and to him alone was shown the
vision by which he was warned that it was time to preach to the Gentiles,
where it is said, “Kill and eat.” For it belongs to the head to eat, by which
it is signified that it belonged to Peter, as head of the Church, to convert
unbelievers. Therefore.

Response. Peter’s preaching took place privately in one household, and that
in Judea, but Paul and Barnabas were chosen for the public ministry of the
Gentiles, Acts 13 and Galatians 2. Therefore in this Paul’s prerogative was
greater.

2. In Acts 8 Philip converts the eunuch, and in Acts 9:29 Paul is said to have
disputed with the Greeks.

3. He falsely says that Cornelius was not a proselyte—see Acts 10:2—and also
falsely says that in Acts 15 Peter asserts that he was the first to preach to
the Gentiles.

Finally, the allegory is ridiculous and foreign to the purpose of the vision,
which Peter himself explains: namely, that thereafter the Gentiles must not
be distinguished from the Jews. Thus “Kill” is said to Peter to remind him of
his office, not to constitute him monarch of the Church.

Objection, prerogative 18. Unceasing prayer was made for Peter in prison,
from which he was miraculously delivered; but we do not read that unceasing
prayer was likewise made for Stephen and James. The reason was that it made a
great difference whether one member or the head was in danger. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because then it would follow that those
for whom the Church prays earnestly are monarchs of the Church. Furthermore,
this puts what is not a cause in place of a cause: it is not said that
unceasing prayer was made for Stephen and James because no such span of time
for prayer was given; many things were also done by the apostles which Luke
passes over in silence.

Finally, unceasing prayer was made for Peter because it was a duty

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 65 | printed col. 130 | bsb10175568]

owed, not because he was monarch.

Objection from Acts 15, prerogative 19. Peter speaks first in the council and
the others follow his judgment. Therefore.

Response. The inference “therefore head of the Church” is denied.

2. The antecedent is denied, because many spoke before him, Acts 15:7.

3. It does not follow: the others followed his judgment, therefore he was
head and president, because the president does not give his judgment first.

4. James gives the judgment in the name of the synod and adds to Peter’s
judgment a direct answer to the question, verse 19.

Objection, prerogative 20. In Galatians 1:18 Paul went up to see Peter as one
greater than himself, and in Galatians 2 to confer with Peter lest he run in
vain; thus he acknowledged Peter as greater than himself.

Response. The inference is denied, because the person who visits another and
confers with him is not therefore his head. Paul went up to Peter to visit him
in a humane and Christian manner.

2. He did not confer with Peter alone, but with James and John.

3. When Paul lists those with whom he conferred, he does not put Peter first.

4. In the same place he says he received nothing from them.

5. Far from acknowledging Peter as Lord, he rebuked him and resisted him to
his face.

6. It does not follow: he went up to confer lest he run in vain; therefore he
did so because Peter was head of the Church. This puts what is not a cause in
place of a cause, because he did this on account of some who doubted the
agreement of doctrine among the apostles. In Galatians 1, however, he teaches
that he was not inferior to Peter, Galatians 2:6.

Objection, prerogative 21. Christ baptized only Peter among the men with his
own hands, just as he baptized only Mary among the women. Therefore.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 66 | printed col. 131 | bsb10175568]

Response 1. The inference is denied.

2. The antecedent is denied, because John 4:2 says that Christ himself did
not baptize.

3. Peter was baptized either by John, just as Christ himself was, or by
Andrew, or by some other who had been John’s disciple and afterward Christ’s;
through them Christ is said to have baptized in the cited passage.

Objection, prerogative 22. Peter alone was ordained a bishop by Christ, while
the others were ordained by Peter. Therefore.

Response 1. The inference is denied.

2. The antecedent is denied, because we read that he was constituted an
apostle with the others, but nowhere a bishop in the sense the Papists intend.

3. If the name *bishop* is taken broadly, Christ constituted all the apostles
bishops, according to Augustine, question 97, *in nominum test.*

Objection, prerogative 23. Peter detected, condemned, and utterly extinguished
Simon Magus, the first arch-heretic, Acts 8; for it was fitting that the
father of the Church should overcome the father of heretics. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because it was the office of all the
apostles, and is the office of all faithful pastors, to detect and condemn
heretics.

2. If Peter is no more the head of the Church than that Simon was head of the
heretics, then he truly will not be head, because no one ever instituted or
acknowledged Simon as head.

3. That Simon was not the first heretic, because the scribes and Pharisees
were heretics detected and condemned by Christ himself.

4. It does not follow: he condemned the prince of the heretics of his time,
therefore he was monarch of the Church, any more than it follows: Alexander,
bishop of Alexandria, condemned Arius, and Cyril condemned Nestorius;
therefore they were heads of the Church.

Objection, prerogative 24. Peter placed his see at Rome

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 66 | printed col. 132 | bsb10175568]

by divine command, while the others were sent into the world; he was therefore
head of the world.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because he was sent into the world with
the others, but no particular mission to Rome exists. Indeed, he was never at
Rome, as will be proved afterward.

2. It does not follow: he was bishop in the principal city of the world;
therefore he was head of the Church, because the consequence from secular
power to ecclesiastical power is invalid.

Objection, prerogative 25. Near the end of Peter’s life, Christ appeared to
Peter and, when asked, “Where are you going?” answered, “I am coming to Rome
to be crucified again,” which was spoken about Peter’s death, not Christ’s.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because Christ appeared to Paul and said
that persecutions of his people unto death were his own, and yet they were
not heads of the Church.

2. What is recounted of that apparition is a fable, for Scripture testifies
that from the resurrection until the judgment Christ will remain in heaven,
Acts 3.

Finally, it does not follow: Christ says that what they were going to do to
Peter was being done to himself, therefore Peter is head of the Church,
because as much is said of all Christ’s servants, Matthew 15:4.

Objection, prerogative 26. Only those churches in which Peter sat and which
he founded have been regarded as patriarchal: the Roman, Alexandrian, and
Antiochene churches by the Council of Nicaea, to which the Jerusalem and
Constantinopolitan churches were afterward added. But the sole cause is the
dignity of Peter, who sat at Rome and Antioch in person and at Alexandria
through his disciple Mark. Therefore.

Response 1. Not even the name *patriarch* is found before Constantine’s age,
while the epistles of Clement and Anacletus are spurious.

2. In Constantine’s age, according to Socrates, *History*, book 5, there were
ten

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 67 | printed col. 133 | bsb10175568]

patriarchates, and therefore the proposition is false.

3. Peter did not sit at Rome.

4. The assumption is denied, because the author of the third epistle
attributed to Anacletus gives another reason for the patriarchate: *The order
of the episcopate is one, although there are those primates who hold the
principal cities, who in certain places are called patriarchs by some.*

Objection, prerogative 27. In the Church we celebrate the feast of Peter’s
chair in honor of the institution of his episcopate, but nothing of the kind
is read concerning the others. Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied.

Furthermore, the true Church does not institute the feast.

3. It is a recent institution.

Objection, prerogative 28. In letters of communion, after the name of the
Father, Son, and Holy Spirit the ancients added the name of Peter. Thus at the
end of the Council of Chalcedon Bishop Atticus has the letters π, ὑ, α, and
ω. Therefore.

Response. This was recently invented.

2. That epistle is spurious, because it is mentioned nowhere.

3. The final ω signifies τῶν θυρῶν, not Peter.

Finally, in other letters of communion there are several such letters;
therefore they signify head of the Church—this does not follow.

Objection. The Fathers often call Peter head and attribute primacy to him.
But these things signify supreme power. Therefore.

Response. We concede that Peter as an apostle possessed supreme power, but
only in common with the other apostles. 2. The term *head* is taken in various
ways, for it signifies:

1. One who excels in gifts, as in 1 Corinthians 12:21.

2. Prelates and doctors. Thus Gregory, book 4 on 1 Kings, last chapter, says
that Paul was made head of the nations.

3. It is taken for dignity, and thus in Chrysostom, homily 3 *To the People
of Antioch*, Antioch is called head of the entire

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 67 | printed col. 134 | bsb10175568]

world. But Peter was not universal monarch of the Church because of
excellence of gifts or because he was a teacher, since he had these things in
common with the others; nor because of dignity any more than Antioch was
monarch of the entire world because it is called head.

Finally, not everyone to whom the name of head and primacy is attributed is
monarch of the entire world.
