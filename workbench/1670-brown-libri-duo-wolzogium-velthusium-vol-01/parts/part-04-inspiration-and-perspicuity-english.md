IV. When Wolzogen girds himself to refute the paradox, on page 7 and following,
he first lays the foundations of his position. He says that certain things
about the Scriptures must be known beforehand; then, what an interpreter is;
and finally, to whom this province of interpreting the Scriptures ought
especially to be entrusted. I shall say nothing about this division of the
work. Let us follow the road by which he leads us. On page 8 he proposes that
three things about the Scriptures must be considered: namely, their author;
the discourse in which they were written; and finally, the nature of the
things subjected to that discourse. He first says certain things about their
author.

*First of all*, he says, *those who make the Scriptures the principle of the
Christian Religion make God the author of the Scriptures without any dispute.
And although the names of the Prophets and Apostles, who were certainly men,
are read as headings to the books, it is nevertheless confessed among all
Christians that they were God's scribes. Not only were their minds divinely
illumined so that they possessed what they should write; their very hands were
also directed by a singular providence, so that they did not hesitate over how
they should write. Therefore there can be no doubt that all the literary
monuments of holy men must be attributed to God as their author.* Reply.
Perhaps you ask, reader, whether anything here deserves blame? Certainly
nothing. Nevertheless, in this very matter it appears more clearly than the
light of midday that Wolzogen is a betrayer of the orthodox cause. For the
sound and indubitable foundations which he lays here he either states so
universally, confusedly, and without limitation, and explains so perplexedly,
that an adversary can easily reject them; or he himself afterward shakes or
overturns them, whether openly, through certain other assertions, or finally
by defending his principal position. Here, indeed, he asserts two things about
the Prophets and Apostles, God's scribes, in order to prove that their literary
monuments must be attributed to God as their author. These things he afterward
either shakes or utterly over-*

[Page 17 | ONB canvas 71 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000071]

*turns.

The first is this: he says that *the minds of the scribes were so divinely
illumined that they possessed what they should write.* But he himself afterward
shakes this on page 81 when he says that *the scribe, who perhaps sometimes did
not know what he was writing, must cautiously be distinguished from the
author.* I ask, did those who sometimes did not know what they were writing
possess—or could they possess—minds divinely illumined so that they possessed
what they should write? So long as they did not know what they were writing,
they certainly did not possess divinely illumined minds, for once divine
illumination is posited, it is impossible that they should not know what they
were writing. We therefore see how he shakes this foundation. But he overturns
it more clearly and openly on pages 125–126, saying *that the sacred
amanuenses themselves, even if they were still alive, could not be regarded as
sure Interpreters of all their writings, since they were often*—note
well—*ignorant of what they were writing and poured forth oracles, sometimes
with an alienated mind.* Here he does not speak doubtfully, but clearly and
positively asserts that they were often ignorant of what they were writing,
and indeed sometimes wrote and poured forth oracles with an alienated mind. It
is not my business to summon these statements for examination here. Later, in
their proper place, it will be shown upon what weak supports they rest. I judge
that I have discharged my office here by showing that Wolzogen elsewhere
weakens or overturns the solid foundation laid here. Once this is done, it is
clear and placed beyond the hazard of controversy that the intention of this
work of Wolzogen's was to betray the truth. (For I speak here throughout only
of this intention, as it will suffice to have warned once; I leave the
intention of the agent to be judged by his own proper Judge.)

The second is this. He asserts that *the very hands of the scribes were
directed by a singular providence, so that they did not hesitate over how they
should write.* Who would not believe that it could readily be inferred from
this that these di-*

[Page 18 | ONB canvas 72 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000072]

*vine writings must be explained in a far different manner from other human
writings, and that this writing must possess a peculiar majesty of style and
other marks or divine tokens? Nevertheless, our Wolzogen here, at least by not
explaining himself cautiously
enough, appears to weaken even these things when he afterward speaks as follows
on page 26: *It is of by far the greatest necessity that we remember—for no
proof is needed in a clear matter—that the Scriptures were written in an
altogether human manner. And although this discourse may possess a certain
peculiar character of gravity, so that even Theologians believe its divinity
can be known from the majesty of its style, nothing prevents the sacred codex
from having been written, in all other respects, from the usage of its own
language just as all other books were. From this it follows that one must
proceed no differently in explaining it than in interpreting other books.* He
has similar words on page 97: *The form and kind of sacred discourse is the
same as that of profane discourse.*

I certainly do not deny that God used signs of words, phrases, constructions,
and the like proper to the language into which he revealed his mind.
Nevertheless, I judge it insufficiently circumspect to say that *the Scriptures
were written in an altogether human manner*, and that *one must proceed no
differently in explaining them than in interpreting other books written in the
same language*, since those sacred Amanuenses had the peculiar aid of the Holy
Spirit in selecting the very words and phrases and applying them to this or
that subject, or using them here and now. If he had intended to defend the
position of the orthodox firmly and consistently, he could beyond all doubt
have explained his mind far better and more safely, so as to furnish no one
even the slightest occasion to suspect him of favoring the foregoing things.
But deceitfully he deals in generalities. He indeed says that the meaning of
his words is sound, but, by his own testimony, no one is the best Interpreter
of his own wo-*

*rds. An Adversary can easily interpret these general statements
far differently while preserving the force of the words and observing ordinary
linguistic usage, and infer from them no few absurdities. But let us proceed.

V. Wolzogen continues on page 9 and indeed lays another solid foundation. For
he speaks as follows: *Since God must be believed neither to do anything in
vain, nor to be able to say a falsehood, nor, finally, to lie—because it is
repugnant to his most perfect nature that a useless thing proceed from supreme
Wisdom, a falsehood from truth, or deception from goodness.* But he not only
shakes this most solid foundation; he endeavors to overturn it as though by
deliberate effort when, on page 24, he labors to prove that *God can deceive if
he wills.* For what else is it for *God to be able to deceive* than for *God to
be able to mislead*? And if deception can proceed from God's goodness without
any repugnance to his most perfect nature, why not misleading too? In whatever
way he finally attempts to varnish the expression *to deceive*, the expression
*to mislead* can plainly be varnished in the same way. Therefore, wherever he
turns, he must necessarily shake this foundation—something no one who had not
resolved to desert God's cause would do or attempt.

If he says that by the words *God can deceive if he wills* he understands
nothing other than *that power and wisdom and the other infinite divine
faculties, which are certainly ample enough to give effect to a will to deceive,
if such a will could exist*, as he speaks in *Censure of the Censure*, page 38,
why can the words *God can mislead* not be explained in the same way? And where
then will this foundation be? You yourself say, Wolzogen, that *for deception
to proceed from goodness is repugnant to God's most perfect nature.* Why, I
ask, will it not also be repugnant to that same nature to say that *deception
can proceed from goodness*, or that *goodness can mislead*, which is the same
thing? For just as no imperfection is actually to be ascribed to God's most
perfect nature, neither can any imperfection be ascribed to it by any right.
Who, then, will free this man from the charge of treacherous betrayal?

[Page 20 | ONB canvas 74 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000074]

Who, I ask, among all the Reformed ever said that God can deceive or mislead
because he possesses wisdom, power, and other infinite attributes? Let
him show, if he can, even one man who speaks this way. But more about this later
in its proper place. It is enough now that I have shown how he himself overturns
the foundation laid here.

VI. From the foregoing he concludes at the same place *that the Scriptures
can and ought to be understood; that pure truth is contained in them,
and that this truth is so presented to one who reads it as he ought that no one
is led into error.* After a few words about human Writers, he adds more similar
things on page 10 and following. In order that it may be clear to everyone how
Wolzogen conducts himself here and explains his position, it must be known
that our theologians, when treating this subject, distinguish the clarity and
perspicuity of Scripture with respect to the object and with respect to the
subject; or, what comes to the same thing, the perspicuity of Scripture is
considered either in itself, as they say, or in relation to us. Thus it is
either objective or subjective. Or, as Luther likes to distinguish it in
Whitaker, controversy 1, question 4, chapter 1, the clarity of Scripture is
either external or internal: the latter is placed in the heart itself, the
former in the words themselves.

But Wolzogen not only makes no mention of this distinction; when he says with
the Paradoxical Essayist on page 102 that *there is no perspicuity called
absolute, but all perspicuity is beyond doubt relative*, he appears clearly
enough to reject this distinction. Thus everything he says here about
perspicuity, or, so to speak, intelligibility, must be understood relatively
and subjectively, or of the perspicuity of the Scriptures in relation to us.
But if his words have this meaning, he speaks neither truth nor according to
the orthodox, nor is he consistent with himself or with truth. But if he speaks
of objective clarity and perspicuity, or of the clarity of Scripture in itself,
the things he says can be admitted, together with what he says on page 10,
namely: *From the first point,*

[Page 21 | ONB canvas 75 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000075]

*that the Scriptures do not mock us, this follows:
what must be laid as a foundation in this whole question is that the Scriptures
are so constituted that they can be understood. For it is vain to ask about an Interpreter
of the Scriptures—who that person may be who presents their meaning—if they
are of such a nature that, whatever effort we finally employ, we are unable to
draw any meaning from them. But from the fact that God does all things by
design, we conclude by an indubitable consequence that the Scriptures ought to
be capable of being understood by men, and so forth.* If, I say, these and
similar things which he has here were said about that objective perspicuity
which our theologians defend against the papists, they must be acknowledged as
true.

Nevertheless, this must be observed: Wolzogen himself also overturns, or at
least for the most part weakens, this very point which he had now indicated to
us must be laid as a foundation in this whole question. For on page 15 he has
these words: *And I would believe every doubt to have been removed here, were
it not that the Scriptures in many places appear so written that the difficulty
is believed to inhere in them, as though they could not be understood. For no
few passages are set before us which, whatever effort is employed upon them,
take up nothing for the Interpreter but despair.* But lest perhaps he say that
he speaks here of subjective obscurity, consider what he has on page 16: *We
nevertheless admit that there are certain things in the Scriptures which we
are unable to attain, not only because of the defect of our minds, discussed
above, or because of the sublimity of the subjects, which we will consider
later, but because Scripture itself, with which we are concerned here, is
endowed with a certain singular constitution.* This, in brief, means not only
through the fault of our minds, but through the fault of Scripture itself.
What could be said more clearly to shake the foregoing? Indeed, on page 16 of
*Censure of the Censure* he confirms this with the same argument the papists
use, namely Peter's statement in 2 Peter 3:16, *en hois dysnoēta tina*. He
renders these words, *there are certain things difficult to understand in
Paul's Epistles*, contrary to the text itself and the Dutch translation. Let
Pola-*

[Page 22 | ONB canvas 76 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000076]

*nus be heard: *Next, the assumption is denied. We concede, when it is rightly
understood, that there are in sacred Scripture certain things difficult to
understand—namely, if it is understood of the subjects which sacred Scripture
treats, in which sense Peter speaks of some things in Paul's Epistles. For
Peter does not say* en hais, *that is, “in which Epistles,” but* en hois,
*that is, “in which subjects”*—pragmasi—*there are certain things difficult to
understand. For* en hois *does not refer to the more remote noun* epistolais,
*which is feminine, but to the immediately preceding pronoun* toutois, *that
is,* pragmasin. *Peter's position, therefore, is that there are certain things
difficult to understand among the subjects about which Paul speaks in his
Epistles, and so forth.* See also similar things in Whitaker, at the place cited
above, chapter 3, page 33, and in Maccovius, *Commonplaces*, page 42.

What now, Wolzogen? Do you fight against the orthodox even with the weapons of
the papists? But he replies further at the same place that he does not
understand these things of matters necessary to salvation, and that there are
certain things short of this necessity for salvation in which God wills to
exercise our faith, which all Christians also maintain. Reply. Had he explained
himself this way in his little book, he would have done better. But there he
makes no mention whatsoever of this distinction, and hence it comes about, as
we shall say, that his discourse was so perplexed. We too know that he concedes
few things as fundamental or necessary to salvation, about which more later.
But this also must be observed: Peter speaks there about matters necessary to
salvation, if knowledge of the last judgment or the coming of the Lord is
necessary, for Peter speaks of these things. Therefore, if that argument drawn
from Peter's words proves Wolzogen's statement, it will also prove that the
Scriptures are obscure in themselves even with respect to matters necessary to
salvation.

Furthermore, he also shakes this foundation elsewhere, namely on pages
171–172: *We also embrace God's truthfulness universally in the latter sense,
but we do not think it can extend so far that no one is left doubtful about
the meaning—not, I say, through the fault of man, but*, note well, *through a
certain singular constitution of Scripture.* And a little later:

[Page 23 | ONB canvas 77 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000077]

*He so composed the words and the discourse that he knew that if a sinner
encountered it, he would be led into
error.* So on page 173: *Indeed, God sometimes speaks in such a way that he
leaves doubtful and suspended even those very people whom he has endowed with
sufficient grace of the Spirit, so that they draw out whatever can be drawn
from the discourse then before them, yet do not draw out all the truth. Let a
man turn and turn the discourse over a hundred times, be free of preconceived
opinions, examine everything the language requires, so that nothing appears to
him, as he considers the text, to have been neglected: nevertheless, God did
not will at that time to be understood, but willed to permit error for a
little while.* So on page 174: *Yet this dispensation of the Divine Majesty,
which is varied and almost infinite, contains every cause of obscurity which
appears to proceed not from us but from Scripture.* He has similar words on
page 176: *Indeed, God openly attests in more than one place both that the
ungodly will find mere darkness in Scripture and that the faithful will not
find all the light, because he wills to punish the former and exercise the
latter. Is there any intention to deceive in warning the reader*—note
well—*that he wrote deliberately in such a way that he does not always will
to be understood?* Do these things not sufficiently indicate that Wolzogen,
like a betrayer, overturns the foundations he himself laid? And who will judge
it necessary to heap up more for this purpose?

Our theologians also distinguish between those things which concern faith and
the conduct of life—that is, dogmas of faith and morals which are necessary
to eternal salvation, and which they assert are so clearly expressed in this
system that they can readily be perceived—and those things which, just as they
are less necessary, are also sometimes less clearly expressed. Although
Wolzogen recognizes this distinction, he makes no mention of it here, where it
was most necessary to use it. Hence it comes about that this Cato, who charges
all other Writers who treated this subject before him with obscurity, has, by
his general and indistinct discourse, so cov-*

[Page 24 | ONB canvas 78 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000078]

*ered with clouds this question which is sufficiently clear and perspicuous
in itself that now it can scarcely be understood. Indeed, without employing
this distinction, many of his statements are
either false or cannot be understood. For let him tell me in what sense these
words which he has on page 12 will be true: *If we employ all the diligence
that can rightfully be required of a person in matters of this kind, I maintain
that the truth can be found.* If he wishes these words to be understood of the
whole of Scripture, let him reconcile this statement, if he can, with the
passages cited above from pages 15, 16, 171–174, and 176, and show in what
sense these words which he had on page 13 and following will be true. If you
ask why he explained himself so indistinctly, I reply that I do not know, nor
can I sufficiently grasp what drove him to this kind of language, unless it
was to betray the cause and at the same time pave a road by which he might
more easily place his Diana—namely, linguistic usage—upon the throne. For he
knew well enough that linguistic usage is the same in all matters, whether
they are necessary to salvation or not.

Moreover, when our theologians speak about the perspicuity of Scripture with
respect to men—that is, considered subjectively, relatively, or internally—
they distinguish between the regenerate and the unregenerate. They say that
some of the latter, namely those furnished with the intellectual faculty alone,
can understand very many things historically and grammatically. Others, aided
by the more common operation of the Spirit, can comprehend more, be more
affected by the things they understand, be illumined, convicted, persuaded,
moved toward many outward good things, and restrained from evils, Matthew
13:20–21; 2 Peter 2:20–22; Hebrews 6:4–5. Yet this is not savingly, but in a
natural or more common manner. For they deny that such people understand
anything with spiritual evidence, clarity, splendor, readiness, reverence of
mind, submission, holiness, much less with certain full assurance of mind and
assent, since they have not yet been regenerated, illumined, led, and taught
by the Holy Spirit. But as for the regenerate, they say that these vividly,
clearly, distinctly, and savingly per-*

[Page 25 | ONB canvas 79 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000079]

*ceive the things necessary to salvation, since they have been born of the
Spirit, illumined, and aided by him as their teacher and guide. Thus they always
presuppose that without the gracious and most special concurrence of the Holy
Spirit, nothing can be understood savingly; and that without his more common
concurrence and cooperation, nothing can be understood in such a way as to
produce some conviction, a certain outward and partial conversion, restraint
from evils, and similar things common to hypocrites and other unregenerate
people. Without God's ordinary concurrence, the natural intellectual faculty
can perform nothing, and thus can understand nothing even historically and
grammatically.

Our Wolzogen, however, likes to speak differently, evidently so that he may
not be understood. He rarely mentions the Spirit and does not attribute to him
the things which must be attributed, as we shall see. I admit that he proposes
an objection on page 12 and, in its solution, mentions both the profane and
those whose minds God has illumined. The objection had two parts: that not
everyone finds the truth and that those who find it do not find all of it. He
responds to the first part as follows: *For those who do not see the truth—
mockers and profane men, and all others who are blind in reading the
Scriptures—see nothing not through the fault of the Scriptures, but through
the fault of their own minds. Since these are covered with the darkness of
natural ignorance, under which I include all impediments to saving knowledge,
they cannot perceive the truth even when depicted with the rays of the sun,
and so forth.*

But who does not see how he opposes both the truth and himself? He opposes the
truth because he does not wish the unregenerate—I believe he understands them,
or at least does not exclude them—to understand anything, whether historically,
grammatically, or in any other way. (Here the discourse is admittedly
instituted not about spiritual and saving understanding, but about
understanding in general, or the understanding by which truth is found, as is
plain to readers from the objection.) They do not understand because they do
not read rightly, and they do not read rightly because they do not employ a
purified mind; that is, as he himself explains in *Censure of the Censure*,
page 12, they *do not employ a mind freed from all impe-*

[Page 26 | ONB canvas 80 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000080]

*diments to knowledge by the power of the Holy Spirit, or a regenerated mind.*
He also opposes himself, for in this discourse he shakes his own
principal assertion and overturns his peculiar position. For if these things
are true, linguistic Usage will not be the sole standard for interpreting the
Scriptures, because mockers, profane men, and other unregenerate people can be
skilled in this usage and therefore can interpret the Scriptures and read
rightly, although they do not yet possess a purified mind and cannot perceive
anything savingly. Therefore, if he adheres to his own position, he must
concede that even the unregenerate, whose minds are covered with the darkness
of natural ignorance and obstructed by other impediments to saving knowledge,
can perceive truth, understand the Scriptures, and interpret them. What sort
of champion of truth is this, who is consistent neither with himself nor with
truth?

Next, on page 14, he comes to those whose minds God illumined with his own
light, and says that *this remaining error of the faithful proceeds from their
remaining ignorance, and that they see when God's light shines upon them and
do not see when it is absent.* These things are indeed true, but he has not yet
given us the whole matter sufficiently explained. He raises another difficulty
on page 15 when he says that *no few passages are set before us which, whatever
effort is employed upon them, take up nothing for the Interpreter but despair.*
By this statement this perplexed Doctor destroys what he said on page 12, when
he expressly asserted either that Scripture must be understood, or the truth
found from it, if we employ all the diligence that can rightfully be required
of a person in matters of this kind, or that futility must be ascribed to God.
Who is like this man, who now affirms and now denies, confidently and without
limitation asserts what he is afterward compelled to destroy? Yet let us see
how he removes the difficulty he has raised. He nevertheless bids the
Interpreter be of good courage and conceive some hope that the difficulty can
be removed. Good; then at last the difficulty will be removed, and even that
passage which appeared to bring the Interpreter nothing but des-*

[Page 27 | ONB canvas 81 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000081]

*pair will be capable of being explained. Not at all, however, for he adds: *It is not that I
wish to maintain that everything in Scripture is impassable.* What cause for
joy or hope is there, then? *But*, he says, *although they are not, nevertheless
God is and always remains endowed with the wisest mind.* This is most true, but
from where can the Interpreter conceive hope that the difficulty can be
removed? He therefore adds: *Although this is true in its own sense, that
nothing was so written by God that, once all those aids to interpretation which
the nature of the passage can bear have been employed, some meaning, and that
from the mind of God, cannot be drawn from it. For it would not be called the
discourse of God if it could admit no meaning from the nature of discourse.*
But although God's discourse always admits a meaning, can we always draw out
that meaning? If we can, the things he has on pages 15, 16, and 12 will be
false. If we cannot, from where can the Interpreter conceive hope that the
difficulty can be removed?

VI. There are also certain other things to be observed in these little pages
where he speaks about the intelligibility of Scripture. They are as follows.

1. He has these words on page 11: *But now, if we could in no way attain God's
meanings from his words, he gave the Scriptures to mortals in vain.* Does he
not thus imply that the Scriptures not only ought to be intelligible and
perspicuous in themselves, which we concede, but also ought to be subjectively
perspicuous and actually understood by us, otherwise they were given in vain?
What, then, will he say about those passages of Scripture which he afterward
calls impassable, and which, whatever effort is employed, bring the Interpreter
nothing but despair? Were these passages given to us in vain? Must we therefore
ascribe futility to God? Far be it. Can it not be inferred by an equal
consequence either that the Scriptures were given to mortals in vain, or that
everyone must possess the spiritual faculty to understand them savingly and
attain God's meanings from his words?

2. *These*, he says, adding the reason for the foregoing, *can by their nature
have no other use than no-*

[Page 28 | ONB canvas 82 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000082]

*thing other than to signify to mortals the determinations of the divine will.* Must it therefore
necessarily be presupposed that all mortals are
furnished by nature with those gifts by which they can savingly perceive the
divine will without any aid of the Spirit? Next, it is most true that the
Scriptures signify to us the determinations of the divine will, both those
which concern faith and those which concern morals. But I wonder, Wolzogen,
what came into your mind when, forgetting this statement, you said on pages
27–28 that *they must all be regarded as histories, of the same kind as those
which some Livy or Polybius could have committed to writing for us.* Did Livy
or Polybius signify to us the determinations of their will? Do the writings of
Livy or Polybius have the force of law?

3. When he is about to explain the foregoing, he adds these words at the same
place: *Therefore, just as when we experience ourselves to be endowed with the
faculty of judgment, and have proved that we received it, like all other
things, from God, of whom we have proved that he does nothing without an end,
we easily persuade ourselves that by the benefit of this faculty we can
perceive the truth, because God would have granted it to us in vain if it could
not perform that for which its nature is uniquely fitted.* Reply. 1. At first
glance it is plain to everyone that this comparison is a disanalogy. For he had
been speaking of the object, namely Scripture itself, but here he speaks of the
subject, namely the human intellect. Where, then, does the force of the
consequence appear? Because our intellect was born fitted to perceive truth,
Scripture was therefore born fitted to be understood by everyone, for both the
intellect and Scripture are from God. This proof or illustration is certainly
fetched from far away. Indeed, unless my mind deceives me, either this added
illustrative argument destroys the truth which it is adduced to prove, or
Wolzogen will not free himself from error. For since it is necessary that he
speak of our faculty of judgment as corrupted after the fall—because he speaks
of that faculty of judgment with which we experience ourselves to be
endowed—he must either main-*

[Page 29 | ONB canvas 83 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000083]

*tain that by this faculty we can perceive every spiritual and supernatural
good, or that Scripture cannot be understood with respect to these
spiritual things. For if he understands the perspicuity of Scripture also of
spiritual things, or of everything contained in it, yet wishes everything
perceived by our natural intellect to be merely natural truth and not
supernatural truth, this illustrative discourse will wrap everything in
darkness. For what sort of illustration is this? Just as, when we experience
ourselves to be endowed with the faculty of judgment, we easily persuade
ourselves that by its benefit we can perceive natural truth, because its nature
is uniquely fitted to effect this, so it must be judged of Scripture that it
can be understood even when it reveals heavenly truth to us, and so forth.

But since he expressly says on page 12 that *God granted us the Scriptures as
an immense benefit in order to teach us heavenly truth*, and when he speaks of
the object of our faculty of judgment he names *truth* in general, without any
added restriction or limitation, it is perfectly obvious to everyone that he
believes that even now, endowed as we are with the faculty of judgment, we can
by the benefit of this faculty perceive supernatural truth. This savors of
Semipelagianism, Arminianism, and Socinianism. What? Shall we say that God
granted us the intellect in vain if even now after the fall it cannot perceive
supernatural things, even though supernatural good has the character of an
object with respect to the Intellect, just as natural good does, and even
though our intellect was not only originally born fitted to perceive this
spiritual object, but was especially given to us for perceiving it? Dr. de
Labadie also rightly noted that these things support Free will and natural
Reason. For if it must be said that God granted us the faculty of understanding
in vain unless by its benefit we can also perceive supernatural good, then it
must likewise be said that God granted us the facul-*

[Page 30 | ONB canvas 84 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000084]

*ty of willing in vain unless it also seeks and wills spiritual good. To these
things he replies on pages 2–3 of *Censure of the Censure*: *I make no
mention to you of free will or of heavenly things. I propose the natural
faculty of judgment outside every use of divine things, and since
the nature of this faculty tends toward the perception of truth, I maintain
that truth must be capable of being perceived by it, or else it was given to
men in vain. But when I say “truth” generally, I do not understand that which
we draw from the Scriptures by the work of the divine Spirit. It is plain that
I said only what all sound Philosophers defend against the family of skeptics:
that there is some knowledge of things, which I judged could be applied to the
Scriptures not without profit.* Reply. 1. Since it is sufficiently plain to
everyone endowed with judgment that no necessity drove you to bring these
things in here—for these things obscure rather than clarify the foregoing,
which were sufficiently clear in themselves if they must be understood of
objective perspicuity—how can suspicion fail to arise that you offered some
monstrous thing to your readers here? This is especially so since you could
have removed every scruple from your readers by adding even one word, but you
so loved to speak generally that your words need much varnish before they
appear true.

But 2. although you make no mention of free will, you nevertheless know, and
yourself admit a little later, that the consequence is valid. 3. You say that
you make no mention of heavenly things, but it is also true that you make no
exception of heavenly things. You speak of the subject and its aptitude for
perceiving the true object. And that *truth* which is the object of the
intellect includes both natural and supernatural truth. Therefore, when you
say that our faculty must be able to perceive truth, since its nature tends
toward the perception of truth, or else it was given to men in vain, you must
say that our faculty must also be able to perceive supernatural truth;
otherwise it was, to that extent or with respect to spiritual things, granted
to us by God in va-*

*in. For if your argument has any force at all, it has force in both cases.
But what if your elder Velthuysen, whose *singular learning in every kind of
Doctrine, mind tenacious of orthodox truth, and exquisite prudence displayed
in a certain consistent course of life* are thoroughly known to you, were to
lend us some light here? Yet on page 6 of *The Use of Reason in Theology* he
says: *To deny that there is such a light, or principles of true knowledge, in
a corrupted man who has not yet been fully restored and made a participant in
the heavenly life is directly to contradict Scripture.* But does he understand
nothing except natural light? By no means, for he recognizes no distinction
between natural and supernatural light. Thus he speaks at the same place on
page 8: *Neither is knowledge of earthly things, which is commonly opposed to
knowledge of spiritual things, darkness; nor is that knowledge a different
kind of light from that of which the knowledge of spiritual things consists,
since each knowledge, both of spiritual and earthly things, is from God, is
truth, and is the rule according to which men rightly order their life, and so
forth. For the same reasons, it is lawful to conceive in the mind only one
kind of light.*

So on page 10: *Likewise, a distinction of light must not be introduced because
the faculty of the means by which knowledge is acquired is different. We
acknowledge that we know some things through revelation and attain knowledge
of other things through natural means established in the world. Therefore,
since the intellect possesses that one light, it can apprehend both those
things which are known through revelation and those which are known through
natural means.* He adds more on page 12: *Nor does the fact that we are
affected differently by the knowledge of a thing introduce a diversity of
light.* And on the following page: *I judge that all those things commonly
called different kinds of knowledge which distinguish a godly and holy man,
truly touched by reverence for the Deity, from an unregenerate man immersed in
his natural vices can conveniently be referred to these differences of
knowledge, in which the nature of light is not involved. The things peculiar
to a regen-*

*erate man indeed come down from above, from the Father of lights and the Holy
Spirit, but they do not make the nature of light or knowledge different; they
cause knowledge to mature into good fruit, and so forth.* I could now adduce
more from that most heterodox little book, but these things suffice to render
Velthuysen's mind clear to us. And if it is lawful to judge the Pastor's
meaning from the elder—who, according to the Pastor himself, has a mind
tenacious of orthodox truth—we cannot free him from Pelagianism and
Socinianism.

It does not satisfy when he says that by *truth he does not understand what we
draw from the Scriptures by the work of the Divine Spirit.* For Velthuysen
himself will admit that we cannot perceive the truths which are revealed
before they are set before us by revelation. But you do not say that the
Spirit is needed for understanding them when they are revealed; you say only
that by the work of the Spirit—understand, his more common work—we draw them
from the Scriptures. Thus, notwithstanding this, he can say that our intellect
labors under no natural and implanted blindness, but can equally easily
perceive every object, whether spiritual or natural, and possess the ideas of
them all within itself. Others too are not afraid roundly to affirm this in
public Disputations, although it contradicts catechisms, confessions of faith,
the writings of the orthodox, and Scripture itself.

4. On page 12 he says: *We judge that one must think about the Scriptures in
exactly this way. God granted them to us as an immense benefit in order to
teach us heavenly truth. I maintain that the truth can be found if we employ
all the diligence that can rightfully be required of a person in matters of
this kind, or else futility must be ascribed to God.* Is it not clear that
Wolzogen believes we can find the heavenly truth contained in the Scriptures
by our own powers, that we do not need the aid of the Holy Spirit, and that it
is enough merely to employ all the diligence that, in matters of this kind,
can rightfully be required of a person?

To these things he replies on page 5 of *Censure of the Censure*: *I am not
yet*, he says, *disputing about the Interpreter of the Scriptures in order to
establish what parts the Holy Spirit has in interpretation.* Reply. What parts
you ascribe to the Holy Spirit in the interpretation
of the Scriptures we will see later in the proper place. Here it is certain
that, although you are treating the nature of the Scriptures, by the words
cited you imply clearly enough that we can draw heavenly truth from them
without the work of the Holy Spirit. For otherwise you could have explained
yourself far differently, as you speak in the passage just cited from *Censure
of the Censure*.

If you had intended to say nothing other than what the nature of the
Scriptures was, you add something new: *Yet because, although these Scriptures
are perspicuous, they are nevertheless often not understood through men's
fault, I therefore added, “if we employ all the diligence that can rightfully
be required of a person in matters of this kind.” What this diligence is will
be seen in its proper place. Here it was enough for me to say that all the
diligence which can be expected of a person must be employed.* Reply. It is
most true that the Scriptures often are not understood through men's fault.
But then what? Since this fault must not be imputed to the Scriptures
themselves, what did that addition of yours have to do with the faults of men?
You were speaking, as you admit, about the nature of the Scriptures; why do
you pass to the faults of men? If the question had indeed been about relative
perspicuity, or perspicuity with respect to men, you would appropriately have
mentioned men's faults. But since, as you yourself say, your sole purpose was
to show that the Scriptures are such as abundantly to contain whatever can or
ought to be expected of them to effect the perception of truth, I see no
necessity compelling you to pronounce anything about men's fault, unless it
was to show how easily, in your judgment, the Scriptures could be understood
by everyone without any aid of the Spirit.

And in this your elder Velthuysen does not disagree with you. At the place
cited above, page 123, he says: *For the same reason it must be maintained that
Scripture is perspicuous in itself, so that all who*—note well—*seriously*

*devote themselves to reading it can through it become wise unto salvation;
nor do they need any other authority in drawing out the meaning of Scripture
than that which they themselves see and find in Scripture.* According to you,
therefore, Scripture will not be perspicuous in itself unless
it is also perspicuous in relation to us. I do not deny that Scripture is the
sole rule, which Velthuysen appears to maintain there, but the words cited
concern the perspicuity of Scripture, and he wishes the Scriptures to be so
perspicuous that all who seriously devote themselves to reading them can
become wise unto salvation. But to confess the truth, these words contribute
little to his purpose and appear rather to have been said in passing, and so
are all the more to be observed.

Nor do the Remonstrants disagree; these are their words in *Confession*,
chapter 1, section 4: *Moreover, the clarity and perspicuity of those same
books*—namely the sacred books—*is so great, especially in meanings necessary
to be understood for eternal salvation, that all Readers, not only the learned
but also the uneducated*—provided they are endowed with common sense and
judgment—*can sufficiently attain their mind, so long as they do not permit
themselves to be blinded by prejudice, vain confidence, and other perverse
affections, but search this Scripture piously and diligently.*

But to return to Wolzogen: I ask why he did not wish to mention the Holy
Spirit. He replies by adding these words: *Since, however, we need the Spirit
of God, and he can be obtained as a gift through pious prayers, it is plain
that this diligence in prayer is also posited where all diligence is posited.*
But this is a mere evasion. For if it had seemed good to him, he could easily
have mentioned the Holy Spirit. Is the aid of the Holy Spirit to be expected
from man? By this aid he perhaps understands nothing other than the general,
indifferent, and undetermined concurrence of the God of the Pelagians, which
is always and everywhere available to creatures about to act, just as the
light and heat of the sun are present to natural things and effective for
various operations, whether they act or do not act. If not, how

can he include the Spirit among the things to be expected from man? But he
says that he includes prayers by which the Spirit too can be obtained.
I reply that it is scarcely probable that Wolzogen himself remembered prayers
when he wrote these things, and the words do not necessarily include them.
Nevertheless, let us grant this. What will follow from it? Do those prayers
always and necessarily include the Spirit? Or is the Spirit of illumination
given to everyone who prays or pretends to pray? Take care not to say so. But
he will say that he understands pious prayers. Yet although it cannot be denied
that God hears the prayers of his people in matters absolutely necessary, and
in other matters when it has seemed good to him, and gives the Spirit to those
who ask, I nevertheless think it must not be said that God immediately gives
even to those who pray piously the Spirit who explains this or that particular
text. Elsewhere Wolzogen says that Daniel did not understand certain things he
himself had written, and attempts to prove this from Daniel 12:8. But there
Daniel prayed to God to make those things known to him, and certainly prayed
piously; nevertheless, God did not grant what was asked.

Next, no one can pour forth pious prayers unless he is godly, and no one can
be godly unless he is regenerate; no one except the regenerate can employ the
things which can rightfully be required. Therefore, when you say that the
Scriptures are often not understood through men's fault, you understand only
the faults of the regenerate. Consequently, this must not be understood of the
faults of the unregenerate. Therefore, notwithstanding their fault, the
unregenerate can find from the Scriptures a truth which the Regenerate cannot
find unless they first remove those faults under which they labor and employ
all diligence, and so forth. Who does not see where these things tend? But
enough about them, if we add only this one point: that from the words of his
elder, at the place cited above, page 70, Wolzogen's true meaning becomes
sufficiently known. *It is also now manifest*, he says, *what is to be
understood by the perspicuity of Scripture in matters necessary to
salvation*—Wolzogen, I note in passing, does not mention this *restric-*

*tion—* *which we rightly press against the papists. For we maintain that the mysteries
of faith necessary to salvation are so delivered in Scripture and are of such
a nature that they can be understood by everyone, and that all people to whom
they are proposed are furnished with those principles and faculties by which
anyone can perceive them, provided he brings due care, attention, and
meditation. By these the grace of God is also obtained and progress is made in
saving knowledge; without this grace no one engages in Theology with fruit. But that
grace, as we said above, is not light, nor does it introduce a new light into
us or Scripture, but leads us so that we will walk in the light of Scripture
and of our intellect.* Pious prayers cannot be included here, because he speaks
of absolutely all people and of those who have not yet obtained grace. Nor may
anyone say that he speaks only about natural perception or understanding, by
which the literal sense is understood, and not about spiritual and saving
understanding. For according to him the knowledge or perception, whether
spiritual or natural, is the same on the part of the thing. In this respect he
wishes saving knowledge to be distinguished from the other: that, as he speaks
on page 69, it *is joined with that operation of God's grace by which the mind
of man is so struck and affected that it does not permit operations of the mind
and thoughts to intervene which so disturb or confuse that knowledge that its
vivid impression upon the mind is dispersed. On the contrary, by dispersing
the darkness of every evil affection, alien thought, and prejudice, it causes*
—note well—*the perception of saving truth to occupy the whole mind.*
Therefore it is the same perception: grace merely causes it to be more
effectual, both extensively and intensively. Consider these things, Wolzogen,
for they are the very words of your elder; and recall your own words also, on
