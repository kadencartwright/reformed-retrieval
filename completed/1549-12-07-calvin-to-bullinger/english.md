# John Calvin to Heinrich Bullinger, 7 December 1549

*A fresh translation from the Latin text of letter 1324 at CO XIII 488–490.
Page notices are editorial and not Calvin's words.*

[CO XIII, p. 488]

To the most distinguished man, Master Heinrich Bullinger, faithful pastor of
the church of Zurich, fellow minister, and most respected and beloved brother.

Greetings. Concerning the printer's criticism, I am deeply grateful both to
you and to our excellent brothers who sent it to you. I wish I had been warned
sooner. Yet better late than never. I shall do what you advise. But because
that piece of writing was short, I postponed it until the time of the fair. It
will be more vexing than laborious for me, because I shall have to quarrel
with our own people. I would rather fight declared enemies. Yet these too are
our exercises. This will be the reason I finish it more briefly.

All my brothers have gladly embraced the formula of agreement. If you wish,
you will have it as soon as possible, signed by the hands of them all.

Because there is no hope that the book *On Avoiding Superstitions* will soon
be reprinted, since the printer says that he still has a great many copies, I
arranged for the supplement you sent to be attached to it. The book will thus
be more saleable. And it is better for it to have been enriched with this
adornment before it is spread any farther.

I want the cause of the Lausanne brothers to be commended to you. Recently,
by a decree of the council, they were forbidden to meet in their customary
way to examine Scripture. Their procedure was that each in his turn would
expound a passage of Scripture, and then each would contribute whatever had
been given him by the Lord. This interchange has thus far been very fruitful
and has kept many to their duty. For the man whose turn it was to expound the
passage was admonished privately if he said anything ignorantly, less aptly,
or out of its proper place. Because that troublesome wrangler Zebedaeus
several times disrupted matters with his disputes, it is utterly unjust that
an excellent institution should be abolished for his sake. The council, at
Viret's request, has indeed somewhat softened this severity.

[CO XIII, p. 489]

Nevertheless, the decree remains by which they are commanded to meet only
once every three months. Meanwhile, the Bernese brothers, as I hear, behave
toward them more imperiously than fraternally, so that nothing enters into
the reckoning except the bare name of the ministry. Yet this is not an
ecclesiastical way of proceeding. But if the Bernese despise that company, I
say nothing except that they misjudge both themselves and those to whom they
are so injurious. That the latter are despised and treated insultingly is
more than clear. Even if there were nothing worse, they repeatedly hear a
master's threats. Nor do the Bernese see, while they seek dominion over their
brothers, how much harsher a yoke of servitude they call down upon themselves.
This depravity does not reign in them all. The worse party nevertheless
prevails. I take the liberty of freely depositing these complaints in your
bosom. There is neither need nor advantage for them to flow any farther. You
alone, in keeping with your prudence and equity, will endeavor to interpose at
least some remedy against these evils. Above all, care must be taken that
those whom you know forget their Zebedaeus and similar favorites.

Recently, when a certain learned and keen brother had been elected to the
office of deacon—the man who translated my little book on judicial astrology
and who voluntarily abandoned his father, a royal councillor in the court of
Paris, together with the hope of a rich inheritance, in order to serve
Christ—their efforts brought it about that the council rejected him. I know
that these actions cannot meet your approval. If you cannot entirely cure
this plague of ill will, effort must nevertheless be made to restrain it to
some extent.

In the last letter Bucer wrote to me, he complained that Hopper was maligning
him as though he asserted the ubiquity of Christ's body. I am the best witness
of how far removed he is from that delusion. If ever you write to the man, you
will admonish him to consider that Bucer has been one of the foremost servants
of Christ in this age, has rendered outstanding service to the church, has
endured many labors, and is now an exile for Christ, and not to harass
inhumanely an old man already at the end of life.

Farewell, most distinguished man and brother to be honored by me in the Lord.
Many greetings from me to your colleagues, and especially to your household.
All my brothers send many greetings to you all. I also wish Master Henry to be

[CO XIII, p. 490]

greeted in my name. Geneva, the seventh day before the Ides of December 1549.

Your John Calvin.

## Translator's notes

1. CO says a letter from Bullinger on which Calvin's opening remarks depended
   has been lost. *Librarii censura* can denote a printer's or bookseller's
   critical assessment; “the printer's criticism” is therefore deliberately
   cautious. The precise writing Calvin had deferred until the fair is not
   recoverable from this letter alone.
2. The *formula consensionis* is the agreement associated with the *Consensus
   Tigurinus*. Calvin says that all his Genevan colleagues accepted it and
   offers to send Bullinger a copy bearing all their signatures. The formula
   itself is not quoted in this letter.
3. *Attexeretur* literally suggests attaching or stitching on the supplement
   Bullinger sent. Calvin's contrast between many unsold copies and a future
   reprinting indicates that the addition was meant for the existing stock.
4. The Lausanne exercise was a recurring ministerial Scripture colloquy, with
   successive exposition and private correction. *In calculum venire* means
   to enter a reckoning or count; *heriles minae* pointedly evokes threats
   issued by a master to servants. Calvin's *similes delitias* calls protected
   favorites like Zebedaeus “similar darlings,” here rendered “similar
   favorites.”
5. Calvin does not name the rejected deacon in his body. CO's third note
   identifies him as François Hotman, who used the name *Franciscus Villerius*
   and was Seigneur de Villiers-Saint-Paul. That identification and the
   accompanying family information are nineteenth-century editorial
   apparatus, not words silently supplied to Calvin's letter.
6. CO's cross-reference to letter 1240 identifies *Hopperus* as the English
   reformer John Hooper. Bucer had complained that Hooper publicly attributed
   to him a doctrine of the ubiquity of Christ's human body. *Traduci* can
   mean being exposed to public reproach; “maligning” preserves that polemical
   force.
7. CO prints the abbreviation *Do.* before *Henrichum* without expanding it.
   It is most naturally the honorific *Dominum*, rendered “Master,” but the
   edition does not identify this second Henry.
8. In the Roman calendar, *7. idus Decemb.* is 7 December: the Ides of
   December fell on the thirteenth, and Roman counting was inclusive.
