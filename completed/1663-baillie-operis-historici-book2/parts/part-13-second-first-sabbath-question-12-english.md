## QUESTION XII

### What is Σάββατον δευτερόπρωτον, “second-first Sabbath,” at Luke 6:1?

Few interpreters pass over this question. Lyra, indeed, and Heinsius, in his customary fashion, do not touch it. Was it because they judged it too easy, or because they judged it too difficult? Who knows, since they conceal their own thinking. Erasmus wrongly mocks, though deservedly, a certain monk and professor of sacred theology who publicly denied, with a great wager, that any mention of a second-first Sabbath was made anywhere in the Gospel. If that daring monk lived today, he could produce as eminent defenders of his crass ignorance from recently published books not merely the Syriac interpreter but also the Arabic, Persian, and Ethiopic, all of whom suppressed the word δευτερόπρωτον in their versions. Casaubon says that the Arabic retained the former part of the compound by translating it “the second Sabbath,” but this is not altogether apparent in our London copy. In any case, δευτερόπρωτον is expressly read in all Greek and Latin codices, whether manuscript or printed. Whether it is one word, as all the Greek copies have it, including Flaminius’s Vatican copy and the London Alexandrian, or two words, “second, first,” separated by a seemingly unnecessary comma, as the recent Latin edition from Rome has it, makes little difference to the matter itself.

In explaining the matter, Cajetan admits a real difficulty. Casaubon cites from John, bishop of Citrus, a Greek proverb about the inexplicable obscurity among the Jews of second-first Sabbaths. Jansenius places so great a difficulty in them that he affirms nothing certain can be found. The Rheims Jesuits pronounce the passage so difficult that Protestants alone, who disdain to be ignorant of anything, have dared to undertake its elucidation. Cartwright rightly retorted to them, however, that many men from the Jesuit Society itself can be found who are bolder than anyone in explaining this passage.

Jerome relates in his letter to Nepotian that when, as a young man, he associated with Gregory Nazianzen as his teacher, he asked what the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον was. But that very great man and the most profound theologian among all the Greek Fathers, having nothing serious to answer, evaded his question with a witty joke.

The latest English annotators, and the Belgian notes before them, propose various opinions without themselves determining which should be embraced. Erasmus and Maldonatus admit that whatever they have read on this matter among either ancient or more recent writers displeases them. It is certain that there is a great variety of opinions here, though I would not call it infinite with Lucas Brugensis, nor affirm with Grotius that there are as many opinions as heads. There are certainly not so many that they cannot be reduced to a few classes, concerning which we shall briefly explain what we think.

The first group, after Ambrose and Bede, whom the *Glossa ordinaria* cites, take refuge in a mystical sense, expounding the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον as the evangelical Sabbath. It is called second because it came later than the legal Sabbath, yet first because it is worthier, more principal, and far more excellent than the legal. This opinion cannot be approved, because at Luke 6:1, when Christ walked through the fields, the evangelical Sabbath did not yet exist, for it was not instituted before Christ’s resurrection.

Second. Others, after Luther and Faber Stapulensis, understand the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον as a new moon or some other feast which was inferior to the ordinary weekly Sabbath and less solemn. For just as the weekly Sabbath was absolutely πρῶτον, “first,” and, when compared with all other feasts, could be called πρωτόπρωτον, “first-first,” so the other feasts, all of which were called Sabbaths in relation to the first and weekly Sabbath, could rightly be called “second-firsts.” Chemnitius deservedly rejects this opinion of Luther himself, because the Sabbath mentioned by Luke was in no way—

[Marginalia: There is a very great difficulty in the question.]

[Marginalia: This Sabbath is not mystical, as Ambrose holds.]

—inferior to the weekly Sabbath, but entirely equal to it and itself the weekly Sabbath, which was superior to the other feasts. On all of those feasts, even Passover itself, it was lawful to prepare what was necessary for food, so that on their most sacred day plucking ears of grain, and much more laborious works, would not have been liable to censure, as may be seen at Exodus 12:16. On the ordinary Sabbath, however, even kindling a fire to prepare food is distinctly forbidden at Exodus 35:3. That Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον, which the Pharisees slanderously claimed the apostles violated by plucking ears of grain, must have been such a Sabbath.

Third, Theophylact interprets it as a Sabbath immediately preceded by another feast, when, namely, a new moon, Passover, Pentecost, or any other feast fell on the Friday of the Sabbath, which they call παρασκευή, on which they prepared themselves for the following Sabbath on Saturday. In that case Saturday was called the second-first Sabbath: first, because it was the weekly Sabbath, the principal Sabbath of all; second, because it followed another Sabbath, namely, the feast falling on the preceding day. The word would be explained suitably enough in this way, but the actual circumstance does not seem to fit well, and so this opinion is commonly rejected. Yet the one reason Calvin, Chemnitius, Jansenius, Maldonatus, Salmeron, and others offer is not firm enough. They suppose that after the captivity the Jews never joined two feasts immediately, so that in Christ’s time no feast could be celebrated on the παρασκευή, or day immediately preceding the Sabbath. They suppose this but do not labor to prove it. Chemnitius alone cites Brugensis for that Jewish tradition, whose authority in so great a matter carries little weight unless supported by reasons or much more ancient testimonies. In any case, I do not approve the opinion for this reason: the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον is one certain and fixed Sabbath, while Theophylact makes it shifting, uncertain, and variable. For according to his view there will be as many δευτερόπρωτα Sabbaths at any time of the year as had either a new moon or any other feast day in the whole year immediately preceding. There could have been a great many such Sabbaths.

Fourth. Chrysostom, in his homily on Matthew (not the fortieth, as Baronius, Chemnitius, Casaubon, and others cite it, but at the beginning of the thirty-ninth, as the Savile edition has it), expounds the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον as a double Sabbath, on which there was διττὴ ἡ ἀργία, a doubled rest, when another feast fell on the weekly Sabbath. Jansenius, Toletus, Lucas Brugensis, and Emmanuel Sa follow this opinion among the papists; Chemnitius and Cartwright do so among our writers, and Junius does not reject it. We nevertheless do not assent, because neither Chrysostom nor his followers establish it by any reason or authority, and the points we brought against Theophylact’s preceding opinion also militate against it. For if the Jews after the captivity were unwilling to join two feast days so that one pressed immediately on the other, much more would they have refused to heap several festivities onto one and the same day. Next, the circumstance in which other feasts could fall on an ordinary Sabbath could have been so frequent that the second-first Sabbath in this sense would become entirely uncertain, variable, and manifold. What we chiefly press, however, is the incongruity of the language. The Greeks do not call double things δεύτερα; δεύτερον means second, but not double. What Chemnitius brings in opposition from Deuteronomy seems instead to establish our reasoning, for the law had indeed been repeated and reiterated a second time, not duplicated. In a second, third, fourth, or, if you wish, hundredth repetition, it always remained one simple law, never a twin or double law. Thus a duplication of the Sabbath, or the addition of another feast to a Sabbath, would not rightly be called the Sabbath’s δευτέρωσις, or seconding. Moreover, even if a double Sabbath could be called δευτεροσάββατον, a “second-Sabbath,” it would in no way be δευτερόπρωτον, that is, a doubling of the first Sabbath, but of another Sabbath. For the feast joined with the Sabbath was not one and the same thing as the Sabbath, but a day of another nature and kind, although both came together under one common class and name, each feast required its own rest, and both were designated by the one general name of Sabbath.

Fifth. Isidore of Pelusium, book 3 of his letters, letter 110, and after him Suidas, Titus of Bostra, and Eucherius interpret the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον as the first day of Unleavened Bread. They call this day a Sabbath because the whole Passover week was festal and thus consisted of seven Sabbaths. The first day of Unleavened Bread was called the second Sabbath because it was the second day of Passover, and also the first Sabbath because it was the first day of Unleavened Bread. Baronius professes to follow this opinion of Isidore, yet he does not seem to have grasped Isidore’s meaning, or at least not to have explained it correctly. First, he makes Isidore affirm that on the first day of Unleavened Bread wheat was ripe for harvest, but Isidore says nothing about wheat, only ears of grain. These must be understood as barley ears, from which alone the sheaf was offered on the first day of Unleavened Bread, since the firstfruits of the wheat harvest would not be ripe before Pentecost. Second, he has Isidore call the second day of Unleavened Bread the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον, although Isidore expressly names it once and again not the second but the first day of Unleavened Bread, distinguishing it from the first day of Passover. Baronius’s carelessness here is all the more noteworthy because he admits that Suidas, whom he adduces as agreeing with Isidore, names the first, not the second, day of Unleavened Bread. Third, Baronius cites Epiphanius and Chrysostom as if they agreed with Isidore, but we have already said how far Chrysostom differs from Isidore—

—and we shall soon hear how much Epiphanius differs. Fourth, Baronius did not notice Isidore’s crass error when he makes the sheaf offered not on the second but on the third day of Unleavened Bread. There is no room to escape here as though Isidore meant the third day of Passover, for in the same place he expressly distinguishes the days of Passover from the days of Unleavened Bread, making the first day of Unleavened Bread the second day of Passover. Thus he ought to have placed the offering of the sheaf, as we read at Leviticus 23:11, on the third day of Passover indeed, but only the second day of Unleavened Bread, or the day after its first.

Casaubon passes over all these errors of Baronius, yet he notes in him another error which he could rightly have passed over, since Baronius himself recognizes and abandons it. From Isidore’s false supposition—that the sheaf was offered on the third day of Unleavened Bread, which was two days after the Sabbath δευτεροπρώτῳ—Baronius inferred that the Pharisees censured the apostles for daring to eat new grain before the offering of the sheaf, contrary to the law. This does necessarily follow from Isidore’s false supposition, but Baronius did not adhere to these points. Instead, he passed to another of Isidore’s premises and teaches that the day on which the apostles plucked the ears was truly a weekly Sabbath. If either Isidore or Baronius had proved this, everything would have been secure, but neither attempts to prove what was incumbent on them before all else. Indeed, a Lapide correctly argues from this very point against the entire opinion of Isidore and Baronius: namely, that the first day of Unleavened Bread was not the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον, because this necessarily was a weekly Sabbath on which God forbade the preparation of things pertaining to food. But the first day of Unleavened Bread was not such a Sabbath, for on every day of Passover and Unleavened Bread it was lawful to prepare things pertaining to food, as we said above. Isidore and Baronius say that the weekly Sabbath fell on the first day of Unleavened Bread that year, but do not prove it.

I add another argument from Jansenius. The Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον did not precede but followed the offering of the sheaf. For had it preceded, the Pharisees’ charge against the apostles would chiefly have censured their violation of that solemn law against eating produce before the offering of the sheaf. But the Pharisees charged the apostles with no such sin. It is therefore certain that the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον on which the apostles plucked ears followed the offering, whereas the first day of Unleavened Bread preceded it. Casaubon says here, in accordance with Isidore’s opinion, that the offering of the sheaf and the apostles’ deed fell on the same day, the former in the morning and the latter in the afternoon. This is far removed from the truth, for besides the fact that Isidore expressly places the apostles’ deed on the first day of Unleavened Bread and, contrary to Scripture, the offering of the sheaf on the third, even if we granted that Isidore placed the offering with Scripture on the second day of Unleavened Bread, the apostles’ deed would still precede the offering not by several hours but by a full day and night.

The sixth opinion, held by many papists and Protestants, is that the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον should be understood as Pentecost, which was the second solemnity after Passover. It was a Sabbath because it was a feast, δεύτερον, “second,” because it followed Passover, the first solemn feast, and also πρῶτον, “first,” because it was equal to the first feast, namely Passover. We would have placed Calvin in this class, because after openly rejecting the opinions of others, he professes to embrace those who understand the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον as the second solemnity—and what could be understood as the second solemnity of the year other than Pentecost? Yet what that excellent man adds about Passover and its firstfruits prevents us from understanding his thought altogether.

Maldonatus and Cornelius a Lapide openly follow this sixth opinion, and before them our Mercator, whom Piscator cites with approval. If Grotius read it from them, I do not know what came into his mind that he boasts of himself as the author of this opinion, while his follower Hammond, as usual, plays the flatterer to him in this.

The English annotators and the Belgian notes relate this opinion among others and seem to approve it as much as any other, but it does not seem sound. The first and chief reason on which all its supporters build is entirely false: namely, that the crops in Judea were not ripe at Passover, so that their ears could be plucked then, but that the firstfruits of the harvest occurred at Pentecost. This is a crass error, though shared by many learned men, who did not observe the two harvests in Judea distinguished expressly enough in various Scriptures—Ruth 2:23; Leviticus 23:11, 15; and Exodus 9:31–32—the earlier of barley and the later of wheat. At the earlier, a sheaf of ears, or עמר, was offered to the Lord; on its manner see Ainsworth on Leviticus 23 from Maimonides, and Chemnitius on Luke 6:1. At the later day, namely Pentecost, loaves of wheaten firstfruits were offered. We grant that the wheat was not yet ripe at Passover, but the beginning of the barley harvest in Judea was always at Passover. In Egypt, where the harvest is no earlier or only a little earlier than in Judea, several days before the beginning of the month Nisan, which corresponds in large part to our March, the flax and barley were in green ears, while spelt and wheat had not yet appeared above the ground because they were late crops, as the passage of Exodus cited above states.

What Grotius brings forward here—that the green ears were offered on the second day of Unleavened Bread merely for the sake of the expression—seems a very rash and profane statement, for—

—the offering of the sheaf was very strictly and solemnly enjoined by God, before all consumption of new produce is expressly prohibited. Indeed, if Maimonides, by far the most learned rabbi in a thousand years, was sufficiently acquainted with his nation’s custom, those ears were not green but ripe. From them, after threshing, an *omer* of flour had to be offered to God on the second day of Unleavened Bread, as may be seen in Ainsworth on Leviticus 23:11.

I offer another argument against this opinion. On the Sabbath δευτεροπρώτῳ it was not lawful to prepare things pertaining to food. But this was lawful at Pentecost, as is evident from Exodus 12:16, where, although abstinence from every work is commanded at Passover itself, the preparation of things pertaining to food is nevertheless permitted. Maldonatus replies that the apostles were rebuked by the Pharisees not because they prepared food, but because they gathered and harvested the ears, a kind of work lawful on no feast. How foolishly and ridiculously! Luke says nothing about collecting or harvesting the ears, but about plucking them, and such a small portion that they rubbed them in their hands. No one has ever translated τίλλειν as “to gather” or “to harvest,” but all translate it, as Theophylact does ἀνασπᾶν, “to draw out” or “to pluck.”

A Lapide replies differently: Pentecost was not simply or always called the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον, but only when the Sabbath properly so called fell on it; otherwise, the Sabbath properly so called that followed Pentecost was the second-first. Reply: no one attempts and no one can prove that the weekly Sabbath fell on Pentecost in the year under discussion. Next, the Sabbath after Pentecost had no prerogative over the year’s other Sabbaths. For although Pentecost was the second of the solemn feasts, it consisted of only one day, unlike Passover or the eight-day Feast of Tabernacles. Thus Pentecost imparted no dignity to any day or Sabbath following it. A Lapide grants this with respect to the law’s prescription, but denies it with respect to the people’s devotion, which rendered the same honor to Pentecost as to Passover. Reply: the people’s superstition, which departs from the law’s prescription, is nothing to us, although not even the Jesuit proves it.

Finally, I argue against this opinion: the context of the account of the Sabbath δευτεροπρώτου at Matthew 12:1 and Luke 6:1 proves that this was the time not of Pentecost but of Passover, as Chemnitius, Jansenius, Barradius, and others prove at length.

When I had advanced this far, I took up Petavius’s strictures on Epiphanius’s heresies 30 and 51 and was glad that, without knowing it, I had him as a σύμψηφον, a voter on my side, in almost all my censures of Isidore, Baronius, and Maldonatus himself.

The seventh opinion, which we also follow, is Epiphanius’s in heresy 30, number 32. He teaches there that the second-first Sabbath was the first ordinary Sabbath after the solemnity of Passover. He distinguishes Sabbaths into natural and legal. He calls the former the weekly Sabbaths instituted by God from the beginning of the world, before the law was given; the latter were the other feasts commanded in the law, on which the Jews rested from servile works. These too are called Sabbaths, though not to be honored as highly as the weekly Sabbaths, as was said. Epiphanius calls the first weekly Sabbath after Passover δεύτερον, “second,” because in time it was second and later than the feast of Passover, and also first because in dignity it preceded Passover and every other extraordinary feast. Casaubon does not doubt that this is Epiphanius’s true meaning, and Petavius also admits it, although he wished to draw Epiphanius elsewhere. Petavius adds, however—in which I think he has no follower—that in heresy 50 Epiphanius fell into Maldonatus’s preceding interpretation. Yet Maldonatus expressly refutes Epiphanius by name as his opponent, although with very futile arguments. It is true that in heresy 51, number 31, Epiphanius calls the day which was seventh from the first day of Passover the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον. Thus Beza, Maldonatus, a Lapide, and most other Protestants and papists interpret Epiphanius in that place as referring to the seventh and final day of Passover. But I have read no one except Petavius who twists his opinion from Passover to Pentecost. Certainly, in the latter passage he refers the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον to Passover as expressly and clearly as in the former, although with this difference: in heresy 30 he affirms that the natural Sabbath following the first day of Passover was the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον, while in heresy 51 he ambiguously suggests that the seventh day from the first of Passover, or the last Sabbath of Unleavened Bread, was the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον. Whether the Sabbath was natural or legal, the interval between those two Sabbaths was only two or three days, as Scaliger states in his short work *On the Emendation of Times*, book 6, page 521. In the year in which Christ passed through the fields, the first day of Passover was the fourteenth of the month Nisan; the second of Passover and first of Unleavened Bread was the fifteenth of that month; the third of Passover and second of Unleavened Bread, when the sheaf was offered as in every year, was the sixteenth of Nisan. The weekly Sabbath that year fell on the nineteenth of Nisan, or March 31. Accordingly, the seventh and last day of Unleavened Bread, or the eighth of Passover, followed only two days later, on the twenty-first of Nisan, or April 2. —Bread was offered. He calls this day the second-first Sabbath because it was the first from the second day of Unleavened Bread, from which God commanded at Leviticus 23 that all the days and weeks until Pentecost be counted.

I am not so surprised that our writers Junius, Piscator, Diodati, and now others generally follow Scaliger as I am that the Jesuits Vasquez and Barradius, and Petavius above all, Scaliger’s antagonist, entirely embrace his opinion.

Some here profess their disagreement, but, as it seems, with no weighty argument. Beza affirms that he agrees with Scaliger about the day but disagrees about the reason for the name, because it rests on no authority or examples. But if that venerable man had read Scaliger’s third *Isagogical Canon* when he wrote this, he would have seen on a single page an abundance of authorities and examples from both Jews and Greeks. Beza is also mistaken when he asserts that he agrees with Scaliger about the day, for Scaliger’s Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον is not the seventh day from the first day of Unleavened Bread, as Beza holds, but the first weekly Sabbath, which followed the second day of Unleavened Bread and preceded its last day.

A Lapide wrongly separates Scaliger from Epiphanius. In any case, the argument he brings here against them both seems to carry no weight: “The Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον,” he says, “was weekly, but the last or seventh day of Unleavened Bread was not.” Reply: Cornelius apparently did not take care to look at either Epiphanius or Scaliger, for otherwise he would not have attacked his own shadow. Both expressly place the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον on a weekly Sabbath.

Maldonatus’s arguments against Epiphanius are no better. First, he says, ears of grain in Judea were not ripe at Passover or in the month of March. Reply: his associate Petavius affirms not only that this is said falsely but that it is expressly contrary to the sacred Scriptures, which often assert that the ears of barley were ripe on the second day of Unleavened Bread.

Second, he says, at the time of Passover people in Judea warmed themselves at a fire, as may be seen at John 18. Therefore the harvest was not then ripe. Reply: at nighttime, especially at midnight, a fire is ordinarily kindled at any season of the year, much more in the month of March.

Third, he says, the sickle was not put to the harvest before the firstfruits were offered at Pentecost. Reply: who does not now know that the firstfruits of Pentecost were taken from the Jews’ second, wheat harvest, just as the Passover sheaf was taken from the first, barley harvest?

Fourth, he says, even in the regions of Africa hotter than Judea, the crops do not ripen at Passover. Reply: in Egypt, which is certainly not the hottest, if not the coldest, of the regions of Africa, barley was ordinarily in the ear at Passover, as is clear to anyone reading Exodus 9.

To those arguments of the Jesuits against Scaliger, Grotius adds two more. The former is that one must not grant that δευτερόπρωτον is compounded from πρώτη and δευτέρα, because no example of a composition of this kind is given. Reply: besides the many examples Scaliger has in the place cited above, what need is there of any example where the matter is manifest? Grotius does not deny that the masculine δεύτερος and neuter δεύτερον are rightly compounded with πρῶτος; what, then, prevents the feminine δευτέρα from founding a compound with the same word, especially since God’s express command to count τῆς δευτέρας provides the best foundation for this composition? Grotius’s latter argument is this: God did not command Sabbaths to be counted from the second day of Unleavened Bread, but only days, from which Pentecost has its name. Reply: God commands not only days but expressly Sabbaths and weeks to be counted from the second day of Unleavened Bread at Leviticus 23:15.

Against this opinion, as Beza explains it, Ludovicus de Dieu offers an argument that touches neither Epiphanius nor Scaliger, who make the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον a Sabbath properly so called and weekly. There was no other legal Sabbath—whether on the first, second, or last Sabbath of Passover—on which it was not lawful to prepare food, notwithstanding the religious observance of those legal Sabbaths. As for de Dieu’s observation about the three Sabbaths in Passover week, others observed it before him, and it contributes nothing to the present question. For grant that the first day of Unleavened Bread was the first Sabbath of that week, the weekly Sabbath after that first day was the second, and the last day of Unleavened Bread was the third; and, if you wish, that the first day of Passover, preceding the first day of Unleavened Bread, namely the fourteenth day of the moon, was the fourth Sabbath of that week: none of this bears on the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον.

Concerning this seventh opinion I shall note only one further thing: the latest English version does not seem the best here. I acknowledge that the old rendering, “a second solemn Sabbath,” deserved correction. It signified that the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον was second, although Luke calls it first; it translated πρῶτον as “solemn”; and it supposed that this Sabbath was referred to some preceding Sabbath as the first solemn Sabbath. We grant that these points are not true and required correction, but the revision they gave does not accord with the opinion before us. They render it “the second Sabbath after the first,” namely after the second day of Passover, which was the first day of Unleavened Bread. Scaliger deservedly called this a Sabbath in inverted order, and Junius judged that it should be stated in the entirely opposite way: “the first Sabbath after the second,” just as Diodati expressly gives it in the text of his Italian version. —as the French version before them has *sabbat second primier*. This is more obscure, certainly, but safer, and no one can censure it, since they follow the authentic original exactly.

Before I bring the question to an end, I shall touch briefly on the eighth and ninth opinions. The Belgian notes, and the latest English notes after them, propose various opinions without themselves determining anything. They have our seventh opinion with its supporting arguments; also Beza’s opinion about the last day of Unleavened Bread, and the opinion of others about a Sabbath on or after Pentecost. What displeases us in this eighth opinion is not merely that it proposes opinions which appear false to us and makes them equal with the true, but especially that it leaves readers uncertain at a crossroads about which of the three paths they should follow.

The ninth and final opinion is found in Baldwin, and in Walaeus from Cloppenburg and Capellus. What we miss in Walaeus is that, although he brings this one opinion to elucidate the passage in Luke, he does not make altogether clear what in it either pleases or displeases him. We, for our part, candidly admit that the whole of it displeases us. Besides its avowed novelty, it affirms that the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον always preceded the offering of the sheaf by at least sixteen days. Grant that two beginnings of the year and months appear in the Jewish lectionary: the first on the twenty-fourth of Tishri in autumn, when the Jews’ civil year began, as from the beginning of the world; the second in spring, at the new moon of Nisan, when the sacred year was commanded to begin by God’s command after the people’s exodus. Cloppenburg nevertheless does not prove that the Sabbaths of the year had two beginnings, such that the first Sabbath of the civil year was called πρωτόπρωτον, “first-first,” and the first of the sacred year δευτερόπρωτον, “second-first.” What he adduces from [Hebrew: התחלת דבר; reading uncertain] in the German work of a more recent rabbi, concerning *Resch Varischon*, “first and first,” does not prove his proposal. For even if we granted his new and subtle exposition of *Resch Varischon* as “first and second,” this would still not be Luke’s δευτερόπρωτον, but πρωτοδεύτερον, “first-second,” which is far different. Second, Cloppenburg is speaking not about the beginning of Sabbaths but about the beginning of the year and months, which contributes nothing to our question. Third, Cloppenburg admits that this Sabbath of his, δευτερόπρωτον, generally falls in the month of February before the first day of Nisan, so that it is not at all the first but the last Sabbath of the year. Fourth, this Sabbath of Cloppenburg occurred many days before the second day of Unleavened Bread on the sixteenth of Nisan, when the sheaf was offered—often a full twenty-two days and never fewer than sixteen—such that the produce could not have been ripe at that time. Learned men dispute whether the barley ears in Judea ripened on the sixteenth of Nisan. But Cloppenburg is the first to affirm that they ripened at the new moon on the first of Nisan, or several days earlier in the month of February. He does indeed cite Josephus for himself, but beyond the author’s meaning and even contrary to the words he himself adduces from Josephus, in which it is affirmed that before the sixteenth of Nisan the Jews had not even touched any crops: οὐ γὰρ, he says, ἥψαντο, “for they had not touched them.” This reasoning is also valid against Capellus. Although he corrects Cloppenburg in some points, he nevertheless follows him in this: that the Sabbath δευτερόπρωτον preceded the second day of Unleavened Bread, always by at least nine days. No learned man will grant him this.
