QUESTION XII.

*Was the Sinaitic legal covenant, which was sanctioned through Moses with the
Israelite people on Mount Sinai, some third covenant specifically distinct
from the covenant of nature and the covenant of grace? We deny it.*

[Marginal heading: Various opinions concerning the Sinaitic covenant.]

I. Theologians hold various opinions here. Some maintain that the Sinaitic
covenant was the covenant of works. Others maintain that it was mixed from the
covenant of works and the covenant of grace. Others maintain that it was
properly neither the covenant of nature nor the covenant of grace, but a third
covenant specifically distinct from both. They say that it was instituted to
serve the covenant of grace and for that reason is properly called
*subservient*. Finally, others—with whom we agree—maintain that it was the
covenant of grace, but promulgated with the law and lying hidden beneath it.
It was sanctioned in an unusual manner of terror and servitude suited to the
condition of the Israelite people and to the age of the church at that time.

[Marginal heading: Cameron’s opinion concerning a threefold covenant.]

II. The celebrated John Cameron appears to have been the first among our
theologians to introduce the opinion concerning a threefold covenant in his
*Theses on the Threefold Covenant*. The very learned Amyraldus embraced it and
attempts laboriously to confirm it in his *Disputation on the Three
Covenants*. Those who follow them give their assent to them and maintain that
there are three covenants properly so called which God contracted with man.

The first is the *natural covenant*, contracted with Adam in his integrity in
the earthly paradise. The second is the *covenant of grace*, revealed in the
gospel to fallen human beings. The third is the *legal covenant*, which was
sanctioned with the Israelites through Moses on Mount Sinai and is called the
old or *subservient* covenant. God entered this covenant with them to prepare
them for the covenant, to kindle in them a desire for the promise and the
evangelical covenant, and at the same time to restrain them from wicked deeds,
as though by applying a bridle, until that time when he would send the Spirit
of adoption and govern them by the law of liberty.

III. They maintain that the nature of this covenant is such that it agrees
with and differs from the covenant of nature and the covenant of grace in
various respects. It agrees with the covenant of nature: 1. with respect to
the *contracting parties*, because in each are God and man; 2. with respect to
the *stipulation* attached to each; and 3. with respect to the *promise*,
which is the same in kind.

It differs: 1. with respect to the *object*, because the covenant of nature
was made with all human beings, but the legal covenant with the Israelites
alone; the former with man in his integrity, but the latter with a portion of
the fallen human race; 2. with respect to the *promise*, which in the first
was a life to be lived in the earthly paradise, but in this covenant a blessed
life in Canaan; 3. with respect to the *stipulation*, for the former bound man
to the obedience owed to the law of nature, but the latter bound him also to
the ceremonies; 4. with respect to the *foundation*, for the covenant of
nature rests upon creation and general preservation, but the legal covenant
upon the election of the Israelite people and their liberation from Egypt; 5.
with respect to the *end*, for the proper end of the former was the demanding
of man’s duty, but that of the latter was preparation for Christ through
conviction of man’s impotence; and 6. with respect to the *Mediator*, for the
former lacked one, but the latter could not be entered without a Mediator.

IV. It agrees with the covenant of grace: 1. in the *Author*, because God is
the author of each; 2. in the *object*, namely, sinful man; 3. in the
*Mediator*, who occurs in each; and 4. in the *end*, because the end of each
is to reveal sin and lead to Christ.

It differs: 1. with respect to the σχέσιν, *relation*, of the *Author*,
because in the legal covenant God is considered simply as one who convicts of
sin and approves righteousness alone, but in the evangelical covenant as one
who pardons sin and renews righteousness in man; 2. with respect to the
*stipulation*, because the stipulation of the former is, *Do this and you will
live*, but that of the latter, *Believe and you will be saved*; and 3. with
respect to the *Mediator*, who in the former was Moses, a mere man, but in the
latter Christ

[Printed p. 232 | Internet Archive PDF 260 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

the θεάνθρωπος, *God-man*. 4. With respect to the *object*, the object
of the former was a man lulled asleep by sin, but that of the latter a
conscience terrified by sin. 5. With respect to the *effects*, the former
generated to bondage, but the latter to liberty; the former led to Christ
indirectly, but the latter directly. The former was a συγγραφὴ, *written
instrument*, against us, but the latter a σκιαγραφία, *sketch*. The former
restrains from sin, but with compulsion; the latter with a voluntary
inclination. See Cameron, *Theses on the Threefold Covenant*.

[Marginal heading: The common opinion of the orthodox concerning a twofold
covenant.]

V. We, however, acknowledge only two covenants specifically distinct from
each other: the *covenant of works*, which promises life to those who do the
works, and the *covenant of grace*, which promises salvation to believers.
Although we confess that the Sinaitic covenant differed from each in the mode
of its administration, we deny that it constituted a third covenant in
substance and species. We maintain that it was nothing other than a new
administration of the covenant of grace, so that it was in reality the same
as the covenant made with Abraham, but differed with respect to accidents and
circumstances.

With respect to its outward administration, it was clothed in the form of the
covenant of works through the law’s severe promulgation. This was not done so
that the covenant of works might be required anew with the sinner, since that
was impossible. It was done so that the violated covenant of works might be
recalled and reproached daily, and thus so that the Israelites might perceive
their sin and God’s curse consequently threatening them, recognize the
impossibility of legal righteousness, and, driven from that hope, thirst all
the more ardently for the righteousness of redemption and be led by the hand
to Christ.

Hence there was in it a mixture of law and gospel: the former to strike
terror into sinners and press down the hard neck of the σκληροτραχήλου,
*stiff-necked*, people, but the latter to raise up and comfort
consciences that had been bruised and stupefied by the sense of sin.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

VI. The question is not whether the cause of salvation was different in the
Old and New Testaments. Those with whom we now deal confess that it was the
same in each and that the fathers were saved in no other way than through
Christ. Nor is the question whether manifold differences occur between this
legal covenant and the evangelical covenant with respect to their
administration. We freely acknowledge this and proved it above.

Rather, the question is whether the difference was essential and such that
the legal covenant must therefore be called a new covenant properly so
called, specifically distinct from both the covenant of nature and the
covenant of grace. They would affirm this, but we deny it.

[Marginal heading: Arguments against a threefold covenant: 1. From Scripture,
which identifies only two covenants;]

VII. The arguments are as follows. 1. Scripture mentions only two covenants,
but nowhere three. It is not for us to distinguish where Scripture does not
distinguish. In Gal. 4:24, under the allegory of Sarah and Hagar, Paul denotes
two covenants: the legal covenant, which generates to bondage, and the
evangelical covenant, which generates to liberty. In Heb. 8, from Jer. 31, two
covenants likewise are recounted: the old, which was violated and made void,
and the new, which is eternal and immutable. Then the apostle repeatedly sets
an antithesis between the law of works and the law of faith, the letter that
kills and the Spirit that gives life. He would not do this with sufficient
precision if some third, intermediate covenant distinct from both existed.

[Marginal heading: 2. From the modes of obtaining blessedness, either through
works or through faith;]

VIII. 2. There can be as many covenants, and no more, as there are ways and
modes of attaining blessedness and communion with God, which is the proper
end of the institution of a covenant. But there are only two such modes:
either through one’s own inherent obedience or through an imputed obedience,
either through works or through faith. The former is through the law, whose
clause is, *Do this and you will live*. The latter is through the gospel,
whose declaration is, *Believe and you will be saved*. No intermediate way of
blessedness can be given. Therefore a third covenant, which pertains neither
to works nor to faith, is invented in vain.

[Printed p. 233 | Internet Archive PDF 261 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: 3. Because the Sinaitic covenant was the covenant of grace;
this is proved 1. from Deuteronomy 7 and 29.]

IX. 3. The Sinaitic covenant is not different from the covenant of grace
previously made with the fathers. This is evident, 1. from Deut. 7:11, 12,
where Moses promises the observance of the covenant made with the fathers in
return for the observance of the law; and from chapter 29, verses 10–13, where
he renews the covenant made at Horeb so that God might be the God of the
people according to the oath made to the fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.
This teaches most clearly that the Sinaitic covenant was made for no other end
than the confirmation of the preceding covenant.

[Marginal heading: 2. From God’s part;]

X. 2. The parties of this covenant prove the same thing. The clause on God’s
part is no different here from the one occurring in the covenant of grace.
For he covenants with the Israelites as their God and likewise as the God of
Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Exod. 3:6; Acts 3:13). This is the very substance
of the covenant of grace on God’s part, which embraces within itself God’s
entire communication toward sinful man to perfect and glorify him (Lev. 26;
Jer. 31; Matt. 22; 2 Cor. 6).

Hence he presents himself not only as the God of his people, but also as their
*Redeemer* (Exod. 20:2); as the merciful *pardoner of sins* (Exod. 34:6, 7);
as the *Sanctifier* who promises the circumcision of the heart (Deut. 30:6,
8); as the *faithful Husband* who exercised marital care for his people
(Ezek. 16:8, 9; Jer. 3:14; Hos. 2:20); as the *kind Father* who begets,
nourishes, and cherishes his children with fatherly love (Deut. 32:6, 8–10;
Isa. 1:2, 3; Hos. 11:1); and as the *gracious King* who willed to place his
sanctuary in the midst of the people so that he might *dwell and walk with
them* (Exod. 25:8; 29:45, 46; Lev. 26:11, 12). That is, he willed to cultivate
communion with them, honoring them with his gracious presence for direction
in distress, protection in dangers, blessing in miseries, and consolation in
sorrows.

Since so many and such great goods cannot be obtained from God except by the
power of the covenant of grace, the Sinaitic covenant, in which he presented
himself in this way, must not have been different from the covenant of grace.

[Marginal heading: 3. From the Israelites’ part;]

XI. 3. The Israelites, who were the other contracting party, sustained no
relation except one that pertains to the covenant of grace. This is true when
they were regarded antecedently in themselves as sinners guilty of death in a
most wretched state, described by a newborn girl lying in her blood (Ezek.
16:4–6). It is also true when they were regarded consequently by grace as the
*seed of Abraham*, to whom the promises had been made in Christ (Gal. 3:16);
as the *people of God*, who had obtained mercy (Hos. 2:1; Rom. 9:25; 1 Pet.
2:10); and as a סְגֻלָּה and λαὸς περιούσιος, a *people of special
possession* (Exod. 19:5), that is, one loved by God above others, which he
claims as his own and tends and guards with great zeal like a treasure of the
most precious things. It is self-evident that all these σχέσεις, *relations*,
pertain to the covenant of grace.

XII. This is confirmed by the fact that the Israelites with whom God
covenants were already under the Abrahamic covenant, which was the covenant
of grace; that is, they were saved in Christ through it. Therefore they could
not have been under a legal covenant. No one can be under two covenants
specifically distinct in their entirety at the same time. Nor does it help to
say that the legal covenant served the covenant of grace, because it could do
this with respect to the administration, but not as a covenant, which is
opposed to and ἀσύστατον, *incompatible with*, grace.

[Marginal heading: 4. From the circumstances of the covenant;]

XIII. 4. There are very many purely evangelical circumstances in this
νομοθεσία, *legislation*. After reading the book of the covenant, Moses
took the blood of the victims and sprinkled the people with it, adding these
words: “This is the blood of the covenant that God made with you” (Exod.
24:8). The apostle teaches in Heb. 9:20 that this was not without mystery. It
was to signify the blood of sprinkling (Heb. 12:24) and the sprinkling of
Christ’s blood, both upon the altar for the satisfaction of justice and the
expiation

[Printed p. 234 | Internet Archive PDF 262 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of sin, and upon the *people* for the application of his merit, the remission
of sin, and justification.

An indubitable argument for this was the elders’ approach to God. They
represented the whole people (Exod. 24), and they not only *saw* him but also
*ate in his presence*. That is, they were admitted to his communion, which
had previously been so severely forbidden to them, and enjoyed his goods.
This could not occur through the legal covenant, which bars a sinner, but
necessarily had to occur through the gospel by the power of Christ’s blood.

Here the twofold manner of legislation that God willed to employ must be
especially observed. The first was terrible, related in Exod. 19, where
barriers are placed around the mountain and access to God lies open to Moses
alone. The other was sweet: the covenant is sanctioned with the blood of
victims, and the elders are called up the mountain with Moses so that they
might enjoy the sight and communion of God (verses 10, 11). The former has a
legal σχέσιν, *relation*, but the latter an evangelical one. These two
σχέσεις, *relations*, are always joined in this administration. The latter
is preferable and principal, but the former accessory and ministerial.

XIV. To this pertains the fact that in Rom. 10:6–8 Paul describes evangelical
righteousness of faith in the same words by which Moses had expressed the
doctrine handed down to the people in the Sinaitic covenant in Deut. 30:11,
12, 14. He does this not merely by allusion and by μίμησιν, *imitation*,
transferring and adapting to the righteousness of faith what had been said
concerning legal righteousness, but properly and by direct proof. This is
because he had designated the righteousness of the law in another way,
namely, that *the one who does these things will live in them*. It is also
because the ease of observing this word does not pertain to the law, which is
a dead letter, but to the gospel, whose word is not only imperative but also
operative through the power of the life-giving Spirit.

Thus the word here must be understood not so much of the law as of the gospel,
in which God there promises pardon to the penitent and the circumcision of the
heart: namely, the “word of faith which we preach,” as the apostle explains in
that passage. “But the righteousness of faith says, Do not say in your heart,
Who will ascend into heaven? etc. For the word is near you, in your heart and
in your mouth,” etc. That is, it is the entire doctrine of salvation, which is
*in the heart* through faith and *in the mouth* through confession. How could
Paul have said this unless he had recognized that this covenant, by whose
power such promises are given, is not so much legal as evangelical?

[Marginal heading: 4. From the ceremonial law, which was wholly typical;]

XV. 4. The ceremonial law, which forms part of this covenant, shows that
everything in this covenant was mystical, typical, representative, and
symbolical of the covenant of grace and all its parts. They were therefore
contained beneath those ceremonies as truth beneath figures (Col. 2:17).
This appeared especially in the sacrifices, which expiated sins in no other
capacity than as types of Christ’s sacrifice. Hence in Lev. 1:9, 13, 17, the
*offering made by fire* is called רֵיחַ נִיחוֹחַ לַיהוָה, *for an odor of
Jehovah’s rest*, that is, a most pleasing odor which causes God, provoked by
sins, to rest and appeases him.

Since this expiation could not occur by the victims’ own power (Heb. 9:9,
10), it must have been effected *through another*, namely, through the Christ
prefigured by them. Nor does it help to say that this worship pertained to
outward purity and immunity from temporal punishments in the earthly forum.
Although this was the use of the ceremonies insofar as they are considered
*absolutely and in themselves*, they had to have another use insofar as they
stood as *figures and images*. In this capacity they stood as moral
instruments by which Old Testament believers, apprehending them by faith,
received the reality together with them, as the apostle attributes to them in
1 Cor. 10:2–4.

[Marginal heading: 5. From the sacraments of the Old Testament;]

XVI. 5. The two sacraments of the covenant of grace already made with the
fathers were joined to the Sinaitic covenant, namely, circumcision and the
Passover. They retained their use under that administration until Christ and
were added as seals for

[Printed p. 235 | Internet Archive PDF 263 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

its sealing (John 7:22, 23; Deut. 16:1, 2, etc.). This could not have occurred
if it had been a third, new covenant specifically distinct in its entirety
from the covenant of grace. For it is absurd to add the sacraments of the
covenant of grace to some other covenant, whether it is called the covenant
of works or the subservient covenant.

[Marginal heading: 6. From an absurdity, because the Abrahamic covenant would
thus be annulled.]

XVII. 6. If the Sinaitic covenant was purely legal, it follows that through
this covenant God annulled the Abrahamic covenant, because a later covenant
annuls an earlier one. Nor could there have been room for another covenant if
the first had always remained in force. But God is so far from having
annulled the former covenant that the Sinaitic covenant was made instead to
confirm it.

Hence the apostle in Gal. 3:17 shows that the law given four hundred thirty
years after the promise given to Abraham could not make the promise void, and
that it was added only because of transgressions until the Seed should come,
to whom the promise had been made (verse 19). This proves that the law sent
people away to Christ as the end toward which it looked, if not in itself and
by its own nature, at least in the Lawgiver’s intention and counsel.

To say that this covenant served the covenant of grace is really to say that
it was not a covenant, but only an administration. For a covenant properly so
called has in itself the power to bind the contracting parties and is not
directed to something else. Therefore, if this administration looked to
something else, as it actually did, this is an argument that it was not a
covenant different in species, but only a different mode of administration
accommodated to the time, place, and condition of the persons.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XVIII. The *Mosaic covenant* can be regarded in two ways: either according to
God’s intention and aim and in its order toward Christ, or separately and in
abstraction from him. In the latter mode it is really distinguished from the
covenant of grace because it coincides with the covenant of works. In that
sense it is called the letter that kills and the ministry of condemnation
when its nature is treated (2 Cor. 3:6, 7).

But it is wrongly abstracted in this way, because it must always be considered
with God’s intention. His intention was not that man should have life from the
law or that the sinner should simply be condemned, but that from the
recognition of his own misery and weakness he should flee to Christ.

XIX. Although in Jer. 31 the Sinaitic and legal covenant is opposed to the new
covenant, the opposition need not concern their essence. It can concern their
accidents, or the difference of their administrations, just as a man is
opposed to himself as standing and sitting. Nor, if remission is promised
under the new covenant, does it follow that the fathers lacked it under the
old, any more than it can legitimately be inferred from God’s promise that he
will be the God of believers under the New Testament that he was not the God
of the fathers. These promises must not be understood absolutely and simply,
but relatively and comparatively with respect to greater clarity and
efficacy.

Then, if a specific opposition is pressed, the covenant is regarded not in
its entire breadth, but only with respect to its legal σχέσιν, *relation*,
and as contradistinguished from the gospel. The purpose is to designate what
the law can or cannot do precisely and in itself when separated from the
gospel.

XX. The Sinaitic covenant was not without Christ, because 1. Moses testified
about him (John 5:45, 46; Luke 24:44; Acts 3:22, 23; 26:22, 23). 2. He is
contained implicitly under the promise of a special possession, a royal
priesthood, and a holy nation (Exod. 19:5, 6, compared with 1 Pet. 2:9, 10),
because without Christ sinners cannot obtain these privileges, as was said
before. 3. He is contained in the preface to the Decalogue, where God is
presented as the God of the people and their Liberator from Egypt, which
could occur only through Christ. 4. He is contained in the sanction of the
covenant through the blood of victims, which foreshadowed Christ’s blood. 5.
He is contained in the legal types and shadows, which were directed
principally to no other than Christ. 6. Christ was the principal aim and soul
of the law,

[Printed p. 236 | Internet Archive PDF 264 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

without whom it is nothing other than a corpse and a letter that kills (Rom.
10:4; Gal. 3:24).

XXI. *The law is said not to be of faith* in Gal. 3:12, not when it is taken
*broadly* and denotes the Mosaic administration, but when it is taken
*strictly* for the moral law abstracted and separated from the promises of
grace, in the manner in which the legalists understood it when they sought
life from it.

For it is proved that faith was commanded even in the Sinaitic covenant. 1.
Christ its object was contained in it. 2. God cannot be worshiped as the first
commandment requires without faith, which purifies hearts, is effectual
through love, and renders us pleasing to God (Acts 15:9; Heb. 11:6). 3. By
convicting man of his sin and impotence, the Sinaitic covenant impelled him
to seek Christ through faith. For through the law, “God confined all things
under sin, so that the promise through faith in Jesus Christ might be given
to all who believe” (Gal. 3:22), and “the law was our schoolmaster unto Christ,
that we might be justified through faith” (verse 24). 4. In Rom. 10:6, 7 Paul
describes the righteousness of faith in the same words by which Moses had
declared the precepts of the law in Deut. 30:11–13, to teach that they are not
simply legal but also evangelical.

Nor, if *faith* is said to be revealed in Gal. 3:23—that is,
*comparatively*, with respect to its clearer manifestation—does it follow that
it did not exist absolutely under the Old Testament.

XXII. The *difference of the condition* expressed in the law and in the
gospel cannot constitute the specific difference of the covenant: the
condition of the former is perfect obedience, but that of the latter is
faith. They were not required in the same way or for the same end. Faith in
Christ was required principally and was chiefly intended. But perfect
obedience under the penalty of death and the curse was required only
subordinately and in relation to faith and the righteousness of faith,
because by convicting man of his sin and impotence it compelled him to seek
the remedy in Christ through faith, as was just said.

Then these two conditions are proposed because they are necessary for the
salvation of sinners: perfect obedience in Christ to fulfill τὸ δικαίωμα τοῦ
νόμου, *the requirement of the law*, without which God’s justice did not
allow life to be given to us; and faith in us, so that Christ’s perfect
obedience and satisfaction might be applied to us and become ours by
imputation. Thus what was demanded from us in the covenant of works is
fulfilled through Christ in the covenant of grace. Nor is it absurd in this
way for justification to occur through works and through faith: through
Christ’s works and through our faith.

Thus the law and the gospel concur together in this covenant in sweet
harmony. The law is not administered without the gospel, nor the gospel
without the law, so that there is, as it were, a legal gospel and an
evangelical law: the gospel full of obedience and the law full of faith. Thus
the gospel does not destroy the law but establishes it (Rom. 3:31) by giving
us Christ, who perfectly fulfilled it. And the law is not contrary to the
gospel, since it looks to the gospel as its end and leads us to it by the
hand.

XXIII. Although Moses can in some sense be called a Mediator in the Sinaitic
covenant because he discharged the role of an intermediary, standing between
Jehovah and the people (Deut. 5:1), nevertheless he could not reconcile the
people to God. It does not follow that the covenant of which he is called the
Mediator was specifically different from the covenant of grace, in which such
reconciliation is obtained. He was only a typical and outward Mediator, not
the primary and true one, from whom alone—his suretyship having been rendered
and accepted by God—the covenant received all its power and efficacy for the
reconciliation of sinners, their liberation from the eternal curse, and the
gift of heavenly life.

According to the σχέσιν, *relation*, of intermediary that he sustained,
Moses could indeed in his own manner give effect to the covenant to some
extent. He acted as the interpreter of the parties’ wills so that through his
intervention the covenant itself might be entered into by their consent. But
he could not go further and effect reconciliation by himself. This belonged
only to Christ, who had to be the εἰρηνοποιός, *peacemaker*.

[Printed p. 237 | Internet Archive PDF 265 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

XXIV. The promise of the land of Canaan given to the people was not primary
and principal, but only secondary and less important. It was added by way of
προσθήκης, *addition*, as an accession to the primary promise that God
would be their God, and indeed as a pledge and symbol of heavenly Canaan, of
which it was a type (Heb. 4), and to which the fathers looked (Heb. 11:15,
16). Therefore, from the difference between this promise and the one given to
us in the gospel, a specific difference of the covenant cannot properly be
inferred, but only a difference of administration. The other distinctions
between the Old and New Testaments introduced here concerning the Spirit of
bondage and so forth, which were treated above, also entail such a difference
of administration.

XXV. It is one thing to speak of the law in itself. It possessed the form of
the covenant of works and was given not for the end of giving life, but to
reproach transgressions, exact a confession of debt, and lead to Christ by the
hand. It is another thing to speak of the Sinaitic covenant itself, in which
the law was given. In the former sense the law is called the handwriting
against us and the ministry of condemnation (2 Cor. 3; Col. 2). But in the
latter sense that covenant possessed λόγια ζῶντα, *living oracles* (Acts
7:38), and contained the saving promises of Christ’s grace.
