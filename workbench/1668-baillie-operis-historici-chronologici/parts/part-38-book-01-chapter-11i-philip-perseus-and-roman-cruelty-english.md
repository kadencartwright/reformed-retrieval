In order to acquire a reputation for restraint and justice no less than for
courage, the Romans retained absolutely nothing for themselves either in Asia
or in Greece. But they did not long persevere in this virtue—or hypocrisy—for
they soon harassed all the Greeks and Asians with an exceedingly proud,
greedy, and cruel tyranny.

> Marginalia, printed p. 223: *The Romans’ hypocrisy*.

They began with Philip. He had observed all the conditions of peace exactly
and had rendered faithful service to the Scipios and the other commanders
against Antiochus and the Aetolians. For that reason the remainder of his fine
was remitted and his son Demetrius, who was a hostage, was returned to him as
a true friend. Nevertheless, he was afterward afflicted by so many insults
and injuries that he perceived clearly that the Romans coveted his kingdom.
To forestall them he prepared for war as diligently and secretly as he could.
He solicited even distant barbarian nations beyond the Ister, won the Greeks’
affections for himself through the Romans’ arrogance, and had laid up an
enormous sum of money and provisions for ten years’ necessities of war. But a
domestic calamity threw all his plans into confusion. While his younger son
Demetrius lived at Rome, his great virtue had won the goodwill of the
senators. Perseus, the elder son, envied his brother this favor with the
Romans and feared him. When many neighbors brought many complaints about
Philip in the Roman Senate, he sent his son Demetrius back to Rome to answer
for him. The Senate declared that for Demetrius’s sake it forgave every
offense of his father. Philip took it badly that his son had more favor than
he did in the Roman Senate. Then, incited by anger and Perseus’s false
accusations that Demetrius had conspired against both his own life and
Perseus’s, the father arranged for him to be killed. But when soon afterward
he discovered the young man’s innocence—too late, after his death—and
Perseus’s slanders, Philip died of anguish, breathing vengeance against
Perseus.

Perseus immediately took possession of the kingdom and obtained from the
Romans confirmation of his father’s treaty. Nevertheless, he persisted in
his father’s hatred of the Romans, making all his preparations for war with
as much diligence as he could. He married Seleucus’s daughter and gave his
own sister in marriage to Prusias the Bithynian. By kindness and various good
offices he bound the Achaeans, Aetolians, Rhodians, and all the Greeks so
closely to himself that, although fear of the Romans kept them from daring to
join arms with him, their disposition toward him was much more favorable than
toward the Romans.

Perseus would most gladly have continued at peace with the Romans, but when
the Senate’s injustice and arrogance made war necessary for him, for two
years he opposed forty thousand Macedonians to two consuls and protected the
boundaries of his kingdom against all the Romans’ force and skill. He was
often close to complete victory, had not his innate fear and greed ruined all
his hopes. He had hired twenty thousand brave men from the Bastarnae, ten
thousand of whom were cavalry. Offended by Perseus’s utterly foolish
stinginess, they returned home. Gentius, king of the Illyrians, had fifteen
thousand men under arms ready to serve Perseus, but Perseus wished to preserve
his treasures untouched for the Romans and lost this aid too through lack of
pay.

In the third year of the war the consul Aemilius Paullus, a prudent and brave
man, defeated Perseus within a few days. Conquered chiefly by his own fear,
Perseus fled to the sanctuary on Samothrace. When all his friends had deserted
him, he finally submitted himself to the consul’s judgment. With his two sons
and Gentius, king of the Illyrians, he was led in chains through the streets
of Rome before the triumphal chariot. He and his son Philip died in prison,
starved to death according to some and deprived of sleep according to others.
His other son, Alexander, first practiced the carpenter’s trade and afterward
that of a clerk at Rome until the end of his life. Thus the entire line of
Macedonian kings perished.

> Marginalia, printed p. 223: *The extinction of the kings of Macedonia*.

After Hannibal, Antiochus, and Perseus had been defeated, the Romans feared
no other enemy and everywhere harassed their friends with arrogant cruelty.
Aemilius Paullus summoned the leading men from sixty-five Epirote cities and
ordered them to hand over all their money to him. He sent his soldiers home
with them as though to receive it. Under this pretext the Romans entered all
the Epirote cities in peace, but then, on a day appointed by the consul, they
rose in arms against the defenseless inhabitants, carried out an enormous
slaughter of innocent people, plundered the cities, and

sold one hundred and fifty thousand people into slavery under the crown. Thus
they avenged Pyrrhus’s war against themselves.

In the public assembly of the Achaeans the Roman envoys named one thousand
leading citizens, whom they ordered to go to Rome and clear themselves before
the Senate of suspicion that they favored the Macedonians. These noble and
innocent men remained for many years in Roman imprisonment and almost all
died in chains. Among them was the historian Polybius, who was released with
difficulty after seven years’ imprisonment.

> Marginalia, printed p. 224: *And upon the Achaeans*.

Because the Rhodians had appealed to the Senate through envoys for peace with
Perseus, after the war they were not only harshly rebuked: the Senate would
even have declared war upon them, had it not learned that the Rhodians
themselves had cruelly killed all those who were more devoted to their country
than to the Romans. Because Eumenes had conducted himself rather generously
toward Perseus, the Romans all but expelled him from his own kingdom. In all
the provinces the Roman beast began to display and employ its iron teeth.
