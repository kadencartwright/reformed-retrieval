Up to this point we have dealt with persons who possess the supreme
administration. There follow persons who possess

a less extensive administration, one that is either judicial or nonjudicial.
The judicial is either established by law or not introduced by law. That
established by law is the administration of ordinary judges. Under the prince
these exercise it either in their own name or in another's. Those who exercise
it in their own name are bishops so called with an added designation, as
archbishops, or bishops simply.

The archbishops, of whom there are two in England, Canterbury and York, are
considered either with respect to their particular dioceses, altogether as the
other bishops are, or with respect to the whole province, according to the
place they hold in the ecclesiastical estate or in the civil estate. In the
ecclesiastical estate they are considered according to the place they hold,
either that common to both archbishops or that peculiar to Canterbury. The
place common to both archbishops is considered either in ordination itself or
after ordination.

In ordination itself, if they were not bishops before, they are consecrated by
four bishops. If they were, their election is confirmed by four bishops.

A bishop, even if he was previously a presbyter, is consecrated anew; an
archbishop, even if he was previously a bishop, is not consecrated anew. In the
practice of the hierarchists, therefore, bishop and presbyter are not of the
same order. For if they were of the same order, as bishop and archbishop are, a
bishop who was previously a presbyter would no more be consecrated than an
archbishop who was previously a bishop. According to the ancient canons, an
archbishop's election was made by all the bishops of the province, the clergy,
and the people; and election, confirmation, and ordination or consecration were
morally one act. The bishops of the province who elected

[1623 printed p. 50 | IA leaf 62 | PDF p. 62]

also confirmed—not merely three or four—and those who confirmed also
consecrated. “And it is certain,” says Spalato, “that the bishops who were the
consecrators were also the ordinary supervisors of elections, confirmers and
approvers of those elected, and consequently their examiners and
investigators.” Anicetus writes to the bishops of Gaul: “That which was
previously stated and commanded concerning the consecration of an archbishop,
namely, that all his suffragans are to ordain him, must in no way be altered;
for one who presides over others ought to be appointed by all those over whom
he presides. If anything else is presumptuously done, there is no doubt that it
lacks force, because the ordination performed otherwise will be void.” Gelasius
establishes the same: “And conversely, if a metropolitan should happen to die
by the lot of the human condition, take care by every means that he is
consecrated by the bishops of the province, as the prescribed form has handed
down; and do not allow anyone to claim for himself what was granted to you by
ancient ordinance.” Likewise Leo I: “When a metropolitan has died and another
must be substituted in his place, the bishops of the province ought to
assemble at the metropolitan city, so that, after the will of all the clergy
and citizens has been considered, the best deacon among the presbyters of that
church may be ordained.” Thus the final words read in Gregory XIII's edition.
The Council of Orléans also commands that the metropolitan be elected by the
bishops of the province with the consent of the clergy and citizens, “because
it is right that one who is to be set over all should be elected by all.”
Balsamon, on chapter 2 of the Council of Constantinople, says: “Observe,
therefore, from this canon that formerly all the metropolitans of the provinces
were self-headed and were ordained by their own synods.” We do not insist upon
confirmation of a bishop by a patriarch, although they acknowledge the Roman
pontiff as patriarch of the West. We do say that the older custom of the
church—electing, confirming, and ordaining in synods of bishops—has become
obsolete and was not restored by the English; and this shows that they employ
the form not of the ancient polity but of the more recent human one. The rites
for consecrating archbishops and bishops are the same; we shall discuss them
below.

After ordination, they are considered insofar as they are metropolitans,
archbishops, or primates—that is, lesser patriarchs.

Mocket comments as follows on this variety of titles: “It is true that today
our archbishops and primates in England have only bishops subject to them, yet
they are adorned with the titles of both archbishop and primate and discharge
the offices of both of these as well as that of metropolitan.” Let the
hierarchists who wish so strongly to commend to us the painted splendor of the
hierarchical order see how they themselves weaken it. They celebrate the
wondrous skill and prudence of the ancients in fabricating the ladder of
hierarchical degrees, yet by their deed they attribute folly

Distinction 54, chapter 6; Distinction 63, chapter 19, *Metropolitano*; Third
Council of Orléans, canon 3; *On the Polity of the English Church*, chapter 4.]

and imprudence to them. For what need was there of so many degrees if one
metropolitan in his province, without any further subordination of degrees,
could discharge three offices—or rather, by virtue of one office, fulfill all
the duties which bishops of three distinct degrees were bound to exercise?
Matthias Stephani settles as follows the dispute between the experts in
imperial and canon law over whether a metropolitan and an archbishop are of
the same degree. He says that some archbishops are the same as metropolitans,
such as those of Milan and Ravenna, and so forth; but that other archbishops
have not only bishops but also metropolitans subject to them, such as those of
Pisa, Aquileia, Grado, and the like. Downham says that an archbishop holds a
middle place between a metropolitan and a patriarch. More correctly, John
Rainolds, the most learned of the English, proves from the *Novels*, in his
conference with Hart, that an archbishop was formerly the same as a patriarch
of a diocese. Above, in Scaliger's *Description*, we saw that the exarch of a
diocese was also called its patriarch. The word *diocese* must not be taken in
the sense commonly used among us, but as the administration of several
provinces. Thus the patriarchs and exarchs of these larger dioceses were the
first to be called archbishops, not the metropolitans subject to them. The word
*archbishop* was used at the Council of Chalcedon, but only to designate the
most celebrated patriarchs, not the metropolitans who occupied lower seats. By
the synod's concession, appeals could be made from the metropolitans to those
patriarchs as well as to the exarch of the larger diocese. In a compound, the
word *archē* can signify primacy among the rest, not rule over others of the
same order. Yet what must be considered is the persons upon whom names are
imposed and the reason for doing so, not the etymon and the proper meaning of
the word. Since in a compound it also signifies rule over others, and not only
primacy among others to designate the first in order or the chief among the
rest of the same order, and since it is attributed in the former sense to
patriarchs—who possessed rule over other bishops and metropolitans, from whom
appeals to the patriarchs were permitted by canons and laws—we say that it is
an ostentatious word and breathes the smoke of the age. Indeed, those who first
imposed it did not deign to communicate it to metropolitans. The name *exarch*,
most modest in itself and signifying an initiator of common deliberations, was
nevertheless rejected by the African fathers at the Council of Carthage. “The
bishop of the first see is not to be called the exarch of the priests, the
prince of priests, the chief priest, or anything of that kind, but only the
bishop of the first see.” Gratian adds, “Nor is even the Roman pontiff to be
called universal,” lest anyone think the preceding words were to be understood
of the Roman pontiff. Saravia says that Longinus brought into Italy

[Marginalia: *On Jurisdiction*, book 3, part 1, chapter 7; *Defense*, book 2,
chapter 1, p. 17; Distinction 99, chapter 3, *Primæ*; *On Degrees*, chapter 25,
section 14.]

[1623 printed p. 52 | IA leaf 64 | PDF p. 64]

a new name of government, full of Greek pride, so that he might be called
exarch. He also admits it likely that the fathers feared that with the name the
bishops would assume the Roman bishop's equal power over their colleagues. But
we have shown from Gratian that the words of the canon are not to be understood
of Roman bishops. The name *archbishop* was communicated to metropolitans, but
later. “I see,” says Filesac, the Parisian theologian, “that the title
*archbishop* was received in the West only at a late date.” In the same place:
“The name *archbishop* was first used in these regions at the First Council of
Mâcon, canon 6; our people abstained from that word for a long time and used it
rarely.” Where Hildebald is mentioned in the preface of the Council of Mainz
under Charlemagne as archbishop of the Sacred Palace, Filesac maintains that
*archchaplain* should be read, and adduces Nicolas Le Fèvre in support. Others
have observed that Ambrose's work *On the Dignity of Priests* is either
spurious or interpolated. When appeals from bishops and clerics to the
metropolitan began, and he who had formerly been only first in order at
assemblies began also to be superior in degree of power and jurisdiction—the
synod's power having been absorbed and transferred to him—the metropolitan
was then everywhere adorned with the more august name of archbishop. You see,
therefore, that the practice of appeals has almost always followed this word.
On account of appeals across the sea, moreover, the African fathers said that
the Roman pontiff wished to introduce the smoky pomp of the age into the
church. With an inflated spirit Tilenus defends the inflated titles of
archbishops and patriarchs. “As if,” he says, “pride resided in signs and
words, and as if the church will at last fare best when its pastors cast away
the titles of bishops, archbishops, and primates and assume the names of those
little brothers who delight to be called Minors and Minims. Apparently it is
not only Plato's Diogenes, but also your ministers, who trample the pride of
bishops with another pride.” Who but some madman will deny that pride can
inhere in signs and words? We have shown that it truly inheres in the names of
archbishop, exarch, and patriarch; that they were first imposed only upon the
most celebrated patriarchs and the exarchs who occupied the vicars' seats; and
that *archē* indicates rule over others who are inferior, even the
metropolitans themselves. Nor was it everywhere communicated to metropolitans
until they were raised to a superior degree of power and jurisdiction. We
retain the names “bishop,” “presbyter,” and “president,” and we retain them in
the sense the Holy Spirit taught us. If anyone were to say that pride inheres
in the titles and nomenclature of Scripture, he would be impious. We condemn
Diogenic pride in persons wherever we detect it. But who are you to judge
another man's servant and perceive Diogenic pride concealed in the soul?
Everyone who knows Tilenus knows that you measure men conspicuous for piety
and true modesty by your own character.

*Paraenesis*, chapter 8.]

We also avoid the hypocritical titles and nomenclature of Minors and Minims.
Bishops sometimes styled themselves “servants of servants,” as Filesac shows
by examples. Paul, bishop of Aquileia, calls himself “the least servant of the
servants of the Lord.” Diederik, bishop of Metz, Boniface of Mainz, Maurus,
bishop of Ravenna, and other bishops in letters sent to Boniface or others
inscribe themselves “servants of the servants of God” or “attendants of the
attendants of God.” “And when Greek bishops speak of themselves,” he says,
“they use a very similar form of humility, ‘our mediocrity.’” Others also
subscribed themselves by the name *sinner*, as did Prætextatus of Rouen,
Germanus of Paris, and others at the First Council of Tours. Meursius also
observes in his glossary that the word *holiness* was used in speech and in
letters to patriarchs and bishops; that in letters a patriarch called
metropolitans and archbishops simply “most holy,” while they called one another
“all-holy.” Thus Tilenus has the Diogenic pride of little brothers, Minors, and
Minims in his own bishops and archbishops. But let us proceed. According to
Zecchius, primates regularly preside over three metropolitans, as kings over
three dukes. John Beleth similarly says: “Primates who preside, or are able to
preside, over three archbishops represent a king, whose power extends to three
duchies. Metropolitans or archbishops are compared to dukes, who preside over
several counts; for archbishops too are said to preside over several bishops.
Bishops bear the likeness of counts.” When the metropolitans' power was
increased, they were also enlarged by the title of archbishop. According to
present usage, the patriarchs are the four most celebrated—the Antiochene,
Roman, Alexandrian, and Constantinopolitan—and the fifth, the honorary
patriarch of Jerusalem. In addition, six patriarchs of lesser peoples are
numbered, adorned with this title by privilege and custom: Aquileia, Grado,
Bourges, Toledo, Pisa, and Canterbury; some add Magdeburg. Matthias Stephani
nevertheless maintains that the name *patriarch* can also be communicated to
metropolitans. “Metropolitans too,” he says, “can in a certain way be called
patriarchs, although they are not true patriarchs, and likewise primates,
although it is certain that appeals can be made from the metropolitans to
them.”
Properly, therefore, primates and patriarchs are those to whom appeals from
metropolitans are permitted. But perhaps enough about words.

2. In consecrating bishops of this kind together with two other bishops.

What we said above about the election, confirmation, and consecration of
archbishops is also true of the election, confirmation, and consecration of
bishops. For a bishop was elected in the presence of both the metropolitan and
the other bishops of the province, although today, says Duarenus, they
customarily approach the metropolitan after the election; and he maintains
that the Nicene canon is not to be understood of the confirmation practiced
among us. “No reason permits men to be reckoned among the bishops who were
neither elected by the clerics, sought by the people, nor consecrated by the
bishops of the province with the metropolitan's judgment,” says Leo. If they
could conveniently assemble, all the bishops of the province were bound to
participate in the election and ordination by canon 4 of the Council of Nicæa;
the consent of those who were absent was required by letter. “But it is
better,” says Pope Lucius, “if he himself, namely the metropolitan, together
with all the rest, elects the man who is worthy, and all alike consecrate him
as pontiff.” But it is always difficult, never better, for an English
archbishop to convene a provincial synod of bishops and by one and the same
labor accomplish the election, confirmation, and consecration—doubtless
because the custom is like that of the papal metropolitans, whom they eagerly
imitate, and not those ancient metropolitans. Although in the age of the Nicene
Synod the provinces were few and very extensive, as Spalato says, the Nicene
Synod nevertheless ordained that all the bishops of the province be present.
If anyone could not conveniently attend, he was to signify his consent by
letters. The metropolitan was nevertheless bound to notify them all, although
the provinces were very extensive. After the Council of Nicæa, when the
provinces were multiplied and they could then assemble more easily, the canons
concerning meetings of synods and the synodical election and consecration of
bishops were also multiplied. The Council of Antioch says: “A bishop is in no
way to be ordained apart from a synod and the metropolitan's presence. Once
this has been arranged in every respect, it is better that all the priests of
the same province whom the metropolitan bishop will summon by letter be
present together.” See also Anacletus and Pope Martin in Gratian's *Decree*, as
well as the synodical book of the Constantinopolitans in Theodoret's
*Ecclesiastical History*, where the sanction of the Nicene fathers is said to
have always remained in force. But the English bishops prefer to idle
luxuriously at court or in their episcopal palaces than to restore the fallen
discipline of the ancients, much less the primitive and apostolic one. By
divine law the consecration of bishops no more belongs to metropolitans than to
other bishops. Spalato maintains that by divine law any bishop can be ordained
and consecrated by any bishop,

3, chapter 5, section 1.]

but that human ecclesiastical law does not permit it, in order to avoid
confusion. “But the provinces,” he says, “have been divided according to the
secular, civil, and geographical divisions, so that the bishops of each
province would ordain the bishops of their own province. And this care has
especially been assigned to the metropolitan of the province, whose first and
chief institution was on account of the ordinations already mentioned.” We
showed above from Jerome that the presbyters elected one of their own number
and, after he was established in a higher degree, called him bishop. In the
second century of the primitive church synods were rarer. Thus bishops were
then consecrated by other bishops who were not yet metropolitans. That other
bishops can consecrate a bishop with the metropolitan's permission is evident
from the first book of the *Decretals*. Indeed, that men consecrated without
the metropolitan's consent and knowledge are nevertheless lawful bishops is
evident from Pope Hilary's rescript to the bishops of Tarragona, in which he
dispenses with the absence of the metropolitan's knowledge and consent in a
bishop's ordination. But neither could metropolitans delegate or permit this
duty to others, nor could a pope dispense with it, if the power of ordaining
belonged to metropolitans by divine law. Sylvester Giraldus writes in the
*Topography of Ireland*: “There had been no archbishops in Ireland, but the
bishops merely consecrated one another, until John Paparo, legate of the Roman
see, arrived not many years ago. He brought four palliums into Ireland.” See
Filesac. It is also evident from the Anglo-Saxon Bede that presbyters sometimes
consecrated bishops on the island of St. Columba. “The island itself,” says
Bede, “is accustomed always to have as its ruler an abbot who is a presbyter,
to whose authority both the whole province and even the bishops themselves
ought, by an unusual arrangement, to be subject, according to the example of
its first teacher, who was not a bishop but a presbyter and monk.” And in the
same place: “From this island, therefore, from this college of monks, Aidan was
sent to instruct the province of the English in Christ, having received the
degree of bishop, at which time Segenius, abbot and presbyter, presided over
that monastery.” Filesac gathers from the Fourth Council of Orléans and Fourth
Council of Carthage that the metropolitan's presence is not necessary in the
consecration of bishops and that it is enough if it occurs with his consent.
Henry Spencer, bishop of Norwich, was consecrated by the archdeacon of Norwich,
as Godwin of Llandaff reports from Neville. William of York was consecrated at
Wilton as bishop of Salisbury by Fulk, bishop of London, in 1247. Under English
law, if the archbishop refuses, either the other archbishop or any other bishop
can be consecrated by other bishops. Canisius writes in his *Summary of Canon
Law*: “A bishop is consecrated by at least three bishops of the same province
at the metropolitan's command, while the other absent bishops give their
consent, although it would be better if the archbishop himself

discharged this ministry of consecration together with his suffragans.” Thus,
if the first and chief institution of the metropolitan is on account of the

As for the rites of consecration, they are presented as follows in the book of
ordination which Mocket clothed with Latin expression. “When the Gospel and
Creed have been completed,” he says, “the elect will be presented by two other
bishops of that province to the archbishop, or to another bishop acting in his
place with his consent” (you see that the presence of the archbishop himself is
not necessarily required), “with this prescribed formula: ‘Most reverend
father in Christ, we present to you this godly and learned man to be
consecrated as bishop.’ Then the archbishop will see to it that the royal
mandate for his consecration is produced and publicly read. Next, the oath
acknowledging the king's supreme power will be administered to the elect.
Finally, he is to take the oath to render due obedience to the archbishop in
the following manner: ‘In the name of God, Amen. I, N., bishop-elect of the
church and see of N., profess and promise all due reverence and obedience to
the archbishop and metropolitan church of N. and his successors. So help me
God through Jesus Christ.’ Then, after exhorting the assembly present to
implore divine aid, the archbishop will address them as follows: ‘It is written,
brothers, in the Gospel of St. Luke, that Christ our Savior spent the whole
night in prayer before he chose his apostles,’ and so forth. Next, as in the
ordination of a deacon, the litany is sung. After these words—‘that you would
appoint bishops, pastors, and ministers of the church,’ and so forth—a short
petition with its response is added. The litany concludes with this prayer:
‘Almighty God, giver of all good things, who by your Holy Spirit have appointed
various orders of ministers in your church,’ and so forth. Then the archbishop,
sitting in his chair, asks the man standing before him to be consecrated the
principle upon which he would undertake this ministry and how faithfully he
will discharge it. Here ‘Come, Creator Spirit,’ and so forth, is sung or
recited. Then he prays. When the brief prayer is ended, the archbishop and the
bishops present lay their hands upon the head of the bishop-elect, as the
archbishop says: ‘Receive the Holy Spirit, and remember to stir up the grace of
God that is in you through the laying on of hands; for God has given us a
spirit not of fear but of power, love, and sobriety.’ The archbishop then takes
the sacred book and hands it to him with these words: ‘Attend to reading,
exhortation, and doctrine.’ The archbishop now prepares himself for the Holy
Supper, in which the newly consecrated bishop and the others will commune with
him. In the manner of a final collect immediately before the blessing, he
concludes with this brief prayer: ‘Most merciful Father, we humbly beseech
you,’ and so forth.” For brevity's sake I have passed over the collects,
litanies, lessons, questions, and prayers themselves. Almost the same order is
observed as in the papacy according to the directions of the Roman
Pontifical.

the consecrator by the supreme pontiff through an oral pronouncement, if the
consecrator himself is a cardinal.” A bishop's consecration ought to take place

two are present, dressed in surplice or rochet. The consecrator sits; the elect,
between the assisting mitered bishops and wearing his biretta and cope, is led
before the consecrator. Here likewise he is led between two assistants. There
the senior assistant turns to the consecrator and says, “Reverend father,” and
so forth. So too here. There the apostolic mandate is read, which the
consecrator's notary receives from the bishop's hand and reads from beginning
to end. So too here. There the elect, kneeling before the consecrator, takes
the oath of fidelity while touching the Gospels. So too here. There the
consecrator, standing with his miter, places the book of the Gospels upon the
neck and shoulders of the elect, while a chaplain supports it, until it is to
be delivered into the elect's hands. A petition to the queen likewise says
that the consecrator ought to place the book of the Gospels upon the elect's

be consecrated with both hands, saying, “Receive the Holy Spirit.” So too here.
There the consecrator gives the kiss of peace to the consecrated man, and he to
his assistants. So too here: together they celebrate the Lord's Supper. The
lessons, litanies, hymns, collects, Gospels, and examination are the same or
nearly the same. There the consecrator, sitting with his miter, puts gloves on
the consecrated man's hands. Here consecrated bishops distribute gloves to
their friends who served as attendants; for the bishop's marriage to his bride,
the church, has now been consummated. In standing, sitting, and kneeling they
observe nearly the same changes as the papists. You see, reader, that the man
to be consecrated takes an oath of canonical obedience, which I think did not
creep into the church before bishops became the pope's sworn clients. Bishops
who were to be consecrated did indeed issue a profession of catholic and
orthodox faith, so that it might be evident that they did not dissent from the
other bishops; they confirmed it by an oath, which the pontiffs and
metropolitans afterward distorted into an oath of obedience. You also see how
the metropolitan by the laying on of hands gives the Holy Spirit, in imitation

of Christ, who after the resurrection stood in the disciples' midst, breathed

3. In convening provincial synods according to a royal rescript.

4. In governing synods and casting the final vote.

We showed above that the duty of convening synods was transferred to the
metropolitan by the bishops' voluntary surrender and does not belong to him by
divine law. Even after the power was transferred, the metropolitan was not
free to convene or not to convene them. It was decreed that provincial synods
be celebrated twice each year, and the times of the year were appointed for
them by canon 5 of the Council of Nicæa. The metropolitan announced the day of
the month and the place, and besides the ordinary synods he also convened
others out of course, whether for ordinations of bishops or for other causes.
For although this prerogative was entrusted to the metropolitan at the Council
of Antioch, with this reason added—“because all those from every quarter who
seem to have business converge upon the metropolis”—it is nevertheless evident
from the Council of Tours and the Third Council of Toledo that the place was
chosen. The experience of the Reformed churches teaches that no metropolitan
is needed to convene provincial synods. In the Scottish church, where there
were no bishops or metropolitans, more national councils have been celebrated
since the first Reformation than provincial councils in England for the last
three hundred years, where they boast of the long and interrupted succession of
archbishops. What prevents the prince's rescript from being directed to someone
other than the metropolitan? So far is an archbishop from being needed to
convene provincial councils that the church seems unable to recover this
liberty unless he is abolished. The English hierarchists do not have true and
lawful councils; even hierarchical synods of bishops, deans, archdeacons, and
men of that kind are exceedingly rare. Bilson lays the blame upon the prince,
and at other times upon the difficulty of the matter itself, because the
bishops are separated from one another by long distances. But I ask whether
they ever earnestly asked the prince to grant by rescript the power of
convening, to divide the dioceses, and to reduce the territorial extent of
their power by dividing it among more counties, so that they might assemble in
synods more conveniently.
Gregory writes to Augustine, bishop of the English: “We wish your fraternity
to ordain bishops in such a way that the bishops themselves are not separated
from one another by long distances, so that it will never be impossible for
them to assemble at the ordination of a bishop.” But it is not in their hearts
to draw in the hems which they have extended so widely.

Bellarmine attacks the aristocratic government of bishops with this argument.
If the church's government were aristocratic, the church would almost always
lack rulers, nor would there be anyone charged with the common good; for all
the prelates are posited as equals among themselves and cannot care for the
common good unless they either assemble or by common consent elect some
magistrate to perform this duty. But councils assemble very rarely and with
the greatest inconvenience. Spalato answers: “No magistrate is needed where
all labor jointly toward one end; nor is their general assembly so frequently
necessary except in the gravest causes of the whole church. Provincial synods,
however, just as they ought, can easily be very frequent; and while the
individual provinces are governed by good government, the whole universal
church remains excellently governed.” Field says nearly the same. For he says
that provincial councils were formerly convened twice each year, as well as
for the ordination of bishops, and that the metropolitan was bound by canons
to convene synods. But unless he is perpetually bound and the church is
ordinarily governed by provincial synods of bishops, the government of the
bishops will not be that aristocratic government which the apostles commended
to the churches. Consequently Bellarmine's argument is not answered unless
fixed provincial councils exist as they formerly did, which was Field's
answer. Gerson complains and asks, “Where are the provincial synods to be
celebrated once or twice a year according to the fathers' constitutions?” The
English archbishops will answer that synods are not needed. We and our shadows,
the archdeacons, chancellors, and officials, will see that the canons hitherto
enacted are put into execution. Bishops have no need of a senate of presbyters,
nor do we need a synodical assembly of bishops: we are monarchs.

It is evident from the synods of the second century that the presidency of
provincial councils was formerly mutable, for there we showed that presidents
were not permanent but elected. If we wish to preserve intact the equality
that ought to exist among nobles equal in power, the nature of aristocratic
government requires a mutable and temporary presidency. A permanent
presidency furnishes an occasion for ambitious contests and disputes, whereas
a rotating one removes it. The transition from a permanent presidency of
order to a primacy of power is easy. Moreover, one who presides in a free
council ought not to be superior in degree of power to the rest. For even if
the exercise of superior power is silent in the synod, the rest are not freed
from fear. An archbishop, insofar as he is metropolitan, is merely president
in order; but in a divided sense, insofar as he is archbishop, he is superior
in power of jurisdiction to any bishop of the province, a power which he can
exercise whenever he pleases. The chief duty of a metropolitan, as the
hierarchists repeatedly declare, is to preside over synods. Yet provincial

governed without metropolitans. The experience not only of the primitive
church but also of the Reformed churches, as I have said, teaches that a
metropolitan president is not needed. Indeed, the examples of the hierarchists
themselves teach us the same thing. At a provincial synod celebrated in London
in 1603, Bancroft, bishop of London, acted as president. The see of Canterbury
was indeed vacant at that time, but it is enough that while the see was vacant
another could discharge this office; and as far as I am concerned, let it
remain vacant forever. It should be observed in passing that the bishop of
London presided in preference to any other by virtue of office, as dean in the
college of bishops. “For,” says Lyndwood, “the archbishop of Canterbury has in
the college of bishops the bishop of London as dean, Winchester as chancellor,
Lincoln as vice-chancellor, Salisbury as precentor, Worcester as chaplain, and
Rochester as cross-bearer.” And in the little book of seventy archbishops, the
bishop of London is designated as the dean for convening synods. In the synod
called the Convocation of the Clergy, which is held when the estates of the
realm meet in Parliament, there is an elected president called the prolocutor.
Whitgift answers: “The archbishop nevertheless retains his authority, although
the lower clergy are permitted to elect their own president, just as a king or
lord keeper retains his authority even if the lower house is permitted to
elect its own president.” See how he equates the archbishop with princes and
officials of the realm. It is enough that in the archbishop's absence another
can discharge this office. Consequently, if a metropolitan is not needed
either for the ordination of bishops or for convening or governing councils,
the causes for which they hold that he was chiefly instituted, he is not
necessary at all, much less of divine institution.
