### Second controversy

The position of the Roman Catholics is as follows. Bellarmine, in book 1 of
*On Justification*, chapter 5, says that justifying faith is not confidence in
special mercy and that historical faith, miraculous faith, and the faith of
promises, which is called justifying faith, are one and the same. The Academy
of Louvain says against the Lutherans, article 9: “The faith by which someone
firmly believes and certainly determines that through Christ his sins have
been forgiven and that he will possess eternal life has no testimony of
Scripture; indeed, Scripture is opposed to it.”

Likewise, the Council of Trent, session 3, chapter 9, says: “No godly person
ought to doubt God’s mercy or Christ’s merit. Yet when anyone looks at himself
and his own infirmity and indisposition, he can fear and be afraid concerning
his grace, since no one can know with the certainty of faith, to which no
falsehood can be subject, that he has obtained the grace of God.”

Likewise, Bellarmine says in book 3 of *On Justification*, chapter 2: “If it is
established that someone has performed or is performing serious repentance,
he can certainly determine that his sins have been forgiven.”

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 38 | image 15575010 | printed p. 36]

In the same place he says that Catholics do not deny that sure confidence is
to be placed in God that sins have been forgiven after repentance has been
performed and the sacraments of baptism and absolution received. See the
remainder where the state of the first controversy is treated.

Our position, however, is this: justifying faith requires not knowledge alone,
but much more confidence, by which we certainly determine not only in general
that God is merciful in Christ, but also that the things promised—that is,
Christ together with his benefits—pertain particularly to us, and that our sins
have been forgiven on account of his merits. We likewise assert that
justifying faith differs very greatly from historical and miraculous faith,
and that it is confidence in special mercy.

Bellarmine, however, both deceives and is greatly deceived when he argues
against us that the object of faith is the whole word of God, since we affirm
this very thing, yet in such a way that justifying faith especially regards
the promises concerning Christ and their fulfillments. Finally, we assert that
in this life we can be certain of the remission of sins and salvation. Yet in
this life our faith is imperfect and weak, because it is variously tossed and
is very often obscured by various temptations. Against these we must always
struggle and pray with the apostles, “Lord, increase our faith,” and with the
man spoken of in the Gospel, “I believe, Lord; help my weakness.”

Our faith, though small, provided that it is true, rests upon the promises of
grace, apprehends Christ, and rests content in him with sure confidence. It can
therefore make us certain of the remission of sins and eternal life. For faith
does not justify because it is

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 39 | image 15575011 | printed p. 37]

great or small, but because it is true and truly apprehends Christ. Finally,
it is never separated from temptations, but although it is often shaken, in
the end it emerges victorious.

It must be noted, however, that the Roman Catholics themselves do not agree
with one another concerning this controversy. Thus Bellarmine, in book 3 of
*On Justification*, chapter 3, attempts to refute both those of his own party
who agree with us and us. For the author of the Cologne *Enchiridion* says in
the treatise on justification: “We confess it to be true that justification
altogether requires a person certainly to believe not only in general that
sins are truly forgiven through Christ to those who repent, but also that they
have been forgiven through Christ by faith to the very person who believes.”
Likewise, Ambrose Catharinus says that a faithful person can possess the
certainty of faith concerning his grace.

There is agreement between us on the following points:

1. In this life someone can and ought to be certain that his sins have been
   forgiven, Bellarmine, book 3 of *On Justification*, chapter 2.

2. Sure confidence that sins have been forgiven is to be placed in God, in the
   same place.

3. A person can be certain of salvation and faith by an extraordinary
   revelation.

We differ from the Roman Catholics, however, in the following points:

1. They assert that historical, miraculous, and justifying faith are the same;
   we assert that they differ greatly.

2. They assert that faith is not confidence in special mercy, and that special
   grace, or the promises, is not the principal object of faith. We, on the
   contrary, acknowledge the whole word of God as the object of faith, but
   especially the special mercy and grace explained in the promises.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 40 | image 15575012 | printed p. 38]

3. They distinguish the certainty of faith from the certainty of confidence.
   They say that the certainty of faith rests upon the authority of God alone
   and that they wish it to exclude doubt in every respect. They say that the
   certainty of confidence rests partly upon the divine promise and partly
   upon one’s own disposition; insofar as it rests upon the divine promise, it
   is certainty simply and without doubt, but insofar as it rests upon our
   disposition, it does not exclude doubt but is conjectural certainty. We
   maintain the contrary. From these points a threefold question arises between
   us and the Roman Catholics.

### The state of the second controversy, therefore, will be this: Is justifying faith the same as historical and miraculous faith?

The Roman Catholics say that they are the same. We, however, assert that
justifying faith is distinct from historical and miraculous faith, relying
upon the following arguments.

1. Justifying faith is peculiar to the elect, but historical faith, by which
   we determine that the things contained in the word are true, is not peculiar
   to the elect; it is common to the devil and to the ungodly. Therefore
   historical faith is not the same as justifying faith. The major premise is
   proved from Romans 9:3 and 10:11; the minor from 1 Corinthians 13:2; James
   2:19; Acts 8:13.

2. If there have been and are many faithful people who do not possess
   miraculous faith but who have possessed justifying faith,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 41 | image 15575013 | printed p. 39]

then justifying faith is distinct from miraculous faith. But the former is
true, as is evident in Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, John the Baptist, and others.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

3. In Acts 8:19 Simon Magus possessed faith, but not justifying faith, even
   according to the testimony of the Roman Catholics themselves. Nor did he
   possess miraculous faith, because he could not perform miracles; indeed, he
   wished to buy from the apostles with money the power to perform miracles.
   He possessed historical faith, however. Therefore the conclusion follows.

4. Judas possessed historical and also miraculous faith when he performed
   miracles, as in Matthew 10, but he did not possess justifying faith.
   Therefore the conclusion follows.

5. If many who possess miraculous faith are to be condemned, then miraculous
   faith differs from justifying faith. But the former is true from Matthew
   7:22. Therefore the conclusion follows. The consequence is evident, since
   no one who possesses justifying faith is to be condemned, Mark 16:16.

6. The object of historical faith is the whole word of God, and its act is to
   determine that the things contained in the word are true. The object of
   miraculous faith is the power of God, while its act is to believe that, on
   account of this power, one can perform anything miraculously. The object of
   justifying faith, however, is the whole word of God, but especially the
   promises; its act is to apply to oneself the things that have been promised
   and to rest upon the promises in confidence. Therefore these kinds of faith
   are distinct.

7. The faith through which righteousness and the inheritance are obtained
   differs from the faith through which they are not obtained. Through
   justifying faith, or the faith of promises, righteousness and the
   inheritance are obtained, as in Romans 3:28 and 4:5, 16. Through historical
   and miraculous faith, however, these are not obtained. Otherwise devils,
   many reprobates, Judas, and false prophets would be righteous

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 42 | image 15575014 | printed p. 40]

heirs, since they possessed both kinds of faith. Therefore the conclusion
follows.

### Objection

The following objection is commonly brought against our doctrine from Hebrews
11.

If the apostle attributes historical knowledge, the performance of miracles,
and justification or the attainment of the promises to the same faith, then
historical, miraculous, and justifying faith are the same. But the former is
true in the passage cited. For he says that the justified understand by the
faith which he defines—that is, by justifying faith—that the ages were formed
by the word of God, which pertains to historical faith. Afterward he says that
by the same faith the saints conquered kingdoms, stopped the mouths of lions,
and quenched the force of fire, verse 33, things that pertain to miraculous
faith. Afterward he says that by the same faith they attained the promises,
verse 39, which pertains to justifying faith. Therefore these kinds of faith
are the same.

Response 1: the proposition is denied. Although justifying faith always
contains historical faith within itself, and sometimes miraculous faith as
well, the converse is not true. Historical faith is often without justifying
faith. Accordingly, the following does not follow: historical knowledge and
justification, or attainment of the promises, are attributed to the same
faith; therefore they are the same faith. It does not follow, I say, unless it
is first proved that wherever historical faith is, justifying faith is also
there. This has been proved false above.

I say, second, that the apostle does not affirm there that all who attained the
promise performed miracles, but only the prophets and a few others.
Accordingly, the following does not follow: some who possessed justifying faith
also possessed miraculous faith; therefore justifying and miraculous faith are
the same. For many who possess justifying faith

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 43 | image 15575015 | printed p. 41]

and have possessed it do not and did not possess miraculous faith, as is
evident in certain prophets and John the Baptist. Many also possess miraculous
faith who do not possess justifying faith, as above. Many likewise possess the
historical faith by which they believe from the word of God not only that the
ages were formed, but also that the whole word of God is true, although they
possess neither miraculous nor justifying faith.

Finally, the apostle explains various exercises of justifying faith in the
faithful. Some of them are peculiar to justifying faith; others are common to
it, miraculous faith, and historical faith. But the following does not follow:
some exercises are common; therefore these different species of faith are the
same. Accordingly, if the minor premise is understood of justifying faith, it
is always and in every respect false.

### Third controversy

The third controversy concerns the object of justifying faith. We agree with
the Roman Catholics that the whole word of God is its object, but we dissent
from them by asserting that faith has the promises especially as its object.
For although faith determines that God is altogether truthful in his commands,
prohibitions, and threats, obediently receives his commands, observes his
prohibitions, and pays attention to his threats, nevertheless true justifying
faith begins from the promise, stands in it, and terminates in it. We therefore
assert that the promises of grace in Christ are especially the object of
faith, relying upon the following arguments.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 44 | image 15575016 | printed p. 42]

1. That which Christ and the apostles testify is especially to be preached and
   believed is especially the object of faith. But they teach that the gospel,
   or Christ—in whom the promises of God are both yes and amen—is especially
   to be preached and believed, as in 2 Corinthians 1:9 and 5:18, 19; Mark 1:5;
   Acts 20:24; 1 Corinthians 2:12; 1 Peter 1:21. Therefore the conclusion
   follows.

2. That on account of whose being known and believed by faith eternal life is
   given is especially the object of faith. But eternal life is given on
   account of Christ known, John 17:3 and 6:47; Romans 10:11; John 3:16, 18.
   Therefore the conclusion follows.

3. If faith will be perfect and will cease when we possess the things promised,
   then the things faith especially regards are the promises. But the former
   is true from 1 Corinthians 13:12, 13; Hebrews 11:39. Therefore the
   conclusion follows.

4. If Paul refers all his knowledge and faith to Christ crucified, then that
   which faith especially regards as its object is Christ and the promises of
   grace in Christ. But the former is true from 1 Corinthians 1:23 and
   elsewhere. Therefore the conclusion follows.

5. If faith especially seeks life and salvation not in commands,
   prohibitions, or threats, but in the promise of mercy; and if faith rests
   content in the ministry of reconciliation, then the promises are especially
   the object of faith. But the former is true, because in the commands life
   and salvation are promised under a condition which we cannot fulfill.
   Therefore faith seeks life not in them, but in the gospel, which is the
   ministry of reconciliation, 2 Corinthians 5:18.

6. If the gospel, whose substance is the promises, is called the word of
   faith; and if, when faith is given to the gospel, it is called the obedience
   of faith, then faith’s principal object is

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 45 | image 15575017 | printed p. 43]

   the gospel promises. But the former is true from Romans 1:5, 16, 17 and
   16:25, 26 and 20:8. Therefore the conclusion follows.

7. Justifying faith must lift a person’s heart up to God, to love and embrace
   God and to trust him; it must also console and pacify consciences, Romans 5
   and 8. But faith cannot accomplish these things unless it especially has
   the promises of the gospel as its object. Therefore the conclusion follows.

8. That which was the principal object of Abraham’s faith is the principal
   object of the faith of all the faithful. But the promise was its principal
   object, Romans 4:10. The major premise is proved from the same chapter,
   verse 23. Therefore the conclusion follows.

9. The objects of faith are the things which are not seen and the things which
   are hoped for, Hebrews 11:1. But these things are contained in the promises.
   Therefore the promises are the principal object of faith.

10. The law is not of faith, Galatians 3:12. Therefore the law, which is a part
    of the word, is not the principal object of faith.

### Objections

Objection 1: the faith which Christ required and in confirmation of which he
performed miracles was justifying faith. But he required a faith which had
something besides the promises as its object, namely, Christ’s omnipotence and
deity. Therefore justifying faith had something besides the promises as its
object.

Response 1: it is denied that the argument establishes its conclusion. For we
concede that something besides the promises is the object of faith, yet in such
a way that the promises are especially its object. Second, the minor premise
is denied,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 46 | image 15575018 | printed p. 44]

for Christ did not require a faith which had only omnipotence and deity as its
object.

Objection in reply, by which the minor is proved from Matthew 9:27: Christ
required from the two blind men only faith in his omnipotence. In Matthew
16:16 Peter truly believed, and believed only Christ’s deity. In Luke 7:9 the
centurion confessed only Christ’s deity and omnipotence. The same is true of
Nathanael in John 1:50, the apostles in John 6:68, 69, and Martha in John
12:27. Therefore Christ required a faith which had omnipotence and deity as
its object, but not the promises.

Response 1: the inference is denied. For he required a faith which had both
omnipotence and deity and also, and above all, the promises as its object.
Second, those very testimonies teach that they believed in Christ as the
Messiah formerly promised by God and now exhibited. For all their confessions
contained in summary everything that was to be believed concerning Christ.
Third, all of them made a confession concerning Christ’s deity and omnipotence
at this time and on this occasion; therefore they believed nothing else
concerning Christ, and faith had nothing else as its object. The inference does
not follow, for the gospel history teaches the contrary, and the faith of all
the faithful has its own degrees and increases.

Objection 2: justifying faith has the same object as miraculous faith. But
miraculous faith regards not the promises or special mercy, but the omnipotence
and deity of the Son of God. Therefore neither does justifying faith regard the
promises or special mercy. The major premise is proved from the final chapter
of Mark, verses 16–17.

Response 1: justifying faith has the same object as miraculous faith, but not
only that object. Accordingly, if “the same” is understood to mean “only the
same,” the major premise is false.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 47 | image 15575019 | printed p. 45]

Second, the passage cited does not prove it, because the evangelist does not say that all who
believe and are to be saved through faith will perform miracles; he speaks
only of the apostles. The following does not follow: some who are saved through
faith possess the power to perform miracles; therefore the object of justifying
and miraculous faith is the same.

Objection 3: the object of justifying faith is Christ’s resurrection, through
which he demonstrated his power, Romans 10:9. Therefore the promises are not
especially its object.

Response: in that passage the apostle establishes the word of faith and Jesus
Christ as the object. What is said of the resurrection is only a part of this
object, a part which does not exclude but includes the other parts.
Accordingly, the consequence is denied.

### Fourth controversy

The fourth controversy is whether faith is confidence in special mercy, or
whether faith is a particular, certain persuasion of the remission of sins and
eternal life.

Bellarmine, in book 1 of *On Justification*, chapter 10, affirms not only that
these particular promises—“my sins or your sins are forgiven”—are not found in
Scripture, but that not even the following general proposition is found:
“Everyone who believes that his sins have been forgiven through Christ is
justified.” “Nowhere,” he says, “does Scripture say, ‘He who believes that his
sins have been forgiven has eternal life.’” Yet in chapter 11 he says: “They
speak rightly of applying the general promises, because of those pronouns
‘our,’ ‘us,’ and ‘me,’ so that each person applies the general promises to
himself

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 48 | image 15575020 | printed p. 46]

through faith. For,” he says, “just as by catholic faith I believe that Christ
died for all, so by the same faith I believe that he died for me, who am one of
all.” He also says that the promises which are found absolutely in Scripture
testify to the sufficiency of Christ’s merit, and that no promise testifies
that Christ’s death takes effect in all without any condition on their part,
since they require faith and the sacraments. Nor can anyone teach from God’s
word that he possesses the faith required for obtaining remission of sins. In
chapter 8 he says, “Faith is justifying although it does not regard special
mercy, and although it does regard special mercy, perhaps it is not
justifying.”

Finally, the Roman Catholics say that by a particular faith they believe their
salvation certainly and infallibly with respect to God who promises. Thus what
prevents them from agreeing with us is our wicked disposition and unworthiness,
which, as they say, prevents us from being certain of the remission of sins and
life, except with conjectural certainty and by hope.

We, however, concede that with respect to ourselves we cannot in any way be
certain unless something beyond what is in us intervenes; from what is in us,
we can do nothing but despair. Yet in the act of justification God regards not
our unworthiness, but the work of reconciliation and Christ’s satisfaction, in
which all sins are covered and pardoned. By his Spirit he works faith in our
hearts. By that faith we are joined to Christ, and thus we can also be certain
that we have been justified in him. How we know that we possess faith from
God’s

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 49 | image 15575021 | printed p. 47]

word was proved above.

It must be noted, however, that writers sometimes take confidence for an
effect of faith and sometimes for the principal act of faith, that is, for the
application of the promises and a particular persuasion of the remission of
sins. We take it in the latter sense here. In faith, therefore, there is not
only knowledge of the word, especially of Christ crucified, together with
assent and a judgment approving as true the things taught concerning Christ,
but also a special application of the universal promises, by which each person
determines that Christ together with his benefits pertains to him. Hence comes
that *plērophoria*, full assurance, of faith spoken of in Romans 4:21 and
Hebrews 10:22. Apprehension in the will follows it, by which, as with both arms,
we embrace what the mind determines pertains particularly to us and rest
content in it. Accordingly, Bellarmine’s claim that faith is only in the
intellect is false.

We therefore assert that faith is confidence, or a certain persuasion of
special mercy, by which each faithful person certainly determines that his
sins have been forgiven in Christ and that he will obtain eternal salvation.
We rely upon the following arguments.

1. What Scripture says pertains to each and every faithful person, faith ought
   to apply to each one. Scripture says that special mercy pertains to each and
   every faithful person, Isaiah 1:18; Acts 2:29; Galatians 1:4 and 3:13, 27.
   Therefore faith ought to believe special mercy and certainly to determine
   that the believer’s sins in particular have been forgiven and that eternal
   life pertains to him.

2. If eternal life is given to no one unless through faith he is specially and
   particularly a participant in mercy,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 50 | image 15575022 | printed p. 48]

   that is, in Christ’s benefits, then faith is a special, certain persuasion
   and an application of the promises. But the former is true from 1 John 5:1,
   5; Mark 16:16; Luke 7:50. Therefore the conclusion follows.

3. What the Holy Spirit testifies particularly in each of the faithful, they
   can and ought to believe certainly and particularly. The Holy Spirit
   testifies in each of the faithful in particular to special grace, adoption,
   the remission of sins, and the fact that each of the faithful is certainly
   to be saved, Romans 8:15, 16. There he posits two witnesses of adoption: the
   Holy Spirit in us and our spirit, that is, our sanctified conscience. See
   also 2 Corinthians 1:21; John 3:13 and 4:13; 1 Corinthians 2:9, 16 and 1:12;
   Galatians 4:6. Therefore the conclusion follows.

4. If the end of Scripture is that each of us should believe and know by faith
   that we have eternal life; if by faith each of us knows that we are children
   of God; and if by faith we ought to believe God’s testimony that in Christ
   he has given eternal life to each of us, then faith is a certain persuasion
   of special mercy, and by faith we can be certain of eternal life and the
   remission of sins. But the former is true from 1 John 5:10, 11, 13, 20 and
   3:16. Therefore the conclusion follows.

5. What we ask for in prayer must be believed to pertain particularly to us.
   Each person ought to ask in prayer for special mercy, the remission of sins,
   and eternal life. Therefore the conclusion follows. The major premise is
   proved from Mark 11:24; 1 John 5:14; James 1:6 and elsewhere. The minor is
   proved from Matthew 6:9; Luke 11:2; Psalm 32:6 and elsewhere.

6. If, as the Roman Catholics maintain, we can be certain of salvation and the
   remission of sins by hope, then we can be certain by faith. Proof of the
   consequence: hope follows faith, and we hope for nothing unless we have
   first believed it by faith. As is the measure of faith, so is the measure of
   hope. Therefore the conclusion follows.

7. If Christ requires a particular

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 51 | image 15575023 | printed p. 49]

   persuasion of particular mercy and the remission of sins, then each of the faithful
   ought to possess it. But the former is true from Matthew 9:2, 28; Mark 1:15;
   Luke 7:50 and 19:9; 1 Timothy 1:5; Hebrews 4:16; 1 John 2:23. Therefore the
   conclusion follows.

8. If each of the faithful perceives by faith that Christ lives in him and
   that he lives for Christ, and if they are certainly persuaded that nothing
   can separate them from the love of God in Christ, then faith is confidence
   and a certain persuasion of special mercy; and they can certainly determine
   that their sins have been forgiven and that eternal life pertains to them.
   But the former is true from Galatians 2:20; Hebrews 3:14 and 6:11, 12, 19;
   Romans 8:38, 39; Psalm 31:15; 1 John 3:14 and 5:13; 1 Peter 2:3, 4.
   Therefore the conclusion follows.

9. If a particular persuasion and application of the promises is commanded in
   Scripture, and if *oligopistia*—timidity and doubt concerning the promises,
   God’s particular presence, and special mercy toward us—is condemned, then
   this confidence and certain persuasion of special mercy must necessarily be
   in all the faithful. But the former is true from 1 Peter 5:9; Ephesians
   6:16; Romans 4:20; James 1:6; Matthew 6:30, 8:26, 14:31, and 16:8; Luke
   12:29. Therefore the conclusion follows.

10. If each and every faithful person, once received into grace, perseveres to
    the end and cannot finally fall from grace, then there must be no doubt
    concerning the remission of sins or salvation. But the former is true from
    Psalm 37:24; Matthew 24:24; Luke 22:32; John 10:28; Romans 8:38 and 11:29;
    1 Corinthians 1:8; Philippians 1:6 and 2:16; 1 Peter 1:5; 1 John 3:9; Jude
    3; Ephesians 1:14 and 4:30, as will be explained more fully in the following
    controversy.
