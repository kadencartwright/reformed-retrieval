Verse 22. Do not say, “I will repay evil.” Wait for Jehovah, and he himself will save you.

In תאמר (*dicas*, you say) there is a metonymy of the subject: a determination stands for the accompanying utterance, which generally attends the mind’s design.

The purpose is to restrain people from vengeance.

The reason for this prohibition is that vengeance and retribution belong to God, Deut. 32.52. For that reason he is called at Psalm 94.1 [the mighty God of acts of vengeance], which is also repeated for greater emphasis. Since, therefore, it belongs to God alone and to those with whom God shares this authority together with the designation “gods,” it is right that all

[1632 witness | PDF p. 446 | printed columns 866-867]

who act in a private capacity should abstain from retribution.

Every act of vengeance, therefore—even its slightest kind—must be judged a violation of the divine will. But because the depravity of our nature is such that, when we have received an injury, we are immediately seized by a certain itching and desire to avenge ourselves, although God’s law does not absolve us because of this titillation of our depraved nature toward vengeance, the Prophet nevertheless takes earnest care that it should not creep and slide further. He therefore commands us not to establish and design within ourselves and within our minds any determination concerning retribution. For because we cherish our own counsels and thoughts, it happens that by this resolution within ourselves, as though by a vehement impulse and incitement, we leap forward to avenge ourselves. Although it belongs to God alone that his counsels and thoughts should be firm and immovable, Psalm 33.11, such nevertheless is human pride and ambition that every son of earth and petty man, setting this before himself, strives with every sinew and the greatest effort to attain the same thing. Hence all people secretly foster within themselves what the proud Persians and Medes once dared to profess: that their decrees ought by no means to be changed. Indeed, they not infrequently disclose this insolence openly when they say that they will do this or that, or will not do it, because it was previously so determined within themselves.

Still greater caution must be employed lest we profess vengeance and retribution in words and speech. For Satan will thus hold us fast to himself by a double obligation so that we do not escape from his snare. Whereas previously merely the sight of the loss we received was driving us toward vengeance, now we seem to be hindered by our reputation and esteem from failing to take vengeance. For although people are lighter than vanity itself and more inconstant, all nevertheless pursue a certain singular constancy in evil deeds—or rather, obstinacy.

See the example of Pilate, who was in no way willing to retract what he had written, Matt. 27.

Above all, however, one must abstain from vows and oaths in these cases. By them, as though by a threefold cord which is broken with difficulty and by stronger and tighter bonds, the devil holds us ensnared. See the example of Herod, Matt. 14.

From all these things it is clear how wickedly and criminally those people act who, as soon as they have received an injury, though otherwise by their nature excessively ready for vengeance, foolishly cast themselves by these little snares into these traps.

From this it is clear how futile is the doctrine of the Pontifical teachers, who say that this teaching about fleeing vengeance is a precept of a certain new law, by no means included among the precepts of Moses and the Old Testament, and by the same token is a counsel whose observers do more than they are bound to do by law. Upon this foundation works of supererogation are built.

In the latter half he prescribes a singular medicine for this disease, namely faith in God. You ask, in what should confidence in God be placed? In this: that God will pronounce judgment for you, defend your just cause, vindicate your reputation and esteem from the reproaches and insults of wicked people and—to embrace everything in one word—save you fully and completely, as it stands in the context. From this it is readily established why people are so ready and prompt to avenge themselves: because they are destitute of faith and confidence in God, from which this patience and expectation arise, or certainly because they are poor and needy in faith in God. For this person thinks that he will suffer the loss of his estate, and that one that he will receive grave injury to his reputation and esteem, unless he immediately rushes upon the person by whom he has been injured and provoked. Nor does he give place to divine vengeance, which he snatches away from God by his own revenge, as though he were better equipped than God himself to avenge an injury.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 447 | printed columns 868-869]

It is, however, most worthy of notice that the deliverance which someone obtains for himself by avenging his own injury is set as a contrary over against this deliverance which God brings to those who wait for him. At 1 Sam. 25.31 Abigail, granting to David by concession that he would obtain his desire against her husband Nabal, nevertheless declares that this victory would be inauspicious and unhappy for him on this ground alone: that he would save himself, but the Lord would not. This ought to warn us not to imbibe an opinion of divine favour toward ourselves merely because we have proved superior to our enemies in pursuing victory, for in this matter very many deceive themselves. For this very deliverance, which they have procured for themselves by their own hand, is accursed by God. What, then, will become of those who contend with others in an uncertain struggle to avenge themselves? David, after all, surrounded by armed soldiers, was certain of victory against an unarmed Nabal.

Moreover, when he says that the Lord will save the person who is oppressed, but not that he will avenge the cause, he seems to warn us what it is that we ought to seek and entreat from God: namely, our own deliverance, not evil for our adversary. This warning is timely, since there are very many who are solicitous not so much about repairing the loss they have received as about inflicting evil.

But if it is not lawful to repay evil with evil, much less is it lawful to inflict evil upon another person of one’s own accord, and least of all to recompense a benefit with an evil deed.

Verse 23. A stone and a stone are an abomination to Jehovah, and deceitful balances are not good.

In לא טובות (*non bonae*, are not good) there is a synecdoche of the general expression for “they are abominable,” which they call μείωσιν (*understatement*).

Concerning this Proverb, see the things noted above at verse 10.

Verse 24. A man’s steps are from the Lord. How, therefore, may a man understand his own way?

In מצעדי (*gressus*, steps) there is a synecdoche for all actions which are performed by a person, whether by going, sitting, going out, returning, and so forth. Likewise, in גבר (*viri*, of a man)—that is, of a strong man or an adult—there is another synecdoche. Here there also seems to be an argument by comparison from the greater, because if the footsteps of even the strongest person are in the hand of God, much more are those of everyone else. In דרכו (*viam suam*, his own way) there is a customary metaphor.

The purpose is that, in the burdens which we undertake or take upon ourselves, having renounced ourselves and our own wisdom, we should depend upon God’s providence and gracious direction.

The first part of this Proverb is taken word for word from Psalm 37.23 and is confirmed by very many scriptural testimonies: Job 14.12, where it is said [that God numbers all the steps of a man], and 31.4, 14.21, and Acts 17.28. It is most clearly and copiously amplified in Psalm 139, where he teaches that a person’s thoughts and words—and likewise his deeds—are known to God before they enter his mind or tongue. This doctrine is set forth more particularly in Ezekiel 1, where the entire administration of affairs upon the earth is so attributed to the Angels, God’s ministers, that the Angels always hold their wings and faces raised toward heaven, observing God’s nod in the administration of all things. This very point was shown not long ago in the senses of sight and hearing from verse 12. It is demonstrated most clearly and vividly in Balaam the false prophet, who, hired to curse the people and supremely intent upon that business, not only could not curse them but, on the contrary, blessed them against his will. He himself freely confessed that he could not utter even a single little word unless God should give his assent that he do so. But if this is true in words, much more will it also have a place in deeds; and if this is true in Balaam, it will be equally firm in all people.

[1632 witness | PDF p. 448 | printed columns 870-871]

Since these things are so, the Prophet concludes by a comparison from the lesser that no one has a grasp of his own life, or what the administration of his life will eventually be. For if he does not know even one footprint of his own way, he does not thoroughly know the whole course of his life; and if even the strongest person will not understand this, much less will the rest discern it. This is also proposed ἐπαναγωγικῶς (*by an ascending inference*) and denied more emphatically. See Prov. 16.16. By this Proverb, therefore, God mocks the vanity of those people who will make determinations within themselves about conducting the greatest affairs—about buildings, war, commerce, and that for many years—and will boldly pronounce what they will do. See James 4.13–15. It is just as if a person disabled in his feet should profess that he would complete a great journey, and so forth; for his feet are merely on loan.

But if in civil affairs, which are more within our control, we are not permitted to speak so confidently, much less may we do so in those things which pertain to piety and holiness, for which we are utterly unfit by nature. See Matt. 26.33–35.

From this it follows that in everything we undertake and begin we must approach, consult, and invoke God, so that these things may turn out rightly and prosperously for us. See Prov. 3.6 and Col. 3.17.
