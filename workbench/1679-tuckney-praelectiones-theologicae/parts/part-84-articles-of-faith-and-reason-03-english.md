WE DENY

1. First, that reason is the discoverer—using this word somewhat apart from
its forensic sense—because:

1. Reason does not discover supernatural matters of faith by itself.
“Things which the eye has not seen,” 2 Corinthians 2:9, would never have
entered the mind of anyone, however ingenious or learned—a triune God and
God incarnate—unless Christ had revealed them, 2 Timothy 1:9–10. These and
similar things were not first discovered by human invention, to speak with
Eulogius,

[1679 new-series fol. 10 | IA leaf 330]

since, as he says, they were “greater than every declaration.”

2. Nor does reason sufficiently grasp or understand them once revealed,
for “we know in part,” 1 Corinthians 13:9. It is therefore perfectly clear,
first, that faith cannot be generated and, second, that it cannot be
perfected by human arguments. Consequently, third, it is exceedingly bold
and rash for anyone—as though he were God's counselor—to undertake the task
of giving the reasons for very many things which must be believed solely
from revelation. Those who dared market such reasons have often labored to
produce dreams in place of oracles.

2. I do not allow reason to be the legislator, making laws or necessarily
imposing anything to be believed or done.

1. It may not legislate beyond the Word in divine worship as regards its
parts and substance. Here, if anywhere, the saying applies: “What is reason
in sacred matters but delirium in affairs?” This is more than sufficiently
evident in Durandus's irrational and truly delirious *Rationale*. Here human
reason—as can be seen in the various forms of worship among pagans and
papists—has been the source and origin of every superstition. Here the
apostle condemns “things having an appearance of wisdom,” Colossians 2:23.
For we must follow religion not where we wish to lead it, but where it leads
us, as Vincent of Lérins very devoutly says.

2. Nor may it legislate against the Word in any other matters. For as we
said earlier that faith does not conflict with right reason, so far be it
from us ever to tolerate reason rising against the Word of faith. Indeed,
we firmly say that reasonings must be overthrown, together with every high
thing which exalts itself against the knowledge of God, and every thought
must be brought into captivity to obey Christ. This is Paul's teaching, as
is this Augustine's: “If a reason is given against the authority of the
divine Scriptures, however acute it may be, it deceives by the likeness of
truth, for it cannot be true.” I want reason to be the servant, not the
mistress.

3. Finally, I sometimes allow reason to be a witness but never the judge.
This is the principal point in the present case.

Here I do not allow:

1. First and generally, that articles of faith should be weighed in the
scales of human reason or bent to it as their standard, as often happens by
violent compulsion and as a certain learned man recently noted in Josephus
the historian. Since each primary thing is the measure of those which
follow, God's truth is the rule of the truth of a thing, while the truth of
the thing is the rule of the truth of intellect and reason. It follows that
divine truth is not to be brought before the tribunal of human reason. On
the contrary, the truth of reason must be brought before the tribunal of
truth in God or disclosed by God. We must not proceed in this way: “This
doctrine is absurd to reason; therefore it conflicts with God's Word.” We
must rather proceed thus: “This doctrine conflicts with God's Word;
therefore it is absurd to sound reason,” as Vedelius rightly noted, whatever
the compiler may have written to the contrary.

2. Nor ought reason to demand that faith always demonstrate its articles
by necessary arguments *a priori*. The archbishop of Tyre took this demand
badly when King Amalric of Jerusalem required him to demonstrate the
resurrection of the dead by reason. Very many matters of this kind are
entirely incapable of demonstration because they depend wholly upon divine
testimony, as will appear later.

3. Nor may faith be resolved finally and ultimately into reason as though

[1679 new-series fol. 11 | IA leaf 331]

reason were the foundation and principle from which it is derived. This is
indeed far too customary among papal scholastics but is unjustly charged
against our theologians by the Ubiquitarians. Meisner's dreadful bogeymen
with which he threatens us in this matter do not frighten us.

4. We are so far from making reason the principle and foundation of faith
that we do not wish faith to lean on reason even as upon a supporting
flute-player. Whatever we grant to reason on the human side for healing or
subduing human blindness or stubbornness—when from its agreement with faith
we show the absurdity of error and the evidence of truth, and in this sense
admit it as a witness—we certainly do not believe that faith and
supernatural truth are so weak in themselves as to need reason's vote or
assistance. Christ does not seek testimony from man in this way, John 5:34.
For his truth is contained within its own, that is, divine lines; and as it
is truly God-breathed, so it is rightly credible in itself. “The Savior's
teaching is self-sufficient and in need of nothing. Greek philosophy, when
it approaches, does not make the truth more powerful but renders the
sophistical attack against it powerless,” as Clement of Alexandria most
correctly says.

5. Although we grant reason the judgment of discretion insofar as it weighs
controversies of faith according to the Word, we entirely deny it the
judgment of decision. The former is only a judgment of capacity, the latter
of authority. The former is the ability and work of the intellect knowing
an object; the latter is the office of a judge deciding a dispute, to which
reason does not rise in supernatural matters.

6. Consequently, least of all should reason be granted such license—or
rather audacity—that it immediately rejects whatever does not appear right
to it, even though it is plainly read in or necessarily deduced from Sacred
Scripture. We grant reason service, not mastery, lest it arrogate the
censor's rod to itself, assume dominion over faith, and strive to subject
divine things to its own official power. What is this but adjusting or
correcting the sun's course by a clock? Since whatever God says is
necessarily most true, if reason argues or thinks otherwise or to the
contrary, it must either be sternly rebuked, “Who are you to answer back to
God?” Romans 9:20, or mocked, “Fool!” and what follows in 1 Corinthians
15:36. Hence the common saying: “What reason cannot solve, faith ought to
laugh at.” In this sense let philosophy be banished, Colossians 2:8; let
petty wisdom, drunk with itself, take a beating. Here God alone must be
heard, and the intellect must be taken captive to obey Christ, 2 Corinthians
10:5.

This lengthy but necessary statement of the controversy and explanation of
our position is now complete. Some confirmation of it has also been woven
in along the way. I shall now finally complete that confirmation as briefly
as possible—with a finger pointing to the sources—by setting out only a few
heads of argument.
