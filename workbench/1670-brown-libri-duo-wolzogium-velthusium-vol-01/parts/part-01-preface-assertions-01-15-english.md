# Preface to the Reader

You will perhaps be surprised, reader, that I, a foreigner, should involve
myself in these controversies. But call to mind that I am a Christian and
what, when the Church and the cause of God are in danger, is to be expected
of all faithful Christians. You will readily perceive that when, according
to my measure, I contend for some divine truth—than which nothing ought to be
dearer to Christ's followers—I am only discharging my duty. Those adversaries
whom I oppose in these little books are known to me by no injury and by no
favor. Indeed, Velthuysen is not even known to me by sight. I wish that I had
never known either of them by the reputation through which they are now known
to me.

There is no need for me now to say much about what should be thought of
Wolzogen's books. I believe that you learned it long ago from the published
judgments on the matter by certain very distinguished theologians and
ministers. After these, I thought that controversy had been laid to rest, at
least for the present. But then I unexpectedly heard a report that certain
men were contriving contrary testimonies and judgments that approved those
books of Wolzogen, *On the Interpreter of Scripture*, and acquitted them of
every charge of heterodoxy or error. Struck by what I heard, and recalling
certain chief and especially noteworthy things that had occurred to me while
reading them before, this thought entered my mind: Can it not be demonstrated
that Wolzogen's books betray and destroy the orthodox position *on the
interpreter of Scripture* that the orthodox have defended until now?

I therefore took up my pen and made the attempt. The further I advanced in
the work I had begun, the more I was confirmed in my opinion: namely, that by
this work the orthodox cause was being betrayed—far be it from me to judge
the intention of the man who produced it. I was not at all moved from this
undertaking by reading the judgments of those other men who wished to favor
Wolzogen. In-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 17 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000017]

*deed, on this occasion my opinion had taken deeper root in my mind. Some of
their writings seemed to me to be invectives against others rather than
serious judgments about Wolzogen's books; others praised the work beyond
measure in sufficiently grand words without indicating any foundation; and,
finally, those which treated the matter seriously and more soberly put
forward nothing that could weaken the censures of others. I perceived
nothing at all that could move me to change my opinion. Thus encouraged and
advanced by these obstacles rather than delayed by them, I continued.

When the work had nearly reached completion, a book written against Wolzogen
by the most learned Johannes van der Waeyen appeared. I read and devoured it
eagerly and clearly perceived that my opinion was amply confirmed. Although
that most acute defender did not treat this point expressly, he nevertheless
demonstrated more than sufficiently that Wolzogen had betrayed the cause he
claimed to defend. He pleased me so much, and seemed to have won every point,
that I judged it superfluous to finish the work I had undertaken. At the
urging of others, however—whom I judged it better to oblige in matters of
this kind than to follow the impulses of my own mind—I completed the
examination.

Once it had been committed to the press, the very solid work of Jacobus
Koelman, doctor of philosophy and minister of the divine Word, finally saw
the light. In it Wolzogen was quite clearly demonstrated to have betrayed the
cause, so that I judged my examination utterly useless. But the die had been
cast: it had already gone to press, and I could not undo what had been done.

Meanwhile, that vernacular book of Velthuysen, *On Idolatry and Superstition*,
came into my hands. After reading its preface, I noted many things that I
determined to touch upon briefly. But when I was seized by fever, I could not
read through the book itself.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 18 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000018]

Afterward, however, when I had recovered somewhat from that tertian fever, I
also read the book through and observed many things of the same bran. I
excerpted those that concern the *libertine-Erastian position* and resolved
to respond briefly to them all. It is not my fault that this work now appears
much later than I had hoped. Had the printer kept his promises, you would
have seen these books last year. But because he was occupied in committing
other intervening works to the press, he was forced to assign these treatises
to no more than one compositor—and that man did not always attend even to
this single work. Consequently, while the press delayed, I had the leisure to
add many things and to treat and confirm more fully those assertions which I
had resolved to oppose to Velthuysen's position. You may freely judge what I
have finally accomplished.

Perhaps someone will blame me for censuring him with so sharp a style. But as
I think myself not at all blameworthy on this account, so anyone who weighs
how fearlessly and insolently he has trampled upon Christ's sacred
institutions and sought wholly to abolish the divinely instituted order will
readily judge that he could not have been treated otherwise.

Finally, so that you may see that I call no one Rabbi and am attached to no
party, but, whoever I may count as friends, always consider truth the greater
friend, I have thought it right to transcribe here part of a writing which I
recently gave to M. de Yvon to read after a discussion with him about
separation, and to others, especially the most celebrated Lady Anna Maria van
Schurman. I am prepared, if God sees fit, to publish more on that question
when an occasion is given. After numerous arguments proving that it is
unlawful to separate from the Church because of scandals among its members,
or because ecclesiastical discipline is neglected or not administered with
sufficient faith-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 19 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000019]

*fulness—which the narrowness of the page does not permit me to recount here—I
added the following theses.

1. Christ's Church on earth is one. *Since there is one God, one faith, one
mediator between God and humanity, Jesus Christ, and one head of the Church,
it necessarily follows that there is also one Church*, says Beza in his
*Confession of Faith*, chapter 5, article 2. *The entire multitude of
Christians*—says Zwingli—*which regards itself as faithful is together called
one faithful people, one Church.* Hence this Church is called the kingdom of
God, Matthew 4:26, 30; the kingdom of Christ, Daniel 7:14; the kingdom of
heaven, Matthew 13:24, 31, 33, 47; the kingdom to be handed over to the
Father, 1 Corinthians 15:24; one body, Ephesians 4:4–5; the house of God, 1
Timothy 3:15; a sheepfold, John 10:16; and a temple, 2 Thessalonians 2:4. See
also Song of Songs 2:2, 4:8–10, and 6:4.

2. This one Church is called catholic or ecumenical, as though spread over
the whole world. *By the name Church*, Calvin says in *Institutes* 4.1.7, *we
often designate the entire multitude of people dispersed throughout the
world who profess to worship the one God and Christ.* See Polanus's
*Syntagma of Theology*, book 7, chapter 7. *We maintain*, says Apollonius,
*that a certain external universal Church dispersed throughout the whole
world is described to us in Holy Scripture*, and so forth. On this ecumenical
Church see the promises in Psalms 22:27, 72:8, and 86:9; Isaiah 2:2–4; Daniel
7:14; and Zechariah 14:9. In the New Testament the word *Church* is also very
frequently used not for some particular church, but for this universal
Church: Acts 8:3; Galatians 1:13 compared with Acts 26:11 and 9:31; likewise 1
Corinthians 12:28; Acts 2:47; 1 Corinthians 10:22; Ephesians 3:10. See Ames's
*Marrow*, book 1, chapter 31, section 19.

3. This Church is not a genus—properly speaking, or a logical genus—but an
integral whole. For it is a particular thing composed of several united
parts, and every-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 20 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000020]

*thing that exists is by that very fact a particular thing. Ames does indeed
say in the *Marrow* that a particular church is a species of the Church in
general. But he seems to take *species* there grammatically, not logically,
because he distinguishes between this Church in general and the catholic
Church; and in relation to the catholic Church he says that a particular
church is a member. He also admits that this Church has its own form, which a
genus as such does not have. Particular churches are all integral parts of
this whole. An integral whole is that which has parts external to parts, and
it is evident that particular churches are distinguished from one another by
place, order, and quantity. Perhaps certain people can be members of this
catholic Church who belong to no particular church. Therefore it is not a
genus. Moreover, it has its own accidents and various changes, and also its
own operations. It is called a kingdom, Colossians 1:13, in which there are
sons, Matthew 8:12; a tabernacle, Revelation 21:3; a building, 1 Corinthians
3:9 and Ephesians 2:21; a temple, Ephesians 2:21 and 1 Corinthians 3:17; a
city, Ephesians 2:19; an army, Song of Songs 6:10; and one bread, 1 Corinthians
10:17. Therefore it is not a genus, but an integral whole.

4. This Church is also an aggregate whole, whose parts are united by a mode
of connection, Ephesians 4:16, as a city or an army is. Thus Trelcatius the
younger, on my page 200 of his *Institutes*, says: *Logically, the Church is
among those things which are called collective and aggregate—that is, such
things as are not one thing absolutely, but contain within themselves two
things, one of which is like a multitude and dispersed matter, and the other
like unity, order, and collection.* Hence we read of members being added to
the Church in Acts 2:41 and of the joining together of the saints in Ephesians
4:12. The whole building is also said to grow, fitly joined together, in
Ephesians 2:21–22. See also Colossians 2:19.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 21 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000021]

5. This aggregate body, or catholic Church, is commonly distinguished into
visible and invisible. This distinction is not entirely sound. For it is not
a distinction of a genus into species; then the Church would not be one. Nor
is it a distinction of an integral whole into members, as though one part of
the Church were visible and another invisible. Rather, it is a distinction
of the whole taken from a part, according to the different nature of the
parts. For all the members of the Church, insofar as they profess the faith,
constitute the visible Church, while those among them who are endowed with
saving faith constitute the invisible Church. Thus this distinction is the
same as that by which the Church is divided into the Church of the called and
the Church of the elect—that is, of those who are called. The former contains
the latter within itself, and so the invisible Church is a part of the visible
Church. See Polanus, at the place cited above, book 7, chapter 9.

It is nevertheless true that, although this distinction does not make
several Churches—one invisible, namely, and another visible—many things can
be said of and attributed to the Church on account of the faithful or
effectually called, who are members of the Church also insofar as it is
invisible, and not only insofar as it is visible, which do not belong to all
members of the visible Church. Hence *Church* is sometimes taken for the
assembly of the faithful elect, Ephesians 5:23, 25–27 and Colossians 1:8, and
sometimes for the assembly of the called who profess the true religion, as in
Acts 8:3 and 9:2 and frequently elsewhere. Therefore we can speak of the
Church both insofar as it is visible and insofar as it is invisible. Conceived
in either way, it can be called catholic.

6. The catholic Church which is invisible consists of the whole number of the
elect—as many as have been, are, or ever shall be—gathered into one under
Christ, its one head. It is the bride, body, and fullness of him who fills all
in all, Ephesians 1:10, 22–23 and 5:23, 27, 32. The visible Church—which is
also catholic and universal under the Gos-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 22 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000022]

*pel, not confined within the borders of one nation as formerly under the
law—consists of all those, wherever in the world they may be, who profess the
true religion, 1 Corinthians 1:2 and 12:12–13; Psalm 2:8; Revelation 7:9;
Romans 15:9–12, together with their children, 1 Corinthians 7:14; Acts 2:39;
Ezekiel 16:20–21; Romans 11:16; Genesis 3:15 and 17:7. It is the kingdom of
the Lord Jesus Christ, Matthew 13:47 and Isaiah 9:7; and the house and family
of God, Ephesians 2:19 and 1 Timothy 3:15, outside of which it is ordinarily
impossible for anyone to attain salvation, Acts 2:47. Thus our British
Confession, chapter 25, theses 1 and 2.

7. The visible Church is quite properly called the Church. 1. The parts of
this aggregate whole are properly visible, for they are called people. As
Ames rightly observes at the place cited above, the elect are not members of
the Church insofar as they are elect, but insofar as they are called. He adds
that the Church which exists on earth, although it is not all visible at the
same time, is nevertheless visible in its parts—both separately in its
individual members and conjointly in its assemblies and congregations. 2. As said above,
the visible Church contains the invisible Church within itself as a part. If
the part is properly called the Church, why not the whole as well? 3. It is
the Church that Scripture describes to us as a field in which there are also
weeds and as a house in which there are vessels for dishonor. Therefore it is
properly the Church. 4. This Church is mentioned throughout Scripture, while
the other, taken in the strictest sense, is mentioned more rarely. It too is
always called the Church by the Holy Spirit. 5. An organic Church is properly
a Church. The visible Church is such, but the invisible Church as such is not.
6. That which never completely ceases to be visible is quite properly called
Christ's Church. But this is the visible Church. *Indeed*, Ames says at the
place cited above, *the Church never completely ceases to be visible. For
although a Church may scarcely appear anywhere so pure-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 23 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000023]

*that someone can resort to it for communion in the same worship in every
respect, nevertheless a Church that is visible in some manner exists in that
very impurity of worship and profession.* 7. The churches of Rome and Corinth,
and so forth, were properly called churches. But these were visible churches,
or similar parts of the catholic visible Church. 8. That in which the Word,
sacraments, and ecclesiastical censures are administered is properly called
the Church. But this is the visible Church, for Christ gave to this Church the
ministry, the oracles, and the sacred ordinances for the gathering and joining
together of the saints, 1 Corinthians 12:28 and Ephesians 4:11–12.

8. The Roman Catholics—and this must be carefully observed—denied that the
invisible Church is really distinct from the visible. They wished saving
promises made only to true believers to have been given to the visible Church,
or the multitude of professors, so that by this reasoning they might more
easily establish the infallibility and perpetual, glorious visibility of the
Church, and finally apply those same promises to their Roman Church. See
Ames, *Bellarmine Enervated*, book 2, *On the Church*, chapter 1, thesis 10.

Hence it is that our theologians, when disputing against the Roman Catholics,
say that only the invisible Church is truly the Church—namely, the one to
which those spiritual promises belong. In saying this, however, they never
asserted that the visible Church is not a true Church ontologically or
metaphysically, for they define it for us, assign its causes, and indicate its
true marks. They never say that the visible Church is not truly the Church;
nor do they say that only the invisible Church is the Church properly so
called. They only mean that the invisible Church alone is true with respect
to God's favor and eternal salvation, because all its members enjoy God's
favor and will certainly obtain salvation. But the question whether the
visi-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 24 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000024]

*ble Church is properly called Christ's Church was not at issue. On both
sides, as is evident, it was received as beyond doubt.

Likewise, they are accustomed to distinguish between the Church taken broadly,
comprehending good and evil people, and taken strictly, containing good people
alone. See Zanchi, *On the Church*; Bullinger, *Decades* 10; and Ravanelli's
*Bibliotheca*. Thus, if at any time they maintain that only the invisible
Church is truly the Church, they understand the Church in this stricter sense.
It is therefore evident that hypocrites are not members of the Church taken
in this strict sense, although they can be and are members of the Church
taken broadly. It is also evident that the invisible Church is only a part of
the Church taken broadly in this way.

Accordingly, when the nature of the visible Church is in question, orthodox
writers disputing against Roman Catholics about the nature of the invisible
Church will give us little help. I would have everyone observe this seriously,
lest, by wrongly applying to the visible Church what those writers said of
the invisible Church, they err miserably in understanding the nature and
constitution of the visible Church.

9. Just as the Roman Catholics denied the invisible Church, so the
Barrowists, or Separatists, once denied really, or as a matter of fact, every
visible Church distinct from the invisible Church, as can be seen in the
books of Barrow, Ainsworth, Robinson, Johnson, and Canne. Smith openly says
that the true form of the true visible Church is partly internal and partly
external. The internal part consists of these: the Spirit, namely, who is the
soul animating the whole body; faith, which unites the members to the head; and love, which
joins the members to one another. The external part, he says, is a vow,
promise, oath, or covenant between God and the saints.

10. Although some Independents differ in no respect from the Separatists just
named on this question of the constitution of the visible Church, they
commonly limit and restrict the assertion about the constitution of the
visible Church in *another-*

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 25 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000025]

*way. They say that only those are members of the visible Church who, so far
as spiritual people can judge according to a judgment of charity resting on
sufficient indications, are true believers and have true communion with
Christ. They commonly maintain that those indications are three: 1. Knowledge
of the fundamentals and of other things necessary to be known for passing
one's life without scandal. 2. Experiential knowledge of the work of grace,
both repentance toward God and faith toward Christ. 3. Conduct not only
unpolluted by scandal, but also free from omission of any known divine command
and commission of any evil. Hence it necessarily follows that they expend
much time examining members who are to be admitted.

But it must be observed that they employ hardly a single argument which does
not confirm the Separatists' position mentioned above just as strongly as
their own and prove that all hypocrites ought to be excluded. As for the
instances they adduce from the New Testament churches, they will never prove
from them what they intend. 1. Those churches could have been called *holy*,
and so forth, by reason of their calling and separation to worship the true
God, and so forth. 2. Even if those designations are taken more strictly, so
that they denote saving grace, nothing prevents the whole from being named
from its better part. They will certainly never prove that every member of
those churches was truly sanctified, endowed with true faith, and united to
Christ, because the contrary is more than probable from the evils rampant
among them. 3. Even if perhaps one church were, as a matter of fact and in the
event, truly sanctified in all its members, they will never prove that the
condition of all churches was the same. And if even one is given of which
this cannot be said, the truth of their thesis perishes. 4. Even if we were
to grant that all the churches mentioned in the New Testament were uniform
in this respect, and that there was not one hypocrite among them all—which
must by no means be said—

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 26 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000026]

they still would not thereby establish the
conclusion they intend. For it can never be proved from this that those
churches were such from their first constitution; much less can it be
concluded that they were constituted under this formal consideration, or
that members were admitted under this sole formal consideration, namely,
that they were endowed with true faith; or that all who possessed true faith,
and purely insofar as they were such, were received, while no one else,
considered under any other formal consideration, was regarded as a member of
the visible Church. 5. If this were once proved, the opinion confirmed would
be that of the Separatists named above, not that of the Independents, which
they themselves wish to distinguish from it. For Scripture nowhere mentions
the restriction or limitation which they employ.

With these few observations made, it is easy to answer all the Independents'
arguments, provided that you add the following distinctions. 1. A distinction
must be made between what is required in the tribunal of God, who also
searches hearts, and what is required in the earthly tribunal, or the
ecclesiastical tribunal, where hidden things are not judged. 2. A
distinction must also be made between what is required for someone to become
a member of the visible Church and what is required for someone to be
regarded as a member of the invisible Church. 3. Relative holiness must be
distinguished from inherent holiness. We read of relative holiness in the Old
Testament, in the sense in which vessels, stones, the temple, and other such
things were called holy, as were people dedicated to God and his service,
such as priests and other Levites. In respect of this relative and covenantal
holiness, the people of Israel are frequently called holy, as separated and
set apart for God, Exodus 31:13; Leviticus 8:8, 21:8, and 22:32. In the same
respect, the children of covenant members are called holy, 1 Corinthians 7:14.
This must be distinguished from true, internal, and inherent holiness, which
consists in the renewal of the heart. 4. A distinction must be made between
members of the Church in first actuality and members

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 27 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000027]

in second actuality; or,
as others speak, between incomplete and complete members. More can be required
in the latter than in the former, for more is required in one who actually
enjoys all the Church's privileges than in one who does not enjoy all its
privileges but is simply a member and is not to be numbered among those
outside.

11. It is well known that Holy Scripture describes the visible Church to us
by similitudes indicating a mixture of good and evil people. It is compared
to a threshing floor in which there is *chaff and wheat*, Matthew 3:12; a
field in which there are *weeds and wheat*, Matthew 13:24–25; a net in which
there are *good and rotten fish*, verses 47–48; and a house in which there are
*vessels of gold and silver and vessels of wood and clay, some for honor and
others for dishonor.* From this it is evident: 1. That Christ acknowledges as
true members of the visible Church some besides those endowed with true faith.
2. That he does not wish the servants to cast out all whom they judge
destitute of true grace, or to regard them as not true members of the Church,
because he further adds, *Allow the weeds to grow until the harvest.* 3. That,
consequently, people other than the truly regenerate can be members of the
visible Church; and that in the ecclesiastical tribunal it is not required
that all members to be admitted be endowed with true faith, not even so far
as spiritual people can judge.

12. Indeed, it is also evident that God has acknowledged as his people and
his Church those whom he reproaches for things incompatible with true grace,
as can be seen in Deuteronomy 29:10–13 compared with verses 2–4, and chapter
31:21, 27. Likewise, in Isaiah 1:2–6 he reproaches them with ignorance,
rebellion, transgression or treachery. He also charges them with being laden
with iniquity, offspring of evildoers, and so forth. He reproaches all of
them with these things, I say, expressly excepting no one. For they were a
sinful nation, a people heavily laden with iniquity; from the sole of the
foot even to the head there was no soundness, verse 6.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 28 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000028]

Notwithstanding these things, he calls them his people in verse 2 and thereby
aggravates their guilt.
It must be observed that God makes no mention of good people on whose account
alone he may be said to have acknowledged them as his people. Indeed, he
calls the very people whom he calls rebels his people.

Likewise, in Revelation 3:14 and following, the church of Laodicea is
acknowledged as a church, although nothing good appears in it, but only that
which is by no means consistent with saving grace is found among them. It
must also be observed here that Christ does not except even one member of
that church when he reproaches them with this, as he had excepted some when
he charged the church of Sardis with nearly the same evil in verse 4, saying,
*You nevertheless have a few names in Sardis who have not defiled their
garments*, and so forth.

Moreover, what is especially not to be passed over in that letter to the
church of Laodicea is that Christ adorns himself with a distinctive and indeed
remarkable title: *These things says the Amen, the faithful and true witness.*
By this title he testifies that he has exactly observed, examined, and
investigated all the members of that church, and yet has seen not one to be
excepted. See our celebrated Durham on the passage.

I therefore cannot sufficiently wonder that certain people judged me to have
spoken rashly when, in a certain private conversation, I said that a particular
church could be truly a visible Church even if perhaps not one person in it
were endowed with true faith. For here an instance is given of a particular
assembly which Christ regards as a church, in which—if from the letter of
Christ himself, the faithful and true witness, it is permissible to judge its
present condition—not even one true believer appears to have existed. Indeed,
it appears that there was not one. For if even one such person had been there,
I cannot persuade myself to believe that Christ would have been unwilling to
adorn him with special praise, or at least expressly to except him, since it
would have been very remarkable if amid so great a multitude of hypocrites
one person had truly adhered to Christ and burned with zeal.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 29 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000029]

Yet with respect to outward things this church appears preferable to many
churches today, because no scandal or open profanity was rampant in it, and
ecclesiastical discipline had not been neglected. For Christ does not
reproach it with what he had charged against other churches in this respect.

Moreover, even if not a single example of such a particular church could be
given, my assertion could have been most true, as will be evident from what
follows. Indeed, I think I can demonstrate that the contrary position lays a
direct road to separation, but I do not wish to strengthen the hands of those
who withdraw by this reasoning. I will say only this: if I held another
position and believed saving grace to be of the essence of a particular
visible Church, so that it were necessarily required that there always be
some true believers in every particular and parish church, without whom it
would not truly be a visible Church, I would necessarily be a Separatist. For
I ought not join myself to any particular church which is not truly a visible
Church; and this or that particular church would never be truly a visible
Church to me until I knew certainly and infallibly that at least one true
believer was in it.

But let them tell me by what infallible sign this will become known to me. If
they say that I ought to judge according to charity, I reply that the
judgment of charity is fallible, and the one whom I judge according to charity
to be a true believer can be a hypocrite. Thus, notwithstanding this judgment,
it can happen that there is not one true believer in that particular church.
Therefore either it must be said that a particular church can be a visible
Church even if there is not one person in it endowed with true faith, or that
I can rightfully cultivate ecclesiastical communion with an assembly which
is not truly a visible Church and join myself to it as a member.

Indeed, the Independents and all those who have not embraced the strict
position of the Separatists described above, as well as that of the
Anabaptists and Socinians, can consistently with their own principles assert
the same thing with me.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 30 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000030]

For they admit that hypocrites are members of the
visible Church and acknowledge that they do not possess infallible judgment—
which was not always given even to the apostles, or else they would not have
admitted Ananias and Sapphira or Simon Magus.

I do not, however, deny that it is probable that wherever God erects a
particular church he has there some elect person or persons, either already
called or yet to be called, and that this is to be hoped. But while those
elect persons have not yet been effectually called, or after the called have
died, the same particular church will remain truly a visible Church. I also
know that God can sometimes send Isaiah to say to a people, *Hear, but do not
understand; see, but do not perceive*, and to bring it about that *the heart
of this people grows fat, their ears grow heavy, and their eyes close, lest
they see with their eyes, hear with their ears, understand with their heart,
turn, and be healed*, chapter 6:9–10.

Ames does indeed say in the *Marrow*, book 1, chapter 32, section 10, that *it
is highly probable that no particular church exists in which the true
profession of faith flourishes without some true believers also being found
in it.* But whatever is highly probable is not always most certain. For
something to be only highly probable is not for its contrary to be impossible;
nor is it absolutely necessary that it be so, nor does it belong to the
essence of the thing.

No one should say that by the same reasoning we suppose that at some time
there may not be one true believer in the catholic visible Church. For there
are very ample and numerous promises concerning Christ's seed, from which it
necessarily follows that there will always be true believers somewhere on
earth. These are members of the catholic visible Church. Consequently, by
the force of these promises there must always be true believers in the
catholic visible Church. But this cannot be said of all local, parish, and

13. Christ, John 3:26; John the Baptist, Matthew 3:5, 6; and the apostles,
Acts 2:38, 41 and frequently elsewhere, admitted to baptism as members of the
visible Church those who embraced and professed the Christian faith, without
any additional investigation or examination. It is replied in vain that
Christ and the apostles were endowed with a special spirit of discernment.
For this does not prove that they admitted people as members only because
they were endowed with saving faith. Christ admitted and called Judas.

Then, as regards the apostles, it is asked whether that spirit of discernment
was something extraordinary and peculiar to the apostles, or something
common and ordinary to all believers. The former cannot be said, because:
1. This is asserted nowhere in Scripture. 2. We nowhere read that such a
thing was promised. 3. Such an extraordinary gift would have been a
transient act rather than a permanent disposition. 4. Such a gift would
undoubtedly have been infallible; how then were Ananias, Sapphira, and others
admitted?

If the latter is said, by what testimony of Scripture will it be proved? It
was either something peculiar to believers of that time, which cannot be
proved, or common also to believers today—which I remember recently reading
was asserted by a certain Quaker, but by no one else.

Next, if they say that it was something ordinary, then they judged the inward
state from outward signs. These signs were either profession of faith and
voluntary subjection to the divine institutions considered simply—that is,
by simple abstraction—or the same profession accompanied by some eminent
effect, or finally the same profession accompanied by certain circumstances
which certainly and infallibly indicated that profession to be, here and now,
a most genuine sign of true grace. If the first is said, we have what we want.

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 32 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000032]

If the second, let them demonstrate that some such effect appeared in all who
were admitted at that time. If the third, let them identify these circumstances
and prove that they are infallible signs of inward grace, or that Scripture
mentions them as the unique foundation or formal reason for this admission.

14. This is certain: although pastors can determine from God's Word in the
abstract that all to whom the various signs of regeneration or non-regeneration
belong or can be applied are regenerate or unregenerate, they nevertheless
cannot, separately or conjointly in a council, judge with judicial authority,
positively determine, or pronounce that this particular person is regenerate
or that one endowed with true faith. Nowhere do we read that Christ granted
this power to pastors; rather, he reserved it to himself. See Romans 14:9–13.

Moreover, we nowhere have described a certain, fixed, and invariable rule
according to which we can render a judicial verdict concerning the inward
state of others. People can indeed examine themselves by the rule of God's
Word and pass judgment upon themselves, because to some degree they know
their own hearts. Sometimes they can also judge, so far as belongs to human
beings, the good condition of certain people from their heroic Christian
actions, and the evil condition of others from their most atrocious scandals.
Yet the latter is very uncertain unless it is established that they are
guilty of the sin against the Holy Spirit, or there appears a long and wholly
uninterrupted course of profane and similar actions.

But I say that no general and universal rule exists by which we can judge the
inward state of every other person, distinguish every hypocrite from true
believers, or discern in all others the least possible measure, so to speak,
of true grace. For who does not know that conversion or

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 33 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000033]

regeneration is an inward work and can be recognized by others only from
outward actions? Yet
these outward actions cannot indicate or certainly demonstrate such a thing
unless they flow from a good principle, tend to a good end, and are done
sincerely and from faith. But this manner of acting does not fall under the
eyes of others.

Who also does not know that many believers are infants, weak, and infirm; and
that some are continually harassed and often overcome by temptations and
corruptions, so that they do what they do not wish, fail to do what they wish,
and the spirit continually fights against the flesh? But who can perceive
this struggle in another? Or who, apart from some Quaker, can say that a
pastor or anyone else can infallibly judge and determine the inward state of
every other person? Where, I ask, is that standard according to which we can
or ought to render a judicial verdict about the inward motions of others'
hearts? Who then will say that God requires such a judgment always to be made
for admitting members of the visible Church?

15. If we say that true and saving grace, as known to us, belongs to the
essence of the visible Church as such, many absurdities will follow. They are
these:

1. The administration of the Word in the visible Church is not an ordinary
means instituted by God to convert the ungodly, because all the members of
the visible Church are already presumed to be converted and endowed with
true faith. Thus a hypocrite is now found in it only accidentally, for if he
had been known to be such, he would not have been admitted as a member.

2. Pastors as such ought to set forth nothing in their sermons that can tend
to humble souls, awaken consciences, convert the people, and win them for
Christ; but only those things which pertain to directing, confirming,
strengthening, and raising with comfort those already converted.

3. Christ instituted no pastors

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 34 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000034]

or teachers for gathering the elect, whom he nevertheless redeemed with his
blood, Acts 20:21.

4. Faith is not an effect of the instituted ministry, contrary to Romans
10:14.

5. By this reasoning many truly godly people would be excluded—those who are
bruised reeds and smoldering wicks—because perhaps they cannot distinctly
describe the manner and method by which they were converted to God; or
because they are harassed by certain corruptions, so that a judgment about
their condition can be rendered only with considerable difficulty; or
because they do not appear to have the express and articulate knowledge of
certain fundamentals and other things necessary to be known for passing life
without offense which some men require of everyone to be admitted.

6. No reprobate, non-elect person, or hypocrite would be or could be a true
member of the visible Church. What then is to be said about Judas?

7. Properly speaking, there would then be no hypocrite in the visible Church;
and if a hypocrite is not found in the visible Church, he will not be found
outside the Church. Thus there would be no hypocrite at all.

8. The Church of the elect and the Church of the called would then extend
equally far, and all the called would be elect. Indeed, the Church of the
elect would extend further, because some elect people cannot be admitted,
since they have not yet been called.

9. It would follow that many who constantly attend the administration of the
Word and sacraments and subject themselves to all Christ's institutions must
be numbered among those outside.

10. There would then be no force in that argument for reforming abuses which
is drawn from their being God's people, although we know that this argument
is frequently mentioned in the Old Testament.

11. Likewise, that argument which moves people to gratitude from the goodness
of God, who grants and freely gives many ecclesiastical privileges, as we
frequently see, would have no force.

12. That aggravation

[Unnumbered preface | ONB canvas 35 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000035]

or intensification of sins drawn from the fact that such
rebellious and wicked people were God's people would then have no place in
the Church, although Scripture shows that the prophets frequently used it.

13. By this reasoning every distinction between the visible and invisible
Church would be abolished.

14. Whenever someone appears destitute of saving grace, he must be
excommunicated or regarded as a nonmember. By what argument this can be
proved is so far hidden from me.

15. Or else an openly wicked person must not be excommunicated, because,
according to Paul in 1 Corinthians 5:12, those outside are not to be judged
by the Church. According to our opponents, such a profane person declares
himself already outside, or no member of the Church, by manifesting himself
destitute of faith.

16. It would also follow that members of the Church are not to be judged,
because the godly are not to be judged, while the ungodly are outside the
Church.

17. It would follow that a true member of the Church can never lose this
privilege, because he cannot fall totally from grace, while this privilege is
founded upon grace.

18. It would follow that every particular church could apply to itself the
promises that concern the catholic invisible Church.

19. Those who had been excommunicated or cast out of the Church would, upon
repenting, have to be baptized again, because previously they were not true
members of the Church and therefore their former baptism was invalid and in
reality no baptism at all.

20. Relative holiness would not be sufficient to constitute a member of the
Church, and consequently infants ought by no means to be baptized.

21. Finally, the true Christian faith publicly received, or sound doctrine,
would then not be an essential mark of the true visible Church.
