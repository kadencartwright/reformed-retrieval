# CHAPTER XX

## Certain statements of Dr. Burmann and several others against what was said above are examined

In order that the statements in the preceding chapter may be confirmed more
fully, I would like here calmly to examine what Dr. Burmann advances for the
opposing opinion in his *Apology*, chapter 2, pages 67–68, together with
certain statements of others.

Our opponents, and especially Dr. Burmann, maintain that a moral command
enjoins nothing which is a free constitution of God—something which God could
have made otherwise if he had wished. Consequently, they maintain that a
weekly Sabbath is not commanded by any moral precept of the Decalogue. When
Dr. Burmann argued thus, *Nothing is at once dogma and moral. A weekly Sabbath
is dogma; therefore*, and so forth, Dr. Essenius replied in *Vindiciae*,
chapter 2, pages 69–70: [*Obedience is nevertheless owed to a free divine
ordinance and constitution. Thus in the state of original integrity it was
owed to the constitution prohibiting the eating of the tree of the knowledge
of good and evil. Then, both under the Old and under the New Testament, it is
owed to each and every constitution concerning instituted worship according
to the character of each administration, and more particularly now to the
order of ecclesiastical polity, baptism, and the Lord's Supper. In all these
there is a free constitution on God's part, but on our part there is the
necessity of a command and necessary observance, and so forth. As for the
things contained in the Decalogue by those ten commanding words, the second
precept in their first table obliges us to observe the worship instituted by
God according to each administration. The third obliges us to glorify God
even by external speech, just as the fourth obliges us to observe a weekly
Sabbath to God.—Meanwhile, all these things as now constituted truly bind us,
although they were freely constituted by God. In view of his supreme power
and freedom, God could have ordered instituted worship otherwise; he could
have refrained from giving or instituting external speech, just as angels do
not properly possess it and the mute are without it, and so forth. Likewise,
although the weekly Sabbath was freely instituted, it nevertheless must be
observed even now under the New Testament, just as it has been revealed and
constituted for this administration.*] So far Essenius. We must now see what
Dr. Burmann replies in the place cited above.

He begins the sixteenth and final paragraph of that second chapter as follows:
[*This chapter displays poison in its tail when on page 70 it says* that the
second, third, and fourth precepts of the Decalogue contain instituted
worship; that all these now truly bind us; yet that God could have ordered
instituted worship otherwise.] I reply: 1. If Dr. Burmann judges this to be
poison, he will condemn others besides Dr. Essenius, for Essenius is not the
only one who offers us this poison, as we saw above. [Zanchi says in the place
cited above: *Since it is therefore established that God has always wished to
be worshiped by men with external worship also, and that men are led to do
this by nature; but that in the time of Moses worship had already been
corrupted among all nations; and that God gave a perfect law by which he
taught especially what in worship he disapproved and what he approved, it is
also evident that by one or more precepts,*

[Printed p. 198 | IA PDF 206 | IA leaf 206 | IA decausadei02brow]

*both every profane worship was prohibited and the use of lawful rites in
worshiping God was commanded. By universal consent, internal religion was
certainly reformed by the first precept. The remaining three must therefore
have been particularly ordained to reform external worship.*] 2. Dr. Essenius
did not say that these three precepts of the Decalogue's first table contain
instituted worship as though instituted worship, considered in the same way,
were contained and commanded in all of them. Much less did he say that none
of these precepts contains anything except instituted worship, in the sense
in which Dr. Burmann understands instituted worship, as we shall hear
immediately. 3. What sort of poison, I ask, is it to say that the second,
third, and fourth precepts of the Decalogue truly bind us now? Will it be
poison to say that God could have ordered his instituted worship otherwise?
Will Burmann not acknowledge this to be most true? I do not think that he
will say instituted worship flowed from God's nature and is immutable, since
he knows well enough that the instituted worship ordained under the Old
Testament administration has now been changed.

Let us see, however, by what arguments he refutes the statements of the
celebrated Essenius. [He says: *Look at those trumpeters for the Sabbath's
perpetual morality, who make the whole first table, with the exception of the
first precept, a matter not of natural but of positive law, dependent upon
God's free ordinance and therefore mutable and dispensable.*] I reply: Those
who say only that the second precept obliges us to observe worship instituted
by God and that the third obliges us to glorify God even by external speech,
and so forth, do not say that the whole first table except the first precept
is a matter not of natural but of positive law dependent upon God's free
ordinance. Burmann therefore wrongly infers this from Essenius's statements.
Essenius intends only to assert that in those commandments—the second and
third, for example—certain things are commanded which, in relation to God,
belong not to natural but to positive law, depend upon God's free ordinance,
and are therefore mutable and dispensable. He does not say, nor can it be
gathered from his words, that nothing is commanded in those precepts except
what is a matter of positive law and is mutable and dispensable. Since he
does not say this, Dr. Burmann wrongly infers from his statements that he
makes the whole first table except the first precept a matter not of natural
but of positive law, and so forth. Burmann himself is therefore trumpeting
things that are not true. That Dr. Essenius's words in this place imply
nothing of the kind can be evident at first glance to an attentive reader. He
explains his opinion more fully elsewhere in *Dissertation on the Perpetual
Morality of the Decalogue*, chapter 2, pages 25–27: [*I never said of the
third precept that it is wholly a matter of positive and not natural divine
law, for I am not unaware that the holiness of the divine nature requires
human speech no less than human reason to serve his glory. It therefore
requires that every blasphemy of the divine name be prohibited, and indeed
that no one employ it rashly, irreverently, or irreligiously.*] A little later
he says: [*I never denied absolutely that the second precept, or every
individual thing pertaining to it, is a matter of law natural even to God
himself. But it is evident from the terms that the acts of instituted worship
are a matter of positive divine law, just as natural worship is a matter of
natural divine law. The second precept concerns those acts. For in affirmative
precepts the contrary negatives are comprehended, and in negative precepts
the affirmatives. When one species of sins is prohibited, kindred species
must be considered prohibited at the same time. When adultery, for example,
is prohibited by the seventh precept,*

[Printed p. 199 | IA PDF 207 | IA leaf 207 | IA decausadei02brow]

*fornication, incest, and every internal and external impurity are prohibited
at the same time, while chastity, whether in marriage or celibacy, is
commanded according to each person's gift and calling. Likewise, when the
second precept prohibits the manner of worshiping God through or in images,
which was not instituted by God but rashly devised by men, every form of
self-chosen worship is prohibited at the same time, and the acts instituted
by God himself for worshiping him are commanded. So our Catechism, question
96.—The second precept therefore concerns those acts which belong to
instituted worship. Some of those acts certainly are mutable and dispensable,
and are otherwise in the New Testament than they were in the Old. 4. Did I
ever say that God could have morally permitted or even commanded images of
himself and the manner of worshiping him through them? On the contrary, I
said the reverse and from God's nature deduced his inability to be
represented, from his simplicity, in disputation 10, thesis 42. I had long
ago learned that truth in the Catechism, question 97: God neither ought nor
can be represented in any way. He cannot be, namely, both because of his
simplicity and because of his infinity.*] On page 31 he says: [*We do not,
however, say that each and every precept of the Decalogue is a matter of law
natural to God himself, since it is evident concerning some that they do not
thus necessarily flow from God's own nature but are rather matters of
positive divine law—for example, instituted worship of God, the weekly
Sabbath, distinct ownerships of property, the degrees of kinship prohibited
in marriage, and so forth, which could have been constituted either otherwise
or not at all.*] Later, in his fourth response, he says: [*I add that the
second precept as well as the first is a matter of divine law natural to us.
Indeed, further, the first is also a matter of law natural to God himself.
Since by his nature he is holy, cannot deny himself, and cannot give his glory
to another, he could not fail to present himself as such to rational
creatures, both in the state of original integrity, suitably to that
condition, and also in the corrupt state, at least so far as suffices to leave
them without excuse, and again in the state of restoration, suitably to that
condition. Similarly, the second precept is also so far a matter of law
natural to God himself that consciences are not bound to any instituted
worship except what he instituted. This dominion, this prerogative, is natural
to him: he reserves to himself the authority freely to prescribe these or
those acts pertaining to his worship, which people consequently must thus
observe when they become known to them—this preaching of the Word, this
administration of the sacraments, and so forth. God's nature certainly did
not necessarily require these things in just this way, and a sufficiently
evident change has been made in them, while the same law remains by whose
force we are obliged to observe them as they are now set before us under the
New Testament, just as those in covenant under the Old Testament were obliged
to the things set before them, and people before the fall to what was
appointed for them. God's most simple and infinite nature also particularly
requires at least that he not be expressed by any image and consequently not
be worshiped in one.*]

And when Dr. Heidanus further argued as follows: [*But what reason shall we
give that he wishes the second precept, by which the worship of images is
forbidden, to belong not to natural but to positive and free law? It is
certain that the Roman Catholics would pay dearly to have the matter so. For
if what is forbidden in that precept belongs not to natural but to positive
and free law, so that it could either not have been forbidden or its contrary
could have been commanded, there is no reason why that which is commanded in
that precept—namely, that*

[Printed p. 200 | IA PDF 208 | IA leaf 208 | IA decausadei02brow]

*God, who is Spirit, be adored in spirit and truth—should not likewise flow
from positive and free law, and thus worshiping him in spirit and truth
should not pertain to the essence of divine worship. Yet spiritual worship is
most directly derived from the worship prescribed by the first precept;
indeed, it is nothing other than a conclusion rightly deduced from the first
precept as its proximate and immediate principle. Once the sense of the first
precept is rightly understood, sound reason immediately infers from it what
is forbidden by the second precept as its necessary consequence. For if
Jehovah God alone is to be regarded as God and worshiped, he certainly ought
to be worshiped only in a manner befitting his majesty and agreeing with his
divine glory (Isaiah 42:8, and so forth). This is a necessary inference and a
natural consequence. If Jehovah alone is to be adored and glorified with
religious worship, then religious worship does not belong to a creature, much
less to an image. Furthermore, reason dictates that God be worshiped as his
nature requires; but God is Spirit, and so forth. Yet to adore toward an image
is not to worship God in spirit and truth but in a bodily manner and in a lie,
and so forth. Therefore when the apostle in Romans 1 censures idolatry among
the Gentiles, he says that they fell into it through abuse of right reason,
and so forth. It is plainly, therefore, a work of reason. Since its conclusion
is the same as the precept's own conclusion, it is now established that this
belongs to natural law. See Isaiah 44:18–19; Deuteronomy 4:12, 15.*]

Essenius replied: [*1. The prohibition against expressing God by any image or
worshiping him in one is altogether a matter of law not only natural to us
but also natural to God himself, as above, and so forth. I conceded not a
particle on this point to the Roman Catholics, and the arguments put forward
against us do not oppose us in any respect. 2. That God, who is Spirit, be
adored in spirit and truth is altogether also a matter of law natural to God
himself, since it is manifestly deduced from God's nature. 3. Just as the
first precept of the Decalogue requires acknowledgment of the true God as he
has revealed himself both by nature and Scripture, divine faith which
excludes doubt, entire love, sincere fear, religious worship, and so forth,
so the adoration of God in spirit and truth, or internal spiritual worship,
ought rather to be referred to that same first precept. In the second it is
presupposed, while the worship of God in an image—and therefore the making of
such an image—or in any other manner devised by men is further prohibited.
At the same time it is commanded that he be worshiped in the manner he
prescribed in his Word, so that the worship instituted by God himself may be
rightly observed. So our Catechism. Ursinus, commenting on it, says: The true
worship of the true God is therefore commanded, and at the same time a rule
is established that we confine ourselves reverently and religiously within
the bounds prescribed by God, adding nothing to worship divinely instituted
and not mutilating it in the least part. 4. That religious worship does not
belong to any creature, and consequently not to an image as its terminating
object, pertains quite plainly to the first precept. The second guards
against worshiping God toward or through an image, where the image is a means
devised by men through which God's worship is rendered to him.*]

He says similar things in the *preface to the reader* prefixed to the second
volume of his dogmatic system, where he thus rebuffs Schoock's slander that he
patronized papal idolatry: [*But with what* (he says) *knowledge and what
conscience does he attribute to me these words, printed in a distinct typeface:
“The second precept pertains to positive divine law”? And he does so as if I
patronized papal idolatry,*

[Printed p. 201 | IA PDF 209 | IA leaf 209 | IA decausadei02brow]

*or as though my opinion were like Vasquez's exception, that the worship of
images is contrary only to positive law. My words in the place cited read as
follows: Natural worship of God, which is required especially by the first
precept, is a matter of natural divine law; but the acts of instituted worship
(note well), which the second precept concerns, and a certain definite time
at which they especially are to be performed, and several other things,
pertain to positive divine law. I said that the second precept concerns, or
commands, acts of instituted worship—namely, through its affirmative part,
which must be elicited from the negative. This assertion is sufficiently
accepted among theologians and agrees with our Catechism, question 96. I said
further that those acts of instituted worship belong to positive divine law,
which is manifestly true from the terms. But I never said or intended to say
that the second precept as a whole, or its formally expressed negative
concerning religious images, specifically images of God himself and their
worship, belongs to positive divine law—as though it could follow from God's
nature that he should command himself to be expressed by some image and
worshiped in or through it, or even morally permit this. On the contrary,
when I previously treated the divine attributes, I deduced his inability to
be represented from his simplicity, so that I might refute that idolatry of
the Roman Catholics all the more from God's nature, under the title “On God,”
disputation 10, thesis 42. Nor may anyone press on behalf of that examiner:
“If the instituted worship to be rendered to God according to the second
precept belongs to positive law, then its opposite—worship invented by men,
such as religious images and the manner of worshiping God in them—is opposed
only to positive divine law and not to natural divine law; and whoever asserts
the former thereby also asserts the latter.” I deny that this follows. It is
the folly of corrupt human nature that when people institute at their own
discretion a worship they regard as divine, they transgress both God's nature
and the law natural to him. The apostle teaches in Romans 1:22–23 that this is
what happened here.*]

We now grasp the opinion of the celebrated Dr. Essenius. We must next see
what Dr. Burmann says.

[He says: *If this is so, the precept prohibiting the worship of images does
not bind under the New Testament unless by a new command given under the
Gospel, for the positive commands of the old law do not bind us unless renewed
in the Gospel.*] I reply: That inference is worthless. As for the proof, a
distinction must be made between those commands which are positive only with
respect to God and those which are simply positive and are not matters of law
natural to man himself. The *former*, which cannot properly be called positive
commands of the old law because they are not peculiar to that administration,
bind even now although not expressly renewed in the Gospel. The *latter* do
not. But, 2. Dr. Essenius never said that the precept prohibiting the worship
of images was a purely positive precept. Indeed, as we saw, he did not even
say that the precept was positive with respect to God himself. This argument
is therefore wholly inconsequent, resting on this false inference: If the
second precept obliges us to observe instituted worship of God, then the
precept prohibiting the worship of images is a matter of positive law in the
same way as all the free ordinances whose observance is enjoined by that
commandment. Dr. Burmann will never prove this inference.

Nevertheless he proceeds, relying on the same hypothesis, and further says:
[*Otherwise all positive commands ever given would bind us, or a reason for
the difference would have to be given—which*

[Printed p. 202 | IA PDF 210 | IA leaf 210 | IA decausadei02brow]

*the Roman Catholics would pay dearly to have so.*] I reply: 1. How little
this argument bears on the matter is abundantly plain from what has just been
said. 2. The reason for the difference has already been given: some things
are positive only in relation to God but are matters of natural law for us;
others are simply positive and are not even matters of natural law for us.
Furthermore, some things positive in relation to God are common to the church
under every administration; others are peculiar to this or that
administration. The *former* do not need a new renewal of their institution,
as the *latter* do. 3. Since, as was said, the precept prohibiting the worship
of images must not be numbered among these commands positive in relation to
God himself, it is evident that the Roman Catholic position is not confirmed
in any respect. To what purpose, then, are these things?

Still wandering in the same error, he further says: [*For if the prohibition
of images is positive, it does not flow from God's nature. Or if it does, as
it certainly does, then this precept is not positive but natural. This is
expressly argued by John 4:24 and Isaiah 42:8. Hence Paul rightly reproves this
image-worship among the Gentiles in Romans 1:21, and so forth, which he would
have done without foundation if this precept were positive. See also Acts
17:24–25, 30. It is therefore shameful to concede so much here against the
truth to Roman Catholics and Gentiles.*] I reply: We have seen Dr. Essenius
acknowledge that the prohibition of images is not positive; Dr. Burmann is
therefore fighting with his own shadow. 2. How will he prove that Paul would
have reproved this image-worship among the Gentiles without foundation if the
precept had been positive in relation to God? Can something which is a matter
of natural law for man not be censured among the Gentiles even though it is
positive in relation to God? Is not the second thing in that catalog of vices
in verses 29–31 something of this sort? Is the prohibition of fornication
natural to God himself? Does it flow from God's nature in such a way that God
would not be God if he even morally permitted that evil?

[He says: *But who of our party has taught that the second precept contains
instituted worship? The precept is directly negative: that an image is not to
be worshiped, nor God in an image. And on what basis? Because God cannot be
likened or compared to anything. What, then, is the precept's affirmative?
To glorify God—that is, to regard God as incomparable (Isaiah 40:13, 18)—and
therefore to worship him in a spiritual manner conformable to his nature.*]
I reply: He can learn from the statements in the preceding chapter who teach
us that the second precept contains instituted worship. Indeed, I believe he
will find few orthodox expositors of the Decalogue who do not teach the same.
2. We know that the precept is framed negatively and expressly prohibits the
worship of images, or rather the worship of God in an image. But he knows that
the affirmative is included in the negative and that these precepts are
expressed synecdochically. Thus, where one species of evil—whether or not it
is the gravest—is expressly prohibited, all the remaining species of the same
genus are forbidden. Therefore, when God here forbids us to use images in
divine worship, or prohibits this manner of worshiping God devised by us, he
also forbids us to worship him in any other manner of our own making.
Conversely, he commands us to worship him only in the manner he prescribed.
3. We know that God cannot be likened or compared to anything, and from this
it can rightly be inferred that he must not be worshiped through images. But
the very reason appended to this precept—namely, that God is jealous—plainly
urges that God must be worshiped in the manner prescribed by him and in no
other whatsoever. 4. To regard God as incomparable and glorify him as such

[Printed p. 203 | IA PDF 211 | IA leaf 211 | IA decausadei02brow]

pertains to the first precept rather than the second, unless it is restricted
to external worship. The sense would then be that the affirmative of this
precept is to glorify God and regard him as incomparable in that external
worship which he himself prescribed. 5. We acknowledge that this precept's
affirmative also includes worshiping him in a spiritual manner conformable to
his nature. But this does not prevent the precept from containing instituted
worship of God, for subordinate things do not conflict.

[He continues: *But to extend this so broadly as to involve in this precept
all rites of worship, the order of ecclesiastical polity, baptism, and the
Lord's Supper, as our author does, tends to make them all moral. The second
precept itself and its intention are one thing, while its particular
determinations and applications, which are distinct from it, are another,
just as in this way the ceremonial and judicial law were distinct from the
moral law.*] I reply: If instituted worship is a particular application and
determination of the second precept, it is not so absurd as he supposes to say
that the second precept obliges us to observe worship instituted by God. 2.
We do not say that all the rites of instituted worship, or everything
pertaining to instituted worship, are of the same nature as the precept
itself, which commands the observance and reception of worship instituted by
God. We know that this precept is not mutable, although those particulars can
be, and have been, changed under the Gospel administration. This nevertheless
does not prevent us from saying that the second precept commands the
observance of worship instituted by God, or concerns worship instituted by
God.

[*So too* (he says) *the Catechism explains these things in question 96.*]
I reply: The Catechism answers question 96, *What does the second precept
require?*, as follows: *That we not express God by any image or figure, nor*
(note well) *worship him in any manner other than that in which he commanded
himself to be worshiped in his Word.* Now when the Catechism says that this
precept requires us not to worship God in any manner other than that in which
he commanded himself to be worshiped in his Word, is it not evident that the
Catechism intends us to be bound by this precept to observe worship instituted
by God and, consequently, that all rites of Gospel worship—baptism and the
Lord's Supper—are now embraced in this precept? In what, then, did Dr.
Essenius err? Or how could Dr. Burmann just now ask who taught him that the
second precept contains instituted worship, when here he sees the Catechism
itself teach this? [Pareus says in explaining this question: *Two things are
contained in the second precept: the precept itself and an exhortation to
obedience. The end of the precept is that the true God, who in the first
precept commanded himself alone to be worshiped, be rightly worshiped—with
that worship, namely, with which it is right for intelligent creatures to
worship him and which pleases him, not* (note well) *with worship devised or
fabricated by men. In other words, it is that the lawful worship of the true
God be preserved uncorrupted and not violated in any way by superstitious
worship. The true worship of the true God is therefore commanded, and at the
same time a rule is established that we keep ourselves reverently and
religiously within the bounds prescribed by God, adding nothing to worship
divinely instituted and not mutilating it even in the least part. The Lord
expressly enjoins this upon us in other places also. True worship of God is
every internal or external work commanded by God, done in faith which
determines that the person and the work please God because of the Mediator,
with this chief end, that God may be glorified. And*

[Printed p. 204 | IA PDF 212 | IA leaf 212 | IA decausadei02brow]

*truly to worship God is to worship him* (note well) *as he commanded in his
Word that he wished to be worshiped.

Conversely, every form of self-chosen worship—that is, fictitious worship—is
prohibited. Thus we must not only refrain from regarding or venerating
creatures or statues as God, but we must not express the true God himself by
any image or figure, nor worship him toward or through statues or by any other
kind of worship* (note well) *which he did not command. For since God condemns
the principal, grossest, and most conspicuous species of false worship—namely,
the worship of himself toward statues—it is evident* (note well) *that he also
condemns the other species of false worship, since this is the fountain of all
the rest. He prohibits the grossest species not to exclude the others but
because it is the fountain of all the others. All forms of worship,* (note
well) *therefore, which were instituted not by God but by men and in which the
same reason for the prohibition is found, are prohibited by the naming of
this grossest species.*]

Finally Dr. Burmann concludes: [*Thus Dr. Walaeus writes very well in “On the
Sabbath,” page 623: The claim established by certain more recent writers of
this age—that the affirmative in the second precept is that we worship God
through hearing the Word, using the sacraments, caring for the poor, and so
forth—is without any foundation, because these are never opposed in Scripture
to the worship of images. Rather, worshiping God in spirit and truth is
opposed to it (John 4:24; 1 Timothy 2:8), just as it is also rightly explained
in our Catechism. See more there, and observe how well these things apply to
our author.*]

I reply: In the place cited the most distinguished Walaeus contends for an
opinion which we shall examine more conveniently later. He maintains that
external worship of God is commanded by the fourth precept, not the second.
But I would like to examine his arguments briefly here. Before the words Dr.
Burmann cites, he writes: [*Indeed, no reason can be given why they judge this
to be commanded by the second rather than the third precept.*] I reply: A very
good reason can be given why external worship of God is commanded by the
second rather than the third precept: the second treats external worship of
God, both the lawful worship which is commanded and the unlawful worship
which is forbidden. The third precept, however, concerns the manner in which
God's names, titles, attributes, ordinances, words, and works should be used:
reverently and with the greatest respect, both in our thoughts and meditations
and also in external speech, writing, and profession.

As for the words cited by Dr. Burmann, we say that the affirmative in the
second precept is that we worship God with that kind of worship which he
himself prescribed. Under the Gospel this comprehends hearing the Word, using
the sacraments, and so forth, because this manner of worshiping God is
prescribed under the present administration. We do not say this without the
best foundation, as is plain from what was said above. 2. Although worshiping
God through hearing the Word, and so forth, is not opposed in Scripture to
the worship of images, worshiping God with worship instituted by him can
rightly be opposed to worship devised by men, of which the worship of images
is only one species, though the grossest. The second precept prohibits not
only worshiping God through images but every worship invented and fabricated
by men. Just as self-chosen worship fabricated by man in general is opposed
to worship prescribed by God, so this species of worship—namely, hearing the
Word—can be opposed to the worship of images, because the latter is prohibited
and the former commanded. Indeed, the worship of images

[Printed p. 205 | IA PDF 213 | IA leaf 213 | IA decausadei02brow]

is opposed to God's ordinances in Habakkuk 2:18–20 and Deuteronomy 12:1–20.
3. Every worship not instituted by God is false and carnal. Those who rightly
worship God with the worship he prescribed therefore do not worship him
falsely and carnally but in spirit and truth. 4. We have already seen how this
is explained in the Catechism. Since the Catechism establishes that this
precept commands us to worship God in the manner in which he commanded
himself to be worshiped in his Word, it is evident that this precept commands
us to worship God by hearing the Word, using the sacraments, and so forth,
unless it is said that these form no part of God's external worship—which the
most distinguished man would never have said.

[*Thus Isaiah 40:22; Acts 17:24; Romans 1. Where Scripture expressly treats
the worship of images, men are recalled by Scripture to consideration of
God's immense glory and his infinite and spiritual majesty, which is asserted
against the finite fabrications of human art.*] I reply: These passages
certainly prove well that it is utterly unlawful and unbecoming to divine
glory and majesty to worship God through images and similar human
fabrications. But it does not follow that worshiping God in the manner he
himself prescribed is utterly unbecoming to God's immense glory and infinite
majesty. Nor can it be proved from this that observance of instituted worship
is not commanded by the second precept of the Decalogue.

He continues: [*Although the first precept also commands the acknowledgment
of God's majesty and immensity, it is properly commanded by the second precept
that we nevertheless adore and worship God as such, both outwardly and
inwardly, in a way worthy of his spiritual nature and immense majesty.*] I
reply: I acknowledge that the second precept requires us outwardly—but not
also inwardly, for that is properly required by the first precept—to adore
and worship God in a manner worthy of his spiritual nature and immense
majesty. But how does it follow from this that the precept does not also
require us outwardly to worship God in the manner he prescribed? These things
are subordinate and do not conflict at all.

[He adds: *Whatever is opposed to a negative precept must be such that what is
commanded in the affirmative part of the same precept can be gathered from
the nature of the thing forbidden. But no one can sufficiently gather from
the prohibition of images that God must be worshiped by hearing the Word and
using the sacraments. It can, however, be gathered that worship conformable
to his immense and spiritual nature must be rendered to him, and that
according to the prescription of his Word.*] I reply: It is true that whatever
is immediately opposed to a negative precept must be such that what is
commanded in the affirmative part of the same precept can be gathered from
the nature of the thing forbidden. Therefore, from the fact that this precept
prohibits worshiping God in a manner fabricated by men, we rightly gather that
this command requires God to be worshiped in the manner he prescribes. 2. It
is true that from the prohibition of images one cannot immediately gather
that God must be worshiped by hearing the Word and using the sacraments. It is
nevertheless gathered by means of the divine institution. From the
prohibition of all worship devised by men and not instituted by God—which
must be understood through the prohibition of images—the command of worship
instituted by God is gathered, and consequently the worship of God by hearing
the Word, and so forth, which is now commanded under this administration and
is worship conformable to God's immense and spiritual nature and to the
prescription of his Word.

[He says finally: *If external worship is opposed to the worship of images,
it necessarily follows*

[Printed p. 206 | IA PDF 214 | IA leaf 214 | IA decausadei02brow]

*that the affirmative part of this precept is mutable, since the patriarchs
worshiped God in one manner before the law was given, in another after the
law was given, and in still another after Christ's ascension to heaven, when
the Old Testament ceremonial worship was abolished. But this is contrary to
the opinion of all orthodox theologians, who acknowledge all the precepts to
be purely moral and immutable.*] I reply: 1. We do not say that external
worship is opposed to the worship of images, for the worship of images is
also external worship, though unlawful. We say that external worship
instituted by God is opposed to the worship of images. And although this
external and instituted worship is mutable and has sometimes been changed,
it does not follow that the affirmative part of this precept must be called
simply mutable. This remains immutable: God must be worshiped in the manner he
himself prescribed. When the particular ordinances are changed according to
the character of the various administrations, that which is properly the
general affirmative part of this precept is not likewise changed. Under every
administration it holds that God must be worshiped with the kind of worship
he prescribes and no other. Thus this moral precept remains immutable,
although those particular ordinances of divine worship which must be observed
by force of this precept for as long as they remain in force and are not
abolished sometimes change. In the same way, as he himself acknowledges, the
force of the fourth precept obliging the observance of one day in seven
remains, although the day which is seventh in order has changed.

Dr. Cocceius, in *Summary of Theology*, chapter 89, censures those learned men
who, in order to distinguish the second precept from the first, distinguish
between natural and instituted worship. They maintain that natural worship
is commanded by the first precept and instituted worship by the second,
through the prohibition of its contrary. He says this is not so and is
dangerous. Let us hear his reasons: [*It is not so, for the making of images
is prohibited not under the consideration that it is not commanded but under
the consideration that it provokes God to jealousy, because our God is
Jehovah and,* as the Catechism explains together with Scripture, *because an
image of God cannot be made.*] I reply: 1. Not only the making of images but
their worship—or rather the worship of God in them—is indeed prohibited,
among other reasons, because God is jealous. But it does not follow from this
that worship instituted by God is not commanded here or that worship devised
by men is not prohibited. Scripture shows God's fervent zeal even for his
instituted worship. See the example in Leviticus 10:1–2, where fire going out
from before Jehovah killed Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu when each took his
censer, put fire in them, laid incense on it, and offered strange fire before
Jehovah which he had not commanded them. Did not God's zeal also appear when
he struck 50,070 of the Beth-shemeshites because they had looked into
Jehovah's ark (1 Samuel 6:19)? And likewise when he struck Uzzah because of
his rash act (2 Samuel 6:7)? 2. Because our God is Jehovah, it also follows
very well that he must be worshiped only with worship instituted by him. 3.
It is true that an image of God cannot be made, but how does this prove that
instituted worship of God is not commanded by this second precept? Who will
deny that an image of other things can be made? Does this precept not prohibit
worshiping God through such images?

[He says next: *To prostrate or incline oneself is a sign of reverence which
nature*

[Printed p. 207 | IA PDF 215 | IA leaf 215 | IA decausadei02brow]

*dictates; it does not depend upon institution.*] I reply: What, I ask, does
this contribute to the point? The act of prostrating or inclining, insofar as
it is a natural sign of reverence, is not prohibited here. What is prohibited
is worshiping God by inclining oneself toward an image, a manner of worshiping
himself which God did not command, while he instituted another far more
spiritual manner. How does Cocceius show that this is dangerous?

[*It is dangerous* (he says) *because it thus implies that this sin is
mutable, since what depends upon a decree is mutable, as though one who adores
an image sins only by claiming for himself God's dominion, since God alone
possesses the power to institute his own worship.*] I reply: It is indeed
implied that many things enjoined by this precept are mutable, which is wholly
free of danger. The person who adores an image as the object of worship
violates the first precept. The person who worships God through or in an image
violates this second precept by denying God's dominion, for it belongs to him
alone to institute his own worship. Does it follow from this that self-chosen
worship is not condemned by this precept? Cocceius will not assert this, for
he immediately adds: [*We nevertheless judge self-chosen worship to be
contrary to this precept, just as establishing anyone by decree, and
self-chosen worship, is contrary to the first precept. For the person who
gives precepts about worship and establishes ordinances of service assumes
what belongs to God. But the person who worships God through his own
inventions is a sorcerer, an enchanter, and makes God like himself.*]
