QUESTION VII

Is Theology Theoretical or Practical?

[Marginal heading: The origin of the question.]

I. The Scholastics were the first to raise this question, and it was debated
at length among them long ago. Some maintained that theology is simply
speculative, such as Henry in *Summa*, article 8, question 3; Durandus in the
prolegomena to the *Sentences*, question 6; and Johannes Rada, controversy 3.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 20 | IA leaf 74 | PDF p. 74]

Others held it to be simply practical, such as Scotus and his followers.
Others said it was neither theoretical nor practical, but rather affective or
dilective, that is, a discipline higher than theoretical and practical
disciplines, since its end is charity, which is not contained under practice;
such were Bonaventure, Alberius, and Aegidius Romanus. Finally, others held it
to be mixed, at once speculative and practical, but more speculative, as the
Thomists held, or more practical, as Thomas of Strasbourg held.

[Marginal heading: And its importance.]

II. The question is necessary not only for understanding the true nature of
theology, but also on account of the controversies of our time, especially
against the Socinians and Remonstrants. They call theology practical in so
strict a sense that nothing in it is strictly necessary for salvation except
what pertains to moral precepts and promises. Consequently, obedience to the
precepts and confidence in the promises comprise the whole of religion as to
its fundamentals, with the knowledge of the mysteries excluded. Their purpose
is plainly to diminish the necessity of knowing the doctrines of the Trinity,
the incarnation, and so forth, and thus more easily to prepare the way for a
common religion through which all people indiscriminately may be saved—that
is, for atheism. Among the orthodox, some also judge theology to be merely
practical, and more judge it to be of a mixed genus; but some make it more
speculative and others more practical. We join the latter and judge that
theology is neither simply theoretical nor simply practical, but partly
theoretical and partly practical, since it joins together the contemplation
of truth and the practice of good. Nevertheless, it is more practical than
theoretical.

[Marginal heading: What a theoretical and a practical discipline is.]

III. A discipline is called *theoretical* when it is occupied with
contemplation alone and has no end other than cognition. A discipline is
called *practical* when it does not stop at the mere knowledge of a thing, but
by its nature and in itself tends toward practice and has operation as its
end. Again, a cognition may be directed toward operation as its end in two
ways: either absolutely and in itself, or relatively and incidentally. A
cognition is directed in itself toward practice when it is referred to
operation according to the nature of the discipline to which it belongs. In
this way cognition of ethical matters is practical, because the nature of
ethics requires that everything treated in it be referred to operation and
use. A cognition is referred incidentally to operation when it is not
practical according to the nature of the discipline to which it belongs, but
only by reason of the end proposed for it by the person who possesses that
cognition. Thus physics is practical incidentally when the person possessing
that cognition directs it toward operation or use. Theology is called
practical not only in the latter sense but in the former.

IV. A science practical in itself is not only one that concerns something
capable of being done and regulates and directs some operation. Otherwise,
the part of medicine that considers the parts of the human body, the diseases
of those parts, and the signs and causes of diseases would be speculative and
not practical, which is absurd, because the ultimate and principal end of
that part of medicine is not the cognition of diseases but their cure. A
science is also practical if it either impels to operation—as the cognition
of the good or evil consequences of virtue or vice impels one toward upright
actions—or is prerequisite to working and acting better, as is the
pathological part of medicine. Theology is called practical not only in the
first sense but also in the second and third, because no mystery is proposed
to our contemplation as something to be believed which does not incite us to
the worship of God or is not prerequisite to ordering that worship
successfully.

V. The arguments proving theology to be either theoretical or practical fail
and restrict it too much if they are taken exclusively of one or the other;
but if they are understood inclusively, both are equally true. For theology
is not of a simple genus, that is, only theoretical or only practical,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 21 | IA leaf 75 | PDF p. 75]

as physics and ethics are in philosophy, but of a mixed genus that includes
both relations.

[Marginal heading: THEOLOGY is theoretical-practical.]

VI. The following prove that theology is mixed, that is, partly theoretical
and partly practical. 1. Its object: God to be known and worshiped, as the
first truth and the highest good. 2. Its subject: the human being to be
perfected by the cognition of truth, by which the intellect is illuminated,
and by the love of good, by which the will is adorned—by faith, which extends
to things to be believed, and by charity, which extends to things to be
practiced. 3. Its principle: externally, the Word of God, which embraces the
law and the gospel—the former proposes things to be done, and the latter
things to be believed and known—and is therefore called the “mystery of
godliness” and the “word of life”; internally, the Spirit, who is the Spirit
of truth and sanctification, of knowledge and the fear of Jehovah, Isaiah
11:2. 4. Its form, which embraces the essence of true religion and requires
the cognition and worship of God. These are joined to one another by an
inseparable bond, just as light and heat in the sun are never separated from
one another. Therefore, cognition of God cannot be true unless practice
accompanies it, John 13:17; 1 John 2:5. Nor can practice be right and saving
unless cognition directs it, John 17:3. Hence Lactantius says in book 1,
chapter 1, “No religion should be adopted without wisdom, nor should any
wisdom be approved without religion.” And in book 3, *On False Wisdom*,
chapter 3: “All human wisdom consists in this alone: that one know and worship
God.” And in book 4, chapter 4: “Religion cannot be separated from wisdom, nor
can wisdom be severed from religion, because it is the same God who must both
be understood—which belongs to wisdom—and honored—which belongs to religion.
But wisdom precedes and religion follows, because knowing God comes first and
worshiping him follows. Thus there is one force in the two terms, although it
appears to be diverse; for the one is situated in perception, the other in
actions, yet they are like two streams flowing from one fountain,” and so
forth. 5. Its end: human blessedness, which consists partly in the vision of
God and partly in the enjoyment of him, from both of which assimilation to
God arises, John 13:17.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VII. For a science to be merely practical, its object must indeed be
something practicable and capable of being done. But for it to be
theoretical-practical, it is enough not that the object be brought into
practice, but that practice operate with respect to it. Thus God is not
something practicable, but practice must be occupied with worship and love of
him. Furthermore, although the material object is not something practicable,
the formal object—namely, God insofar as he is supernaturally revealed in the
Word—can be called partly theoretical and partly practical, because he is
revealed both as one to be known and as one to be worshiped.

VIII. *Speculative* and *practical* can be differentiae of the inferior
disciplines which the natural faculty of the intellect is capable of
reaching, so that an object cannot be determined both to contemplation and to
practice at the same time. But since theology belongs to a higher and more
eminent order, it is not confined within these narrow limits of nature, but
can easily embrace the speculative and the practical within its scope. In the
same way, the common sense eminently contains the differences of the external
senses, and rational life in the human being contains vegetative and
sensitive life.

IX. When eternal life is said to consist in the knowledge of God, John 17:3,
and happiness in the vision of him, this indeed shows that theology is also
speculative, since it has many theoretical objects. Yet one cannot therefore
infer that it is merely speculative, because this cognition is not only
theoretical but practical, 1 John 2:5, and in the usage of Scripture *vision*
denotes not only cognition but also enjoyment.

X. A discipline can be called practical either with respect to its ultimate
end, because it is directed toward operation as its end, or also with respect
to its object, because it is occupied with a practicable object that falls
under the will and action

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 22 | IA leaf 76 | PDF p. 76]

of the human being. Theology is called practical in the former sense, not the
latter, because besides merely practical matters it also has many theoretical
matters which constitute the doctrines of faith.

XI. Although the cognition of God and his attributes is not practical in the
strict sense in which *practical* denotes what regulates some operation so
that the thing known is done—as cognition of the law is the rule of obedience
and morally good acts—it is nevertheless practical insofar as *practical*
denotes what moves and impels toward operation, so that even if the thing
known is not done, it nevertheless incites to moral action. For God presents
himself to us to be known so that we may worship him; he has revealed his
power to us so that we may revere it, and his goodness so that we may love it.

XII. Theology is considered either abstractly in its object or concretely in
its subject. Although an ungodly theologian does not transfer it into
practice, it does not cease to be practical in itself, because the subject's
abuse does not overturn the object's legitimate use.

XIII. The theology of the blessed in glory cannot be called merely
theoretical, because blessedness embraces not only the apprehension of the
highest good through vision, which is in the intellect, but also the enjoyment
of it through love, which is an act of the will.

XIV. Theology is theoretical-practical in such a way that it must not be
called merely practical but also theoretical, so that the cognition of the
mysteries is an essential part of it. 1. God commands and urges the cognition
of truth no less than obedience to the precepts, Jeremiah 31:34. Indeed,
eternal life is also placed in the cognition of God, John 17:3. Nor should it
be said with Schlichting that the passage concerns cognition of the will
revealed through Christ in the gospel but not cognition of the nature. For
the text teaches that it concerns not only the will but principally the
nature: “that they may know you,” and so forth, namely, that you are the true
God, which denotes the nature, not the will. 2. Faith arises from knowledge,
and religion depends upon faith, Romans 10:17. 3. The Apostles' Creed, which
according to the Socinians contains the summary and articles of religion,
treats only things to be believed, not things to be done. 4. The cognition of
God is put for the whole of worship, Jeremiah 31:34; Isaiah 53:11; 1 John 2:3.

XV. Nevertheless, it is clear from the ultimate end, which is practice, that
theology is more practical than speculative. For although all mysteries do
not regulate operation, they nevertheless impel toward operation. Nothing is
so theoretical and remote from practice that it does not incite to wonder at
and worship God; nor is contemplation saving unless it is brought back to
practice, John 13:17; 1 Corinthians 13:2; Titus 1:1; 1 John 2:3–4; Titus 2:12.
