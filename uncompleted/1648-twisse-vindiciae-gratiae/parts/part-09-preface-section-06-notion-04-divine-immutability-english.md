SECTION SIX NOTION IV

PERKINS

God is not changed; and the things that are changed, with all their circumstances definite and fixed, are not changed without his immutable decree.

ARMINIUS

God’s decree is twofold: that of efficacious action and that of permission. Both are immutable. Apart from one or the other of these, and without the definite and fixed determination of this or that decree, a creature, however free, can neither change itself by its own act nor receive any change from elsewhere. But it is not absolutely necessary that God set these and no other limits of change, as though the creature—if it were possible, with God unconcerned about the change—could either change itself or be changed from elsewhere to such a point that God could not bring it into order and have an occasion for illustrating his glory. For to him for whom nothingness itself ought to have been material for declaring his glory, any change whatever of a thing produced by him out of nothing ought to serve the same end.

To what end these things serve I certainly do not understand. For Arminius did not dare deny what Perkins proposed, but he seems to have wished to add something which Perkins omitted—namely, that God has set other limits of change also besides those which Arminius himself, as though interpreting Perkins, explained in terms of God’s effecting and permissive decree. First, however, tell me: by what license of speech is this “bringing the creature into order” said to be “setting limits of change”? For example, to bring the creature into order is either to punish its sin and thereby illustrate the glory of his justice, or to forgive it and thence display the splendor of his mercy.

[Praefatio; printed p. 26; BSB canvas 34]

But is this to set a limit to sin? God punishes even in this age, but in doing so does he always set a limit to sin? No; rather, by this very means he sometimes gives free rein to sinners, especially whenever the punishment inflicted is spiritual. Then, whenever God brings a creature into order and thereby illustrates his glory, does not this very thing occur according to God’s immutable decree, in accordance with Perkins’s thesis?

2. Consider how far Perkins is from denying that God can bring a creature that has strayed beyond bounds into order and thereby illustrate his glory: he has determined that whatever it may be is brought about by God’s immutable decree for no other end than the illustration of his glory. Thus God’s decree concerning the acts of the creature, whether to be effected or permitted, and the illustration of divine glory are not to be counterdistinguished as diverse limits of the creature’s change set by God, but rather subordinated to one another, since for the illustration of his glory he decrees whatever he decrees will occur concerning creatures, whether by effecting it or by permitting it to occur. Hence Arminius’s phrase “as though the creature could change itself to such a point that God could not bring it into order,” although perhaps it can refute this examiner’s own fiction, certainly attempts in vain if it tries to invalidate some supposition of Perkins, since Perkins puts forward no supposition of that kind.

3. Then what he says—“if it were possible, with God unconcerned about the change”—is altogether beside the point. For Perkins is treating of the decree, whether effecting or permissive, as Arminius interprets him. Therefore, if, for form’s sake, he wished to exclude what Perkins said, he ought to have excluded both God’s efficient action and his permission, not merely his concern. But Arminius perceives that this would be stated so absurdly—namely, that something should be done by the creature with God neither effecting nor permitting it—that he preferred to string together things with little coherence (for perhaps he could deceive the reader because of the obscurity of the matter and his style) rather than pronounce grossly incongruous assertions.

4. But, unless I am mistaken, this is what he wishes to signify: although God did not concern himself with what the creature did, nevertheless, if it acted wrongly, he could restrain it. Thus, although Adam were so left to himself that he acted or omitted something altogether without God, he nevertheless would not pass beyond the bounds of divine providence, because God could bring the sinning creature into order and thereby illustrate his glory. But what have we to do with chimerical suppositions of this sort—indeed, suppositions of things which Arminius would readily grant neither exist nor can exist—namely, that God does not concern himself with the things done by human beings? What of the fact that this hypothesis cuts its own throat? For God exercises no providence over the same things for which he has no concern; therefore, the things for which God has no concern must by no means be subject to his providence. Next, God cannot bring a sinning creature into order unless this has been decreed by God from eternity; but if it has been decreed from eternity, by that very fact he must concern himself with when and how those things are to be attended to. Finally, even if Arminius can suppose that God has no concern for things that occur as regards their production by God, he cannot suppose that God has no concern for them as regards their permission by God. But if God permits something, and does so for this end—if it is a sin—that it may be a means of illustrating his own glory, this is precisely what Perkins means when he says that even in the case of a creature’s change, whatever it may be, it does not occur without God’s immutable decree, and that it occurs for the illustration of his glory—a point he expresses throughout, although he passes over it here. For it was not necessary for him to express it, since scarcely anything is better known among Christians than that God does or permits whatever he does or permits for his own sake. Yet Arminius does on occasion acknowledge, compelled by the evidence of this truth, that God permits even sin for this end.
