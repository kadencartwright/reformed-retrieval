QUESTION XI.

THE TRUTH OF SATISFACTION

*Did Christ truly and properly satisfy God’s justice in our place? Affirmative,
against the Socinians.*

I. Having asserted the necessity of satisfaction, we must discuss its *truth*.
The Socinians here not only call it into doubt but expressly deny it, although
otherwise they seem to wish to retain the name “satisfaction” and often use it
to create a false appearance. They use it, however, in a far different sense
from the orthodox, as will be evident from the state of the question.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. The question does not concern satisfaction of just any kind and
*improperly so called*, by which Christ satisfied all the conditions imposed
upon him by God’s will for procuring our salvation. To this extent they do not
deny but admit Christ’s satisfaction, as Crellius professes against Grotius,
chapter 6. Rather, it concerns *penal satisfaction, properly so called*, by
which he satisfied not only the divine will but also justice, taking our sins
upon himself. They deny this; we assert it.

III. Second, the question does not concern a metaphorical satisfaction made by
the injured party’s acquittance, which obtains something as a favor through
mere indulgence, as it were. The adversaries do not deny this and often thrust
it forward to deceive the simpler. Rather, it concerns the true and proper
satisfaction made through

[Printed p. 376 | Internet Archive PDF 404 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

payment of an equivalent price, which meritoriously obtains the accused
person’s deliverance from the judge’s justice. We proclaim this from God’s
Word, but they obstinately reject it.

IV. Third, the question is not whether Christ died *beneficially for us*, that
is, *for our good*, so that his death profits us in various ways. They freely
grant this. Rather, did he die *substitutively for us*, that is, *in our place*,
so that, substituted in our stead, he received upon himself the penalties owed
by us? This we maintain.

V. Finally, the question is not whether Christ is our Savior either *by the
proclamation of doctrine*, because he announced the way of salvation to us, or
*by the example of his life*, because he confirmed that way by virtues and
miracles, or *by power and efficacious communication*, because he will actually
give salvation at his appointed time. Socinus grants all these things to
Christ in *On the Savior*, book 1, chapters 5–6. Rather, was he Savior most
properly by satisfaction and merit? The adversaries openly attack this with
great effort; the orthodox steadfastly defend it and prove it invincibly by
various arguments.

[Marginal heading: The truth of satisfaction is proved: 1. From Christ’s redemption;]

VI. The first argument is drawn from those passages in which Christ is said to
have redeemed us, and indeed at the price of his blood. For where a price
properly so called—and a fully sufficient one—is involved, true satisfaction,
properly so called, must be given, since a price expresses a σχέσιν toward
distributive justice. Various passages prove this: “You were bought with a
price,” ἀγοράσθητε τιμῆς (1 Cor. 6:20); “You were redeemed from your vain way
of life, not with corruptible things, gold and silver, but with Christ’s
precious blood, as of a blameless lamb” (1 Pet. 1:18–19); “Christ gave himself
for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity” (Titus 2:14); “In whom we
have redemption through his blood” (Eph. 1:7); and “The Son of Man came to
give his life λύτρον ἀντὶ πολλῶν, a ransom-price for many” (Matt. 20:28).
Therefore the name Jesus was given to him because “he would save his people
from their sins” (Matt. 1:21).

VII. Although *redemption* is sometimes taken in Scripture for simple
deliverance granted freely without the intervention of any price—as Moses is
called λυτρωτὴς (Acts 7:35), and God is said to have redeemed his people from
Egypt (Deut. 7:8; Ps. 77:15 and elsewhere)—it does not follow that it must be
taken in that sense in this argument. Indeed, many considerations prove that
it must be understood properly and strictly. 1. This is the primary and most
proper meaning of the words λυτροῦν and ἀπολυτροῦν, from which we must not
depart without necessity. Nor does the adversary deny this in *On the Savior*,
book 2, chapter 1: “To redeem someone properly signifies nothing other than to
deliver a captive from the hands of the one detaining him by giving a price.”
2. The condition of the man to be redeemed demands this, since he is captive
not only to the devil and death but also to sin, both as to guilt and as to
stain, and to the law and divine justice—namely, ὑπόδικος τῷ Θεῷ and a son of
wrath. Therefore he could not be delivered from it unless satisfaction were
given. 3. This redemption was made by the intervention of a price (1 Cor.
6:20). Why mention λύτρου or τιμῆς if no price was given? The added claim that
this is said improperly because we are delivered freely at his appearing is
assumed without proof. Indeed, the contrary is soundly gathered from the fact
that this price is compared with the most precious things—gold, silver, and
gems—which have the character of a true price properly so called (1 Pet.
1:18). 4. Not only is λύτρου mentioned, but also ἀντιλύτρου, which denotes not
just any price but one equivalent to the debt, such as indicates an exchange
of the debtor and true compensation for the debt. This is the force of the
preposition ἀντὶ, which here denotes substitution

[Printed p. 377 | Internet Archive PDF 405 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and is also the sense in which Aristotle uses ἀντιλυτροῦν in *Ethics*, book 9,
chapter 2: to redeem another’s head with one’s own head.

VIII. Hence it is evident that this redemption was accomplished neither by
simply *free manumission*, as when slaves are set at liberty by masters, nor by
*mere might and power*, as captives are rescued from enemies’ hands, nor by a
*bare exchange*, such as commonly occurs in war, but by *just satisfaction*,
as when a surety pays for a debtor. For although free deliverance has place
with respect to *us*, who are saved freely and by God’s grace (Rom. 3:24; Eph.
2:8), deliverance by power with respect to the *devil*, from whose tyranny we
have been led out by the might of Christ the victor and conqueror (Col. 2:15),
and exchange with respect to *Christ*, who was substituted in our place,
nevertheless with respect to *God the judge*, just satisfaction had to
intervene.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because Christ died for us;]

IX. The second argument is deduced from those passages where *Christ is said
to have died for us*, not only *for our good and advantage*, but also *in our
place* by substitution properly so called (Rom. 5:6–7; 8:32; 1 Pet. 3:18).
That this and no other is the phrase’s meaning here is gathered as follows. 1.
This is the ordinary signification of the preposition ὑπὲρ used here, which,
when applied to a person, denotes substitution among the Greeks: ἀνάθεμα ὑπὲρ
ἀδελφῶν, “accursed in place of the brothers” (Rom. 9:3); “hardly will anyone
die for a righteous man,” that is, in his place (Rom. 5:7), as the Syriac
renders it by חֲלָף. 2. Elsewhere it is expressed by ἀντὶ (Matt. 20:28) and by
ἀντίλυτρον (1 Tim. 2:6), which necessarily implies substitution. Thus a head
was given for a head, as the law of retaliation required: ὀφθαλμὸς ἀντὶ
ὀφθαλμοῦ, “an eye for an eye” (Matt. 5:38). 3. He is said to have died for us
in a singular way incommunicable to all others, in which neither Paul nor Peter
can be said to have died and been crucified for us (1 Cor. 1:13). Yet each can
be said to have died for our good, namely, for our edification and the
confirmation of faith. Therefore a *vicarious death* must be understood, which
could belong neither to the apostles nor the martyrs. Although the apostles
can also be said to suffer for the church (Col. 1:24), it does not follow that
the account is the same as Christ’s death. They suffered to edify, confirm, and
console the church, as 2 Corinthians 1:6 expresses it, ὑπὲρ τῆς ὑμῶν
παρακλήσεως, “for your consolation”; but Christ alone suffered to redeem it.
And although we are commanded to lay down our lives for the brothers (1 John
3:16), as Christ laid down his life for us, so that we do not refuse to face
the danger of death, and even constantly to undergo death itself, whenever
God’s glory, our neighbor’s salvation, or the church’s edification seems to
demand it—as was seen in the martyrs—from this it is rightly gathered that in
this respect we ought to imitate Christ’s example. It does not follow, however,
that the account of our death for the brothers is altogether the same as that
of Christ’s death for us. For we cannot give an ἀντίλυτρον for the brothers to
deliver them from eternal death (Ps. 49:7), nor reconcile them to the Father
and cleanse them from sins by our blood, as Christ did. Thus our death is
compared with Christ’s according to one end, not all ends—according to the
*example of love*, but not according to the *merit of satisfaction*. The word
καθὼς implies likeness, not equality, as it often does elsewhere (Matt. 5:48;
6:12).

[Marginal heading: 3. Because he bore our sins;]

X. The third argument is from passages in which Christ is said to have *borne
our sins* and to have been afflicted, wounded, and killed for them, as in 1
Peter 2:24: “He bore our sins in his body on the tree.” This is taken from
Isaiah 53, which the Chaldean Paraphrase and the ancient Jews acknowledge to
treat the Messiah: “He himself bore our sicknesses and carried our sorrows; he
is crushed for our iniquities; the chastisement of our peace was

[Printed p. 378 | Internet Archive PDF 406 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

upon him; Jehovah caused the iniquity”—that is, the penalty for the
iniquity—“of us all to fall upon him”; and verse 10, “when he makes his life
אָשָׁם, a victim for sin,” and so forth. That all these imply satisfaction is
gathered from various considerations. 1. From the phrase *bearing sins*.
Although “to take away” and “to bear” are sometimes placed by metonymical
transfer for removing and forgiving (Ex. 34:7; Num. 14:18), it does not follow
that the phrase must be taken so here. Indeed, there are very weighty reasons
why we must not depart from the primary and most common meaning, according to
which Socinus himself acknowledges in *Lectures*, chapter 21, that bearing
sins is the same as *bearing the penalties of sins*. For not only does the verb
נָשָׂא occur, which is sometimes referred to the simple removal of sin, but
also סָבַל, which denotes the carrying and bearing of an imposed burden and so
clearly indicates the bearing of punishment. 2. From the *manner of bearing*,
which is described as being crushed and wounded (vv. 5–6) and as the *casting
of them upon him*. This could not be said unless he took the penalty of sin
upon himself. Likewise, Peter says that he *bore them in his body on the tree*,
namely, through undergoing punishment. 3. From the likeness: he is said to
have borne sins in the way the victims bore them, since he made his life an
אָשָׁם, a victim for sin (v. 10), namely, by undergoing death and shedding
blood in sinners’ place. 4. Everything indicating true satisfaction occurs
here: the *impelling and meritorious cause*, namely, our sins, not his—he was
crushed for our transgressions (vv. 4–6); the *bearing of punishments*, because
he bore our sorrows (v. 4); *imputation by God the judge*, since Jehovah caused
the iniquities of us all to fall upon him (v. 6); *Christ the surety’s
voluntary undertaking*, since he was afflicted and did not open his mouth,
and so forth (v. 8); and *expiation of sin and full payment of the debt*,
because he laid down his life for guilt and was taken from judgment and
confinement (vv. 8, 10). Why would all these things be said if he furnished us
only an example of patience and love and not the price of satisfaction?

XI. Although in Matthew 8:17 Isaiah’s oracle is said to have been fulfilled
when Christ healed bodily sicknesses, which he did not bear but only removed,
it does not follow that the same must be said of the soul’s sicknesses, or
sins, because the accounts of the two differ. For to heal the former Christ
himself did not have to become sick; Christ’s power alone was required. But to
heal the latter justice was required in addition: he first had to bear and
receive them upon himself in order to remove them from us. Hence the prophet
introduces him as wounded and crushed. This was not necessary for healing
bodily sicknesses, but only for bearing the soul’s sicknesses. From this it is
not difficult to gather what the Holy Spirit regarded in this oracle and how
it can be said to have been fulfilled when Christ healed bodily sicknesses.
For it treats primarily and directly spiritual weaknesses and sicknesses, or
sins, whose penalties were imposed upon Christ so that he might pay them in
our place. But because bodily sicknesses belong to the penalties of sins, it
also regarded them secondarily and subordinately, since he possessed the right
to heal them. Thus what the prophet said generally of all sicknesses, Peter
applies specifically to the sicknesses of the soul, and Matthew to bodily
sicknesses. Matthew does not exclude but rather includes the healing of the
soul’s sicknesses, showing that when the cause is removed the effect is also
removed, since these posit each other: sicknesses of the soul draw bodily
sicknesses after them, while bodily sicknesses presuppose sicknesses of the
soul. Thus Christ is said to have borne both, but differently according to the
different nature of the subject: the former by removing them through efficacy
without bearing them through suffering; the latter in both ways, by bearing
and removing them. Nor, if Matthew says that Christ healed bodily sicknesses
so that the prophet’s prediction might be fulfilled, can it legitimately

[Printed p. 379 | Internet Archive PDF 407 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

be inferred that the Holy Spirit regarded only them, because it is well known
that in Scripture things are said to be fulfilled not only when they are
perfectly and ultimately completed, but also when they begin to be fulfilled
with respect to some part.

XII. Here belong the passages in which Christ is said to have been made *sin*
and a *curse* for us (Gal. 3:13; 2 Cor. 5:21). For why would he be said to have
been made *sin* by God the judge—that is, a victim for sin (Lev. 5:9)—and a
*curse*—that is, the subject of the curse and execration which the law
threatened against sinners—and this not for himself, who was most holy and
always supremely beloved by the Father, but for us, unless, substituted in our
place, he received upon himself the whole curse of the law owed to our sins,
that he might bear it and by bearing remove it, and thus procure for us the
blessing, that is, remission of sins and the right to life with God? What did
this contribute to an example of patience or the confirmation of doctrine? Who
does not see that Christ’s *true substitution* in our place, and from it true
satisfaction and expiation of sin, is indicated here? Nor can it be objected
that *Christ is said to have been made a curse, not because he properly bore
that curse, which could not fall upon him as blessed and most innocent, but
because he underwent the punishment of the cross, which was cursed under the
law*. The apostle’s very words and the redemption from the law’s curse which
he procured for us by his death prove the contrary. For how could he be said to
have been made a curse—and indeed to redeem us from it—unless he took it upon
himself? Nor does his character as God’s only begotten and blessed Son stand in
the way, because he did not sustain it in and for himself as the Son of God,
but as our surety for us, whose person he sustained. With respect to us, he who
was blessed forever can be said to have sustained the penalty of the curse for
our sins.

XIII. Hence it is clearly gathered in what sense he is said to have been
delivered *for our sins* (Rom. 4:25): not merely *occasionally*, as Socinus
wishes, because our sins were the occasion of the death he suffered, or
*finally*, as if Christ died only to draw us away from sins or render us
certain of their remission, but *meritoriously*, so that sins were the
impelling meritorious cause for which he was consigned to death to bear their
penalty and remove their guilt. For he is said to have been delivered for sins
in the same way sacrifices were offered for sins, namely, on account of their
guilt and for its removal. Thus the *guilt* of our sins was the meritorious
cause of Christ’s death, and *its expiation and abolition* the final cause.

[Marginal heading: 4. From Christ’s sacrifice;]

XIV. This is further confirmed, fourth, from *Christ’s sacrifice* offered on
the cross, of which the Holy Spirit speaks so often (Isa. 53:10; John 1:29;
Eph. 5:2 and throughout Hebrews). For why would Christ so often and expressly
be set before us as a true priest properly so called, far more excellent than
all the Levitical priests, who appeased God’s wrath by offering himself and
obtained eternal salvation for us, unless to represent more clearly, as the
truth of the ancient figures, the full expiation of sin furnished by his
satisfaction? For since by those expiatory victims doctrine was not confirmed,
an example of obedience and love was not given, a covenant was not struck, and
God’s wrath was not appeased by themselves, they must have been instituted
primarily to represent Christ’s sacrifice and satisfaction. This is proved
specifically: 1. From the character of the *priest*, which Christ sustained
here, since he was appointed ἐν τοῖς πρὸς τὸν Θεόν, in things to be performed
before God, namely, to appease him by some ἱλαστικῷ sacrifice. 2. From the
character of the *victim*, which was substituted in the

[Printed p. 380 | Internet Archive PDF 408 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

sinner’s place to bear the penalty of death owed to him. The rite of *laying
on hands* and *confessing sins* over the victim’s head for it to bear them
proves this. 3. From the sacrifice’s threefold effect: with respect to *God*,
by ἱλασμὸν, or the *appeasement* of his wrath; with respect to *sin*, by
καθαρισμὸν and ἁγνισμὸν, the *expiation* and removal of guilt; with respect to
*man*, by ἄφεσιν, or *remission*, which followed from God’s propitiation and
sin’s expiation. For the person could not be delivered and obtain pardon
without substitution of a victim, God could not be appeased without shedding
blood, nor sin expiated without undergoing punishment.

XV. In vain Volkelius and others object here: 1. *Not all propitiatory
sacrifices foreshadowed Christ’s sacrifice, but only the solemn annual
sacrifice customarily offered on the day of atonement* (Lev. 16), *which
contained no satisfaction, since this could proceed neither from the victims
themselves nor from the high priest’s person*. Paul, however, opposes not just
one particular propitiatory sacrifice but all of them to Christ’s sacrifice
and deduces their abolition from it (Heb. 7:27; 10:4–5, 11). Nor could the
continual sacrifice offered daily or the other ἱλαστικὰ sacrifices of lambs
which served private persons have regarded anything other than foreshadowing
the offering for us of God’s spotless Lamb. Nor is it an obstacle that they
were not offered for all in common but for individuals particularly. For as
the sacrifices made for all in common signified that Christ would become the
propitiation of all his own, so those made for individuals particularly
indicated that individual persons burdened by sin must seek and obtain
propitiation in Christ’s sacrifice. Although these sacrifices contained no
satisfaction properly so called before God, since a human soul is too precious
to be redeemed by animal blood, they nevertheless gave a typical and
ceremonial satisfaction with respect to the purity of the flesh (Heb. 9:13).
This is ascribed neither to the offered victim separately nor to the offering
priest, but to both conjointly by God’s own ordinance.

XVI. 2. They object that *expiation is nothing other than full deliverance
from the death of sin, which must be attributed to Christ’s death not
meritoriously but only exemplarily and declaratively*. But here cause is
confounded with effect, and what belongs to the judge who delivers with what
belongs to the surety who pays for the accused. The judge grants deliverance,
but the accused—or another in his name—makes expiation. Hence follows the
cleansing of the offense and removal of guilt through bearing one’s own or
another’s punishment. Further, if death stood only declaratively toward
making expiation for sin, it contributed no more than the legal victims,
which could testify to the same thing, yet Paul expressly denies that sins
could be expiated by them. Thus this expiation would have to be ascribed no
less to his resurrection than to his death, which Scripture never does. Add
that declaration regards men, but expiation God. The former belongs rather to
the prophetic office, the latter to the priestly. Nor, because expiation is
sometimes attributed to God himself (Deut. 32:43; Ps. 51:9), who nevertheless
does not make satisfaction, must the account of the expiation attributed to
Christ as victim immediately be the same. It must be understood differently
according to the subject to which it is attributed. With respect to God, *to
expiate* is to admit the expiation made by the priest through appeasement and
the remission of sin. But in priest and victim, it is to procure expiation
meritoriously by shedding blood and undergoing vicarious punishment.

XVII. 3. They object: *Sacrifices were offered only for lighter sins committed
through thoughtlessness or error, not for graver and voluntary sins, for which
no sacrifice was instituted. But Christ’s death was borne for all sins without
distinction.* This is assumed without proof. Indeed, in the annual sacrifice
on the day

[Printed p. 381 | Internet Archive PDF 409 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

of atonement the goat is expressly said to have borne all the iniquities of
Israel’s children (Lev. 16:21–22), and elsewhere sacrifices are read to have
been offered not only for those committed בִּשְׁגָגָה, *through error*, but
also for voluntary sins denoted by the names אָוֶן, אָשָׁם, פֶּשַׁע, and the
like. Nor, if in Hebrews 9:6–7 the priest is said to offer ὑπὲρ τῶν τοῦ λαοῦ
ἀγνοημάτων, “for the people’s ignorances,” does it follow that voluntary sins
are excluded, because ἀγνόημα is a name given to sins of every kind, since
*everyone who sins is ignorant*. Hence sinners are commonly called *fools* and
ἀνόητοι in Scripture. In the Septuagint, ἄγνοια often corresponds to פֶּשַׁע,
אָשָׁם, and מַעַל, which denote wickedness and rebellion. If no sacrifice is
read to have been instituted for certain graver voluntary sins—homicide,
idolatry, adultery, and similar sins committed בְּיָד רָמָה, *with a high hand*
and through pride—it was because God willed them to be punished by capital
punishment, and so the sinners had no need of this remedy, since their death
was like a certain public expiation.

[Marginal heading: 5. From reconciliation with God;]

XVIII. Fifth, we argue from our *reconciliation* with God, which Christ
procured for us by his death. For since this reconciliation supposes the
settlement of the disagreement between God and the creature on account of sin,
it could not have occurred without a satisfaction removing the twofold
obstacle encountered: on God’s part, justice, which had to be satisfied; on
men’s part, sin, whose guilt had to be expiated by undergoing the penalty owed.
The apostle commonly teaches that Christ procured this reconciliation for us
(Rom. 5:10; 2 Cor. 5:18–19; Col. 1:20–21, and elsewhere).

XIX. The adversaries’ exceptions amount to this: *This reconciliation is said
to occur by our conversion to God, not by appeasing divine wrath, because God
is not said to be reconciled to us, but we to God. Indeed, he himself is said
to procure our reconciliation, which is a sign not of one wrathful, but of one
already appeased.* But many things refute this πρῶτον ψεῦδος of theirs. 1.
Scripture sets forth a twofold enmity and alienation: not only on men’s part,
who by sin have become θεοστυγεῖς, that is, haters of God (Rom. 1:30), and his
enemies in evil works (Col. 1:21), but also on God’s part by the wrath revealed
from heaven against all ungodliness (Rom. 1:18), from which men are by nature
*sons of wrath* (Eph. 2:1). God is said to have eyes purer than to behold evil
(Hab. 1:13) and even to be unable to endure falsehood and so forth (Ps. 5:4–6).
Therefore, as there was alienation on both sides, reconciliation had to be
furnished on both sides: on God’s part by turning aside wrath, and on men’s by
conversion. The apostle clearly teaches this in 2 Corinthians 5:18–20 when,
from the fact that God reconciled us to himself through Christ (v. 18), he
shows that the apostles in Christ’s name exhort sinners that they too be
reconciled to God. 2. If reconciliation were nothing other than conversion,
it would better be credited to Christ’s glorious life than his bloody death,
and no reason could be given why the apostle sets forth sanctification as the
end of reconciliation (Col. 1:22), since nothing can be both its own means and
end. 3. The reconciliation understood is one made through the *non-imputation
of sin*, founded upon its imputation to Christ when he was made sin for us (2
Cor. 5:18, 21), and through Christ’s substitution in our place by dying for us,
as is gathered from comparison with one who undertakes to die for a good man
(Rom. 5:7). This implies satisfaction, not simple conversion. 4.
Reconciliation is made through peacemaking by blood (Col. 1:20) and ἱλασμὸν (1
John 2:2). But this does not indicate conversion, but primarily the appeasement
of God’s wrath procured by a victim’s death.

XX. Although Scripture commonly refers this reconciliation

[Printed p. 382 | Internet Archive PDF 410 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

to men with God rather than to God with men, because offenders need
reconciliation to the offended, this does not exclude but supposes God’s
reconciliation with us. An offense cannot exist without injury to justice,
which must first be appeased before God can reconcile men to himself and admit
them to the communion of his grace. Nor, if God himself is said to have
procured this reconciliation, does it follow that he was not wrathful but
already appeased and propitious toward us. It indeed follows that he was moved
by a love of benevolence toward us to decree this reconciliation, but not
immediately that he be regarded as already appeased and reconciled. Rather, he
was to be reconciled and appeased concerning our sins, with which he could not
but be angry.

XXI. In vain it is further added that *Christ is called our ἱλασμὸν and
ἱλαστήριον, not because he appeases God’s wrath toward us, but because he
testifies that God is already appeased and not at all wrathful*. For Christ’s
blood was shed not to attest but to obtain the remission of sins, as occurred
in the Old Testament’s propitiatory sacrifices. Otherwise Christ would have
had no need to die and shed his blood, since he could sufficiently testify to
this by his doctrine and life. Nor, if the ark’s covering is called
ἱλαστήριον improperly and declaratively, because God declared from it his
benevolence toward the people, must the same be said of Christ. This is
attributed to him most properly, not so much passively as actively, because he
made propitiation for us by his blood, so that what was foreshadowed only
typically and symbolically at the mercy seat through sprinkling the victims’
blood, Christ furnished truly and properly by shedding his own blood. Although
by his continual intercession in heaven he obtains for us the application and
fruit of this propitiation, he therefore cannot be said to have made it only
in heaven. Nor does Hebrews 2:17 mean this. There Christ is not said to
ἱλάσκεσθαι, or expiate sins, in heaven, but only that for this reason he had to
be made like his brothers in all things, so that he might become a faithful
priest τὰ πρὸς τὸν Θεόν εἰς τὸ ἱλάσκεσθαι τὰς ἁμαρτίας τοῦ λαοῦ. He did this
by his death and passion, as the following words indicate.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the nature of Christ’s death;]

XXII. Sixth, the same is confirmed from *the nature and circumstances of
Christ’s sufferings and the kind of death* he bore, in which nothing was
lacking for true and full satisfaction. 1. Not the *essence or kind of
punishment*, because the death threatened by the law was inflicted upon him:
not a common and ordinary death, but a violent and most bitter one, inflicted
as a punishment and cursed by God himself. In it, besides the utmost disgrace
and the sharpest pains inflicted upon his most holy body, his soul was seized
with horrible dread and sadness and struggled with such anguish and fear that
an angel had to appear to strengthen him, and sweat flowed from his body like
clots of blood. He offered prayers and supplications with loud crying and
tears to him who was able to save him from death (Heb. 5:7), and with a most
sorrowful voice complained that he had been forsaken by God the Father—not as
to dissolution of the union, participation in holiness, and support of power,
but as to withdrawal of the vision of joy, suspension of comfort, and full
enjoyment and sense of happiness. These things are such that they can have no
other legitimate cause than avenging justice demanding from Christ the fullest
satisfaction for us, unless we wish Christ to have been softer and more timid
than innumerable martyrs. They bore without fear and cheerfully and with no
testimony of sadness and dread the same most bitter bodily death, if not an
even more bitter one with respect to the most exquisite tortures—a thought
Christian ears cannot endure. 2. Not *time*: although its duration was finite,
its value was infinite from the dignity of the suffering person. 3. Not the
*identity of the sinful person*: although the law demands this κατὰ

ἀκριβοδίκαιον, the gospel according to paternal ἐπιείκειαν admits substitution,
as said before, and is satisfied that sin be punished although the sinner is
not always punished.

[Marginal heading: 7. From God’s attributes.]

XXIII. Seventh, when satisfaction is asserted, there are wonderfully
illustrated *mercy*, which was so great that he did not spare his own Son in
order to spare us; *justice*, which demanded the very blood of God’s Son that
it might remain inviolate; and *wisdom*, which found the marvelous way of
reconciling mercy with justice and solving the knot otherwise insoluble. The
conscience terrified by the sense of sin, judgment, and the divine curse is
also calmed. But if satisfaction is denied, besides calling into doubt God’s
own *truth*, since he so often threatened sinners with death and curse, and
greatly obscuring *justice* itself, since it would not only justify a convicted
criminal while sin goes unpunished but also bestow the amplest rewards upon
him, no few absurdities follow: 1. Our redemption can be attributed no less to
the apostles’ and martyrs’ death than to Christ’s, since they too confirmed
the gospel’s doctrine by their blood and gave us extraordinary examples of
patience and obedience. 2. Christ saved us by his life and miracles rather
than by his death, since the proclamation of doctrine and example of life were
far more illustrious. 3. The priestly office on earth is removed and confounded
with the prophetic and kingly offices. 4. The Old Testament faithful were not
saved through Christ, since the examples of his virtues and proclamation of
his doctrine did not reach them.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XXIV. Although the word *satisfaction* does not occur expressly and αὐτολεξεὶ
in Scripture, it is enough that it is contained equivalently and implicitly
under other words which either have no meaning or imply true satisfaction
properly so called, such as ἀπολύτρωσις, ἀντίλυτρον, ἱλασμός, τιμή, κατάρα,
θυσία, προσφορά, and the like, of which we spoke above.

XXV. Just as Christ principally assumes a twofold σχέσιν with respect to the
faithful—that of *surety and sponsor*, who had to pay for them, and of *head
and Lord*, who willed to work in them by animating and governing them by his
Spirit—so in his death and sufferings he proposed to himself a twofold end:
first, to pay divine justice the *price of redemption* for us; second, to
provide an *example for imitation* in following his footsteps. Hence his
sufferings can be regarded either as *satisfactory* or as *exemplary*.
Therefore, although they are set before us as an example (1 Pet. 2:21), and
Christ’s death for us is compared with the death which at God’s command we
ought to undergo for the brothers (1 John 3:16), it does not follow that there
was no true satisfaction in his death, because positing one end does not
exclude but rather supposes the other.

XXVI. It is one thing to speak of proper satisfaction and payment, another of
another’s and vicarious satisfaction. The former removes all remission and
grace, but the latter does not. Thus satisfaction can coexist with grace, so
that grace is shown to one while satisfaction is exacted from another. Hence
when remission and satisfaction are referred to the same person they are
ἀσύστατοι, incompatible, but not when referred to different persons.
Satisfaction has God for its object, remission men. Satisfaction is made to
God’s justice, and therefore sin is freely remitted to the sinner. Thus justice
and mercy kiss each other: the former is exercised against sin, the latter
toward sinners. Remission is free with respect to us because just satisfaction
was exacted from the surety. Nothing is exacted from us because full payment
has been made.

XXVII. If Christ made satisfaction, it does not immediately follow, precisely
speaking, that he made satisfaction to himself as Christ, but as God and the
Son of God. Thus he does not give and receive payment as the same and in the
same respect: he gives it as θεάνθρωπος and receives it as Λόγος; he gives it
as mediator and receives it as judge. It is not absurd

[Printed p. 384 | Internet Archive PDF 412 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

for the same person to make satisfaction to himself when the subject is not
private satisfaction, by which private loss is compensated or a monetary debt
paid—for thus he would receive from his own and pay himself—but public
satisfaction, by which a public injury is repaired. Thus a judge who has
violated the law as a private person can make satisfaction to himself as judge
by paying, either personally or through another, the penalty threatened by the
law. This is how matters stand here.

XXVIII. Although Christ did not bear eternal death but only a temporal death
of three days, he nevertheless paid no less what we owed with respect to the
infinity of the punishment. For although it was not infinite in duration, it
was equivalently infinite in value because of the infinite dignity of the
suffering person. It was not the suffering of a mere man but of true God, who
acquired the church with his own blood (Acts 20:28), so that what finite time
lacks is supplied by the condition of the divine person, which adds infinite
weight to the temporal suffering. Yet it would be wrong to gather from this
that, because the suffering person was infinite, one drop of Christ’s blood
could have sufficed for our redemption. Although every suffering possesses
*infinite value subjectively* on account of the sufferer, only death possessed
infinite value *objectively* with respect to the judge inflicting it. The
dignity of the person can increase the dignity of the punishment he suffers,
so that the more illustrious the person who suffers, the more severe it is
reckoned; but it cannot compensate for the very kind of punishment sanctioned
by the law. Death alone corresponds to the law and agrees with justice.

XXIX. Although despair and raging are joined with the punishments of the
damned, it does not follow that Christ, in bearing the punishments owed to sin,
had to be exposed to them. They do not belong to the essence of punishment as
it is inflicted by a judge or borne by a most holy surety, but have the
character of an adjunct accompanying it because of the fault of the suffering
subject. When he sees the impossibility of struggling free from the punishment
inflicted upon him, he easily falls into despair and rage. This was entirely
foreign to Christ, who was certain of his deliverance and resurrection and,
amid the torments inflicted by God, always testified to his confidence in God.

XXX. Although a death of infinite value was owed for each sinner, it could
nevertheless suffice to redeem all the elect, because the account of monetary
payment differs from that of penal satisfaction. The former is estimated only
from the thing paid, whoever makes the payment, and cannot suffice for several.
But because the latter is estimated from the character of the person making
satisfaction, the worth of the price also increases according to his dignity
and can suffice for several. Although a coin is worth no more in a king’s hand
than in a captive’s, the king’s head and life far surpass in price the life of
a worthless slave. Thus David’s life is valued at more than half the army (2
Sam. 18:3). So the one Christ must be valued more highly than all together.
The dignity of the infinite person drains and absorbs all the infinities of
punishment owed by us. 2. It is nothing new that what is necessary for one can
also suffice for many when the subject is things communicated indivisibly.
The one sun, necessary to illuminate each individual, nevertheless suffices to
illuminate all. One victim sufficed both for priest and people. By one annual
propitiatory sacrifice the sins of the whole people were expiated, although as
many expiations were required as there were Israelites, because that single
expiation belonged to all in such a way that by God’s appointment it also
belonged to each. 3. Scripture is so express in this matter that no one can
deny it unless he wishes to contradict the Holy Spirit. For what would Isaiah
teach by saying that God cast upon him the iniquities of us all (Isa. 53:6),
Paul by saying that one died for all (2 Cor. 5:14), and elsewhere that by one
offering he perfectly completed those who approach God (Heb. 10:14), unless to
indicate that Christ’s one death

[Printed p. 385 | Internet Archive PDF 413 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

sufficed for full satisfaction for all the elect, just as Adam’s one
disobedience constituted many sinners (Rom. 5:18–19)? Finally, one cannot make
satisfaction for many of the same order, but nothing prevents it when they are
of different orders. One peasant cannot make satisfaction for many peasants,
but a prince can for many peasants. If this holds among creatures, all of whom
are finite and mortal, how much more between creatures and the Creator, between
whom there is an infinite distance?

XXXI. The rule which Ezekiel gives in chapter 18 concerning the soul that sins
being itself punished cannot be understood absolutely and simply. Otherwise
every imputation of sins would have to be removed, although Scripture
establishes it by so many examples. Rather, it must be understood in a
qualified sense in the ordinary way of providence, not the extraordinary way
of grace; or in the particular affair of the Jews with whom the Lord was
dealing, to stop their mouths lest they complain that they undeservedly bore
punishments for their parents’ sins, not in the common government of men, in
which God testifies that he will visit the iniquities of the fathers upon the
children (Ex. 20:5).

XXXII. So far is the doctrine of Christ’s satisfaction from being a doorway
to impiety and a pillow for security and negligence that it is the most
efficacious means of holiness and the death of sin itself. Christ proposed
this end to himself among others: that, dead to sin, we might live to
righteousness, living no longer to ourselves but to him who died and was
raised for us. Paul urges this so often (Rom. 6; Titus 2:14; 1 Pet. 2:24). See
more in the disputations *On the Truth of Satisfaction*, Parts I through IX.
