QUESTION V.

THE ASSUMED NATURE.

*Was the human nature assumed by the Λόγῳ, Word, like ours in all things
except sin, and was his flesh taken from the substance of the blessed Virgin?
Or did it descend from heaven? We affirm the former and deny the latter,
against the Anabaptists.*

[Marginal heading: Various errors concerning Christ’s humanity: that of the
Manichaeans;]

I. Just as Christ’s divinity has been assailed by the fury of various
heresies, so Satan has stirred up many enemies against his humanity. The
*Manichaeans* and *Marcionites* maintained that Christ was not truly a human
being, but only apparently and phantastically so, and thus turned his humanity
into a specter.

[Marginal heading: That of the Valentinians;]

The *Valentinians* held that he indeed had a body, but one sent from heaven
and not received from the Virgin, and that the Virgin’s body was like a channel
through which Christ’s body passed.

[Marginal heading: That of the Apollinarians;]

The *Apollinarians* believed that he indeed had flesh, yet not that he took it
from the Virgin. Rather, they held that in the incarnation something of the
Word was changed into flesh and that divinity informed the flesh in place of a
soul.

[Marginal heading: That of the Anabaptists.]

II. Following closely in the footsteps of all these, the modern
*Anabaptists* deny that Christ took flesh

[Printed p. 271 | Internet Archive PDF 299 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and blood from the substance of the blessed Virgin, in the Franconian
Colloquy, article 3, and the Emden Colloquy, article 4. The Belgians and
Germans agree in the negative position that Christ did not assume flesh and
blood from Mary’s substance, but they disagree in their affirmative account of
where he assumed it. The Germans confess that they do not know. The Belgians,
however, declare more plainly that Christ’s flesh is from the Word of life,
from the Father’s seed, and that this seed is the Word of God.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

III. The question is not whether Christ had flesh, was a human being, and can
be called one. Rather, was he a human being like us in all things except sin?
And did he have flesh from the blessed Virgin and not from elsewhere? They
deny this; we affirm it.

[Marginal heading: Christ is proved to be a true human being and like us,]

IV. The reasons are: First, he is everywhere called *man and the Son of Man.*
This could not truly be said if he did not have a nature like ours, that is,
one taken from human beings.

[Marginal heading: 1. Because he is called man and the Son of Man;]

Although the angels who appeared in human form are sometimes called human
beings κατὰ δόξαν, *according to appearance,* because they appeared as
such to those who saw them, it does not follow that the same can be said of
Christ. Nowhere is it said of them that they were Sons of Man, that they
became flesh, or that they participated in flesh and blood.

[Marginal heading: 2. Because he is called the seed of the woman and of
Abraham, the fruit of the womb, and so forth;]

V. Second, he is called “the seed of the woman” (Gen. 3:15), “the seed of
Abraham” (Gen. 12:3; 22:18), “the son of Abraham” (Acts 3:25), “the son of
David and the fruit of his loins” (Luke 1:32; Rom. 1:3), “the seed of the
Virgin and the fruit of the womb” (Luke 1:31, 42), and “made from a woman”
(Gal. 4:4). But how could he be designated by these names unless he had a
nature like ours from the very substance of the woman and the Virgin? Nor can
it be said: 1. *He is called the son of Abraham or David because he was
promised to them.* For then he could equally be called the son of all the
faithful, to whom he was also promised. 2. *He is the son of David in the same
way that he is called the son of Joseph.* For he is called the son of Joseph
only equivocally, because he appeared to be such in the opinion of others,
“ὡς ἐνομίζετο,” *as was supposed,* as Luke says. But he must be called the
son of David properly and univocally, since he arose from the fathers and
from David’s seed according to the flesh (Rom. 1:3; 9:5). 3. Or, *He is called
the fruit of Mary’s womb because he was conceived in her, not from her.* For
not only the *place of conception* but also its *material* is denoted, as is
explained in Rom. 1:3 and Gal. 4:4 when he is said to have been *made from a
woman.* Nor, if Christ was Mary’s Lord according to the Spirit, does it follow
that he could not be her Son according to the flesh. 4. Or, *The seed is to be
understood as spiritual, not natural.* For it must be understood as the kind
of seed by virtue of which he was to arise from the fathers according to the
flesh (Rom. 9:5).

[Marginal heading: 3. Because he became flesh and our brother;]

VI. Third, he was made a participant in flesh and blood, and from this he is
called our brother (Heb. 2:10, 14, 16). Flesh and blood cannot here be taken
simply for weakness. The truth of human nature is necessarily implied,
because the passage concerns the human being of whom the eighth Psalm speaks,
the one who had to taste death for all, who had to be our brother and like us
in all things (v. 17), and who was of one blood with us. “For he who sanctifies
and those who are sanctified are all from one” (v. 11), namely, from the same
stock or human nature, which verse 14 calls “flesh and blood.” In the Old
Testament the high priest who sanctified the people was likewise of the same
nature as the people (Heb. 5:1), as were the firstfruits with the rest of the
fruits. Thus the passage teaches that Christ was fit to suffer for us and by
his passion to sanctify and redeem us as our Goel, our kinsman-redeemer. But
how could he be said to be from one with us, or be declared our brother like
us in all things, if he did not have human nature, or flesh ὁμοούσιον,
*consubstantial with ours*? Nor is it an objection that this nature is also
common to the reprobate

[Printed p. 272 | Internet Archive PDF 300 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

since Christ assumed it for the sake of the children and could not have
assumed any nature other than the one common to all.

[Marginal heading: 4. Because he assumed the seed of Abraham;]

VII. Fourth, “he assumed the seed of Abraham, not angels” (Heb. 2:26). Here τὸ
ἐπιλαμβάνεσθαι properly means *to apprehend and assume,* or *take up,* and
is rightly referred to the assumption of a nature into the unity of the
Person. The sense, then, is that the Son of God assumed not an angelic but a
human nature, and specifically, according to the promise, a nature from
Abraham’s posterity. That this is the genuine sense is shown by the apostle’s
purpose and the sequence of his discourse. Here he intends to prove the truth
of Christ’s human nature and his ὁμοουσίαν, *consubstantiality,* with us by
his participation in flesh and blood, which was altogether necessary for
redemption. Since in verses 14–15 he had said that Christ shared flesh and
blood to deliver us, he now teaches how and from where he participated in
that flesh: namely, by assuming Abraham’s seed, that is, not human nature in
general, but in particular a nature derived from Abraham, according to the
promise made to him. The arguments Socinus and others bring forward to evade
the force of this interpretation do not oppose it. 1. “The apostle did not say
ἐπελάβετο in the past tense, but ἐπιλαμβάνεται in the present, although
the act of assuming a nature is momentary, not continuous.” But since the
hypostatic union endures and is preserved forever, and preservation is a
certain continuous reproduction of the thing, the present tense is not used
improperly. Add that the substitution of the present for the past occurs
frequently, especially in narratives of deeds, so that a thing may appear not
so much completed as taking place. 2. “He would prove the same thing by the
same thing. For being made like us, which follows, is the same as assuming our
nature.” But the following words, *Therefore he had to,* contain not so much
an inference from what is asserted here as an affirmation of the necessity of
the thing asserted there on account of what follows, *so that he might be a
High Priest,* and so forth. 3. “The seed of Abraham is a collective
expression, denoting many, namely believers (Rom. 4:16; Gal. 3:29).” But
*seed* is also spoken of one individual, and not just once; and Paul teaches
that it must be understood particularly in this way here (Gal. 3:16).

VIII. Τὸ ἐπιλαμβάνεσθαι cannot here be referred simply to help and
protection, because this verb nowhere occurs in that sense. Nor can Rom. 15:7
pertain here. For προσλαμβάνειν εἰς δόξαν, *to receive into glory,*
is one thing, and ἐπιλαμβάνειν σπέρμα, *to assume seed,* is another.
There Christ is said to receive into glory, or to make them participants in
heavenly glory; here he is said to assume seed, or to become a participant in
flesh and blood. 2. Angels are excluded from this ἐπιλήψει, *assumption,*
but angels are not excluded from the care of Christ the Head in his exalted
office, for they are members of the church triumphant (Heb. 12:22). 3. Christ
assumed seed on earth, as a comparison of the preceding and following verses
teaches; therefore he did not first do so in heaven, as our adversaries would
have it.

[Marginal heading: 5. From Christ’s genealogy;]

IX. Fifth, Christ’s genealogy is given, from which he is said to have arisen
from the fathers according to the flesh (Matt. 1; Luke 3; Rom. 1:3). This
would be falsely attributed to him if he had taken the substance of his flesh
from anywhere other than the blessed Virgin.

[Marginal heading: 6. Because sin must be punished in the nature that
sinned.]

X. Sixth, if Christ was not made like us in all things with respect to the
identity of his nature, he could not truly redeem us, since sin had to be
expiated in the same nature in which it was committed.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XI. Christ is called the *Lord from heaven* (1 Cor. 15:47), not by reason of
the material of his body, but by reason of: 1. The *Person of the Λόγου,
Word,* and the divine nature, which manifested itself in flesh; 2. The
*efficient cause,* namely, the *Holy Spirit,* from whom his humanity was
conceived; 3. The *condition of Christ’s life* on earth, which was not earthly
and worldly but divine and heavenly

[Printed p. 273 | Internet Archive PDF 301 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

in holiness and power, not in body. The antithesis established in John 3:31 and
8:23, when “Christ is said to come and to be from heaven, while human beings
are from earth,” does not prove that his flesh descended from heaven. It only
opposes naked and mere human beings born from earth to Christ θεανθρώπῳ,
*the God-man.* Christ is opposed to Adam in 1 Cor. 15 not by reason of the
flesh, according to which he is called the son of Adam (Luke 3:38), but by
reason of Person and office: as the Lord from heaven to a mere, natural man,
and as the author of righteousness and life to the author of sin and death.

XII. One kind of *likeness* consists only in *external appearance,* but
another in *internal truth* and natural properties. Christ is said to have
been *made in the likeness of man* (Phil. 2:7) not in the former but in the
latter sense, just as Adam is said to have begotten Seth in his own likeness
(Gen. 5:3). And Elijah is called ὁμοιοπαθὴς ἡμῖν, *subject to the same
passions as we are* (James 5:17), although he truly and not merely apparently
had human affections. And if Christ is said in the same place to have been
found σχήματι, *in appearance,* or in bearing as a human being, the truth of
his human nature is not therefore denied, but is bound all the more firmly.
For that ὡς, *as,* denotes not only likeness but truth, as often elsewhere
(John 1:14; 2 Cor. 3:18). Nor, if he is said to have been found in outward
bearing as a mere human being and a sinner just like others, is it denied
that he was a true human being with respect to the identity of his nature.
Finally, who would deny that he was a human being when he was found in
bearing as a human being and humbled himself even to death?

XIII. Christ’s Τὸ ὁμοούσιον, *consubstantiality,* with us consists in
identity of nature and essential properties, not in the mode of subsistence
that his human nature lacked. Christ’s particular human nature was therefore
physically complete in substantial being and in its integral parts, but not
metaphysically complete with respect to its mode of subsistence.

XIV. The same one who ascended into heaven after the resurrection had
descended from heaven (Eph. 4:9) with respect to the Person, not with respect
to both natures of the Person. He descended as Λόγος, *Word,* through his
signal manifestation and ascended as θεάνθρωπος, *God-man.* Nor is it
necessary for the ascent to answer in every respect to the descent, because
the subjects differ: the one is true and local, the other only metaphorical.
The *bread that descended from heaven,* formerly foreshadowed by the manna
(John 6:33), does not signify the human nature in the abstract, but the Person
in the concrete. That Person is sometimes spoken of in relation to the divine
nature, as when he is said to have descended from heaven, and sometimes in
relation to the human nature, as when Christ’s flesh is said to be given for
the life of the world. The bread who gives is the Person of Christ; that which
is given is Christ’s flesh.

XV. Those who imagine that women are ἀσπόρους, *seedless,* and deny that
they contribute actively to generation oppose not only the school of
physicians but also Scripture. Scripture asserts that the power of
procreation was conferred upon both woman and man (Gen. 1:28) and attributes
seed to women (Gen. 16:10; 24:60). Hence a brother and sister born of the same
mother are consanguineous even if they are born of different fathers (Lev.
18:9–10).

XVI. In Matt. 12:47–48 Christ does not deny simply that Mary is his mother.
Rather, he prefers his spiritual mother and brothers to his carnal relatives,
attesting that the former hold a higher place with him.

XVII. Christ is said to have been conceived from the Holy Spirit not
*materially,* but δημιουργικῶς, *creatively,* and efficiently. Here the
preposition *from* denotes the *efficient cause,* because he was conceived by
the Spirit’s power and extraordinary operation, not the *material from which*
he was conceived.

XVIII. The *Son of the living God* cannot be called Mary’s son according to
that by which he is the Son of God. But because he assumed human nature from
her into the unity of his Person, he is rightly and truly called the Son of
Mary in this respect as well. Thus Mary can truly be called θεοτόκος,
*God-bearer,* or *mother of God,* *Deipara,* if the word *God* is taken

[Printed p. 274 | Internet Archive PDF 302 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

concretely for the whole personal Christ, who consists of the Person of the
Λόγου, *Word,* and a human nature. In this sense she is called the *mother
of the Lord* (Luke 1:43), but not precisely and abstractly with respect to the
deity. Thus she is called the mother of God *specifically,* that is, the
mother of him who is God, but not *reduplicatively,* insofar as he is God.

XIX. Whatever is born from flesh is flesh (John 3:6), namely, according to the
order of nature and in a natural manner, or by ordinary and univocal
generation, but not outside that order and in a supernatural manner, as
happened here. Hence, although Christ derived his origin from Adam the
sinner, he did not derive from him either imputed or inherent sin. For he did
not descend from Adam by the force of the general promise, *Be fruitful and
multiply,* but by the power of the special promise concerning the *seed of
the woman.* And although he was in Adam with respect to nature, he was not in
him with respect to Person and moral state, or the federal relation by which
all Adam’s descendants except Christ come to participate in his sin.
