[1679 printed p. 302 | IA leaf 310]

Having now at last resolved the second question we proposed—namely, whether
Christ's righteousness is imputed to us as righteousness in our
justification—the third follows: Which righteousness of Christ is it that is
imputed to us as righteousness?

For Christ's righteousness is manifold: 1. either the essential
righteousness by which he is God, or that which he has as man; 2. and this
is either all the universal righteousness inherent in him or only his
mediatorial righteousness; 3. or finally, as they are commonly called, his
active or passive righteousness. We can appropriately conduct an inquiry
into all of these, as others do.

First, are we made righteous by Christ's essential righteousness, by which
he is God?

Second, is it his universal righteousness, or only his mediatorial
righteousness?

Third, is only his passive righteousness imputed to us, or his active
righteousness also?

We begin with the first.

Are we justified or made righteous by the essential righteousness by which
Christ is God?

In the previous century Andreas Osiander invented and defended the
affirmative position. He was sufficiently learned, but of a turbulent
temperament and less than sober life, if we trust what Calvin and
Melanchthon wrote about him. While he was vigorously arguing his cause, he
was seized by a sudden epileptic seizure and died. Similarly, Funck, his
principal champion, was beheaded for disturbing affairs in the commonwealth
at Königsberg. According to Bucholzer, before his death Funck lamented
himself with this couplet and warned others:

[1679 printed p. 303 | IA leaf 311]

Learn from my example to discharge the office entrusted to you,

And flee the ways of meddlesome men as you would the plague.

But let us leave the men aside; it is their opinions and judgments that we
must examine. Osiander's position did not prevail for long. Within a few
years of its origin it was pierced through by learned and judicious men, and
it expired almost at the same time as Osiander, Funck, and a few other men
of the same sort.

Especially in our own age, most fertile in every error, I would not have
wished to dig up opinions already condemned and consigned to hell that
belonged to idly troublesome men. Yet Osiander's position on justification,
which we will discuss briefly, has partly never ceased to live among the
Roman theologians and partly revived among our present-day enthusiasts, to
the church's profound injury—or should I say plague. Let us therefore
proceed immediately to the matter.

Although Osiander had fought nobly against the Interimists of his age,
nevertheless, as in several other heads of doctrine—namely, the oral and
corporeal presence and eating of Christ in the Supper, which he affirms with
the Roman theologians—so especially in the matter of justification he
devised many unsound teachings. He first defended them fiercely against his
colleague Mörlin and then against others.

Among these he advanced certain heterodox views on Adam's original
righteousness in the state of integrity. He said it did not consist so much
in the infused and created uprightness and holiness of the whole person,
but that:

1. Adam was the essential image of God, which belongs to Christ the Son
alone, Hebrews 1:3, as Osiander himself, contradicting himself, also seems to
assert. For,

2. He maintains that Adam was created according to the image of Jesus Christ
as God's idea and primary exemplar.

3. Consequently, in the third place, he says that Christ would have been
incarnate even if we had not been going to sin in Adam, though he would not
have been crucified. But since these points do not closely concern the
matter in hand, let us pass them over. We are now treating the justification
of fallen humanity. In defining it, Osiander passes on one side into the
camp of the Roman theologians and on the other into that of the enthusiasts.

He agrees with the Roman theologians on some points and far surpasses them
on others. There is also a point on which he contradicts them.

1. He contradicts them by affirming that Christ justifies us and is our
Mediator only according to his divine nature. The Roman theologians confess
that the *principium quod* of mediatorial operations is the whole theandric
supposit, but maintain that their *principium quo* is only the human nature,
not the divine. They thus favor Stancarus instead. When Osiander attributed
mediatorial justification only to Christ's divine nature, Stancarus rushed
to the opposite extreme and attributed it only to the human nature, or to
Christ insofar as he is man. Thus “while fools flee one vice, they run into
its opposite.” When Arius denied the unity of nature in the three persons,
Sabellius introduced a confusion of the persons to refute him. Similarly,
when Nestorius held that there were two persons in Christ, Eutyches on the
other side confounded the two. But these matters are beside the way; let us
return to the path. On this point, then, Osiander opposes the Roman
theologians.

2. On several points concerning this subject he agrees with them entirely.

[1679 printed p. 304 | IA leaf 312]

1. He declares faith to be merely a preparation for justification.

2. He wants the remission of sins to be an appendix to justification, or
rather its forerunner.

3. Most importantly, he mocks taking the word *justification* in a forensic
sense in this controversy. Just as the Roman theologians blow away our
justification through the imputation of Christ's righteousness and
contemptuously call it imaginary, so Osiander says that those who teach that
we are counted righteous merely because of the remission of sins teach
things colder than ice.

4. Conversely, he vigorously contends with the Roman theologians that our
justification consists in righteousness inherent in us, and thus confuses
justification with sanctification. On these points Osiander and the Roman
theologians agree completely concerning justification.

5. On some points concerning this subject he also far surpasses them.

1. He holds that Christ's passion and obedience justify us not in themselves
and directly, but only accidentally. He supports this with the remarkable
argument that the obedience of Christ through which the remission of sins
was obtained for us was performed more than fifteen hundred years ago, when
Christ dealt with God; but our justification, by which we are made
inherently righteous, is present when Christ deals with us. How splendid!
As though something long past could not be the direct cause of a most remote
effect, or as though Adam, because he was created almost six thousand years
ago, were therefore the father and cause of all of us now living only by
accident.

2. He affirms that Christ is called righteous and was made God's
righteousness for us not because he fulfilled the law, but only because he
was born from eternity as the righteous Son of a righteous Father. This was
to prepare the way,

3. for his principal dogma, which is not only heretical but blasphemous: that
we are justified by inherent righteousness—not, as the Roman theologians
maintain, by created and infused righteousness, namely divine qualities
produced within us by the Holy Spirit's efficacy, but by what is his Helen,
Christ's essential righteousness by which he is God. Since the divine
essence is one and unique in the three persons, he therefore places our
justification in the essential indwelling of all three persons in us, by
which God makes us righteous and moves us to act righteously. To this he
drags Jeremiah 23:6, “The Lord our righteousness,” as well as Paul's words,
“We have become the righteousness of God in him,” 2 Corinthians 5:21, and
several other similar passages. In these especially consists both Osiander's
difference from the Roman theologians and his agreement with them.

But on this last point he agrees entirely with the ancient Manichaeans and
present-day enthusiasts, and it is plainly a confusion. He blasphemously
asserts an essential indwelling of the divine nature within us—not that by
which God is commonly said to be essentially, or according to his essence,
present and existing in all created things, but an essential union of his
divine nature with ours, indeed a transfusion into us and substantial
mixture with soul and body, by which Christ pours himself into us and makes
us, as it were, part of himself. To this he violently twists several
testimonies of Holy Scripture: “Christ lives in me,” Galatians 2:20; “Christ
is in you,” 2 Corinthians 13:5; “He who eats my flesh remains in me, and I in
him,”

[1679 printed p. 305 | IA leaf 313]

John 6:56; and “I in them and you in me,” John 17:23. Thus what the apostle
attributes to Christ as his peculiar possession in Colossians 2:9—namely,
that “deity dwells bodily in him”—Osiander blasphemously communicates to all
the saints. He even does not hesitate to assert: “Every spirit that does not
profess”—not, as the apostle says in 1 John 4:3, “that Jesus Christ has come
in the flesh,” but which does not confess—“that Christ has come in the flesh
in this manner,” as Osiander blasphemously defines it, that is, into saints
clothed here with flesh, “is not from God.” Behold the heart of Zenodotus,
behold the liver of Crates! Behold Osiander's position—or rather his
delirium, his mind, shall I say, or his blasphemous madness? He concedes that
the remission of sins intervenes in the justification of a person as though
it were its lower appendix, or rather that it long precedes justification as
a forerunner, having been procured and accomplished in Christ's passion
itself. But in the first and highest rank, as Calvin correctly says, he
placed this specter of essential righteousness. He asserts that by it alone
we are righteous and that all who think we can be justified by anything
other than Christ's own essential righteousness by which he is God—or by
the one living God alone, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—most grievously err.

I have been rather lengthy, hearers, in this extended disclosure of
Osiander's position—or rather blasphemy—and of this crab's claws, because a
more particular explanation could suffice as its refutation.
