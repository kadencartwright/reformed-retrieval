# CHAPTER XXVIII

## The fourth precept of the Decalogue is not Ceremonial

From what has been said thus far it is abundantly evident that the fourth
precept of the Decalogue is not Ceremonial. For what is properly moral is not
Ceremonial; but in *Chapters XXII and XXIII* we have already demonstrated that
this precept is Moral. Next, what is not partly Moral and partly Ceremonial
cannot be called Ceremonial. But in the *preceding Chapters* we have already
made it evident that this precept is not partly Moral and partly Ceremonial.

It follows further, therefore, that this command cannot be said to be wholly
Ceremonial. This can also be demonstrated from the nature of the *Ceremonial
law*, explained above in *Book I, Chapter VIII*, and from what was said in
*Book III, Chapter XX*, where we proved that the law of the Sabbath is not
Ceremonial. It is well known that the Scholastics give a threefold reason why
the ceremonies ought to cease after Christ's advent: 1. because they are
obscure in their signification; 2. because they are imperfect in their
efficacy; 3. because they are burdensome in their observance. None of these
can rightly be said of the fourth precept. Therefore nothing else remains to
be done here but to resolve the Adversaries' objections, by which they labor
to conclude that this precept is figurative and typical.

*Objection 1.* [The chief thing which this precept commands according to the
letter is the sanctification of a strict rest. But rest, as rest, is not in
itself a virtue, nor is it commanded because it is a virtue. And when rest is
sanctified, it is not employed for its own sake, but is applied to a spiritual
use; and in those days a spiritual use in things of this kind was figurative
and typical of spiritual things.]

[Printed p. 298 | IA PDF 304 | IA leaf 304 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Response 1.* This whole argument rests on the false foundation that the
chief thing commanded in this precept is the sanctification of a strict rest.
This precept does indeed command that the Sabbath be sanctified, but the
sanctification of the Sabbath is something other than the sanctification of
rest. First the sanctification of the Sabbath is commanded, and then
cessation from daily works is enjoined as a necessary means. What, then, is
intended by this *sanctification of rest*? 2. We shall hear in its proper place
what must be said about that strict rest. I should only like to understand
what the *sanctification of strict rest* is. 3. It is true that rest, insofar
as it is rest, is not in itself a virtue, nor is it commanded because it is a
virtue. But what follows from that? If it is commanded as a necessary means
to the sanctification of the Sabbath, or to the observance of a solemn Time,
virtue and obedience require that it be observed; and it is a moral, not a
typical, duty, as was shown above. 4. It is true, moreover, that this rest is
not employed for its own sake, but for the sanctification of the Sabbath. Yet
to infer from this that the use of this rest was figurative and typical is to
beg the question. For this rest had a place before any typical or figurative
things were instituted, namely, before the fall. Whatever may be said about
the time of the Old Testament does not touch the observance of the Sabbath in
its first institution, much less the fourth precept of the Decalogue.

*Objection 2.* [This holy rest was a sign to the Israelites that they were the
Holiness of God. But Scripture speaks in this way only of types or figures.]

*Response 1.* Even if all of this were granted, it would not follow that the
fourth precept is entirely Ceremonial, for this rest is not the chief thing
enjoined in the fourth command, as will be evident later. 2. Not all signs are
types, as was said. 3. Although that rest may have been a type for the
Israelites, it does not follow that this rest considered absolutely, or this
rest in relation to the sanctification of the Sabbath at its first
institution, was typical. Much less does it follow that the fourth precept is
merely typical.

*Objection 3.* [The weekly Sabbath is of the same nature as the other
Sabbaths.]

*Response.* This is denied. *Jonston proves the contrary on page 14* as
follows: [I. Because it is expressly distinguished from the feasts in
*Leviticus 23:38: These are the feasts besides the Sabbaths; and Numbers
28:10, 11, 16*, where God distinguishes the sacrifices of each Sabbath from
the sacrifices of the feasts. II. The definition and requirements of a feast
do not apply to the Sabbath. 1. Feasts were instituted in memory of some
particular benefit conferred by God upon the Church at that time. But the
Sabbaths concerned the worship of God in general and the remembrance of any
of God's benefits, *Psalm 92:1, 2*. 2. The Sabbaths were to be observed by
everyone, but the feasts could not be observed by everyone, for the unclean
were barred from the feasts in *Numbers 9*, though they were nevertheless
bound to observe the Sabbath. 3. The Sabbaths were to be observed everywhere,
and could be observed at home and abroad, in the wilderness, captivity, and
exile. But the feasts could be observed only at the place chosen by God,
*Deuteronomy 16:2, 5, 6, 16*. 4. The feasts required a particular worship and
sacrifices, *Numbers 28 and 29*, but the Sabbaths required only the doubling
of the daily sacrifice, *Numbers 28:10, 11*. 5. The Sabbath could not be
postponed or transferred to another day, but the feasts could, *2 Chronicles
30:2, 3, 13; Numbers 9:10, 11*.] Add to these that the Sabbath was instituted
before the fall, but the feasts were not, and that explicit mention of the
Sabbath is made in the Decalogue, but not of the feasts.

*Objection 4.* [The fourth precept is called a sign that God sanctified the
Israelites, *Exodus 31:13, 17; Ezekiel 20:12, 20*.]

[Printed p. 299 | IA PDF 305 | IA leaf 305 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Response.* This is frequently objected to us, and we have also frequently
answered it. In the passages cited there is no mention of the fourth precept.
There is indeed mention of the Jewish Sabbath, but these are distinguished.
A certain Adversary ridicules this distinction, however, so let us hear his
reasons.

1. He says: [It is not evident that God changed the fourth precept by
subsequent commands.] *Response.* Nor does it need to be evident that the
fourth precept was changed. For it does not need to be changed, and indeed is
not changed. It always commands that one day in the weekly cycle be observed.
Whatever was attached to the seventh day under the legal state does not
affect the fourth precept itself, but that particular appointed day, namely,
the last day in the weekly cycle. And the change of this last day does not
produce a change in the fourth precept. Much less can anything attached to
this particular day produce a change in the fourth precept.

2. He says: [Who will believe that God willed to command the observance of
the Sabbath as a moral duty, and immediately afterward, by a new command, to
make that same observance a sacrament?] *Response 1.* How will it be evident
that this observance of the Sabbath was ever a sacrament? 2. The Adversary
himself will admit that the law of nature demands that some time be dedicated
to God and divine exercises. Let him now say how this can happen—namely, that
God willed to command the observance of time as a moral duty, and afterward
by a new command to make that same observance ceremonial, or, if we must
speak with him, sacramental—and he will satisfy this objection. 3. We say,
therefore, that the Jews could observe the Sabbath morally insofar as it was
one day in the weekly cycle, which is what the moral precept demands; observe
that particular day of the week because of the determination made by God;
and, finally, observe that same particular day in a certain manner because of
some ceremonial thing attached to it. I explain this as follows. The
observance of the moral law was a moral duty, but that very moral law was also
a Pedagogue to Christ, and the Jews were bound to use that law in this way.
The Reader sees, therefore, that God can command the observance of the moral
law as a moral duty, and nevertheless afterward, by a new command, make that
same observance mutable in a certain respect.

3. He says: [According to this distinction a Jew could satisfy the fourth
precept and nevertheless violate the Sabbath when he did not observe the
Sabbath as a sign.] *Response.* It has not yet been established for me how the
Jews were bound to observe the Sabbath as a sign. But even supposing this, no
absurdity follows from the argument. A duty considered as a complex whole can
be commanded, according to different respects, in different moral precepts,
much more in a moral and a ceremonial precept. And when one command is
violated, another is not by that very fact formally violated. Thus the Jews
could consecrate one day in the weekly cycle to divine exercises according to
the tenor of the fourth precept, yet fail to sanctify the day which God
appointed according to the character of that administration—for example, by
not doubling the daily sacrifice according to the ceremonial law which
commanded this. By omitting this double sacrifice, however, they would not
sin properly and immediately against the fourth precept, but against another
law of God which specifically commanded it.

4. He says: [Where did God institute as a sacrament that which was a moral

[Printed p. 300 | IA PDF 306 | IA leaf 306 | IA decausadei02brow]

duty?] *Response.* Let those who hold that the Sabbath is a Sacrament show
this. I will not say it. 2. Although we grant that the Jewish Sabbath was a
sign, we do not say that that Sabbath was a sacrament; much less do we say
that the moral element of the fourth precept was converted into a sacrament.
What, then, is the point of this reasoning?

5. He says: [A moral duty and a sacrament are opposites. Therefore a command
cannot be observed both as a moral duty and as a sacrament.] *Response 1.* Let
all of that stand. 2. What, then, will the Adversaries themselves answer, who
grant that it is moral in the fourth precept that some time be consecrated to
God? Did those who observed the Sabbath as a sacrament not consecrate some
time to God? And insofar as they did this, did they not observe that moral
precept? And if so, was not the same command observed both as a moral duty and
as a sacrament?

6. He says: [According to this opinion the fourth precept did not teach
perfectly what was required for sanctifying the Sabbath. Where, then, is the
perfection of the law and of the law's commands?] *Response.* Does the
Adversary think that the Decalogue taught all the ceremonies? Or does he think
this law must be called imperfect because it did not teach everything
required for divine worship in that administration? Where did we get this
Theology?

7. He says: [Then it will not be permissible to seek an explanation of this
precept from other passages of Scripture which speak of the Sabbath, because
we will be uncertain whether the discussion there concerns the Sabbath of the
fourth precept or the Jewish Sabbath.] *Response.* There is no inference, and
the argument proves nothing. For it is easy to distinguish between the weekly
or Decalogical Sabbath and the Jewish Sabbath which had those ceremonies
attached to it. One who knows how to conceive and distinguish that
administration properly also knows what pertained to that administration as
such. And even if we did not properly understand this, our ignorance ought
not prejudice the truth.

8. He says: [The text of *Exodus 31* speaks of the fourth precept in its
entirety, and the Sabbath is there called a sign in order that the Jews might
be stirred to observe the fourth precept, as is evident from a comparison of
verses 15, 16, 17, and following.] *Response.* That passage does not treat the
Decalogical Sabbath considered in itself, but the Sabbath of the Old
Testament, which they were also bound to observe by force of the fourth
precept. And even if it is called a sign for this end, that they might be
moved to observe the fourth precept according to that administration, it
still will not follow that the Sabbath of the fourth precept is not
distinguished from the Jewish Sabbath. When they worshiped God with the
ceremonial worship then prescribed, they observed the second precept; yet the
worship enjoined by the second precept is distinguished from ceremonial
worship.

9. He says: [*Ezekiel* calls that Sabbath a sign which had been given in the
wilderness, *chapter 20:11, 12*. He therefore has in view the Sabbath which
was given on Mount Sinai.] *Response.* It has not yet been invincibly proved
that by those Sabbaths, indicated by the plural number, the weekly Sabbath is
understood. And all the ceremonial Sabbaths were given in the wilderness. 2.
The Sabbath pronounced on Mount *Sinai* had been given long before that time,
as we proved above in *Book III*. Therefore, if the Prophet has that Sabbath
in view, it is evident that he speaks of the Sabbath as it was observed in
that administration, not as it is considered in itself.

[Printed p. 301 | IA PDF 307 | IA leaf 307 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Objection 5.* [The Sabbath is numbered among the ceremonies in *Matthew 12
and Mark 2* and is compared with them; otherwise Christ's argument would have
had no force.]

*Response 1.* Why can a moral thing not be explained among ceremonial things,
when the ceremonial law, not yet abrogated, obliges no less than the moral
law? 2. In a positive moral precept a dispensation can be made no less than in
a ceremonial precept. Christ's argument against the Pharisees therefore had
force, even though the law of the Sabbath was not in every respect equal to
the other ceremonies. 3. Whatever is said about the old Sabbath will not
likewise be true of the fourth precept of the Decalogue. Therefore this
inference is empty, for the fourth precept, as has frequently been said, does
not immediately and directly command that Sabbath, but one day in the weekly
cycle; and it commands that Sabbath, or that Seventh day, indirectly.

*Objection 6.* [The Priests profaned the Sabbath in the temple and were
blameless, *Matthew 12:5*. Hence it is accepted among the Jews that *there is
no Sabbath in the Temple*.]

*Response 1.* This argument will prove just as much that the Sabbath was not
to be observed with holy rest as that the precept concerning the Sabbath was
only Ceremonial, because it is not permissible to violate even a ceremonial
precept as long as it remains in force. 2. Therefore Christ does not speak of
formal profanation, for it is contradictory that a sin be committed and the
sinner be blameless. In the same way it is contradictory that the precept
concerning the Sabbath be violated and no sin be committed. He speaks instead
of material profanation; or rather, he argues *ad hominem*. For the Pharisees
thought that the Sabbath was violated by every external work, and that it was
not permissible to perform any external work even when extreme necessity
pressed. Christ therefore says: If the Sabbath is profaned by every external
work, then the Priests profaned the Sabbath; and if, notwithstanding this,
they were blameless, why can my Disciples not also be blameless? [*Here,
therefore* (*Calvin says in Marlorat at this place*), *is an improper manner of
speaking in which Christ accommodates himself to his hearers. For when the
law commands men to abstain from their own works, it does not forbid them to
be engaged in sacred ones. But Christ concedes as true what might appear so
to the untaught, being satisfied that the labors of the Temple do not offend
God.*] See also *Pareus* at this place. [The word *bebēlōsi* (*he says*) means
*they profane*: not because they truly violated it, but because according to
the accusation of those men they had to be said to violate it. For if any
work violates it, surely a greater work violates it more. But slaughtering
and burning victims is a greater work than plucking and eating a few heads of
grain. Yet those works did not violate it at all, because they were commanded
by God and were the worship of God.]

*Objection 7.* [*The Sabbath was violated by circumcision, and ἀνείρεσις
σαββάτου διὰ περιτομῆς ἐγίνετο*—“the abolition of the Sabbath occurred through
circumcision”—says Epiphanius, book 1, heresy 30, no. 31. And Chrysostom, in
Homily 49 on John, says that *the Sabbath was especially violated by
circumcision. Hence that saying among the Jews*, circumcision drives away the
Sabbath. *The Jews therefore judged the Sabbath to be of the same nature as
circumcision, and preferred the latter to the former, since it was earlier by
birth.*]

*Response.* The same response which satisfied the preceding objection can be
applied here. For the Sabbath is said improperly to have been violated by
circumcision, which was administered on that day according to God's command.
The administration of a sacrament is a part of divine worship. 2. We do not
linger over the empty opinion of the Jews, nor does it follow from their
circumcising eight-day-old infants on that day that they thought the Sabbath
was a mere ceremony. 3. Because it was permissible to circumcise an infant on
the Sabbath, does it therefore follow that the fourth precept, which enjoins
the observance

[Printed p. 302 | IA PDF 308 | IA leaf 308 | IA decausadei02brow]

of one day in the weekly cycle, is purely and simply Ceremonial? Who will give
us a confirmed inference of this kind?

*Objection 8.* [The Sabbath rest was a memorial of God's rest from the works
of creation and a sign of that common benefit of creation.]

*Response 1.* *Primerose denies this in part 2, chapter 7, section 11.* 2.
Rest from works necessarily follows every Sabbath, or every feast day. The
sanctification or dedication of a day always includes rest. Hence even the
Gentiles abstained from works on feast days. Therefore rest on that day could
not have been instituted primarily as a sign or memorial of God's rest after
the works of creation. But the grant of six days for our works, and of one day
for rest and holiness, was a certain memorial—secondarily, that is—of the
creation of all things in six days and God's rest on the seventh day. 3. Let
this be granted. Does it follow that that rest was a mere ceremony? Is it not
as fitting or necessary now as it was formerly to preserve a firm and
immovable memory of God's works in the span of six days and of his rest on
the seventh day? Can every memorial of things or actions whose remembrance
is always equally necessary be called a mere ceremony? 4. Since the memory
of creation pertains commonly to everyone, whether Gentiles or Jews, it is
more probable that this memorial was not Ceremonial, but rather Moral.

*Objection 9.* It was a memorial of the Israelites' deliverance from Egypt,
*Deuteronomy 5:15*.]

*Response 1.* These are the words of the text: *And you shall remember that
you were a servant in the land of Egypt, but that Jehovah your God brought
you out from there with a strong hand and an outstretched arm; therefore
Jehovah your God commands you to observe the Sabbath day itself.* It can no
more be proved from this that the fourth precept is a mere Ceremony than that
the entire Decalogue is a ceremony, because such things are prefixed to the
entire Decalogue, as is evident. 2. Not every reason which moves one to render
obedience to a precept indicates the end of the precept. Therefore this
reason attached here does not say that the Sabbath was instituted for this
end, that it might be a memorial of such a deliverance. 3. Much less does
this attached reason say that this precept is a merely Ceremonial precept,
since a general duty can be urged by a special and particular reason. 4. Nor
is this reason introduced simply to urge obedience, but to move and stir
Superiors to do their duty, that is, to provide a Sabbath for their servants,
as the context proves and the words themselves demonstrate: *that* (*he
says*) *your male servant and your female servant may rest; and you shall
remember that you were a servant—therefore Jehovah commands you to make a
Sabbath.* לשמור את יום השבת, “to keep the Sabbath day.” 5. Finally, it will
never be proved from this that the fourth precept, which is distinguished
from the Jewish Sabbath, is Ceremonial.

*Objection.* [It was a sign of their sanctification, and a mark by which
they were distinguished from every other people. And they were bound to
sanctify the Sabbath as such a sign, *Exodus 31:13; Ezekiel 20:12*.]

*Response 1.* It was not a sign of internal and infused sanctification,
because then it would have been a sacrament properly so called, the contrary
of which we proved long ago. 2. If by sanctification is understood the
external distinction and separation of the Jews from the other Gentiles, it
does not follow that the Sabbath was properly a Ceremony, because the whole
Decalogical law served this distinction, *Psalm 148:19, 20*. Further, 3.

[Printed p. 303 | IA PDF 309 | IA leaf 309 | IA decausadei02brow]

*Ezekiel 20:11, 12* says this not only of the Sabbath but also of the other
laws and statutes, all of which were signs by which it might be known—by the
Gentiles, that is—that God sanctified them. Thus verses 19 and 20 say: *that
it may be known that I am Jehovah your God.* For by walking in God's statutes
and observing his ordinances, they publicly testified that God was their God
and that they were God's peculiar people, no less than they did by observing
the Sabbaths. By sanctifying the weekly Sabbath they also testified that they
worshiped the true God, who made heaven and earth in six days, rested on the
seventh, and sanctified it. 4. Therefore the Sabbath was not some *sealing*
sign, but a *distinguishing* one, such as Christian love in *John 13:15*. And
indeed this is said of the Sabbath because all the worship of God is denoted
by the Sabbath. 5. Not every sign of a present thing is a ceremony, especially
when the thing signified is not something transitory, but abiding. 6. Since
this sanctification has a place now no less than it did formerly, why should
the sign not also have a place, if not as the same individual, at least in the
same kind—just as sacraments in general are perpetual though the individual
sacraments of the Old Testament have been changed? Does it not follow from
this that we also ought to have a weekly Sabbath as a sign of our
sanctification, though that Sabbath of the seventh day which the Jews observed
is changed? 7. This character was something adventitious to the Sabbath, an
appendage notwithstanding which the Sabbath itself could be moral. 8.
Although we grant that the very Sabbath of the seventh day which the Jews
observed was a Ceremonial sign, it still does not follow that the Sabbath of
the fourth precept and the fourth precept itself were Ceremonial.

*Objection 11.* *It was a sign and figure representing and prefiguring our
rest from sins.*

*Response 1.* But this thing supposedly prefigured and typified existed,
according to the Adversaries, before the sign itself was instituted. For they
hold that the Sabbath was not instituted from the beginning, and yet from the
beginning men were bound to abstain from sin. 2. This rest from the works of
sin was present to the Jews and pertained to them no less than to us. It
therefore could not be foreshadowed by the Sabbath as something future. 3. On
this reasoning we now ought to have no external Sabbath at all. Nevertheless,
the Adversaries themselves admit that the Moral element of the fourth precept
requires the institution of certain days for the public worship of God. 4.
How is it proved that the Sabbath was constituted a type of a rest of this
kind? Do the sacred writings indicate any such thing? Or must we invent types
without Scripture? 5. Although the Jewish Sabbath had a typical character
attached to it, it does not follow that the weekly Sabbath was in itself a
mere Ceremony. Much less does it follow that the fourth precept is a merely
Ceremonial precept.

*Objection 12.* [The Sabbath prefigured the spiritual rest of believers in
Christ, which consisted partly in present peace of conscience and partly in
the future enjoyment of glory.]

*Response.* This is said without proof, for Scripture does not teach it to
us. 2. As for peace of conscience, believers enjoyed it before the time of
*Moses*, and consequently before the Sabbath was instituted, according to the
Adversaries. But a type is a type of a future, not a past, thing. 3. It was
also something present to the Jews. Therefore their

[Printed p. 304 | IA PDF 310 | IA leaf 310 | IA decausadei02brow]

Sabbath did not prefigure such a thing, for a type is not a figure of a
present thing. 4. As for glory, it is still future, and consequently the type
must still continue. For the type of a future thing must continue until the
thing prefigured arrives. Therefore we too ought to have a weekly Sabbath. 5.
Even if we grant that this typical character was attached to the Old Sabbath,
it still does not affect the fourth precept of the Decalogue.

*Objection 13.* [The Sabbath rest was a type of our rest in heaven, which is
therefore called *Sabbath-rest by the Apostle in Hebrews 4*.]

*Response 1.* Not every likeness makes a type. The Apostle makes only a
certain allusion by way of a likeness. Heaven itself is called paradise, yet
it does not follow that paradise was a type of heaven. Christ's union with
believers is compared to marriage by way of likeness. Shall we therefore say
that the marriage of *Adam* to *Eve* was a type? Christ is called the *Sun of
righteousness*. Shall we therefore say that the sun is a type of Christ? 2.
If rest on the Sabbath day is a type of rest in heaven, then the
sanctification of the Sabbath will also be a type of heavenly holiness, but
this is not said. 3. It will never follow from the Apostle's words that the
fourth precept commands nothing other than what is a type. 4. If the weekly
Sabbath is a type of heavenly rest, it ought not to be abolished entirely,
for a type must always have a place until the thing itself, shadowed and
signified by that type, is obtained.

*Objection 14.* [It was a type of Christ's rest in the tomb after the works
of redemption had been completed on the cross.]

*Response 1.* Scripture indicates no such thing to us, and we must not invent
types without Scripture. 2. Even if we grant that the Jewish Sabbath was such
a type, it does not follow that the same can be said about the weekly Sabbath
or the fourth precept, as has been said. 3. The works of redemption were not
yet finished when Christ descended from the cross. So far as acquisition is
concerned, Christ completed his work by dying; yet his rest in the tomb
belonged to his death and is commonly referred to his humiliation as its
completion.

*Objection 15.* The fourth precept is purely typical because it is presented,
not negatively like the three preceding ones, but affirmatively and uniquely
in this form: *remember to sanctify the Sabbath day*; and its cause is added:
*because God ceased from his work on the seventh day*. This is unlike what we
see in the others, which are implanted in the mind and known of themselves.

*Response 1.* This precept is indeed presented affirmatively, as is the fifth.
But it is no more to be called Ceremonial on that account than the fifth. 2.
That unique form, *remember*, and so forth, will never prove that this precept
is purely and simply Ceremonial. 3. The cause added does not make the precept
Ceremonial, but rather proves that it is Moral, because it preceded all
typical ceremonial things. The reason added to the fifth precept, though it
can in a certain manner be called ceremonial, nevertheless does not make that
precept ceremonial. Much less will the reason attached to the fourth precept
do so, since it cannot in any manner be called ceremonial. 4. Not every
precept which is not implanted in the mind and known of itself is Ceremonial.
Otherwise we shall say that the tenth precept is Ceremonial, because *Paul
says in Romans 7:7* that he would not have known covetousness to be sin unless
it had been revealed to him by the law.

*Objection 16.* Attention must be given to the Analogy of faith, which in all
the riddles

[Printed p. 305 | IA PDF 311 | IA leaf 311 | IA decausadei02brow]

of the Old Testament—to which the Sabbath belongs—must be the guide to the
true senses. What, then? He who commands the sinner to cease on that day
which, in the cycle of days, corresponds to the one on which God had shown
that man was made for God alone and to serve him alone—does he not plainly
say that the sinner's works depart from that purity and uprightness of man in
which that day had found him, and that man was made a servant of the creature
whom he ought not serve? Next, does he not say that man needs God's
sanctification in order to be righteous and no longer to serve the creature?
And then that he will obtain this by God's benefit? Finally, when the cause of
that sanctification has appeared, will he then rest both from that accusation
and from desire? And thus is this cessation, which is the accusation of sin,
converted into a type of that rest? Especially if God himself calls that
cessation a sign of sanctification—which was not then taking place, because
sin was still being accused.]

*Response.* The most illustrious *Essenius abundantly satisfied this
objection in his Dissertation on the Decalogue and the Sabbath, chapter 5,
section 5*, in the following manner: [1. I distinguish the use of the Sabbath
in the state of integrity from its use after the fall; and again under the
administration of the Old Testament from under that of the New Testament. 2.
It has not yet been proved that the Sabbath belongs entirely to the riddles
of the Old Testament; we, on the contrary, demonstrate the opposite. 3. All
men ought religiously to serve God alone, but not all ought to serve God alone
in every respect. Servants also serve masters, *Exodus 21:2, 6; Colossians
3:22*. Indeed, free people serve Parents, and subjects Magistrates, in a
certain manner. Meanwhile, the fourth precept addresses free people and
servants alike. Nor is the reason added that by his rest God demonstrated
that all men ought to serve God alone. It is rather that God rested from his
works on the seventh day, and therefore willed that day to be commonly holy
and restful for men. 4. While sinners cease on the Sabbath from works properly
pertaining to the support of this life, they ought undoubtedly to meditate
that their works depart from the due purity and uprightness. But they ought
also to meditate upon the same thing on other days. Indeed, religious
exercises depart from the due purity and uprightness no less, in proportion,
than our other works; yet we must not rest from them on the Sabbath. Therefore
the Sabbath rest is not properly or adequately to be referred to such an

formerly found man in his original purity and uprightness. But neither do I
admit as a certain foundation the claim that the Sabbath was afterward
ordained properly and adequately for acknowledging departure from that purity
and uprightness in which that day had found man. 6. We grant that sinful man
needs God's sanctification and ought to meditate upon this need of his both on
other days and also on Sabbaths. But what the Opponent wishes by no means
follows from this. 7. We similarly grant that man obtains neither
sanctification nor justification except by God's gracious benefit through
Jesus Christ. But what inference contrary to us follows from that? 8. Christ,
God manifested in the flesh, has now appeared as the cause of that
sanctification and has also completed everything required for its acquisition.
Therefore the accusation of the handwriting of ordinances which was against
us has now ceased, *Colossians 2:14*, as has the longing for the Messiah as
one who was to come and fulfill those things. Meanwhile, the application of
this sanctification has not yet been fully completed. We still desire it and

*Objection 17.* *Augustine says this in book 3, Against Two Letters of the
Pelagians, chapter 40*, as follows: [*For if Christ took away from us that
most grievous yoke of many observances, so that we are not circumcised
carnally, do not sacrifice animal victims, do not rest even from necessary
works on the seventh Sabbath as the cycle of days returns, and other things of
this kind, shall we therefore say that the Decalogue does not pertain to us,
with the exception of the carnal observance of the Sabbath, which signifies
spiritual sanctification and rest?*]

*Response 1.* I will not say that *Augustine* speaks here only of the carnal
observance of the Sabbath and cessation from necessary works, neither of
which we affirm to be commanded in this precept, since it enjoins spiritual
and divine sanctification and grants liberty to do necessary things. I will
not say this because I see that he removes this precept entirely from the
register of the Decalogue. For he enumerates the ten precepts in the same
place as follows: *Who, indeed, would say that Christians ought not to observe
that religious service be rendered to the one God, that an idol not be
worshiped, that the Lord's name not be taken in vain, that parents be honored,
that adulteries, murders, thefts, and false testimonies not be perpetrated,
and that another man's wife—indeed, any property whatever—not be coveted?* 2.
I will nevertheless say this: all who rely on his authority must necessarily
either divide the tenth precept into two, as he does here; or, with certain
Jews, present the preface as a distinct precept; or offer us an Ennealogy in
place of the Decalogue. *Augustine* too must do this, since elsewhere, as we
saw above in *Chapter I of this Book*, he holds that the first and second
commands—as we count them—are only one. 3. It is also certain that *Augustine*,
and those who follow him in this matter, favor the opinion of *Hesychius*, who
speaks as follows in *book 7 of his Commentary on Leviticus*: *The letter of
the ten commandments is clear, worthy to be kept and carried out. Nor is the
command of the Sabbath contrary to this, because although it is inserted
among the ten commands, it is nevertheless not one of them.* And to preserve
the number ten intact, he agrees with certain Jews, adding this: *If, then, we
set down as a particular command, I am the Lord your God who brought you out
of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage, we will find the complete
number of ten commandments.* Therefore let those who hold that this command
is wholly ceremonial see how they can otherwise remove this difficulty, and
let them seriously consider the words of *Gerhard Johannes Vossius, On the
Division of the Decalogue, thesis 2*: [*Moreover* (*he says*), *in this we all
agree: there will be no complete distribution if Christians cut out from the
Decalogue the statements which command the Sabbath, since this would conflict
with God's words in Deuteronomy 12:32, where he forbids us either to add
anything to his precepts or to take anything away.*]
