### Chapter XIII

#### Third argument

Page 118.

If election is conditional, depending upon the will of human beings, it is
also uncertain. But it is most certain, in keeping with the nature, will,
power, and counsel of God (2 Tim. 2:19; Rom. 9:11; James 1:17; Num. 23:19; Isa.
14:27; 46:10). Therefore it is not conditional.

The Remonstrants’ answer comes down to these four things. 1. They deny what is
assumed in the proposition and taken as admitted: namely, that on their
position election is conditional. 2. They deny and refute the consequence of
the proposition, which implies that faith, as the condition of election, is
the cause of its uncertainty. 3. They point out a most serious disadvantage
of this inference. 4. They say that the passages of Scripture favor them. I
shall consider these in order.

In the first part of that answer, the question is no longer about what is
right, but only about the fact. “Let this argument,” they say, “be hurled
against those who say that election is conditional. This is not our
position.” Let us therefore see from both the principles and the conclusions
which they acknowledge as their own whether this belongs to them, though they
recognize it as absurd and refuse to own it. First, they must establish that
the decree of election is either conditional or absolute. But they deny that
it is absolute, and in this very treatment of the first thesis they sometimes
profess that this is the very thing they try to refute, namely on page 428.
Therefore either they defend conditional election or admit no election.
Second, they establish a conditional will as a true species of the divine
will. These are the words of Simon Episcopius, who is one of the Remonstrants

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 88 | printed p. 78]

and also professor at Leiden, in his Disputation on the Will of God, thesis
25.3. This conditional will is either the same as that which they call
antecedent, or at least accompanies it as the proper act of the will
accompanies a natural affection, so that it is the foundation of the whole
gospel, of the promises, exhortations, calling, and grace offered. For they say
that all these things occur because God wills salvation for all people. It is
therefore also the common foundation of election itself. 4. From this follows
that general decree which they attribute to God, “I will to save believers.”
This can be understood in two ways: either as a simple, general axiom and
statement, in which sense he wills nothing for the creature—as Arminius
rightly observes against Perkins on page 196—but wills two things, faith and
salvation, to cohere indissolubly with each other; or it can be taken as
directed toward people themselves, which is necessary for the decree’s
efficacy. In this sense it becomes a compound, connected, or conditional
axiom: “If they believe, they will be saved.” The Remonstrants call this
conditional decree both a decree and conditional election. “God,” says
Nicolaus on page 23, “decreed to confer salvation under the condition of faith
to be performed by those to whom he would apply salvation.” And on page 101:
“Nor indeed do I understand how a particular decree of election can be called
more certain than that conditional decree itself to save anyone if he
believes, for he determined certainly concerning all under a certain
condition.” On page 12: “We call those actions of God conditional which he
performs when a human action intervenes.” On pages 21 and 23: “The ground,
intention, and will of application is conditional.” They also say that this
election is nothing other than the gospel or the promise of the gospel
(Arminius, *Declaration of His Position*, p. 48; Nicolaus, p. 195;
Remonstrants’ *Collation*, p. 66). “Whoever denies that the gospel promise is
conditional has also denied the gospel itself,” says Nicolaus Grevinchovius
on page 138. What, then, will those who deny that this election is

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 89 | printed p. 79]

conditional not dare to deny? 5. From this one decree, “I will to save
believers,” two others emanate, one concerning faith and the other concerning
salvation, each of which is also conditional. “I establish that the decree
concerning the production of faith in this person is not absolute but ordered
and conditional,” says Nicolaus on page 117. Yet they call this decree
election to faith, which has the free concurrence of the human will as a
condition demanded and prerequisite. The other decree concerning salvation
to be conferred is made concerning someone who now actually believes. It is
the election to life of particular, definite people whom God sees believing
or foresees will believe. This election can be considered either as regarding
believers who are pilgrims on these lands, or as regarding only those who
have already finished life. If it is referred to people contending on earth,
as it continually is in Scripture, then this election too is, on the
Remonstrants’ position, conditional, incomplete, and revocable. Thus Nicolaus
Grevinchovius on pages 136–37: “As the goods of our salvation are incomplete
in this life, are continued when faith is continued, and are revoked when
faith is renounced, so election is incomplete in this life, not definitive,
not irrevocable.” Therefore the election Scripture means when it customarily
adorns particular believers living in various churches with the name “elect”
is, in the Remonstrants’ opinion, conditional. But that other complete and
consummated election regards a person as having already traversed the whole
course of life, beyond the prescriptions of laws and the duties of all
conditions unless they have first been performed. They admit that no
experience or fruit of it can be had on earth. Although this election, I say,
is not then referred to a future contingency and doubtful outcome, it must
nevertheless be called conditional with respect to the past and performed
condition by reason of which it exists. Nor indeed do I see how the
Remonstrants can say that this election was absolute from eternity,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 90 | printed p. 80]

when they affirm that in time it is conditional, contingent, and
indeterminate. For as the faith, perseverance, and salvation of Peter while
he is now alive are, so also is his election at the same time; but the faith
and perseverance of the living Peter are plainly contingent. I have made both
points sufficiently clear: that they both make election conditional and call
it so. This is also proved openly from the fact that, although they have made
the decrees of justification and election conformable and uniform, they
nevertheless establish justification as both conditional and revocable. Thus
far concerning the first part of the proposition; the consequence follows.

Second, therefore, they say that faith is not considered in election as a
condition or uncertain event, but as a present and certain gift conferred, and
thus that foreseen faith does not make election uncertain. But 1. faith in
that election which is directed toward the living is not regarded as present,
or at least not only as present, but also as something future. For, as I showed
before, the general election of believers and election to faith regard faith
as still future; incomplete election regards it as partly present and partly
future. 2. That in faith which has the proper character of a condition in
relation to the divine will concerning our salvation is not regarded as God’s
gift. The Remonstrants will not say that God prescribes, exacts, and demands
from a person the performance of his own gift precisely as such. For then the
promise would have its entire standing from the will of the one promising.
But jurists rightly warn that under the condition “if I will, if I give,” no
promise or obligation arises. What they mean is something else when they say
that God decreed to confer salvation under the condition of faith to be
performed by us, not given by himself. 3. Nicolaus Grevinchovius contends on
page 138 that the condition of faith, as it is prescribed to us, not only is
but also must be of uncertain outcome. Every condition prescribed by law

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 91 | printed p. 81]

is mutable and contingent on our part, so that we can perform it or not
perform it, for it is prescribed to a free creature. Therefore, if our
election depended upon that condition, it could not be other than uncertain.

Since they could not break the argument’s force, they try to discredit it by
its disadvantages.

“If,” they say, “everything conditional is uncertain, then God’s eternal
decree concerning the sending of his Son into the world was also uncertain,
because it depended upon whether the first man would sin or not sin from a
free and mutable will.” I answer that the grounds are not equal—indeed, not
even similar. For the proposed argument was instituted concerning a condition
prescribed, demanded, and to be performed for this purpose: that the promise
which is made upon it should become valid. The nature of such a condition
requires that it always render the thing to which it is attached uncertain
until it is actually fulfilled. For, as jurists customarily define it
correctly, this condition is an addition which suspends the act. Their example,
urged in many words as though it were parallel, concerns a condition not
prescribed, not demanded, and not performed for this purpose, but merely
antecedent as an occasion without which the thing would not occur. Such was
the conditional relation which Adam’s sin bore to Christ’s mission.

They twist their necks, as it were, to force the testimonies of Scripture
proposed by our side into their service. 1. From 2 Timothy 2:19 they argue in
this way: “God’s firm foundation—that is, election—stands, having this seal,
‘God knows his own.’ Therefore the firm foundation of our election to
salvation stands and rests upon foreknowledge, by which God knows his
own—that is, believers.” But this is a play of wit rather than a proof. For 1.
the text does not say “it stands upon the seal” or “rests upon the seal,” as
they report it, but “God’s foundation stands, having this seal,” and so forth,
so that its stability is referred not to the seal but to the constancy of God
who laid

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 92 | printed p. 82]

this foundation. 2. Nor does the word “seal” have any force for transferring
the character of the foundation to foreknowledge, as though election rests
upon foreknowledge because foreknowledge seals election. For the one who
believes seals that God is true (John 3:33); yet God’s truth does not rest and
stand upon human faith. 3. It is remarkable that here they derive
foreknowledge from the simple verb “knows,” when just now they would not allow
us to derive foreordination from “appointed” in Acts 13:48. Yet in the latter
passage no interpreter fails to understand foreordination, while here the most
learned, from the usage of Scripture and analysis of the context, understand
“knows” to mean “loves.” 4. This seal has two parts: “The Lord knows those who
are his,” and “Let everyone who names the name of Christ depart from
iniquity.” These two must not be torn from each other in the act of sealing.
Therefore, if the foundation of election rests upon the first, it also rests
upon the second—that is, departure from iniquity or reformation of life.

2. From Romans 9:11 they prove that election rests upon faith, because it says
“not from works, but from the one calling”—that is, they say, from faith. If by
faith they understand the faithfulness and truthfulness of God who promises,
they speak correctly but say nothing for their own case. But if they wish the
faith of the person called to be understood by “God who calls,” everyone who
either examines the passage or hears its words will miss a sound mind and good
faith in them.

3. At the end they add some questions which contain nothing new, and I need
not answer them. Yet I shall append an answer to one or two. “Who,” they ask,
“ever denied that God is immutable in his nature and will?” and so forth. I
answer that, after Conrad Vorstius, Simon Episcopius seems almost to have
denied this. In his *Disputation on the Will of God*, thesis 7, he affirms that
there are many various and diverse volitions of God, and that he determines
some of them at a certain time. Nor is it sufficiently certain whether the
contrary is maintained by the Remonstrants in this very passage. For they frame the
question

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 93 | printed p. 83]

in this way: “Who ever denied that God is immutable in his nature and will,
these being joined together with his infinite power and the firmness of his
counsel?” Here they have framed the latter clause—“these being joined
together,” and so forth—with such an ambiguous construction that it either
establishes a difference between one will and another, as though every proper
and free will of God did not have infinite power and firmness of counsel
joined to it so as to be immutable, but only a certain will did; or else it
can be taken as a confirmation of the truth proposed concerning the
immutability of the divine will. They appear to speak in the former sense,
because elsewhere they have similar teaching. Thus Nicolaus Grevinchovius on
page 271: “It is certain to us that God intends the conversion and salvation
of many in whom he does not attain it.” On page 272 he gives this reason for
that frustration: because God does not wish to apply omnipotent force in
converting a person. And in this very collation on pages 326–27 the
Remonstrants say that the decree to effect something powerfully is of a
different kind from the decree to bring a person to faith and salvation.
However this may be, this entangled answer leaves the proposed argument
untouched. For the reasoning was: the divine nature is immutable; therefore
the will is immutable. Nor is power lacking; therefore the counsel is firm or
immutable, and consequently, with respect to the outcome, does not depend upon
the uncertain choice of a person. The Remonstrants answer that nature and will
are immutable when joined with infinite power and firmness of counsel. What
does this do to the argument? They add further, “What, will the certainty of
our election be inferred from this? No one denies it.” Yet Nicolaus
Grevinchovius, following Arminius, argues on pages 100 and 102 that election
must not be called certain, because certainty pertains to knowledge and
election to will. Serious men would not quarrel about words if they favored
the thing itself. I have sufficiently shown before that they make uncertain
and mutable all that election which regards the living and whose fruit and
experience

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 94 | printed p. 84]

we have in this life. Nor can the reader desire any clearer demonstration
than the reasoning mentioned above: in the Remonstrants’ position the decrees
of election and justification are uniform and conformable; but justification,
on their position, is uncertain and revocable; therefore election is also.

### Chapter XIV

#### Fourth argument

Page 120.

If foreseen faith, obedience, and perseverance are to be held as the cause of
election and antecedent conditions, then the Apostle’s objection which he sets
against his teaching on election in Romans 9:14—“What then shall we say? Is
God unjust?”—would have no color. For no appearance of injustice inheres in a
decree of this kind, by which those are elected who in foreknowledge are
regarded antecedently as believing, obeying, and persevering. Nor, if the
Apostle had thought so, would he have resolved that objection in any other way
than by giving the clear and evident ground of both election and reprobation
from faith, obedience, and perseverance foreseen in some and not in others.
But the Apostle proposes a plausible objection and answers in another way.
Therefore.

The Remonstrants reply with many things here, but they do not weaken the
argument.

1. “Not all objections,” they say, “must be plausible. The Apostle invents
many crude ones in Romans 3:1–8; 6:1; and 1 Corinthians 15:32. Nor did the Jews
have any grounds for making such an objection to the Apostle, who confirmed
his teaching by such powerful miracles.” But 1. Aristotle correctly warned
long ago in *Rhetoric*, book 2, penultimate chapter:

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 95 | printed p. 85]

“Just as in sophistical disputations a cavil is admitted when someone argues
from what is said in a qualified sense to what is said without qualification,
so in rhetoric the same deception proceeds from what is probable in a
qualified sense to what is probable altogether.” For this is the very fallacy
they employ. That is called probable which appears to be so from an apparent
likelihood. What is probable without qualification, therefore, is what appears
so to everyone, or to nearly everyone; what is probable in a qualified sense,
*ti eikos*, is what appears so to some and carries a certain appearance of
truth. The argument is instituted concerning the latter, as is apparent from
the words “would have no color.” The Remonstrants answer concerning the former,
and thus captiously rather than to the point. Second, the things which a serious
and weighty man, much less an Apostle, thinks ought to be raised against
himself must be plausible in some respect and to some people. For it belongs
to the dignity and candor of a disputant, when he wishes to refute his
opponent’s arguments, to fight “either against all, or against the greatest,
or against the more illustrious.” To select those which are easily refuted is
the practice of sophists, not serious men. Third, the objections which the
Apostle anticipates in Romans 5 and 6 and 1 Corinthians 15 carried so much
color and appearance with them that they deceived the minds of many by false
plausibility, as is apparent from the passages themselves. Fourth, their
statement that “the Jews had no grounds for objecting in this way to an
Apostle renowned for miracles” has no appearance or plausibility of reason.
It is granted that no ground for this deed was just, but in their words they
could meanwhile bring forward some appearance of plausibility. Indeed, this
is Satan’s constant custom, and that of his workers: deceitfully to transform
both their things and themselves into the appearance and likeness of truth so
that they can deceive more effectively.

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 96 | printed p. 86]

The greater the Apostle’s authority was, and the firmer his teaching, whether
from miracles or otherwise, the more it must be thought that his opponents
would employ every art and turn themselves into the best appearance or
likeness when they went to attack his position.

2. They relate that “the Apostle said nothing from which it can plausibly be
inferred in any way that God is unjust; therefore the objection is most unfair
and need not be anxiously excused.” All believers know that it is most unfair,
indeed, as Calvin rightly says, a monstrous madness of the human mind. But to
an unbeliever nothing appears more unjust than to deal so unequally with
equals, as the Apostle had taught that God did in the immediately preceding
words, verses 11–13.

3. They say that this objection could have, and did have, an appearance of
plausibility—despite that teaching on election and foreseen faith—from the
fact that the Apostle had taught that believers, not workers, were elected,
and that Gentiles were now called to salvation by God, who had previously
adopted the Jews as a people. If this objection can be inferred with some
plausibility or appearance from that teaching about election from foreseen
faith, then either the Apostle did not teach that doctrine, at least in the
earlier part of that chapter, or else what the Remonstrants had just affirmed
was false: that the Apostle said nothing from which it could plausibly be
inferred in any way that God is unjust. 2. In that general principle, “I will
to save believers,” God is said to will absolutely nothing for the creature
(Arminius against Perkins, p. 196); therefore it can appear to carry no
semblance of inflicting injury. 3. What is done to someone willing carries no
semblance of injury, but the exclusion of the Jews from Christ Jesus and faith
in him happened while they themselves were willing and desiring it. 4. If the
new covenant is merely conditional and equally common to all, Jews as well as
Gentiles—which is the Remonstrants’ position—so that there is no disparity in
divine election and dispensation before a person has supplied a different
condition,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 97 | printed p. 87]

this would be so far from any appearance of injustice that nothing more
fitting could be devised to refute that accusation. Nor would the Apostle
have needed to refer so human a law to God’s absolute power, when by a simple
explanation of the matter he could have stopped the objector’s mouth.

4. By a certain two-headed argument they exclude the position which gives a
somewhat deeper ground for this objection. “It must be said,” they claim,
“that the objection arose either because Paul had cited the words of Moses
and Malachi in verse 13, which the Jews nevertheless read daily without
offense, or because he had taught that Esau was thus reprobated, though the
Jews would not be very concerned about his affairs. Nor would it then have
been sufficiently refuted by the words that follow: ‘I will have mercy upon
whomever I have had mercy,’ and, ‘It is not of the one who runs or wills, but
of God who shows mercy.’” But 1. the Jews read many things daily which, when
applied to their true purpose, they nevertheless could not hear without the
greatest offense. We have an example in Luke 20:17, 19, where they take
offense at the Psalmist’s words, “The stone which the builders rejected has
become the head of the corner”; and in Acts 28:25, where they are said to have
been unable to endure hearing Isaiah’s prophecy but departed when Paul had
spoken that word. 2. The unbelieving Jews were not as concerned about Esau’s
person as about themselves, nor as much about themselves as about the
condition of the whole human race, which they thought the Apostle excessively
abased and subjected to God’s choice through the Apostle’s argument concerning
divine election prior to all human works, an argument as much illustrated as
confirmed by the examples of Jacob and Esau. 3. The refutation which the
Apostle opposes in verses 15–16 is amply sufficient to establish believers so
that they are not carried away by objections of this kind, and also to blunt
the pride of others who have not yet taken Pharaoh’s words for themselves:
“Who is Jehovah?”

5. They note two things at the end of this argument. 1. The teaching

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 98 | printed p. 88]

which they hand down removes every excuse from the reprobate and is therefore
just, equitable, and true. 2. If the reprobate are not rejected on account of
foreseen unbelief, they wish to have explained to them the ground by which
every excuse can be removed. I answer: 1. The reasoning from the removal of an
excuse on the supposition of any particular thing to its general equity and
even truth is exceedingly weak. If all people were restored to an upright
state, and some deserted it again and were rejected on that account, would
they not have something about which to complain? Yet it does not follow for
that reason that this is equitable, just, and true. 2. This can seem equitable
to a person so that he himself has nothing about which to complain, while it
is meanwhile unjust to God and material for complaint at least in that part
in which it would ascribe the proper and first cause distinguishing believers
from unbelievers not to God’s election but to a person’s. 3. This one thing is
sufficient to remove every excuse from a person: he never suffers except from
sin, and never sins except from the will.

### Chapter XV

#### Fifth argument

Page 123.

If election is conditional and made with regard to faith, the performance of
this condition depends either upon God’s grace alone, upon human choice alone,
or partly upon God’s grace and partly upon human choice. But it does not
depend either wholly or partly upon human choice; therefore it depends upon
God alone. Consequently election is not conditional with respect to antecedent
causes and conditions.

Here the Remonstrants observe first of all that a sheer contradiction is found
between the earlier statement, which says that if election is conditional it
depends either upon God’s grace, and so forth, and the later one,

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 99 | printed p. 89]

which says that if election depends upon God’s grace alone it is not
conditional. “These,” they say, “are plainly contradictory.”

But this contradiction is partly invented by the Remonstrants and partly
taken from their own words. For 1. the argument does not say that if election
is conditional, it—that is, election—depends either upon God’s grace alone,
and so forth. Rather, if election is conditional, then that condition depends
either upon God’s grace alone, and so forth. Nor is it said in the latter part
that if election depends upon God’s grace alone, it is not conditional.
Rather, if faith depends upon God alone, then election is not conditional with
respect to antecedent causes or conditions. A contradiction is the affirmation
and negation of the same proposition. Here the propositions are different;
therefore there is no contradiction. 2. They ought to acknowledge as their own
the contradiction they have formed, and for two reasons. For they deny that
election is conditional while at the same time wishing faith to be the
condition of election. They also say that faith proceeds from God’s sheer
grace, yet that God requires an antecedent condition so that he may be able to
return to favor with us or acknowledge us as his own.

The other things brought forward here either have been examined before or
must be referred to the fourth thesis as their proper place.

From the Catechism and Confession an argument of this kind is formed. If faith
is the fruit of the Spirit, it is also the fruit of election, for the gift of
the Spirit himself is the fruit of election (1 Pet. 1:3; Gal. 4:6; John
14:17). But faith is the fruit of the Spirit (Gal. 5:22; Catechism, questions
21 and 65; Confession, article 16). Therefore it is also the fruit of election.
The Remonstrants do not resolve this argument according to the rule of
disputation by denying or distinguishing some part, but cut it into certain
small pieces so that it can be attacked more easily. 1. They form a new
argument in this manner: “Whatever the

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 100 | printed p. 90]

Holy Spirit works in us and is the fruit of the Spirit is also the fruit of
election; but the Spirit effects faith, and it is the fruit of the Spirit;
therefore.” They think the proposition of this syllogism must be denied,
because certain fruits of the Spirit are common to others together with the
elect (Heb. 6:4–5). But 1. fruit indicates a root, and “fruit of the Spirit”
indicates the Spirit inhabiting, giving life, and taking root in the heart
where that fruit arises. Common endowments, therefore, although they are
effects of the Spirit, must nevertheless not be called his fruits, nor
properly effects of the Holy or sanctifying Spirit precisely as such, because
those temporary believers never had a root in themselves (Luke 8:13). 2. The
living, sole, constant faith of the elect flows from the Spirit on a
different ground and with a different purpose than that fleeting faith which
is communicated to others. Hence those endowed with it are said to have been
called by a special action according to purpose (Rom. 8:28). 2. “The Spirit,”
they say, “of adoption as sons is not received by unbelievers but by believers;
yet it does not follow that this spirit is the fruit of election, since those
who believe are elected to salvation and made sons of God through faith.”
Indeed, 1. even from this it follows that if believers are first elected and
then become sons of God, sonship and the spirit of sonship or adoption are the
fruit of that election. For either something intervenes between faith and
election, so that there is not always an election of all believers, or the
spirit of sons must follow that election of believers as its fruit. 2. The
Spirit precedes faith as cause precedes effect (Gal. 5:22). But a certain
adoption as sons precedes the Spirit himself: “Because you are sons, God gave
you the Spirit” (Gal. 4:6). The Remonstrants concede in this very answer that
election is prior to that adoption which occurs in time, and the thing itself
teaches it clearly enough. For no one first adopts a stranger as his son and
then chooses him. Both laws and human customs testify that this is backwards.
Rather, those to be adopted are first chosen, and then

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 101 | printed p. 91]

afterward are customarily enrolled in the state of sons. Thus in the abundance
of his mercy God first elected us as sons by a certain eternal adoption and
then regenerates us through the Spirit, from which living faith and hope arise
(1 Pet. 1:3). Therefore the Remonstrants are mistaken or deceive when they
contend that these two passages (Gal. 4:6; 1 Pet. 1:3) are in no way against
them.

I cannot sufficiently marvel at what follows. “As for the Confession,” they
say, “article 11 is for us, as we have clearly shown in our arguments. We are
therefore prepared to write and subscribe publicly what the brethren
establish here, namely that election was made in Christ with no account taken
of our works.” For 1. I clearly proved above, in the constitution of the case
and under argument 2 of this second part, that good works are no small part of
the condition which these people say must be prerequisite for election to
follow. It is plainly shameful to see them here vainly dissembling this again.
2. When they say that in their arguments they have clearly shown that this
article makes for them, they show clearly that they care little what they say
provided it benefits their case. For there they show nothing by proof but
merely affirm baldly that whatever is contained in the article concerning
mercy and justice, and concerning election in Christ without respect to
works, wholly supports their position (p. 71). It is also proved clearly
enough that this article makes especially against them by this argument. The
whole deliverance from sin and destruction is the fruit of election; the
Confession openly teaches this. But effectual calling and conversion to faith
and true obedience are a part or degree of that deliverance, or resurrection
from sin and death; this also is confessed among all Christians. Therefore
effectual calling and confession to faith are the fruit of election. 3. These
same people sometimes condemn this very part of this Confession, which here
they do not shrink from claiming as favorable to them, as contrary to the Word
and harmful to piety. Thus

[1630 Amsterdam witness | BSB canvas 102 | printed p. 92]

on pages 7–8 they propose the second position which they condemn in such a way
that, once those amplifications which they inserted to arouse hostility are
removed, it contains nothing other than the meaning of this article. For the
article they condemn teaches—setting aside those redundancies which the
defenders of that doctrine no more customarily employ in explaining the
position than the Confession itself does—that God in his decree regarded the
human race as corrupted in sin and therefore guilty of the curse; that from
this fall and condemnation he decreed by grace to redeem and save some to
display his mercy, but to leave others under the curse by his just judgment
for the manifestation of his justice. This is that position which they
expressly attack. They do mix in something concerning infants, but Arminius
himself saw that it was foreign to the matter and therefore omitted it in his
declaration of the same opinion (*Declaration of His Position*, p. 45). They
also append something about no account being taken of faith and unbelief,
which is falsely charged against our side unless they mean account as an
antecedent cause or ground. I have described the true sum of the condemned
position. Now let us hear that Confession which they say favors them in their
attack on the former position: “We believe that after the whole offspring of
Adam was plunged into destruction and ruin by the fault of the first man, God
demonstrated himself to be such as he truly is, both merciful and just:
merciful, indeed, in delivering from condemnation and destruction, and in
saving, those whom in his eternal counsel, according to his gratuitous
goodness, he elected through Jesus Christ our Lord without any respect to
their works; but just in leaving others in that fall and destruction into
which they cast themselves headlong.” Let the reader compare these words with
the former ones, and he will easily see that neither do the Remonstrants—at
least in this part—favor the Confession, nor does the Confession favor them.
