QUESTION XVII.

ON THE FORMULA OF BAPTISM.

Must the formula of baptism prescribed by Christ be observed in its
administration? And what does it signify?

I. The question is necessary both because of the papists, who deny that the
formula was prescribed by Christ and maintain that it is only from tradition
that a fixed formula of words is to be used—Bellarmine, book 1, *On Baptism*,
chapter 3—and because of the fanatics, who oppose the ordinary formula because
a certain magical power seems to be ascribed to it.

[Marginalia: The formula of baptism was prescribed by Christ.]

II. We establish two points. First, the formula was prescribed by Christ and
delivered to the church. This is clear (1) from Matthew 28:19, where Christ’s
purpose was to deliver the rite to be observed in this sacrament, so that he
might teach not only what was to be done but also how it was to be done, which
is necessary in prescribing a rite. (2) The church, which used such a formula
from ancient times, could not have adopted it on its own, because, as the
adversaries acknowledge, it cannot institute a sacrament. Therefore it received
and employed that formula from Christ’s ordinance and the apostles’ practice.
(3) From the parallel: in the institution of other sacraments under both the
Old and New Testaments, God used a set formula, which therefore cannot be
denied to baptism.

[Marginalia: And it must be retained.]

III. Second, that formula must be retained. (1) It contains the word of promise
proper and essential to baptism, which forms an essential part of the sacrament.
(2) The formula of the Holy Supper delivered by Christ must be observed
exactly; hence it is repeated in the same words by the Evangelists and Paul.
(3) From its object and end: the former is the Trinity distinctly set forth in
baptism; the latter is faith in the Trinity, to which we are bound through
baptism. (4) From practice: it is clear that this formula was always observed
from ancient times, and thus the various corruptions of the Arians were
countered.

IV. Although the apostles are not expressly recorded as baptizing with this
formula, it does not follow that they omitted it. It was enough that they
baptized according to Christ’s command, since there should be no doubt that
they conformed themselves to Christ’s prescription, nor is it probable that
they diminished anything in their master’s explicit words. The passages
adduced to the contrary, when the apostles are said to have baptized “in the
name of Christ” (Acts 2:38 and 8:16) or “into Christ” (Rom. 6:3), do not teach
with what formula they baptized. Rather, these statements are best referred
either to the efficient cause, so that the sense is that they baptized by
Christ’s command and authority, just as they are said to have performed
miracles in Christ’s name; or to the final cause, that is, into faith in him
and his doctrine, just as the Israelites are said to have been baptized into
Moses, that is, into his doctrine (1 Cor. 10:2). Add that “to baptize in the
name of Christ” is spoken synecdochically and includes the whole Trinity, as
Ambrose, book 1, *On the Holy Spirit*, chapter 3, observes after Basil, *On the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 345 | IA leaf 379 | PDF p. 379]

Holy Spirit*: “The designation of Christ is a confession of the whole, for it
declares God who anointed, the Son who was anointed, and the Spirit who is the
anointing” (ἡ τοῦ Χριστοῦ προσηγορία τοῦ παντός ἐστιν ὁμολογία, δηλοῖ γὰρ τόν
τε Χρίσαντα Θεὸν, καὶ τὸν Χρισθέντα υἱὸν, καὶ τὸ Χρίσμα τὸ πνεῦμα). Or, to
baptize in Christ’s name is to baptize by his command and authority and thus
in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, as Christ commanded.

[Marginalia: Which formula is better, that of the Greeks or that of the Latins?]

V. If it is asked about the formula of the Greeks and that of the Latins—the
former of which is expressed in the third person, “Let the servant of the Lord
be baptized, and so forth,” while the latter is expressed in the first, “I
baptize you”—which is to be preferred? I answer that although this may seem
indifferent, since the essence of the words is retained in both, nevertheless
the latter appears more suitable both with respect to the minister baptizing
and with respect to those baptized. With respect to the former, because in
this way the authority granted to him by Christ, whose act he performs in the
administration of the sacrament, is shown more clearly. With respect to the
latter, because the sacraments are to be applied to each one in particular
(Acts 2:38–39), which is illustrated by the parallel in John 20:22, where the
remission of sins is applied individually to each person.

[Marginalia: What the formula of baptism signifies.]

VI. Furthermore, this formula comprehends in a few words everything sealed to
us in this sacrament, which is referred to these three things: (1) God’s
institution; (2) the promise of grace; and (3) the pledge of the believer’s
duty. Just as the *name of God* is commonly used in three ways in Scripture—
either for God’s authority, for his power, or for his command—so to be baptized
in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit is: (1) To be baptized
by his authority and ordinance, so that we may know this rite was not devised
by men and is not administered by a man’s private authority and judgment, but
by the institution of God himself, who is one in essence and three in persons.
(2) To be baptized *in the name*, that is, in the power and efficacy of the
Trinity, indeed that power through which the individual persons concur in
procuring our salvation: and thus in the love of the Father, who appoints and
provides salvation and its means; in the love of the Son, who obtains it for us
by his merit; and in the love of the Spirit, who confers and applies it by his
grace. (3) To be baptized *in the name*, that is, by his command, into faith in
and the doctrine of the Trinity, and into trust in, worship of, and obedience
to him, so that here the grace of God who promises and the duty of the person
who pledges obedience are joined together.

Therefore, when the pastor says, “I baptize you,” it is the same as if he were
to say: In God’s name I declare that this water with which I wash you is a
symbol of your admission into God’s covenant and his church; that the Father
accepts you as a son, the Son as a member of his body and a brother whom he
redeemed by his blood, and the Holy Spirit as a guest whom he regenerates by
his grace and with whom he wills to dwell forever; and, in turn, a symbol of
your pledge of duty toward the triune God, so that you may adore the Father,
the Son, and the Holy Spirit with worship and obedience, and consecrate
yourself wholly and forever to the adorable Trinity. Thus to be baptized in
the name, or into the name, of God is to be initiated and consecrated into the
religion and worship of God, so that we may enjoy his grace and live according
to his prescription. Moreover, baptism is not said to be administered “in the
names” but “in the name,” so that unity in the Trinity and the Trinity in unity
may be designated more clearly. For the three persons have one essence,
majesty, and power, which is efficacious in baptism, as in the external works
that terminate upon creatures. The character of these works is that they are
common to the whole Trinity, because they arise from essential properties, not
personal properties by which the persons are related to one another.
