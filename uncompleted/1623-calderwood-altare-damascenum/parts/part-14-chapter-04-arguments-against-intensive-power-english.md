Our arguments against the intensive power of the princely bishop follow. We
placed that intensive power above in the singular power of ordination and of
directive and

[1623 printed p. 194 | IA leaf 206 | PDF p. 206]

corrective jurisdiction over presbyters and their flocks. That they can use
the counsel of presbyters diminishes their principate no more than a senate or
royal council diminishes a king's monarchy. Bellarmine says, *For certainly
Solomon had a body of twelve elders as counselors in 3 Kings 12, and Ahasuerus
used the counsel of wise men in all matters in Esther 1; yet they were no less
kings on that account.* Elsewhere he also says *that the authority of bishops
is not in the body of presbyters as that of a consul in the senate, but as
that of a prince in a council of counselors subject to himself.* We showed
above that our hierarchists have the same opinion. Since, therefore, `τὸ
κράτος καὶ τὸ κῦρος`, the dominion and decisive authority, rest with one bishop,
nothing is diminished from his singular power even if he uses the counsel of
others. For, as they themselves maintain, he is not bound to follow the
greater number of opinions. Bellarmine asserts, *Moreover, that the ancient
bishops did nothing without the counsel of the presbyters was useful and
salutary, but not necessary.* The hierarchists assert this very thing and show
it by their practice as well. For bishops do not consult the senate of
presbyters as they once did in the ancient church; indeed, they declare that
there is no need after so many canons of councils have now been composed.
They do not wish the senate of presbyters to be of divine institution so that
it might counsel the bishop as it would a consul, still less so that it might
direct and govern the bishop. They therefore reject both uses of a senate. Do
they not, then, contend that by divine right the princely bishop is that
genuine bishop about whom the controversy is concerned? It therefore makes
no difference, when we dispute about divine right, whether he uses a
presbytery voluntarily or under compulsion from the canons. For they assert
that they are not bound by divine right. The law of the canons is only
mutable human law, which they themselves also say has become obsolete or at
least has passed out of use. Since, therefore, human law is mutable and has
been changed according to their judgment, while we seek an immutable and
perpetual law, we assail with these arguments the princely bishop whom they
wish to be endowed by divine right with the singular power of ordination and
of corrective and directive jurisdiction.

1 Argument. Nowhere in the Scriptures do we find a bishop other than an
ordinary pastor—not a presiding bishop, still less a prince. Therefore, by
divine right no one has singular power of directive and corrective ordination
over others. The antecedent is proved by an induction of examples and
testimonies. Archippus, Epaphroditus, Timothy, Titus, and so on with the rest
were not such bishops, but were either evangelists or presbyters—that is,
ordinary pastors—without such jurisdiction. And when the apostle enumerates
the gifts and offices given to the church in Romans 12, 1 Corinthians 12, and
Ephesians 4, he does not mention this bishop; the same is true of other
passages. The consequence of the argument is necessary, for otherwise it
would follow that Christ the lawgiver omitted the church's principal ruler,
upon whom the government of every particular church depends. That is absurd.
Since, therefore, they were not

instituted by divine right, they must have been introduced by human authority.
For what is not from heaven is from men, Matthew 21:25–26.

2 Argument. This is taken from Christ's command in Matthew 18: “Tell the
church.” Christ does not say that if he will not hear you or those two
brothers whom you took with you, *tell the bishop,* but *tell the church.*
Christ wishes the offense to be brought to several, not to one, as the
gradation shows. For Christ proceeds from one to two, and from two to several.
Drusius says that the same things are found in the book *Musar*, 221, though a
little differently: *He who reproves his companion ought first to do it
peaceably between himself and the other alone, with gentle words, so that he
does not put him to shame. If he repents, it is well; if not, he ought to
reprove him sharply and put him to shame between himself and the other. If he
does not repent, he ought to bring companions and put him to shame in their
presence. If he makes no progress in this way either, he ought to put him to
shame before many and make his offense public.* We see, therefore, that one
must proceed from one to two and from two to several, not go back to one. They
say that “church” can signify one particular person—namely, a public person
who is virtually a multitude and assembly and possesses its authority. But
besides the fact that nowhere in the Scriptures can they show this use of the
word “church,” it is opposed not only by the gradation just discussed but also
by the promise that the sentence will be ratified. For he speaks in the
plural—*whatever you bind*—not in the singular, *whatever you bind.*
Bellarmine says, *“Tell the church” signifies, “Bring it to the public judgment
of the church,” that is, to those who bear a public person in the church. For
Chrysostom expounds it in this way: “Tell the church,” that is, the prelate.*
Our Tilenus replies, *Chrysostom uses the plural number, `προέδροις, καὶ
προεστῶσι`, “to the chairmen and those presiding.”* Junius says, *Indeed,
Chrysostom says “to the presidents, that is, to those presiding,” `προέδροις
καὶ προεστῶσι`; that is, to the ecclesiastical synedrion, or the college
constituted from presbyters and elders.* Sutlivius says, *By “the church” is
understood not one particular person (for the rationale of the word forbids
this) but several persons presiding over the church. But it is impossible for
only one supreme monarch of the church to be designated by the name “church.”
For, first, this is opposed by both the name and nature of the church, which
is a congregation of several and, if we speak properly, cannot consist in
one. It is opposed, second, by the interpretation of the Fathers, who with one
voice wish not one pontiff but bishops and presidents of the church—or, as the
Fathers of the Council of Basel speak, the council of those presiding over
the church—to be designated.* Chrysostom on Matthew 18; Aeneas Sylvius, in
the acts of the Council of Basel. He treats there the supreme tribunal in
the universal church, but the arguments also apply to a particular church:
namely, that the name and nature of the church, which is a congregation of
several and cannot consist in one if we speak properly, forbid one person to
be understood by “church.” Otherwise Bellarmine could have replied, If by the
word “church” one person is understood in a

[Marginalia: *Praeter. in Novum Test.* Matth. 18.; *De Pont.* l. 1. cap. 6.; Nota 5.; Nota 19.; *Sut. de Pont.* l. 1. c. 5.]

particular church, what prevents one supreme monarch from being designated by
the same word, who presides with power over the ecumenical church? Moreover,
this passage must properly and primarily be understood of particular
churches, not of the ecumenical church, whether essential or representative.
For it is impossible to bring offenses before the essential ecumenical
church. An ecumenical council is rarely convened and is an extraordinary
assembly. Christ sends the faithful to an ordinary assembly and the remedy
most readily at hand. In the Old Testament the word *Kahal*, likewise
*Gnedah*, and the Greek version's word *Ecclesia* signify the whole assembly
of the people, whether civil or ecclesiastical; or an assembly or body of
elders and rulers of the people; or of prophets. *God stands in the assembly
of the mighty and judges among the magistrates,* Psalm 82:1. *So that the one
who kills may not die until he stands before the assembly for judgment,*
Numbers 35:12. *Then the assembly shall judge between the killer and that
avenger of blood according to these judgments,* verse 24. *Then the elders of
his city shall send, take him from there, and deliver him into the hand of
that avenger of blood, so that he may die,* Deuteronomy 19:11. Those called
elders in one place are called the assembly in another. So Joshua 20:4 and 6.
He saw *Lakahath hanebiim*, an assembly of prophets—*Lakahath* by metathesis
for *kehillah*, in Greek an *Ecclesia* of prophets—in 1 Samuel 19:20. The
Septuagint renders the same Hebrew words by *synedrion*, as in Proverbs 26:26:
*His malice will be revealed in the assembly*; the Septuagint has, *in the
synedrion.* And conversely the Syriac renders the Greek word *synedrion* in
Matthew 5:22 by *kenushta*, a word which elsewhere signifies an assembly and
a synagogue. Since, therefore, the word “church” and its corresponding words
*Gnedah* and *kehillah* never signify one person individually but one
collectively—that is, several gathered into one assembly—it is evident that
Christ sent the offended brother not to one person individually, but to
several gathered into one.

3 Argument. This is taken from Christ's prohibition in Luke 22. *Christ
expressly forbade the apostles all dominion over their brothers and a
principal monarchy among them. For when there was a contention among them
about rank, he said to them, “The kings of the nations exercise dominion over
them, and those who exercise authority over them are called benefactors. But
you shall not be so.”* Defending the monarchy of the Roman pontiff, Bellarmine
replies that the Lord did not remove monarchy from the church, but rather
instituted it, and only admonished them that it differed from the civil
monarchy of the nations. He wished his vicar to preside, but without the lust
for domination found in the kings of the nations, who are generally tyrants,
command their subjects like slaves, and refer everything to their own
advantage and glory. Whitaker replies: *Christ sets before them the example of
the kings of the nations, not merely so that they may flee ambition, but so
that they may understand royal power to be wholly alien to themselves. This
can be gathered from the words without obscurity. For although
`κατακυριεύειν` and `κατεξουσιάζειν`, which are the*

[Marginalia: *Sutl. de pontif.* l. 1. c. 7.; *De Pontif.* quaest. 1. cap. 3. sect. 1.]

[1623 printed p. 197 | IA leaf 209 | PDF p. 209]

*words Matthew uses, sometimes signify immoderate rule, Luke omitted the
prefix in both verbs. But `κυριεύειν` and `ἐξουσιάζειν` are said of those who possess
power and dominion, not only of those who rule insolently or tyrannically,
etc. For not everyone who obtained a principate among the nations ruled
cruelly and unjustly; indeed, the justice and clemency of many are praised.
Nor does the prefix `κατὰ` always indicate something evil or perverse.* In the
same place: *If, therefore, nothing such as a civil monarchy ought to exist in
the church—a point Bellarmine himself concedes—then one man's dominion over
all others is removed not only by Christ but also by Bellarmine himself.* But
what do these things have to do with a bishop, you will say? Certainly, if
this argument militates against the pope's monarchy in the church, it also
militates against lesser men, whether metropolitan or bishop. The disciples
were not dreaming about supreme authority, but about authority subordinate
under Christ; and Christ places before them the example not of emperors but
of kings, Luke 22:25, and princes and magnates, Matthew 20:25. Pope Callistus
himself cites this passage in his letter to the bishops of Gaul against the
arrogance of metropolitans who judged the subjects of their coprovincial
bishops without those bishops' consent or presence. *Whatever it is necessary
to do or arrange concerning the cases of the coprovincial bishops and the
needs of their churches, clergy, and laity, let it be done by the common
consent of the coprovincial pontiffs, not with any pomp of domination but with
the most humble and harmonious administration, just as the Lord says, “I came
not to be ministered to, but to minister,” and elsewhere, “He who is greater
among you will be your minister.”* If a metropolitan who presumes to
excommunicate, judge, or do something, as it says there, against someone in a
diocesan's parish without the diocesan's counsel and consent exercises
dominion as princes and magnates are accustomed to do, then does not a bishop
whom they say is not bound by divine right to the consent and harmonious
administration of the presbyters exercise dominion like the magnates who
exercise power among the nations? Callistus requires not only humble but also
harmonious administration. Bernard, writing to Eugenius, cites this passage
to the same end: *Peter could not give you what he did not have; what he had,
he gave: solicitude over the churches. Was it dominion? Hear him: “Not
exercising dominion over the clergy,” he says, “but being an example to the
flock.” And lest you suppose that this was said from humility alone and not
truth, the Lord's word is: “The kings of the nations exercise dominion over
them; but you shall not be so.” It is plain that dominion is forbidden to the
apostles,* etc. And finally he concludes, *This is the apostolic pattern:
domination is forbidden; ministry is enjoined.* Thus Bernard plainly teaches,
says Whitaker, *that humility is not required in domination, but domination
itself is prohibited. Yet Bellarmine permits them to exercise dominion, if
they are humble and modest and mild in their domination. Christ spoke of the
thing itself,* says Junius: *“They exercise dominion”; he did not speak of the
manner—“In this or that way they*

[Marginalia: Sect. 2.; Caus. 9. quaest. 3. cap. 7.; *De consid.* l. 2.; *De Pontif.* q. 1. c. 3. §. 3.; *Iun. de pont.* l. 1. c. 9. not. 81.]

[1623 printed p. 198 | IA leaf 210 | PDF p. 210]

*exercise dominion.” “They exercise dominion; but you shall not be so,” he
says, that is, “You will not exercise dominion.” It is a simple negation of
the thing proposed.*
Tilenus likewise says, *Christ prohibits not only `τὸ ποῖον`, the manner of the
thing, but also `τὸ ὄν`, the thing itself, and removes both the thing and the
manner of it.* Bellarmine objects, *But one who says, “Do not preside as that
man does,” signifies, “You shall indeed preside, but differently from him.”
Then is it not clearly added in this passage, “He who is greater among you,
let him become as the lesser, and the leader”—in Greek `ἡγούμενος`, that is,
the commander and prince—“as the one who ministers”? Therefore, one leader
had been designated by the Lord.* Tilenus replies: *The Lord therefore did
not restrain but inflamed that ambitious passion in his followers, and
instilled in the disciples as either a command or advice what Tacitus says of
Otho in Histories 1: “Everything servilely for domination.” If this is impious
even to think, it must by all means be said that Christ had regard to their
minds and the thoughts which, `φιλοπρωτεύοντες`, desiring first place, they
turned over within themselves. And Matthew interprets it thus, expressing
Christ's mind in this way: “Whoever wishes to be great”—not whoever will be
great by my institution, but by his own wicked desire.* The thoughts which
had crept into the disciples' minds concerned not tyranny, but superiority
and greatness of power. We therefore see that not only our men, but also the
hierarchists, adduce this passage to assail the pope's dominion in
ecclesiastical matters and cases; and by the same right we adduce it against
the bishops' usurped dominion—not to say tyranny. For bishops also exercise
dominion alone in their dioceses and by divine right have no one superior in
degree of power—neither pope nor archbishop. But if by human and canon law
they have subjected themselves to an archbishop, by the same law they can
shake off his yoke; yet by divine right they are monarchs, unless they teach
that archbishops were also instituted by divine right, or acknowledge that
provincial synods ought by divine right to be fixed and ordinary, to which
injured presbyters may appeal as often as necessary, and they may be
acknowledged and restored. I will
conclude with the words of Spalatensis: *He promises them thrones in glory,
and does not say even a word about a throne of the church. He indeed ought to
have drawn their minds away from the temporal dignities which they were also
imagining for themselves, but he ought not to have been silent about a true
and proper ecclesiastical throne, if he was leaving any to them under the
description of throne and principate. Nor ought he only to have removed their
ignorance of a wicked disposition, because they fashioned temporal dominion
for themselves; he ought not to have permitted them to labor any longer under
ignorance of a pure negation either, but they should have been instructed
what greatness they were to have among themselves, especially then, at almost
the very moment of his ascent into heaven,* etc.

4 Argument. This is taken from the practice of the apostolic churches, such
as those of Corinth, Thessalonica, and Asia. In the Corinthian church not one
but several exercised discipline. The power to excommunicate the incestuous
man rested with several, not with one.

[Marginalia: *Tilen.* not. 66.; *Bel. de pont.* l. 1. c. 9.; Not. 67.; L. 1. cap. 11. num. 3.]

[1623 printed p. 199 | IA leaf 211 | PDF p. 211]

The hierarchists say that the apostle excommunicated him alone, because he
says `κέκρικα`, “I have judged.” But the verb `κρίνειν`, “to judge,” does not
here signify a decree or sentence of a judge giving judgment in the external
forum, as ought to be done in excommunication, but only Paul's judgment and
approval concerning the usefulness and necessity of excommunicating so
wicked a man. The sentence had not yet been passed when Paul wrote the
epistle; he only showed that the Corinthians themselves ought to pass it.
Moreover, others are no more excluded by the word `κέκρικα` than in Acts
15:17, when the apostle James says `ἐγὼ κρίνω`, “I judge.” Here it is expressly
said, *when you and my spirit are assembled in the name of the Lord.* But you
will say that the Corinthians could not annul Paul's command and sentence;
the necessity of obeying rested upon them. *For I also wrote for this end,
that I might know the proof of you—that is, whether you are obedient in all
things,* 2 Corinthians 2:9. It followed incidentally that they were bound to
excommunicate, namely, because Paul had shown the necessity and usefulness
beforehand and because of the apostolic judgment, which was certain.
Nevertheless, the Corinthians were free not to excommunicate if we speak of
absolute freedom; and notwithstanding Paul's `κέκρικα`, the man would not
have been excommunicated if he had not in fact been excommunicated by the
Corinthians. The Corinthians would indeed have sinned in this way by not
obeying the apostle's admonitions in so clear a matter, but the power not to
excommunicate nevertheless rested with them. Moreover, Paul's command does
not take away their power but stirs it into action. One who discharges his
office at the command and admonition of others, especially those placed in a
higher rank, is not thereby deprived of his own power. They next object that
to deliver to Satan here is nothing other than to hand the body over to Satan
to be afflicted by sickness or torments, as some of the Fathers interpret
it, and that the apostle alone could do this. But this interpretation is only
a conjecture. For it is not likely that Paul, absent and present in spirit
alone, could hand the incestuous man over to Satan to be tormented. Moreover,
this interpretation ascribes to Paul not the singular power of
excommunication, but only those extraordinary parts with which the apostle
was endowed and which elsewhere he calls `ἐνεργήματα δυνάμεων`, operations of
powers, in 1 Corinthians 12:9, as when Peter killed Ananias and Sapphira by a
word. For they admit that one excommunicated was at the same time delivered
to Satan in that sense. But there are other things which prove that the
ordinary power of excommunication was common to several. He accuses them of
connivance and sloth because they bore that wicked man in their assembly for
so long. *And you are puffed up and have not rather mourned, so that the one
who committed this deed might be removed from your midst,* 1 Corinthians 5:2.
Chrysostom says, *He does not accuse them because they did not report it to
him, but because they did not mourn that he might be removed, showing that
this ought to have been done even without an admonisher because the sin was
evident.* Lest anyone suppose that Paul is to be understood as speaking about

[1623 printed p. 200 | IA leaf 212 | PDF p. 212]

some extraordinary judgment—as though he reproached the Corinthians because they
had not prayed that some divine vengeance would remove the man—he says in the
last verse, *Therefore remove that wicked man from among yourselves.* It
therefore lay within their power to remove the wicked man from the church,
even without the apostle's admonition. Yet because they were failing in their
duty, he rebukes and admonishes them sharply so that at last they might
exercise the power entrusted to them. He repeats the same command in other
words when he says, *Purge out the old leaven,* that is, as Theophylact
interprets it, *Remove this adulterer from the midst and drive away the other
criminals.* Third, he indicates that he had written before that they were not
to associate with fornicators; he adds that they should not even eat with a
brother who is a fornicator, idolater, drunkard, or extortioner, verses 9, 10,
and 11. They could therefore put into effect in particular cases what the
apostle had written in general, order one convicted by a lawful judgment to
depart from the church, and thereafter also cast out all drunkards,
extortioners, and fornicators. Fourth, he draws his argument not only from the
epistle previously written to them, but also from their ordinary power: *Do
you not judge those who are inside?* verse 12. He uses the verb `κρίνειν`,
which in this passage signifies a judicial sentence in the external forum, as
the antecedent and consequent statements show. The antecedent is when he says,
*For what does it concern me to judge outsiders?* In one signification, a
judge is spoken of *as anyone discerning or knowing, especially according to
some speculative or practical disposition—as a geometer is a judge and
judges figures and their properties, a physician the healthy and the sick,
and a prudent person things to be done and avoided.* In this way anyone can
judge, even concerning outsiders. But when he speaks about a brother who is a
fornicator, idolater, etc., or those who are inside—that is, members of the
church—he acknowledges an ordinary power of judging. He indicates not only
that common power of discriminating judgment, but one that is greater and
more proper. The consequent statements also confirm this judicial power when
he says that God judges outsiders and adds, Therefore remove that wicked man.
It is as though he were to say: The Lord will judge the fornicators outside,
or, as Hebrews 13:4 says, the Lord will condemn fornicators and adulterers;
but do you not judge those who are inside? Therefore remove that wicked man
from among yourselves. Removing the wicked man is an effect of external
judicial power. Oecumenius says on this passage, *He then gives the reason why
he does not forbid associating with Greek fornicators or misers and the rest.
For judgment concerning them,* he says, *must be left to God, but we correct
our brothers.* Fifth, it is clear from 2 Corinthians 2:6 that an `ἐπιτιμίαν`,
or ecclesiastical censure, was imposed by several, not by one: *This rebuke
proceeding from those several,* `ἡ ὑπὸ τῶν πλειόνων`, *is sufficient for such
a man.* It is apparent that he had not been excommunicated. For Paul says
that this `ἐπιτιμίαν` is sufficient. If they had delivered him to Satan,

[Marginalia: *Marsil. pata. defens. pacis* dict. 1. cap. 2.]

removed him, and purged him out, nothing further would remain. Whether this
`ἐπιτιμία`, penalty, is the same as `ἐπιτίμησις`, rebuke, or is in addition
some public censure makes no difference. However it is understood, it was
carried out by several, and it was a step toward excommunication which the
incestuous man appears to have forestalled by notable repentance; Saravia
thinks the same. Ecclesiastical writers called the stages observed by the
ancient church in admitting the fallen to holy communion `τόπους ἐπιτιμίων`,
places of penances—namely, `πρόκλαυσιν`, preliminary weeping; `ἀκρόασιν`,
hearing; `ὑπόπτωσιν`, prostration; and `σύστασιν`, standing together. See
Casaubon concerning them. `Ἐπιτιμία` sometimes signifies nothing other than
a biting and quarrelsome rebuke. It appears that once judgment had begun they
not only rebuked him, but barred him from the sacred Supper and proceeded
toward expulsion. He was brought to repentance by these means, and they
therefore proceeded no further. Finally, those who could reconcile and give peace to a fallen
man could also excommunicate an obstinate one. But the former lay with
several, 2 Corinthians 2:7. Therefore the latter did too. In the Thessalonian
church likewise, the exercise of discipline lay with several. For the apostle
says in 2 Thessalonians 3:14, *If anyone does not obey our word through the
epistle, mark this man and do not keep company with him so that he may be
ashamed. Yet do not regard him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.*
Others read it thus: If anyone does not obey our word, mark this man by an
epistle—as if the sense were, If anyone does not obey our word, notify me by
an epistle. But the Greek interpreters Oecumenius, Theophylact, Basil, and the
Syriac embrace the former reading. Nor does the apostle say `σημαίνετε`,
signify or indicate, but `σημειοῦσθε`, that is, mark by a public judgment and
do not keep company with him. Nor does he say indefinitely `δι᾽ ἐπιστολῆς`,
by an epistle, but `διὰ τῆς ἐπιστολῆς`, through this epistle. It is as though
he were to say: If anyone does not obey our word through this epistle—that is,
does not obey this epistle of ours—and afterward continues to walk
disorderly, contrary to what we have commanded in this epistle, `τοῦτον
σημειοῦσθε`, mark this man, that is, mark him by ecclesiastical judgment, and
do not keep company with him. They could not avoid his company unless he had
first been convicted and condemned. If he had merely been reported to Paul
by epistle as one walking disorderly, they would not immediately have
abstained from his company. This interpretation is illustrated by other
passages of Scripture. *I praise you because you retain the traditions just
as I delivered them to you,* 1 Corinthians 1:2. *You know what commands I
gave you,* 1 Thessalonians 4:2. Likewise, *He who despises these things does
not despise man, but God, who gave his Holy Spirit,* 1 Thessalonians 4:8.
Whitaker says, *But the apostle testified in the same chapter that he received
from the Lord what he prescribed. Now, if anyone resists, he admonishes that
the man must first be*

*marked, second excommunicated, and third avoided so that he may be ashamed.*
We spoke above about the Asian churches when we treated the angels of the

5. The apostles themselves did not exercise singular power of ordination and
jurisdiction. They did not impose hands alone, as is evident from 1 Timothy 4,
where Paul imposed hands upon Timothy together with the presbytery. Nor did
the several apostles convened in the council at Jerusalem define the
controversies without the presbyters, but together with them. Neither did
Paul alone excommunicate the incestuous man. But they oppose these words of
the apostle: *What do you wish? Shall I come to you with a rod, or in the
spirit of gentleness?* 1 Corinthians 4, and in 2 Corinthians 13:10: *I write
these things while absent so that when present I may not act with summary
severity according to the authority which the Lord gave me,* etc. The rod and
power are the same, and Paul affirms the power given to him, Bellarmine says.
Moreover, he says, Paul alone delivered Alexander and Hymenaeus to Satan—that
is, excommunicated them and permitted them to the devil to be tormented—as he
himself says in 1 Timothy 1. Junius replies that the rod was either common and
ordinary to the ecclesiastical ministry, or singular to the apostles and
outside the ordinary course. The common and ordinary rod belonged to him as
one man, inasmuch as he was an apostle of Christ instructed with singular
authority and testimony and the power of the Spirit; but this cannot rightly
be drawn by anyone into a precedent. Others also say that that extraordinary
power, given for a time to the nascent gospel church and possessed by them as
apostles over the whole church, must be distinguished from the power they
held in common with the presbyters. *According to the common power Peter was
a `συμπρεσβύτερος`, fellow presbyter,* 1 Peter 5:1. *According to the singular

that miraculous power which the Lord imparted to them, as when Peter killed
Ananias and Sapphira by a word and Paul alone excommunicated Elymas; or in

between constituted and unconstituted churches: *I firmly believe that the
apostles and evangelists would have done nothing concerning matters and
churches already constituted and distributed into parishes under bishops

against the will of the local pastors and bishops.* He adds a third case when
he says that they shared counsels with the pastors and presbyters of the
churches concerning matters about which they had no command from the Lord,
but which were placed at their disposal. If in constituted churches the
apostles administered by the common consent and counsel of the presbyters and
pastors all ordinary matters pertaining to the common state of the church,
and did nothing by their own judgment and command even though they were
endowed with extraordinary gifts and supreme prefecture in the church, how
much more ought prelates to handle ecclesiastical affairs in accordance with
the common opinion of the presbyters?
