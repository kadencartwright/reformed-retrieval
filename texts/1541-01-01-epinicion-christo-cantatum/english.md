# A Victory Song Sung to Christ

John Calvin — 1 January 1541

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
`latin.txt` and checked against the 1866 *Calvini Opera* facsimile. The prose
preface and all sixty-one elegiac couplets are included. The English keeps one
line for each Latin verse but does not attempt to reproduce Latin quantitative
meter or impose rhyme. See the translator's notes after the text.

## A Victory Song Sung to Christ

By John Calvin, on the Calends of January in the year 1541

### John Calvin to the reader

When our merchants recently returned from the Lyon fairs and brought back the
edict of a certain inquisitor-monk, confirmed by a decree of the court of
Toulouse—in which that madman, following the customary practice of his kind,
condemned every pious book—I was surprised to see that my verses too (for that
is what he called them) were listed in its catalogue. Four years ago, when I
was at Worms, I had composed a short poem for amusement. When I privately gave
it to a few friends to read, only three copies, so far as I know, or at most
four, were transcribed at the time. Those copies, as I now infer from the
edict, later circulated more widely. Otherwise, I would have thought buried
what lay neglected among my papers; indeed, I would venture to affirm that
there are not two people in this city who had previously read it with my
knowledge. I certainly had no other intention than that it should be
suppressed altogether.

But because this raging monk, and the court of Toulouse no saner than he,
while wishing to prohibit its reading, are causing it to become common
knowledge that I wrote some poem—and because one person after another keeps
asking me what it is and what it is like—I preferred to publish it at once.
In this way I would relieve both good men of the trouble of searching and
myself of answering. At the same time I wished to admonish this foolish monk
and others like him about what they accomplish by raging against us in this
way. This, to be sure, is what they achieve by their officious harassment:
against their will and amid all their noise, many things come to light that
perhaps would otherwise have remained hidden.

## Victory Song

Behold: Father Janus rises and begins the new year,\
which custom bids us welcome with favorable omens.[^1]

This is that day which, by ancient custom, renews\
the former face of Rome's government.

On it new fasces rise, curule chairs as well,\
and conspicuous ivory gleams with the pair of consuls.

May the year rise for us under happy auspices;\
this day must be adorned with solemn favor.

Then let the devout people hail Christ as King\
and proclaim him with praises worthy of him.

He indeed rules and reigns without end, nor holds\
an empire bounded by years or months.

His glory is eternal, the power of his kingdom eternal,\
which no day varies or diminishes.

Yet since he governs years and months and days\
and continually orders time's changing turns,

it is right to begin the newborn year from his praises,\
so that it may prosper throughout its unbroken course.

What is more, he now shines with such wondrous power,\
like a consul newly entering his illustrious office.

He displays himself, illustrious beyond his wont, like one\
who returns, a great-hearted victor, after subduing his foe.

So it is: brandishing golden scepters in his victorious right hand,\
he gives his enemies signs of dreadful destruction.

He bids us exult in hope of certain salvation\
and shows that the day of triumph is already here.

Therefore, let devout tongues break unwelcome silence\
and not be still at the turning point of joy so great.[^2]

This confidence, indeed, will be a mockery—an empty song\
to our enemies, like the song of a snow-white swan.[^3]

Perhaps they will even say, “What madness is this? Will Christ\
be victor before battle has even been joined?

Has anyone won a triumph from a nation still untouched?\
Who has taken laurel before the battle line was routed?”

They will therefore believe that we, half-dead, sing a funeral song\
of the tomb before the pyre has been kindled.

There is some ground for it, I confess: with the kingdom's strength intact,\
proud ungodliness remains upon its lofty seat.

Unharmed remains the man, swollen with counterfeit honor,\
who arrogates to himself the right of supreme pontiff.[^4]

Indeed, by a nod he rules and bends great monarchs,\
raises and lays low, terrifies and cheers.

With a signal of his finger he convulses the spacious world\
and is no less fierce today than he was before.

Nor is he any less fierce when he scatters Christ's wretched sheepfold\
or vomits foul poison from his lying mouth.

No less does he rage against the saints, and now, as before,\
everywhere dyes his murderous hands with innocent blood.

But because death is life and the cross Christ's victory,\
no dreadful threats will hinder our joys.

He remains true to himself and will remain so eternally—\
he who is accustomed to conquer, not be conquered, by suffering.

When he is oppressed and suffers and is counted past hope,\
from that he prepares splendid trophies for himself and his own.

Now earthly pride foolishly exalts itself; but soon,\
driven by its own madness, it will rush headlong down.

But that holy blood, vindicator of divine honor,\
will be like seed for a generation yet to be born.

Christ's voice is a sword, and the breath of his mouth a lance;\
therefore he can fell the foe with a sudden word.

For a fifth *lustrum* now, at last, he brandishes this sword to and fro,\
wielding it with mighty hand, not without slaughter.[^5]

Satan mocked the blows that he had borne before,\
as did the pope, commander of the sacrilegious army.[^6]

Now they have learned more fully with what sharp point it pierces; at last,\
each groans, wounded by an unfamiliar blow.

Hence the pope's henchman, stunned and thunderstruck,\
hangs in suspense, and the terrified, profane crowd quakes.

Hence it is filled with noise and varied uproar,\
and the treacherous camp of its unspeakable leader trembles.

Again and again they shift in changing movements, like one\
who, his strength now broken, attempts the final battle.[^movement]

Meanwhile ours is the confidence of a settled mind,\
and firm repose stands established in a tranquil heart.

Who here can deny that Christ, to destroy his foes utterly,\
draws forth flaming missiles with unconquered hand?

Who can deny this memorable deed of the victor? Who\
would begrudge laurel garlands to so great a commander?

When battle is fought, the soldier claims a share\
of the honor for himself and does not let it all belong to the commander.

What if Christ, then, should claim for himself the praise of the conquered foe\
and choose to conquer without human valor?

To conquer a foe by force is a great thing; what of felling him with a nod?\
To drive him back is praise; what glory to rout him merely by standing!

Look: by resting he fells, and by silence routs,\
those who proved to be like rabid beasts.

Are these not the men whom people everywhere knew only lately\
to have vomited grim threats from bitter lungs?

Why does the rage conceived within remain shut up?\
Is it because savage anger lacks resources to match?

With what defense do we not see them equipped? With skill\
in wickedness, with swords, shamelessness, and tricks.

We are a small, unwarlike, naked and unarmed band,\
like a flock of sheep before terrifying wolves.

Is the victory of Christ the King not wondrous,\
because it sustains our hearts with fearless strength?

Therefore, his temples bound with triumphal laurel,\
let him stand conspicuous on the throne of a four-horse chariot.

Let those thoroughly subdued, their fury laid aside, follow the chariot—\
those who wage war against his sacred majesty.[^7]

Let Eck, red and swollen with yesterday's Bacchus,\
present here a back subdued by harsh lashes.[^8]

Let idle Cochlaeus bend his untamed head here—but first\
acquire the brow he has always lacked.[^9]

Let Nausea, who breeds disgust with his wordy books,\
now bear the yoke in silence, his mouth clamped shut.[^10]

And let the owl who long hid beneath the name Pelargus\
press the harsh wolf-toothed bit even with his savage beak.[^11]

Not because they are preeminent, but because in this contest\
the shameful cohort appointed them its standard-bearers.

Therefore, whoever fights beneath the triple tiara,\
let the whole camp present itself here with its leader.

Cunning foxes, lions stained with blood,\
and the insolent pack of barking dogs—

great men and base rabble, each in his own rank—\
let them learn to bow their necks beneath an unfamiliar yoke.

But you of better stamp, holy poets\
for whom the lovely Muse pours forth sweet measures,

sing Christ's renowned triumph in magnificent\
song. “Io Paean!” cries the rest of the crowd.[^12]

Pious ardor for study accomplishes what nature denies:\
that I attempt, O Christ, to sound your praises.[^13]

## Translator's notes

[^1]: The opening Christianizes the ceremonies of the Roman New Year. Janus,
    the fasces, ivory curule chairs, and two incoming consuls belong to Roman
    civic imagery; Calvin redirects the day's acclamation toward Christ's
    eternal kingship.

[^2]: *Cardo* is literally a hinge or pivot. “Turning point” retains the
    image, although the compact phrase *cardine laetitiae* could also mean
    “at the central occasion of such joy.”

[^3]: The swan was conventionally believed to sing at death. Calvin voices
    the adversaries' view that the Reformers' triumphal confidence is really
    the death song of people about to perish.

[^4]: The unnamed figure is the pope. The later “triple tiara” makes the
    reference explicit.

[^5]: A *lustrum* is a five-year period. “A fifth *lustrum*” points broadly
    to the Reformation's progress from 1517 into its fifth quinquennium; it
    should not be flattened into an exact statement that twenty-five complete
    years had elapsed.

[^6]: The Latin uses a singular verb with Satan and the pope and then calls
    them *uterque*, “each” or “both.” The syntax is compressed by meter, but
    the following couplet confirms two agents.

[^movement]: In *iam fracta tentat proelia summa manu*, both pairs of words
    can be construed across the line: either “with his strength broken, he
    attempts the final battle” (*fracta manu*, *proelia summa*) or “with his
    utmost strength, he tries the broken battle again” (*fracta proelia*,
    *summa manu*). The translation adopts the former but records the genuine
    syntactic ambiguity.

[^7]: The CO page clearly prints the single word *Edomiti*, not *et domiti*.
    It is the masculine plural perfect participle of intensive *edomo*, “to
    subdue thoroughly”: Christ's vanquished opponents follow his Roman-style
    triumph as captives. It is not the ethnonym “Edomites.” *Numen* here
    denotes Christ's divine majesty or power.

[^8]: Johann Eck was a prominent Roman Catholic controversialist. *Iacchus*,
    a name for Bacchus, means wine by metonymy; Calvin depicts Eck as red and
    swollen after drinking.

[^9]: Johannes Cochlaeus was another prolific opponent of the Reformers.
    *Frons* means “brow” or “countenance” and can carry the moral sense of
    modesty or shame. The taunt says he must first acquire the “brow” he has
    always lacked.

[^10]: Friedrich Nausea, later bishop of Vienna, is named. Latin *Nausea
    ... fastidia* makes a pun: Nausea produces aversion or nausea through his
    verbose books.

[^11]: Ambrosius Pelargus is the target. *Pelargus* evokes Greek *pelargos*,
    “stork,” while Calvin calls him a *bubo*, an owl. *Lupata* is a severe bit
    furnished with points likened to wolves' teeth.

[^12]: “Io Paean” is the classical cry of triumph or the refrain of a victory
    hymn, transferred here to Christ.

[^13]: The 1544 witness separates this final couplet from the preceding sixty.
    Its Latin prints a period after *ardor* even though the following *ut*
    clause completes its syntax. The translation uses a colon to make that
    dependence visible while preserving the detached coda.
