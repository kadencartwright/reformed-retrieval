# CHAPTER VII (continued)

2. Enough has now been said about this mark of excellence. Another follows,
which must be joined with the preceding one and concerns the other
circumstances of its promulgation. These are as follows.

1. The multitude of the people, consisting of six hundred thousand armed men
besides wives, children, and the mixed multitude mentioned in Exodus 12:38 and
Numbers 11:4, was certainly a great assembly. Moses led it out to meet God at
the foot of the mountain (Exodus 19:17). The rabbis say much about this
encampment, which is held in the highest renown among the Jews, and dispute
among themselves about its order. They commonly describe it in this way: the
priests or firstborn stood nearest to God, that is, to the mountain (verse 22);
then the chiefs with the other elders of the people; after them all the men;
then the women and children; and finally the proselytes. Aben Ezra places them
all in this order: first, the firstborn, בכורי; 2, the heads of the tribes or
chiefs, ראשי שבטים; 3, the elders, הזקנים; 4, the officers, השוטרים; 5, all
the men, כל איש ישראל; 6, the male children, הטף; 7, the women, הנשים; 8, the
proselytes or sojourners, הגרים.

2. This multitude was forbidden to touch the mountain in verses 12–13: *You
shall set a boundary around this people, saying, Take care that you do not
ascend this mountain or touch its edge. Whatever touches this mountain shall
certainly be put to death; no hand shall touch it*, and so forth. This was so
that they would not break through to Jehovah in order to see him (verses 21,
24).

3. They were all sanctified both morally and ceremonially (verses 10, 14),
that is, properly disposed and affected with the reverence due for hearing that
law. For God says, *Go to the people, that you may sanctify them today and
tomorrow, and let them wash their garments, that they may be ready for the
third day.*

4. With the people thus prepared to receive the law, God descended on the
third day itself in the fire itself. The mountain emitted its smoke like the
smoke of a furnace, and that whole mountain shook. There were sounds,
lightning, and a dense cloud over that mountain. Moreover, the sound of the
trumpet was exceedingly loud, so that the whole people trembled (Exodus
19:16, 18). All the people perceived the sounds and lightning, the sound of
the trumpet, and the smoking mountain itself. Perceiving them, they moved from
their place, stood far off, and were so afraid that they said to Moses, *You
speak with us and we will hear, but do not let God speak with us, lest we die*
(Exodus 20:18–19).

They stood before Jehovah while that mountain burned with fire to the summit
of heaven, with darkness, cloud, and thick gloom, and God spoke to them from
the midst of that fire (Deuteronomy 4:10–12). Jehovah spoke face to face with
them on that mountain from the midst of the fire, Moses standing between
Jehovah and them, for they were afraid because of that fire. God pronounced
those words to the whole congregation from the midst of the fire, cloud, and
thick gloom with a great voice (Deuteronomy 5:4–5, 22; read further verses
23–27). Indeed, so great was the people's consternation, so great the terror of
those wonders, and so dreadful the sight that appeared, that Moses himself,
accustomed to hearing divine voices, said, *I am terrified and trembling*
(Hebrews 12:21).

When all these things are taken together and properly considered, anyone can
see that everything about these laws appears singular. By this terrible mode
of promulgation God willed to deter that rebellious people, and everyone to
whose ears the report of that promulgation would come, from violating this law,
and to signify how highly he valued its observance. If these laws had in fact
been of the same nature and kind as the others enacted by Moses, so that they
were to perish and be abrogated with them, what purpose did all these things
serve? No one will say that God does anything in vain, and it is certain that
he does nothing without the best reason.

Let someone therefore tell me what was or could have been the reason for this
admirable promulgation of these laws, unparalleled in all memory. Why did God
will to pronounce these laws and no others in this way? If they had not been
moral and perpetually binding, would they have received a promulgation which
by its nature indicated the contrary? This solemnity quite clearly indicated
that God did not will these precepts to be consigned to oblivion or held in
contempt. On the contrary, he willed the memory of these precepts to remain
fixed and the people to employ every care and diligence in observing them in a
holy manner. Furthermore, it plainly declared that these commands were not to
possess the power to bind for only some period, as others were to last only
until the time of reformation. Otherwise the same solemnity of promulgation
would have sufficed in both cases.
