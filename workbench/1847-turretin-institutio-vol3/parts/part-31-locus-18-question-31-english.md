QUESTION XXXI.

Does the church possess legislative power properly so called to enact laws
that bind conscience in themselves? Or does it possess only ordering power to
sanction constitutions and canons for good order? We deny the former and
affirm the latter against the papists.

I. The second part of ecclesiastical power is legislative or ordering power,
which concerns the sanctioning of laws or constitutions that ought to be in
force in the church for good order and the observance of legitimate
government. Concerning this we also have a controversy with the papists.

II. Here, as elsewhere, they err by excess and exalt this power beyond measure.
They contend that the church possesses the right to enact laws properly so
called which bind conscience in themselves and cannot be violated without
mortal sin even when scandal is absent, and to which obedience is therefore
owed on account of the authority of those who command. We, however, judge that
the church has been given no properly legislative power by which it can enact
laws that bind conscience directly and in themselves. It has only ordering
power by which it can establish constitutions and canons for the preservation
of peace and good order. These bind conscience not directly and in themselves,
but only indirectly in a case of scandal. They are not laws enacted by a
prince, but merely order established by ministers—not about the essentials of
Christ’s kingdom, but only about external accidents and indifferent things.
Nor are they good in themselves, but only because of circumstances and their
relation to the end.

[Marginalia: The state of the question.]

III. Concerning the state of the question, note: (1) Acts of divine worship
are of two kinds. Some do not differ from worship itself—namely, piety and
holiness, in which worship essentially consists. Others are ordered toward
worship as aids or instruments for performing it more conveniently and
declaring it outwardly. The former kind can be instituted and commanded by God
alone, who alone is prince and legislator and has command over conscience; the
church possesses no right to add, remove, or change anything. Acts of the
latter kind are either necessary and therefore also instituted by God, at
least with respect to their existence—for example, that assemblies

[1847 vol. III printed p. 243 | IA leaf 277 | PDF p. 277]

be held, the Word preached, and the sacraments administered—or contingent and
free with respect to their particular form, which depends upon what is
honorable and suitable for each particular church: for example, that
assemblies be held and sacraments administered at this or that time and place.
The former, being necessary, depend upon God alone, and the church has no
discretion or power over them. God has left the latter, as intermediate and
indifferent things, to the church’s discretion to the extent that divine
truth, Christian simplicity, and public edification are always considered.

IV. (2) Laws properly so called, which are made with jurisdiction, must be
carefully distinguished from canons or constitutions, which pertain only to
direction. Laws sanction the essence of divine worship, and only God, as Lord
of souls, can give them. Constitutions direct a person in the practice of
worship by prescribing its order and manner with respect to place, time, and
other external circumstances, which can be variously changed as edification
requires. Laws bind conscience directly and in themselves, and their violation
brings guilt. Constitutions bind only indirectly and mediately in a case of
scandal and contempt. Thus guilt arising from their violation is not properly
because the constitution was violated, but because the authority ordained by
God was neglected and scandal was given. In enacting laws, ministers are only
heralds and envoys who promulgate the prince’s laws and report God’s commands,
addressing the faithful in his name. In constitutions, however, they are
directors of external worship in matters pertaining to good order. Although
God prescribed generally that everything be done decently and in order, he
sanctioned nothing specifically but left it to the prudence of pastors to
arrange these things as they see expedient for edification. There is such
variety in minds and conflict in judgments and dispositions that, unless
people were bound by definite rules as by bonds and bridles, they would easily
be divided among themselves and the church’s unity dissolved. Because there
was no particular precept about innumerable and mutable matters dependent upon
circumstances of times, places, and persons, God wills governors of the church
to provide for peace, edification, and propriety by establishing what seems
most suitable for the customs of peoples and the circumstances of times and
places.

[Marginalia: I. It is proved that the church does not possess legislative
power. 1. From James 4:12.]

V. With these points established, we include our doctrine in two propositions.
The first is: “Pastors have no right to enact laws properly so called that
bind conscience.” The reasons are, first, because there is one legislator
(James 4:12), namely, the supreme and unaccountable one who has right over
conscience because he can save and destroy not only the body but the soul
(Luke 12:5). Pastors are mere agents and heralds who have no right to enact or
change laws, but only the duty to promulgate them and urge their observance.
The prince’s and the herald’s command in political matters is called the same
command, but in different respects: the prince sanctions and prescribes, while
the herald only declares and announces. So in sacred matters the command’s
authority comes from God alone, while only its promulgation is left to
ministers. The distinction between a primary and secondary legislator is
futile, because conscience has no one between itself and God by whom it may be
known and judged. As it lies open to God alone, so it can be judged by God
alone. And just as no one is subordinate to a bridegroom in relation to his
bride, so no one can be subordinate to Christ with respect to authority over
the conscience which he joined to himself in spiritual marriage and over
which he therefore possesses the right of a bridegroom.

VI. The analogy of political legislators subordinate to God the supreme
legislator has no place here, because there is a great difference between the
political magistrate and ecclesiastical ministry. (1) Under God, kings and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 244 | IA leaf 278 | PDF p. 278]

magistrates are lords properly so called who have power over people’s bodies
and goods and therefore can dispose of their lives and goods. Ministers are
not such; they possess only ministry, not rule, and consequently no right over
conscience. (2) In the affairs of this temporal life, dominion does not hinder
salvation or deprive us of God’s grace. It makes people miserable, but not
evil, and what we suffer can coexist with integrity and a good conscience.
Therefore we are bound to submit even if commands are unjust and harsh. But in
matters of conscience concerning faith, piety, and God’s worship, no one can
usurp command over conscience, nor are we bound to obey anyone, for otherwise
we would be bound to error and impiety and thus incur eternal punishment, and
consciences would be stained with faults without guilt because they would be
bound to obey superiors absolutely. (3) In the political order external
obedience is sufficient even without the conscience’s inward assent, because
political authority concerns only bodily and external goods. In matters of
faith and piety, however, the heart’s assent is required, without which nothing
can be acceptable to God. Thus, although properly legislative power exists in
political matters, the same does not hold in ecclesiastical matters.

[Marginalia: 2. Because nothing may be added to the law.]

VII. Second, because nothing may be added to or taken from the divine law
(Deut. 4:2; 12:32). If nothing could be added to the Mosaic law, much less can
anything be added to Christ’s evangelical law, as Revelation confirms (Rev.
22:18). Nor can it be said, as Bellarmine does (*On the Roman Pontiff*, book 4,
ch. 17), that this is commanded only to the faithful people and not to pastors,
because every addition whatsoever, made by anyone, even an angel, is condemned
(Gal. 1:8). Nor can such an addition coexist with Scripture’s perfection, as
was seen in its place.

[Marginalia: 3. Because human commands are forbidden in Isaiah 29 and Matthew
15:9.]

VIII. Third, because all human commands are forbidden (Isa. 29; Matt. 15:9):
“In vain they worship me, teaching doctrines which are nothing but human
commands.” It cannot be objected that commands of the church are not human but
in some manner divine, whether with respect to inspiration or to the divine
authority from which they proceed. The human commands rejected here by Christ
were commands of the elders and governors of the Jewish church, who professed
that they were called and sent by God and inspired by the Holy Spirit. This is
all the more so because at that time the Jewish church was the only true
church, whose governors therefore could not but be considered ministers of
God. Thus all commands not found in the Word of God, which alone is divine and
God-breathed, are human commands condemned by Christ and Isaiah.

[Marginalia: 4. Because pastors are bound to the law.]

IX. Fourth, because pastors are subordinate judges bound to the law. They must
not cross the limits of the authority granted to them or offer anything beyond
the prince’s commands. Hence, when Christ sent the apostles, he expressly
commanded them to teach the nations to observe what he had commanded them
(Matt. 28:19–20): “Teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you.”
They are commanded to teach Christ’s commands, not their own, and not commands
he would later give, but those he had already given—“all that I commanded”—so
that he would leave no room for anyone to impose as Christ’s command anything
beyond the Word already handed down to them. Thus elsewhere, speaking of the
gift of the Holy Spirit, Christ says that he would bring to their remembrance
what he had already said to them (John 14:26). Christ’s statement elsewhere,
“He who hears you hears me,” does not prove that governors of the church can
forge new commands and burden consciences with new laws. Rather, ministers
speaking Christ’s words are to be heard as Christ. When those who speak
Christ’s commands are heard, Christ is heard; but Christ is not heard when
those who speak their own commands rather than his are heard.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 245 | IA leaf 279 | PDF p. 279]

[Marginalia: 5. Because the faithful must not become servants of human beings.
Galatians 5:1; 1 Corinthians 7:23.]

X. Fifth, because the faithful, once liberated by Christ, must not be brought
back under a yoke of servitude or become servants of human beings (Gal. 5:1; 1
Cor. 7:23). This would happen if human beings had the right to enact laws
properly so called that bind conscience. It cannot be said that this liberty
concerns only liberation from the slavery of sin and the yoke of the Mosaic
law, but not from obedience to prelates, to which Christ himself subjected us.
For Christian liberty, by subjecting us to Christ, thereby exempts us from
every other yoke and every law not given by Christ. The obedience he wills to
be rendered to teachers must always be understood with the condition that the
teachers prescribe nothing other than what Christ gave them in his commands.
Those who claim the right to enact new laws do not meet this condition.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XI. It is one thing for laws to bind conscience immediately and in themselves;
it is another “for obedience to be owed to laws for conscience’s sake.” Divine
laws alone bind conscience in themselves, because God alone has command over
conscience. Obedience for conscience’s sake is owed to human laws, whether
political or ecclesiastical, because conscience commands us to obey God, who
instituted magistracy and ministry. Laws whose command is properly and
directly over conscience bind conscience; but laws observed for conscience’s
sake rest on another’s authority, that is, the authority of another law.

XII. Matthew 16:19 does not sanction legislative power, but only grants
ministers authority to preach the gospel and exercise discipline in Christ’s
name, by whose power believers are loosed and unbelievers and impenitent
persons bound, as was seen elsewhere. John 20:21 proves it no better. It
establishes the authority of the calling of those sent, but does not make them
lords of our faith, whom Christ willed to be ministers of our joy (2 Cor.
1:24), for a principal cause does not impart its own power to instruments but
acts according to their condition and manner. The word “as” denotes likeness
and analogy between the mission of Christ and that of ministers, not equality.
The likeness is in the call to ministry and the ministry of the Word, not in
the mediatorial work or royal and legislative power, which is peculiar to
Christ and incommunicable to others.

XIII. The apostolic constitution sanctioned at the Jerusalem council (Acts 15)
was not a new law but repeated from Moses. With respect to fornication it was
of perpetual right and necessary in itself; with respect to strangled things,
blood, and things sacrificed to idols, it was sanctioned only relatively, for
charity and the necessity of avoiding offense, and only for a time, as was
proved in Locus XII, Question 26, theses 20–21. Nor, because it is called a
burden, does it follow that it is a law properly so called that binds
conscience. This is understood only relatively, by reason of a positive
precept which the faithful were bound to observe to avoid scandal. Otherwise,
Paul himself testifies that conscience is not bound in itself by such a
prohibition (1 Cor. 8:8, 13; 10:23, 25, 27).

XIV. The law against ordaining men twice married (1 Tim. 3:2) is not a
positive law enacted only by the church, but a divine and eternal law
established by God himself, the author of nature, who commanded two, not more,
to be in one flesh. Even if Paul had established this law, it would not follow
that any laws whatsoever can be established by any minister or church.

XV. Nothing else can be gathered from Hebrews 13:17 than that obedience is
owed to teachers so long as they themselves hear Christ and speak God’s words.
If they lead us away from Christ, they must be anathema to us. Thus two causes
concur in the ministry of the Word: God is the principal efficient cause, and
the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 246 | IA leaf 280 | PDF p. 280]

instrumental cause is a human being. Whoever obeys the latter while he is
moved by the efficient and principal cause obeys God himself.

XVI. When the apostle speaks of precepts and traditions which he gave the
faithful to observe, he means either Christ’s own precepts (1 Thess. 4:2; 2
Thess. 3:6, 14) or ecclesiastical institutions about indifferent matters
concerning propriety and good order. These must indeed be observed to preserve
good order, but not from an obligation of conscience, as in 1 Corinthians
11:2.

XVII. The example of the Levitical priests in Deuteronomy 17:12 is wrongly
adduced here, because their commands did not bind the people except insofar as
they taught the law handed down by God, of which they had been particularly
appointed by God as defenders and guardians (v. 11): “According to the
prescription of the law which they teach you and according to the judgment
which they tell you, you shall act.”

[Marginalia: II. The proposition concerning the church’s canons.]

XVIII. The second proposition is: “Although pastors possess no legislative
power properly so called, or right to enact laws that bind conscience, they do
possess power to establish ecclesiastical canons and constitutions for good
order and propriety. Although these must be observed to preserve good order,
they do not bind outside a case of scandal and contempt.” This is the other
part of ecclesiastical government, occupied with establishing external polity
or good order so that all things may be done orderly and decently in God’s
house (1 Cor. 14:40). It is treated in 1 Corinthians 11:34 and 16:1. The
apostolic constitution in Acts 15 and various things sanctioned by the
apostles concerning church government pertain to it.

XIX. The object of this directive power is not the doctrine of faith and
worship itself, which rests upon the Word of God alone, but other matters
about which no express Word of God is given. These are either merely
indifferent and free, or ambiguous and doubtful, about which God handed down
nothing definite in particular, as in the case of which the apostle speaks in
1 Corinthians 7:10, 12; or they concern the manner of external worship, that
is, determinations of circumstances without which the precepts of the first
table and public exercises of piety in ecclesiastical assemblies can scarcely,
if at all, be duly observed and performed. To these belong the time, place,
form, and rule of prayers, sermons, administration of the sacraments, fasts,
and alms, all of which ought to be instituted and practiced not tumultuously
but duly and becomingly for the church’s edification. Since they are
indifferent in themselves and do not directly touch the mystery of our
salvation, they do not bind consciences in themselves and are not so fixed and
immovable that they cannot be rescinded and changed when the church’s use
requires it.

XX. Although the authority of these constitutions is great in the church, we
deny that it is absolute rather than limited, equal to divine laws, or that
their transgression is as grave a sin as the transgression of divine laws. We
deny that they can change the nature of things so that matters indifferent in
themselves become necessary and of perpetual right; that any part of Christian
righteousness, much less any merit before God, consists in them; or that they
extend beyond ecclesiastical polity or good order, which are not in themselves
acts of piety, but only serve piety for another reason and accidentally. We
also say that these constitutions must not be measured from themselves but
from divine laws, so that they do not hinder those laws in any way but rather
serve them. Further, no sin can exist in transgressing them except indirectly:
not because of the act itself, as in murder or fornication, but because of
something else—if the transgression arises from contempt for legitimate
authority, since obedience is commanded by God and its neglect is sin; if it
involves neglect of a brother’s edification; or if it arises from impiety and
license. If all these are absent, there will be no sin in their transgression.
Thus church laws do not change the natures of the things themselves so that
they cease to be indifferent. When they are prohibited, only their use, which
is entirely free, is restricted so that it may be accommodated to the
church’s necessary order

[1847 vol. III printed p. 247 | IA leaf 281 | PDF p. 281]

and judged by Paul’s rule: “All things are lawful for me, but not all things
are expedient” (1 Cor. 10:23). Because the circumstances of all times and
places are not the same, and the same things are not always expedient
everywhere, laws of this kind are not everywhere or always valid, nor is their
use perpetual. The reason for the command, not so much the commander’s
authority, must be considered.

XXI. If these statutes and rules are called laws, “law” is taken broadly. The
fathers variously call them *canons*, *ordinances*, *directives*, *forms*, or
*definitions*. Sometimes, however, they are distinguished: “canons” properly
designate dogmas of faith; “forms,” the formulas or rites of liturgies; and
“definitions,” the chapters or decrees pertaining to clerical discipline. In
later times all decrees of this kind were designated by the name “canons.”

XXII. The primitive church used a twofold law: divine and canonical. It used
the codex of Holy Scripture for divine law and the codex of canons for
canonical law. Hence two codices were customarily placed in the midst of the
assembly at councils so that by their law the bishops might examine dogmas of
piety among themselves, refute emerging heresies, and decide questions
pertaining to ecclesiastical discipline and sacred polity. Hence these words
are repeatedly found in the acts of the Council of Chalcedon, Constantinople
in Trullo, and others: “The sacred and inviolable codices of the gospel having
been set in the midst before them.” The Synod of Chalcedon itself also mentions
the codex of canons, cites canons of the fathers from it, and pronounces on
controversies by their law. Canon 1 says: “We have judged it right that the
canons established up to this time by the holy fathers in every synod be
observed.” Relevant is Novella 131, *On Ecclesiastical Canons*, where the
emperor says in section 1: “We therefore sanction that the holy ecclesiastical
canons expounded and confirmed by the four holy councils have the force of
laws; for we receive the decrees of the aforesaid holy councils just as the
sacred Scriptures and guard the canons as laws.” This is that genuine ancient
law and body of canon law sanctioned in the empire by ecclesiastical and
imperial power. The papal body of canon law was gradually imposed upon it by
usurpation alone, without the authority of the churches and empire and indeed
against it.

XXIII. The collection of these canons was varied and manifold. Some include
the eight books of apostolic *Constitutions* under Clement’s name. In *On
Concord*, book 3, chapter 2, Pierre de Marca says that everyone today is
persuaded they were the production of a third-century writer; Blondel agrees
in the preface to his *Apology for Jerome*. Dallæus, however, judges that they
were written only in the fifth century, shortly before the decree of Gelasius
at the Roman Council in 494. They also include the *Canons of the Apostles*,
which learned men long ago observed to be spurious and which Dallæus holds
were collected only in the fifth century—the century of the books of the
*Constitutions*—perhaps by the same author, from canons of various councils.
It is certain that a codex of canons existed before the end of the four
councils, as is gathered from acts 4 and 11 of the Council of Chalcedon and
admitted by Baronius at the year 341. Into it were gathered the canons of the
first four ecumenical councils—Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, and
Chalcedon—and the canons of particular councils approved by ecumenical
councils: Ancyra in 308, Neocaesarea in 316, Gangra in 326, Antioch in 341,
and Laodicea in 364. The first two preceded the Council of Nicaea and the
latter two followed it, but they preceded the other three ecumenical councils.
This is the *Codex of Canons of the Universal Church*, which Jean du Tillet,
bishop of Meaux, published in France; after him Christophe Justel, jurist,
published it at Paris in 1610; and Henri, Christophe’s son, had it reprinted in
1661 in the *Bibliotheca Juris Canonici

[1847 vol. III printed p. 248 | IA leaf 282 | PDF p. 282]

Veteris* (*Library of Ancient Canon Law*). To this must be added the *Codex of
the African Church* and Photius’s
*Nomocanon* with Balsamon’s scholia, edited in Greek and Latin by the same
Justel, and many others of that kind which appeared at various times and
places. Dionysius Exiguus, Isidore Mercator, Burchard of Worms, Ivo of
Chartres, and finally Gratian labored in collecting them. Gratian titled his
work *The Concord of Discordant Canons*, which was held in high esteem among
the papists.

XXIV. There is a great difference between ecclesiastical canons and the
documents of faith and morals contained in Scripture. The latter are, as the
ancients say, “the unbending canon of truth.” Ecclesiastical canons, however,
as positive human law established only for the sake of external order, can be
changed and rescinded by the same consensus by which they were sanctioned,
with more suitable ones substituted in their place as times and persons
require. Tertullian expressed this well in *On the Veiling of Virgins*: “The
rule of faith is wholly one, alone immovable and irreformable, etc. But while
this law of faith remains, the other matters of discipline and conduct admit
the novelty of correction.” They differ from civil laws in various ways. (1)
Civil laws flow from sovereign power, but canons only from ministerial power.
(2) Laws, however harsh and less than equitable, must be obeyed for
conscience’s sake so long as they demand nothing shameful and contrary to
God’s commands, even when scandal is absent; protest against more inequitable
canons is permissible. (3) Laws are armed with punishments properly so called,
but canons are not; nor must ecclesiastical censures be numbered among
punishments properly so called.

XXV. Moreover, careful attention must be given to these canons: (1) That they
not proceed beyond the sphere of indifferent things, since in eternal and
necessary matters God alone is our legislator (James 4:12). (2) That by their
complexity and number they not burden and hinder God’s worship more than they
adorn and advance it. (3) That they not tend toward tyranny and superstition,
or in any way run counter to the Word of God, Christian liberty, and the
analogy of faith, or cast a snare upon consciences as though the whole course
of salvation turned upon them. (4) That no opinion of merit, worship, or
necessity be attached to them, and that they not be regarded as divine worship
itself, since they are only its aids and appendages.
