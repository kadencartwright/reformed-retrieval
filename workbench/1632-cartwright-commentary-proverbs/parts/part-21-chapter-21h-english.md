Verse 19. It is better to dwell in a desert land than with a woman of contentions and indignation.

In כעס (*indignationis*, indignation), מדינים (*contentionum*, contentions), and מדבר (*deserto*, desert) there is a metonymy of the adjunct.

The purpose is the same as that of verse 9 of this chapter.

This Proverb seems to be the same as the one found in verse 9. And indeed they have a great

[1632 witness | PDF p. 474 | printed columns 932-933]

affinity, but meanwhile they differ in meaning just as they do in words. For in the preceding Proverb he set before us only a contentious wife; now he has also added one who is prone to unrest of mind, wrath, and indignation.

There are people who, although prone to anger, nevertheless do not always bring it forth openly, but digest it within themselves and with their own mind, so that they are harmful to no one so much as to themselves. For you may see those who, although wrathful, nevertheless bear a mind averse to quarrels and torment themselves rather than others; likewise those who quarrel vigorously while they are not inwardly moved by anger. Therefore, something different is said in this Proverb than in the preceding one. But when wrath is added to a mind desirous of contending, that contention is rendered more barbed—like a sharpened whetstone—and intolerable. For thus it will come about that quarrels cannot be confined within private walls but break out into shouts and loud cries. Next, since indignation and perturbation of mind are exceedingly ungovernable, it happens that, as they contend, frequent torches of abuse and flames of threats fly from their mouths.

Then the accompanying disadvantage of such a wife is set forth as greater than that of one who is only contentious. For it is a greater disadvantage to dwell in a desert land than in a corner of a roof. In the latter, everything which pertains to the sustenance and maintenance of life can be supplied, if you except that one disadvantage of staying under the open sky; but in a desert land there is the greatest lack of all the conveniences of life. In a corner of a roof a person can enjoy companionship, of which he must necessarily be deprived in the desert. Finally, in a corner of a roof he is free from the danger of death to which he is exposed in the desert because of wild beasts and serpents which roam there, just as Moses testifies happened to the Israelites travelling in the desert. And Solomon elsewhere judges it more tolerable to meet a bear bereaved of her cubs than a fool in his folly.

This very thing must be transferred to a contentious and indignant husband; and this evil is so much greater and more grievous to a gentle, well-conducted wife than to a husband, because a wife, through the frailty of her sex, is more easily broken and crushed than a husband.

From these things it is further evident how great and how divine a gift they have received from God to whom a comfortable and pleasant yoke of marriage has fallen.

Verse 20. A desirable treasure and ointment are in the dwelling of the wise man, but a foolish man will swallow it up.

In שמן (*unguentum*, ointment) there is a synecdoche of the part for delicacies. In יבלענו (*absorbebit*, will swallow up) there is a metaphor from huge beasts, or whales, which swallow down some great mass of food all together and at once.

The purpose is, by exhorting us to thrift and economy together, to dissuade us from the profusion of goods.

By this Proverb the godly are encouraged toward prudent foresight in the administration of the household by gathering and storing all those things which pertain to the sustenance and maintenance of this life. Hence Christ at Matt. 13 compares a good Minister of the word with a prudent father of a household who has old things and new in abundance with which provision may be made for the family. And it is reckoned to Joseph's praise that he stored so great a quantity of grain that it was of use for many years afterward. For since the land does not always bear its fruits equally, and God sometimes, in order to exact punishment for sins, withholds his blessing and withdraws the rain, and since the things which

[1632 witness | PDF p. 475 | printed columns 934-935]

are now offered for sale will afterward not easily be procured, it is joined with godly wisdom to store up at home at the opportune time those things which serve necessary use.

From this it is evident that by those words, “Give us today our daily bread,” we are not prohibited from making provision for ourselves for a year, or even for many years, especially in strongholds and cities liable to siege; but rather we are taught that the fervour of prayers for the conveniences of this life ought no more to be extinguished because we have everything laid up and stored in cellars and storehouses than if we were wholly destitute of them.

By what he adds concerning ointment it is understood that the liberal use of God's creatures is granted to people, and that not only in their pure and bare use, just as they were established by nature—among which are found many exceedingly delicate things—but that the use of those things is also granted to us which are made by the perfumer's craft out of many costly substances joined and united into one body; ointment is in this class. See Isa. 39.2. For since Christ is the heir of the world, and the godly are coheirs through faith and possessors in good faith, to whom does a truer right to these things belong than to the faithful person?

By the statement that this is in his dwelling, people are taught that they ought to provide for their families in such a way that they have no need always to send to the market, shops, or apothecaries, but have stored at home those things which serve either the necessary or the liberal use of life, in such a manner that new supplies are always brought in before the old have been exhausted through their moderate use and prudent dispensation.

Although the labour and foresight of a wise man are required for this matter, nevertheless the source and author of this bestowal upon the godly is God, as at Prov. 3.10 and Lev. 26.4, 5, 10.

The former hemistich is confirmed by the latter from opposites—namely, because the fool swallows it up. Therefore he does not deny that the fool sometimes has an abundance of these things, but he squanders it and consumes it through gluttony or through untimely and continual banquets.

Solomon therefore condemns to folly these prodigal bankrupts whom the common people call great cultivators of human society, or (to use their own expression) good companions, “Good fellowes”; indeed, he further affirms that, in the likeness of brutes and after the manner of beasts, they do not so much eat the things which are brought into granaries, cellars, and storehouses as gulp them down and swallow them up. Yet the Apostle at 1 Tim. 4 designated the moderate use of all these things by the word “participation.”

Nevertheless, it must not therefore be said that all those who have an abundance of these things are immediately wise and endowed with true wisdom; rather, for the wise dispensation of these things a certain gift of wisdom is required, which is conferred from heaven.
