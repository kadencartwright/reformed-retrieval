# John Brown, *Libri duo contra Wolzogium et Velthusium*, Volume II (1670)

> **Working edition in progress:** This directory contains an AI-assisted,

> English translation. It is not a critical edition or publication-ready text;
> every reading and translation should be checked by a qualified human editor
> against the cited ONB facsimile.

## Work and volume identity

- **Author:** John Brown of Wamphray (c. 1610–1679), printed as Johannes Broun
  in the ONB catalog record
- **Collective work:** *Libri duo. In priori, Wolzogium ... In posteriori,
  Lamberti Velthusii sententia Libertino-Erastiana ...*
- **Volume used here:** Volume II only, with the divisional title
  *Libertino-Erastianæ Lamberti Velthusii Sententiæ, De Ministerio, Regimine, &
  Disciplinâ Ecclesiasticâ ... Confutatio*
- **Date:** 1670
- **Cataloged collective imprint:** Amsterdam, Henricus ab Aquisgrano
- **Subject of Volume II:** Lambert van Velthuysen’s position on ecclesiastical
  ministry, government, and discipline

The divisional title leaf says that the refutation was written “by the same
author” but does not repeat Brown’s name or the collective imprint. Those data
come from the ONB record for the bound two-volume work. No text from Volume I,
Brown’s work against Wolzogen, is transcribed or translated here. Wolzogen is
mentioned once within the selected Volume II text because Brown compares his
treatment of orthodox theologians with Velthuysen’s.

## Part 01 boundary and source map

Part 01 is Brown’s first complete argumentative unit in the body: his statement
and synopsis of Velthuysen’s position, the first of the three steps Brown
announces on printed p. 4.

- It begins with the body heading and `Narus Satanas quàm necessarium...` on
  printed p. 1 / ONB canvas 9.
- It carries Brown’s introduction and his detailed extraction of Velthuysen’s
  claims concerning church power, excommunication, ministry, ordination,
  preaching, sacraments, and the secular magistrate.
- It includes Brown’s final compact synopsis and ends with the complete sentence
  `Plus potest unus aliquis ... quàm plures docti multarum horarum, imò, &
  dierum spacio, confirmare ac demonstrare possunt.` at the top of printed p. 20
  / ONB canvas 28.
- The next paragraph, `Nos tamen compendiosam aliquam inibimus rationem...`,
  begins Brown’s transition to his second announced step, comparison with the
  Reformed confessions, on the same page and is excluded.

The unit therefore spans exactly twenty printed pages, pp. 1–20 / ONB canvases
9–28, with only the upper portion of p. 20 included. The unnumbered divisional
title on canvas 7 and its verso on canvas 8 are mapped here as preliminary
matter but are not part of the twenty-page body unit.

## Part 03 boundary and source map

Part 03 completes the confessional survey and then contains Brown’s first two
assertions concerning Christ’s mediatorial kingdom.

- It begins with `Confess. Wirtemberg cap. de ordine` in the lower portion of
  printed p. 37 / ONB canvas 45.
- It completes the Württemberg and Bohemian material, cites Polish synods and
  the Basel Confession, and concludes the survey with chapters 23, 30, and 31
  of the British Confession.
- `ASSERTIO I` proves that Christ as mediator is king and possesses a kingdom;
  `ASSERTIO II` distinguishes that mediatorial kingdom from his universal
  kingdom as God.
- It ends with the complete sentence `De secundâ assertione jam satis.` in the
  upper portion of printed p. 57 / ONB canvas 65. The following heading,
  `ASSERTIO III`, and its text on the same page are excluded.

The unit has twenty-one synchronized markers for printed pp. 37–57. Because
only the lower portion of p. 37 and upper portion of p. 57 belong to it, the
unit comprises approximately twenty full pages of text without splitting
Assertion II.

Part 17 is Brown’s complete `ASSERTIO XIV`, arguing that the conversion of

## Editorial and translation method

Long *s*, ligatures, line-end divisions, and typographic `u/v` are normalized.
Obvious typographic contractions are expanded, while common reference
abbreviations are retained. Substantive spelling, wording, names, numbering,
quotations, and foreign-language matter are retained. Running heads,
bare page numbers, signatures, catchwords, ornaments, and non-textual bleed from
adjacent pages are omitted. Page markers identify the volume, printed page,
and one-based ONB canvas. The duplicate ampersand printed on p. 1 is retained.

The short Greek expression on p. 9 is retained in the Latin and explained in a
translator’s note. Brown’s quotations from Velthuysen are distinguished
typographically, but their wording remains Brown’s Latin presentation rather
than a new transcription of Velthuysen’s Dutch books.

The English is a full translation of the accompanying Latin, not a summary.
It preserves Brown’s polemical attributions as historical claims rather than
endorsing them. Biblical quotations are translated directly from Brown’s
printed Latin wording. No modern translation has been used as a wording source.

## Part 17: conversion through the ministry of the word

### Latin source

[Volume II; printed p. 258; ONB canvas 266 (lower portion)]

ASSERTIO XIV.

Inter alios hujus Ministerii, verbi pastoribus à Christo commissi, fines &
effectus, subordinatos Dei gloriæ & intermedios, hic; qui est hominum ad Deum
conversio, & cum Deo reconciliatio, inter maximos numerandus est.

Non dicimus, ministerium verbi per pastores administratum, esse causam
conversionis aut regenerationis principalem, absit. Hanc prærogativam soli
Deo, & gratiæ efficaciæ ascribendam in solidum esse, constanter affirmamus:
Sed tantum esse causam instrumentalem, seu medium, quo Deo visum est uti, ad
hunc effectum producendum. Nec tamen volumus, quando instrumentum appellamus,
vim aliquam naturalem ei ascribere; ac si physicè producere posset effectum:
sed tantum medium morale à Deo institutum volumus, quo talis à Deo producitur
effectus. Nec in hoc negotio verbi administrationem ab efficaciâ Spiritus
separamus: nam sine eâ prorsus inefficax erit. Instrumentum seu medium, licet à
principali agente distinguatur, effectum tamen nunquam producere potest nisi
quando à principali agente applicatur & adhibetur, & tum effectus principaliter
à principali causâ pendet. Denique non dicimus hoc esse unicum ac solitarium
Dei, in hoc opere, medium, absit. Limites Omnipotenti non præfigimus. Novimus,
Eum non modo posse, sed & re verâ nonnunquam actu uti aliis, ad suos
convertendos,

mediis. Sed dicimus hoc esse medium præcipuum ac maximè ordinarium, quod, ad
fidem ingenerandam, adhibet. Seu, in hunc finem specialiter à Deo institutum
esse hoc medium, ministerium scil. & verbi administrationem; ac propterea non
modo non contemnendam esse hanc Christi ordinantiam, sed & magni semper

ASSERTION XIV.

Among the other ends and effects of this ministry of the word committed to
Pastors by Christ—ends subordinate to God’s glory and intermediate in
character—this one, namely the conversion of human beings to God and their
reconciliation with God, must be numbered among the greatest.

We do not say that the ministry of the word administered through Pastors is
the principal cause of conversion or regeneration—far from it. We constantly
affirm that this prerogative must be ascribed wholly to God alone and to the
efficacy of grace. We say only that it is an instrumental cause, or a means
which God has seen fit to use to produce this effect. Yet when we call it an
instrument, we do not mean to ascribe any natural power to it, as though it
could physically produce the effect. We mean only a moral means instituted by
God, through which such an effect is produced by God. Nor in this business do
we separate the administration of the word from the efficacy of the Spirit,
for without it the administration will be wholly ineffectual. Although an
instrument or means is distinguished from the principal agent, it can never
produce an effect unless it is applied and employed by the principal agent;
and then the effect depends principally upon the principal cause. Finally, we
do not say that this is God’s sole and solitary means in this work—far from it.
We do not set bounds for the Almighty. We know that he not only can use other
means for converting his own, but sometimes actually does so in very fact.

[Volume II; printed p. 259; ONB canvas 267]

But we say that this is the chief and most ordinary means which he employs to
engender faith. That is, this means—namely, the ministry and administration of
the word—was specially instituted by God for this end. We therefore say not
only that this ordinance of Christ must not be despised, but that it must
always be highly esteemed. We shall confirm this from various testimonies of
Scripture, against Velthuysen as well as the Enthusiasts, Anabaptists, and
Schwenckfeldians.

1. Mark 16:15–16: “And he said to them, Go into the whole world and preach the
Gospel to every created being. He who believes and is baptized shall be
saved.” It is evident here that Christ gave his disciples the power to
announce the Gospel and sent them to preach, and this for the end that the
elect should believe. The generation of faith, therefore, is an end of the
announced Gospel.

2. John 17:20: “But I pray not only for these, but also for those who will
believe through their word”—that is, the word of the Apostles. It follows that
certain people will believe, and that this will be brought about through the
word of the Apostles: that is, through the announcement of the Gospel, or the
administration of the word by the Apostles and, in this respect, by their
successors, continued until the end. Christ therefore presupposes that the
words of Ministers are the ordinary means by which those for whom he poured
out prayers to God are brought to faith.

3. The same is evident from Christ’s parable in Matthew 13 about the seed which
the sower sowed. Christ himself interprets that seed and says that it is the
word of the Kingdom in verses 19, 20, 22, and 23; and he says that the good
soil is the person “who hears the word and applies his understanding, who then
bears and yields fruit—one indeed a hundredfold, another sixtyfold, and another
thirtyfold.” That is, it is the person who is converted to faith and produces
good works. This conversion takes place through the word of the Kingdom heard
and blessed by the Spirit.

[Volume II; printed p. 260; ONB canvas 268]

Therefore some are saved through this word, while others who hear the same
word remain idle professors, hypocrites, and at most temporary believers, not
converted.

since it is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes.” As the
And so forth. As the preceding verses show, the Apostle had resolved and often intended to go to

mockery, besides the fact that he preached such doctrine, he nevertheless says

follows that those who withdraw themselves from hearing preaching deliberately
reject God’s power, as it were, and drive his delivering hand far away from

5. Romans 10:14–15, 17: “How then will they call upon him in whom they have not

how will they hear without one who preaches? And how will they preach unless
they have been sent? Therefore faith is from hearing, and hearing through the
word of God.” No text clearer than this can be brought forward to confirm our
thesis. For by a most elegant progression the Apostle openly shows the usual
order which God observes in converting human beings. Faith, he says, is
required for the proper invocation of God, and faith is not ordinarily
produced except through the word

[Volume II; printed p. 261; ONB canvas 269]

heard—that is, the word of God announced by a preacher, namely a sent preacher.
The Apostle therefore concludes that faith is from hearing and hearing through
the word of God. What could have been said more clearly? Velthuysen’s replies
will be examined later; see the Belgic Annotations on the passage.

6. 1 Corinthians 1:21: “For since, in the wisdom of God, the world did not know
God through that wisdom, it pleased God through the foolishness of preaching
to save those who believe.” God therefore instituted preaching—which many in
former times, and some now, among whom Velthuysen must be numbered first,
regarded and still regard as foolishness—so that through it, as his primary and
ordinary means, he might save his own. “He expressly mentions preaching,”
Pareus says on the passage, “because the Gospel is the power of God insofar as
it is preached and heard. For faith is from hearing, and hearing through the
word of God, namely the word preached, as is said there: This is the word which
we preach. This declares the dignity and efficacy of the Ecclesiastical
ministry.” Moreover, it is evident from the preceding verses that the Apostle
is speaking here of preaching itself, or the administration of the word. “For
Christ did not send me,” he says in verse 17, “to baptize, but to preach the
Gospel.” And in verse 18 he says, “The word of the cross is foolishness indeed
to those who perish, but to those who are saved, that is, to us, it is the
power of God.” From this we can draw another argument. For the word of the
cross—that is, the preaching of the Gospel—is called the power of God, meaning
God’s efficacious means for converting his own and bringing them to salvation.
Thus the phrase “the foolishness of preaching” means “foolish preaching,” by a
Hebraism. In the same way Christ is afterward called foolishness to the
Gentiles in verse 23, insofar, namely, as he is preached in the Gospel.

From this it is sufficiently apparent how ridiculous Velthuysen is when he
says on pages 339–340: “The opposing party supplies no text from which it may
appear that God especially wills to regenerate human beings through the
preaching of the word performed by Pastors.” For the Reader now sees from how
many passages of Scripture it can be clearly demonstrated that this is God’s
principal and ordinary way of converting human beings and bringing them to
Christ. What does he say to Romans 10? “The Apostle wishes only to prove,” he
says, “that the calling of the Gentiles to the fellowship of the Gospel cannot
take place unless God wills and commands it, and therefore that no one can
invite them unless God orders it, expressing this in these words: How will
they preach unless they are sent?” Reply. Pareus says on the passage that it
has been disputed whether these things are to be understood of the Jews or of
the Gentiles; preserving the judgment of others, he refers them to both—that
is, to “all who call upon him” from verse 12—because “he speaks indefinitely of
everyone: no one can call upon one in whom he does not believe, and so cannot
call upon God or Christ unless he believes in him, whether he is a Jew or a
Greek. The inferential particle *therefore,*” he says, “depends upon what
precedes: since with the heart one believes unto righteousness and with the
mouth confession is made unto salvation, and the mouth speaks from the
abundance of the heart, how will they call upon him in whom they do not
believe?” And so forth. 2. But what if we suppose him to be speaking of the
Gen-

[Volume II; printed p. 271; ONB canvas 279]

tiles? This always remains true: they cannot believe in God or Christ unless
they hear of him; they cannot hear unless there are people to preach; and no
one can preach unless he is sent. And so it will always follow that hearing
the word announced by a preacher is necessary as the external instrument by
which faith is ordinarily obtained. “For the Apostle is speaking of the
ordinary way of salvation,” Pareus says. “Reading the divine word is useful,
but belongs to a few; hearing belongs to everyone. Therefore, just as the
invocation of God is necessary for salvation, and faith in Christ is necessary
for the invocation of God, so hearing the word is necessary for faith. He is
speaking of the hearing which takes place with the ears, not that which takes
place with the heart, although both are required for faith, as is evident from
the following member.” And a little later: “For hearing the word, therefore,
the preaching of the word is needed. Heralds and Teachers are needed to make
the word resound in people’s ears. — And here the necessity of the
Ecclesiastical ministry is founded, since God does not now will to speak to the
Church from heaven, but through Heralds of the word.”

Finally, I ask, how does this reply differ from the Socinians’ reply?
Volckel, *On the Sending of Ministers,* page 99, says: “The meaning of these
words is that no one can preach in any manner—that is, announce a new
matter—unless he is sent, that is, unless this duty is imposed upon him by
God.” What other escape does Velthuysen devise? “Or one must say that the
passage speaks of the sending of the Apostles, with which the calling of
Pastors has nothing in common. And since God willed the Gentiles to become
participants in Christ’s grace, that will and the Gospel had to be announced
to them if they were to believe, and God predestined them from eternity to this
faith. The Apostle expresses this in these words: How will they believe unless
it has been preached to them? By preaching he understands the announcement of
the Gospel, whether made orally, in writing, by private exhortation, or in a
pub-

[Volume II; printed p. 272; ONB canvas 280]

lic assembly.” Reply. 1. Velthuysen certainly does not know what he ought to
say. This must be answered, he says, or that—which is the mark of a doubtful
and wavering mind. 2. By what argument, or even probable showing, does
Velthuysen prove that this must be understood only of the Apostles and their
sending? Does not the statement, “How will they call upon him in whom they have
not believed?” have its truth now no less than formerly? Is not the statement,
“And how will they believe in him of whom they have not heard?” also of eternal
truth? Is not the statement, “How will they hear without one who preaches?” as
true now as formerly? With what appearance of probability, therefore, can
Velthuysen say that this sending is to be understood solely of the sending of
the Apostles? Was that oracle in Isaiah 52:7 a prophecy only about the sending
of the Apostles, or to be applied only to their sending? Although it can
rightly and deservedly be applied to them in the first place, all others must
not therefore be excluded. “From this,” Pareus says on the passage, “we must
learn how great a treasure is offered in the Gospel, how highly the preaching
of the Gospel must be valued, and finally with how much honor, love, and
gratitude the Heralds of the Gospel are to be embraced by us.” Thus that
conclusion in verse 17 always remains true in every age: “Therefore faith is
from hearing, and hearing through the word of God.” Accordingly, if this
sending is to be restricted to the Apostles, it would follow that faith is not
from every hearing of God’s word, but only from hearing the word through the
preaching of the Apostles. For all these questions have the force of denials,
and the whole progression goes from the denial of causes to the denial of
effects. The progression proceeds thus: How will they call upon him in whom
they have not believed? And how will they believe in him of whom they have not
heard? And so forth. Thus there is no invocation now, no faith, unless the
Apostles have been sent. Is no faith now to be expected among unconverted
Gentiles unless Apostles are sent anew? We too are Gentiles,

[Volume II; printed p. 273; ONB canvas 281]

and many among us do not believe; must Apostles therefore be sent? Does
Velthuysen, with certain others, expect an age of the Holy Spirit? 3. Many who
now live have also been predestined to faith. Therefore, for them to believe,
they must hear the Gospel announced. Therefore there must also be people who
preach. Therefore these people too must be sent. Consequently, the preaching
of those who are sent is the ordinary means by which human beings are brought
to faith. But those sent must be people other than the Apostles. 4. Although
God can bless the announcement of the Gospel made privately or through
letters, this is nevertheless not God’s ordinary or principal way. I readily
concede, however, that it has its use and sometimes—in a time of persecution,
for instance—very great fruit. Nevertheless, public preaching, or preaching in
assemblies, is more ordinary and is to be preferred when it can be obtained.

To the passage in 2 Corinthians 5:20 he replies on page 341 that this is either
something peculiar to the Apostles or common to all Christians. Reply. A third
option is given: namely, something common to all Pastors, as we proved above.
Where, when, or in what respect are all Christians said, or can they be said,
to be God’s ambassadors or to serve as ambassadors in Christ’s name? Does every
private person have the word of reconciliation committed to him? He indeed can
and ought, in his own manner, to instruct and teach his neighbor, but
Velthuysen’s conclusion does not follow from that.

He replies to Acts 6:4, “But we will continue in prayers and the administration
of the word,” which I do not know by whom it was alleged. It is easy to invent
empty or frivolous objections, but anyone who acts this way acts with little
candor. Let us nevertheless hear what he replies. “It is not denied,” he says
on pages 341–342, “that reason and order require that one who is fit to teach
should devote himself to this work and not allow himself to be distracted by
other things which are not so necessary. But how does this prove that there is
any particular power, authority, or character in the pastoral

[Volume II; printed p. 274; ONB canvas 282]

office which has nothing in common with those things found in individual
Christians and does not flow from the members of the Church, who nevertheless
elect the Pastor, give him this character, and, so far as order can permit,
also reserve it for themselves?” Reply. I do not know who adduces this passage
for this purpose, but in any case these things are clear from the text. 1.
Distinct Ecclesiastical offices are given. 2. The office of preaching is
distinct from the office of Deacons. 3. Different offices or functions thus
require different and distinct administrators. 4. Every Minister must
discharge his own office faithfully and give himself wholly to it. 5. The
office of preaching so occupies the whole person that he is not permitted to
devote himself to other matters. 6. These duties cannot be performed by
private members of the Church; otherwise public Ministers would be elected in
vain. But now to his reply. I answer: Let him tell me whether the power,
authority, or character of the Apostles—about whom that text speaks—had anything
in common with the things which belong to individual Christians. Did they
receive their power from members of the Church? Were they elected by the
people? Because Pastors in the present day are elected by members of the
Church, does it therefore follow that Pastors receive all their power,
authority, and character from them? What, I ask, is that power or authority,
and what is that character which the members of the Church reserve for
themselves? What else are these things than the dreams of a diseased brain?

From all these statements of Velthuysen which we have now refuted, it can be
obvious to anyone how highly he values the Ecclesiastical Ministry and that he
can rightly be charged with abolishing the entire ministry of the Gospel.
Indeed, he scarcely acknowledges any ministry instituted by Christ. Whatever
he may say, this is more than sufficiently evident from the points already
refuted. It is true that he says on page 344: “The pastoral office is to be
regarded as an institution of God, just as

[Volume II; printed p. 275; ONB canvas 283]

the office of the Magistrate is.” But I do not see how he can reconcile this
with the preceding points unless he says that he acknowledges this much: for
the sake of order, it is necessary that some suitable person be nominated to
perform those public duties for a certain time, and that he then return to his
former condition; another is then to be nominated to perform the same public
duties, and another after him, for the sake of order and to avoid confusion.
In the same way, in ecclesiastical assemblies, for the sake of order now one
person and now another is chosen to preside, who returns to his former
condition when his allotted time has passed. Thus, to avoid confusion in the
public exercises of Religion—lest everyone, namely, speak, teach, and pray
publicly, and so forth—perhaps he wishes one person to be nominated who would
discharge these duties on this occasion or for some fixed and designated
period. When that time established by the Electors has elapsed, he would
return to his former, that is, private condition. Then another nominated
person would succeed him, or one elected by those to whom this power belongs,
and would be occupied in those exercises for a time. After him another would
be elected, and another by turns. For the whole assembly is now holy, and
therefore every member possesses the immediate potential to perform all these
duties; and when the electors will it and order permits it, he will actually
discharge this office. Unless Velthuysen thinks this, it is impossible for him
to be reconciled with himself. And if he does think this, it is abundantly
clear what sort of ministry he acknowledges. Moreover, the statements which
follow must be observed. In the same place he says: “This office is especially
necessary for those who feel that they are edified more by hearing preaching
than by reading the Bible or a good Author.” What, is this office necessary
only because of them? Was this office instituted by Christ because of such
people, whom Velthuysen judges superstitious? Or can that be regarded as an
office instituted by Christ which is necessary only, or principally, for
superstitious people of that kind? Did

[Volume II; printed p. 276; ONB canvas 284]

Christ will to institute some office because of superstitious people and to
nourish their superstition? Can one who says this rightly say that the
ministry is an institution of God no less than the office of the Magistrate?
Or should he not rather be considered to speak contradictions? Furthermore,
if this office was instituted by Christ, it was instituted for some end and
for the performance of some particular actions. For every public office is
instituted for particular actions; a particular office is not instituted for
actions common to everyone or to be performed by everyone. I therefore ask:
What actions are proper and particular to this function or office? Not the
preaching of the word, not the administration of the Sacraments, not the
exercise of discipline. What then? Did Christ institute the ministerial office
in vain? If everyone were permitted to exercise all the functions of the
Magistracy, to what end would the office of the Magistrate be instituted? We
have already seen that, according to Velthuysen, everyone is permitted to
perform all the things which we judge to belong to the pastoral office. This
is further evident from pages 325–326, where he has the following: “It must be
noted that in Preachers, whom we judge to be instituted only for the sake of
order, there is nothing more than in other Christians who know Christ, have
been knit together in love, and have the full riches of complete assurance of
understanding unto the knowledge of the mystery of God and of the Father and
of Christ, Colossians 2:3. Therefore they have no need to submit themselves to
the discipline of others and are no less holy or participants in the Holy
Spirit. Accordingly, it is not only false but also incomprehensible that there
resides in the office of the preacher any authority, special holiness, more
efficacious operation, or special administration which is not found in other
Christians. Those who set up such a preacher introduce the papacy again
through the back door and impose upon us the yoke under which the Jews were in
the Old Testament and

[Volume II; printed p. 277; ONB canvas 285]

our Fathers under the Papacy.” Hence it is evident that he acknowledges the
ministry to have been instituted in no other way than the Socinians themselves
can and will acknowledge it. For Theophilus Nicolaides speaks thus in chapter
10: “It is therefore apparent that this calling or appointment in no way
belongs to the nature of the teaching office. But if it was formerly employed,
or is now employed, this is done in a well-constituted Church for the sake of
preserving order and decorum.” And nowhere, so far as I know, does Velthuysen
understand a divine order or decorum, that is, one founded upon a divine
command or practice. Moreover: 2. What kind of Ministers or Pastors are those
in whom there is nothing other than what exists in private members of the
Church? Must Christ be said to have instituted Ministers of that sort? 3. What
kind of public pastoral office is it whose holder has no more authority over
private members of the Church than they have over him? 4. No Ecclesiastical
office was instituted by Christ which does not render the person invested with
it specially sanctified and set apart for Christ. 5. Can that be regarded as
an institution of Christ which ordinarily has no greater efficacy to produce
an effect than anything else? Such a thing can be said of a human institution,
but it is impious even to suppose it of a divine institution. 6. Did Christ
institute an office to which no particular action is annexed? Can that be said
to be an office instituted by Christ which has no administration proper or
particular to it? 7. Neither the ceremonial yoke nor the papal yoke consisted
in this: that in the Church there were Ministers distinct from others,
instituted by God and set over the Church, possessing authority granted by God
and exercising it according to God’s prescription. Nor do those who now
acknowledge such Ministers introduce the Papacy or the ceremonial yoke; they
desire Christ’s institutions to be observed in holiness. Finally, from this it

[Volume II; printed p. 278; ONB canvas 286]

is evident what sort of ministry Velthuysen acknowledges and how he
acknowledges it to have been instituted by Christ, so that there is no need to
add more.

I do not deny that Velthuysen sometimes says that election is required in the
person who discharges that office. But 1. nowhere, so far as I know, does he
say that this Election is of divine right, was instituted by Christ, or is
necessary by reason of a divine command or practice. 2. Indeed, in the preface,
page 63, and often elsewhere, he says that it is done only to avoid confusion
and for the sake of order. For by right, namely, everyone without exception
can perform all these things, just as in a private gathering of Christians
everyone who is suitable can pray. But because there would be no order, only
sheer confusion, if everyone prayed at once, they elect or nominate the person
who will pray first. As long as he prays, no one else will be permitted to do
so, otherwise confusion will not be avoided; after him comes another, and so
on. In the same way, according to Velthuysen, anyone can be elected to the
sacred ministry. And because the greatest confusion would result if everyone
discharged it at once, someone must be elected; while he discharges it, another
will not be permitted to undertake that office, since order does not require
it of one who has not been called or nominated. And this is the sole foundation
of Velthuysen’s Election: namely, an order which is merely human and a human
institution for avoiding confusion. 3. Beyond this election Velthuysen
requires nothing else. He holds that there is no sending, no solemn setting
apart,
consecration, or appointment of persons to perform this office. Indeed, on page
30 of his preface he expressly says “that every such thing is nothing other
than the invention of certain canons from Christian prudence,” and that now
under the New Testament there is no sending or consecration to an
Ecclesiastical office. Indeed, he holds that there is no spiritual power or
authority in the Church, nor any spiritual, sanctified,

[Volume II; printed p. 279; ONB canvas 287]

set-apart, or divinely instituted person who, by reason of that institution
and sanctification, is qualified to administer some part of divine worship
which others are not permitted to undertake. On the contrary, he holds that
the whole congregation is holy and that each person, insofar as the
aforementioned order and personal fitness permit, is allowed to administer
every part of divine worship; and that each Church possesses the power to
commit this administration to whomever it wishes. Who that says these things
and wishes to speak consistently can say that the Ecclesiastical ministry is
an institution of Christ? He holds that everything is civil and purely
political, for he acknowledges nothing Ecclesiastical or spiritual distinct
from the political. The reasoning which Velthuysen employs to prove this must
also be noted. Because we do not acknowledge papal consecration to be
necessary, therefore, he says, there is no sending of persons or solemn
setting apart for this office according to Christ’s institution. Because we do
not acknowledge that this right of sending and consecrating Pastors belongs
solely to the Roman Church, he therefore thinks it must be said that no Church
has the right to send and ministerially to give power for discharging the
Ecclesiastical office, or that there is absolutely no authoritative sending,
appointment, setting apart, or ordination by divine right. Rather, if there is
anything of that sort, it is all a human invention for observing order and
avoiding confusion. 4. In what respect, I ask, does he differ from the
Socinians? Smalcius, *Against Frantzius on Ecclesiastical Order,* disputation
4, says: “Nor are we so dull and obtuse that we administer anything in our
Churches without legitimate order. Indeed, whatever truly belongs to order
and decorum is observed much more properly and purely by our people than by
the Lutherans.” And Socinus, Letter 2 to Radecke: “Furthermore, to elect some
one person in each assembly to explain God’s word to the others and from it to
exhort them to true piety and holiness of life

[Volume II; printed p. 280; ONB canvas 288 (upper portion)]

is Apostolic; and, as it seems to me, no reason can be brought forward why it
ought not to be done today.” Theophilus Nicolaides, chapter 10, page 106: “For
Socinus is not speaking of what belongs to the very nature of a Minister and
must necessarily be done, but of what pertains to preserving a certain
honorable order and can merely be done, tacitly assuming in the meantime that
the person approved by others is worthy of that office.” This is more than
Velthuysen concedes. Let Andreas Radecke also be heard in *Notes Refuting the
Gordian Knot,* chapter 4, folio 8, as cited by Peltius: “We concede that no one
can discharge any office without the approval of others. But by this reasoning
a sending is destroyed”—pay attention, Velthuysen—“for just as in a free
Commonwealth it is permissible to elect a person fit to discharge an office,
so in Christ’s Church, which is most free, it will also be permissible to elect
a person fit to teach others to that office and thus set him over themselves.”
We could add more, but these few points can make clear to anyone not blinded by
prejudice how well Velthuysen and the Socinians agree in this matter, whatever
he may say to deceive the unwary and mislead all those who do not investigate
everything precisely enough or weigh it seriously. Enough concerning this
Assertion. The following comes next:

ASSERTION XV.

In discharging this office, and especially in announcing the Gospel, all
Pastors are bound to conduct themselves faithfully according to the canon of
God’s Word and to acknowledge no other first, primary, and principal rule,
norm, or standard.

Perhaps the Reader will think it superfluous to propose this Assertion, since
no one can be so destitute of understanding as to deny it. But let him recall
what we cited above from pages 33, 34, and 35 of Velthuysen’s preface, and he
will see that it is worthwhile to confirm this

[Volume II; printed p. 281; ONB canvas 289]

Assertion also. I will repeat his position here. “Again,” he says, “the members
of that congregation are free to draw up the confession of faith upon which
they unanimously agree, and to choose for themselves Teachers who will regulate
the congregation in doctrine according to that confession and, in addition,
administer the sacraments. It is wholly contrary to the nature and origin of
the office of a Teacher that anyone elected and appointed in the Church should
have the power to teach anything contrary to that confession, as if he had
from God the power, authority, and commission, as a pastor and in that
condition or capacity”—which no one receives except through the mediation of
human beings, who also limit it to a particular confession—“to admonish the
congregation to embrace every doctrine and position which he judges in his
conscience to agree with God’s word. That indeed is the duty of every Christian
insofar as he is considered a free person, but not of a Teacher as such in a
Church which has a confession. Where, however, a Church has no confession, the
Teacher is free from that obligation. Moreover, he has no other power or
authority or other kind of commission than those others who are elected by the
congregation for the sake of order.” You now see the position Velthuysen
cherishes in his bosom; indeed, how freely and boldly enough he expresses and
declares what he thinks: namely, that Pastors are to be bound to the confession
which the Church composes, whether it is pure or impure, orthodox or heterodox,
godly or ungodly. For he makes no distinction, proposes no exception, and adds
absolutely no explanation or restriction. And the reason which he adds permits
us to add or suppose no limitation, cosmetic covering, or favorable
construction. For the person has been Elected as pastor or teacher by the
Church, from which he derives all power and authority, and he is bound to this
confession. I do not deny that Churches may—and indeed I judge it useful that
they should—draw up confessions of their faith.

[Volume II; printed p. 282; ONB canvas 290]

I do not deny that Pastors may subscribe to them, provided and insofar as they
agree with God’s word. I do not say that either pastors or others may teach
anything, whether publicly or privately, which conflicts with this confession
when it is well founded upon God’s word. Yet I do say that it is utterly false
to claim that no elected pastor may contradict any confession set before him
by the Church in which he was elected, or that this confession is his ten-foot
measuring rod and sole rule according to which he must form all his doctrines,
reproofs, admonitions, instructions, and so forth. For although we suppose that
confession to be most pure, it is nevertheless at most nothing other than a
regulated rule. With these things premised, I bring forward the following
arguments to confirm my proposed Assertion and at the same time refute
Velthuysen’s statements.

1. This conflicts with the origin of that pastoral office which—to turn back
upon him the weapon he had launched—is imposed upon pastors. For the office and
function which they discharge in the Church was instituted by Christ alone,
and they derive it not from human beings but from Christ alone, Ephesians
4:11–12; 1 Corinthians 12:28–29; Acts 20:28. They are indeed elected by the
Church and ordained by the Presbytery. Thus through their mediation that office
or Ecclesiastical function is applied to this or that person; nevertheless,
the office itself is derived from Christ alone and is conferred upon the
Rulers of the Church by Christ alone. Therefore, if they have the office itself
from Christ alone, they also have all power, authority, and commission from
him. Velthuysen will not deny the inference. The conclusion, therefore, is
that pastors do not depend upon the Church and do not have their instructions
from the Church but from Christ. They must therefore comply with him, execute
what he enjoins, regard his word as the sole rule of their administrations,
and acknowledge no other except in subordination to it.

[Volume II; printed p. 283; ONB canvas 291]

2. If what Velthuysen says were true, in a case where the Church is constituted
and has published a confession pastors would be mere bondmen and servants of
the Church, not of God or Christ. They would have all their instructions from
human beings—namely, from the Church—not from Christ. They would preach what
the Church ordered, not what Christ commanded. All doctrines, exhortations,
reproofs, and so forth would be supported and confirmed by the authority of
that confession, not by the authority of the Word. They could not then use
this preface: “Thus says the Lord,” but “Thus says the confession; thus says
the Church; thus you say.” Who does not see how absurd and abominable these
things are?

3. This conflicts with the nature of this office. For (1.) those who discharge
this office are called *Pastors*. But Pastors as such are not directed by the
sheep, nor do they have the duties of their office prescribed by the flock,
but by the Lord of the flock. (2.) They are *Overseers*, who are not ruled by
the laws of those whom they compel to duty and over whom they preside, but by
the law of him who set them over others and made them overseers. (3.) They are
*presidents* and *governors*; therefore, with respect to their instructions,
they do not principally depend upon those over whom they are set. (4.) They are
*Stewards*; therefore they do not have their laws from the household but from
the Lord. (5.) They are *Ambassadors*; but who says that an ambassador has his
commission from those among whom he discharges his embassy and not from his
Lord, whose ambassador he is? (6.) They are *Heralds*, and the person who said
that such men ought to proclaim nothing except what the subjects prescribe,
and not the decrees of the prince, would be a blockhead. (7.) They are *Angels*
and messengers. What then? Ought they to announce nothing except what those to
whom they are sent desire? Ought they not rather to expound the will of the one
who sends them?

4. By this reasoning every relation which pastors as such have to God and
Christ would be abolished. For (1.) they would no longer be called *Men of
God*, but *Men of the Church*. (2.) Nor would they be *Messengers of God*,
contrary to Malachi 2:9,

[Volume II; printed p. 284; ONB canvas 292]

because they would be permitted to announce nothing but what the Church wills.
(3.) They would not be *Ministers of God*, contrary to 2 Corinthians 6:4; Acts
16:16; Titus 1:1; 2 Timothy 2:24; 1 Thessalonians 3:2, nor *Ministers of
Christ*, contrary to 2 Corinthians 2:23; Romans 15:16 and 1:1; Philippians 1:1;
1 Corinthians 4:1; Colossians 1:7; 1 Timothy 4:6, but ministers *of the Church*.
(4.) They would not be laborers hired by God to work in the vineyard. (5.)
They would not be God’s Fellow Workers, contrary to 1 Corinthians 3:9. (6.)
They would not be *Ambassadors of God*, nor discharge an embassy in Christ’s
name, contrary to 2 Corinthians 5:20. (7.) They would not be Ministers in the
Lord, contrary to Ephesians 6:21 and Colossians 4:7.

5. Ecclesiastical pastors have a far different subject matter prescribed for
them. They were appointed heralds for the sake of the Gospel, 2 Timothy 1:11.
They are heralds of redemption through Christ, 1 Timothy 2:7. The word of
reconciliation has been placed in them, and they have the ministry of
reconciliation, 2 Corinthians 5:18–19. They are stewards of the mysteries of
God, 1 Corinthians 4:1. They are Ministers of the New Covenant, 2 Corinthians
3:6; Ministers of righteousness, 2 Corinthians 11:15; Ministers of the Gospel,
Ephesians 3:7; Colossians 1:23. Hence Paul calls the Gospel “his Gospel,”
Romans 2:16 and 16:25; see also 2 Corinthians 4:3; 1 Thessalonians 1:5, because
this alone was incumbent upon him and he cared for this alone: namely, that he
might announce the Gospel. He was set apart for this, Romans 1:1; in the Gospel
he worshiped God, Romans 1:9; he labored in God’s Gospel, Romans 15:16. The
stewardship of the Gospel was entrusted to them, 1 Corinthians 9:16–17. Why
should I mention more passages? It would be an endless task to cite all those
which show that the subject matter of the Ministers’ commission is far
different from any confession drawn up by human beings. See 1 Corinthians
15:1; Luke 8:11; Titus 1:3; Ephesians 3:8–9.

6. This conflicts with the relation pastors have to the flock. For they are
*leaders* and *rulers* of the flock who speak God’s word, Hebrews 13:7;
therefore they must not be taught by the people. They are *Watchmen*, Ezekiel 3
and 33, so that when the signal is given they may warn the people of danger,

[Volume II; printed p. 285; ONB canvas 293]

whether in doing so they contradict the confession or not. They are *Pastors*
who must take heed to the whole flock, Acts 20:18, and keep watch, verse 29,
opposing grievous wolves who do not spare the flock, whether the confession
permits it or not. They are the *light of the world*; therefore they do not have
their light from the world but from elsewhere. They must spread the light of
true doctrine and by it dispel the world’s darkness, whether the confession
supplies the material or not.

7. This conflicts with the end of the ministry: both the principal end, namely,
God’s glory, and the less principal or intermediate end, namely, the eternal
salvation of human beings. For it cannot but detract from God’s glory to prefer
a rule fashioned by human beings to the Rule proposed by God himself and to
make the handmaid the mistress. It likewise conflicts with the salvation of
human beings, which Pastors must aim at above all. For they are sent for this
end: to open eyes, to turn people from darkness to light and from the power of
Satan to God, Acts 26:17–18. They were given for the building up of Christ’s
body, Ephesians 4:11–13. But this salvation and saving doctrine are drawn from
God’s word alone; therefore from this word of God they must seek both the
matter and the rule of all their sermons. They must preach the Word faithfully
so that in this way they provide for the salvation of the people, even though
they contradict the confession.

8. This conflicts with the practice of the earliest Pastors, who, making
little of the judgments or opinions of the people among whom they discharged
their office, faithfully announced God’s Word and the word of the Gospel, Acts
13:46, 48; 1 Thessalonians 2:3, 4, 9; 2 Thessalonians 3:1; James 1:21; Acts
13:26, 44 and 4:31; 2 Corinthians 2:17 and 4:2.

9. This conflicts with that faithful administration which is required in all
Pastors, the rule of which faithful administration is not and cannot be any
confession drawn up by human beings. In 1 Corinthians 4:2, “it is required in
stewards that a person be found faithful.” But he must be faithful

[Volume II; printed p. 286; ONB canvas 294]

in dispensing doctrine prescribed by the people. They must not peddle God’s
word, but speak concerning Christ as from sincerity, as from God, in God’s
sight, 2 Corinthians 2:17. They must not falsify God’s word, but by a
declaration of the truth commend themselves to every human conscience in God’s
sight, 2 Corinthians 4:2. They must not preach themselves but Christ Jesus,
verse 5. In all things they must approve themselves as God’s Ministers, 2
Corinthians 6:4, through the word of truth, verse 7. The servant whom the Lord
set over his household must be faithful, Matthew 24:45 and 25:21; Luke 12:42.
Timothy was faithful in the Lord, 1 Corinthians 4:17, and Onesimus, Colossians
4:9. Through mercy the Lord enabled Paul to be faithful, 1 Corinthians 7:25; 1
Timothy 1:12. Tychicus was likewise faithful, Ephesians 6:21; Colossians 4:7,
as was Epaphras, Colossians 1:7. Those to whom the Gospel is entrusted must be
faithful, 2 Timothy 2:2. How then can those servants of God be faithful who
observe not God’s commands but those of human beings, and who conform
themselves not to God’s word but to the word of the flock? See also Titus
2:7–8; 1 Thessalonians 2:3–6; Jeremiah 23:28.

10. This conflicts with that outstanding saying, never to be consigned to
oblivion, in Acts 5:29: “We must obey God rather than human beings,” and 4:19;
and with that saying in Galatians 1:10: “If I still pleased human beings, I
would not be Christ’s servant.” For the person who does not faithfully announce
God’s word because perhaps the confession says something else—truly, he
pronounces himself to be the servant of human beings rather than of God and
openly declares it better to obey human beings than God. See 1 Thessalonians
2:4, 6.

11. This conflicts with what we have in Acts 20:17: “For I did not shrink from
announcing to you the whole counsel of God.” Christ’s servants must fulfill
God’s word, Colossians 1:25, and the ministry which they received from God,
Colossians 4:17. They must keep the faith, 2 Timothy 4:7. What if the confession
is imperfect, erroneous, or heterodox?

[Volume II; printed p. 287; ONB canvas 295]

The person who refuses to contradict it will not be faithful and will not
announce the whole counsel. See also 2 Timothy 4:5.

12. This conflicts with the perfection of Holy Scripture, for it will not be
regarded as a perfect rule if, setting it aside, we consult another norm. But
if even a false and erroneous Confession may not be contradicted, Scripture
will not be the supreme and exact standard for Pastors; that confession will
be. Yet this is openly false, as is clear from Psalm 19 and 2 Timothy 3:16–17:
“All Scripture is divinely inspired and useful for doctrine, for reproof, for
correction, and for discipline in righteousness, so that the man of God may be
perfect, perfectly furnished for every good work.” This, therefore, is the
sole rule of Pastors. It perfectly supplies the matter of doctrine, reproof,
correction, and discipline. The person who observes this rule exactly will be
perfect and perfectly furnished for the work of the ministry. But the minister
who sets it aside and discharges his office according to another rule errs and
is imperfect.

13. Paul indeed commands that Timothy, Titus, and other Pastors not attend to
fables and never-ending genealogies, which provide questions rather than God’s
edification, 1 Timothy 1:4; that they turn away from profane and old wives’
fables, 1 Timothy 4:7; that they withdraw from those who are diseased concerning
questions and disputes about words, 1 Timothy 6:4–5; that they turn away from
profane babblings about empty things and from contradictions, verse 20 and 2
Timothy 2:16; also from foolish and uninstructed questions which generate
disputes, 2 Timothy 2:23; that they not attend to Jewish fables and commands of
human beings who turn away from the truth, Titus 1:14; and that they restrain
foolish questions and genealogies, contentions and legal disputes, which are
unprofitable and vain, Titus 3:9. But he never commands them not to contradict
a human confession by proposing the truth truly, candidly, and faithfully.

[Volume II; printed p. 288; ONB canvas 296]

14. Those who preach the true faith and set true doctrine before the brothers,
although some depart from the faith and attend to deceiving spirits and
doctrines of demons, as Paul says in 1 Timothy 4:1, 6, will be good Ministers of

15. Velthuysen’s statements conflict with the Apostle’s words in 1 Timothy

16. Who, recalling those most solemn adjurations of Paul in his Epistles to

17. This also conflicts with those precepts of Paul in 2 Timothy 1:13: “Hold

[Volume II; printed p. 289; ONB canvas 297]

18. Must the Pastor give an account to God? Hebrews 13:17; 2 Corinthians

ASSERTIO XVI.

Ecclesia est Coetus seu Societas à Republicâ prorsus, suâ naturâ, distincta.

Quia Adversarii ægrè hoc fateri volunt, probare conabimur: & eò magis, quòd
hoc substrato fundamento, faciliori negotio confirmabuntur quæ sequuntur.
Assertio nostra confirmatur,

1. Quòd dicat Christus *Joan.* XVIII. 36. Regnum suum non esse ex hoc mundo,
*Respondit Jesus, Regnum meum non est ex hoc mundo, si ex hoc mundo esset
Regnum meum, Ministri mei certassent ne traderer Judæis: nunc autem Regnum
meum non est hinc*: ubi duo apertè asseruntur à Christo, 1. Eum habere Regnum,
*Regnum meum*, inquit bis. 2. Regnum hoc, quod suum vocat, distinctum esse, ac
diversum ab iis Regnis, quæ sunt ex hoc mundo: & hinc ergo sequitur
*Ecclesiam*, quæ est Christi Regnum, longè differre à *Republicâ*: *Respublica*
enim est ex hoc mundo, *Regnum* Christi non item. Si autem *Ecclesia* quâ
talis, esset pars *Reipublicæ*, aut à *Republicâ* non distincta, quo sensu
potuerit Christus dicere Regnum suum non esse de hoc mundo?

existere; erunt realiter distinctæ Societates. At verum prius. Ergo &c.
Consequentia Majoris negari non potest: nam nullum majus fingi potest argumentum
ad probandam realem aliquam distinctionem, quàm est mutua separatio. Si ergo
separari possit *Ecclesia* à *Republicâ*, & ab eâ separata existere, seu per se;
distincta erit à *Republicâ* Societas. Quod autem Minor vera sit, hinc liquet,
Quia

primitiva *Ecclesia*, quâ talis, non faciebat partem *Reipublicæ*; à

realiter distinguitur *Respublica* ab *Ecclesiâ*. Et si *Respublica* suâ naturâ
ab Ecclesiâ distinguatur, ab illâ rursus realiter distinguetur *Ecclesia*.

4. Hæ Societates prorsus distinctis reguntur legibus: *Ecclesia* enim regitur
legibus Christi, & canonibus Ecclesiasticis, à Rectoribus Ecclesiasticis, juxta
regulas generales à Christo præscriptas, conditis. *Respublica* autem civilis,
legibus politicis, & municipalibus, à Magistratu politico latis, gubernatur.
Quæ distinctis gubernantur legibus Respublicæ, pro distinctis habentur. Ergo
distinctæ erunt Societates *Ecclesia* & *Respublica* civilis.

5. Materia proxima *Reipublicæ* civilis, sunt homines ut sic considerati, seu
quatenus sunt animalia sociabilia. Materia autem proxima *Ecclesiæ*, sunt
homines Christiani, qui & quatenus veram profitentur Religionem, seu fidem in
Christum. Quæ autem ex distinctâ constant materiâ proximâ Societates, distinctæ
sunt. Ergo distincta est *Ecclesia* à *Republicâ*. Civis politicus est homo
politicus legibus Reipublicæ obstrictus, & ejusdem civitatis immunitatibus &
beneficiis

[Volume II; printed p. 292; ONB canvas 300]

gaudens, virtutemque civilem seu politicam exercens. Civis autem Ecclesiasticus
est homo Ecclesiasticus, legibus Christi & Ecclesiæ obstrictus, & ejusdem
immunitatibus & beneficiis gaudens, virtutemque Ecclesiasticam exercens. Longè
ergo differunt hæ duæ civitates.

6. Causa principalis remota *Reipublicæ* civilis, est Deus naturæ author. Causa
autem principalis remota *Ecclesiæ*, est Deus gratiæ author, seu in Christo
propitius.

7. Causa principalis proxima *Reipublicæ* civilis, est affectus naturalis, quo
præditus est homo, natus ad societatem cum aliis colendam, causa autem
principalis proxima *Ecclesiæ*, est affectus supernaturalis, quo præditus est
homo Christianus. Distinctam ergo agnoscunt hæ Societates originem. Distinctæ
ergo sunt.

8. Causa minus principalis *Reipublicæ* civilis, est indigentia eorum quæ ad
vitam hanc nostram naturalem & civilem spectant. Hinc *Cicero de Republicâ*
apud *Augustinum de civitate Dei* lib. 2. cap. 21. populum vocat *cœtum
multitudinis juris consensu & utilitatis communione sociatum*. Causa autem
minus principalis *Reipublicæ Ecclesiasticæ*, est necessitas & indigentia eorum
quæ ad bonum animæ spectant, & sine quibus vita spiritualis non potest benè
conservari & foveri.

9. Ratio naturalis præcipuum est *Rerumpublicarum politicarum* fundamentum;
nullum enim extat positivum Dei mandatum talem aut talem Rerumpublicarum
erectionem, aut constitutionem injungens. Ratio autem naturalis *Ecclesiæ*
fundamentum sternere non potest; ea enim, quibus præcipuè innititur Societas
Ecclesiastica, prorsus ignorat ratio. Distinctæ ergo & longè diversæ sunt hæ
Societates.

10. Si *Ecclesia* non sit jure divino distincta Societas à *Republicâ*, tum
invito aut non consentiente Magistratu

[Volume II; printed p. 293; ONB canvas 301]

politico, non licet Ecclesiam instituere, aut communionem Ecclesiasticam
colere. At hoc absurdum. Ergo &c. Majoris consequentia hinc patet. Quod quæ
politica sunt, & ad Rempublicam quâ talem spectant, ad judicium &
determinationem Magistratus politici propriè pertinent, nec debet ullum
collegium purè politicum, sine ejus approbatione & authoritate institui. Quod
ad Minorem attinet, Ejus veritas hinc patet, Quòd scil. Christus & Apostoli non
modò non consentientibus, sed & invitis Magistratibus politicis qui tum
Imperium tenebant, Ecclesiam instituerunt. Ergo non erat illicitum. Ergo
Ecclesia erat distincta Societas à Republicâ.

11. Si *Ecclesia* non sit Societas realiter distincta à *Republicâ*, tum
abolenda erit prorsus Ecclesia, quandocunque Magistratibus politicis visum
fuerit, nec contranitendum erit vel patiendo; nam si sit collegium merè
politicum, Magistratus potest id abolere, quando commodum Reipublicæ id judicat,
quemadmodum potest collegia alia Mercatorum, Fabrorum & ejusmodi prohibere, nec
tenentur ii mercatores aut fabri in conscientiâ renuere, & propterea vel
mulctam aliquam pati. Et hoc forsan est quod volunt ac optant adversarii, ut
scil. aboleretur prorsus Ecclesia ac abigeretur; odio enim Vatiniano eam
prosequi videntur, quam autem ob causam, ipsi optimè norunt. Verùm an dicent
cedendum esse Magistratibus, quando omnem destruere conantur Ecclesiam, & omnem
Christianorum conventum, & quando ipsam Nominis Christi professionem
prohibent? Si sic, damnandi erunt primitivi Christiani, qui propter hanc
causam, mille periculis sese exposuerunt, & tandem martyrio coronati sunt.
Mirerisne quid illis in mentem venerit, quòd mortem, imo mortem acerbissimam
maluerint subire, quam Magistratibus in re purè politicâ (si modo talis fuerit)
cedere. Quid? an dicemus eos

[Volume II; printed p. 294; ONB canvas 302]

omnes in rebellione nefariâ mortem obiisse? Dicent forte adversarii, obstrictos
fuisse Christianos, lege Christi expressâ & positivâ, ad professionem istam
continuè celebrandam, & ad alia quam plurima, quæ præstare non potuerant, nisi
constans coleretur christiana communio, & mutua Societas. *Resp.* Hoc nobis
satisfaciet: Quia hinc satis patebit, Societatem Ecclesiasticam non niti jure
humano, sed divino; & in expresso Dei mandato fundari: nam qui finem instituit,
instituit & media, sine quibus finis ille acquiri non possit. Quando ergo
Christus tales fines instituerit, quos assequi non possunt Christiani, nisi
mediante Societate aut cœtu Ecclesiastico, Hunc etiam, procul dubio, cœtum
instituit Christus. Ergo cœtus hic non est politicus, sed à politico distinctus,
nam per cœtum politicum quâ talem, non potuerunt Christiani hos fines
præscriptos assequi; & id satis noverat Christus, quando novum instituit Regnum
à Regnis hujus mundi distinctum.

12. Distinctio harum Societatum evincitur ex diverso earum fine. Negari non
potest, finem *Reipublicæ* proprium, esse rerum humanarum ac politicarum
conservationem, ac procurationem, communem scil. utilitatem, auxilium mutuum,
vitæ commoditatem: *confluxerunt enim homines* (ut loquitur *Hoenonius* disp.
1. pol.) *ut operas conjungentes suas, ea facilius pararent, quorum vita
mortalis indiget*. Finis autem Ecclesiæ, est bonum Christianorum quâ talium,
seu bonum spirituale, quod animas spectat, & vitam æternam. Quis ergo negabit,
has Societates, quarum proprii fines adeo discordes & tam longè à se invicem
differentes sunt, esse etiam distinctissimas? Frustra objiciunt, teneri
unumquodque Reipublicæ membrum finem spiritualem in omnibus intendere. Quia
tales intendit fines spirituales, non quatenus civis, sed quatenus civis
Christianus. Christianitas qualificat,

[Volume II; printed p. 295; ONB canvas 303]

regulat, animum civis disponit, & ad omnia piè ac more Christiano agenda, ducit
ac dirigit; aliud tamen longè in hoc intendit, quàm bonum Reipub. externum,
licet & hoc in subordinatione ad majus bonum intendat, bonum scil. æternum ac
spirituale & internum. Quare nisi probaverint adversarii, Christianitatem nihil
aliud, præter bonum Reipublicæ temporale & externum, intendere, nihil evincent.
Hoc autem probatum iri non expectamus.

13. In *Republicâ civili* multi privilegiorum capaces non sunt, quales sunt
Servi nondum manumissi, & alii. In *Ecclesiâ* autem nulla est distinctio Servos
inter & Dominos, aut liberos *Col.* III. 11. *ubi non est Græcus & Judæus,
circumcisio & præputium, Barbarus & Scytha, servus & liber, sed omnia & in
omnibus est Christus.* Vide etiam 1 *Cor.* VII. 21, 22.

14. Hinc aliud elicimus argumentum. Quod sc. distincta sint harum Societatum
privilegia ac jura, quibus potiuntur singula membra. Etenim qui membrum est
Reipublicæ politicæ, & jus habet *in re*, seu propinquum, ad privilegia ista
omnia, eo ipso tamen non fit membrum *Ecclesiæ*, non habet jus, nec *in re* nec
*ad rem*, ad ea privilegia Ecclesiæ propria, alioquin dicendum Atheum, perfidum,
apertè profanum, ac scandalosum, posse *actu* & *de jure* participem fieri sacræ
cœnæ, imo etiamsi non sit Christianus vel nomine tenus, aut baptizatus. Frustra
dicet adversarius talem parvi facere illud privilegium, & nunquam petere velle.
Nim non est improbabile vel profligatissimum, modò nomine tenus Christianum,
hoc petere velle privilegium, & quidni & atheus non baptizatus, ut eo magis
omnia Dei instituta conculcaret & contemptui exponeret, hoc petere possit? Et
posito *possibili in esse*, nullum sequitur impossibile. Dein etsi actualem
privilegii fruitionem non peteret, haberet tamen jus & *ad rem* & *in re*. Et
si peteret, nullo jure, posset

[Volume II; printed p. 296; ONB canvas 304]

hoc jus ei denegari. Quo quid absurdius fingi potest? Et vice versâ omnia
membra Ecclesiæ, essent eo ipso membra *Reipublicæ*. Quod itidem absurdum.

15. Societates illæ quæ longè distinctos habent officiarios, oportet sint etiam
distinctæ. At tales sunt *Respublica* & *Ecclesia*. Distinctos habent
officiarios hæ Societates, nec est Minister Ecclesiasticus, quâ talis,
officiarius politicus: nec est Magistratus quâ talis aut eo ipso, minister
Ecclesiæ aut officiarius Ecclesiasticus. Si Ecclesia non esset à Republicâ
distincta, quorsum institueret in eâ Christus novos ac distinctos ministros?

16. Omnes Ministri Ecclesiastici sunt juris divini, & speciatim à Christo
instituti, nec licet alios de novo creare. Officiarii autem politici ab hominum
pendent arbitrio, & potest eorum numerus vel augeri vel minui, prout hominibus
visum fuerit. Distinctæ sunt ergo illæ Societates, non una & eadem.

17. Non licet Ministris Ecclesiasticis res politicas attingere, id enim apertè
prohibuit Christus, qui & ipse noluit Magistratus politici munere fungi, *quis
inquiens me constituit judicem aut partitorem Luc.* XII. 14. necesse est ergo
ut Ecclesia, cujus illi sunt Ministri constituti, sit à *Republicâ* distincta.

18. Affirmant Adversarii *Ecclesiæ* non competere potestatem externam
coactivam. Hæc autem benè competit *Reipublicæ politicæ*. Quomodo potest ergo
*Ecclesia* eadem esse cum *Republicâ*. Societas?

19. Habent singulæ suas distinctas offensas, nonnunquam materialiter, semper
formaliter diversas, peccatur in *Rempublicam*, aliter quam in *Ecclesiam*,
peccata in *Ecclesiam* commissa sunt scandala, quâ talia.

20. *Respublica* est pars Regni Christi, quatenus Deus est Patri co-æqualis;
*Ecclesia* autem ad Regnum ejus spectat Mediatorium. Vide Assertionem

[Volume II; printed p. 297; ONB canvas 305]

secundam, ubi fusius hoc confirmatur. Sunt ergo distinctæ hæ Societates.

21. Epitheta *Ecclesiæ*, *Reipublicæ* non sunt attribuenda. Sunt ergo diversæ
Societates ac distinctæ. *Ecclesia* dicitur *Sponsa* Christi; *Respublica* quâ
talis hoc non gaudet titulo. *Ecclesia* dicitur *corpus* Christi; *Respublica*
non item. *Ecclesia* est *domus Dei* Viventis; *Respublica* quâ talis hoc non
ornatur titulo. *Ecclesia* super Petram ædificata est, & portæ inferorum eam
non superabunt; Hæc autem non conveniunt Reipublicæ vide *Mat.* XVI. 18.
*Ecclesia* est columna & stabilimentum veritatis 1 *Tim.* III. 15. de
*Republicâ* hoc dictum non legimus. *Ecclesia* candelabrum nuncupatur *Apoc.*
I. 20. ubi quæso ita nominatur *Respublica*?

22. In Ecclesiis non licet fæminis publicè docere, aut iis præesse 1 *Cor.*
XIV. 34. 1 *Tim.* II. 11, 12. præsunt tamen nonnunquam Reipublicæ.

Plura adduci possunt argumenta hanc distinctionem confirmantia, sed hæc
sufficere possunt. Facile etiam esset, hanc distinctionem vel à primordiis
mundi repetere. Nam *Gen.* VI. 2. de *filiis Dei* à *filiis hominum* distinctis
legimus. Hi autem filii Dei, membra erant Ecclesiæ. Vide *Pareum* in locum. Et
quando Ecclesia in familiis *Noachi, Abrahami*, & aliorum Patriarcharum erat,
licet ibi fuerit coalitio materiæ Ecclesiæ & Reipublicæ, manebant tamen hæ
Societates formaliter distinctæ; nam sacrificia, circumcisio cæteræque divini
cultus partes, ab iis familiis, quatenus familiæ erant, non peragebantur, sed
quatenus Ecclesiæ.

Verùm postquam Dei populus ex Ægypto esset eductus, licet iidem tum
*Rempublicam*, tum *Ecclesiam* constituerint, luculenter apparuit inter has
Societates formaliter consideratas distinctio. Quia autem adversarii hoc
negant, breviter sic confirmamus.

*voluit observari.* Distinguebatur ergo *Respublica Judaica* ab *Ecclesiâ

ASSERTION XVI.

The Church is an Assembly or Society wholly distinct by its own nature from
the Commonwealth.

Because the Adversaries are reluctant to admit this, we will endeavor to prove
it, and all the more because, once this foundation is laid, the things which
follow will be confirmed with less difficulty. Our Assertion is confirmed:

1. Because Christ says in John 18:36 that his Kingdom is not of this world:
“Jesus answered, My Kingdom is not of this world. If my Kingdom were of this
world, my Ministers would have fought that I might not be delivered to the
Jews; but now my Kingdom is not from here.” Here two things are openly asserted
by Christ: 1. That he has a Kingdom—“my Kingdom,” he says twice. 2. That this
Kingdom, which he calls his own, is distinct and different from those kingdoms
which are of this world. It therefore follows from this that the Church, which
is Christ’s Kingdom, differs greatly from the Commonwealth. For the
Commonwealth is of this world; Christ’s Kingdom is not. But if the Church as
such were a part of the Commonwealth, or were not distinct from the
Commonwealth, in what sense could Christ have said that his Kingdom is not of
this world?

2. If the Church and Commonwealth can be separated from each other and exist
separately, they will be really distinct Societies. But the former is true.
Therefore, and so forth. The consequence of the Major cannot be denied, for no
greater argument can be devised to prove a real distinction between things
than their mutual separation. Therefore, if the Church can be separated from
the Commonwealth and exist apart from it, or by itself, it will be a Society
distinct from the Commonwealth. That the Minor is true is clear from this:
because

[Volume II; printed p. 291; ONB canvas 299]

the primitive Church, as such, did not form part of the Commonwealth. It was
harassed by the Commonwealth and by the Commonwealth’s Rulers. They persecuted
it, attempted to destroy it, did not protect it with their laws, and had no
care for it. Nevertheless, it was the Church and actually subsisted. Scarcely
any Nation is so savage, barbarous, and uncultivated that it does not have some
form of political Commonwealth; yet there are many Nations in which no Church
has yet been instituted. And when a Christian Church is instituted among them,
the nature of the Commonwealth is not changed, nor does that Church, formally
and as such, become part of the Commonwealth. Rather, it remains by its own
nature distinct from the Commonwealth.

3. It is certain that the Commonwealth as such is not the Church; otherwise
every Commonwealth would also be a Church, which is openly false. Therefore,
the Commonwealth is distinguished by its own nature and in reality from the
Church. And if the Commonwealth is distinguished by its own nature from the
Church, the Church in turn will be really distinguished from it.

4. These Societies are governed by wholly distinct laws. For the Church is
governed by Christ’s laws and by Ecclesiastical canons enacted by
Ecclesiastical Rulers according to the general rules prescribed by Christ. But
the civil Commonwealth is governed by political and municipal laws enacted by
the political Magistrate. Commonwealths governed by distinct laws are
regarded as distinct. Therefore, the Church and civil Commonwealth will be
distinct Societies.

5. The proximate matter of the civil Commonwealth is human beings considered
simply as such, or insofar as they are social animals. But the proximate matter
of the Church is Christian human beings who profess the true Religion, or
faith in Christ, precisely insofar as they do so. Societies which consist of
distinct proximate matter are distinct. Therefore, the Church is distinct from
the Commonwealth. A political citizen is a political person bound by the laws
of the Commonwealth, enjoying the immunities and benefits of that city, and
exercising civil or political virtue. But an Ecclesiastical citizen is an

[Volume II; printed p. 292; ONB canvas 300]

Ecclesiastical person bound by the laws of Christ and the Church, enjoying its
immunities and benefits, and exercising Ecclesiastical virtue. These two cities
therefore differ greatly.

6. The remote principal cause of the civil Commonwealth is God, the author of
nature. But the remote principal cause of the Church is God, the author of
grace, or God propitious in Christ.

7. The proximate principal cause of the civil Commonwealth is the natural
inclination with which a human being is endowed, being born to cultivate
society with others. But the proximate principal cause of the Church is the
supernatural inclination with which the Christian is endowed. These Societies
therefore acknowledge a distinct origin. Therefore, they are distinct.

8. The less principal cause of the civil Commonwealth is the need for those
things which pertain to this natural and civil life of ours. Hence Cicero in
*The Commonwealth*, as quoted by Augustine in *The City of God*, book 2,
chapter 21, calls a people “an assembly of a multitude united by agreement in
law and community of advantage.” But the less principal cause of the
Ecclesiastical Commonwealth is the necessity and need of those things which
pertain to the good of the soul and without which spiritual life cannot be
properly preserved and fostered.

9. Natural reason is the principal foundation of political Commonwealths, for
there exists no positive command of God enjoining the erection or constitution
of this or that kind of Commonwealth. But natural reason cannot lay the
foundation of the Church, for reason is wholly ignorant of the things upon
which Ecclesiastical Society principally rests. Therefore, these Societies are
distinct and widely different.

10. If the Church is not by divine right a Society distinct from the
Commonwealth, then it is not lawful to institute the Church or cultivate
Ecclesiastical communion when the political Magistrate is unwilling or does
not consent.

[Volume II; printed p. 293; ONB canvas 301]

But this is absurd. Therefore, and so forth. The consequence of the Major is
clear from this: political matters, and those which pertain to the
Commonwealth as such, properly belong to the judgment and determination of the
political Magistrate, and no purely political association ought to be
instituted without his approval and authority. As for the Minor, its truth is
clear from this: Christ and the Apostles instituted the Church not only
without the consent of the political Magistrates who then held supreme power,
but even against their will. Therefore, it was not unlawful. Therefore, the
Church was a Society distinct from the Commonwealth.

11. If the Church is not a Society really distinct from the Commonwealth,
then the Church must be utterly abolished whenever political Magistrates see
fit, and there will be no duty to resist even by passive suffering. For if it
is a
merely political association, the Magistrate can abolish it whenever he judges
this advantageous to the Commonwealth, just as he can prohibit other
associations of Merchants, Craftsmen, and the like. Nor are those merchants or
craftsmen bound in conscience to refuse, and consequently to suffer some fine.
And perhaps this is what the adversaries will and desire: that the Church be
utterly abolished and driven away. For they seem to pursue it with a Vatinian
hatred; for what cause, however, they themselves know best. But will they say
that one must yield to Magistrates when they attempt to destroy every Church
and every assembly of Christians, and when they prohibit the very profession
of Christ’s Name? If so, the primitive Christians must be condemned, for on
account of this they exposed themselves to a thousand dangers and were finally
crowned with martyrdom. Will you wonder what came over them, that they
preferred to undergo death—indeed, the most painful death—rather than yield to
Magistrates in a purely political matter, if indeed it was such? What? Shall
we say that they

[Volume II; printed p. 294; ONB canvas 302]

all died in wicked rebellion? Perhaps the adversaries will say that the
Christians were bound by Christ’s express and positive law to maintain that
profession continually and to perform very many other things which they could
not have performed unless constant Christian communion and mutual Society were
maintained. *Reply.* This will satisfy us, because it will be sufficiently
clear from this that Ecclesiastical Society rests not upon human but divine
right, and is founded upon God’s express command. For the one who instituted
the end also instituted the means without which that end cannot be obtained.
Therefore, since Christ instituted such ends as Christians cannot attain
except by means of an Ecclesiastical Society or assembly, beyond doubt Christ
also instituted this assembly. Therefore, this assembly is not political but
distinct from the political assembly, for Christians could not attain these
prescribed ends through a political assembly as such. Christ knew this well
enough when he instituted a new Kingdom distinct from the kingdoms of this
world.

12. The distinction between these Societies is demonstrated from their
different ends. It cannot be denied that the proper end of the Commonwealth is
the preservation and management of human and political affairs: namely, common
advantage, mutual assistance, and the convenience of life. For “human beings
came together,” as Hoenonius says in political disputation 1, “so that by
joining their efforts they might more easily procure the things which mortal
life needs.” But the end of the Church is the good of Christians as such, or
the spiritual good which concerns souls and eternal life. Who, then, will deny
that these Societies, whose proper ends are so discordant and differ so widely
from each other, are also entirely distinct? They object in vain that every
member of the Commonwealth is bound to intend a spiritual end in all things,
because such a person intends those spiritual ends not insofar as he is a
citizen, but insofar as he is a Christian citizen. Christianity qualifies and

[Volume II; printed p. 295; ONB canvas 303]

regulates the citizen, disposes his mind, and leads and directs him to do
everything piously and in a Christian manner. Yet in this it intends something
very different from the external good of the Commonwealth, although it also
intends this in subordination to a greater good: namely, the eternal,
spiritual, and internal good. Therefore, unless the adversaries prove that
Christianity intends nothing other than the temporal and external good of the
Commonwealth, they will prove nothing. But we do not expect that this will be
proved.

13. In the civil Commonwealth many are not capable of privileges, such as
Slaves not yet manumitted, and others. But in the Church there is no distinction
between Slaves and Masters, or free persons. Colossians 3:11: “where there is
neither Greek nor Jew, circumcision nor uncircumcision, Barbarian nor Scythian,
slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all.” See also 1 Corinthians 7:21–22.

14. From this we draw another argument: the privileges and rights of these
Societies which their individual members enjoy are distinct. For one who is a
member of the political Commonwealth and has a right *in re*, or a proximate
right, to all its privileges does not by that very fact become a member of the
Church. He has no right, either *in re* or *ad rem*, to those privileges proper
to the Church. Otherwise it must be said that an atheist, an unbeliever, a
person openly profane and scandalous, can actually and by right become a
participant in the sacred supper, even if he is not a Christian even in name,
or baptized. The adversary will say in vain that such a person makes light of
that privilege and will never wish to seek it. For it is not improbable that
even the most abandoned person, provided he is a Christian in name only, may
wish to seek this privilege. And why could not an unbaptized atheist seek it,
so that he might all the more trample upon all God’s institutions and expose
them to contempt? When something possible is posited as actual, no
impossibility follows. Then, even if he did not seek the actual enjoyment of
the privilege, he would nevertheless have a right both *ad rem* and *in re*.
And if he did seek it, this right could be denied him by no law.

[Volume II; printed p. 296; ONB canvas 304]

What greater absurdity can be imagined? And conversely, all members of the
Church would by that very fact be members of the Commonwealth. This is likewise
absurd.

15. Societies which have widely distinct officers must also be distinct. But
the Commonwealth and the Church are such. These Societies have distinct
officers: an Ecclesiastical Minister as such is not a political officer, nor
is a Magistrate as such, or by that very fact, a minister of the Church or an
Ecclesiastical officer. If the Church were not distinct from the Commonwealth,
to what purpose would Christ institute new and distinct ministers in it?

16. All Ecclesiastical Ministers are of divine right and were specially
instituted by Christ, nor is it lawful to create others anew. But political
officers depend upon human choice, and their number can be either increased or
diminished as human beings see fit. Therefore, those Societies are distinct,
not one and the same.

17. Ecclesiastical Ministers are not permitted to handle political affairs,
for Christ openly prohibited this and himself refused to discharge the office
of political Magistrate, saying, “Who appointed me a judge or divider?” Luke
12:14. Therefore, the Church, whose appointed Ministers they are, must be
distinct from the Commonwealth.

18. The Adversaries affirm that external coercive power does not belong to the
Church. But this certainly belongs to the political Commonwealth. How, then,
can the Church be the same Society as the Commonwealth?

19. Each has its own distinct offenses, sometimes different materially and
always formally. One sins against the Commonwealth in a different manner from
sinning against the Church; sins committed against the Church are, as such,
scandals.

20. The Commonwealth is a part of Christ’s Kingdom insofar as he is God,
coequal with the Father. But the Church pertains to his Mediatorial Kingdom.
See the second Assertion,

where this is confirmed more fully. Therefore, these Societies are distinct.

21. The titles of the Church are not to be attributed to the Commonwealth.
Therefore, they are different and distinct Societies. The Church is called
Christ’s *Bride*; the Commonwealth as such does not enjoy this title. The
Church is called Christ’s *body*; the Commonwealth is not. The Church is the
*house of the living God*; the Commonwealth as such is not adorned with this
title. The Church is built upon the Rock, and the gates of hell will not
overcome it. But these things do not belong to the Commonwealth; see Matthew
16:18. The Church is the pillar and support of the truth, 1 Timothy 3:15. We do
not read this said of the Commonwealth. The Church is called a lampstand,
Revelation 1:20. Where, I ask, is the Commonwealth so named?

22. In Churches women are not permitted to teach publicly or preside over
them, 1 Corinthians 14:34; 1 Timothy 2:11–12. Yet they sometimes preside over
Commonwealths.

More arguments confirming this distinction could be advanced, but these can
suffice. It would also be easy to trace this distinction even from the
beginnings of the world. For in Genesis 6:2 we read of the “sons of God” as
distinct from the “sons of men.” But these sons of God were members of the
Church. See Pareus on the passage. And when the Church existed in the families
of Noah, Abraham, and the other Patriarchs, although there was a coalescence of
the matter of Church and Commonwealth, these Societies nevertheless remained
formally distinct. For sacrifices, circumcision, and the other parts of divine
worship were not performed by those families insofar as they were families,
but insofar as they were Churches.

But after God’s people had been brought out of Egypt, although the same people
constituted both the Commonwealth and the Church, the distinction between
these Societies considered formally appeared plainly. But because the
adversaries deny this, we confirm it briefly as follows.

1. The Jewish Commonwealth was governed by political and forensic

[Volume II; printed p. 298; ONB canvas 306]

laws. For the sake of distinction, we now call them judicial laws, which, as
Tilenus says in the *Syntagma*, “primarily and of themselves pertained to
ordering the polity of the Israelite people.” These were forensic laws, or, as
Bucanus has it in *Common Places*, locus 18, question 22, “precepts concerning
external actions by which the civil society of the Israelites had to be
governed, or Israelite civil law.” “Political law,” says Johannes Polyander in
the *Synopsis*, disputation 18, thesis 49, “is that which determines the duties
of Magistrates and subjects of the Israelite Commonwealth by fixed civil
constitutions and enforces them upon rebels by corporal punishments, according
to the norm of both tables of the Decalogue, and especially the second.” The
divine forensic or judicial law, according to Polanus in the *Syntagma of
Theology*, book 6, chapter 10, page 1151, “is the law which gives precepts
concerning political government among the Jewish people: that is, concerning
the order and duties of Magistrates, judgments, punishments, contracts, the
distinction of possessions, and so forth, enacted for this end, that justice
among citizens and public tranquility might flourish and that God’s laws might
be vindicated from contempt. Therefore, this law concerned only, or at least
principally, external bodily polity or civil government, not faith and
Religion.” “Judicial laws,” says Maccovius in *Common Places*, chapter 12, “are
laws concerning the law of contracts and the punishments of those who have
offended, enacted for this end: that justice among citizens and public
tranquility might flourish, and that God’s laws, both moral and ceremonial,
might be vindicated from contempt.” These laws therefore pertained to the
Israelite people insofar as they formed a Commonwealth. But insofar as they
were the Church, they were governed by the ceremonial law. For these
ceremonies were laws concerning certain orders and rites to be observed in the
Church, as Maccovius says in the place cited above, chapter 13. Polanus in the
place cited above describes this law thus: “The law which gives precepts
concerning the external worship of God, such as God formerly willed to be
observed in the Church of the Old Testament.”

The Jewish Commonwealth was therefore distinguished from the Jewish Church.

2. They had distinct acts and distinct exercises. For insofar as they were a
civil Commonwealth, not insofar as they were the Church, they cultivated
justice, punished the wicked with death, and so forth. Insofar as they were the
Church, not insofar as they were the Commonwealth, they worshiped and invoked
God, offered sacrifices, and administered sacraments.

3. They had distinct assemblies: some insofar as they were the Church, to
worship and praise God, both at Jerusalem so many times each year and in
the Synagogues on every Lord’s day, Acts 13:27 and 15:21. But they had quite
different assemblies insofar as they were a political Commonwealth, to handle
those matters which pertained to the Commonwealth’s good, to wage war, to
negotiate peace, and the like.

4. To perform these distinct acts, they had distinct appointed officers. For
Priests and Levites were instituted to celebrate worship in Ecclesiastical
assemblies. But the King and other political judges were ordained to
administer the other political acts.

5. The supreme Ecclesiastical Minister was the High Priest. But the supreme
political officer was the King, or Supreme Magistrate, although sometimes one
and the same person extraordinarily discharged both offices, as we see in
Samuel.

6. Even the King was not permitted to discharge the Priest’s office and thus
confound the duties which God had distinguished. Both Saul and Uzziah suffered
severe punishments for this usurpation.

7. The form of the civil polity was changed several times while the form of
the Church always remained the same. For the form of the civil polity was one
thing when Moses ruled, another when the Judges held power, and another again
after Kings were appointed. Finally, it was another after the return from
captivity. Yet

[Volume II; printed p. 300; ONB canvas 308 (upper portion)]

the form of the Church was not changed so many times.

8. To this very day, although they have lost every form of civil polity, they
nevertheless have and retain some form of the Church.

9. Some were regarded as members of the Church and were participants in
Ecclesiastical privileges, since they were admitted into the Congregation of
Israel and called Jews, yet they were excluded from not a few political
privileges for which others were eligible. For “Proselytes of righteousness,”
as Selden clearly shows in *The Law of Nature and Nations according to the
Discipline of the Hebrews*, book 2, chapter 2, were solemnly admitted by
circumcision, baptism, and offerings. They were called Jews (hence that
expression, “a Hebrew of Hebrews,” Philippians 3:5, to distinguish native
Hebrews from those Proselytes) and regenerated. For afterward, he says, no
former kinship or blood relation remained to them, nor did they regard any
brother, sister, father, mother, children, or any relations by birth whatsoever
as their own. They were also participants in Ecclesiastical privileges
according to God’s express law, Leviticus 19:33–34; Numbers 15:14–16. Yet, as
Selden teaches in the place cited above, book 2, chapter 4, page 167, they were
ineligible for Magistracy and Prefecture, both every kind of civil office and
military command. Therefore, although they were called Jews after the name of
their native country had been changed, they were nevertheless not to be
regarded simply as Jewish citizens. We could add more from the same author,
