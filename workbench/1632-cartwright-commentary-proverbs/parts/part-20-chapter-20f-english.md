Verse 19. Do not mix yourself with one who reveals a secret, who walks about with detraction, and with one flattering with his lips.

Since a human being is by nature a κοινωνικόν (*social*) animal, one must act circumspectly so that he enters into fellowship with those with whom he may cultivate a common life without distinction. For this purpose Solomon supplies us with wholesome counsels. Elsewhere he warns that fellowship must not be held with fools, lest we become like them; likewise he has forbidden us fellowship with a wrathful person. In this place he gives other precepts by which, once instructed, we may make a choice of companions. He commands first that we beware of a detractor, who is described by his effects: walking about and running here and there, and then revealing a secret. On account of both of these, every closer union of life ought to be hateful to us, since by the former he is proved to be idle and slothful, pursuing no honourable kind of life. Concerning this, as concerning the other effect, see what was written previously at Prov. 11.13.

He teaches further that another kind of people must be avoided, namely, those who are devoted to foolish speech. They ought to be just as hateful to you as tasteless and unseasoned food is to the sense of taste, since their words are destitute of the salt and grace which they might confer upon those who hear them; besides the fact that, as the Apostle relates at 1 Cor. 15.33, “evil conversations corrupt even good morals.”

But he especially rebukes those who resolve to speak everything according to the desire and will of our mind and to flatter us in everything. In this, because of our innate φιλαυτίαν (*self-love*), we are prone to fall. See the example of Ahab, who, seeking counsellors and Prophets who would give counsel according to his desire, perished miserably in war.

Verse 20. The lamp of one who curses his father or mother will be extinguished in the blackness of darkness.

In מקלל (*maledicentis*, of one who curses) there is a metaphor transferred from bodily things which are light to contempt for parents. In אישון (*nigredine*, in blackness) there is a metonymy of the adjunct. In חשך (*tenebrae*, darkness) and נר (*lucerna*, lamp) there is a metaphor of light for prosperity and of darkness for adverse circumstances.

The purpose is to keep children within obedience to their parents.

The duty of children toward their parents, through whose work they were brought forth into this light and by whose labour, care, and expense they were reared, is so great that to violate it even in thought is not free of grave sin. To be undutiful toward parents by one’s face and the furrowing of one’s brow is not far from crime; to assail them more lightly with words is more serious; but to defile them with curses is a heinous deed and atrocious outrage. And since the duty of children toward their parents is not bounded even by the limits of death—as is most fully established in

[1632 witness | PDF p. 444 | printed columns 862-863]

the sons of Rechab—to employ such insolence against them while they are alive that they even tear them apart with curses is full of horror. And if failing to cover the nakedness of parents is numbered among crimes, in what place will it be reckoned to uncover that person’s covered nakedness, or even falsely to impute and impose nakedness upon him?

The parents themselves are often guilty of this dreadful crime when they embrace their children with excessive indulgence and employ neither severity nor authority toward them, but become accustomed to and associate with them as with their own equals and companions. The result is that, having shaken off the bridle of due reverence, they leap upon their parents with curses no less than upon their own equals. Indeed, there is no lack of fathers who will be the instigators of their children’s cursing their mothers, nor of mothers who will incite them to cast reproaches against their fathers.

This crime is condemned by its attached punishment. At Exod. 21.17 and Lev. 20.9 it is expressly said that the person who curses his father or mother must be punished with death. From this the atrocity of the crime is proved. Solomon, however, in other words increases the punishment owed to them and declares a judgment more severe than that which is expressed in the law. For it is assuredly just that the light of the person who rises proudly against those who, after God, were the authors of his seeing the light should be extinguished—especially since he ought to be a lamp to his parents, 1 Kings 12.15, whose brightness he attempts to remove.

But when he says that such a person must be overwhelmed and covered by the blackest darkness, he thereby teaches that God’s most just judgment upon contumacious and rebellious children does not rest content with their death but pursues them further, even to hell. Even if through the foolish indulgence of the Magistrate and God’s forbearance he should prolong his life to a hundred years, nevertheless he himself will be accursed, Isa. 65.20.

Verse 21. An inheritance hastened in the beginning will not be blessed in its outcome at the end.

In נחלה (*haereditas*, inheritance) there is a metonymy of the species for every possession, because an inheritance is the principal possession.

The purpose is to establish a manner for the acquisition of secular goods.

All who love riches are carried toward acquiring them by their desire for them and their ungovernable longing. By that very fact they hunt for every opportunity by which they may accumulate them, but permit no opportunities offered to pass by, fearing in their faithlessness that no opportunity will ever offer itself again. Therefore, having set aside all justice toward their neighbour and cast the fear of God behind their back, they immediately seize whatever comes their way or whatever they can scrape together. What we repeat in a vernacular Proverb happens to them: [whatever comes into the net is fish.]

Therefore, if an opportunity concerning grain or merchandise offers itself, they immediately seize it in such a way that, scorning the common good, they sell off their own commodities as dearly as they can.

At Amos 8.4–6 the Prophet compares this kind of people with those who, because of their vehement and burning zeal for acquiring riches, pant just like people who in a swifter race are scarcely able to breathe and are nearly deprived of breath. He further describes them by their effect, namely, that they bear with distress and impatience the expenditure of Sabbaths and new moons upon divine worship, because they judge all that time to perish which they do not spend in heaping up riches.

An example of this matter exists in Achan. Coming upon a precious gold-bordered garment and silver shekels forbidden by God, he immediately laid his hand upon them, thinking that such an opportunity to enlarge his estate would never recur and that in one

[1632 witness | PDF p. 445 | printed columns 864-865]

moment he was to be enriched by this sacrilege. Another example exists in Jezebel and Ahab, who were suddenly enriched with an inheritance by the wicked death of Naboth. Thieves and robbers are not unlike them, for they trust that in one moment they will be raised from extreme poverty to a great abundance and affluence of things. See Prov. 1.11–13.

People who inquire into the years of their fathers and ancestors, resentfully bearing the prolongation of their life on earth because while they are alive the inheritance and patrimony does not devolve upon them, are entirely of the same flour.

Now let us see the disadvantages of this wickedness. Indeed, the Prophet has not pursued all of them in this place, but only that one by which people are customarily moved most greatly. For something more grave is noted at 28.20, where it is taught that the person who hastens to grow rich contracts the grave guilt of sin. But in this place he briefly censures the loss by which those who pant so greatly after riches are punished in this age: namely, that in the end and at the last the hastened inheritance will not be blessed. From these words it appears that this zeal for heaping up riches can at first succeed, but that in the end, once God’s blessing has been withdrawn, those riches are dissipated and go away into smoke. Therefore see what God threatens at Amos 8.7. It is also noted in the history what befell Achan, who, if he had waited a little while until Ai had been conquered, could have been enriched lawfully. It is likewise known what befell Ahab from an inheritance acquired so greedily. Experience further teaches what is accustomed to befall thieves and highwaymen, and those heirs who desire the immature and premature death of their parents and ancestors. See 2 Samuel concerning Absalom and Adonijah, besides the fact that it has almost passed into a Proverb that there is no third heir to ill-gotten goods, and what is worn smooth in a common vernacular Proverb: “Hast maketh wast.” Devastation is joined to haste. What is accustomed to happen to people who greedily swallow food which has not been well chewed beforehand and ground by the teeth happens to all these people: they regurgitate what they devoured so greedily; or, if they retain it, they are nevertheless not nourished well and suitably by it, since it is converted into corrupt humours.

This, however, must be understood in such a way that it does not embrace within its scope those who by God’s singular favour are suddenly enriched, such as those who are enriched with the spoils of enemies in a just war, or by God’s singular blessing, as we read happened in Abraham, Isaac, and especially Jacob and Job.

It must be noted, moreover, that the duration of goods and heirs and their transmission to posterity depend upon divine blessing, not upon human foresight or diligence. Since this blessing is alienated by human avarice but summoned by obedience to God in the moderate and lawful acquisition of possessions, who does not see that an accelerated possession is useless for stability and duration? See Psalm 127 and Hag. 1.
