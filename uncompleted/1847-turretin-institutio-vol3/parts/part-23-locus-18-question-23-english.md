QUESTION XXIII.

How many kinds of calling to the ministry are there, and is an ordinary
calling always necessary? We distinguish.

I. In order that the various questions commonly raised concerning the calling
of pastors may be resolved more easily, the various distinctions of calling
which frequently recur must be set out first.

[Marginalia: General and special calling.]

II. First, we presuppose that the subject here is not the general calling to
Christianity, by which people are called into the church and which is common
to all believers. It is the special calling to the ministry which is proper to
pastors, by which certain men are chosen for the public ministry and, after
their life and doctrine have been examined, are consecrated to God by a solemn
rite and installed in the office. Nor is the subject the private preaching of
the gospel, by which individual believers are bound by the common law of
charity to teach and admonish their brothers and neighbors and lead them to
faith and salvation. It is public preaching with authority, the necessity of
which is laid upon those who by a special calling are consecrated to the
public office of the ministry.

[Marginalia: Internal and external.]

III. Second, this special calling is either internal or external. The internal
calling is that by which the heart itself is stirred by God to devote itself
to the work of the ministry, concerning which Paul speaks in 1 Timothy 3:1.
It is more a disposition of the soul to receive a calling than a calling
properly so called. By it a person is conscious before God that he is driven
to discharge this office not by ambition, greed, or any other carnal
affection, but by sincere love of God and zeal for the edification of the
church. The external calling is that by which a person so disposed is
expressly called to exercise the office.

[Marginalia: Immediate.]

IV. Third, this external calling is either immediate, coming immediately from
God himself without human intervention, such as the calling of the prophets
in the Old Testament and the apostles in the New. Such was Paul’s calling, of
which he says in Galatians 1:1 that he was called neither “from men nor through
a man,” “but through Christ and the Father.” He contrasts his calling not only
with an unlawful and merely human calling, which is from men, but also with a
lawful yet mediate calling, which is through men. The other kind is mediate.
Although it is originally from God as its primary author, it nevertheless
comes through the agency of people whom God uses as instruments when he calls
through the church. Such is the present calling of sacred ministers. An
immediate calling, moreover, can occur in two ways: either absolutely and
without any intermediary, when God himself appears to people and speaks with
them immediately, as he called Moses and various prophets in the Old
Testament and John the Baptist in the New (John 1:33)—in this way the apostles
were called by Christ—or when he announces his calling to them through
intermediaries, as he called Aaron through Moses and Elisha through Elijah.
Although we do not doubt that an inward inspiration of the Spirit concurs in
this calling, it is not sufficient unless an external manifestation and
confirmation are added, either through an appearance of God himself or through
a declaration of the divine will joined with the agreement of the proposed
doctrine with the doctrine revealed by God in the Word. Otherwise it would be
confused with the impostures of fanatics who boast of divine inspirations and
revelations.

[Marginalia: Ordinary.]

V. Fourth, a calling is again either ordinary or extraordinary. An ordinary
calling is one which customarily occurs through ordinary means,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 184 | IA leaf 218 | PDF p. 218]

such as the calling of priests in the Old Testament and pastors in the New.
This in turn is either good insofar as it was instituted by God, or evil and
corrupt insofar as it was vitiated by depraved human customs and practices.
The latter is either absolutely and in every respect so evil that it cannot be
reformed but must be abolished altogether, such as the calling of the pope and
cardinals, or evil only in part, so that although it has something vicious
from human fault, it still retains something good from God’s original
institution, such as the calling of Roman bishops. Now *ordinary* is taken
either properly, for what agrees with the order originally and divinely
instituted, or equivocally, for what has been publicly received by
long-standing custom, whatever its character. Thus according to these
different relations a good or an evil calling can be called ordinary.
*Extraordinary* is also used in several ways: 1. for a calling which occurs
immediately from God, without human agency and without observing the received
order, such as the calling of the prophets in the Old Testament and the
apostles in the New, and so coincides with an immediate calling; 2. for a
calling which does not follow the corrupt custom and practice received in
some church. This will be treated more fully below.

[Marginalia: With respect to doctrine and the manner of calling.]

VI. Fifth, an extraordinary calling is again considered in different ways:
with respect to doctrine, the office, or the rites and manner of calling. With
respect to doctrine, it occurs when something new, previously unknown and
unheard, is preached. Such was the calling of Moses, who was sent to
promulgate the law and liberate the people.

[Marginalia: The office.]

With respect to the office, it occurs when the office is entirely new and
without succession. Such was the calling of John the Baptist, who had no
successor.

[Marginalia: The rites and manner of calling.]

With respect to the rites and manner of calling, it occurs when neither the
doctrine nor the office is new, but the manner of calling is new and unusual.
Such was Elijah’s calling. The apostles’ calling can be called extraordinary
in all three ways, because they had to bring a new doctrine concerning the
Messiah’s advent, they held a new office which ended in their persons, and the
manner of their calling, immediately by Christ, was wholly extraordinary.

[Marginalia: With respect to gifts and ordination.]

VII. Sixth, an extraordinary calling can again be so called either with
respect to the gifts and qualities required in those called to discharge the
office, and with respect to the success and marvelous effects produced through
God’s ministers, or with respect to the election itself and ordination to the
office. The calling of the prophets and apostles was extraordinary in both
ways, for the gifts themselves, the election, and the appointment to office
came immediately from God. But there is a certain calling which, although
ordinary with respect to the office itself and the election and ordination
which can be performed by people according to the received order, is
nevertheless rightly called extraordinary with respect to the eminent and
singular gifts above the ordinary measure with which those called to it were
equipped, and with respect to their marvelous success and wholly extraordinary
effects. Such was the calling of the first Reformers, as will be said later.

VIII. An extraordinary calling has various marks. (1.) It is joined with
extraordinary gifts and with the marvelous efficacy of God’s power, which was
exercised sometimes through miracles and sometimes through the efficacy of the
Word itself, in demonstration of the Spirit and power. (2.) It occurs freely
and indiscriminately and is tied neither to nation, family, age, condition and
quality, nor sex. Thus Samuel was called while still a boy, Elisha when
gray-haired, Moses though slow of speech, Amos though a herdsman, and various
women were distinguished by the gift of prophecy. (3.) Its use is either in
the institution of the church, to found and gather it at first, or in its
restoration, to reform it when fallen and corrupt.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 185 | IA leaf 219 | PDF p. 219]

[Marginalia: A calling to Reformation and to the public ministry.]

IX. 7. One calling is to Reformation, by which a person is bound to purge his
faith and worship of all errors and superstitions by which they could have
been corrupted and to retain pure religion without any stain. Another is to
the ordinary and public ministry of the gospel, which consists in preaching
the Word, administering the sacraments, and exercising holy discipline. By
common right the first belongs to all Christians, since there is no one who
is not lawfully called through his baptism both to the glory of God and to his
own salvation, to reject errors opposed to the nature and purity of true
faith, and to stir up his brothers to the same. The latter, however, is not
common to all but proper only to those specially called to it, and no one
ought to intrude himself into it. The reason for this distinction is not
obscure. Reformation consists in acts of faith and charity which belong to
everyone and from which no one can be exempted, since no one can say that he
is not bound to exercise faith and charity. But preaching the Word,
administering the sacraments, and exercising discipline are acts of public
authority which no one should perform in his own name but in another’s. They
therefore require a particular calling which confers such power.

[Marginalia: The ministry with respect to its essence and accidents.]

X. 8. The sacred ministry can be viewed either with respect to its essence and
the things necessarily required to constitute it, or with respect to its
accidents, which concern its good or evil state and condition. What is
essential to the ministry cannot be changed without its becoming another
ministry. But while the essence remains intact, it does not cease to be the
same ministry, although various and manifold changes occur in its accidents.
The essence of the ministry consists in retaining and teaching every saving
truth pertaining to the preservation of faith and piety; administering the
true sacraments instituted by Christ; and keeping the people under lawful
government. Its accidents make its condition good or evil. It is good when
the preaching of the Word is pure and entire, without any admixture of error
or superstition; when the sacraments are rightly celebrated according to
Christ’s institution, without addition, subtraction, or alteration; and when
the Christian people are governed holily from God’s Word and not tyrannically.
Its condition is evil, by contrast, when the truth preached is mixed with
errors and superstitions, the sacraments are altered and corrupted in various
ways, and the government is either confused or unjust and tyrannical. Nothing
is more desirable than the good state of the ministry or better suited to
preserving faith, holiness, peace, and happiness in the church. Nothing, by
contrast, is more dangerous than its depraved state or demands greater
caution. Yet it must not be thought that the ministry immediately fails and
is extinguished by any corruption whatever or by its depraved condition, just
as life is not immediately extinguished but can subsist amid weakness and
disease.

XI. 9. The consideration of the ministry chiefly and in itself is different
from that of the ministers who exercise it, just as it is one thing to speak
of the office of the magistracy and another of the persons who bear it. The
office always remains the same, but the persons change. With respect to the
former, the ministry is an immediate, purely divine institution. With respect
to the latter, it is also a human institution; and although the calling of
ministers is originally from God, it is only mediately so through human
intervention. In the former respect, there is no doubt that in instituting the
ministry God ordered it in the best condition with respect both to essentials
and accidents, in right and in fact, not only by laying all the duties of that
office upon people but also by selecting and calling men to fulfill them
rightly. In the latter respect, the case is not the same for those called by
people. Because people attend only to externals and cannot search the inward
movements of the soul, the ministry can be entrusted to those less suited to
it,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 186 | IA leaf 220 | PDF p. 220]

who can corrupt this sacred office in various ways, whether through ignorance
or malice. If God had always called people immediately as he did the apostles,
the ministry would never have departed from its first institution but would
always have remained pure and uncorrupted. But once callings began to be made
through people, they could easily be corrupted. God never promised to attend
such human callings with a spirit of infallibility so that they would always
remain pure and free of every corruption.

XII. 10. The condition of an established church must be distinguished from
that of a church being established, and a tranquil and well-ordered condition
from one disturbed and corrupt. Many things are lawful when the commonwealth
is disturbed which would not be lawful in a peaceful and well-composed state,
where everything is rightly ordered and done according to the laws. For then
necessity, which has no law, makes many things lawful which would have been
blameworthy had the necessity been absent.

[Marginalia: State of the Question.]

XIII. With these points established, we descend to the proposed question. (1.)
The question is not whether a calling to the ministry is necessary, for this
was proved in the preceding question. It is whether an ordinary calling is so
simply and absolutely necessary that without it there can be no lawful
preaching of the Word and administration of the sacraments. The papists
affirm this; we deny it. (2.) The question is not whether, despite the lack of
an ordinary calling, it is lawful for anyone in every circumstance and at
every time, and in the condition of an established and well-ordered church,
to preach the Word publicly and perform the other duties of the sacred office.
We proved above against the fanatics and enthusiasts that this cannot rightly
be done. Rather, the question is whether, in a case of inescapable necessity
and in the disturbed and corrupt condition of the church, no one may discharge
those duties of the sacred office except those who have been called in the
ordinary way and have received a commission from the rulers of the church.
The papists affirm this; we deny it.

XIV. They maintain that an ordinary calling and commission are so necessary
that if it should happen that a visible church with a public ministry were
corrupted by heresy and idolatry and expelled true believers from its midst,
it would be better for those godly people to remain without pastors and
external worship than to claim the right to create pastors and give them power
to preach the Word and administer the sacraments, as the author of the book
*On Prejudices*, chapter 5, speaks. Hence from the lack of a calling and
commission they are accustomed to condemn our Reformers as schismatics and
usurpers who ought not to be heard. We judge, however, that although an
ordinary calling is necessary in an established church, it is not so necessary
in a church being established or one that is corrupt that believers cannot
discharge those duties by an extraordinary calling. Such a calling is
extraordinary, if not with respect to the office and doctrine, at least with
respect to the act of calling and the received order; and if not from public
authority and with respect to a solemn rite, at least from the private charity
laid upon each person by God through the general calling.

[Marginalia: It is proved that an ordinary calling is not always necessary.
1. From the general duty of believers to seek their own salvation and their
neighbor’s.]

XV. That this is so is gathered from more than one argument. First, everyone
is bound to embrace and defend truth and to reject falsehood and error, not
only from his own heart but also from the hearts of his neighbors and from the
society in which he lives. Falsehood has no right in itself to be believed or
taught; indeed, it always deserves aversion and hatred. The graver and more
dangerous the falsehood which infects the very foundation of religion, the
more zealously it must be avoided and opposed. Thus every Christian is under
a necessity to seek and retain truth and reject falsehood, both in himself for
his own salvation and with respect to others in seeking the edification of
his neighbor. According to his ability, by the law of charity, he should teach,
exhort, and lead an erring brother back to the way. God had already commanded
this in the law, lest we “bear sin in our brother and neighbor”

[1847 vol. III printed p. 187 | IA leaf 221 | PDF p. 221]

(Lev. 19:17); that is, that we should admonish him fraternally and dissuade
him from those things by which he sins against God and his own salvation. The
apostle has this in view when he wishes “each to exhort the others” (Heb.
3:13). This power is not properly a public office with authority, but the
general duty of charity by which we are bound to one another to care mutually
for one another’s salvation. The necessity of this duty increases all the more
when the public office which should serve this purpose either has not yet been
established, or, if established, has been so corrupted and depraved that it
teaches falsehood in place of truth. Then, in that condition, everyone ought
not only to care for the things belonging to his own salvation by retaining
acknowledged truth and courageously casting off received errors, but also to
promote with all zeal the things pertaining to his neighbor’s salvation.

[Marginalia: 2. From a case of necessity.]

XVI. Second, the necessity of a case makes lawful many things which otherwise
would not be permitted, both in household and political affairs. If a
doorkeeper in a house, overcome by lethargy or apoplexy, cannot keep thieves
from the doors, any member of the household may take the keys and shut the
doors against the robbers. If the one entrusted with feeding the family
denies bread to the children, who would say that another member of the
household may not, if able, furnish them bread lest they die of hunger? If a
father himself rages to the destruction of his children, should not his
weapons be taken away, the children removed, and a guardian appointed without
awaiting the magistrate’s judgment? Indeed, a friend or neighbor can undertake
this until provision is made by public authority. Where a fire rages, who
doubts that it belongs to everyone to bring water to extinguish it, especially
when those to whom this care belonged stand idle? Thus in a commonwealth, if
the rulers and officers sleep as the enemy approaches, or if the gatekeepers
open the gates to him, may not any citizen seize the keys from the traitor’s
hand to shut the gates against the enemy and call the people to arms? If
soldiers see the enemy rushing upon them while their commander sleeps or
treacherously colludes with the enemy, will they remain idle and await their
commander’s order? Will they not assemble at the standards on their own,
deliberate how to repel the enemy, and omit nothing on their part so as not to
fail in their duty? When the matter itself calls, what need is there to seek
an external voice? Indeed, if under the appearance of obedience they stood
idle, forgetful of the oath sworn to the prince, they would deservedly be
tried for treachery and betrayal. Here mere awareness of the crime defiles,
and no one clears himself unless he resists the approaching evil according to
his ability. Nor should the oath sworn to the commander stand in the way,
because the earlier and far stronger oath sworn to the prince binds him more
closely. He is not bound to obey the commander except insofar as the commander
proves faithful to the prince. The case is the same here. A believer is bound
to obey the rulers of the church, but only so long as they obey God and preach
his Word purely and entirely. But when they themselves depart from the truth
and rise against God, the supreme prince, one who wished to follow them would
be a rebel against God. The lower order must be retained while the higher
remains inviolate; but when the chief matter and salvation are at stake, one
who followed those who err and did not withdraw from them would wrong God,
himself, and his neighbor. The papists cannot deny this. Just as they wish the
decrees of the Fathers to be inviolably observed when no necessity presses,
so in a case of necessity they admit that many things can be done which would
otherwise be unlawful. Thus in a case of necessity they grant a layman, even
a woman, the power to baptize, and a private person the power to hear
confession if no priest is present.

[Marginalia: 3. From examples.]

XVII. Third, various examples are available from which it is evident not only
that the thing can be done, but that it has not rarely occurred when, in a
case of necessity and when the matter concerned either establishing or
restoring the church, not a few discharged the sacred office without an
ordinary calling. Here belongs what is said in Acts 8:4 of those who, after
Stephen’s death,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 188 | IA leaf 222 | PDF p. 222]

were scattered here and there by the wind of persecution and preached the
gospel. This is said specifically of certain Cypriots and Cyrenians in Acts
11:20–21, who went to Antioch and evangelized the Greeks; the Lord’s hand is
said to have been with them, so that a great number believed and turned to the
Lord. In Acts 18:25–28 Apollos, taught the way of the Lord by Aquila and
Priscilla, publicly refuted the Jews by demonstrating from the Scriptures that
Jesus is the Christ. Ecclesiastical history has many more examples of this
kind. Here belongs what Theodoret, book 1, chapter 23, and Sozomen, book 1,
chapter 15, relate concerning the young men Aedesius and Frumentius. Carried
to India, they preached the gospel and not only brought the king and queen to
faith in Christ, but also established public assemblies for divine worship.
They also tell of a captive woman who converted the Iberians to Christ, first
bringing the queen to faith and then the king through the queen. In seeking
the conversion of his whole nation, the king “became the apostle of his own
people,” as Rufinus says in book 10, chapter 11. Thus Theodoret relates in
book 4, chapter 14, that the Samosatan believers, deserted by their pastors,
discharged the pastor’s office. And in chapter 24 he relates that Aphraates, a
lay monk, as monks then were, went out during the Arian heresy to protect the
believers against that deadly plague. When Emperor Valens was angry with him
for leaving the monastery, he replied: “If I were someone’s daughter and sat
in my chamber caring for the house, and then saw a flame fall upon it and set
it ablaze, what should I do? Should I remain inside and neglect the burning
house, or leave my chamber, run up and down, and bring water to extinguish the
fire? etc. This very thing, O Emperor, is what we are doing.” Victor of Vita,
in book 1 of the *Vandal Persecution*, observes that Manturianus and
Saturnianus, while detained among the Moors, were preachers of the gospel.

XVIII. If today believers were carried by a storm to the most remote regions
of the world, cast ashore by shipwreck among barbarous peoples wholly foreign
to religion, and compelled by necessity to remain there without any hope of
returning to their own people, who would not admit that by the law of charity
they ought to teach the pagans the faith of Christ? And if several of them
were converted, would they not be permitted to choose a pastor for themselves
to establish a church and devote himself to its edification and instruction?
Would it be better for that light to be extinguished without effect than to
establish a ministry for the consolation and salvation of that people,
although this could not be done according to the customary order? Likewise,
if in a most severe persecution of some church or province all the pastors
should either be killed or so dispersed that they could not easily be
gathered, and other pastors could not be obtained from elsewhere, would it be
wiser in that case for the faithful people to remain without a pastor and
external worship, and so to suffer the light of the gospel and all religion
to perish, than for them to be permitted to choose and appoint some suitable
private person from their own body by whom they could be fed and taught,
although the received order could not be observed? Indeed, who does not see
that God’s glory and the salvation of believers demand the very opposite? One
can no more ask by what right, authority, and calling these things are done
than one can ask a citizen what calling he has to oppose an invading enemy
when the officers are traitors, or to extinguish a fire consuming a house when
others stand idle. The case of necessity and the safety of the commonwealth,
which ought to be the supreme law, require it. This is the same as asking a
person what right he has to obey God and oppose Satan, or what calling he has
to seek his own salvation and that of his neighbors. No one fails to know that
this is an indispensable necessity.

[Marginalia: Sources of Solutions.]

XIX. It cannot be said that if an external calling is absent, a wide door is
opened to confusion and every disturbance. A disturber is one who neglects an
order that can be observed, not one who desires order with his whole heart but
cannot investigate and attain it because of the utter disturbance of affairs.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 189 | IA leaf 223 | PDF p. 223]

The man who rashly and unreasonably neglects a well-established order should
be called a disturber, not one who is compelled by unconquerable and
inescapable necessity to omit it. Thus it is not to favor sedition in a city
or camp when a citizen rises on his own initiative and without orders against
a treacherous magistrate, or a soldier against a rebellious centurion, if the
evil can be met in no other way. Order is subverted and disorder reigns only
when someone permits himself this and thinks it lawful outside such a case.

XX. An external calling is always required in a judge so that authority may
be conferred upon him, because the exercise of a political office has its
force and efficacy from the authority of the one exercising it. But the power
and efficacy of an ecclesiastical function do not depend upon the authority of
the one exercising it, but upon the nature of the Word which he preaches.
Hence an external calling is not always required.

XXI. Although the priests under the Old Testament did not exercise their
office without a special calling, the same does not therefore follow for the
pastors of the New Testament. Their office was restricted to a particular
family and to particular circumstances of place and time, while the gospel
ministry is free from all those carnal circumstances. Again, salvation did not
depend upon the legal priesthood. The interruption of that priesthood did not
bring the loss of souls after it, because the ministry of the prophets was
also given. But the Word of the gospel and its preaching are necessary for
salvation, so that there can be no faith without it (Rom. 10:17): “For faith
comes from hearing, and hearing through the Word of God.”

XXII. One who withdraws without the society’s consent from the doctrine and
worship received in that society cannot be said to rend the unity of the
church and incur the crime of schism. True unity does not consist in errors
and false forms of worship, however common and long received, because these
not only fail to promote the society’s good but destroy it like epidemics
which ravage society rather than preserve it. Thus a unity of society is
broken, but it is a depraved and vicious unity. A corrupt church has a double
bond, one to good and the other to evil: one by which it is constituted in the
being of a church, the other by which it is corrupted. The former binds people
to one another and to God; the latter, by binding them to one another,
separates them from God. The first must be preserved by us with every effort,
and no one can break it without guilt. But the second is unjust: no one has
the right to establish it, and everyone is bound to break it. Indeed, the
first bond demands the dissolution of the second, for the more closely anyone
wishes to adhere to Christ, the more earnestly he must break every bond of
error and impiety.

XXIII. It is one thing that no one can preach without a calling and mission;
another that no one can discharge this office without an orderly calling.
Paul asserts the former in Romans 10:15, but not the latter. It is certain
that no one uncalled and without a mission ought to intrude himself into
teaching, just as no one can perform the duties of an ambassador unless he is
sent by the prince. But that mission need not always be made by God in the
ordinary and received manner. It is indeed true that in the well-ordered state
of the church the order established by God must be observed in every respect.
But in a disturbed state, when through human vice and fraud there is such
confusion that the established order cannot be preserved and no reformation
can be expected from those who preside over the church because they themselves
are the authors of the prevailing corruption, then nature itself, reason, law,
and practice teach that what is principal, most important, and necessary must
be sought by every way and means. The duty of piety and charity incumbent upon
all, and necessity—or God himself, who casts people into that necessity—not
only do not forbid this, but command it and give the power to seek what
promotes one’s own salvation and the edification of one’s neighbor, although
the received rites and orders cannot be observed.
