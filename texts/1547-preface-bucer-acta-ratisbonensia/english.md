# Preface to Bucer's Proceedings of the Second Regensburg Colloquy

1 October 1547

This is a fresh English translation of the complete Latin preface printed in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX (Corpus Reformatorum
XXXVII), pp. 851–854. It was translated from the normalized Latin and checked
against the page images. No modern English translation supplied its wording.

The CO title and running head are not translated as though they were part of
Calvin's text. The English title above is a conventional identifier. The
salutation to the pious reader is authorial text; the manuscript's separate
address to Oswald Myconius belongs to the covering letter and is not included.
Page markers precede the English corresponding to each complete Latin word
that was divided at a printed-page turn.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 851]

John Calvin, greetings to the pious reader.

Since this book is being made public by me, and since it could appear absurd
that I should have assumed so much right or license over another man's work, I
have thought it my duty to explain briefly here the reasons that prompted me
to publish it. Nearly two years have now passed since the second disputation
about religious controversies was convened at Regensburg. There those who had
been chosen from each side conferred with one another. Although I ardently
desired to learn thoroughly the entire course of the disputation, as it had
been conducted or at least begun, I understood at the same time that an
agreement had been made that the proceedings should not be published until a
specified time. I therefore restrained the desire for knowledge by which I
was spurred, until some nonsense or other was brought from Louvain, adorned
with that title so that it might carry greater authority.

Then I thought I should delay no longer before asking that excellent servant
of Christ and venerable brother, Martin Bucer, not to begrudge giving me
anything he had written about these matters to read privately. I obtained what
I wanted, both by the privilege of our friendship and because, overcome by the
reasons I advanced, he yielded. At the same time, however, he asked me, I
admit, not to let what he had deposited as it were in my bosom spread any
farther. Yet it turned out well that I obtained far more than I had hoped.

The principal question being debated among us today, and on which a good part
of the others depends, concerns justification by faith. I found it in this
work not only treated and explained fully, carefully, and learnedly—as is
everything that proceeds from this man—but also found everything belonging to
this subject arranged with such skill, expressed with such gravity, and
composed with such moderation that it ought to satisfy even readers otherwise
hard to please. My sole grief was that this treasure, which ought at once to
have been brought out for the common good of the church, was being unworthily
suppressed. Several times it occurred to me either to urge

### [CO IX, p. 852]

the author himself to publish it, or at least to ask him to allow me to do so.
But because there was scarcely any hope that the former could be wrested from
him, and because in the latter case too I feared that he would prove
reluctant, I refrained from both. I thought this the wiser course, lest a
refusal curtail my freedom. Meanwhile, turning over in my mind the three
reasons I shall state, I resolved that at last I must dare something at my own
discretion.

First, it would be exceedingly unjust if all the lies the adversaries pleased
to invent should circulate with impunity in people's hands—lies by which they
not only defame pious and learned men, men unlike themselves, but even insult
Christ himself and his doctrine almost as though celebrating a triumph—while
no voice was raised for the truth to refute their slanders. The loss of our
own reputation would indeed matter less. In fact, this is our condition: the
more we endeavor to approve ourselves to the Lord with a purer conscience and
more sincere faith, the worse the report we often receive before the world.
But when open attacks are made on the doctrine itself, it is certainly no time
to remain silent.

And Bucer was now surely beyond the danger that anyone might find fault with
him if, provoked by his adversaries, he had published this book himself. For
when they allow themselves every freedom to lie and swagger shamelessly, who
will impose on us the necessity of silence? Nevertheless, because I was freer
to act, I preferred to take this burden upon myself rather than persuade him
to exercise his own right—a legitimate right, to be sure, though perhaps not
beyond dispute.

A second consideration was added. If the Lord should ever look with favor
upon his church, tossed by such savage storms, so that a serious discussion is
restored concerning the questions disputed today in religious doctrine, I saw
that this learned work would be of no small assistance in explaining them. No
small result would be achieved if the dispute over this head of doctrine—that
is, the justification of man—were removed. Yet there will be no impartial and
equitable judge who, after reading the book and calmly

### [CO IX, p. 853]

weighing everything, does not acknowledge that Bucer devoted himself
faithfully to this end: that nothing should be lacking for a godly
reconciliation. For he carefully avoided the two faults that commonly cause
the greatest hindrance in debate: either serving ostentatious display or being
swept away by contentiousness. Readers will find, I say, that whatever this
prudent man, far from inexperienced in the doctrine of godliness, judged
pertinent to the matter, he pursued soundly rather than ostentatiously. He was
so prepared for the contest that the reflections he produced here came from a
calm mind, aroused by no heat. Would that those who are deeply hostile to us
could bear to read these things with so composed a mind.

It is sufficiently well known how bitterly those who attack our whole
doctrine assail this part of it. Yet we know for certain that we teach nothing
that is not attested by firm and sure testimonies of Scripture. We do not
bring our own conjectures here; we do not play with sophistries; we do not
invent clever colors through which what we say might appear plausible.
Instead, we confess plainly before people what God's own judgment dictates to
us whenever our consciences are summoned before his tribunal. If those who
now assail us so bitterly would call themselves back to such an examination,
that very same conviction which compels us to speak as we do would surely
restrain and tame their ferocity.

But many have long been exercising whatever ingenuity they possess in this
arena—something most unfitting—so that they might corrupt and distort by
various slanders what we have endeavored to draw from the pure fountains of
Scripture and set forth. Therefore, those who desire holy and unfeigned
concord should permit us to obtain at least this from them: that an honest
explanation of our case be allowed a place, an explanation not buried under
the far from honest taunts of our adversaries.

### [CO IX, p. 854]

This book will contribute much toward that end, because it will not only open
the way to understanding the matter, but will also disperse the many clouds
with which Satan has until now attempted to obscure the manifest light—indeed,
the Sun of righteousness himself.

Finally, even if the book were of no use for such a proceeding—and we should
by no means despair of that use—its usefulness will extend more widely. This
head of doctrine that it handles embraces not only the eternal salvation of
human beings, but also the glory of the divine goodness that has manifested
itself to us in Christ. Indeed, the very office of Christ is set forth in it,
in which, of course, the chief sum of our wisdom consists. As for how
successfully our Bucer has treated the exposition of this subject, I prefer
that readers judge from the reading itself.

For my part, when so outstanding a work was in my hands, I thought that I must
by no means allow it to remain suppressed forever. I held back for some time,
waiting to see whether a better opportunity might be offered. But there were
many reasons—which need not be recounted—why further delay seemed wrong. Bucer,
I hope, will grant me this confident liberty on account of our friendship. If
at first he does not approve what I have done, there will be something that
handsomely compensates for his slight and brief offense: the whole church of
God will thank me. Its interests had to be more highly regarded than that I
should comply with one man's wishes at the cost of so great a loss to it. The
author himself too, because of the singular zeal for building up the church
with which he burns, will—once he has duly evaluated my decision—not only be
reconciled to it, but will also rejoice that I have taken over his part.

Geneva, 1 October 1547.

## Translator's notes

1. The preface was not actually published with Bucer's book. The CO
   prolegomena preserve correspondence showing that Bucer requested the
   arrangement described in `README.md`, later abandoned it, and issued his
   book himself in 1548. The narration in the preface is therefore part of the
   unrealized publication plan, not an independently reliable account of how
   Calvin acquired and read Bucer's manuscript.
2. “Proceedings” in the conventional title renders *acta*. The CO editors
   supplied that title; the manuscript opening is the salutation *Ioannes
   Calvinus pio lectori S.*
3. “Adorned with that title” translates *illo titulo ornatae*. Calvin does not
   reproduce the title or identify the Louvain publication here. His derisive
   *nugae nescio quae* is rendered “some nonsense or other.”
4. “Justification by faith” renders *iustificatio fidei*. Later on the same
   doctrine is called *iustificatio hominis*, “the justification of man.”
5. “Perhaps not beyond dispute” renders *non forte liquido*. Bucer's right to
   publish was intrinsically legitimate, Calvin says, but not necessarily
   unclouded in view of the colloquists' agreement to delay publication.
6. “Learned work” translates *lucubratio*, literally work produced by
   lamplight or night study. Here it designates Bucer's carefully composed
   treatise, not merely a draft.
7. “Conviction” renders *sensus* at CO IX 853: the inward awareness of divine
   judgment produced when conscience is summoned before God's tribunal.
8. Calvin pairs *ingenua* with *minime ingenuis*: an “honest” or “candid”
   statement of the Reformed case is being smothered by opponents' decidedly
   dishonest taunts. The translation repeats “honest” to preserve the
   contrast.
9. “Sun of righteousness” translates *iustitiae sol* and retains Calvin's
   probable allusion to Malachi 4:2. In Calvin's syntax, Satan's clouds obscure
   not only the conspicuous light but Christ himself.
10. “Such a proceeding” renders *talis actio*. It refers most naturally to the
    hoped-for renewed formal discussion of disputed doctrine described on
    p. 852, although the Latin noun is broad enough to mean an undertaking or
    course of action.
11. “This confident liberty on account of our friendship” renders *fiduciam
    hanc amicitiae nostrae*. The phrase acknowledges Calvin's presumptuous use
    of a friend's manuscript while presenting the friendship as grounds for
    expecting pardon.
12. “I have taken over his part” translates *me suas partes occupasse*. It can
    mean assuming Bucer's role or responsibilities and also carries a hint of
    preempting them, an ambiguity suited to the preface's publishing fiction.
