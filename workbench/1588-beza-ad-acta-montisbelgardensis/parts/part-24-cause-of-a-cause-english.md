## Part XXIV: “Whatever Is the Cause of a Cause Is the Cause of Its Effect”

Reply to the argument, “Whatever is the cause of a cause is the cause of its effect”

[Marginal note: On annotation 8.]

This is that principal argument by which some, deceived, cry out that we make God the author of evils unless we join them in separating God’s foreknowledge from his decree and permission from his will in things unjustly done by human beings. They fail to observe Horace’s saying, “While fools avoid one vice, they run into its opposite,” as we showed at the beginning of this treatise. Before exposing the emptiness of their argument, I shall discuss two things our adversary objects against us here. The first is that I allegedly said the first humans sinned because God willed it. I did not say that. I said that they fell—that is, departed from that first and happiest origin—neither against God’s will, nor without his knowledge, nor under his idle foreknowledge, and consequently with God truly willing and decreeing it. Although this fall, so far as they were concerned, was truly an enormous sin, which God deservedly continues to avenge in the entire human race, nevertheless so far as God’s will and decree are concerned we must conceive no fault here, since God always decrees well and justly whatever is committed even by sinful instruments. I said and say that the first parents’ deprivation of their native goodness could have occurred only from the decree of God willing it so and ordaining this change toward the best end, as I showed before. But their sin proceeded from nowhere but Satan who persuaded and themselves who voluntarily obeyed him, with the graver sin because they deserted God with a will still free and not yet enslaved to sin. Finally, as Augustine says excellently in more than one place, what is evil in itself does not possess the character of evil insofar as it depends upon God, but insofar as it is also good that evils exist, which otherwise God would not permit to exist. He certainly does not permit them unwillingly, but—

—willingly. In the second place, Andreae rebukes me because I say elsewhere that humanity had to fall. This again needs explanation. Adam did not possess that necessity of sinning to which the first parents were subjected after sin, which they propagated to us, and from whose bondage we are gradually manumitted through the grace of Jesus Christ, to be fully liberated at last on the final day. Adam must instead be said necessarily to have fallen by another necessity, namely with respect to God’s ordering, which cannot be frustrated. Yet this does not change the natures of things, but determines each thing according to its nature—a subject we shall discuss more fully elsewhere. Let us now come to the matter.

Andreae formulated the foundation of this argument in these customary scholastic words: “Whatever is the cause of a cause is also the cause of its effect.” For this assertion to avail against us, the following minor premise and conclusion must be added: “But if we believe Beza, God’s decree and will are the cause of the evil will of human beings, whose effect is sin. Therefore God will thus have been the cause of sin.” Although I shall discuss this matter more fully later, I first reply concerning the major premise of this syllogism. When we attribute to God the name of cause—and indeed the primary, universal, immutable, and most efficacious cause—we must assess it not from the nature of created causes, however excellent, but from God’s most exalted majesty, incomprehensible even to angels. Nor must it be connected with any secondary causes in a kind of interwoven chain according to the Stoic imagination. It must be considered as the cause widely and profoundly distinct from them which uses all created things as instruments, sometimes according to their nature, sometimes beyond and even contrary to their nature, as he pleases. It is consequently by no means simply subjected to that axiom, because whether he uses good or evil instruments, his own work is always just.

To the minor premise I reply that God is indeed the moving cause of the will, even of evil people, but not insofar as it is evil—or, as they speak in the schools, according to its character as a being but not according to its defect. Aquinas speaks truly about this, though not in especially good Latin, in *Summa theologiae* I, q. 49, art. 2, reply 2: “The effect of a defective secondary cause is reduced to the primary cause, which is not defective, as regards what the effect possesses of being and perfection, but not as regards what it possesses of defect. Thus whatever movement exists in limping is caused by the power of motion, but the crookedness in it does not arise from the power of motion.” And I–II, q. 79, art. 1, reply 3: “The effect of an intermediate cause proceeding from it insofar as it is subject to the order of the first cause is also reduced to the first cause. But if it proceeds from an intermediate cause insofar as this departs from the order of the first cause, it is not reduced to the first cause. God therefore is the cause of every action insofar as it is action; but sin names a being with a certain defect arising from the created cause, namely from the will corrupted and departing from the first agent’s order.” To make this almost visible to the eye, we must first establish—

—that all created things are instruments of God, who is never idle but always acts. Next, the difference between good and evil actions occurs only in instruments endowed with intelligence and reason, such as angels and humans. Some are good by their own nature, as are angels who did not fall; some by grace, as are regenerate humans insofar as God’s Spirit governs them; others are evil not from creation but from the voluntary corruption of nature, as are angels who did not remain in the truth and humans as they are born. Hence both kinds possess the character of an instrument insofar as God works through them, but the character of a cause insofar as they move themselves from an intrinsic principle. Insofar as they are instruments and are moved by God, the effect God obtains from them is always good. Insofar as they are causes and act by their own motion, the work of those whose will agrees with God’s will is also good, as when good angels execute God’s commands and regenerate humans move themselves by that rectitude of will which God created in them by grace. Conversely, what God does through evil instruments is likewise always good, but what they do as causes is always evil, even when they do what God wills to be done—whether this divine will has been revealed to them, as is clear in the histories of Job and Ahab, or their evil mind and purpose alone suggest it to them. There is always opposition between God’s will and the will of these evil instruments, even when those wills seem to agree, as we shall say in its place.

It is beyond dispute that God’s complete power and will intervene both in the movement and in the proper action of good instruments, so that he truly acts both through and in them. But how God moves evil instruments and what his action is here remains disputed, since it is certain that God does not will iniquity but is the enemy and avenger of sins. Certainly, if what we said about God as the first cause moving evil instruments is understood merely of that divine power by which the order of nature is preserved in all created things and the wills of voluntary creatures are set in motion, we must cross into the Peripatetic camp, which recognizes only universal providence. Since not even sparrows fall without their Creator’s will, and the outcome of lots itself, as Solomon testifies, comes from God, it must be confessed that even those evil wills are moved not merely universally but expressly, by a fixed and definite divine impulse, to perform this or that act. That this can occur only through God’s powerful and efficacious will and decree we have proved above by many arguments and authorities, and it is clear from the following express testimonies in which the Holy Spirit speaks. “You did not send me here,” says Joseph, “but God” (Genesis 45:8). Thus he raised Pharaoh—

—up (Exodus 9:16) and hardened him (Exodus 4:21), and did so willingly (Romans 9:18). Thus he delivered David’s wives to Absalom (2 Samuel 12:11). Thus he incited David’s heart to number the people (2 Samuel 24:1). Thus he commanded Shimei to curse David (2 Samuel 16:10). Thus David calls his adversaries the Lord’s hand and sword (Psalm 17:13). Thus the Lord himself calls the Medes and Persians his sanctified ones and instruments of his wrath (Isaiah 10:5; 13:6). Thus he calls the ten tribes’ defection his own work (2 Chronicles 11:4). Thus the king of the Chaldeans is compared to a saw and axe (Isaiah 10:15). Thus Job says without blaspheming, “The Lord gave, and the Lord has taken away” (Job 1:21). Thus the godly are afflicted by God’s will (Romans 8:29; 1 Peter 3:17; 4:19). Thus Herod and Pilate assembled to do whatever the Lord’s hand and counsel had determined beforehand should occur (Acts 4:28). Therefore Christ could not be captured except at his hour (John 7:30; 8:29; 12:27). Thus he is said to have been delivered up by God’s definite counsel and preceding decree (Acts 2:23). Thus he was wounded by God (Isaiah 53:5), and thus God did not spare his own Son (Romans 8:32).

Thus finally God gives kings in his wrath (Nehemiah 9:37). He causes them to err (Job 34:30; Isaiah 63:17), mingles a spirit of error (Isaiah 19:14), hardens and turns hearts, blinds eyes, and makes them drunk with the wine of dizziness (Exodus 4:21; 7:5; 9:12; 10:1; 11:10; 14:4; Deuteronomy 2:29; Joshua 11:20; 1 Samuel 2:25; 2 Chronicles 22:7; Psalm 105:25). He delivers them to a reprobate mind (Romans 1:18), sends a powerful delusion so that the lie is believed (2 Thessalonians 2:11), deceives prophets (Ezekiel 14:9), and sends evil spirits with a command to harm and efficacy granted to deceive (1 Kings 22:22–23; 2 Chronicles 18:21; Job 1:12; 2:6).
