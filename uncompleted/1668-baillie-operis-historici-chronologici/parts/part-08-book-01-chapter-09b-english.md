in Asia itself, terrified by a new fear he withdrew from Sardis to Ecbatana, so

Sardis, however, he made the sorrowful beginning of a new tragedy. The

she regarded as the source of the evils. At the solemn festival of a Persian
king's birthday, it is forbidden to deny anyone anything. Seizing this
occasion, Amestris came as a suppliant and demanded that Masistes' wife be
given to her. Although the king did so with anguish, he nevertheless yielded.
He summoned his brother and urged him to divorce his wife, offering him his
own daughter in a new marriage. Masistes refused and entreated the king not to
force him to abandon a wife most dear to him; the king insisted and threatened
him. Meanwhile Amestris had the wretched woman dragged into the palace by
attendants and, with a woman's cruelty, gouged out her eyes, cut off her ears,
nose, and lips, and sent her back to her husband. He fled toward the Bactrians,
hoping with their help to obtain vengeance for his brother's savagery, but was
dragged back on the road and compelled to die.

Herodotus has no more, but Diodorus Siculus takes the torch from him and will
carry the light before us in what follows. Greek and Persian affairs are bound
together by so tight a connection that anyone who wished to separate them
would necessarily tear a thoroughly united body into pieces. Pardon us,
therefore, if we present together matters that cannot be separated without
violence.

The battles of Plataea and Salamis initiated great changes in Greece. They
transferred the leadership from the Lacedaemonians to the Athenians and
furnished material for new disturbances both within and outside Greece. The
rise of one is the destruction of the other. When the Spartans saw the power
of the Athenians in its youth during the Persian War, they tried to suppress
it at its very birth, lest, if it gained strength, it should bring destruction
upon their own supremacy. But the more obstacles the Laconians put in the way
of Athenian growth, the greater the Athenians' daily advances in authority and
power became.

When the common council of the Greeks was debating to whom the first honors
for courage in the battle of Salamis should be awarded, no one doubted that
they belonged to the Athenians. Nevertheless, lest the Athenians' glory,
already luxuriant of its own accord, should exalt itself too greatly, the
Lacedaemonians arranged for the first spoils to be awarded to the Aeginetans.
Yet lest they offend Themistocles, to whom alone the victory was due, they
summoned the man to Sparta and heaped the highest honors and gifts upon him.
Because Themistocles had deigned to accept these things, those who envied the
public glory of the Athenians had the people strip him of the command of the
fleet, which they conferred upon his rival Aristides.

Pausanias, guardian of the Spartan kingdom, then commanded the common fleet of
the Greeks. He labored vigorously to liberate both the islands and the
mainland of Ionia from the Persian yoke. But the man's pride and arrogance
toward the allies were so great that most began to recoil from Spartan
leadership and to incline with great eagerness toward the Athenians. For
Aristides' justice and humanity were such that nearly all Ionia placed itself
in his hands and allowed a tribute to be imposed upon it by his counsel. The
Athenians would administer that tribute, deposited at Delos, according to
their prudence, so that funds would not be wanting for the common war against
the Persians.

The Spartans raged that the command of the sea was visibly being wrested from
their hands, but so great a mass of troubles oppressed them at home that they
had no leisure to deal with the Athenians abroad. Pausanias was seized by a
mad desire to betray Greece to Xerxes. He sent many letter carriers about this
business, and none returned: immediately after delivering their letters they
were all killed, as the documents they carried commanded. It happened that
one of the carriers, suspecting that he bore his own death in his breast,
boldly unsealed the tablets. There he read of both the betrayal of his country
and his own destruction, and reported the whole matter to the ephors. They
gave it no credence until the informer, by the following device, produced the
accused man confessing. The carrier fled to a sanctuary as though accused of
a crime, and Pausanias came to learn the cause. The ephors were hiding behind
the tapestries. There, as though alone with Pausanias, the carrier assailed him
for having sent him to an evident death and showed him the broken-open tablets.
Pausanias did not deny the deed; he feigned grief over it and begged the man to
conceal his designs. Having drunk in these things with their own ears, the
ephors reported them to the Spartan people. When word of this reached
Pausanias, he fled to the sanctuary of Minerva. The people surrounded the
temple. They did not dare violate the holiness of the place; but lest this
criminal and betrayer of his country escape, they blocked every entrance to
the temple with bricks. Pausanias' own mother laid the first brick, and thus
hunger killed the wretched man.

While the Spartans were occupied with these matters, the Athenians, at
Themistocles' urging, were diligently rebuilding their city's walls, which the
Persians had demolished. Envoys immediately arrived from Lacedaemon, in truth
to forbid the work, because they feared the growth of this

The excellent man continued to serve his country. He asked the people for
authority to accomplish what he was considering in his mind—something most
useful, but impossible to do if it were divulged. The people marveled at the
man's ingenuity and ordered him to disclose his thought to Xanthippus and
Aristides: if they approved, he would have full authority. They reported that
Themistocles' plan was just as he himself had declared it: honorable, useful,
and possible to accomplish. Not even then did the people dare entrust their
resources to the man's hands, fearing that a man of such genius might devise
something against the liberty of the city. They therefore ordered him to lay
his plans before the public. He refused; they desired him at least to prove
them to the senate. When he had done so abundantly, they granted him authority
to do whatever he pleased. He immediately employed all the city's labor to
construct the harbor of Piraeus. This place was suited to receive a very large
fleet; without it all the Athenians' aspiration to rule the sea would be vain.
If the Spartans had learned of the design, they would undoubtedly have bent
all their strength to prevent its completion. But Themistocles' unexpected
speed, both at the harbor of Piraeus and at the walls of Athens, forestalled
every effort of his enemies. Once a Spartan fleet was standing in a harbor
near Athens. Themistocles informed the people that, if they permitted it, he
could perform an action most useful to the city—for he intended to burn the
Spartan fleet. He was ordered to prove his design to Aristides. Aristides
reported to the city that what Themistocles intended was useful but unjust;
the people therefore prohibited the deed. Themistocles' ability certainly
raised his country's naval power to such greatness that the Spartans
themselves thought it necessary to yield the sea voluntarily.

But what reward for his good service did this great man receive from his
fellow citizens? At the expulsion of the Pisistratids a law of ostracism was
enacted, by which any person, wholly without fault, could be driven into exile
for five years if six thousand citizens at a public assembly inscribed his
name upon their shards (which they called ὄστρακα). Themistocles was the first
man cast out by this exile of the sherds, without any pretext of any crime,
but either from envy or fear that his surpassing ability might someday
persuade him to seize dominion over his fellow citizens. The Lacedaemonians
immediately went to destroy the now-afflicted Themistocles, remembering how
much harm one man's ability had done to their affairs and fearing that, if
restored to his country, he would utterly overthrow them by his counsels. They
therefore ordered him to plead his case before the common council sitting at
Sparta on the charge of betraying his country to the Persians and sharing a
design with Pausanias. Though wholly innocent of any crime, the man dreaded
approaching Sparta, his mortal enemy, and fled to the king of the Molossians.
Envoys followed from Sparta, demanding that the betrayer be surrendered to the
Greeks. Fearing the Spartans on behalf of the Molossians yet pitying an
innocent man, the king warned Themistocles to flee secretly, first furnishing
him abundantly with royal money for the journey.

After many hardships and dangers, Themistocles came to the wealthy Pythius in
Lydia, his former host, by whom he was conducted to Xerxes at Ecbatana in a
closed carriage, like a mistress of the king whom no one on the road dared to
inspect. Xerxes received the man very kindly, enriched him with a great
abundance of slaves and money, and moreover gave him a noble wife and, as her
dowry, three ample cities in Ionia: Lampsacus, whose most excellent wine was
for his drink; Magnesia, where the finest grain was for his bread; and Myus,
in which the most savory fish were for his food. Yet even these royal favors
scarcely preserved Themistocles from immediate destruction. The king's
sister, whose twin sons had perished in the battle of Salamis, heard that the
author of that disaster had arrived and flew into the court filled with fury.
She assembled an immense host of people, some of whose relatives had fallen
in the Greek war. They surrounded the king and cried out seditiously that
Themistocles should be delivered to them so that they might tear him to pieces
alive at once. Xerxes scarcely obtained permission for him to be heard and to
plead his case on an appointed day before the senate of the Persians. When the
day came, Themistocles, now instructed in the Persian language, argued so
effectively that he proved his case even to Persian judges.

Themistocles pledged his word to Xerxes that he would lead an army into
Greece. But when the time came to swear an oath to the king against his
country, amid the sacrifices he took in his hands a cup of bull's blood,
drained it, and died immediately after drinking. This excellent man chose to
do injury to himself rather than to either the king who was his friend or the
country he loved, however ungrateful it was.

Meanwhile the Athenians had placed Cimon son of Miltiades in command at sea.
At Cyprus he defeated a Persian fleet of three hundred triremes, whose spoils
filled Athens with wealth. On the same day Cimon sent several thousand chosen
men of his own force, aboard the captured ships and under the arms and in the
garb of the captive Persians, against the king's land army. By their efforts
he won before dawn a second victory more splendid than the first.

While the Athenians felt the most favorable breeze of fortune in every
respect, Spartan affairs were sinking in the opposite direction. The city of
Lacedaemon itself was shaken by so dreadful an earthquake that, in the ruins
of its houses, besides their possessions, more than twenty thousand people
perished. This disaster in their rear was followed by a servile war. The
Helots, oppressed by the harshest servitude, rose against their already
afflicted masters; the Messenians joined them, and they fought the Spartans
with varying fortune and were not defeated before the tenth year.

At the very beginning of Artaxerxes' reign, he was met by the Egyptian
rebellion. While the fickle people saw the traitor Artabanus ravaging the royal
house, they took Inarus, a Libyan by birth, as their king. By great promises he
enticed the Athenians to him from neighboring Cyprus. With their aid he routed
Artaxerxes' forces several times. The king sent Megabazus to Lacedaemon laden
with a very large quantity of gold to persuade the Spartans to invade Attica,
so that the Athenians might thereby be recalled from Egypt. But the leading
city of the Greeks gave no hearing to the barbarian. Deceived in this hope,
the king therefore turned to actual force and ordered the largest army to be
led to the Nile. The terrified Egyptians surrendered. Thus the Athenians were
deserted by their allies, but
they did not desert themselves. Their triremes were stationed above Memphis.
When the Persians rendered these useless by draining the channel in which they
lay, the Athenians burned them with their own hands and formed themselves into
a circle, fully prepared for both battle and death. The barbarians considered
how much of their own blood the death of those most courageous men would cost
them, and agreed to grant the desperate men not only life but also entirely
free departure. Making long journeys on foot toward Cyrene, most of them
reached home unharmed.

Artaxerxes was very favorably disposed toward the people of God. In the
seventh year of his reign Ezra, and in his twentieth Nehemiah, came to their
people at Jerusalem, where, furnished with royal letters and gifts, they
devoted much labor to adorning the temple and fortifying the city. God
rewarded this kindness of Artaxerxes with many great benefits, and he attained
a profound old age in great peace. What Ussher relates from Ctesias about the
rebellion and battles of Megabyzus, while other authors pass it over, seems to
me more like fables than truth.

He maintained complete peace with the Greeks, granting liberty to the Ionians;
but the Greeks turned their arms away from the Persians and against one
another. After Pericles had removed Cimon son of Miltiades from their midst,
an almost implacable war between the Athenians and Lacedaemonians drew all the
Greeks onto opposing sides, until they had worn themselves down equally with
mutual wounds.

The Peloponnesian War, than which no greater war occurred among the Greeks,
took place fifty years after the Persian War. Diodorus has several internal
disturbances of the Greeks peculiar to his account, which at last erupted into
this conflagration. Thucydides also touches very briefly on all of them. We,
however, pass them over untouched as matters of lesser importance and proceed
at once to the heart of the subject. In what follows we shall press closely
upon the footsteps of Thucydides alone, although, to our regret, the fates did
not permit this most weighty of the Greek historians to accompany us for any
considerable stretch of the road.

Honors commonly change character for the worse. The Athenians, who at first
conducted themselves most fairly toward their allies and the rest of Greece,
soon inclined toward arrogance, began to rule cruelly and greedily, and sought
supremacy over the rest of Greece. Many tried in vain to check their violence,
for no one was equal to that work until, at the urging of the Corinthians, the
Spartans took it in hand.

sent a fleet to ravage the Peloponnese. They brought whatever movable property
there was in the countryside into the city and settled securely within the
walls. This was the method of the war: each year at the beginning of summer,
Archidamus came to lay waste the Athenians' fields, and Pericles sent a
ravaging fleet into the Peloponnese.

In the second year of the war, an epidemic plague nearly reduced Athens to a
wilderness. A singular kind of disease spread from Ethiopia through Egypt,
Syria, Asia, and most of Greece. It attacked the healthiest people, assailing
first the head, then the chest, and finally the belly, until it had ravaged
their lives by drying up their organs with internal fevers. Any who recovered
lost all memory of their former life. While the enemy raged with sword and
flame outside the walls of Athens, the plague burned with far greater fury
within. So great was the slaughter of mortals in the streets, in the temples,
on the ships, and everywhere that it aroused great pity in the observer—pity
all the greater because almost no one who approached the sick more closely
escaped the affliction. Even the ravenous beasts were certainly most careful
to abstain from the corpses of the dead; if any dared touch one, it immediately
paid for its rapacity with death. It is remarkable that not a spark of this
disease crossed into the Peloponnese.

Although Thucydides is celebrated as the foremost Greek historian, my purpose
allows me to dictate to you very little from my extensive reading of him. Our
design does not permit us to touch upon his arguments and political
observations, in which he appears to surpass every writer both before and
after him. He does indeed contain very many events, but few of them are
distinctive enough to deserve a place in the briefest synopsis of universal
history. These, nevertheless, are not unworthy of mention.

The war before us abundantly demonstrates that the character of the Greeks
was no gentler than that of the barbarians, for each of the two belligerent
sides raged against the conquered with the utmost barbarity. The Mytilenaeans
turned nearly all Lesbos away from alliance with the Athenians. Because of
this crime, when Mytilene was afterward compelled to surrender, one thousand
of its leading citizens were sent to Athens and immediately punished with
death. Cleon even obtained a decree to kill every last mortal who had remained
in the city. A trireme was sent to communicate to the army this decree, most
unworthy of the Greek name. With very great labor Diodotus wrung a reversal of
the sentence from the people, but in vain, unless a second trireme, setting
sail the next day and pressing hard behind the first with the greatest exertion
of its oars, should reach Mytilene at the very moment when the governor was
preparing to execute the earlier decree. This haste preserved the Mytilenaeans
from death, but it saved neither them nor the rest of the island from a
servitude that exceeded every Persian, indeed Scythian, servitude in severity.

The Spartans were no more humane than the Athenians. After a long siege they
forced the Plataeans by famine to surrender. Although the city had served all
Greece well, the Spartans not only razed it to the ground but also raged with
the sword most cruelly against all its inhabitants. We cease to marvel at the
barbarity of Greeks against Greek enemies when the deeds of fellow citizens
against one another come before our eyes. In a certain naval battle, the
Corinthians took several hundred Corcyraeans alive. They immediately put most
of them to death and freely sent a few home to advance the Peloponnesian cause
on Corcyra against the Athenians. By their devices these men kindled a flame
of sedition which the greatest rivers of citizens' blood were insufficient to
extinguish. While the common people favored Athens and the nobility Corinth
and Sparta, such massacres occurred and such a Hydra of evils arose that even
Thucydides could scarcely express them.

One may rightly marvel at the strength of the single city of Athens. For eight
successive years its enemies had ravaged its fields, the plague had consumed
many thousands of its citizens, and a great part of its expenditure had been
lost in fitting out various fleets. Nevertheless, in different places the
Athenians supported several armies: in Thrace, in the Peloponnese, at Corcyra,
now on one and now on another island of the Aegean, in Sicily, and elsewhere,
by both land and sea. They made no mention of peace and even rejected an
offered settlement.

The Leontines in Sicily, while fighting the Syracusans and feeling themselves
inferior in strength, called the Athenians to their aid. The Athenians embraced
the alliance, thinking that this war would open a path for them to subjugate
not only Sicily but also Africa and Italy. For that people, intoxicated by the
unexpected breezes of favorable fortune, contemplated nothing less than
dominion over the world. After the Sicilians had thoroughly battered one
another with mutual disasters, fearing that the Athenians would rush upon
both sides in their now-weakened state, toward friendship

On one occasion, as a fleet was making for Sicily, Demosthenes (not the
orator), one of its commanders, came abreast of Pylos after leaving Malea

army as he pleased for several days around the Peloponnese, and that he thought
it advantageous to fortify Pylos, a place especially well suited for harming
Sparta. The others laughed at the man's proposal and said that they had no
leisure to undertake the labor of fortifying deserted promontories. By no
words could Demosthenes make his counsel please anyone. Meanwhile a storm
arose and they were forced to enter the harbor of Pylos. When the army had
remained idle for a considerable time, the very nature of the place—though
Demosthenes was no longer wasting words in the attempt—stirred in the soldiers
a desire to build the fortification. Of their own accord, therefore, and
without anyone's command, they all heaped together mud and stones; before the
sixth day they completed a stronghold which drove the Peloponnesians to
despair and nearly to a suppliant petition for peace. At first the Spartans
scorned Pylos as a trifling matter; but when they considered the position of
the place, they came by land and sea to attack it. Demosthenes had remained
there with a guard of five ships while the rest of the army departed for
Sicily. When he saw a great number of enemies approaching, he sent men to
recall his forces from their voyage to his aid. Meanwhile the most courageous
Spartan commanders tried to break in from both land and sea, but such was the
nature of the place that, although the defenses had been built only recently
and hurriedly, a small band sufficed to defend them until the fleet arrived.

An island lay across the mouth of the harbor. The Spartans transported four
hundred citizens onto it so that the place might be more closely blockaded,
and arranged ships on both sides so that no access to the besieged men would
lie open. But when a naval battle was joined, the Athenians not only drove the
Spartan fleet from its position; by surrounding the entire island with their
ships, they also held all the soldiers upon it as though enclosed in a net.

Captured Pylos dug deep into the Spartans' foot like the sharpest thorn, for
from it the Athenians could make raids into the very heart of the Peloponnese.
The loss of their foremost citizens on the island was also grievous to them,
but above all they were terrified by the occupation of Cythera, an island
lying opposite the city of Sparta itself. Although these disasters had not
broken them, their former fierceness was greatly checked. After arranging a
truce at Pylos, they sent men to Athens to negotiate peace. The Athenians,
however, were swollen by Fortune's recent favors and would accept peace only
on the most unjust terms. Their arrogance soon caused them to regret this:
after the Thebans broke them in a great battle at Delium and Brasidas punished
them severely many times in Thrace, they lowered their crests. At last an
agreement was reached in this way. Brasidas the Spartan and Cleon the Athenian,
the chief firebrands of the war, fell in the same battle in Macedonia. The
Athenians feared Fortune's wheel, which was now turning, while the Spartans
daily felt the disadvantages of Cythera and Pylos, besides having before their
eyes the desertion of their slaves and the danger to their captive citizens.
Both sides, worn down by ten years of war, first made peace and afterward a
treaty for fifty years.

This peace was the seed of a new war. The Corinthians and very many of the
Peloponnesians despised the Spartans' faintheartedness in making peace and
prepared to form a new alliance among themselves against both Sparta and
Athens, with Argos as its head. But this plan did not hold, because the Thebans
and Megarians, who were among the allies of no slight power, recoiled from the
popular government of the Argives, since they themselves were governed by
aristocrats on the Spartan model. In any event there was no need of alliances
against Sparta and Athens, between whom a mutually savage war would soon
appear, notwithstanding the recently concluded and faithless peace.

Under the agreement the Spartans were to return to the Athenians what they had
taken in the war, but they found its performance impossible. In Thrace the
citizens held Amphipolis, and in Boeotia the Thebans held Panacte. Neither
group could be persuaded to deliver these cities into the hands of their
former Athenian masters. The Spartans nevertheless obtained from the Thebans

In ancient history I find nothing about Darius Nothus. I shall therefore pass
over what more recent writers contain, holding most of it of little account
because I do not know their sources. Yet I find the following matters in the
records of ancient history.

Xerxes alone was Artaxerxes Longimanus' legitimate son; besides him there were
seventeen natural sons. Sogdianus, the eldest of these, arranged for Xerxes to
be killed through the deceit of a eunuch. A few days later, a great part of the
nobility, hating the deed, joined Ocho, one of the seventeen bastards. After
Sogdianus was killed, Ocho seized the kingdom and changed his name to Darius.

For the nineteen years in which Darius Nothus held the Persian scepter,
Ctesias relates several court seditions; at that time he practiced medicine in
Darius' household. But Thucydides, Herodotus, Xenophon, and other contemporary
historians were so occupied with the Greeks' wars that they had no leisure to
listen for disturbances among the Persians.

Whoever reads with judgment the twenty-seven years of deeds of the Spartan and
Athenian peoples, for as long as the Peloponnesian War lasted, will declare
that either side—Athenians or Lacedaemonians—could more easily have subjugated
the Persian kingdom with fewer forces, fleets, commanders, and funds than
those with which they attacked one another. The only fruit was that now one
side and now the other approached the brink of immediate ruin, and that both
repeatedly came as suppliants to their common enemy, the king of Persia, to the
disgrace of the Greek name, imploring his help to crush one another—peoples who
in concord appeared able to occupy the empire of all Asia with no great
difficulty.

Darius Nothus was succeeded in power by Artaxerxes Mnemon, the eldest of his
sons, whom Parysatis had borne to Darius while he was still a private man. The
same wife afterward bore Cyrus to him when he was king. As soon as Cyrus grew
up, he proved to be a man of the greatest spirit; Artaxerxes was of a much
gentler disposition. Cyrus was dearer to his mother, Artaxerxes to his father.
Xenophon has much about this younger Cyrus, but because his most elegant
writings are lengthy, we have no leisure for them at present. We are therefore
compelled to draw what follows, with less labor, from Diodorus Siculus and
Plutarch.

Parysatis desired to secure the kingdom for Cyrus and tried to persuade her
husband, while he still lived, that the sovereignty belonged to Cyrus by the
same right as it had once belonged to Xerxes, because he was the first son
born while his father reigned. But Darius' judgment remained fixed on
conferring the kingdom upon Artaxerxes after himself. When Cyrus lost his legal
claim, he turned to violence and deceit. While his brother was preparing, as
was customary for Persian kings at the beginning of a reign, to put on the
sacred robe of Cyrus, Cyrus lay hidden in ambush and sought to take his life.
But word of the plot reached Artaxerxes first. When Cyrus was to be beheaded
for the crime, his mother embraced him and did not allow herself to be pulled
away from the accused man until with tears she had wrung pardon for one son
from the other.

The Thebans met him, trusting in the advantage of a position they had chosen.

soon returned home. This fear in so courageous a man made the Thebans confident

In the following year, when the same Arcadians summoned Epaminondas and he was
preparing to lead an army back into the Peloponnese, all who favored the Spartan
cause converged upon the Isthmus as though a second Xerxes were advancing. They
fortified the narrow passages with wall and ditches and, when the works were
complete, placed guards upon them. But Epaminondas' courage broke through
everything. No great-hearted man looks for corners: he openly attacked the
part of the wall where the Lacedaemonians stood, drove away the guards, and
became master of the fortifications. He ranged with an armed force through the
Peloponnese wherever he pleased, attacked Corinth, and crossed the city's
walls. The citizens rushed inside their houses, but the courage of Chabrias and
the Athenians cast the Thebans out of the captured city.

At this time extremely grave seditions raged in most of the cities of Greece,
and especially at Argos, where the people, incited by demagogues, put about
twelve hundred of the aristocrats to death.

After convening a council at Thebes, Epaminondas persuaded the citizens to give
attention also to maritime affairs, so that they might wrest not only the rule
of the land from the Lacedaemonians but dominion over the sea from the
Athenians. They built one hundred triremes. Going out with them, Epaminondas so
terrified the Athenian admirals by his very name that they did not dare engage.
But with Epaminondas' life all these efforts of the Thebans vanished into air.

The man's last expedition was as follows. Arcadia was burning with civil war.
The Mantineans brought forces from Lacedaemon against the Tegeans, while the
Tegeans summoned Epaminondas. When he came into Laconia and learned that the
Spartan youth were sitting in enemy country while besieging Tegea, he resolved

near to being delivered into Epaminondas' hands.

While Epaminondas survived, peace could not take root in Greece, for his valor
filled the Thebans with a desire to rule the others. Neither the Athenians nor
the Spartans, however, could endure masters, for they still bore their former
spirit, although their ancient courage was greatly diminished. But when

While the Greeks were stabbing one another with mutual wounds, Artaxerxes

troubles by working privately upon most of the rebellious satraps so that, by
betraying one another, they were all brought back to their former obedience.
The difficulty was greater in Egypt. By offering high pay Tacon had brought
many thousands of veterans from Greece into his service. By public resolution
the Lacedaemonians sent Agesilaus, now in extreme old age, to Tacon's aid.
Observing Agesilaus' short stature, the curvature of his foot, and his great
neglect of bodily appearance after the Laconian custom, Tacon despised him.

The foolish Egyptian's insults pricked the great-hearted man's spirit deeply
enough, but they did not stab so far as to drive that weighty man to the crime
of betrayal. Raleigh casts this and other reproaches upon Agesilaus without
desert and, so far as I know, without anyone's authority. It is true that
Nectanebos, Tacon's nephew, stirred the Egyptians to rebel behind his back while
Tacon himself fixed his eyes upon the Persians alone. As a result, Tacon fled
to Artaxerxes because his life was in danger and was received by him kindly,

honor and several hundred talents of gold and sent home. But on the journey he
was seized by disease and died on the African shore itself. For lack of honey,
his body was preserved in wax, carried back to Sparta, and buried with splendid
funeral rites.

Meanwhile domestic calamities gave Artaxerxes' long life a tragic end.

desire. Soon afterward, however, envy drove him to repent, and he arranged for

celibacy. Enraged by this insult, Darius seethed in spirit against his father,

three hundred and sixty concubines. To heal Tiribazus' wounded spirit, however,

Ochus concealed his father's death for several months until, by a forged
document issued as though his father were still alive, he had caused himself
to be proclaimed king throughout the provinces.

At the very beginning of his reign, he filled the palace with the slaughter of
many relatives. He buried his sister Ocha alive; shut his uncle with eighty
sons and grandsons inside an enclosure and pierced them with the soldiers'
javelins; and from the mere caprice of his mind destroyed many others who were
suspected of no crime. Thus the cup of the Persians was filled, and, as their
crimes reached their summit, the Macedonian sickle was sent into Asia's now
ripened harvest.

While Ochus reigned, Bagoas, satrap of Syria, grievously afflicted the Jews.
The high priest Joannes feared that his brother Jesus, an intimate of the
eunuch, would use Persian power to cast him out and be made high priest, and so
killed him in the temple itself. Because of the high priest's crime, the eunuch
afflicted the entire Jewish nation with many evils.

The Egyptian Nectanebos had added Phoenicia to his kingdom. But when Ochus came
with an army to Sidon, first Sidon was delivered to him by betrayal and then
the rest of Phoenicia through fear. Indeed, Nectanebos was seized by cowardly
terror, abandoned Egypt though it was excellently fortified against every
force, and fled into Ethiopia. Thus Ochus recovered and peacefully secured his
entire empire without difficulty, but divine vengeance did not permit him to
enjoy the fruits of this peace for long.

Bagoas, a eunuch and commander of the soldiers, was Egyptian by birth. Ochus
harshly oppressed the conquered Egyptians and transported many thousands of
Jews from Egypt to Babylon and Hyrcania. But what above all else stirred the
Egyptians' bile against Ochus was his insolent contempt for their god Apis. He
sacrificed Apis to an ass and, like a common calf, had him butchered by a
cook's hand, roasted, and set upon his table. Moved by this among the other
causes of Egyptian anger, Bagoas removed Ochus in the twenty-third year of his
reign, had his body cut into small pieces and given to cats to eat, and made
sword hilts from his bones. He substituted another corpse upon the royal bed
and interred it in the tombs of the kings as the true one. So says Aelian, who as
usual remains silent about his source.

All Ochus' sons were removed with him except Arses, the youngest, whom the
eunuch placed upon the throne. But as the young man grew up, the eunuch feared
that he would avenge his father's murder and arranged for him also to be
killed. He then set Darius Codomannus over the kingdom as the last king of the
Persians.

Ancient authors, including those of the best repute, differ over whether
Codomannus was descended from Cyrus' family and was connected by any kinship
with the Persian kings. In any case, it is certain that Bagoas' devices placed
him on the throne. As soon as he sat upon it, his first concern was to take
vengeance upon Bagoas for his crimes against the former kings, fearing that
this criminal man, already accustomed to shedding royal blood, would someday
add him also to his predecessors as a new victim.

While these tragedies were being enacted in the Persian court, everything in
Greece was advancing toward the execution of God's decree to transfer the
empire from the Persians to the Macedonians. Philip of Macedon was at this time
wholly occupied with the Sacred War, which occurred as follows.

alliance by gifts.

Philip's history eminently deserves consideration. Before he was king,
Macedonia was a kingdom held in the greatest contempt, with the narrowest
borders, the least strength, and very many parricides among its petty kings.
Philip was the fourth of Amyntas' sons, but when his brothers had removed one
another by mutual deceit, the government of his country came to him. The savage
Illyrian nation then ruled widely. Most of the petty kings of Thrace paid it
tribute, and among the others the Macedonian had been defeated in various
battles. The Illyrians held Philip as a pledge for the Macedonian tribute and,
when they had to make peace with the Thebans, deposited him at Thebes. The
Thebans entrusted the young guest to Epaminondas' father for his education. He
in turn committed both Philip and Epaminondas to a certain Pythagorean for
instruction. In that school the boys imbibed the principles of virtue which
raised each to become the greatest man of his age.

Philip received a kingdom of Macedonia that was very small in itself and
shattered by every evil of Fortune. The Illyrians had removed from both throne
and life the one brother whom domestic hands had left him. The king of the
Thracians was trying to give the Macedonian scepter to Pausanias, a young man
of royal stock. The Athenians offered their assistance to Argaeus in acquiring
the kingdom, and frequent wars had worn away whatever strength the Macedonians
possessed. The young Philip remained at Thebes almost as a captive, but divine
providence rescued the man from this Iliad of evils. On receiving news of his
brother's death, he secretly fled Thebes. When he came into Macedonia, he
raised up the people, overwhelmed by troubles, through frequent speeches;
accustomed them to Theban discipline in war, first exercising them in smaller
battles; and then established the Macedonian phalanx. By its courage all
Macedonia drove out the Illyrians. Philip appeased Pausanias and the Thracians
with gifts and turned the Athenians away from Argaeus by permitting their
colony Amphipolis to enjoy liberty. Finally, in a few years he raised the
Macedonia he had received in deepest affliction to the summit of flourishing
fortune. By force and craft he added most of the surrounding nations to the
former kingdom, including nearly all the cities in Thrace, the Chersonese, and
Piera, for dominion over which both Athens and Sparta had poured out many
rivers of blood since the time of Xerxes.

The orator Demosthenes roused the Athenian people against Philip by various
invectives, but every attempt against a man whom Fortune's breeze—or rather
Providence—was driving toward the greatest achievements under full sail was
vain. Philip frequently employed golden weapons: if any walls withstood
ballistas and engines of war, he ordered an ass loaded with gold to batter
them. Such then was the Greeks' φιλαργυρία, or love of money, that in every
city men could be found willing to auction off their country for gold. Philip
certainly attained such power through the force of both courage and money that,
when the Spartans accepted from the Phocians the patronage of sacrilegious
robbery, the Thebans saw no one in Greece except Philip to whom they could
safely entrust their cause. At first he sent the Thebans slender forces, for he
wished them to be subdued several times and cast aside the exceedingly proud
spirit they had taken from the battles of Leuctra and Mantinea. When he
perceived that this had been accomplished, he immediately set out for Delphi
with a huge army. The Phocians did not dare meet him, but without battle
offered whatever terms Philip demanded. Thus without danger he simultaneously
obtained the highest reputation for courage and piety in Greece. He restored
the Amphictyons to the temple and imposed an annual fine upon the Phocians for
their sacrilege, by the severity of which that impious people would be cast
forever into the abyss of poverty.

quantity of gold in the treasury at Persepolis. The sum is marvelous but
credible if one considers the countless offerings which for several centuries
had flowed there every day without any outflow. No crime is more serious than
impiety: although what you worship should be an evil spirit, yet if you regard
it as a god, you cannot violate it without the stain of profanation. Diodorus
Siculus certainly demonstrates more than once that all who touched Apollo's
gold suffered heavy penalties.

From the very cradle Alexander was furnished with every gift of mind, body,
and Fortune—if anyone on earth ever was—for acquiring dominion over the world.
His body was extremely strong and healthy, though not large, and his
constitution was so perfect that when he sweated he exhaled a pleasant scent.
For several days after death he remained as though still alive, with vivid
color and a fragrant odor.

Once a horse named Bucephalus was brought from Thessaly to his father for the
price of thirteen talents, more than thirty thousand pounds in our currency.
It was so fierce that no skill and no force could make it endure a rider. When
Philip was preparing to cast aside the beast of untamable ferocity as useless,
the young Alexander observed that the greater part of Bucephalus' fierceness
arose from fear of his own shadow. He gently turned the horse toward the sun so
that it could not see the shadow, then soothed it with soft clucking sounds,
mounted, and made it walk slowly for a time. At last he applied his heels, let
the reins go, and drove it into a headlong gallop. Gradually checking it, he
brought it back to his father sufficiently obedient. Philip wept for joy,
kissed the boy's head as he dismounted, and said, “My son, seek another kingdom
for yourself, for Macedonia cannot contain you.”

The instruction of his teacher added much to a greatness of spirit which was
already almost supreme by nature. Philip wrote to Aristotle that he rejoiced
not so much that a son had been born to him as that the boy was born at a time
when he could enjoy Aristotle as his teacher. Aristotle appears to have
undertaken Alexander's education with his spirit embittered against the
Persians by a personal injury. For several years he lived with his friend
Hermias, a eunuch and petty ruler in Ionia. When Memnon, satrap of Ionia,
contrary to his pledged word delivered the eunuch to death, Aristotle and the
other philosophers associated with him scarcely escaped the bloody hands of
the executioners by swift flight.

When Alexander came of age, he could not have wished for more splendid
opportunities to accomplish great deeds. The Persian court threatened itself
with ruin through its own crimes. His father had compelled all Greece and the
neighboring barbarians in Europe, if not beneath the yoke of his empire, at
least into a friendly alliance against the Persians. Alexander also had under
his standards his father's veteran army, flourishing with many victories and
every excellence of military skill. Yet all these preludes of flattering
Fortune would have vanished into smoke had Alexander's own supreme courage,
through the manifest breath of divine power, not drawn back his father's
Fortune as she fled from him and then held her captive within his arms until
his final breath. For as soon as Philip died, the greatest obstacles were
immediately placed before Alexander's undertakings.

After Codomannus obtained the Persian scepter, he prepared everything for war
more vigorously and prudently than the effeminate kings of the Persians had
been accustomed to do for many years. Memnon the Rhodian, brother of Mentor,
held the dominant place in his counsels. He advised Darius to transfer the war
into Macedonia, so that the Greeks might fight at home rather than in enemy
country. To carry this noble and useful counsel into effect, Memnon himself was
placed over all the coastal provinces and equipped with great forces by both
land and sea. Most of the barbarians Philip had subjugated had broken into
open rebellion; the loyalty of all the Greeks was wavering, and the
Macedonians themselves breathed sedition.

the Macedonian kingdom and one thousand talents of gold. When Parmenion's
diligence had uncovered the crime, through his efforts Alexander also had

the strength they possessed. Darius reviewed more than six hundred thousand
fighting men from the upper provinces especially on the plains of Mesopotamia.
Alexander also held various levies of soldiers from both Greece and Asia and

they immediately fled in fear, leaving the passes open and undefended to the
greatly delighted Alexander. From there he flew with great haste to Tarsus to
rescue the city from a fire set by the satrap Arsanes.

The Cydnus, a river of the clearest and coldest waters, cuts through the middle

and sweat, Alexander stripped off his arms and clothes in sight of the whole
army and threw himself naked into the Cydnus. His sinews immediately stiffened,
and such a sudden numbness seized every limb that he was nearly dead and only
half alive when his attendants carried him into his tent. The camp immediately
panicked as though Alexander had died or was about to die at any moment. When
he first recovered consciousness from his faint, he was tormented by great
anguish. Darius was reported to be approaching within a few days, while the
physicians predicted that Alexander's illness would be not only dangerous but
prolonged. His weakness made him unequal to the initial labors of battle; the
absence of this one man exposed the army as prey to its enemies, for he truly
was the spirit of his men, from whom they all appeared to draw courage in
battle. In this desperate crisis Philip the Acarnanian, his chief physician

from the man's rashness. By letter Parmenion warned the king to beware of

indignant rather than terrified expression. As Philip had promised, on the
fourth day, amid the final despair of all his allies, the king presented
himself for the soldiers to see. A longer stay in Cilicia was nevertheless
necessary to restore his health fully.

Bessus, his kinsman, was governor of the Bactrians, and Nabarzanes of the

commander. It could be resumed as soon as the Greeks had been driven from Asia

their Bactrian and Hyrcanian camps from the rest of the army. But soon, the
more effectively to deceive Darius, an honest and simple man, they feigned
great repentance for their rashness, even with many tears. Reconciled to
Darius, while the march to Bactra was hastening onward, they resolved to
accomplish by force what they had not been able to do by craft. They determined
among themselves to lay hands upon Darius. Patron still commanded four
thousand of the fifty thousand Greek mercenaries. The conspirators' designs
somehow leaked out to him, and he had them reported to Darius, advising him to
entrust his safety to Patron and the most faithful Greeks. Darius knew well
that Patron was the truest informer and most friendly counselor, but he
nevertheless refused the offered aid. He foresaw that Bessus would seize upon
it as a plausible pretext for his crime, as though he had justly laid hands
upon Darius because the king, distrusting his own men's loyalty, had placed
his hope of safety in foreign mercenaries. Yet this prudence did Darius no
good. As soon as Bessus noticed that the Greeks had left him, he surrounded
the king with his men and placed golden chains upon the captive; indeed, he
cast the bound Darius into a filthy wagon to be dragged with him into Bactra.

When Alexander heard of Darius' danger, he pursued Bessus day and night with
the swiftest Macedonians and with marvelous speed, intending to rescue Darius
from the cruel hands of his own men. When Bessus heard that Alexander was
approaching, even though his force was far smaller and his men were not merely
wearied but failing from the hardships of the road, Alexander's fame terrified
him. He scattered his army and fled, but first he abandoned Darius—who refused
to follow his dispersed men on horseback—half alive in his filthy wagon,
pierced by many wounds. Alexander arrived a few hours after Darius died and,
with tears, laid his own cloak over the dead man. He entrusted the body to
Darius' mother to be carried to Ecbatana and buried with every royal honor.
Bessus had assumed the royal name and insignia while Darius was still alive
and was hurrying into the northern provinces in hope of assembling a new army.
To crush this serpent in the egg and avenge the horrible murder of Darius,
Alexander followed Bessus' tracks as swiftly as he could.

At that time Alexander permitted the very many—indeed, nearly all—of his
soldiers who desired it to return to their country, giving them magnificent
gifts worth many thousands of talents. A large new reinforcement came to him
from Greece every year; but Alexander was now greatly changed from the man he
had been while Darius lived. He had adopted the worst Persian customs. Bands
of more than three hundred sixty prostitutes and concubines, together with a
crowd of eunuchs and catamites, publicly followed the royal court. He had
wholly adopted Persian luxury in food and dress and far surpassed their
drunkenness at drinking parties. And lest anything be wanting to his madness,
Alexander publicly desired to be worshiped as a god and a son of Jupiter. The
old soldiers of Philip and most Greeks abhorred these things and grieved

To erase this miserable tragedy from the memory of the sorrowing army, a march
into Bactra and Hyrcania was announced for the capture of Bessus. While merely
crossing the Paropamisus in the Caucasus, many hardships from snow and cold,
hunger and labor, oppressed the soldiers. Alexander possessed singular
strength of both mind and body in enduring toil. He walked on foot with them;
to inspect each man, he went now among the first, now the middle, and now the
last, pouring life into the half-dead by speech and example. Once the mountains
had been crossed, kinder soil and sky revived the almost-dead soldiers. After
traversing several thousand stades, they came to the Oxus, a river both deep
and broad. Since no material for building bridges or rafts could be found
there, Alexander carried the entire army across in five days upon skins and
sewn tent-hides filled with straw. When the Bactrians perceived that the king,
whom neither mountains nor rivers had delayed, was present, they seized Bessus
and delivered him bound to the king. It was decreed that Bessus should pay the
penalty for parricide by many tortures on the very spot where he had killed
Darius, and the full execution of the sentence was entrusted to Oxyathres,
Darius' brother.

The Scythians beyond the Tanais had joined the Bactrians. Quintus Curtius,
deservedly numbered among the first class of Latin historians, takes this
Tanais to be the river which, above the Danube, divides Asia from Europe; and,
if we believe him, this was the opinion of Alexander and his most skilled
geographers. Yet the matter itself and the entire history demonstrate that
the Asiatic Tanais (in truth, the Jaxartes) was separated from the European
river by the Caspian Sea and several tens of thousands of stades. In any
event, Alexander crossed this river also, partly on skins and partly on rafts,
and drove the Scythians from the farther bank with great slaughter. After he
returned to this side of the river, he fought many battles, bloodier than
those in any other province of Asia, against the Bactrians and Sogdians. They
were often subdued and often rebelled. He destroyed Cyropolis, where an almost
fatal wound was inflicted on the king's neck, and many other cities, and
founded new Alexandrias in place of those destroyed. At last, broken by the
slaughter of several hundred thousand, they remained at peace.

Alexander was refreshing his army at the Oxus after returning from Maracanda.
Well filled with drink at a banquet, he boasted of his achievements and
belittled his father beyond measure. Clitus, his nurse's brother, equally
drunk, contradicted him in everything with an utter lack of restraint in his
speech. At last Alexander's anger blazed, and he rushed furiously upon Clitus.
The other guests at first restrained him from violence with difficulty, by
entreaty and force; but when Clitus afterward put himself into the raging
man's hands, Alexander snatched a spear from an attendant and pierced the side
of the man who contradicted him. When the wine had evaporated, this killing
sank so deeply into Alexander's mind that for three days he rejected every
consolation and all food. On the fourth day, almost forced by his friends and
persuaded by Callisthenes, he took food; after twelve days of deep mourning,
he set his mind upon continuing the war.

To free the army from this lamentable destruction by punishments of guilty and
innocent alike, a journey into India was decreed, partly from desire for glory
and partly from desire to occupy what remained of the Persian Empire. Until
they reached the Indus, few and tolerable difficulties presented themselves;

presented itself. Porus, a man of the greatest spirit and body, occupied the
farther bank with eighty elephants, three hundred scythed chariots, and a
large army of courageous men. After part of the Macedonians crossed the river
in a dark, stormy night with great alarm, a savage battle began. Porus was
finally defeated and came into Alexander's hands. Asked how he wished to be
treated by the victor, he replied, “Royally.” Asked again whether he desired
anything else, he said that his first answer included everything. Alexander,
admiring the man's supreme virtues, restored his kingdom and everything else
he had lost and received him into the company of his friends.

The defeat of Porus opened to Alexander all India on this side of the Ganges,
but when he prepared to cross the Ganges, the whole army protested. For,
besides the exhausted men's intense desire to end a war that was being waged
without any benefit—excepting the commander's boundless greed for empty
glory—the Ganges was the largest river in all India. On the farther bank the
king of the Gangaridae prepared to meet their advance with three thousand
elephants, two thousand chariots, thirty thousand cavalry, and two hundred
thousand infantry. Coenus delivered an earnest speech for the soldiers,
begging the king to make an end of wars at last. Alexander was persuaded most
unwillingly, and to the army's great joy a return to the west was decreed; but
on the way he wished to explore the south and the Indian and Persian Ocean.

On the nearer bank of the Ganges he ordered camps built twice as large as
their true size, together with soldiers' couches, horses' stalls, and altars
of the gods, all of such appearance and size that they might persuade a
credulous posterity that Alexander's soldiers had been giants and monstrous
men. Taxiles and Porus competed over which would build more and better ships
from their immense forests. With this fleet Alexander sailed down the Hydaspes
to the confluence of the Acesines with the Indus. Where these three extremely
large and swift rivers met, the whole fleet was severely battered, and the
admiral's trireme was all but swallowed by an immense whirlpool, placing
Alexander's life in immediate danger. The army frequently disembarked from the
ships to subjugate neighboring peoples. They fought dangerous contests with
the Oxydracae and Mallians, the most warlike of the Indians. When a city had
been captured and ladders were applied to the walls of its citadel, Alexander
was the first to stand upon the top of the wall. When the multitude following
him broke the ladder, every weapon of the enemy was hurled against Alexander
alone. To escape this danger he summoned a greater, leaping with terrible
audacity from the wall into the citadel amid the enemy. He balanced his body
so as to land on his feet, then stabbed one or two of the strongest men who
approached him, while the weapons of a great many overwhelmed him from a
distance. With his back turned to the wall, for some time he protected himself
from mortal wounds with his shield. But at last an arrow pierced both shield
and breastplate and gave him a deep, broad wound in the chest above the
nipple. As his strength failed from this wound, he sank upon his knees; yet
when an Indian ran up to strip him of his arms, he recovered his strength and
stabbed the assailant. When many others prepared to kill him from a distance
with their weapons, Peucestes, Leonatus, and two other attendants arrived.

and for some time resisted every attacker, until two attendants were killed
and Leonatus and Peucestes severely wounded. Meanwhile the Silver Shields
broke through the wall and put to death not only those who met them but every
person of every age and sex. They carried Alexander to his tent as though
dead. After the arrow was cut and the broad point extracted from his rib, the
surgeons' careful treatment scarcely restored him to health after many days.

When they came to Ecbatana, their feasting increased. Hephaestion contracted a
fever from drunkenness and, during the fever, indulged his appetite against
his physician's advice; he died on the seventh day. Nearly mad with excessive
grief, Alexander fastened the wretched physician to a cross, then built at
Babylon, at a cost of twelve thousand talents, a tomb surpassing royal
splendor. After consulting Ammon, he dedicated temples, altars, and solemn
vows to Hephaestion as no minor god.

As he approached Babylon, the Chaldaeans advised him not to enter, announcing
with complete certainty that it would be fatal. Terrified by their warning, he
wandered around the city for some time. At last Anaxarchus' arguments persuaded
him of the vanity of the Chaldaeans, and he entered with the most solemn pomp.
For several months he dissolved in the most magnificent pleasures while
meditating new wars. With great industry he was preparing a fleet of one
thousand great ships with which he would subjugate first the Carthaginians and
the entire coast of Africa, and then Spain, Italy, and Gaul. He sent home with
great honor, gifts, and mutual tears many Macedonians worn down by age, labor,
and wounds, under the command of Craterus and Polysperchon. In their place he
formed a new phalanx under Peucestas from thirty thousand young men of like
age (*ὁμηλίκων*) drawn from the Persians and the strongest peoples of Asia.
While he devoted himself wholly to these undertakings, behold, fate suddenly
halts everything.

He very often drank so profusely with his friends that the whole following day
scarcely sufficed to sleep off the previous day's debauch. Once, when he left
a banquet of Bagoas rather late, Medius the Thessalian led him to a new
drinking party. There were twenty guests. The king drank first to each one,
and each drank first to the king. Not satisfied with this, he called for the
cup of Hercules, which held two congii, and challenged Proteus the Macedonian.
When both had drained the cup, Proteus took up the Herculean vessel and drank
first to the king again. Draining it a second time, the king suddenly fell
into a severe illness. Carried home on May 12, he suffered from a fatal fever
and died on the twenty-second, in the year 323 before the birth of Christ,
according to Ussher.

No poison was needed here, although many weighty authors advance this account
of poison, contrary to the testimony of many others equal to them in
seriousness. They report that the Macedonians were greatly displeased by
Alexander's new customs, especially his cruelty toward Parmenion, Philotas,
and Callisthenes, and that Antipater began to fear greatly for himself and his
family. This was especially so when the king, wearied by Olympias' complaints,
summoned him to Asia with a new reinforcement of Macedonian youth and put
Craterus in his place as governor of Macedonia and Greece. Parmenion's murder,
they say, frequently stood before Antipater's eyes. Thinking it therefore
necessary to forestall Alexander with a fast-acting poison, Antipater gave his
son Cassander water from a certain Macedonian rock shown to him by Aristotle,
which corroded iron and everything else except an ass's hoof. He ordered
Cassander to share the design with no one except his brothers Jollas and
Philip and Medius the Thessalian.
