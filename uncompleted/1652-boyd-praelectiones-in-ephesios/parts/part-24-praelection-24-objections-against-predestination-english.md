[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 137 | De Prædestinatione p. 93 (from Lecture XXIV opening)]

And thus far we have handed down the whole doctrine of predestination with
the precision that God has granted us. It now remains for us to set forth and
answer certain arguments of those who oppose this doctrine, so that its truth
may thereby be all the more firmly established and may shine all the more
brightly, when it becomes clear that our adversaries have been unable to
bring forward or devise anything of almost any weight to assail it. Moreover,
so that this disputation does not wander into uncertainty but is confined
within fixed boundaries and limits, we shall arrange the objections against
the doctrine already delivered by us into these four classes. The first will
contain arguments attacking the very form of the divine decree. By abolishing
reprobation, some people attempt in these arguments to undermine election as
well by one and the same act, since election cannot stand without its other
part. The second class will consist of arguments that can be referred to the
impelling cause of each decree. The third class of objections will concern
the means that God wisely ordained for the execution of his decree. The last
will contain arguments directed against the immutable condition of both the
elect and the reprobate.

[Marginalia: Transition. Lecture XXIV. Principal division of the objections
that follow.]

As regards the first class, first, the adversaries of this truth clamor and
make accusations rather than objections. By these adversaries I mean the
Synusiasts and Pantachusians, for the Scholastics and the papal theologians
themselves—at least the less corrupt and perverse among them—partly agree with
us and with this truth. They accuse us of teaching that by a certain absolute
decree of God a few particular people were elected to salvation, while the
rest were appointed to the fire of hell, God having created them in order to
destroy them. Since this accusation consists of three parts, our answer must
also have three members. As for the absolute decree that they charge us with,
we make this distinction. “Absolute” can be taken in two ways. First, it can
mean that which has no relation to anything, and which acknowledges or
includes no higher cause, as it is opposed to what is relative. Second, it
can mean that which is opposed to the hypothetical or conditional, which
posits and determines nothing simply, but only under a condition. In the
former sense we deny that either part of the divine decree is absolute—I
mean either election or reprobation—but affirm that each includes its own
causes, reasons, and relations, as we have already taught abundantly above.
In the latter sense, however, we assert that all God’s decrees are absolute.
For to say that God determined nothing simply, but only conditionally and
hypothetically—on this arrangement, “If people are of one kind or another,
or if they do this or that, they will be saved, but if not they will
perish”—is to invent a decree by which our God left everything as if in doubt
and suspense, determining nothing certain and settled about anyone. This is
really to abolish every decree, or to make his eternal and immutable will,
to which all his ways have been known from all eternity, depend on the
uncertain outcome of things themselves or on the human will. That is, it
makes the first cause of all things depend on those secondary and contingent
causes. Nothing more absurd could be devised, not even by Epicurus with his
fortuitous collision of atoms.

[Marginalia: First class of objections. Objection 1. First member of the
answer. The distinction of “absolute.” Confirmation.]

Next, as regards the small or great number on either side, that article has
been answered above, both by clear testimonies of sacred Scripture and by
setting forth the causes of the divine counsel, so far as it was lawful for
us. Third, as for the claim that we teach that God created the greatest part
of humanity in order to destroy it eternally, we answer as follows. First,
the two acts of reprobation must be carefully distinguished, as was done
clearly enough above; in this way the accusation will readily be answered.
Next, we say that it is false and slanderous to assign the destruction of
people as the cause of their creation. We do not speak so carelessly. We do
indeed confess that the reprobate are created and raised up by God, as the
apostle says, so that in their just destruction God’s glorious justice, and
indeed his wrath and power, may be displayed. The apostle himself expressly
taught us this in Romans 9, though the innards of every creature should be
tormented and burst at it,

[Marginalia: Rejection of the second member. Third member. The destruction of
the creature is not properly the final cause of the divine decree.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 138 | De Prædestinatione p. 94]

in the person of the tyrant Pharaoh. Indeed, Solomon says in Proverbs 16 that
they were made for the day of evil. But we say that it is one thing to will
to destroy a person as a person or a creature, and another to punish a sinner
as a sinner. God has justly decreed the latter for the end already stated,
but the former is incompatible with divine goodness. God does not delight
even in the death of a sinner insofar as the sinner is his work and creature,
although his justice requires that the person suffer eternal punishment
insofar as he is a sinner. Therefore, the perverse expression which they
slanderously fasten upon us must be corrected.

[Marginalia: Proverbs 16:4.]

In truth, whatever these people shout and rail against us, they plainly
disclose their evil and their soul’s inward disease: they cannot rest in
God’s own counsel, so clearly revealed in his word, nor humbly subject their
mind and judgment to it. They should be angry with God rather than with us
because he did not conform his decree concerning his creatures to their will
and judgment. This is the real source of all their complaint and accusation.
Since they cannot sincerely submit themselves to God in this matter, they
attempt to drag his word and will over to their own perverse opinion. Let it
suffice to have warned you in passing about the perverse disposition of
nearly all heretics.

[Marginalia: Note the censure.]

The second objection, intended to establish what they call universal
election and to abolish the decree of reprobation, is this: If God wills all
people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth, and likewise
if he does not will any to perish but all to proceed to repentance, then he
has rejected or reprobated none from eternal salvation, but has instead
elected all to salvation, because he wills all to be saved. Therefore he has
decreed to save them, for who resists his will? But the former is true, as
stated in 1 Timothy 2 and 2 Peter 3. Therefore the latter is also true. There
is a twofold way of answering this objection: first concerning “all,” and
then concerning God’s will. That is, the antecedent of that inference can be
distinguished in various ways, any one of which will readily answer the
argument.

[Marginalia: Objection 2. 1 Timothy 2:4. 2 Peter 3:9.]

First, we say that both passages should be understood not universally of all
Adam’s descendants and all ages of the world, but properly of those who have
lived in this last age of the world after Christ. This is evident from a
comparison with other passages. In particular, Acts 17 says, “Having passed
over and overlooked the times of that ignorance, God now announces to all
people everywhere that they should repent.” Likewise Colossians 1 speaks of
“the mystery which was hidden from ages and generations but has now been
made manifest to his saints, to whom God willed to make known among the
Gentiles the riches of that glorious mystery.” It also says, “We proclaim
him, admonishing everyone and teaching everyone, that we may present everyone
perfect in Jesus Christ.” Likewise, in 2 Corinthians 6, the apostle expounds
and applies to the Corinthians that passage from Isaiah 49, “In the time of
good pleasure I heard you, and on the day of salvation I helped you.” “Behold,
now is the acceptable time,” he says; “behold, now is the day of salvation!”
That is, it is the time of the New Testament, in which the gospel must be
preached to all nations. The Lord himself defines and designates this time
very clearly in John 12: “And I,” he says, “when I have been lifted up, will
draw all things to myself.” Therefore, even if we concede that God now, in
the time of the New Testament, wills all to be saved, it by no means follows
that he always willed the same throughout all the past ages of the world.
Consequently, sayings of this kind need not be extended to absolutely all
people, but only to those who lived after Christ’s coming in the flesh. Thus,
at least as regards the rest, election and reprobation will remain.

[Marginalia: First member of the answer. Interpretation 1. Acts 17:30.
Colossians 1:26, 27. 2 Corinthians 6:2. See also 1 Timothy 2:6, where he calls
Christ “a testimony in its own times.” John 12:32.]

Next, passages of this kind may be expounded with Augustine in Letter 107 in
the way that the apostle’s statement in 1 Corinthians 15 is expounded: “Just
as all die in Adam, so all will be made alive in Christ.” This does not mean
that absolutely all people will be made alive, but that none will receive
eternal life except those who are in Christ. As many, therefore, as are made
alive are made alive in Christ—all those, namely, who are in Christ—just as
all those who are in Adam die. Likewise, when it is said that God wills all
to be saved, it must be understood of all who are saved, so that we know that
all who are saved obtain salvation only because God wills it.

[Marginalia: Interpretation 2. 1 Corinthians 15:22.]

But we more willingly embrace another of Augustine’s interpretations, which
he sets forth in chapter 103 of the *Enchiridion to Laurence*: that we read
and translate not so much “all” as “any.” This is, as people commonly say,
not so much every individual of each class as every class of individuals:
people from any status, nation, and condition, without exception or
distinction of any lot or order. Augustine therefore says: “It was said, ‘who
wills all people to be saved,’ not because there was no person whom he did
not will to be saved—he was unwilling to perform mighty miracles among those
whom he says would have repented if he had performed them—but so that by ‘all
people’ we may understand every kind of person, distributed through every
sort of difference: kings, private persons, nobles, commoners, high and low,
learned and unlearned, the able-bodied and the weak, the intelligent, the
slow-witted, fools, the rich, the poor, those of middling means, males,
females, infants, children, adolescents, young adults, older people, and the
aged; those constituted in all languages, all customs, all arts, all
occupations, and in an innumerable variety of wills and consciences, and
whatever other differences exist among people.”

[Marginalia: Interpretation 3. Augustine, *Enchiridion*, chapter 103.]

Aristotle himself recognizes and explains this equivocation in the word
“all” in book 2, chapter 2 of the *Politics*. He says that its doubleness or
ambiguity—by which it is taken sometimes collectively for all considered
together and universally, and sometimes distributively for each
individual—will give rise not only to fallacies and contentious syllogisms in
the schools, but also to quarrels and disputes in that Socratic republic whose
model he refutes there. I admit that here the word has another meaning,
different from both those senses. In the same way it is said in Matthew 9
that the Lord “went through the cities preaching the gospel and healing every
disease and every sickness among the people”—that is, any disease, and every
kind of disease. Haymo also says that the apostle there put the whole for a
part, as in the Gospel where the Lord says, “If I am lifted up from the earth,
I will draw all things to myself”; for he does not draw, nor has he drawn, all
people to himself, but all the elect, from every kind and nation. Likewise,
Jerome, commenting on Romans 5, interprets the statement “every person is a
liar” of a large part. “For elsewhere too,” he says, “the apostle writes,
‘teaching every person and admonishing every person,’ not because they taught
everyone—for how many there are who to this day have heard neither the
apostle’s teaching nor his name—but because he teaches and admonishes all who
are in the Church.”

[Marginalia: Aristotle, *Politics* 2.2, near the beginning. Matthew 9:35.
Haymo on 1 Timothy 2. Jerome on Romans 5.]

Finally, the passage in Peter especially, which is enlarged by the contrasting
member when he denies that God wills any person to perish but wills all to
come to repentance, appears less suitably explained by the preceding

[Marginalia: Interpretation 4.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 139 | De Prædestinatione p. 95]

interpretation. We ought to explain it according to the judgment of charity
expressed by the apostle, based upon the divine command which the apostles
had to call all people to faith and repentance, and not according to a
judgment of God’s hidden counsel and the infallible certainty dependent upon
it. Peter, therefore, like Paul and the others, considers God’s revealed will
that the gospel be preached to all and that all be called without anyone’s
exception or exclusion from God’s saving grace. From this he concludes that
God wills all people to come to salvation and the knowledge of the truth,
while leaving God’s secret judgments to God himself. Likewise, Paul calls all
who profess faith in Christ in the churches to which he writes “elect,” even
though many of them afterwards fell away from sound and right faith. He does
so not because he himself was mistaken or deceived others in this respect,
but rather because he wished to teach us that our ignorance of God’s secret
counsel and hidden judgments must neither obstruct the affection of charity
in us nor prevent us from thinking and speaking about others according to
its rule.

[Marginalia: Confirmation.]

Therefore, just as we ought to regard those whom we see have been called by
God, have followed his call, and have embraced faith in Christ as people made
partakers of salvation and consequently elected by God for this very end, so
too we ought by the same rule of charity to judge that God wills to save and
bring to repentance those to whom he has commanded the gospel to be preached
and whom he has commanded to be called. Since he has commanded that all be
called without distinction, waits for all so kindly and patiently, and
invites them to conversion and repentance, we, together with the apostles
themselves, must conclude that God wills all people to be saved and to come
to repentance. This concerns our own affection and ministry, for he wills us
to judge this way about everyone. It also concerns those who are themselves
called and invited, for he wills them to determine that his will toward them
is that they are called, invited, and awaited by him so that they may be
saved, and that they should not inquire more deeply into his will concerning
them than what he has revealed to them. Thus Augustine says in *On Rebuke and
Grace*: “Not knowing who belongs to the number of the predestined and who does
not, we ought to be so moved here by the affection of charity that we will
all to be saved. Likewise, insofar as it concerns us, who are unable to
distinguish the predestined from the non-predestined, and for this reason
ought to will all to be saved, medicinally severe correction must be applied
by us to all so that they do not perish.”

[Marginalia: Argument from an equal case. Augustine, *On Rebuke and Grace*,
chapters 15 and 16.]

Next, as regards the latter distinction concerning the will by which God can
be said to will all to be saved, I remember touching upon it above, and so
there is no reason for me to linger over it here. God does not will salvation
in all people in the same manner, degree, or end, but differently in the
elect and in the reprobate who have been outwardly called—that is, those who
live in the time and light of the gospel. For, as we warned above in the first
distinction, all those universal sayings, however they may be distinguished,
must always be restricted to the times of the New Testament. In the latter,
God wills only by commanding and offering the outward means of salvation,
without the inward grace of his Spirit, and so by justly requiring from them
what, by his secret decree, he does not will mercifully to bestow, but instead
wills to leave them in their own blindness and hardness. In the elect,
however, he wills in an entirely different way: by decreeing effectually to
confer upon them and bring about what he commands and requires, and to do so
mercifully according to the counsel of his election. Why he wills outwardly
to call others whom he has decreed to forsake has nonetheless its own certain
and just causes and reasons serving the execution of his counsel, as we taught
above. He does not will to disclose in this life his whole counsel concerning
the election or rejection of people, which he has decreed to reserve for the
day when the books will be opened. Meanwhile, he wills people to attend to
the will revealed in his word. His will revealed in the word is that all
should be called without distinction and brought to faith after Christ’s
coming into the world. If very many who are called are nevertheless unwilling
to come, God has willed to forewarn and fortify us from the same word so that
we do not suppose anything happens apart from or contrary to his counsel.
Whether those called come or do not come, nothing in this respect happens
apart from his eternal and secret decree by which he has wisely ordained all
things concerning everyone. Those who do come and persevere must attribute
this only to his gift and grace, for he called them according to his purpose.
Those who do not come, or who later withdraw and fall away, must attribute
this only to their own fault and malice. Thus the latter may complain only
about themselves, while the former glory not in themselves but in the Lord.
Let this suffice concerning the second argument.

[Marginalia: Second member of the answer. The distinction of the will.
Rejection of an objection.]

The third argument for asserting universal election, at least with respect
to all those to whom Christ’s gospel is preached, is drawn from universal
redemption. For if Christ effectually redeemed all people, or effectually
died for all, then plainly all were also elected, as is clear. But the former
is true; therefore so is the latter. The minor premise is proved as follows,
by one argument in substance, but one that can be framed in two ways. The
first is a simple proof: Whatever each person is bound to believe for
salvation is necessarily true. But each person—at least each of those to whom
the gospel is preached—is bound, for salvation, to believe that Christ died
effectually for him. Therefore this is necessarily true. The other proof
leads to an unacceptable consequence, in this form: Whoever is condemned
because he did not believe that he was effectually redeemed by Christ is
condemned because he did not believe what is false—at least according to the
opinion of those who deny that Christ effectually redeemed any of those who
are condemned. But all unbelievers are condemned because, as the Lord says in
John 3, “they did not believe in the name of the only-begotten Son of God.”
Therefore all unbelievers are condemned because they did not believe what is
false. The conclusion is absurd, so one of the premises must be false. It is
not the minor premise, which we have expressly from the Lord himself in the
Gospel. Therefore it is the major. Thus the hypothesis upon which our
position rests is false—namely, that Christ did not effectually redeem any of
those who are condemned—and its contradictory is true: that Christ
effectually redeemed even some of those who are condemned, far be the
blasphemous expression. In my judgment this sophism, although by no means
persuasive—for I think it will not in any respect shake or move even the
conscience of those who lack the skill to answer it expertly—is nevertheless
woven of a considerably subtler thread than the other arguments devised on
this subject.

[Marginalia: Argument or objection 3. Note the sophism of Ægidius Hunnius.
John 3:18. Our judgment.]

We therefore answer the simple proof, first, by distinguishing the major
premise according to the common distinction of both truth and necessity into
what is simple and what is from a hypothesis, in this manner: Whatever anyone
is bound

[Marginalia: First member of the answer. First distinction.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 140 | De Prædestinatione p. 96]

to believe simply and with no condition posited is also simply true; this we
concede. But if one is bound to believe something only on this hypothesis—if
he wills to be saved, or in order that he may be saved—it is by no means
necessary for it to be true simply and without any hypothesis. It suffices
for it to be true only on the same hypothesis, with the same condition added:
namely, if he is saved, or if he will be saved. We prefer this answer to the
others as the easiest, most direct, and clearest of all.

It is also possible to answer by another distinction of the extreme term in
the same major premise, as follows. It is true in at least some manner,
namely, with respect to merit and sufficiency. Yet it is by no means necessary
for it to be true in every manner, namely, also with respect to efficacy and
application. For in order that what someone is bound to believe may be true
in this way, it is not enough that he is bound and obliged to believe it; he
must, in addition, actually believe. Since he does not believe, it is not
surprising if the obligation to believe indeed remains, but the thing which
he remains obliged to believe becomes false with respect to him—that is, with
respect to its application and efficacy toward him. For the truth of this
application toward him arises from and depends upon his own faith: by faith
he applies to himself the thing he believes and renders it effectual.
Therefore, by believing or not believing, he renders that thing true or false
for himself. It is easy and ready at hand for anyone to apply these
distinctions to the objection’s conclusion, and so we shall forgo that labor.

[Marginalia: Distinction 2.]

Finally, there are those who further distinguish both the major and the minor
premise as follows. They say that it is true with respect to God’s intention
in imposing the obligation, but not always true with respect to the outcome.
They illustrate this with the example that Jonah preached to the Ninevites
that destruction would come within forty days, and was therefore bound to
believe this by the will of God obliging him to obey, although it was not true
with respect to the outcome. I confess that I do not adequately understand
this distinction. I see clearly enough that God’s command thereby obliged
Jonah to believe, just as it obliges the reprobate to believe the gospel; but
I do not see how that obligation renders the thing itself true, which is the
knot that still remains to be untied. If it was false with respect to the
outcome, it was always false, whether Jonah was bound to believe it or not,
because Jonah’s preaching was nothing other than the prediction of that
outcome, which nevertheless did not follow.

[Marginalia: Distinction 3. Our censure.]

If someone should object concerning this example of Jonah, “Jonah was bound
to believe that Nineveh would be overthrown within forty days, because he was
sent by God to preach this to them. But this was false. Therefore, by God’s
command and will he was bound to believe what was false,” we think the answer
should be given as follows. He was bound to believe it in this manner: not
simply and absolutely, but with the exception of an impediment, namely, the
Ninevites’ repentance, which he knew well enough would hinder their
destruction and the effect of that denunciation. But this was false, not
simply and absolutely, but upon the positing of an impediment, namely, the
conversion of the Ninevites. Therefore he was bound to believe it not simply
and absolutely, but with the exception or removal of the impediment; and
similarly, it was false not simply and absolutely, but upon the contrary
positing and supposition of the impediment. There is nothing absurd in this.
It would only be absurd for him or anyone else to be bound to believe simply
what is simply false, or, with the exception and removal of an impediment, to
be bound to believe something that remains false even when the impediment is
excepted and removed. This has no place in this example or under any other
command of God.

[Marginalia: Answer to the example of Jonah. First distinction. Amplification
from the contrary.]

Nor is there any reason for someone to insist here that it will therefore
follow that God made his decree concerning the preservation or destruction
of the Ninevites depend upon their will and conversion—that is, that he
determined nothing certain about them in his counsel, but only something
hypothetical and conditional: “I shall destroy them unless their repentance
introduces an impediment, but if they repent, I shall spare them.” You
yourself condemned this above as absurd, because it makes the immutable
decree of divine providence depend upon the uncertain will of human beings.
We answer that nothing of this kind follows. The distinction given, as is
clear to anyone considering it, agrees and is entirely consistent with God’s
simple and unmoved decree to preserve the Ninevites from that disaster. In
his most wise counsel, he had ordained this very denunciation of destruction
as the means by which he would bring them to repentance and, once they had
been brought to repentance, preserve them. He had decreed to spare the
repentant, so that by their example he might reproach and heighten the iron
hardness and impenitence of his own most obstinate people, by comparison with
the contrary faith and repentance revealed in the Gentile Ninevites.
Therefore he willed the Ninevites to repent, and for this end he willed their
sudden destruction to be announced to them. It follows only that the most
wise God held not his own will or the outcome of his counsel, but the mind of
his servant Jonah, in suspense for a time and made it depend, so to speak,
for a while upon the positing or removal of that impediment. Jonah did not
know whether the Ninevites would repent or not, and consequently what God had
determined within himself concerning them—whether to preserve or destroy
them—because God judged it fitting not to disclose his whole counsel to Jonah
before the event itself. There is nothing absurd in this, as we taught
abundantly above.

[Marginalia: Objection. Rejection. Note the end. Concession.]

We can also formulate our answer with this distinction: Jonah was bound to
believe it because of God’s command imposing this duty upon him, not as a
decree of God determining with certainty what would happen to the Ninevites,
but as a threat and a denunciation of just judgment. There is nothing absurd
in such a threat being false with respect to the event, because it always
includes the condition of repentance, whether tacitly or expressly.

[Marginalia: Second distinction.]

Now to return to our subject: the minor premise of the same simple proof—for
thus far we have been occupied with the major—may be distinguished with some
writers in the following way. By God’s command everyone is bound to believe
that he has been redeemed through Christ, yet, they say, the elect and the
reprobate are bound to this in a different manner and for a different end.
The former are bound so that by believing they may become partakers of
salvation. The latter are bound so that by not believing they may both become
liable to God’s just judgment and be rendered without excuse before him, and
this by the intention of God himself who commands belief or requires faith.
For a twofold divine command can be posited. One is a command of obedience,
whose act God wills in everyone—that is, which he simply requires from
everyone. Such are the precepts of the moral law, and also faith in Christ
with respect to everyone to whom the gospel is preached. The other is a
command only of proof or testing, whose act

[Marginalia: Resumption of the subject. Fourth distinction. The distinction
of divine commands.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 141 | De Prædestinatione p. 97]

or execution he does not will, but only a display of obedience and a trial of
faith, such as the command to sacrifice Isaac and any similar commands found
in Scripture. In turn, as regards the command of obedience, God does not will
the act or performance itself in the same manner in the elect and the
reprobate, nor for the same end. In the reprobate, he wills obedience only by
commanding, enjoining, and requiring it as a thing holy and just in itself,
and by approving the thing itself as conformed to his justice, even though he
knows that they will never perform it. In the elect, however, he additionally
wills it by giving what he commands, performing what he requires, bringing
about what he approves, and paying on their behalf what they themselves owe.
As regards the end, in the reprobate he wills by commanding and requiring,
partly so that he may restrain them by a certain outward discipline and
partly so that he may expose their innate disobedience and unbelief, convict
them of rebellion and obstinacy, and shut their mouths before his judgment,
teaching that they perish by their own fault because they were willing
neither to fulfill his law nor to embrace by faith the grace offered to them
in the gospel. In the elect, however, he wills by performing, granting faith,
and beginning new obedience, so that through these means he may bring them to
gratuitous salvation. Thus in the former he wills for the glory of his
justice, but in the latter for manifesting the praise and riches of his
glorious grace.

[Marginalia: Subdistinction.]

If you infer that the reprobate, when left to themselves, could not obey the
divine commands either by doing or by believing, we indeed confess it, but we
assert that they were bound to obey nevertheless. Their inability, which
they brought upon themselves by their own fault when they lost the ability
originally granted by God, must be imputed not to God but to themselves.
Consequently, they can neither be excused before God by that inability nor
be released from the debt that God justly requires from them. Moreover, what
they omit of their duty and commit against God’s law or gospel, they do not
do under compulsion or unwillingly, but gladly and of their own accord. All
the while they are conscious of their freedom, although falsely, by which, as
it seems to them, they could observe God’s law if they were willing—as is
clear from the Israelites voluntarily taking this upon themselves in
Deuteronomy 5—and could believe in Christ preached to them. Consequently, no
pretext for sin and no excuse for unbelief is left to them. Bernard rightly
says in Sermon 70 on the *Song of Songs*: “The teacher was not unaware that
the weight of the command exceeded human strength, but he judged it useful
that by this very fact they should be admonished of their insufficiency.
Therefore, by commanding impossible things, he made people not transgressors
but humble, so that every mouth might be stopped.” Thus the first, simple
proof has been abundantly answered and refuted.

[Marginalia: Rejection of an objection. Deuteronomy 5:27. Bernard.]

As regards the major premise of the latter syllogism leading to an
unacceptable consequence, it will be easy to answer from what was said, in
this way. If the premise is understood as, “He is condemned because he did
not believe what is false simply and from the nature of the thing to be
believed, without taking unbelief into account,” the major premise is denied.
But if it is understood as, “He is condemned because he did not believe what
is false on the hypothesis of his unbelief and with his faithlessness taken
into account, which is the proximate cause of that falsity, a falsity which
otherwise belongs to the thing itself only accidentally,” there is nothing
absurd in it. For faith renders true, and faithlessness and unbelief render
false, the same proposition or assertion applied to a certain designated
person, insofar as it is such—that is, by reason of that application. I mean
the faith and faithlessness of the person in relation to whom that proposition
is said to be true or false. Therefore, there seems to be nothing absurd in
someone being condemned because he did not believe something which, with
respect to himself, he himself rendered false by not believing, but which
would otherwise have been true for him if he had believed. This was already
explained above by the second distinction previously given.

[Marginalia: Second member of the answer. Prior distinction.]

Nor is there reason for anyone to insist and object that we make the truth of
the divine promise depend upon human faith or faithlessness and render it
changeable like a chameleon—that is, now true and now false according as
people believe or reject it. It follows only that the truth of that particular
application, by which God’s promise is restricted and adapted to one
particular person or another, depends upon his faith, so that unless he
receives it by faith it cannot be true for him—that is, cannot pertain to
him. This is so far from conflicting with the sure and immovable truth of the
promise itself as it is set forth in the gospel that it confirms it all the
more. For an applied promise is not proposed in the gospel to one person or
another except under the condition of faith, for example, “If you truly
believe, Christ has effectually redeemed you and by his death has acquired
life and salvation for you.” Otherwise, gospel promises are formulated
universally and indefinitely with respect to all believers: “Whoever believes
will be saved.” This is the same as saying, “Whoever believes and has
embraced Christ’s benefit by true faith is one for whom Christ specifically
died, and Christ effectually redeemed him.” Therefore it is not surprising
if the unbeliever renders false for himself and with respect to himself what
is set forth in the gospel as true only with respect to each believer—that
is, if he shows that it in no way pertains to him—while the truth of the
gospel promise meanwhile remains always firm and unmoved.

[Marginalia: Rejection of an objection. Confirmation of the answer.
Conclusion of the answer.]

The same distinction can also be conceived and expressed in a shorter and
more direct way, by denying that he is condemned because he did not believe
what is false insofar as it is proposed to him as something to be believed.
For it is not false in this respect, as it is the object of his faith—that is,
of the faith that ought to be in him. Rather, because he did not believe what
is false insofar as it is rejected by him through faithlessness and is the
object of his unbelief: this we willingly admit as in no way absurd.

[Marginalia: Distinction 2.]

In a word, first, the promise must here be distinguished from its application
to a particular person, because the promise can be true in itself while its
application to this or that person is false. The truth of the promise itself
depends upon the firm and unmoved will of the one promising, but sincere
faith that receives and embraces the things offered makes the application
true, whether the person has that faith from himself or from elsewhere—for
this is irrelevant to our present subject. Second, when the promise is false
with respect to a person, that falsity does not belong to the promise in
itself, nor even to the promise as applied to an unbelieving person—that is,
it does not arise from the nature of the thing itself. For that promise is by
nature fitted and disposed to be true of that very person no less than of any
other. Rather, the falsity happens to it through the fault of the person who
does not believe, so that the promise adapted even to that unbeliever deserves
to be called false only accidentally.

subtlety, but are easily shattered because of their emptiness. They cannot
