# BELLARMINE ENERVATED

## Book One: On the Word of God

### CHAPTER I

#### On Bellarmine's Confession Concerning Holy Scripture

##### THESIS I

As we undertake a concise examination of the things Bellarmine opposed to the
Protestants' confession from Scripture or reason, we encounter first, by way
of introduction, that confession of Bellarmine which must be carefully
weighed: *The prophetic and apostolic books are the true Word of God and the
certain and stable rule of faith*, book 1, chapter 1. Both in itself and in the
proofs appended to it in chapter 2, this confession strongly opposes the Roman
Catholics' cause in various ways.

2. For, since all Scripture is here divided into *prophetic and apostolic
books*, we first take up this consequence: those books commonly called
Apocrypha among us are neither prophetic nor apostolic; therefore, they are
not parts of Holy Scripture or of the written Word of God.

3. No one will affirm that they are apostolic. That they are not prophetic is
proved: 1. From the fact that they were not delivered to the Jews, to whom all
the oracles of God set forth through the prophets were entrusted, Romans 3:2.
For no books other than those written in Hebrew were delivered to the Hebrews,
which cannot be shown of these

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 12 | printed p. 2 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

books. Nor were they ever in the Hebrew canon, of which the Jewish Church was
the most faithful custodian. 2. From the fact that they were not among those
prophetic books which Christ approved by his testimony as the only true and
genuine ones, Luke 24:44; Matthew 13:35; 24:15; and 27:34. These were no other
than the books from which the Jews thought they had eternal life, something
they never thought about these books.

4. It also follows of itself that, if these books are the Word of God because
they are prophetic and apostolic, this is so because the Word was revealed to
them by God through the Spirit, Ephesians 3:5, and they also spoke and wrote as
they were moved by the same Holy Spirit, 2 Peter 1:21 and 3:2. Therefore, the
word of the Roman Pontiff, or of any human beings who are not moved by the
Spirit of God in a similar manner, must not be regarded as the Word of God. But
if the Roman Pontiffs were moved by the Spirit of God in a manner similar to
the prophets and apostles, no reason could then be given why the rescripts of
the pontiffs and their decretal epistles should not be part of the canon and
of Holy Scripture. If there were also an unwritten Word of God deposited with
the Roman Pontiff, then for the Church's good he would be moved by the same
Spirit to commit that same Word to writing, after the example of the prophets
and apostles.

5. In the second place, we take up these consequences concerning the *rule*.
1. A rule or measure of a thing must be *known and readily usable*, Suárez,
*Metaphysics*, part 2, disputation 11, section 3, because it must be suited to
making the thing known. Since Scripture, therefore, is the rule, it is not
obscure and difficult as the Roman Catholics maintain. 2. A rule of proportion
has parity with the thing of which it is the rule. Therefore, if Scripture is
the rule of faith, faith does not extend beyond Scripture, and thus unwritten
traditions have no bearing on faith. 4. A rule which is such by its nature
possesses in itself the character of an exemplary cause and so is prior in
nature to that which it regulates. Therefore, if Scripture is the rule of
faith, it is also prior to faith. If it is prior to faith, then it is prior to
the faithful as well; and if prior to the faithful, then prior to the Church,
which is the assembly of the faithful. Thus, the Roman Church will fall from
that higher place in which it customarily places itself by a certain wicked

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 13 | printed p. 3 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

presumption, as though it existed before and above Scripture. 4. A rule admits
of no division—that is, it is constituted so that it may not be increased or
diminished in the least. Those who add their traditions to Scripture, or take
away anything prescribed in it, therefore violate Scripture. The Roman
Pontiffs are manifestly guilty of both crimes. 5. If Scripture is the rule of
faith, then those who can err in the exposition of Scripture can also err in
faith and commend a false rule of faith to others. It is plainer than to need
proof, however, that the Roman Pontiff and the Roman Church interpret many
parts of Scripture foolishly and impiously. The Roman Pontiff can therefore
err disgracefully in his decrees, even those concerning faith and morals. 6.
Since the nature of this rule is to be a precept, whatever is admitted without
the direction of this rule, whatever appearance it may otherwise have, is an
anomaly and aberration. Accordingly, both all the Roman Catholics' works of
supererogation and most of their dogmas—about the worship of images and
creatures, purgatory, the Mass itself, and the Roman Pontiff—must necessarily
be acknowledged as errors, at least. 7. Since Scripture is not one precept but
a system of precepts directed toward the same end (namely, that we should live
well toward God), it must contain in itself the whole doctrine of theology.
Whatever the Roman Pontiff presses upon us without Scripture must therefore be
foreign to true theology. 8. Finally, since it is the rule of faith, those who
snatch this rule from the hands of the faithful must be enemies of the
faithful.

6. We also take up those qualities Bellarmine concedes to Scripture: that it
is the *certain and stable rule of faith*. For: 1. If it is a certain rule in
itself, then it can make us certain of the will of God without definitions by
the Church. 2. Then all the uncertain and deceitful decrees of human beings
must be examined by this rule. 3. Then it does not admit various and uncertain
meanings. 4. Then a certain determination of all controversies which arise
concerning faith must be sought from Scripture. 5. If it is also a stable
rule, then nothing which was unknown in the age of the apostles is necessary
or useful for us to believe.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 14 | printed p. 4 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Then pontifical dispensations are pernicious impostures. 7. Finally, the
Church's stability depends entirely upon its constant adherence to this rule.

7. In most of the arguments by which that confession is confirmed, it will at
once be apparent from this brief comparison that Bellarmine expressly cuts the
throat of his own cause all the more. Bell.: *Moses, the prophets, Christ
himself, John, and the apostles either confirmed divine dogmas themselves from
Scripture or certainly exhorted others to read Scripture.* Prot.: But the
Roman Catholics confess that they cannot confirm from any Scripture their
invocation of the saints in heaven, worship of sacred images, offering of an
unbloody sacrifice for the living and the dead, and similar dogmas and
practices; Azor, *Moral Institutes*, part 2, book 4, chapter 18. Nor do they
exhort others to read Scripture; instead, they bar and deter them.

8. Bell.: *Holy Moses teaches quite plainly that controversies arising among
the people of God are to be adjudicated from the law of the Lord*, Deuteronomy
17. Prot.: He therefore plainly condemns our Roman Catholics, who do not wish
controversies to be settled from Scripture and deny that this can be done.

9. Bell.: *Christ refers the Pharisees not to some inward spirit but to the
divine Scriptures*, John 5. Prot.: Those who refer people in doubt to the Roman
Pontiff, the Fathers, councils, or anything else besides Scripture therefore
do not follow Christ.

10. Bell.: *John the Baptist employed as the witness of his commission not the
judgment of an inward spirit but Isaiah the prophet*, John 1. Prot.: Let the
Roman Pontiffs, therefore, produce some prophet or apostle as a witness to that
transcendent power which they claim for themselves—but without fraud and
deception and without making both themselves and their audience laugh.

11. Bell.: *It is recorded of the Bereans, not without praise, that when they
had heard the apostle Paul, they searched the Scriptures daily to see whether
these things were so*, Acts 17. Prot.: It therefore cannot be recorded without
the greatest shame that the Roman Catholics permit very few to read Scripture,
so highly commend implicit faith, blind obedience, and devout ignorance, and
cannot endure the examination of Scripture in any way.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 15 | printed p. 5 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

12. Bell.: *The apostles everywhere proclaim the authority of Scripture.*
Prot.: Those Roman Catholics, therefore, are anything but apostolic, since
they blaspheme Scripture as *a mute teacher, a dead and killing letter, dead
ink, ink-born theology, a wax nose, or a sailor's trousers*.

13. Bell.: *Who will assure me that the Anabaptist is not lying when he says
that the Spirit has breathed upon him?* Prot.: Who will likewise assure us
that the Roman Pontiff and his Roman Catholics are not lying when they say
that they have received by the Lord's living voice that vast heap of doctrines
and rites, although they are utterly bare and destitute of every testimony of
Scripture?

14. Bell.: *The truth of the prophecies, their incredible harmony, God
himself, Scripture itself, and countless miracles testify that Scripture is
most certain and most true.* Prot.: There are therefore other reasons, besides
the authority of the Church, by which faith is stirred up in us and the
certainty of Scripture is established. Indeed, in Scripture itself are many
indications of divine wisdom, truth, and perfection, while the Church remains
entirely silent.

15. Bell.: *Since Holy Scripture is the most certain and safest rule, someone
who neglects it and entrusts himself to the judgment of an inward spirit, often
deceptive and always uncertain, will certainly not be sane.* Prot.: If it is
the safest rule, then the Roman Catholics lie gravely and perniciously when
they loudly declare that the common people learn all heresies and many evil
practices from reading Scripture. If this rule is also the safest, to what good
end do they press other rules upon us? Do they love to play games in so serious
a matter and amid such great danger? Finally, if it is madness for someone to
entrust himself to a deceptive and uncertain judgment, who can excuse from
madness those who suspend their faith upon the decision of the Roman Pontiff?

16. Therefore, the conclusion Bellarmine directs against the Libertines seems
to apply equally to the Roman Catholics: *Those who despise Holy Scripture
fight against Moses, the prophets, the apostles, Christ himself, and God the
Father.* To this we add another conclusion:

*Namely, that the Roman Catholics' religion has no certain and stable rule.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 16 | printed p. 6 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

For first, they establish as the *universal rule of faith all those things
which are proposed for belief by the Church as revealed by God*, while leaving
uncertain which was the true Church, which things it proposed previously, and
which it will propose hereafter. Second, they establish seven grades of
propositions to be believed: *1. Those which are plainly contained in
Scripture. 2. Those which are plainly deduced from it. 3. Those which are
possessed by apostolic tradition. 4. Those which have been defined by lawful
councils confirmed by the pope. 5. Those which are proposed as matters of faith
by the Apostolic See. 6. Those which are proposed and asserted as matters of
faith by the unanimous consent of the Fathers. 7. Those which are evidently
deduced from the last three grades.* Vincentius Filiucius, following
Torquemada, Castro, and Cano, *On Cases of Conscience*, treatise 22, chapter 1.
Scripture, however, together with the things deduced from it, is rendered
uncertain by the canonization of an uncertain Latin version and by the
uncertain interpretation of the Church. Divine, apostolic, and ecclesiastical
traditions possess only as much credibility as their nearest transmitters.
How would an uneducated person be certain which councils are lawful, who is a
lawful pope, and that his authority is so great? Regarding the unanimous
consent of the Fathers, who will make us certain in which matters it is found,
and how it produces divine faith in them?

Induced by considerations of this sort, the Roman Catholics themselves
acknowledge that *something false can be proposed in such a way that—according
to their principles—a person is obligated to believe that falsehood with
divine faith. For example, if a heresy were proposed to a rustic by twenty
bishops reputed Catholic, as though it were Catholic truth and defined in a
council, it would be evidently credible to him and he would be bound to
believe it; otherwise, he would sin.* Alexander Pesantius, on 2.2, question 1,
article 1, disputation 4.

### CHAPTER II

#### On the Canonical and Apocryphal Books

##### THESIS I

Prot.: Holy Scripture is contained in those books which were written by holy
men of God, infallibly moved by the

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 17 | printed p. 7 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Holy Spirit both as to the matter and as to the form of writing, 2 Peter 1:21,
and delivered to the Church to be themselves the rule of faith and morals.
For this reason they are also called, by way of eminence, canonical, sacred,
and divine. These books are either those of the Old Testament, delivered by
the prophets to the ancient Church, or those of the New Testament, delivered
by the apostles to the Christian Church. We have no controversy with
Bellarmine about the canonical books of the New Testament. Nor is it disputed
concerning the Old Testament whether those books are truly canonical which
the Jews received formerly and the Reformed churches receive at present. But
because some other books were rashly joined to them by ignorant and incautious
people—first in Greek versions of the Bible and later in Latin versions after
the time of the prophets—and were held in some honor, they have therefore
recently been canonized by the Roman Catholics. Bellarmine affirms that this
was done lawfully and rightly, although not a few of the more learned Papists
themselves protest. We reject them because they were neither written nor
approved by the prophets; were not written in Hebrew; were neither delivered
to nor received by the Jewish Church; were in no way approved by Christ or the
apostles, but tacitly excluded by the canon they approved; do not reproduce
the majesty of Holy Scripture; and finally, in most of them various things are
contained which manifestly conflict with the prophetic books, with themselves,
and with the truth.

2. Bell.: *The ancient Fathers seek testimonies from these books.* Prot.:
Fourth Esdras is also cited more than once by Ambrose, and yet it is without
doubt not canonical. Third Esdras is cited by many Fathers—these are
Bellarmine's words in chapter 20. Genebrardus, a most zealous Papist, shows in
his *Chronology*, year 3742, that the same and other books, which by
Bellarmine's confession are apocryphal, are cited by still other Fathers.

3. Bell.: *The Fathers cited testimonies from some of these books as from
sacred, divine, and canonical Scriptures.* Prot.: 1. Many of the ancients,
such as Melito, Epiphanius, Hilary, Jerome, and Rufinus, openly followed the
Hebrews when expounding the canon of the Old Testament, among whom these books
are not in the canon. *Bellarmine confesses this in chapters 20 and 10.* Yet
these men

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 18 | printed p. 8 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

are among those who sometimes cited certain disputed books under these titles.
2. The same titles are also sometimes ascribed by certain ancients when citing
the writings of the Fathers, pontiffs, and councils. *Bellarmine affirms and
proves this here in chapter 4 and book 2, chapter 12.* There he also says that
Gratian calls the epistles of the pontiffs *Holy Scriptures to distinguish
them from profane writings, and canonical in a sense other than that in which
the Gospels and Psalms are so called.* Why, therefore, would the Fathers not
also have called these books sacred, divine, and canonical to distinguish
them from profane writings, in a sense other than that in which the Gospels
and Psalms are so called? These are also Bellarmine's own words in his little
book *On Ecclesiastical Writers*: *The book entitled The Shepherd, although it
is cited under the name of Scripture by Saint Irenaeus in book 4, chapter 37,
and is adduced in testimony by other most ancient writers, such as Clement of
Alexandria and Origen, nevertheless was never in the canon of the sacred and
divine books.* 3. They could not believe with divine faith that these books
were truly canonical, because the Church had not proposed this to them,
something absolutely necessary—according to the Roman Catholics' position—to
constitute an object of faith. But because they were joined with the canonical
books and were regarded as such by certain less learned people, they therefore
sometimes called them canonical, either when speaking popularly with the
common people, as they also did other apocryphal books—Esdras, Hermes, and *The
Shepherd*—or in a secondary, improper, and analogical sense.

4. Bell.: *The authority of these books is confirmed by apostolic tradition*,
chapters 10 and 20. Prot.: It is remarkable that under the New Testament the
apostles should have handed down a canon of the Old Testament different from
the one ever delivered to that Church. 2. Nothing of the sort appears in the
writings of the apostles. Rather, the contrary appears when they approve the
Scriptures as perfect, just as they had been received by the Jewish Church. 3.
None of the Fathers reports that these books were regarded as canonical from
apostolic tradition. 4. If such a tradition had been received in the Church
as apostolic, then many Fathers, in expounding the canon of the Old Testament,
would not have followed the Hebrews, who opposed this tradition. Yet we heard
Bellarmine confess above that they did so.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 19 | printed p. 9 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

5. Bell.: *The decrees of councils declared these books to be canonical.*
Prot.: While Jerome was alive, no general council had yet determined anything
about the books of Tobit, Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus, and Maccabees. Jerome
therefore asserts that they are not canonical. *These are Bellarmine's words
in chapter 20.* Augustine also knew that the matter had not yet been defined
by a general council. *Bellarmine, in the same place.*

6. Bell.: *The Third Council of Carthage places these books in the canon.*
Prot.: 1. This Council of Carthage was only provincial. 2. Its twenty-sixth
canon—*the bishop of the primatial see is not to be called the chief of the
priests, the supreme priest, or anything of that sort*—is not approved by the
Roman Catholics. 3. The sole reason the council gives for this judgment is
that they received those books from the Fathers to be read in the Church.
Thus, it calls them canonical equivocally, insofar as it wishes them to be
received with the canonical books so that they may be read in the churches.
4. If it understood them to be truly canonical, then it contradicted the
Council of Laodicea, and it also placed Third and Fourth Esdras in the canon,
which Bellarmine admits are apocryphal. For in the Greek and Latin edition
received at that time they were parts of the books of true Esdras. Finally, it
acted altogether rashly when, without any special reason, it placed in the
canon those books which had been issued five or six hundred years earlier and
which the Old Testament Church, the apostles, and all preceding councils had
left outside the canon.

7. Bell.: *The Church can declare which book is canonical: 1. From the
testimonies of the ancients. 2. From its similarity to other canonical books.
3. From the common sense and, as it were, taste of the Christian people. The
Church—namely, the Council of Trent—observed these three things in declaring
these books canonical.* Prot.: These three things were just as well known to
Melito, Hilary, Epiphanius, Jerome, Rufinus, the Council of Laodicea, and many
others who left these books outside the canon as they were to

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 20 | printed p. 10 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the Council of Trent. 2. No testimony can be produced even from one author who
lived in the age when these books were issued and who testifies that any of
them is canonical. 3. Bellarmine admits that the testimonies of the ancient
Fathers about these books vary. How, therefore, could the council declare from
their testimonies that these books were canonical rather than not canonical,
especially when those who deny that they are canonical, such as Jerome, speak
expressly, set themselves to treat this very matter, and give firm reasons for
their position? 6. The similarity of some of these books to the prophetic
books is no greater than the similarity of darkness to light, and the
similarity which is found to some extent in the rest does not suffice for
their canonization. 5. If the taste of the Christian people has such weight in
discerning canonical books, the Roman Catholics are excessively insolent and
profane when they so often ridicule that taste under the name of the *private
spirit*. 6. The people of Christ have no different Christian taste now from
that which they had formerly at the Council of Laodicea and earlier, when they
left these books outside the canon. 7. Since all these means of making the
declaration are uncertain, the declaration of the Roman Church, which rests
upon them, cannot possess the certainty of divine faith.

8. Bell.: *The councils and Fathers placed the Book of Esther, such as they
customarily read it, in the sacred canon. But their copies contained the seven
disputed chapters; therefore, they placed these seven chapters in the canon as
well.* Prot.: 1. The two books of Esdras which the Roman Catholics acknowledge
as apocryphal were contained in those same copies, joined to the true books of
Esdras, as Bellarmine himself admits in chapter 20. Yet it does not follow, as
he himself says in the same place, that the ancient councils and Fathers who
placed two books of Esdras in the canon understood by the name of two our
three. Because these spurious chapters were joined to the true ones, it came
about, as Sixtus of Siena observes, that very weighty writers placed the whole
Book of Esther outside the canon. *Bellarmine also notes this in chapter 7.*
Therefore, either for this reason the additions must be rejected from copies
of the canonical books,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 21 | printed p. 11 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

because they cast such suspicion upon a canonical book; 3. or else those who
place a book in the canon without qualification do not thereby regard as
divine every patch sewn onto it. 4. If some admitted the spurious part in
addition to the true, they did so by the same error through which others
rejected the true part because of the spurious.

9. Bell.: *It is also probable that these chapters were once in the Hebrew
text and then fell out by some accident.* Prot.: 1. That which is confirmed by
no conjectures and disproved by many reasons is not probable. 1. Why should
these chapters have fallen from the Hebrew canon more than others? 2. Whose
testimony should be trusted concerning the Hebrew canon more than that of the
Hebrews? But they deny it, and Jerome agrees with them. 3. They could not have
fallen out without the gravest fault of the Church. Yet the mark of such
negligence cannot be branded upon the Jewish Church without an atrocious
injury. Bellarmine would not charge the Christian, catholic, and especially
the Roman Church with such a crime.

10. Bell.: *It is objected that these latter chapters do not cohere with the
earlier ones and even conflict with them.* Prot.: Various things are objected
against this position which Bellarmine never attempted to answer. 1. Things
once related in the first chapters are related again in the additions without
reason. 2. The man continually called Ahasuerus in Esther is always called
Artaxerxes in the additions. 3. In the additions the plot against the king is
said to have been made and detected after a certain dream which Mordecai had
in the second year, chapter 11, verses 2–10, compared with 12:1. But in Esther
it is said to have happened in the seventh year, chapter 2:16. Bellarmine
answers this third objection by saying that the dream occurred in the second
year and the plot in the seventh. Anyone who looks at the passage will
immediately see that this is a mere dream. 4. According to the additions,
chapter 11:2, Mordecai was a great man in the king's court even before he
dreamed about the plot against the king—that is, three years before it was
made—although the contrary plainly appears from the Book of Esther. 5.
Ptolemy and Cleopatra are mentioned in chapter 11:1, which can in no way fit
the time of Mordecai or the prophets. 6. In the additions Mordecai is said to
have received gifts for disclosing the plot

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 22 | printed p. 12 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

immediately after making the disclosure and before he had incurred Haman's
hatred, chapter 12:5–6. This plainly conflicts with the authentic history. 7.
In the additions Haman is said to have been hostile to Mordecai because of the
eunuchs, chapter 12:6, while the true history relates another cause. 8. In the
additions Haman is called a Macedonian, chapter 16:10; in the authentic text,
an Agagite, chapter 8:3. Finally, what is said about the king's anger against
Esther in chapter 15:7 does not agree with the true history in chapter 5:2,
nor does the substance of the letters given in chapter 8 agree with the
epistle reproduced in chapter 16.

What has been said about the joining of those seven chapters in the same
copies as the Book of Esther must also be applied to the Book of Baruch added
to Jeremiah and to the additions to Daniel, which Jerome calls *fables* in his
preface to Daniel.

11. Bell.: *Jerome calls the narratives contained in these chapters fables
because histories were called fables by the Jews.* Prot.: 1. He expressly
denies that they exist among the Hebrews. 2. He calls those who regard them as
part of Daniel unlearned. 3. He says that he would have preferred to omit them
when translating Scripture. 4. He was unwilling to publish them unless a
throat-cutting obelus was placed before them. 5. He also relates of Origen,
Apollinaris, and other ecclesiastical men and doctors of Greece that they
denied being obligated to answer Porphyry for things which possess no
authority of Holy Scripture. Therefore, when he calls them fables and speaks
consistently with himself, he affirms that they are invented narratives or
Jewish comedies. 6. *He received this designation not from the Jews, who have
no such word to denote a history, but from Porphyry himself, and approved it
together with others.* Jerome's words are these: *We ought to know, among
other things, that Porphyry objects that the fable of Susanna contains “to cut
from the mastic tree and to saw from the oak,” an etymology which fits the
Greek language better than the Hebrew. Eusebius and Apollinaris answered him
to the same effect that the fables of Susanna, Bel, and the dragon are not
contained in the Hebrew, and so forth. We have marked these visions with an
obelus. Origen, Eusebius, Apollinaris, and other ecclesiastical men and doctors
of Greece*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 23 | printed p. 13 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*admit, as I said, that these visions are not found among the Hebrews and that
they are not obligated to answer Porphyry for things which provide no
authority of Holy Scripture.* As to the mitigation of this judgment for
appeasing the resentment of the unlearned, which Bellarmine cites from the
second *Apology against Rufinus*, Erasmus rightly observes on that same
*Apology*: *Here Jerome answers craftily, for it is plainly apparent from the
preface itself that this is what he thought.*

12. Bell.: *They object that in the story of Susanna Daniel is introduced as a
boy.* Prot.: This is not the only objection made against that story. Rather:
1. It ascribes to the Jews judicial assemblies with the power of life and
death during the captivity. 2. In the land of Babylonia it ascribes to a
little boy authority which no private person ever exercised in Judea—namely,
recalling a condemned person to judgment and instituting a new hearing of the
case. 3. They make this Daniel a little boy, although while still a boy he was
being educated in the king's palace, apart from the Jews. 4. It makes the
elders so childish in their consideration of the case and in their sudden
referral of the whole case to the judgment of a younger boy.

13. Bell.: *The narratives about Daniel remaining only one night in the lions'
den in chapter 6 and remaining there six days in chapter 14 do not conflict.
For, first, Daniel was sent into the den twice, once by Darius the Mede and
again by Cyrus. 2. It is not improbable that there were two Daniels and that
the Daniel who freed Susanna, destroyed Bel, killed the dragon, and was in the
lions' den for six days was not the same as the one treated in the preceding
chapters.* Prot.: It is not credible that Daniel was in the lions' den twice,
since the first time that scheme turned out so badly for its authors, and
Darius also promulgated an edict about reverencing the God who had delivered
Daniel from the lions, chapter 5. It is in no way probable that either Daniel's
enemies would attempt this again or the king himself would permit it. 2. Cyrus
does not fit the character in the fable, for he neither remained in Babylonia
nor lacked great favor toward Daniel. 3. It is also remarkable that, although
Darius sought Daniel the next morning at the first light of dawn, Cyrus did
not ask what had happened to Daniel until six days

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 24 | printed p. 14 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

later, nor learned it from others. 4. If the Daniel treated in the additions
was different from Daniel the prophet, then the additions themselves are also
a book different from the canonical book of Daniel the prophet and are thus
apocryphal. 5. It could not be that this new Daniel lived in the time of Cyrus
and received a meal from the prophet Habakkuk, as chapter 14 relates.

14. Bell.: *Against Tobit it is objected that in chapter 5 Sarah is said to
have lived at Rages, a city of the Medes, but later, in chapter 9, when Tobit
had come to the place where Sarah was, he is then said to have sent the angel
to Gabael at Rages. It is answered either that there were two cities named
Rages in Media, or that Rages denotes not the city itself but a neighboring
place.* Prot.: When Bellarmine imagines without any probable reason that there
were two cities named Rages, driven to the wall he does the same thing he did
before when he imagined two Daniels. Nor does the text allow some place near
the city of Rages to be understood by Rages, for it expressly says “at Rages,
a city of Media.” It is objected, 2. that Tobit ascribes a lie to the angel of
God when he makes him affirm that he is Azariah son of Ananias, from the
children of Israel, of the tribe of Naphtali, from the captivity of Nineveh,
chapter 5:7. It is objected, 3. that Tobit ascribes to the same angel—and
himself approves—that magical invention that smoke from the heart of a certain
fish drives away every kind of demon from either a man or a woman so that it
never approaches them again, chapter 6. It is objected, 4. that chapter 14
falsely affirms that the house of God was burned during Tobit's life, as is
plainly apparent by comparing Tobit 1 and 2 Kings 17–18. Bellarmine thought it
wisest to answer absolutely nothing to these and similar objections.

15. Bell.: *The Book of Judith has an excellent testimony from the Council of
Nicaea, as Jerome witnesses.* Prot.: 1. Jerome says only that such a thing is
*read*, leaving its credibility with the author. 2. Jerome himself, who
approved the decrees of this council, disapproved the Book of Judith, as
Bellarmine admits in chapter 10. 3. If this had been so, the Council of
Laodicea would not have left this book outside the canon. 4. If it approved
this book, it approved it as it then existed in the Greek edition; but in the
Greek edition it contained a false-

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 25 | printed p. 15 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

hood about the temple having been destroyed at that time. Therefore, either
it did not approve the book or it approved a falsehood. The proposition is
Bellarmine's in chapter 7; the assumption is his in chapter 12. He therefore
cannot reject the conclusion.

16. Bell.: *The most difficult objection against this book concerns the time
to which this history can belong. But it seems it must be said that the history
of Judith occurred in the time of Manasseh after he had returned from
captivity.* Prot.: This conflicts, first, with the fact that in chapter 5
Holofernes asks about the Jews as though they were an unknown people. 2. In the
whole history of Judith there is no mention of Manasseh, nor in the history of
Manasseh any mention of Judith, Holofernes, or an invasion of this sort.

17. Bell.: *Ecclesiasticus is attributed to Solomon.* Prot.: It is certain
that Solomon was not the author of that book. 1. The book itself testifies
that another was its author, chapters 1 and 50:28. 2. It was written in Greek.
3. It mentions Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Zerubbabel, chapter 49.

18. Bell.: *The Books of Maccabees are cited with honor by many.* Prot.: We do
not deny it. But first, they were written in Greek. Second, according to
Jerome's judgment they had Josephus as their author. Third, whoever the author
was, he acknowledges that he abridged the five volumes of Jason of Cyrene, a
profane man, book 2, chapter 2. 4. He sought the readers' delight and his own
praise from the adornment of the history, in the same place. 5. Finally, he
concludes with an excuse and a plea for pardon concerning his manner of
writing. These things—even to say nothing of other matters conflicting with
Scripture—sufficiently show that this writing proceeded from a human spirit.

19. Bell.: *The book of Hermes entitled The Shepherd must be judged
apocryphal, although Origen thought it divinely inspired and Tertullian,
Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, Athanasius, and Cassian cite testimonies from
it. So also Third and Fourth Maccabees, as well as Third and Fourth Esdras,
although Fourth Esdras is cited by Ambrose, and Third by Athanasius, Augustine,
Clement of Alexandria, the author of the Imperfect Work, and Cyprian.*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 26 | printed p. 16 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Prot.: Bellarmine was therefore playing games with us until now when he wished
to prove that other disputed books were canonical because the Fathers
sometimes cited testimonies from them. Nor is there any firm argument against
the books which the Papists confess to be apocryphal that does not also have
force against the rest.

20. Bell.: *They exist neither in Hebrew nor in Greek.* Prot.: Neither were
the other disputed books written in Hebrew, while the Greek language pertains
no more to the Old Testament than does Latin.

21. Bell.: *They are placed in the canon by no council.* Prot.: Neither were
the other books ever placed in the canon by any ancient general council, as
Bellarmine expressly admits is true of councils older than Augustine. He
readily concedes it concerning the rest when he urges no general council in
approval of all these books except his wretched Council of Trent. This is just
as if he produced the Jesuit nation as approving these books.

22. Bell.: *The Fourth Book of Esdras contains certain fabulous things in
chapter 6 about the fish Enoch and Leviathan, which the seas could not hold.*
Prot.: 1. We showed above from Jerome the fables about Susanna, Bel, and the
dragon which are found in the additions to Daniel. 2. What is related in 2
Maccabees 2 is also a manifest fable: namely, that Jeremiah commanded the
tabernacle, the ark, and the altar of incense to make the journey with him to
Mount Nebo, which is in the land of the Moabites, and there hid them in a
certain cave. He declared that the place would remain unknown until God again
gathered his people, but that the Lord would then reveal it. Even if we grant
that these things could have been done by Jeremiah—something which in no way
fits the history—how will he be excused from falsehood when Jeremiah is said
to have promised that the ark would be found again with the other things when
the people were gathered again? Bellarmine answers: *This must not be
understood of that gathering which occurred in the time of Cyrus, but either
of the last time shortly before the day of judgment, or mystically, so that
Christ is understood by the ark.* But the first interpretation entails

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 27 | printed p. 17 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

that the Jews are to be gathered before the day of judgment for that
ceremonial worship which depended upon the tabernacle, the ark, and the altar
of incense, something foreign to the Christian faith. By a mystical
interpretation, however, even that fable concerning Enoch and Leviathan can be
defended. For Gregory in the *Moralia*, the *Ordinary Gloss*, Thomas Aquinas in
the *Summa*, part 1, question 64, article 3, reply 2, and part 3, question 8,
article 7, and all the Roman Catholics in their allegories maintain that the
devils are understood by Behemoth and Leviathan in Job 40–41. How easy it is
to add that the sea could not contain all the devils, either because of the
tumults they would stir up there or because they had to traverse the earth to
seduce human beings.
