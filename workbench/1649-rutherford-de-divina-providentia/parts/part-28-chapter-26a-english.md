CHAPTER XXVI

Whether physical predetermination, by which God premoves wills to the entitative
act of sin, makes God the author of sin; whether it is Fate or Manichaeism.
Whether GOD’s general and indifferent concurrence truly frees God from
participation in wickedness, according to the doctrine of the Jesuits and
Arminians. Dr. Ruiz, Arriaga, Suárez, Raynaudus, James Arminius, and others are
called to take their parts.

1. The Jesuits and Arminians devote themselves wholly to wrapping the
defenders of efficacious predetermination and the Reformed Churches in the
charges of θεαμαρτησίας, “sinning against GOD,” Manichaeism, Libertinism, and
astrological fate; and now the battle grows hot. With GOD’s help, let us now
see what they accomplish.-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 384; IA PDF page 443; BSB canvas 436]

*No one has heaped up more against us in this harvest than the learned and
keen Jesuit Didacus Ruiz—but certainly, as is evident to one who reviews his
writings, with a conscience far from good. For he maintains assertions about
GOD’s action concerning sin, especially in his volume on providence, as hard
and severe as anything to be read in the writings of Calvin, Beza, or our
theologians.

[Marginal summary: GOD’s concurrence which predetermines, and as it were
physically concurs toward the entitative acts of sin, is vindicated.]

Dr. Ruiz: *GOD does not, according to the Calvinists, offer counsel toward evil
in the manner of a law. Thus GOD does not commend but prohibits the material
acts of sin. Yet, according to the opinion of those who hold that GOD
predetermines the material acts of sin, he offers private counsel—as when a
robber does not openly, but nevertheless privately, commend robberies to his
companions on the ground of their utility or pleasantness. For he
predetermines a material act in the created intellect, and GOD physically
determines, moves, and excites both practical judgments to consider what is
useful and temporal loves—which not even the Devil does—and does so in order
to determine the intellect to the material acts of hatred of GOD.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 27, section 2.]

I answer: 1. God gives neither public nor private counsel toward sin. To
determine the intellect physically is a physical act, but to give counsel is
an ethical act. 2. On the Jesuits’ opinion, God, by a true causality—although a
collateral one—codetermines the intellect together with the intellect itself
toward the physical act of knowing and considering temptations on the ground
of utility. Not even the Devil does this. Therefore, on the Jesuits’ view, God
offers counsel and commends sinning just as much as on our view. 3. The entire
chorus of Theologians teaches that GOD’s end, intention, and will is to concur
toward every material act of sin so that the act occurs—unless perhaps the
Jesuits wish God to concur involuntarily and accidentally toward any positive
acts. The only question concerns the manner of concurrence: does he concur by
codetermining or by predetermining? On both sides each concurrence is
acknowledged to be causative. Apparently these two poor prepositions, *con-* and
*pre-*, either convict God of sin or absolve him of the charge.

2. Ruiz: *If GOD’s will is efficacious so that a human will is predetermined
to the material act of hatred of GOD, the very attempt to restrain oneself
from hatred of GOD will be an attempt to violate omnipotence and frustrate
GOD’s efficacious will. And suppose someone certainly knew GOD’s decree of
predetermination to an act of hatred of GOD. If he restrained himself from the
act, he would violate-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 385; IA PDF page 444; BSB canvas 437]

*the natural precept pertaining to GOD’s honor, which forbids violating GOD’s
Omnipotence and efficacious will.* Let another Jesuit, Mæratius, come to his
aid: *“Just as one who speaks falsely, so far as it lies in him, makes GOD a
liar, so one who efficaciously wills what GOD by antecedent will wills not to
occur would, so far as it lies in him, make GOD powerless and mutable, and
does injury to GOD.”*

[Marginal reference: Lodovicus Mæratius, treatise *On the Goodness and Evil of
Acts*, volume 1, disputation 11, section 1, figure 8.]

I answer: 1. There is no need of a fiction here. It was revealed to Peter that
he would deny the Savior, and from Zechariah 13 to the Apostles that,
scandalized by CHRIST’s passion, they would desert CHRIST the Shepherd. These
sins virtually include hatred of GOD. Therefore, if Peter and the Disciples
attempted not to deny and not to desert CHRIST, they would strive against the
general concurrence of GOD by which God concurs toward the material acts of
denial and desertion, and so they would struggle against GOD’s efficacious
will concerning the fulfillment of those prophecies. They would then make God
mutable and powerless and would violate a natural precept. Yet no one can deny
that God, foreknowing the future cooperation of free choice toward the
material act of sin and foretelling those acts, efficaciously willed them to
occur. Thus this argument, when turned back, will burden the adversaries as
much as us.

[Marginal summary: GOD’s efficacious will, armed with omnipotence, as such is
not our rule.]

2. I ask where this natural precept, by which we are forbidden to resist GOD’s
efficacious will, is written. It is a fictitious precept. For we are not
obliged to conform our wills to GOD’s will in the thing willed, as Filiucius,
the great Jesuit who was once Professor at the Roman College, teaches. Only
conformity with GOD’s revealed will obliges us.

[Marginal reference: Filiucius, volume 2, treatise 21, chapter 11, figure 384.]

Ruiz: *An individual material action includes moral goodness according to its
generic, specific, and numerical account. Morality must therefore be traced
back to GOD as the first moral agent. Consequently, if God predetermines this
material action of hatred of GOD, which contains evil within itself, he can be
the moral cause of sin.*

I answer: 1. The antecedent is false. An individual material action considered
in the merely physical order does not include numerical moral goodness, but
only entitative and Metaphysical goodness. And the individual material action
of sin, considered in the physical order—in which consideration alone God
predetermines it—does not include evil. Thus the consequence is empty: God
cannot be the moral cause of sin. 2. None of us speaks this way, that God is
the physical cause of sin, for sin as such and essentially-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 386; IA PDF page 445; BSB canvas 438]

*is a moral effect and does not have a physical cause, properly speaking. (The
Latin witness accidentally repeats *habet*, “has.”)

Ruiz: *One who, compelled by grave fear, swears falsely and detests the evil of
the act would wish the evil cut away from the act and only the material action
left to him. Therefore GOD will be the author of sin if he efficaciously wills
the material act of sin, even if he does not will the evil.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of GOD*, disputation 27, section 4.]

I answer: 1. I deny the consequence. One who perjures himself in this way has
only an optative will averse to the evil, but he efficaciously wills the
perjury. He therefore sins by willing what is contrary to GOD’s law. But God
is bound by no law that would prevent him from willing the material act of
sin. Here I set against him the learned Scotist Theodorus Smising, who speaks
as follows: *“I say that GOD’s cooperation toward an act of sin, considered in
respect of its physical matter and form, is a sign that GOD wills the act of
sin so considered, in which there is nothing unbecoming, because the act so
considered is a being and a good.”* And the fact that God is above the law
releases him from guilt, while the fact that man is subject to the law
implicates him in wickedness. But a man subject to the law cannot will the act,
since it is forbidden by the law, without necessarily and essentially willing
sin.

[Marginal reference: Smising, *Theological Disputations*, volume 1, treatise 3,
disputation 5, question 2, folio 24.]

3. Ruiz rises up as follows: *Granted, though it is not conceded, that GOD was
bound by absolutely no law of his own not to predetermine a creature to the
material actions of hatred of GOD and perjury, at most this would defend God
against its being imputed to him that he is the author and moral cause of
those actions. For effects which are not directly and expressly willed, nor
really caused by an action directed to that end, are therefore not imputed
except insofar as an obligation to avoid them intervenes. But when effects are
expressly and formally willed by an efficacious choice, and are directly and
efficaciously caused by that choice, no obligation is in any way required for
them to be imputed. According to Calvin, however, GOD directly and formally
wills and really causes the material acts of hatred and perjury.*

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, part 1, disputation 26, section 5.]

I answer: 1. What was denied is not proved. It only follows that the material
acts of hatred of GOD are not imputed to God as a moral cause. 2. GOD’s direct,
express, and efficacious volition, by which he wills that an act of hatred of
GOD exist or occur, does not make God the author of the evil which follows the
act accidentally. Nor does GOD’s hypothetical and inefficacious volition, by
which God inefficaciously wills the act, free God from participation in
wickedness any more than an efficacious volition implicates him in wickedness.
For one who, hypothetically and inefficaciously, and compelled by the
determination-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 387; IA PDF page 446; BSB canvas 439]

*of another’s will, wills an act of hatred is just as much a cause of the
hatred as one who wills that act efficaciously. More and less do not vary the
species but the degree. And in truth, because immunity from law and the office
of the universal cause free God from wickedness even though he concurs
causatively toward an act of hatred of God—as the adversaries confess—the same
immunity equally frees God even though he concurs predeterminatively. For
predetermination harms freedom no more than general concurrence or
codetermination. Smising concedes this: *“GOD’s eternal determination,”* he
says, *“does not alter the mode of the second cause by its eternal antecedence
any more than it would alter it by temporal concomitance. The reason for each
is that the antecedence and concomitance of certain things, precisely as such,
posit nothing absolute in them, but either a relation or a mere extrinsic
denomination.”*

[Marginal reference: Smising, volume 1, treatise 3, disputation 6, *On
Providence*, figure 14.]

3. No obligation is required for the causation of a material act to be imputed
to God where GOD efficaciously and directly wills that act. We concede that
the causation of the act is imputed to God and that GOD is the cause of the
material act; the Jesuits and Arminians do not dare deny this. But this is a
merely physical imputation. What does this have to do with the moral
imputation of the evil which is in question? If the Jesuit wishes the evil to
be imputed to God without any obligation merely because the act is imputed to
him, that is precisely what we utterly deny. For where there is no law, there
is no obligation and no transgression, as Paul teaches in Romans 5. No law is
laid down for GOD—not even the law of God’s eternal holiness—prohibiting him
from efficaciously willing this physical act. Indeed, to imagine moral
imputation where there is no law is plainly contradictory, for then an
obligation both would and would not be laid down for God. Thus neither general
nor special concurrence makes God the cause of sin, because every concurrence
of God is released from an obligation to concur.

Ruiz: *When something is directly willed, if an obligation intervenes it is
imputed to the one willing it. For an obligation makes such an effect
connected with the object willed, not only with respect to existence, but also
with respect to real valuation. A will cannot embrace an object whose
consequent effects it is bound to avoid—GOD cannot embrace an act because he
is bound to avoid the evil consequent upon the act—unless it loves that object
with a higher valuation than the observance of its obligation, and thus unless
it postpones the obligation in order to pursue the object which it values
more. Therefore GOD cannot will the material act unless he sets a higher value
on the act than on the observance of his holy obligation, by which he is bound
to avoid and abhor the evil of the act—unless he is less deprived of his
obligation-*

[Marginal reference: the same, section 4.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 388; IA PDF page 447; BSB canvas 440]

*than of the material act. By contrast, when effects are directly willed and
caused, they are necessarily imputed to the will even though no obligation
intervenes, just as our good works are imputed to GOD as their author and
primary moral cause, although he is bound by no obligation either to produce
or not produce them.*

I answer: 1. This is false: *“A will cannot embrace an object whose consequent
effects it is bound to avoid unless it loves that object with a higher
valuation than the observance of its obligation.”* A Judge cannot avoid good
laws even though he is bound to hate the murders which follow from those laws.
Nor does it follow that the Judge wills and values the laws more highly than
his obligation by which he is bound to hate the murders that follow such
laws.

[Marginal summary: Someone can will an act and detest the consequence of the
act.]

For the sake of entering a treaty of peace, we may receive an oath from Turks
even though we know they will swear idolatrously by false Gods. Yet it does not
follow that we value peace more highly than the obligation to detest an
idolatrous oath. God can will and embrace the material act of hatred of God
even though the evil of hatred follows that act. It is not thereby established
that GOD values that material act more highly than the observance of his
obligation by which he is bound to avoid that evil. For if by τὸ *vitare*, “to
avoid,” you mean the hatred with which God pursues evil, this is utterly false:
the hatred with which God pursues evil does not oblige God not to will the
material act efficaciously. If by τὸ *vitare* in GOD you mean preventing evil
from ever existing, then something false is assumed—namely, that an obligation
rests on God where none rests on him. Therefore it does not follow that, in
efficaciously willing a material act, God values that act more highly than the
observance of his obligation by which he is obliged to prevent evil. Such an
obligation is a Chimera. No one who has so much as greeted Theology from its
threshold will say that God is obliged to prevent sin.

2. Good works are imputed to GOD even though he is by no means obliged to
produce them in us. For obligation is not the cause of imputability simply,
but the cause of culpable imputation. There is a great difference between an
effect’s being imputed to some cause and an effect’s being imputed culpably to
a cause. Imputation in good things certainly has a place apart from every
obligation. Therefore, when God by his grace effects good works in us, they
are truly imputed to him for praise, and the praise is all the more abundant
because he is moved to effect them in us by no obligation but by sheer grace.-*

[Marginal summary: The absence of obligation is not simply the cause of an
effect’s not being imputed, but is the cause of a moral effect’s not being
imputed culpably.]

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 389; IA PDF page 448; BSB canvas 441]

*But to imagine culpable imputability in some cause wholly apart from an
obligation by which someone is subject to a law—or to something corresponding
proportionally to law, such as the most holy nature of the Holy One of
Israel—is an infinite contradiction. Hence, as above, I say that the material
acts of sin are imputed to God apart from every obligation constraining GOD to
produce them, just as our good works are imputed to God without any obligation.
But what follows from this against us? Will the evil of the acts therefore be
imputable to God even though GOD is not subject to a legal obligation? Ah, the
consequence is empty. Because God effects a good, must the evil accidentally
connected to that good be imputed to him? You will wring water from a pumice
stone more easily than you will wring this inference from our doctrine.

[Marginal summary: Imputation differs in good and evil things.]

5. Ruiz: *The obligation of the first and most general cause excuses GOD so
that an evil action is not deservedly imputed to him, although it pertains to
him to offer general concurrence. The reason is that GOD, so far as he himself
is concerned, does not directly and expressly will to produce such a species
of action, which is really contrary to the law. Rather, he would prefer that
it not be produced. He offers a concurrence which of itself inclines toward
good, but which is twisted toward evil contrary to its intention.* Fasolus
says, *“He decrees of himself and antecedently to concur toward evil, but only
consequently, after the free application of the created will and determined by
it; and on his part he is prepared by the same decree to concur toward good.”*
*“General concurrence,”* says Ruiz, *“so far as it is in itself has the power
to abstract from goodness and evil. Determination does not exclude evil but is
the primary cause determining that a real form is drawn out, from which evil
necessarily follows.”* *“An indifferent cause,”* says Arriaga, the Spanish
Jesuit, *“if it concurs as determined by another cause, is not strictly called
the cause of the thing.”* One who kills a man with a sword cannot impute the
murder to the person from whom he bought it, because, says Pennotus, the use of
a sword is indifferent toward good and evil, as is concurrence, and the will
abuses a concurrence indifferent of itself.

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, in the same place.]

[Marginal reference: Hieronymus Fasolus, volume 1, tome 2, on question 14,
article 13, doubt 11, figure 171.]

[Marginal reference: Ruiz, *On the Will of God*, disputation 51, section 3.]

[Marginal reference: Arriaga, *Course of Philosophy*, physical disputation 10,
section 1, subsection 5, figure 45.]

[Marginal reference: Pennotus, *Propugnaculum*, book 8, chapter 14, figure 12.]

I answer: 1. But if, by the obligation of the first cause, GOD loves that
object—namely, the material act—and by his holy nature is bound to avoid the
consequent evil, *“Then he loves such an object”*—recognize your own words,
Ruiz—*“whose consequent effects he is bound to avoid, with a higher valuation
than the observance of his obligation.”* That is absurd. 2. If preferring that
the evil act not occur frees GOD from participation in guilt, then our
predetermination also frees him, because when it is posited-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 390; IA PDF page 449; BSB canvas 442]

*God does not merely prefer that evil not occur—which would be a feeble and
sickly will—but by his approving will efficaciously detests evil as an
abhorrent thing. 3. I leave the Jesuits to settle this among themselves. If a
man, says Mæratius, offered another man toward an evil action such a
concurrence—indifferent and determinable—as GOD offers, he would sin and would
be a cause of the sin. Thus someone who helps another climb through windows,
knowing that theft or murder will follow from that climb, would be a cause of
the theft, even though something other than theft could have followed from
that climb. Unless they say here with us that GOD is freed from wickedness
because he is above the law, they will never dissolve this argument of
Mæratius.

[Marginal reference: Mæratius, volume 1, treatise *On the Goodness and Evil of
Human Acts*, disputation 11, section 7, conclusion 4.]

4. If the obligation of the first cause excuses GOD so that an evil action is
not deservedly imputed to him, then our predetermination—without which the
first cause’s Dominion of providence will never stand—will render GOD still
more excusable. For predetermination is an office of the first cause and does
not overthrow freedom.

[Marginal summary: The office of the first cause, even with respect to any
secondary causes, does not implicate God in participation in wickedness.]

5. When Ruiz says that GOD, so far as he himself is concerned, does not
directly and expressly will to produce such a species of action as is really
contrary to GOD’s law, he sufficiently intimates that God indeed, so far as he
himself is concerned, does not will sin, but nevertheless does will it so far
as it is in another, and wills it after he has now been determined to will it
by the depraved action of the will. The mystery lies in these adverbs,
*directly* and *expressly*. Thus God indirectly and virtually, and as though
compelled and contrary to the optative will which inclines toward good, wills
sin to occur and desires it to be produced *as such*. This certainly amounts
to coming almost entirely over to our opinion. But if physically concurring
toward the act makes GOD the cause of sin, then this opinion of the Jesuits
and Arminians is worse than ours, because God is compelled by his office as
the most universal cause to will sin and cause sin. Yet it is more servile to
be compelled to cause sin than to cause it freely.

[Marginal summary: To what extent the obligation of the first cause does
nothing to implicate GOD in participation in wickedness.]

6. To examine each point here more precisely, I ask what the obligation of the
first cause is. I believe it to be nothing other than a fitting liberty by
which it is fitting and suitable for God, as first cause, to concur with all
secondary causes, and thus with a will deviating toward the material act of
sin. But the question still remains why it is fitting for God by physical
causality to promote an evil act. Whence comes the obligation by which God is
as it were bound to cocause evil? Does this cocausing not oppose GOD’s holy
nature? By no means, the Jesuits confess. But certainly in a man or an Angel
subject-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 391; IA PDF page 450; BSB canvas 443]

*to the law, such cocausing would be sin, as Mæratius rightly says. Yet if the
man were a first cause above the law, as God is, it would not be sin. But this
is to say that GOD is not stained by participation in guilt when cocausing a
material act precisely because God is subject to no law. Welcome, then,
Jesuit—you arrive among us as a longed-for guest.

7. Material acts are produced by God through a true positive causality.
Arminius does not dare deny this, nor do the Jesuits deny it. Thus Arminius:
*“I answer that I by no means remove from GOD’s efficiency an act which is not
committed by the creature without sin. Indeed, I openly confess that GOD is
the cause of all acts which are committed by creatures.”* But I assume that
whatever is an object of the productive power in God is also an object of the
volitional power, for whatever God does he also wills. Yet Arminius wishes the
material act itself to be prohibited by God. But then God will be the producer
of what he prohibits, that is, of sin. Let the Adversaries consider this.

[Marginal reference: Arminius, *Answer to Perkins*, page 73, 1.]

8. Ruiz says: *“GOD’s indifferent concurrence is of itself inclined toward
good, but is twisted toward evil contrary to its intention.”* But that the
concurrence is of itself indifferent to both a good and an evil action, as
these Masters teach, and that it is of itself inclined toward good, seem to me
ἀσύστατα, “inconsistent things.”

9. Fasolus says: *“GOD, determined by the second cause, concurs and on his part
is prepared by the same decree to concur toward good.”* But God is prepared to
concur toward good only conditionally, if the created will determines itself
to good. Yet in the same way God is of himself prepared by the same decree to
concur toward evil if the will determines itself to evil.

[Marginal summary: According to the adversaries’ principles, GOD’s general
concurrence confers causality on sin just as much as our predetermination.]

10. Let the Reader consider whether God determines by the same decree to
promote Peter’s confession and Judas’s betrayal with no different actual
motion. 11. General concurrence in second act is determined. Therefore Ruiz’s
claim that *“general concurrence of itself has the power to abstract goodness
from evil”* is false. Nor does our determination include evil as a cause
includes an effect any more than GOD’s general concurrence includes it.
Although predetermination is the primary cause by which a real form is drawn
from the subject, from which evil necessarily follows, it is nevertheless not
the cause of sin. For here it is the primary cause in such a way that the
will’s liberty remains unimpaired in the act. God predetermines the will—as
though pregnant and about to give birth—to four acts in such a way that God
predetermines the will which is about to break forth into act A, B, C, or D,
and which will in fact break forth of itself only into A, not B, C, or D—even
if we suppose a will-*

[Chapter XXVI, Part A; printed page 392; IA PDF page 451; BSB canvas 444]

*which is going to act without any decree and concurrence of GOD—so that it in
fact bends only toward act A, not B, C, or D. For it cannot be denied that
there are certain degrees of necessity: God is just by a greater necessity
than the sun will rise tomorrow. Hence if the necessity arising from
predetermination is a necessity *ut 6*, “as six,” and a necessity of exercise,
by which it is necessary that the will act on the hypothesis that it acts, is
also a necessity *ut 6*—which I think must necessarily be said—then
predetermination drives the will toward A, not B, C, or D, by no stronger bond
of necessity than the will itself drives itself toward A and not toward B, C,
or D. Thus predetermination in no way harms liberty.

[Marginal summary: How the necessity of predetermination agrees with the
necessity of the exercise of a free act.]

12. If what the Spaniard Arriaga says is true—*“an indifferent cause, if it
concurs as determined by another cause, is not strictly called the cause of
the thing”*—then the habits of faith, love, and repentance are not strictly
the causes of acts of faith, love, and repentance. For a habit is indifferent
and exerts influence as determined by another cause. Nor, by Arriaga’s
reasoning, is GOD’s general concurrence strictly a cause of the acts of faith
and love. Therefore, strictly speaking, there is no other cause of the acts of
faith and love than pure free choice. Long live Pelagius, then! For
persuasions from the word are not strictly real causes, since they act only
morally.

[Marginal summary: Arriaga, by denying that God’s concurrence is a cause of
acts, overthrows God’s providence.]

13. Neither determination nor indetermination belongs to the essence of a
cause, as Arriaga and Pennotus know. Otherwise Saul, moved and determined by
Doeg’s wicked counsel and slanders, would not strictly be a cause of the
killing of JEHOVAH’s priests, nor would GOD’s justice, determined by
impenitence, strictly be a cause of the damnation of profligate people. And if
a craftsman makes a sword for a madman and knows that, if he lacked this
sword, he would not kill a man, and knows certainly that, if it is handed to
him, he will kill a man with it—if he then hands over the sword, he is a cause
of the killing, although the use of the sword itself is indifferent of itself.
God knows that people cannot elicit the material acts of sin if his
concurrence is absent. He knows that they will perform such acts if concurrence
is given, and nevertheless he concurs. Therefore, although the concurrence is
indifferent of itself, in this case GOD, in concurring, will nevertheless be a
cause of the material acts.
