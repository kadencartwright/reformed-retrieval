QUESTION IV

Whether the Pope is Peter’s successor

Bellarmine, book 2, *On the Roman Pontiff*, chapter 11, says that the Pope
succeeds Peter not only in the episcopate but also in the primacy over the
universal Church.

We assert the contrary, relying on these arguments.

Argument 1. If Peter was neither head of the Church nor bishop of Rome, while
the Pope boasts that he is both, then the Pope is not Peter’s successor. But
the former has been proved above. Therefore.

Argument 2. Peter was an apostle and, by virtue of the apostleship, possessed
primacy over all the churches together with the other apostles, Matthew 28:19.
But the Pope is not an apostle, because the apostles did not ordain apostles as
their successors, but pastors and teachers. Therefore, the Pope did not succeed
Peter in the primacy.

Argument 3. If the Pope received the primacy from the fathers because Rome
ruled the whole earth, then he did not receive it by succession from Peter. But
the former is true, according to the Council of Chalcedon, action 16. Therefore.

Argument 4. If Scripture says absolutely nothing about the Pope’s succession,
and ecclesiastical writers hand down nothing certain about it,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 72 | printed col. 146 | bsb10175568]

then it is not to be received as an article of faith. But the former is true,
because some name Peter and Paul and then other bishops; some name Peter alone;
some name Peter as a bishop, others do not; some name Linus immediately after
Peter, others Clement, and still others Cletus and Anacletus before Clement.
Carranza therefore says in his *Summary of the Councils*, page 13: *In a matter
so perplexed, I commit the judgment to the reader’s discretion.* For what
pertains to this, see the preceding question.

Argument 5. If the Pope succeeds Peter neither in doctrine nor in morals, then
he is not Peter’s successor. But the former will be proved later, where we
treat the Antichrist. Therefore.

Argument 6. If Peter ordained the bishops of Alexandria and Antioch, as they
concede, and if this was never granted to the Pope, as they are also compelled
to confess, then the Pope does not succeed Peter in the primacy. But this is
so. Therefore.

Argument 7. If the Pope succeeds heretics, idolaters, schismatics, men who were
excommunicated and anathematized, then he did not succeed Peter—unless, perhaps,
we say that such men discharged the same office as Peter. But the former can be
proved by an induction. Pope John XXII denied the immortality of souls. The
Council of Constance condemned and deposed John XXIII and called him the devil
incarnate. The Council of Basel found Pope Eugenius guilty of schism and
heresy. Pope Marcellinus burned incense to idols. Pope Liberius subscribed to
the Arians. The Roman clergy excommunicated Pope Anastasius as a heretic.
Sylvester II sacrificed to the devil. Hildebrand, who was also called Gregory
VII, was rejected by the Council of Worms and is said not to have been elected
by God. The Church anathematized Pope Dioscorus after his death, and so forth.
See Gratian, cause 24, question 2, and the histories of Platina, Nauclerus, and
others.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 73 | printed col. 147 | bsb10175568]

Indeed, Bellarmine himself concedes in the preface to his books *On the Roman
Pontiff* that several popes were less than upright.

Argument 8. Whatever primacy Peter possessed did not exempt him from
subjection to the other brethren, before whom he gave an account and by whom
he was sent, Acts 8:14 and 11:3. But the Pope proclaims himself to be above the
Church. Indeed, Hostiensis, in the Extravagants, in the canon *On Election*,
says that the Pope’s consistory is the same as God’s, that the Pope can do
everything God does, and that even if he should consign countless peoples in
throngs together with himself to the first bondage of hell, it is forbidden to
say, “What are you doing?”

Argument 9. Peter’s primacy did not exempt him from paying tribute even to a
heathen, Matthew 16, last verse. Indeed, all are commanded to be subject to the
magistrates, Romans 13:1. But the Pope’s primacy not only exempts him from
subjection; as they maintain, it also makes him superior to kings and emperors.
Thus Martin V pressed Sigismund’s neck beneath his feet; Pope Zacharias deposed
Childeric, king of the Franks, and put Pepin, the father of Charlemagne, in his
place; and Gratian, distinction 96, says that the Pope can depose the emperor
and transfer the empire.

Argument 10. If Clement or Linus succeeded Peter, then he was appointed as
successor either by Peter himself or by someone else. But neither can be said.
Therefore. The assumption is proved. He was not appointed by Peter, because a
bishop is not permitted to appoint his own successor, as the Council of
Antioch, canon 23, and Gratian, question 1, cause 8, teach.

2. If Peter ordained Clement or Linus as head of the Church and as his
successor, then either Peter divested himself of that office and was subject to
Clement or Linus as head of the Church; or, if Peter also remained its head,
the Church at the same time

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 73 | printed col. 148 | bsb10175568]

had two heads. But both alternatives are absurd. Nor was he appointed by
someone else, because they cannot show by whom. Further, no one except Peter
possessed primacy over the universal Church, and no one can give what he does
not possess. Therefore.

Argument 11. If Clement, Linus, or anyone else succeeded Peter in the primacy
and was head of the Church, then James, John, and the other apostles who
survived Peter were subject and obedient to Clement as head of the Church. For,
as they maintain, Peter died in Nero’s reign, but John much later, in
Domitian’s. Yet the latter is absurd, because they were among the foremost
apostles, called immediately by Christ, and were equal to Peter himself, if not
preferred to him, Galatians 2. Therefore, it is not probable that they obeyed
an ordinary bishop called by men. Further, as a sign of their greater
authority, the Church included the writings of James and John in the canon but
excluded Clement’s from it.

Argument 12. The privilege of primacy is either personal, local, or mixed. But
none of these can be said. Therefore. The assumption is proved. It is not
local, because Antioch would then be Peter’s see, for he first sat there, as
they concede. Therefore, even if he departed elsewhere, the privilege of the
place remains. It is not personal, because it would then not be local, and thus
the bishop of Rome would have no right above other bishops. It is not mixed,
because then it is granted to the place only on account of the person who is
there and only for as long as he is there. But the Papists maintain the
contrary, as does Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 1, who maintains that the popes
were bishops of Rome even while residing in France. Indeed, according to
Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 12, the dignity of the primacy was personal at
first, but later became local or mixed. Yet it is asked when, by whom, and by
what right. And if, as they maintain, the dignity became local because Peter

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 74 | printed col. 149 | bsb10175568]

changed his see, then when the popes resided in France the see was changed.

Argument 13. If the bishop of Rome possesses the primacy because he succeeds
Peter, then the other bishops who sit in other places would likewise be higher
or lower in rank according to the apostles whom they succeeded. Thus the
bishop of Ephesus would be second, the bishop of Jerusalem third, and so on
with the others; and the bishop of Alexandria, who succeeded no apostle, would
not be second.

Argument 14. Christ, as they maintain, appointed Peter as his successor, and
Peter appointed Clement or Linus. But now the Pope is appointed neither
immediately by Christ, as Peter was, nor by his predecessor, as Clement was by
Peter, but by the cardinals—and he has been appointed in that way for only a
few centuries. Yet it is asked: from where do the cardinals possess that power?
If from God, let them produce the documents; if from men, from whom and by what
authority?

Argument 15. If the Pope were Peter’s successor because Peter taught and died
at Rome, then he ought much more to be Paul’s successor, because Scripture
testifies that Paul taught at Rome; they maintain that he died there; and
Scripture does not testify that Peter was at Rome and taught there. Therefore.

OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. By divine right, Peter ought to have someone succeed him who has
the same right. This can be no one other than the bishop of Rome. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because the apostles, who were called to
an extraordinary office, did not ordain ordinary successors.

2. James was an apostle whom Clement in the same epistle calls *bishop of
bishops, governing all the churches*

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 74 | printed col. 150 | bsb10175568]

*that have been founded everywhere by the providence of God.* Indeed, Paul
numbers him among the foremost apostles before Peter in Galatians 2. Yet his
successors in the episcopate of Jerusalem, whom history enumerates, did not
have such a right and dominion bestowed upon them.

3. Every right of Peter’s was either personal or belonged to his office. The
latter was common to the other apostles, who held the same office in common;
the former, however, is not transmitted to successors. Yet Eusebius and others
call him the chief of the others because of the greatness of his virtue; but
virtues are personal gifts that are not transmitted to posterity.

4. The assumption is denied, because if the apostles have any successors, all
faithful and true bishops are their successors. Finally, someone is called a
successor in two ways. 1. With respect to time, namely, one who is later in
time. Thus Paul is called the apostles’ successor, but Ambrose, on 2 Corinthians
12, says: *He is lesser than his predecessors the apostles, not in dignity, but
in time.* 2. With respect to office, as when one bishop succeeds another. Thus,
with respect to time, if Peter was bishop at Rome, the bishop of Rome was his
successor; but not with respect to office, because Peter’s was extraordinary.

Objection 2. It is certain that the pontiff exists for the sake of the Church,
not the Church for the sake of the pontiff, according to Augustine, book 1, *On
Pastors*: *That we are Christians is for our sake; that we are leaders is for
yours.* But the Church is no less in need of one pastor than in the time of the
apostles—indeed, it is in still greater need, since Christians are more
numerous and worse. Therefore, the succession of the pontificate remains.

Response. If you take *pontiffs* for the leaders about whom Augustine speaks,
the whole argument is conceded. 2. Even if we concede that Peter was a
universal monarch to whom, together with the other apostles, the care of all
Christ’s sheep was entrusted,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 75 | printed col. 151 | bsb10175568]

and that this was for the sake of the Church, it nevertheless does not follow
that such a
pontificate exists now. It no more follows than this: at the beginning the
apostles, prophets, and evangelists existed for the sake of the Church;
therefore, they ought to exist now. For if the Church needed ministers and
extraordinary gifts of this kind as it did when it was first planted, Christ,
who knows what is expedient for the Church, would give them.

Objection 3. The Church is one and the same at every time. Therefore, the form
of government, which is the form of the commonwealth, ought not to have been
changed. If, therefore, there was one supreme ruler and head in the time of the
apostles, there ought likewise to be one in the present time.

Response. This begs the question, for we deny that in the time of the apostles
there was any one man who was the supreme head of the Church besides Christ.
2. A change in the form of the Church’s government does not change its unity,
for it was governed in one way in Christ’s time, in another in the apostles’
time, and in another afterward, and yet it remained one.

Objection 4. In the last chapter of John Christ said to Peter, *Feed my sheep.*
But the office of pastor is ordinary and perpetual, because it ought to last as
long as the sheepfold, and the sheepfold remains until the end of the age.
Therefore, Peter’s successors ought to remain just as long in the supreme
pastoral office.

Response. This is a deception arising from the ambiguity of *pastor*. For it is
sometimes taken for ordinary ministers alone, sometimes for extraordinary
ministers, and generally for all who feed Christ’s flock. The duty of feeding
is indeed always perpetual, but not every rank of pastors is, because the
extraordinary pastors have ceased. 2. It does not follow that because Peter is
told, *Feed the sheep*, that charge belonged exclusively to him and his
successors, any more than it follows that because Paul told Timothy, *Guard the
deposit*, this belonged exclusively to him and his successors.

Objection 5. The Lord entrusted all the sheep to Peter not only with respect to
place, but also with respect to time, since Christ ought to have provided for
us no less than for the ancients.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 75 | printed col. 152 | bsb10175568]

Therefore, when he said to Peter, *Feed the sheep*, he addressed all Peter’s
successors in him.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because he did not entrust all the sheep to
Peter alone, but to him together with the others. 2. If they are speaking of
Peter’s person, it is false that all the sheep were entrusted to him with
respect to place and time, because he was an apostle only of the circumcised,
Galatians 2.

3. If by the name *successors* he means the Pope, it is false, because Peter,
as an apostle, had no successor. If he had any successors, however, he would
have all true bishops as successors. Here is a parallel: when Christ said,
*Teach all nations*, he entrusted all nations to the apostles. Therefore, he
did so not only with respect to place, but also to time, because he provided
for us no less than for them. Thus, if the argument is sound, it also follows
that the other apostles had successors.

Objection 6, from 1 Corinthians 12:21. If there is in the Church some head that
cannot say to the feet, “I have no need of you,” then there is some head besides
Christ. But the former is true. Therefore. The proposition is proved. This head
is either Christ or someone else. But it is not Christ, because this could not
truly be said of Christ, for he could say to the other members of the Church,
“You are not necessary to me.” Therefore, it is someone else, and consequently
the Pope.

Response. 1. The proposition is denied. Further, its proof rests on a false
explanation of the passage, because by the likeness of the human body the
apostle teaches the coherence of the members of the Church, which the diversity
of gifts does not prevent. For he compares the more excellent members to those
who have received more gifts, and the less excellent members to those who have
received fewer; and thus he concludes that, just as the more excellent members
cannot do without the others, so in the Church those who possess more excellent
gifts ought not to despise others. They therefore err by referring what is said
in the protasis about the head to the apodosis about the Church. 2. They err by
making the parts

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 76 | printed col. 153 | bsb10175568]

of the comparison carry force beyond the author’s purpose.

3. They err by thinking that the difference between the members consists only
in ecclesiastical offices, because Paul is speaking about all spiritual gifts;
indeed, Thomas applies this passage to kings. 4. They err by thinking that this
head is the head of the Church, when the apostle says only that it is a head in
the Church. Finally, why is more weight placed upon the singleness of the head
than upon the fact that there are two feet, two hands, and so forth?

Objection 7. In the Old Testament there was a succession of high priests, and
the priesthood of the Old Testament was a figure of the priesthood of the New.
Therefore, a succession in Peter’s see ought now to be preserved.

Response. There is more in the conclusion than in the premises. 2. This begs
the question, because it is asked whether Peter was the high pontiff. 3. The
assumption is denied. And although we concede that the priesthood of the Old
Testament was a figure of the new, it does not follow that, because there was a
fixed succession of Aaron in the Old Testament, there is therefore a
succession of some fixed person in the New. For Aaron prefigured Christ, the
eternal priest, the perpetuity of whose priesthood was signified by the
perpetual succession.

Objection 8. There is and has been no one who in any way asserted himself to be
Peter’s successor, or was regarded as such, except the bishops of Rome and
Antioch. But the bishop of Antioch did not succeed Peter in the pontificate of
the whole Church. Therefore, the bishop of Rome did. The assumption is proved.
One succeeds only someone who yields his place either through natural death,
deposition, or resignation. But while Peter was alive and exercising the
pontificate, he left the church of Antioch and fixed his see at Rome. Therefore,
the bishop of Rome succeeded Peter when he died at Rome, both in the episcopate
and in all his dignity and power.

Response. This begs the question in several ways, because it is asked whether
Peter was at Rome, whether he was bishop of Rome, and whether he died at Rome.
2. Neither the bishop of Rome nor the

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 76 | printed col. 154 | bsb10175568]

bishop of Antioch succeeded Peter, nor did the bishop of Rome call himself
Peter’s successor before they began to contend ambitiously for the primacy. 3.
The enumeration in the proof of the assumption is insufficient, because
succession can also occur through transfer.

Objection 9. If the bishop of Antioch had succeeded Peter in the supreme
pontificate, he would have been the first bishop. But the Council of Nicaea,
canon 6, declared the bishop of Antioch the third patriarch, and he never sought
a higher place. Therefore.

Response. Neither succeeded Peter in office, though both did with respect to
time. 2. The same council distinguished all the patriarchs by their own
boundaries and thus declared them equal in power. 3. Neither the patriarchate
nor the episcopate in question exists by divine right, much less the papacy.
Finally, it does not follow that because the bishop of Rome is preferred to the
bishop of Antioch, he therefore succeeded Peter in the pontificate.
