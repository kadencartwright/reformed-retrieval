## To James, King of England, Scotland, France, and Ireland

To the most noble and august King: grace and peace from God the Father and our
Lord Jesus Christ, unto every happiness—in this life, piety and justice; and in
the life to come, immortality and glory.

Our sorrow, most noble King, at the setting of the most serene Queen
Elizabeth—a sorrow scarcely capable of consolation, arising both from her
absence and our longing for her and from the inrush of countless evils in fear
and expectation of which we were held—your rising in our heaven not only
eased, but entirely dispelled. For she, like the brightest Moon, had once
illumined our darkness in the Marian times, darkness deeper than Cimmerian;
and she had no sooner set than Your Highness, like a certain Sun, shone upon
the pious and religious citizens of your kingdom. It would shine upon them
all, unless they should set their eyes against the light both of true doctrine
and of your life, lived blamelessly from your earliest years.[^1]

That you hold the scepter of all Britain without dust or bloodshed, as though
by a truly divine wand—a thing which otherwise, to use the Prophet's words,
could in no likelihood have happened without the greatest conflict, garments
rolled in blood, and finally flames and fire—has been accomplished by the
incomparable benefit of our God, one that can never be sufficiently
proclaimed. It is especially a benefit to you, most serene King: just as the
peace and safety of your citizens deeply gladdens you, so it would have kept
you sorrowful and sad to behold everything belonging to your subjects covered
in slaughter and fire. It is likewise an extraordinary and singular benefit
to us, who had come to expect for ourselves and our families a sentence of
death, the loss of our goods, the violation of our women, and finally
Babylonian confusion.

Here, therefore, Your Highness and we alike must strive with every effort—or
rather, every exertion—to leave to all posterity a testimony to the grateful
remembrance of our hearts toward God, who has turned rains into clear weather,
darkness into light, and storms and whirlwinds into calm tranquility. Since
God willed Your Highness, after himself, to be the minister of so great and so

marvelous a bounty toward us, all our rightful duties, already owed to you by
your title as our sovereign, are now due by a still tighter bond, if a tighter
one can be formed, because we enjoy both peace and holiness so unexpectedly
and beyond all hope.

Yet to the common duty of your citizens and subjects is added a certain duty
peculiar to me, by which Your Highness holds me and everything of mine bound
to you, once God is set apart. For it has never been possible for me to forget
that by Your Highness's own letters you deigned, some twenty years ago, to
call me to a professorship of theology in one of your universities. The fame
of your virtue, which had then begun to shine—and has now grown illustrious and
spread its rays far and wide—would have made me fly there with the utmost speed
and eagerness, had I not been prevented by the pastoral bond by which I was
then bound to the English church at Antwerp.

It was also an instance of rare and incomparable humanity that, once raised
to the throne, you lowered your eyes to prisons very far distant from you.
Although otherwise wholly occupied in the administration of the kingdom, you
deigned to write letters for the release of Udall and me from the imprisonment
in which we were held because of our zeal to restore a holier discipline in
our church—as I learned from a report beyond doubt.

For this reason I wished these reflections of ours on Ecclesiastes, witnesses
to my highest reverence, to appear under your name, most powerful King, so
that, hidden under your protection, I may remain sheltered and safe against
the slanders of enemies and unfair critics—hunters after petty words rather
than students of facts and truth. May the Lord Jesus preserve Your Highness
unharmed, raised to this most ample realm to the unbelievable joy of every
godly citizen, and daily increased and adorned with heavenly gifts of the
Holy Spirit suited to the discharge of the royal office. And may that same
Lord, having rescued Your Highness for the church and commonwealth from every
treacherous conspiracy both of anointed and thoroughly anointed priests and
of the Jesuits—the prince-slayers of our age, if that expression is as sound
Latin as it is true—guard and preserve you unharmed for as long as possible.

Your Majesty's most humble subject,

Thomas Cartwright.

## To the Reader

The question concerning the highest good of man is an ancient one, ranged
over the fields of philosophers of every kind. They have taken such widely
varying positions on the matter that they have filled the world with several
hundred opinions about it. Ordinary people, meanwhile—the unlearned crowd of
human beings—have fashioned a happiness for themselves according as each is
swept away by sense, disordered passions, and pursuits. But what judgment can
the blind make about colors, miserable and unhappy people about happiness, or
those who are situated in evil, as John says, about the highest and greatest
good? We must instead turn our attention to what the blessed God, the best and
greatest God, pronounces about blessedness and about the highest and greatest
good.

Although this is explained here and there and at intervals in several books
of the sacred page, it is discussed uniquely and continuously throughout this
whole book of Ecclesiastes, and by that discussion it is defined with complete
certainty. In investigating it the Prophet proceeds in such a way that, after
dispersing and refuting the shadows and masks of happiness belonging to the
wise and foolish, the learned and unlearned alike, he vividly depicts true and
unvarnished happiness by its fruits both of piety toward God and of justice
toward human beings.

This book, therefore, has been amply commended by the very scribe and, as it
were, chief recorder of the Holy Spirit—the wisest of all mortals who have ever
existed or will exist hereafter. It ought also to be commended most highly by
this subject of happiness, so expansive and so elegantly described.

Your zeal, however, ought not to be delayed or broken by the rash and
inconsiderate judgment of certain people concerning this book. From certain
statements sprinkled here and there, they have imagined that it somehow
tastes and smells of the leaven of the Epicureans, Aristippeans, and atheists.
The eunuch's question in Luke's Acts is in fact decidedly ingenious and very
acute: “Does the Prophet speak in his own person, or in that of someone else?”
That question, skillfully and intelligently raised concerning the passage of
Isaiah that the eunuch was reading incorrectly, should be transferred and
applied to the right interpretation of all the Prophets.

So far is this book from opening a school for Epicureans and those who locate
the highest good in pleasure that, on the contrary, in chapter 2 it regards
the pleasure-seeking life as a kind of madness and frenzy. And since in
chapter 12 it sets down as the sum of everything the fear of God and the
keeping of his commandments, while the whole book cries out that everything
under the sun is utterly vain, it is manifest that this doctrine does not
proceed from the workshop of atheists. When the book occasionally enjoins the

<!-- Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PP6 | address to the reader, conclusion -->

liberal enjoyment of food and drink, it does not do so as though it measured
everything by the sensation of pleasure. Rather, it does so while reproving
miserly and joyless people, who deprive themselves of the necessary and due
supports of this life rather than fail to reach and obtain the great mass of
money they covet and have fixed upon in their minds.

But I would rather that you learn the treasures of this book from Solomon
himself than from my own slight commendation. Besides the homilies, I have
cast the entire book into a paraphrase, partly so that the Prophet's sequence
and ordering may be understood better; partly so that busier readers, who
have no leisure to read the whole book, may obtain what they seek in a shorter
compass without being diverted from other business for too long; and, as for
readers more learned and practiced in this theological course, so that by
reading my writings they may not be drawn away from works of greater and
better weight.

Farewell, most courteous reader. Bring to your reading the same mind that I
bring to my writing, that is, one sincere and loving the truth.
