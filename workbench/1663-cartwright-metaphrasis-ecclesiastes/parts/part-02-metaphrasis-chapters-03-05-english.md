CHAPTER III.

VERSE 1. For everything there is its season, and a time for every purpose
under the heavens.

Having explained the things I experienced in myself to prove the vanity of
human life, I now address what I also observed in others within the common
society of life. Concerning these things, let this universal statement stand,
which I gathered from attending to each particular: God has appointed a time
for everything. Just as no one can hasten and anticipate it, so no one can
delay it or prevent it when it must now be fulfilled. This holds good both in
natural affairs and in civil affairs, which are conducted by human will and
deliberation. I attribute this alteration of things through the revolution of
time only to things beneath the heavens, excluding those above the heavens,
which remain firm and unchanged.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA17 | printed pp. 17–18]

VERSE 2. A time to give birth, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time
to uproot what was planted.

I wish to illustrate this general statement by enumerating and reviewing its
parts, beginning first with things that occur by nature's impulse. A definite
and appointed time exists when an infant formed in the womb comes forth into
the light at birth. There is likewise a time when a person either perishes by
a violent death or, having completed the course of life, yields to nature.
What is seen in things governed by Nature may also be perceived in those
conducted by human will and choice. There is a time to plant trees and sow a
crop; afterward that planting and sowing is followed by uprooting.

VERSE 3. A time to kill, and a time to heal; a time to tear down, and a time
to build.

There is a time when people, directed by divine impulse, strike with whips and
rods and sometimes even inflict a mortal wound. At another time, in a reversal
of circumstances, they apply their minds to the work of healing. There is a
time when they demolish erected buildings, which not much later they restore
and rebuild.

VERSE 4. A time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to
dance.

There is a time when, under the pressure of calamity, people are squalid with
grief and tears; then again, drenched with gladness, they break into laughter.
There is a time when everything abounds with mourning and wailing; then that
exultation of mind returns which bursts forth in bodily movement or dancing,
as a witness to the mind's rejoicing.

VERSE 5. A time to cast away stones, and a time to gather stones; a time to
embrace, and a time to withdraw far from embracing.

There is a time to cast aside stones and other materials prepared for the
construction of buildings when the work begun is interrupted; then again, by
collecting the same things after they have been scattered and cast aside, to
resume the interrupted work. There is a time when spouses enjoy mutual
companionship and conjugal embraces; there is also a time when, because of
illness and other intervening causes, they are restrained from mutual
embraces.

VERSE 6. A time to acquire, and a time to lose; a time to keep, and a time to
cast away.

There is a time very well suited to gathering wealth. Then again times arise
when not only are we not permitted to make a profit, but necessity requires
us to lose what we have acquired. The same principle applies when someone can
store and preserve what labor has obtained; on the other hand, we are
sometimes driven into such straits that we are forced to expose what we have
preserved as spoil for others.

VERSE 7. A time to tear, and a time to sew; a time to be silent, and a time to
speak.

There is a time when you unsew what was sewn and sew what was unsewn, just as
there is a time when one is permitted to speak and, on the contrary, a time
when silence is imposed.

VERSE 8. A time to love, and a time to hate; a time for war, and a time for
peace.

Among private persons love and hatred have their alternating turns, just as
kingdoms and commonwealths receive an opportunity to join battle with an enemy
and to establish peace with the same. It would be a long journey to go through
everything. I shall conclude in a word: nothing exists in the whole universe,
whether governed immediately by God through the law of Nature or directed by
him through the intervention of human deliberation and will, which is not
subject to manifold change.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA19 | printed pp. 19–20]

VERSE 9. What advantage is there for the one who acts, in that at which he
labors?

What, then, will labor and industry profit a person when he strives to obtain
his desires, since nothing is brought to the desired end unless God favors
it, on whom the accomplishment of all things depends? I do not say this so
that he should cast aside zeal for labor and give himself over to idleness and
sloth, but so that he should not trust his deliberation and labor in such a
way that he neglects to seek God's aid and blessing by prayer. Just as God
holds times and the moments of times within his hand and power, so, moved by
the prayers of his people, he dispenses them for their benefit.

VERSE 10. I saw that this is the occupation God gave to the sons of men, that
they might occupy themselves in it.

If someone objects here that human affairs are turned at random by fortune's
revolving wheel, he entangles himself in error not because of the sequence of
events but because of his own ignorance. For I know very well that God
entrusted mortals with this business, that they might occupy themselves in
it.

VERSE 11. He makes each and every thing beautiful in its time; he also sets
the age, for as long as it lasts, before their mind—except for what man does
not attain—the work that God himself performs, from beginning to end.

He did this so that by diligent investigation of everything they might learn
that God made each thing beautifully and skillfully in its own time. Just as
he entrusted them with this business, so he willed that, as they attentively
considered the causes and outcomes of things, the whole of this should lie
open and exposed before their minds throughout his entire administration,
from beginning to end—except only those things which God, concealing them
from human beings, reserved for his own knowledge.

VERSE 12. I know that there is no good within their power, except that each
person should rejoice and do good in his life.

Again, some object that godly people, entangled in this mutability of things,
have nothing of their own by which they may be distinguished from the
ungodly. I know very well that amid so much change there is nothing better
than that, entrusting everything to God, they should be cheerful and glad
after living a godly and righteous life.

VERSE 13. Indeed, whenever a person eats and drinks and enjoys good from all
his labor, that is the gift of God.

I do not wish this to be understood as though it came to them either by their
own merit or at their own discretion, since whoever lives comfortably from
his labor by eating and drinking receives that very thing—however small it
may seem to human beings—as God's free gift.

VERSE 14. I know that whatever God does endures forever; nothing can be added
to it, and nothing can be taken from it; and God himself acts so that people
may fear before his face.

Finally, some rise up to charge God's administration of human affairs with
imperfection because of this frequent alteration into contrary conditions. I
reply that it is by far the most perfect administration. First, I know that
everything God does is eternal in accordance with his decree made before the
foundations of the world were laid, and also in its use and the end to which
it is directed. Second, it is so complete and perfect in all its parts that
nothing can be added to it or taken from it. If someone should infer from this
that all deliberation, caution, and care in conducting affairs are thereby
removed, let him receive this answer:

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA21 | printed pp. 21–22]

God does this so that, restrained from evil deeds by fear of him as by a
bridle, they may devote themselves to godliness.

VERSE 15. What was already exists, and what will be has already existed; and
finally, God himself brings back things that follow one another.

Although human blindness causes everything amid such frequent change to
appear to be carried upside down at random and in disorder, nevertheless, for
those whose eyes God has opened, everything is administered in the most
orderly and fitting manner. For what once existed now recurs, and what will
exist hereafter existed long ago. Just as in the revolution of the heavenly
spheres each part succeeds another, so that what has now been driven onward
returns again in its own time, so in his administration of human affairs God
repeats and calls back in their time things that have passed and gone by.

VERSE 16. For still, looking under the Sun, I saw wickedness there in the
place of law—wickedness, I say, there in the place of justice.

To confirm further the vanity of everything beneath the Sun, I observed
wickedness running rampant under the appearance and name of law upon the very
seat of law and judgment, as though in the sanctuary of Justice, and injustice
being practiced in the place sacred to justice—to say nothing of the private
contracts of human beings.

VERSE 17. I said within my mind: God himself will judge the righteous and the
wicked, because there is a time for every purpose, and the one who presides
over this whole affair is there as well.

Although everything may appear to be thrown into confusion by the will and
caprice of wicked people, nevertheless, within my own mind—at the very time
when wickedness held dominion—I determined that God would judge both the
righteous and the wicked according to each one's deeds. For although wicked
people have their own time, when the reins are loosened and they have power to
do whatever they please, God is nevertheless present and presides over this
whole affair, so as to bring each thing into judgment at the proper time.

VERSE 18. I had said within my mind, according to human reason, when God
himself revealed them and I saw that these men were beasts toward one another.

From this confused disorder of affairs—when this human violence and fraud was
revealed to me by God, through which I perceived that man is a beast to man in
his desire and attempt to do harm—I reasoned within my mind, following the
judgment and argument of my depraved and corrupted reason:

VERSE 19. The outcome of the sons of man is like the outcome of the beast,
and they have the same outcome: as this one dies, so that one dies; and there
is the same breath in them all. Therefore man has no superiority over beasts,
for all these things are vanity.

That in their mutual attempts to destroy one another, as also in all other
respects, the condition of man and beast is equal; that one and the same
outcome awaits both man and beast; that they are not distinguished in death,
in drawing and releasing breath; and finally, that there is nothing in which
man surpasses the beast that prevents both from being equally subject to
vanity.

VERSE 20. Each of them goes to the same place; each is from dust, and each
returns to dust.

Both hasten to one and the same place; both, taken from dust, are dissolved
into the dust from which they were formed and fashioned.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA23 | printed pp. 23–24]

VERSE 21. Who perceives the human spirit that ascends above, and the spirit
of the beast that descends below into the earth?

If someone should perhaps object here that when the spirits of both depart
from the body, the human spirit is carried upward into heaven, while the
beast's is pressed downward into the earth, I ask who can be found who is able
to confirm that he has seen this with his eyes?

VERSE 22. Therefore it seemed to me that there is no good except that a person
should rejoice in his works, because this is his portion; for who will bring
him to see what will be after him?

Therefore, if the outcome of man and beast is the same, I conclude that there
is no better or more desirable good for a person than to put far away every
care of obtaining another life—which does not exist anywhere—and to give
himself wholly, wherever he turns, to cheerfulness and a pleasure-seeking
life. For this is the only fruit he can receive from his labors. For has
anyone ever been found who, returning alive from the dead, could inform him
about the things that take place after death, so that, warned by this report,
he might prepare himself to enjoy good in the age to come?

VERSE 23. But again, when I saw all the oppressed who are afflicted under the
Sun, behold, there were the tears of those who are oppressed, but there was no
comforter for them, nor any strength to escape from the hand of those who
oppressed them; in short, there was no comforter for them.

Turning the eyes of my mind away from the more powerful, who tyrannically
oppress those subjected to them, I directed my thought to the oppressed, whom
they wear down by their injustice under the Sun. Please attend to what I found
among them. Although all places everywhere were filled with the tears, groans,
and sighs of those who are oppressed, no one was found to apply consolation.
And although the oppressed lacked the strength by which to free themselves
from the tyranny and power of their oppressors, no one came running to relieve
them or even dared to approach them more closely.

VERSE 24. At first I praised the dead, who are already dead, above the living,
who are still alive.

Then I—though following the perception of the flesh and of depraved
reason—began to praise the condition of those who had died long before above
that of those who were still among the living.

VERSE 25. But afterward one who has not yet existed, who has not seen the
worst deed that is done under the Sun, seemed better than both.

What is more, I preferred above both the living and the dead the condition of
those who were never born, since they have experienced none of the evils done
under the Sun and directed against the inhabitants of the earth; whereas
those who have finished life, like those now afflicted while living, were once
forced to endure the very same evils the living now bear.

VERSE 26. For I saw that every labor and all the uprightness of one's deeds
was a cause of envy toward a man from his neighbor. This too is vanity and
affliction of spirit.

Afterward I turned my mind to the consideration of another evil. I discovered
that people are oppressed not only by those who are more powerful and armed
with authority, but also by their equals and sometimes even by those inferior
in virtue, honor, or wealth. Because they employ skill in administering
affairs joined with innocence of life, they are burdened by envy and loaded
with injuries arising from envy. These things concern both the oppressors and
the oppressed, those who envy and those who are envied, and finally the
perverse judgments of the flesh arising from both groups. Everything I have
said is marked by the greatest vanity and miserably wounds the human mind.

CHAPTER IV.

VERSE 1. The fool folds his hands and consumes his own flesh.

An idle and slothful man reveals his idleness by folding his hands and arms
and abstaining from labor. Although in his own judgment he seems wise to
himself because he avoids the envy arising from deeds excellently done, he
must nevertheless be regarded as plainly foolish and senseless, since by
avoiding one disadvantage he plunges headlong into many, for he destroys
himself entirely through idleness. First, he shatters the powers and sinews of
his mind through idleness; next, he enervates the powers of his body, which are
preserved by the exercise of labor; finally, when his indolence has squandered
and consumed all his goods, he eventually perishes from hunger and starvation.

VERSE 2. He says, Better is one palm full of rest than two fists full of
trouble and affliction of spirit.

So that he may not seem to be mad without reason, he disguises his sloth by
claiming that it is better to be content with little, provided one leads a
peaceful and quiet life—for in this way he dresses shameful idleness in an
honorable name—than to live amid a great abundance of possessions joined
with trouble and anguish of mind.

VERSE 3. But again I saw that this was vanity under the Sun.

When I applied my mind to these things, I discovered that this too is the
greatest vanity under the Sun.

VERSE 4. There is someone alone who has no second, who has neither son nor
brother; yet there is no end to all his labor, nor is his eye satisfied with
riches, so that he says: For whom do I labor and deprive my soul of good? This
too is vanity and an evil occupation.

Opposed to him in appearance is the man who, though alone and solitary and
possessing no heir or successor, neither son nor relative, nevertheless sets
no measure or end to his labor. His desire to amass wealth is so boundless
that even if he enjoyed everything he saw with his eyes, he would not be
satisfied. Among the other causes of such unlimited and insatiable desire is
this: he does not call himself to account or ever inquire within his own mind,
For what purpose do I wear myself out with labor and deprive myself of
pleasure? Although this may appear more attractive in the judgment of many
than the former vanity of leisure and idleness, in reality and in God's
uncorrupted judgment it yields nothing to the other in shamefulness and
deformity.

VERSE 5. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward from their
labor.

From the grave disadvantage of the miser who seeks solitude, it is perfectly
clear that the society of human life is beneficial to everyone. For two
persons associated with each other are better off, even if they are weaker
and poorer, than one who, trusting in himself and his riches, spurns the
society of life that God beneficially granted. The association of two contains
great protection both for undertaking labor and for preserving possessions
obtained by labor.

VERSE 6. For if they fall, one of them raises his companion; but woe to the
one who is alone when he falls and no second person is present to raise him.

To recount every benefit of this society one by one would require an endless
discourse. Therefore I shall select a few from that boundless number, from
which it will be easy to judge the rest. If one falls either in mind or body,
whether through illness or the loss of his fortune, the other, if present,
will help him either by counsel or effort. But destruction threatens the
solitary person, bereft of all friends and dependents, since he has no one to
restore him when he has fallen.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA27 | printed pp. 27–28]

VERSE 7. Indeed, if two lie down, they become warm; but how would one alone
become warm?

This union and sharing of duties is profitable not only by day but also at
night. For one who lies alone and does not attain the warmth suitable for
sleep and rest will, when a companion is added, grow warm through the
additional heat, whereas otherwise he remains rather cold.

VERSE 8. If someone is stronger than one of them, both will resist him; and a
threefold cord is not quickly broken.

Finally, society is profitable not only in a peaceful and quiet life, but also
in struggle and conflict—at home and in the marketplace, and abroad in war.
For someone who overpowers either one by strength will be overcome when a
companion comes running to his aid. If such extraordinary fruit comes from
the union of only two, far richer consolation in life will be received from
the association of many with one another, provided it is godly and honorable.
For they experience what is celebrated in the proverb: an intertwined cord is
not easily broken.

VERSE 9. Better is a needy and wise youth than an old and foolish king who no
longer knows how to be admonished.

The opinion has prevailed among everyone that the condition of life enjoyed
by kings and princes is by far the happiest; yet this is convicted of manifold
falsehood. First, even if royal eminence is full of dignity—especially when
gray hair is added, venerable in itself and making a king still more worthy of
respect—nevertheless, if he is foolish and neither wise in his own judgment
nor willing to be guided by the sound counsel of others, a stubbornness that
usually accompanies old men, I would prefer to him a youth who is also poor,
two conditions greatly exposed to contempt, provided that he is wise. If the
youth errs through the defect and weakness of his age, he can easily be led
and driven away from his error.

VERSE 10. For the former comes out of chains to reign, while the latter is
born poor even in his own kingdom.

If this is less perceptible to those who do not consider attentively the
youth's wisdom and the other's foolishness as the root, it can nevertheless
be easily recognized in each one's outcome as in the branches and fruit. For
you may see that youth of whom I spoke raised, on account of his wisdom, from
the lowest and most abject condition of life and even from the stench of
prison to kingship or princely rule; while that foolish king is thrust and
cast down from the royal throne into a workhouse or prison, or some other
condition of life full of disgrace.

VERSE 11. I saw all the living who walk under the Sun joining themselves to
that second youth who will stand in the king's place.

Next, however wise the king may be and however moderately he may rule his
subjects, I nevertheless observed, as I directed the eyes of my mind toward
every part of the world, that almost all subjects, as their zeal toward the
present king cooled, diligently applied their minds to securing the favor of
the heir or successor.

VERSE 12. There is no end to any people; and posterity too will not rejoice in
the one who rules over them, for this too is vanity and affliction of spirit.

Certainly almost every common people is of such a disposition that it never
knows how to rest content with the princes who occupy the kingdom and handle
the helm of the commonwealth. Just as those who lived long ago became weary of
the kings who preceded them, so posterity shows little fitting respect to the
heir in whom they had delighted so greatly when he administers the kingdom
after his father's death, regarding him like a torch that has burned out.

CHAPTER X. [sic; Chapter V]

VERSE 1. Guard both your feet when you go to the very house of God, and be
ready to hear rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools, for they do not
perceive that they are doing evil.

Now that the masks and shadows of happiness in this life, which people
eagerly embrace, have been uncovered and refuted, we must see wherein true
blessedness in this life consists. I choose to assign it to the fear of God,
whose chief part is the worship of God. So that a mere likeness and painted
image may not deceive us in place of the reality and truth itself, before you
set even one foot inside the Temple or another place of divine worship, you
must free your mind from depraved desires so that it may be fit for godly
exercises. Once inside, although divine worship has many kinds and parts,
nothing is greater than listening diligently and attentively to the word
purely presented in the Church, so that you express in deeds what you have
heard. I give this warning because many are found who foolishly place the sum
of divine worship in offering sacrifices and performing other ceremonies.
Their condition is all the more deplorable because, though calling divine
wrath down upon themselves, they are confirmed in their error, since they do
not understand that they provoke God with false worship; and, what is worse,
they persuade themselves that they are offering worship pleasing to God.

VERSE 2. Do not be hasty with your mouth, and do not let your mind hurry to
utter anything before God himself; for God himself is in the heavens, but you
are upon the earth. Therefore let your words be few.

After attentive hearing of the divine voice—to which the proper reception of
the Sacraments is attached and appended as though it were a seal—comes the
direction of our speech to God. Here reflection upon the divine majesty and
greatness, joined with due meditation upon our own most abject condition,
will supply both of these precautions. The first is that we should rush
nothing forward either with our mouth or with our mind, which is the source of
speech; the second, that we should not employ a vain and idle repetition of
one and the same thing.

VERSE 3. For just as a dream comes from much occupation, so the voice of a
fool comes from a multitude of words.

For foolish and senseless speech can no more be separated from that vain
idleness of speech than a dream can be separated from one who was continually
engaged in many occupations during the day.

VERSE 4. When you have made a vow to God, do not delay to fulfill it, for he
does not delight in fools. Fulfill what you have vowed.

Having spoken about every kind of speech addressed to God, I descend to one
particular kind, concerning vows. If it should ever happen that you make a vow
to God about some lawful matter within your control, either to strengthen
faith or to testify gratitude, do not delay to render it at the proper time.
For God does not delight in those whose hands are too grasping and tight to
fulfill in deed what they promise God in words, since he regards them as
nothing other than fools. If delaying the fulfillment of vows displeases God,
see to it that you do not make the vow you pronounced entirely null and void
in actual fact.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA31 | printed pp. 31–32]

VERSE 5. It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not
fulfill it.

For it is better not to have vowed at all than not to fulfill a vow made to
God.

VERSE 6. Do not permit your mouth to bind your flesh to sin, and do not say
before that angel that it was an error. Why should God himself grow angry
because of your voice and destroy the work of your hands?

Take care that with your mouth, one very small member of the body, you do not
bring guilt upon yourself in both body and mind. You do this when you make
vows improperly, or try to rescind and invalidate vows properly made by
calling them an error or an act of imprudence. In this matter, picture
yourself uttering these specious pretexts before Christ, the Angel of the
eternal covenant, who knows thoroughly these evasions and hiding places; and
he, justly inflamed, will utterly ruin both you and your flesh, together with
everything that belongs to you.

VERSE 7. For just as vanities belong to a multitude of dreams, so they will
inhere in many words; but revere God himself.

For vanity hateful to God is no more absent from this rashness, especially
the multiplication of vows, than the emptiest phantasms and momentary,
vanishing little images are absent from a multitude of dreams. There is one
remedy for these errors in the whole worship of God, as also for all the other
vanities set forth thus far: if you devote yourself to true and unfeigned fear
of God.

VERSE 8. If you see the oppression of the poor and the overthrow of law and
justice in the province itself, do not be astonished at that purpose, for one
high official watches above another, and indeed the Most High is above them.

If you see the oppression of the poor—such as the godly and true worshipers
of God generally are—under the appearance of law and judgment, not only in a
single city but throughout an entire province or kingdom, so that an appeal
to a higher court and tribunal gains not the slightest benefit, do not be
disturbed by this perverse zeal of human beings, so that you either lack
counsel or call God's providence into doubt, and especially so that you do not
fall away from God and his lawful worship. Let it come to your mind that God's
loftiness surpasses the height of every king and emperor. God's ministers and
attendants, the blessed Angels, are far superior to the subordinate ministers
of kings and princes, and can easily place the godly in safety when they have
been delivered from human tyranny.

VERSE 9. Now the profit of the earth is for everyone; the king is served from
the field.

Now I shall return to the interrupted discussion of the vanity of everything
beneath the Sun. It is worthy of observation that great profit exists in the
cultivation of the earth. Its usefulness reaches everyone without exception,
to such an extent that even a king is in a certain manner compelled to serve
agriculture.

VERSE 10. He who loves money is not satisfied with money, and he who loves
any abundance will not be satisfied with its yield. This too is vanity.

The case of money is very different. For a person who, led by a zeal for
money, obtains a large quantity of it is nevertheless not satisfied by it
when oppressed by hunger. And however much he may burn with the love of
accumulating riches, no benefit comes to him from money if you consider money
itself purely and simply, apart from exchange. Although the condition of the
one who enjoys produce received from a field is better than that of the one
who has amassed a great quantity of money, nevertheless, for establishing
happiness in this life, both are vain.

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA33 | printed pp. 33–34]

VERSE 11. When this good increases, those who consume it increase. What
benefit, therefore, comes to its owner except the sight of his own eyes?

For, to speak of the better of these two, in proportion as someone abounds
plentifully in the fruits of the earth, the number of those prepared to
consume them increases. Thus, if you except the one empty sight of them all,
what benefit, I ask, remains to their owners beyond that enjoyed by their
household servants?

VERSE 12. Sweet is the sleep of one who serves, whether he eats little or
much; but the rich man's abundance does not permit him to sleep.

Someone might perhaps object here that the master's condition is far better
than his servant's, since the master can drink and eat to fullness in the
greatest leisure and rest. But I would regard the condition of the one who
exercises himself in continual labor as better, since he sleeps peacefully
and sweetly whether he has eaten a smaller or fuller supper, than that of the
rich man who, enticed by the variety of dishes at an uncertain supper, eats
to fullness but does not rest at night because of his satiety. For either he
does not sleep, or, if he does, his sleep is restless and troubled.

VERSE 13. There is a grievous evil that I saw under the Sun: riches preserved
for their owner to his own harm.

Now to return to the person whom I said was carried away by zeal and passion
for riches, especially money: examining in mind and with my eyes the conduct
and manner of life of mortals, I observed among this class of people, in
addition to the vanity that clings to those who both use the comforts of this
life themselves and generously share their use with their own people, the far
greater trouble and grief that afflicts those who suffer from this pest and
plague of avarice. For either the riches are preserved to the grave harm of
their owner:

VERSE 14. Or those riches perish in an evil undertaking, so that the son he
has fathered has nothing in his hand.

Or the riches perish while their owner is still alive and looking on, even
though he applies great effort, care, and diligence to place them in safety.
This makes him all the sadder and more troubled because, relying on the riches
he had acquired, he directed his mind to marriage and had children by his
wife, but discovers that everything has been lost both to himself and to his
children, so that nothing remains for him from the great abundance of wealth
he amassed.

VERSE 15. As he came forth from his mother's womb, naked he goes away again
as he came, and he carries away nothing from his labor that he may bear in his
hand.

Thus it happens that, just as he came naked from his mother's womb into this
world, so he will depart from the world unable to take with him any of the
things he obtained by great labor.

VERSE 16. Therefore this too is a grievous evil: exactly as he came, so he
goes away. What advantage does he have because he labored for the wind?

This very thing—to say nothing of the other disadvantages—brings grave
grief: exactly as he came naked, so he will depart and expire stripped of
everything. No advantage from all his labor and toil remains to him, any more
than if he had heaped up light husks or dust of no weight, which the wind
suddenly scatters with a single blast. For when these avaricious people are
deprived of their goods by some calamity, they cast aside nearly every hope of
a better condition, and their eagerness and readiness of mind for making a
living are entirely extinguished; whereas the godly, trusting in God, often
recover their lost wealth, sometimes in greater abundance.

VERSE 17. And has he also eaten throughout all his days in darkness—that is,
with much indignation, grief for himself, and burning anger?

The loss of his goods burns him all the more bitterly because, in order to
make his accumulated wealth greater, he lived sparingly and harshly throughout
his whole lifetime, deprived himself of pleasure, and repeatedly wounded his
mind with indignation, grief, and burning anger over a missed opportunity for
profit. What else is this but to pass one's life perpetually in darkness and
in deprivation of the light that refreshes and cheers the mind?

VERSE 18. Behold what I saw: it is a good and beautiful thing to eat and
drink, and to enjoy good from all one's labor at which one labors under the
Sun during the number of the days of one's life that God himself gives, for
this is one's portion.

I do not wish these words to be understood as though there were no lawful use
of money and riches. For I would have everyone observe what I have learned by
inquiry and by the use of things: it is both advantageous to the one who uses
them and seemly and conducive to a good reputation in the judgment of others,
provided their judgment is not entirely corrupt, if a person so eats, drinks,
dresses, and sleeps that from these things he not only serves the necessity
of this life but also allows something for honorable pleasure, so that he may
more easily bear the trouble of labor. This is especially so since the life
God has granted us to live under the Sun is confined within narrow spans of
time and is measured in days rather than years, and above all since this is
the whole of what he can receive from all his labor, seeing that he will
depart naked from this life and leave behind all the things he obtained with
great care and labor.

VERSE 19. Indeed, whenever God himself gives a person riches and resources,
and grants him the ability to eat from them, receive his portion, and rejoice
in his labor, this is the gift of God.

For whoever receives riches and resources freely from God—without whose
blessing all human skill and labor are fruitless—and whose mind God also
enlarges so that, in order to bear the trouble of labor, he incurs such
expenses for food and clothing as to cheer his mind by enjoying those goods,
according to the portion that is just and due to him, for it is not right to
swallow and devour everything: I ascribe this readiness and inclination of
mind toward honorable uses, however sacrilegiously people claim it for
themselves, to God's generosity no less than the bestowal of riches itself.

VERSE 20. But if he does not have much, he will nevertheless remember the days
of his life, for God himself responds to the gladness of his mind.

If someone should object here that it is not difficult to enjoy gladness of
mind amid a great abundance of riches and possessions, while this is not
equally granted amid poverty, I reply that this joy of mind is received even
in the right use of a slender fortune, provided it never falls from his memory
that God, who alone knows how to fill minds with gladness, will compensate for
this slenderness of possessions with a greater measure of joy.
