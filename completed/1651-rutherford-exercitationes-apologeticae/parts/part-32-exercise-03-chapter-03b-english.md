4. I now come to the second point. It is the failure to cure native
obstinacy; this failure to cure comes from God alone. For God alone cures.
Therefore, by the law of affirmation and negation, God’s failure to cure will
be the adequate and total cause of native obstinacy not being cured—not of
native obstinacy simply, but of obstinacy not being cured. In the same way, a
physician who does not cure all curable diseases, even though by skill in his
art he can cure them, is not the cause of the diseases; he can

[1651 logical printed p. 488 | IA leaf 506 | PDF p. 506]

nevertheless be called the physical, though not the moral, cause of the
diseases not being cured. For if a physician is bound and hired to cure, and
then does not cure, he is the cause of the disease not being healed: not only
the physical cause, because he did not cure it, but also the ethical and moral
cause, because he did not cure it even though he was bound to cure it by his
office. God, then, is considered here in three ways: 1. physically; 2.
ethically, as acting from his good pleasure alone; 3. ethically, as an agent
bound to act by a debt of justice. In the first way, God is the physical cause
of native obstinacy not being cured, since he can cure it in everyone and yet
does not cure it. Therefore he is the cause in the way the sun, by its
absence, is said to be the cause of night, since by its presence in our
hemisphere it is the cause of day. In Arminius’s comparison about a building’s
collapse because supports were not placed under it, he argues sophistically
from positing a physical cause to positing an effect that is not physical but
moral—things that no art can confound. For God’s denial of predetermining
grace, which is the physical cause of privative and culpable nonconversion,
is in no way causally terminated in culpable nonconversion, but only in
negative nonconversion, as a physical cause in a physical effect. The corrupt
will, which is the ethical cause, is terminated in culpable nonconversion, as
a moral cause in a moral effect, but in no way in negative nonconversion,
which is a physical, not a moral, effect. Thus the

[1651 logical printed p. 489 | IA leaf 507 | PDF p. 507]

cause why nothing is written on a given sheet of paper is, I answer, the pen
that does not blacken it; the absence of blackening, and the absence of
skillful non-blackening, comes from the writer withholding his art. Therefore
from this causation by God there follows bare physical and negative
nonconversion, not, properly speaking, privative and culpable nonconversion.
For guilt in no way follows from a precisely physical cause. Guilt is a moral
defect and therefore cannot have its origin except from a moral and deficient
cause. Thus from the physical failure to place a support there follows the
physical collapse of a building, as Arminius’s comparison has it. If a master
is bound to sustain his servant’s house with supports placed beneath it, the
house’s collapse is morally imputed to the master who does not place the
supports that by covenant or pledged faith he ought to have placed. The
Arminian comparison presses this latter sense, yet concludes nothing. For God
converts no one from a debt, but from his own good pleasure. In the second
way, if God is considered as acting from his good pleasure—and God is only
such an agent here—nonconversion is in no way morally imputed to God. For by
no covenant, decree, or office did God bind himself to convert men. No such
decree or covenant of God exists from any promise: [Do this, and I will call
you efficaciously]; [He who does this will receive the reward of conversion
as compensation for the deed in his labor]. Since the Arminians and Jesuits
have never demonstrated this in the whole of Scripture, or from any attribute
of God, down to this

[1651 logical printed p. 490 | IA leaf 508 | PDF p. 508]

day, nor are they able to show it, God is not bound to convert anyone who
wills to be converted, even on that hypothesis. Neither Adam before the fall,
nor the angels who fell, nor any man has God’s prevenient motion in his own
power; nor is the lack of God’s efficacious motion morally imputed to anyone.
No law commands me either to have it or not to have it. For then acts of God
would be commanded to creatures—what, good God, could be more foolish? Are we
commanded to bind God to act or not to act? Therefore a great difference lies
between our being commanded acts that result causally from God’s motion—from
God’s efficacious motion, I say—and our being commanded the efficacious motion
itself. The former is equitable; the latter, inequitable. Indeed, God works in
us both to will and to accomplish, Philippians 2:13, ὑπὲρ τῆς εὐδοκίας, *for
his good pleasure*: therefore not from debt, since it thus pleased God,
Matthew 11:26, and therefore not because it was owed. Consequently, in the
third way God can in no respect be considered here as an agent acting from a
debt of justice or natural equity.

5. I proceed to the third point. It is the voluntary love and volition of
uncured obstinacy, and it is a sin and a consequence of uncured obstinacy.
Culpable nonconversion comes from it. Therefore from God’s free and absolute
failure to cure native obstinacy, and from God’s entirely free nonconversion
of the man, culpable nonconversion and resistance offered to God

[1651 logical printed p. 491 | IA leaf 509 | PDF p. 509]

when he calls follow, not as an effect from a cause, but as a consequent from
an antecedent. I say there are two reasons here why resistance and culpable
nonconversion ought not to be cast upon God as their moral cause. 1. No cause
is morally guilty because it does not act unless it is bound to act by a debt,
law, or covenant. But God is not bound to act here, nor is he bound not to
act; rather, he has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills. 2.
Between God’s nonconversion and resistance there intervenes a voluntary love
of native obstinacy and the free volition of the corrupt will. By it a person
who is bound to obey God when he calls, and therefore to believe and repent,
does not will to believe and repent. Grevinchovius therefore argues badly:
The denial of predetermining aid (he says) is the adequate cause of the
lack of the good use of grace and of resistance. But the denial of
predetermining aid comes from God alone. Therefore the lack of the good use of
grace, and thus the whole of resistance and nonconversion in man, comes from
God alone. Reply 1. The denial of predetermining aid is not the cause of the
lack of the good use of grace and of resistance—indeed, it is no cause at all.
How, then, is it the adequate cause? Let him prove this: when we ask for
proofs, we shall hear utter silence. We grant that resistance follows from
the denial of predetermining aid as a consequent from an antecedent; we deny
that it follows as an effect from a cause. If he had proved this, he would
have injured our cause.

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, pp. 250–251.]

[1651 logical printed p. 492 | IA leaf 510 | PDF p. 510]

For between the denial of predetermining aid and resistance there intervenes
the free volition of a depraved will, from which resistance causally arises.
But since resistance arises from a cause morally bound to act, while God, who
denies predetermining aid, is not a cause morally bound, God contributes
nothing effective—no causal being at all—to resistance. We therefore deny
that the culpable nonuse of grace, privative and culpable nonconversion, comes
causally from God.

6. But they fasten upon our saying that the will cannot will anything else or
in any other way than as it is subject to God’s predetermining motion.
Therefore, since that predetermination is not in our power, nonconversion is
imputed to God, not to us, who can do nothing else or in any other way than
what the determining grace drives us to do. Reply. [The will subjected to the
determination of grace cannot will anything other than what it wills] is
equivalent to this: [the will actually choosing this and not that cannot
choose anything other than what it chooses]. For God’s determination and the
human determination agree, in a wonderful union, upon the same object in
number, measure, and intention. There is only this difference: God’s
determination is external, equivocal, nonvital, and principal; the human
determination is internal, univocal, vital, and subordinate. For if the lack
of divine predetermination, in the will of an unconverted man willing
something else—or, as regards eternal futurition, in the will that would will
and be inclined—

[1651 logical printed p. 493 | IA leaf 511 | PDF p. 511]

stood in the way and drove it forcibly elsewhere, the argument would prove
something. But God’s predetermination bends the converted man’s will toward
the very thing toward which, as regards eternal futurition, that will bends
itself vitally, electively, and subordinately. And although the unconverted do
not have God’s predetermination in their own power, they are nevertheless
rightly guilty and blameworthy, because their depraved will tends most freely
toward the same thing toward which God’s gracious non-predetermination tends,
according to the decree of reprobation. They are thus liable to condemnation
by this very fact: their will consequentially, interpretatively, and in the
effect itself—namely, culpable free nonconversion—subscribes to gracious
non-predetermination; that is, they place their seal upon God’s free denial of
determining aid. Now the conversion of Judas and the nonconversion of Judas
cannot both have eternal futurition, for that is simply impossible.
Accordingly, God’s predetermination, coming upon Judas’s inclination and
determining it while preserving freedom, toward what Judas in fact chooses
with the whole vicious impulse of his freedom, in no way impairs either
Judas’s guilt or his freedom. When the higher cause is physical and released
from law, while the lower cause is moral and subject to law, even though the
higher cause physically influences the lower, yet, if the lower is free, the
lower can be guilty while the higher is not. Thus suppose an absolute monarch
who is above some positive human law consents that a subject violate that
law.

[1651 logical printed p. 494 | IA leaf 512 | PDF p. 512]

And I suppose that the same subject, of himself and of his own accord, would
violate that law even on the supposition that the monarch who is above such a
law did not consent. Here the king is not guilty, while the subject is guilty
of violating the law. The reason is that guilt comes from the obligation of
the one who, being a rational agent, is subjected to law. But guilt does not
arise formally from any physical influence; otherwise the sword, poison, and
other things that contribute physical causal influence to a murder would be
subject to guilt, which is ridiculous.

7. The fourth point is faith itself and the supernatural act. That it exists,
and that it is supernatural, comes from nothing other than the actual
efficiency or efficacy of divine grace. Therefore, as Alvarez noted, God
alone is the cause of conversion. For God alone calls efficaciously, but he
is not the sole cause of the free act of conversion. The human will must not
be denied its own subordinate, elective, and vital activity here.

8. But the Arminians do not explain themselves sufficiently concerning the
way in which God is bound to furnish his concurrence to everyone who is
called. I have never been able to establish from their writings whether this
obligation of God is founded upon God’s natural equity or upon God’s free
promise. For God helps us when we make good use of common grace by efficacious
grace either from a free ordinance and positive promise or from natural
equity. If it is from a free ordinance, he can then fail to give efficacious
grace and yet

[1651 logical printed p. 495 | IA leaf 513 | PDF p. 513]

impute the fault to us; and thus God will reap where he did not sow, which the
Arminians maintain is contrary to God’s natural equity. But if it is from the
necessity of natural equity, then after the first gracious act God will
necessarily concur toward the next, and because of that act, from a debt of
justice, toward the one after it, and so on to infinity. After the first
gracious act, therefore, it would follow that no one ever sins thereafter;
but the daily falls of the saints and the sacred oracles cry out against this.
By the same reasoning it would also follow that after the first act of
disobedience God ought, by a debt of justice, to deny efficacious grace toward
the third and fourth acts. Thus a person who once sinned cannot but always sin
thereafter by the order of justice. The fact that many ungodly people turn
themselves toward the fruit of a sounder life makes it evident that this too
is utterly false. But they say, on the hypothesis that the will first by
nature furnishes its own influence, God promised that he would also furnish
his efficacious influence. Yet God then furnishes his influence not by a free
ordinance or liberal promise but by natural equity; indeed, he cannot fail to
furnish it even in the most wicked apostates. Otherwise there would be an
action of the second cause without the influence of the first cause. And what
does it profit a holy person to have elicited many acts of obedience if all
those acts are compensated not by efficacious motion, but only by that motion
by which, if he willed, he could in the future have in his own power

[1651 logical printed p. 496 | IA leaf 514 | PDF p. 514]

an efficacious motion of God? For I remember Arminius saying: *It appears to
me* (he says) *that this promise may perhaps be contained in that saying of
Christ, [to him who has, it will be given], by which God pledges of his own
accord that he will illuminate with supernatural grace the person who uses
the light of nature rightly, or at least uses it as little badly as he can.*
From this text of Arminius it follows that, by his promise, God is bound to
grant efficacious converting grace to those who have been upright and
humbler; for to him who has, it will be given. And according to Arminius, an
act of conversion will be given to a person who has many good acts of nature.
But then through this promise, at least after conversion, he would never sin.
For the truthful God would furnish a person who has one gracious act with
efficacious motion toward another gracious act, and after that toward another.
Yet I know the Arminians will reply that, by God’s promise, it is given to the
one who has provided he wills to receive it and has willed freely to
contribute his own influence. Reply. But then it will also be given to the one
who does not have. Christ, however, says that what the one who does not have
once had will be taken away. For God’s efficacious motion will be given to one
who has no good acts, provided he himself wills freely to contribute his
influence. Indeed, if the devil and the most abandoned apostates should will
freely, could the Creator and first cause deny his efficacious influence? I
believe he could not; indeed, if he could, there would be an act of the second
cause without the action of the first. Thus now, Arminians, [to him who has,
it will be given] becomes for you: it will be given to the one who does not
have; and [from the one who does not have, it will be taken away] becomes for
you: to the one who does not have

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkinian*, p. 758.]

[1651 logical printed p. 497 | IA leaf 515 | PDF p. 515]

it will be given, not taken away. Yet we did not succeed in obtaining this
from the Arminians here: that we should leave God his absolute freedom in
conversion. Nevertheless, they do not deny God’s freedom in sending the
Savior to redeem the human race, although he absolutely denied such a remedy
to the fallen angels, Hebrews 2:16. Nor will they deny that God could have
called each and every person in such a way that they would be converted with
their freedom unimpaired.

9. Objection 2. If freedom remains under this determination, it remains either
with respect to receiving the extrinsic determination or with respect to its
own influence. Not the first, because, as Grevinchovius says, the will is
passive in receiving God’s determination, and passion as passion is not a free
act, as Corvinus says. Nor can the second be said, for a pen is not called
free in writing because it is guided by a free agent. Nor will our will be
free if God as principal agent so turns it that it does not will, choose, and
reject in such a way as to be able not to will, not to choose, and not to
reject.
Thus Marcus Antonius de Dominis. Reply 1. Our writers do not teach that
freedom formally consists in receiving the divine determination. For no law
commands us either to have or not to have that determination of God. 2. After
the will has now received God’s determination, it is not properly in potency
to act, because it is now actually acting freely. No more does

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, pp. 245–246.]

[Marginalia: *Against Molina*, ch. 3, fol. 242.]

[Marginalia: Marc. Ant., Archbishop of Spalato, *On the Ecclesiastical
Republic*, book 7, ch. 11, fig. 412.]

[1651 logical printed p. 498 | IA leaf 516 | PDF p. 516]

God’s determination overturn freedom than freedom’s own actual exercise
overturns freedom; and yet freedom actually acts freely with respect to its
own influence. 3. Our will is not called free by participation because it is
the instrument of God as a free agent. For a pen writes by no innate choice
and judgment. But God uses the will in such a way that the will itself acts
electively, vitally, and from the practical judgment of reason. They object
nothing else to us here than the Pelagians once objected to Augustine and
Prosper: *We defend God’s work: neither by his power is anyone unwilling
forced into either evil or good; effort belongs to one who does not resist.* The
Pelagians claim to defend God’s work; the Arminians, the nature of freedom.

[Marginalia: book 1, *Against Two Letters of the Pelagians*, ch. 18, and book
2, ch. 6.]

Objection 3. Natural causes have the power not to operate in the divided
sense. Thus they can cease from motion if God ceases to move them. Indeed, a
white wall in the divided sense can be black, because together with whiteness
it retains potency to blackness. Therefore power in the divided sense is not
enough for the active power of choice. Indeed, what else is that power in the
divided sense than the power to lack grace if it is not given, or to lack the
consent toward which grace moves if the grace given is removed? In this way a
stone projected upward can, in the divided sense, lack the impulse by which it
is moved. Grevinchovius. Reply. Following Suarez, his teacher, Grevinchovius
raves. For 1. we do not establish the bare and simple cessation from an act
of refusing and willing as

[Marginalia: Grevinchovius, *Against Ames*, p. 160.]

[1651 logical printed p. 499 | IA leaf 517 | PDF p. 517]

the active power of freedom in the divided sense, but rather a cessation from
the mind’s judgment and by freedom’s elective power. If he wishes such a power
to be present in a stone or wall, he must become a mushroom and a stone. 2. It
is also absurd to call the power of freedom in the divided sense the lack of
grace. For it is not within our power either to have or to lack efficacious
grace. They wrongly thrust upon us that distinction between the composite and
divided senses insofar as it can in its own way be applied to natural causes.
For we say that God’s gracious determination toward a given act and freedom’s
actual dissent cannot be combined at the same time, and yet under that
determination there remains the elective power to reject. No such power
toward blackness remains in a white wall. What? Since passive potency can be
given in the composite and divided senses, can active and elective potency
therefore not be given in the will? Indeed, the very contrary follows. Suarez
says that this antecedent necessity binds freedom no less strongly than if it
were a merely natural cause. He does not blush to wander in a serious matter;
for it in no way follows. Although God inevitably necessitates freedom’s
event and effect, he does not superimpose inevitable necessity upon freedom,
because it belongs to God’s infinite perfection and omnipotence that he can
necessitate freedom and not overturn it, just as freedom in exercise
necessitates itself and does not overturn itself. For when freedom itself

[1651 logical printed p. 500 | IA leaf 518 | PDF p. 518]

vitally removes its indifference, in the same way predetermining grace removes
that same indifference externally and nonvitally, since God as principal
agent does not bend freedom toward one angle other than that toward which
freedom bends itself subordinately. Accordingly, if they say that God
necessitates freedom, I shall say that freedom then necessitates itself in the
same way. This necessitation does not take away freedom, and therefore neither
does the other. But they will say that God first, principally, and from
eternity determines the free act, and therefore human freedom cannot elicit a
different act. Reply. God first, principally, and from eternity determines
the free act, but not toward another act—rather, toward the very act,
numerically the same, toward which the will determined itself in time. For
God’s eternal determination regards that act as future and as to be posited
freely, though not as already posited. A conditional decree, by contrast, is
said to have this sort of respect and relation to a free act, not as to be
posited but as already posited—indeed, posited antecedently to God’s decree;
this plainly overturns God’s providence.

Objection 4. If God’s determination precedes human volition, there is already
a willing before the man wills. Nor is it necessary that the will will; it
only receives willing passively from the omnipotent one. Thus Lessius. Reply.
There is an equivocation in the word *already* and in the word
*determination*. *Already*

[Marginalia: Lessius, *On Efficacious Grace*.]

[1651 logical printed p. 501 | IA leaf 519 | PDF p. 519]

properly denotes an order of nature. Determination is either external and
effective, which in this matter belongs to God alone, or internal and formal,
which belongs to the created will. I therefore distinguish as follows. If the
human will is [already]—that is, prior in the order of nature—determined,
namely externally and effectively by God, it is not necessary that the will
will because there is already a willing: I deny this. If they mean that God
wills to effect willing in us earlier in time than we will, let them seek
phantoms with whom to fight; we conceive no such thing. But God prior in
nature determines the will, and he alone does so in the genus of external
efficient cause and in the solitude of a predetermining cause. The will
itself, however, later in nature and simultaneously in time determines itself
in the genus of formal cause, and it alone does so in the solitude of a formal
and vitally choosing cause. Therefore three things must be distinguished
here. 1. The will’s vital and elective propensity, which is both natural and
from the will, or supernatural and divinely infused; here the will is only in
a state of potentiality. 2. The actualization of that potency, which is
effectively from God. And 3. the actual volition, which formally, without any
interval of time, is from the will, but from the will now actualized and
determined externally and effectively by God. Therefore it in no way follows
that it is unnecessary for the will to will. It follows only that the
efficiency of τὸ *velle*, willing, is prior in nature to τὸ *velle*, the
willing itself. This is necessary, not absurd, just as the efficiency of a
cause

[1651 logical printed p. 502 | IA leaf 520 | PDF p. 520]

is prior in nature to its effect.

Objection 5. If God determines free acts, he also determines the powers, for
the powers are the causes of the acts. But if the powers are determined, the
power of willing and not willing in no way remains. Thus the Remonstrants.
Reply. I affirm that the will’s particular powers are commensurated to future
acts in number and determined to the praise of glorious providence by him who
numbers the hairs of the head and the drops of rain. But the will’s universal
power remains indeterminate. 2. These doctors do not speak accurately enough
when they call potency the cause of acts. A cause is more excellent than its
effect, but potency is less excellent than act. When the cause is posited, the
effect is posited; but when potency is posited, the act is not posited.
Speaking accurately, therefore, the cause of a free act is a determined
potency, not potency simply.

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Writings of the Synod on the Operation of Grace*,
art. 4, pp. 178, 575.]

Objection 6. If such determining efficacy is posited, earnest endeavor and
godly diligence in doing well will be taken away, for we shall always wait for
such efficacy. Reply. Someone cannot fail to be diligent so long as he does
not perceive such efficacy in conversion; therefore the contrary will follow
instead.
