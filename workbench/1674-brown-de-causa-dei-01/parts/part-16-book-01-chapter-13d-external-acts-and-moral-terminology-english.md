In the following paragraph on pages 7 and 8, he strives at length to show that
no external actions, whatever connection they may have with inward virtue,
are properly moral actions or virtues, but rather actions commanded by virtue.
For example, he denies that acts of beneficence or martyrdom are moral actions,
since they can occur even in an unregenerate person; 1 Corinthians 13:3.
Indeed, he denies that such acts are morally good even when they are performed
from virtue. His reason is that he does not wish even an internal act to be
morally good except insofar as it is elicited by virtue. From this he concludes
that, accurately speaking, nothing is morally good or a virtue except love of
God and one’s neighbor. It is not worth examining these matters at greater
length, but it will suffice to note these few points. 1. We are not speaking
here of either an external or an internal action considered in the *Physical*
order, but as it is considered in the order of *morals*. 2. The question does
not concern what is required for an action, whether internal or external, to
be acceptable and pleasing to God; on this point, I hope, we agree. 3. Rather,
the question concerns the nature of the action commanded and the nature of the
precept which enjoins such an action: namely, whether it is moral or purely
positive. 4. We know that love of God and one’s neighbor is the sum of the
moral law. But to infer from this that no external actions are properly moral
is to erase almost every precept of the Decalogue from the list of morals. For
no external actions commanded in the *second*, *third*, *fifth*, *sixth*,
*seventh*, or *eighth* precept will be moral. What wonder, then, if he denies
that observance of the Sabbath is moral? 5. If no external actions are, properly
speaking, morally good, then neither will any external actions be morally evil;
they will only be commanded by vice. Thus idolatry, superstition, contempt of
parents, perjury, parricide, the murder of children, homicide, sodomy, sexual
relations between males, adultery, theft, robbery, false witness, and so forth
will not be morally evil. Is this a new doctrine, unheard of until now? 6. When
Christ said that love of God and one’s neighbor is the sum of the Law and the
Prophets, or of the moral law, he plainly implied that there are other moral
actions which flow from these loves. 7. What will the adversary say about love
of ourselves? Christ says that the sum of the second table is that we love our
neighbor as ourselves. He therefore presupposes that we too are to be loved,
indeed above our neighbor, since love of ourselves is, as it were, the
measuring rule of the love with which we ought to regard our neighbors. Will
this action, then, also be morally good? 8. If the external actions commanded
in the *First*, *second*, *third*, *fifth*, *sixth*, *seventh*, *eighth*, and
*ninth* precepts must not be called moral even when they are in some measure
conformed to the divine rule and command—I say “in some measure” because I
acknowledge that even our best works are imperfect—that is, insofar as they
flow from a love of God and one’s neighbor which is not perfect, what, finally,
must they be called? Will they be purely positive? He will not assert this,
because he says that only those external actions are positive which cannot in
any way be determined by right reason and have no connection with it. He does
indeed say that they can be referred to morals. But how can things which are
not moral be referred to morals? Is it in a manner different from that in
which even ceremonies can be referred to morals? Who now creates confusion?
9. Who does not see that this doctrine overturns everything

[1674 logical printed p. 171 | IA leaf 189 | PDF p. 189]

he said above, indeed the thesis itself, namely, that “the moral, properly
speaking, is that which flows from the image of God either created together
with the human being before the fall or restored by grace after it, and
therefore that which results from the nature and attributes of God and from
the human being’s natural state under God,” and so forth? 10. Even if we may
not be so discriminating, let the adversary enjoy his liberty, speak as
precisely as he wishes, and call nothing morally good except what he wishes to
be named so. It will be enough for me to speak with ordinary people. As for the
substance of the matter, it makes no difference to me whether the fourth
precept about observing the Sabbath is called moral or not, provided it is
conceded to be of the same nature as the other precepts of the Decalogue and
to bind us no less than the others do. I will readily concede to him that
observance of the Sabbath is not acceptable to God and does not adequately
agree with the command unless it is performed from virtue and has all the
requisite conditions joined to it.

What else does he have? “From this,” he says on page 8, “a most useful
distinction can also be perceived between virtue itself, or the obedience of
the mind, and a commanded action, especially in connection with positive
precepts. For example, abstaining from a particular forbidden fruit is an act
commanded by virtue, or by love of God’s dominion, once the divine precept is
posited. Yet that abstinence is not virtue, nor is it the love of God’s
dominion which belongs to the image of God. For that love of God’s Dominion
can exist in a human being without such a precept and therefore without such
abstinence. Thus that love of God’s dominion is virtue, while the abstinence
which proceeds from that love, once the precept is posited, is not virtue but
an action commanded by virtue. So the obedience by which I circumcise, wash,
or offer is virtue and a moral action; but circumcision, washing, and offering
are not virtue or moral action, but an external action which I perform from
virtue. Likewise eating and drinking to the glory of God are actions which
proceed from virtue, but they are not virtues or moral actions. Virtue is in
the mind alone and consists in love of God; hence it is moral and alone is
moral. An external action is neither good nor evil, but becomes good when
joined with virtue and evil when separated from it. Thus the moral virtue
which performs an action is one thing, and the external action performed from
that virtue is another. Although it is performed from virtue, it is
nevertheless neither virtue nor a moral action. Thus loving the glory of God
is morally good, but eating to the glory of God is not a moral action; it is a
natural or voluntary action, although proceeding from and directed by moral
virtue, which nevertheless always remains natural in itself even if virtue
directs that natural action to a moral end. These matters are so clear that I
do not know whether anything clearer can be given.”

Reply: 1. However clear the adversary thinks these matters, so that he does
not know whether anything clearer can be given, no one with even the slightest
experience in this controversy can fail to see that, as far as the substance
of the matter is concerned, scarcely anything more obscure could have been
put forward. He has told us all this so that we might perceive that most useful
distinction between virtue itself and the action commanded, especially in
positive precepts—a distinction which, so far as I know, no one denies. But
why does he restrict his discussion here especially to positive precepts when
he said the same thing above of all the other precepts insofar as they commanded
external actions? For we heard him speak as follows: “As for external actions,
they either have a connection with internal virtue and can be determined by
it—as, for example, the virtue of liberality has connected with it an act of
beneficence determined by right reason—or they cannot in any way be determined
by right reason and have no connection with it, as, for example, washing and
circumcising. The former are referred to morals; the latter are positive. Yet
the former actions are not properly moral actions or virtues (N B), but

[1674 logical printed p. 172 | IA leaf 190 | PDF p. 190]

are rather commanded by virtue—the virtue itself, or its internal action, is
therefore one thing, and the action performed from that virtue is another.
Internal virtue is in the mind, as is its elicited act—an external action (N B)
is not virtue—thus, for example, an act of beneficence or martyrdom is not
virtue—the virtue in me is what produces the external act, which is not
morally good in itself even (N B) when it is performed from virtue—for how
could an external act be morally good in itself when not even an internal act
is morally good except insofar as it is elicited by virtue?—therefore,
accurately speaking, nothing (N B) is morally good or a virtue except love of
God and one’s neighbor—external acts are morally good only concomitantly, so
to speak, because they accompany virtue and are commanded by it, so that they
belong to virtue rather than being virtue itself, since they occur equally in
good and evil people.” Further: 2. Here he says that “the external action is
neither good nor evil, but becomes good when it is joined with virtue and evil
when separated from it.” Yet immediately afterward he adds: “The moral virtue
which performs an action is one thing; the external action performed from that
virtue is another, which, although it is performed from virtue, is nevertheless
neither virtue nor a moral action.” He thus either speaks inconsistently or
distinguishes between a good action and a morally good action. Next: 3. He says
that even eating and drinking to the glory of God are not moral actions. He
must consequently say that the precept which commands us to eat and drink to
the glory of God is not a moral precept. Moreover: 4. What sort of action, I
ask, will it be? “Natural,” he says, “and always remaining natural.” There is
therefore no moral difference between eating and drinking to the glory of God
and eating and drinking to the glory of an idol, for the action of eating and
drinking always remains natural in either case. Are these matters so clear that
nothing clearer can be given? Further: 5. Here, as above, he says that nothing
is virtue or a moral action except love of God. Thus, as above, everything
commanded in the Decalogue except love of God—he does not mention here love of
one’s neighbor or of ourselves—will be positive, since it is not moral; and he
has not yet indicated a third kind of action distinct from the moral and the
positive. 6. Finally, when he wishes nothing to be called virtue except love of
God, he contradicts all the Philosophers, who name far more virtues, as is
abundantly clear from those who treat ethics. Indeed, he contradicts Scripture,
which nowhere takes ἀρετὴν, “virtue,” in this way, as is clear from Philippians
4:8; 1 Peter 2:9; and 2 Peter 1:3, 5. What, can nothing clearer than these
things be put forward?

What this Author says after the matters now examined, concerning positive
laws on pages 9 and 10, we will consider later. On page 11 he begins a new
paragraph in this way: “All who have ever used that word have also possessed
this conception of morality.” Reply: I do not think he will say that all who
have ever used that word possessed the conception of morality which he has
just expressed and which we have examined. Let him produce even one person,
whether among the Philosophers, Scholastics, or Theologians, who says that no
external action is moral and that the inward love of God alone is moral. I do
not remember ever reading such things.

What else? “Since,” he says, “that word does not belong to Scripture and is
therefore liable to many ambiguities, it would have been far better to abstain
from that word and to employ only λόγοις διδακτοῖς πνεύματος ἁγίου, ‘words
taught by the Holy Spirit.’ The Holy Spirit does not use these terms, but calls
the things which are called morals in the schools the precepts ‘which if a
person does, he will live in them’; while the things positively and freely
commanded by sovereign authority he calls δόγματα, ‘decrees’ or ‘ordinances.’
If anyone nevertheless wishes to use the word moral, he is surely bound to use
it in the sense which has been given it by

[1674 logical printed p. 173 | IA leaf 191 | PDF p. 191]

those who customarily employ it.” Reply: We acknowledge that this word is not
a word of Scripture. He knows, however, that in Theology we also use other
words which are not found in Scripture, such as *Trinity*, *Sacrament*, and
certain others. Nor is this the only reason why the word is liable to so many
ambiguities. For just now we saw him use the word *virtue*, which does belong
to sacred Scripture, in a sense far different from any in which we know it to
be used anywhere in Scripture, and thus render it ambiguous. 2. We saw above
what must be said about the terms which he would prefer to be used here and
which he says are *λόγοις διδακτοῖς πνεύματος ἁγίου*, “words taught by the Holy
Spirit,” and showed how poorly he applies them here. Nowhere in Scripture do
we read that the precepts “which if a person does, he will live in them” and
*decrees* are so opposed to each other that together they exhaust everything
included under the name of divine laws; that the former denote only those
things which he calls *morals*; and that the latter denote only, and all, the
things positively and freely commanded by sovereign authority. For the word
*dogma*, or *dogmata*, is used of decrees of political rulers in Luke 2:1 and
Acts 17:7. In Acts 16:4 it denotes Ecclesiastical Decrees enacted ministerially
by Ecclesiastical Governors. The adversary, I hope, will concede that one of
these decrees, namely that concerning *abstention from fornication*, is moral,
or is to be numbered among the things “which if a person does, he will live in
them.” And in Ephesians 2:15 and Colossians 2:14 this word denotes only
Ceremonial laws, although some, as *Calvin* testifies on the passage, think
Moral laws are meant there. Hence it is clear that the word *dogmata* does not
always denote only positive laws; nor does it ever denote all of them, for it
nowhere denotes *forensic* laws, which were likewise positive, or were
positively and freely commanded by sovereign authority. Further, in Ephesians
2:15 the Apostle calls those ceremonies τὸν νόμον τῶν ἐντολῶν, “the law of
commandments.” Will we therefore say that the word ἐντολὴ, “commandment,”
denotes only *Ceremonies*? See, I ask, Romans 7:8, 9, 11, 13; 2 Peter 2:21;
Matthew 15:3, 6 and 20:40; Mark 7:8, 9; John 13:34; and not a few other passages
of Scripture. Nor can he say that this word, ἐντολὴ, “commandment,” signifies
*Ceremonies* only once and is therefore not so suitable for denoting things
opposed to *Morals*. For in Scripture it more often denotes Ceremonies and the
things positively and freely commanded by sovereign authority than does the
word *dogmata*, as may be seen in Hebrews 7:5, 16, 18 and 9:19; John 10:18 and
12:49. As for the things “which if a person does, he will live in them,” we
showed above that the precept not to eat the forbidden fruit—which he concedes
to be positive—is to be numbered among them. 3. When he wishes to speak
precisely, he uses the word *Moral* in a way far different from anyone else ever
known to have used it, as we have just seen. Why, then, does he fault someone
for using this word otherwise than he wishes? Since he cannot be ignorant that
this word is employed in different senses by different people, it ought to be
enough for him to know the sense in which we take it. But let him show how we
depart from the common sense.

“We owe the origin of that word,” he says, “to Aristotle, not to Paul or the
Holy Spirit. His doctrine concerning morals, τῶν ἠθῶν, was named ἠθικὴ,
‘ethics,’ from customs. Hence his *Nicomachean Ethics*, *Eudemian Ethics*, and
*Great Morals*, by which until now no one has understood anything except those
morals which are known by nature. How Aristotle himself describes natural
right, which is the same as the moral, may be seen in *Nicomachean Ethics*,
book 5, chapter 7, where he teaches that political right is partly φυσικὸν,
‘natural,’ and partly νομικὸν, ‘legal’—taking the word law more strictly, as he
sometimes calls this right as established τὸ νομικὸν, ‘the legal.’

[1674 logical printed p. 174 | IA leaf 192 | PDF p. 192]

The former has the same force everywhere and not because it is accepted or not accepted.
As for the latter, it initially makes no difference whether it is done in this
way or another, but once it has been established it does make a difference,
as, for example, that a prisoner be redeemed for a mina, that a goat be
sacrificed, or two birds. This distinction reproduces moral and positive right
exactly.” Reply: 1. Whoever gave rise to this word, it makes little difference,
provided we take it in a sufficiently suitable and indeed more common sense.
2. Even if no one understands by the *morals* treated by *Aristotle* anything
other than things known by nature, will it follow that *Aristotle* knew
everything which could be known by an uncorrupted nature? If not, why can
other things known by pure nature, or by nature illuminated by the light of
grace and God’s word, not be called morals even though they escaped
*Aristotle*? Indeed, among these morals known by nature, *Aristotle* also
numbers things which we regard as moral vices. Must we therefore be condemned
because we do not apply the word to everything to which *Aristotle* applies it?
3. In that distinction of political Right he distinguishes Natural Right only
from the Legal or Positive Right of human beings, not of God, of which he was
entirely ignorant. And because *Aristotle* knew nothing of these matters, but
spoke only about human positive laws and distinguished them from natural laws,
does it validly follow that no right positive with respect to God can be
called moral with respect to us? What did *Aristotle* know about faith in the
mediator? Because this did not become known to him by nature, must it therefore
not be regarded as a moral virtue? The adversary himself will deny this. 4. But
what if we abide by the distinction assigned by *Aristotle*? I see no
disadvantage which will follow. For if the adversary permits us to expand the
words “That which has the same force everywhere” so that their meaning is
“that which has, or ought to have, the same force everywhere,” I will embrace
the distinction. And he will not, I hope, reject this expansion. For if only
that which actually has the same force everywhere is called moral, he will
admit very few morals. Yet there are very many things for which it greatly
matters from the outset whether they are done in this way or another, although
they do not actually have the same force everywhere, as is well known.

“The Scholastics,” he continues on page 12, “borrowed this word in the same
sense from Aristotle. Thus Thomas too, part 2 of the second part, question 57,
article 2, divides right into natural and positive, the former being
proportioned to the human being by the very nature of the thing and the latter
by agreement or common consent. In part 1 of the second part, question 100,
article 1, he proves that all moral precepts belong to this law of nature,
concluding from Romans 2 that when the Gentiles do by nature the things of the
law, this must be understood of things pertaining to good morals. Therefore all
the MORAL precepts of the law belong to the law of NATURE, as it is worthwhile
to see more fully in the body of the article.” Reply: If the Scholastics say
nothing other than what *Aristotle* says, it is already clear from what has
been said that They say nothing against us. 2. *Thomas* says no such thing in
part 2 of the second part, question 57, article 2, nor in part 1 of the second
part, question 57, article 2. It does not now occur to me where I might find
these statements. But wherever he says this, the words do nothing against us.
For there, as is clear from the words cited, he divides only *human Right* into
Natural and Positive. Will the adversary conclude from this that “some Right
positive with respect to God cannot be Natural with respect to us”? 2. *Thomas*
does indeed say in part 1 of the second part, question 100, article 1, that all
moral precepts pertain to the law of nature. But in the body of the article he
says that some of these morals require a subtler consideration of reason and
are judged to require observance only by the wise. He acknowledges that these
belong to the Law of Nature, yet in such a way that they require instruction
by which those of less understanding are taught by the wiser. He says that
there are others for judging which human reason

[1674 logical printed p. 175 | IA leaf 193 | PDF p. 193]

requires divine instruction, through which we are taught concerning divine
things. Thus in question 99, article 2, he answers this objection: “The divine
law ought to come to the aid of the human being where human reason fails, as
is clear in matters which pertain to faith and are above reason. But human
reason seems sufficient for moral precepts. Therefore moral precepts do not
belong to the old law, which is the divine law.” He answers: “It was fitting
for the divine law not only to provide for the human being in matters which
reason cannot reach, but also in matters concerning which human reason happens
to be impeded.—Regarding other moral precepts, which are like conclusions
deduced from the common principles of the law of nature, the reason of many
went astray, so that the reason of many judged lawful certain things which are
evil in themselves. Hence it was necessary for the authority of the divine law
to come to the aid of the human being against each defect,” and so forth. We
cited above further passages from *Thomas* which confirm our position; it is
not necessary to repeat them here. Nor do the passages which the Adversary
later cites from the same *Thomas*, part 1 of the second part, question 99,
articles 2, 3, and 4, do anything against us. For we acknowledge that Morals
are acts of virtues, that Ceremonies are divine determinations and are
distinguished from Morals, and that Morals belong to the dictate of the law of
nature, with the addition of *Thomas’s* explanation discussed above.

What he next cites from *Cajetan* on article 1 of question 100 is more opposed
to him than to us. First, Cajetan says that the designation “morals” in this
passage is understood to include not only things which are derived from pure
natural reason as conclusions from principles, but also things which accord
with it when the light of faith is added. Second, we do not say that everything
which depends upon a pure institution is moral, but that some things which are
natural as regards their foundation and require some determination so that
their practice may be inculcated and explained more clearly and distinctly can
be called morals—especially when that determination is disclosed by God’s
works and example, or is presented to be read in the very law of creation, and
is proposed to everyone in every state of the Church for observance as
something agreeable to human nature.

As for *Vásquez* and *Medina*, from whom he later cites some words, their works
are not in my possession. The words cited here, however, were examined by us
above.

He knows well enough that *Scotus* and *Alexander of Hales* are cited in favor
of our position. Although he pleases himself by saying that *Scotus* has
little authority here because he labors under a perpetual desire to contradict
*Thomas*, it is nevertheless established both how great his authority is among
all the Scholastics and how often he contradicts *Thomas* with good reason. Nor
is it enough to say that *Alexander of Hales* stands alone here and improperly
calls a particular divine determination concerning morals “the moral of
discipline.” I know that he also attempts to draw these men to his side, and I
cannot now respond to the passages cited because the books are not at hand.

The words of *Jacob Altenstaig* in his *Theological Lexicon*, page 488, saying
that “morals pertain to natural right,” do not oppose us, for we acknowledge
that morals pertain either to *Right Natural to God* or to *Right Natural to
Us*. By right natural to us, however, we understand the Primeval, not the
Corrupted.

As for *Molinaeus*, who says in *Admonition*, chapter 3, page 45, that “what
belongs to positive right is that of which no trace is found in nature, such
as observance of the seventh day,” and so forth, although his authority carries
little weight with me, I nevertheless do not see how his words oppose us in
the case now at hand. For whatever he thinks about observance of the seventh
day, which we are not now disputing, we can concede that a thing of which no
trace is found in nature belongs to Positive Right. As for the Sabbath, we
showed partly above and will show more fully later that some trace of it is
found in nature—in created nature, that is.

[1674 logical printed p. 176 | IA leaf 194 | PDF p. 194]

*Antonius Cregutus*, whom he cites later in *The Revealer of Secrets*, chapter
1, harms him more than us. For he refers *repentance, faith, grace, mercy*, and
so forth to *hypothetical* commands, which are opposed to *absolute* ones. The
adversary himself will surely not deny that all of these are morals. He also
says that things which were indifferent become necessary once the divine will
is posited because they put on moral goodness, in which he contradicts the
adversary himself. The rest does nothing against us.

*Heidegger* is on our side concerning the dietary law when in chapter 5,
section 5, he defines the Moral “by reference to those acts of a rational
creature which, because of their agreement or disagreement with rational
nature—or rather, with the precept of God—have moral turpitude or moral
necessity.” He also says that “for a moral precept it is sufficient that reason,
from the innate principles of justice and honesty, judges some act to agree
with the holy and immutable will and nature of God.” For it is easy to show
how these statements can be applied to the Sabbath. Indeed, *Heidegger* himself
explains his meaning in chapter 6, section 9, page 99, saying: “No one who has
even lightly noticed the difference between them will readily compare this
precept with the precept concerning the Sabbath. In the Sabbath it was moral
that some day in the week be dedicated to God.—For precepts are not to be
determined from the reason appended to them, but from the foundation of the
one prescribing or from the final cause. Thus from another point of view the
precept concerning the Sabbath is moral because natural reason commanded it.”

Finally, he cites *Ames* in his own support, *On Conscience*, book 4, chapter
22, section 2, where Ames proves that an Oath was not part of either the
ceremonial or judicial law, because it had nothing peculiar to the Jews, but
belonged to moral right, which pertains to Christians just as much as to Jews;
as though we were saying that something is moral because it was peculiar to
the Jews. Nor do the things he says in book 5, chapter 1, section 17, oppose
us, when he says that “all moral precepts belong to natural right, except for
the determination of the day in the fourth precept, which belongs to positive
right.” For we do not say that the determination of precisely the seventh, or
last, day in the week belongs to natural right. Yet we affirm that the fourth
precept concerning the weekly Sabbath belongs to Right Natural to Us
notwithstanding this determination, although it is something positive with
respect to God: “and it is therefore an entirely immutable institution,” as
the same *Ames* says in *The Marrow*, book 2, chapter 15, section 6, “so that
with respect to our duty and obligation it has precisely the same force and
character as those things which are of their own nature matters of Moral and
Natural right. And thus the Scholastics rightly say that it belongs to Moral
Right, not that of Nature, but of Discipline.”

There is therefore no basis for him to gather from his statements that “it is
clearer than liquid fire that among all authors who have used it, the word
moral denotes things which belong to natural law and the virtues and are
opposed to positive law; and that it follows that anyone who wishes to dispute
the morality of some precept must also use that word in its customary sense,
unless he wishes either to lord it over words alone, to play with mere
ambiguities, or to mock with λογομαχίαις, ‘verbal battles.’” For although he
says that he has consistently taken that word in this way, and implies that
the Reverend Theologian and Colleague whom he opposes here only lords it over
words, plays with mere ambiguities, and mocks with λογομαχίαις, “verbal
battles,” nevertheless everyone can see from what has been said above that he
corrects all who have ever written about morality when, properly speaking, he
wishes nothing to be called moral except love of God and nothing to be regarded
as virtue except this single act. That very celebrated Man did not deny that
the word *Moral* denotes things which belong to natural law and the virtues,
although he said that it denotes other things besides only those which

[1674 logical printed p. 177 | IA leaf 195 | PDF p. 195]

the adversary had said belong to Natural Law and the virtues. Nor had he denied
that Moral Laws are opposed to Positive ones, provided the terms are properly
explained. For he never said that what is positive with respect to God is
Natural to God, or the reverse. Nor did he say that what is Natural to us is
for that very reason Positive, or the reverse, although he said that what is
Positive with respect to God can be Natural to us.

The adversary also criticizes the statement of that very Celebrated man that a
distinction must be made between the *Moral* and the *Moral κατ’ ἐξοχήν*,
“preeminently,” although he had not invented this distinction; for the not
undistinguished Theologian *Wendelin* also has it, as the adversary himself
acknowledges. But did we not hear him above enumerate several degrees of
morality? Did he not say just as much when he distinguished morals into
immediate and mediated, subdivided the mediated again, and finally reduced all
morals, properly speaking, to one internal act? Can we not draw from these
words more distinctions among morals than the single one which he criticizes
here, and therefore turn the charge made against him with greater justice back
upon himself? But why pursue this, since each person is free to explain his
meaning in the manner he judges best? The person who raises such an outcry
about a word and stirs up a useless dispute appears to love logomachies. But
leaving these matters, I proceed to others.
