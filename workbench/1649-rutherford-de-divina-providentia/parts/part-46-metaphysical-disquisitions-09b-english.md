Indeed, God cannot command human beings and angels to believe that GOD does not
exist, that GOD is not one, is not infinite, is not merciful, and so forth.
First, God cannot command us to believe that contradictories are simultaneously
true, because *the same is not the same* resolves into this: *The first and
infinite identity is identical*, just as a negative principle in created things
resolves into an affirmative principle in the first being. Such is this:
*GOD exists*; *the first identity is identical*, because of that

most delightful unity and harmony in God, it is unfitting that God should be
able to will—and thus to command—that people believe something true which is
opposed to the infinite first truth: *GOD exists*; *GOD is one*; *GOD is
infinite*. It is repugnant that GOD should be able to command or approve our
believing such a thing, because the object of GOD’s approving will is moral
good, which God approves as good, and what God commands is good and true. But
for us to believe *GOD does not exist*, *he is not infinite*, or *the devil is
GOD* is intrinsically and by necessity of nature impious, false, insulting to
the first truth, and intrinsically opposed to it.

Second, when God created the angelic and human natures, in that same act he
co-created the intellect’s tendency toward the first and most delightful
fountal truth, and the will’s and affections’ tendency toward the purest ocean
of the first lovable one. In this way I believe GOD’s free good pleasure is the
first rule and cause of created moral rectitude. We therefore do not raise with
the sharp-sighted and subtle William of Ockham the question: *Can God forbid
love of himself and command hatred of himself?* For this is to ask whether, on
the hypothesis that God commanded love of himself and forbade hatred of
himself, he could in that case forbid love of himself and command hatred of
himself. When God created the angelic intellect and the wills of rational
creatures, in that same creative act he co-created the habitual and intrinsic
principle and command not to believe contradictories simultaneously true, and
the command not to hate the most delightful and purest fountain of the first
lovable one.

Although the command forbidding the eating of the tree of knowledge is not
engraved together with the rational soul as is the command to love God and
believe the first truth, the two nevertheless have this in common: on the
hypothesis that God has commanded love of himself and forbidden hatred, he
cannot fail to command the former or forbid its contrary unless he should
offend against his own immutable nature, which is impossible.

Nor does it follow that, once GOD creates a rational creature, it is properly
free for God *to command or not command that a person should not know GOD,
should possess or retain no theology, natural synteresis and conscience, or
should suppress and condemn GOD’s truth and law in unrighteousness.* For
creation essentially includes the co-engraving of natural law. In the same act
by which a person is created, a possessing creature is also created which,
precisely because it is rational, is opposed to

natural synteresis and conscience. Thus, given the supposition that a human
being exists, it would follow that God could will to command *that a human
being not retain natural conscience* after, in the act of creating, he had
commanded the person to retain *natural synteresis and conscience*. This must
not be said. Indeed, from GOD’s natural dominion over existing rational
creatures there results the creature’s natural subjection to the Creator,
because servants are related to a lord and creatures to the Creator.

Someone may reply: *On your position, then, God could have founded a rational
creature differing in species and nature from the rational creature that exists
in this order of things; and in the same act he could have co-created a
different, contrary principle and command forbidding love of himself,
commanding hatred of himself, and requiring the creature to believe that he is
not the true GOD but that the devil is the true GOD.*

I answer, first: one who denies that God can create infinitely many species of
rational creatures distinct from those which now exist in this order of things
confines the Holy One of Israel, who cannot be circumscribed, within the far too
narrow rails of his wretched little reasoning. The same applies to one who
denies that God can establish infinitely many rational creatures essentially
superior or even inferior to human beings and angels.

But it does not follow from my position that God could, in the same creative
act, co-create in a rational creature a principle for believing
contradictories—namely, that the true God exists and that the true God does not
exist. I believe God cannot command this or co-create such a command. It seems
to me that if God created an intellect with such an innate command and
principle, that intellect would be both rational and nonrational, and therefore
an object simply incapable of creation and so not contained within the breadth
of divine inward power.

Second, if such a rational creature, distinct in species and nature from human
beings and angels, were created with a co-created principle for hating God,
that hatred of GOD would not be the hatred of GOD which is now forbidden as an
intrinsic evil to the rational creature which now exists. Perhaps it would not
even be sin, but a pure turning away or an innocent non-tendency of such a
rational appetite toward God. It would be like the sun’s tendency from rising
to setting, or a motion ten degrees backward: although such a motion would be
contrary to the sun’s motion decreed by God and established in its natural
local progress, it would not therefore be a culpable motion in the sun. If God
co-created a prin-*

of things, he would be the author of sin, which is blasphemous. But if the
co-creation of such a principle were possible, that principle would be good and
holy, and its natural motion good, just, and holy—not hatred of GOD or sin.

God can also do or freely refrain from doing what the rational creature which
now exists cannot do or refrain from doing without the guilt of sin, because
God is above the law whereas we are subject to it. For in contributing an
influx to the entitative act of sin, God is not a concause of sin; a human
being, however, would sin if he did this.

God is not bound to interpose a physical impediment when Ahithophel and the
traitor Judas end their lives by hanging themselves; but brothers and neighbors
who know they are attempting such a crime of wicked slaughter are bound to
interpose not only a moral but also a physical and violent impediment. Yet I
cannot conceive that any intellect is creatable which God could command to
believe that GOD is not GOD, because God himself can neither will nor believe
this, nor command others to believe what is contrary to the first uncreated
principle—namely, *God is God* and *the uncreated identity is identical*.

[Marginalia: Subjection to law, and non-subjection, necessarily vary the
character of sin.]

Someone can press the objection: *Although “God does not exist,” “God is not
infinite,” and “God is not omnipotent” are contradictories, and although God can
neither will nor command such things, God can nevertheless command a creature
to believe that he does not exist. For believing that God does not exist is
nothing repugnant to God’s nature; indeed, it is possible to believe that God
does not exist and is not infinite. But if this were repugnant to God’s nature,
God would not permit such an act of denying God to exist. For nothing contrary
to God’s free decree can exist; much less can any act repugnant to God’s nature
exist. Therefore it does not involve a contradiction on the part of the divine
nature for God to command the denial of himself, or that a creature believe God
does not exist.*

I answer: I must confess my weakness and ignorance. Yet after consultation with
men of the most fertile genius in this renowned academy, I think it must be
said that there is a certain fittingness which follows GOD’s nature and natural
mode of acting. Thus, although the acts of believing that GOD is not GOD or is
not infinite—or perhaps of hating the first lovable one—are not repugnant to
God’s nature, for I judge it wholly incompossible with GOD’s nature and
existence that acts repugnant to his nature should exist,

[Marginalia: Certain things are good prior to GOD’s free good pleasure because
of what is fitting to GOD.]

[Metaphysical Disquisitions, Question IX, Part B; printed page 573; IA PDF page

yet God also cannot, when the moral fittingness of acting is considered,
command or fail to command such horrible acts. Rather, because of the infinite
fittingness and congruity and the supremely excellent manner of acting,
commanding, and forbidding in God, God cannot command a creature to believe
*God does not exist*, *the devil is God*, or *God is not omnipotent*. For
whatever God commands, precisely because he commands it, either is morally good
from the act of commanding, or is good prior to that act by reason of
fittingness. Thus, for a father to slay his son is good from the act of
commanding in God, who commanded Abraham to slay Isaac; or a thing is good
prior to the act of commanding because of the mode of congruity and the
fittingness of God’s manner of acting in all his actions.

Consequently, in conformity with such fittingness, God could not command a
creature to believe *the Creator is not GOD* or *the devil is equal or superior
to GOD*. For although such acts are not contrary to GOD’s nature as hot is
contrary to cold—since in that case they would destroy the nature itself, and
their existence would be utterly and absolutely impossible—they are
nevertheless, with respect to God’s manner of acting, dishonorable and
incongruous with the divine nature. Nor is it fitting that God command
something to be done which overturns that first principle, *God exists*, and
*it is impossible for the first being not to exist and not to exist*. Indeed,
he would be mutable if he commanded belief in contradictories, because at once
he would will what he commands to exist and, by his approving will, would will
it not to exist—which is false and contradictory.

But someone may object in this way: *The act of believing that God is God,
infinite, and the first lovable one, and that a creature is not God, is a
created act, even though its object is uncreated. Therefore God can forbid that
act to the creature. Either God commands the creature by necessity of nature to
believe that he is God and the first lovable one, in which case such acts are
good prior to all God’s free good pleasure, and that good pleasure will not be
the cause of such an act because God commands it by necessity of nature; or God
commands the creature freely and from no necessity of nature to believe that
God is God, in which case he can forbid and refrain from commanding such
belief. Just as he freely forbids eating from the tree of knowledge, he can
also command Adam to eat from that tree alone and not from the others. Thus God
freely commands a creature to believe that God is God, and can also freely
command the contradictory—namely, to believe that God is not God, is not
infinite, and is not truthful. For whatever God freely wills and commands a
creature, he can also freely will and command its contradi-*

*—ctory, as induction shows. God freely creates the world and wills the world
to exist; therefore he can will the world not to exist, for the object of God’s
free volition and decree is either this or not this—whatever is true under the
disjunction.*

I answer: it is utterly false that God can command a creature to believe that
God does not exist, whether freely or by necessity of nature. For whatever it
is repugnant for God to will by his approving will, it is repugnant for God to
command. Whatever God commands, he wills as morally good and as the creature’s
due obligation. But it is repugnant that God should be able to will a creature
to believe that he is not the infinite good. For because God necessarily both
exists and wills himself to exist, God cannot fail to exist without an impious
and execrable contradiction. If he could will a creature to believe he did not
exist, he could will the creature to commit the abominable crime of atheism and
gigantic unbelief. But for God to will this is repugnant to his own nature.

[Marginalia: GOD can command only what is good and the creature’s duty.]

Second, that created act—namely, the act of believing that *GOD is GOD*—cannot
exist prior to GOD’s free good pleasure, because every created being
presupposes the Creator’s free efficiency, just as an effect presupposes its
first cause. But it does not follow that God can will and command a creature to
believe its contradictory, namely, that *GOD is not GOD*. For the object of
GOD’s free decree is not universally and always a being under a contradictory
disjunction. It is such an object in many instances, but not in all. For when
fittingness—that is, a fitting manner of acting and willing—prevents it, God
cannot command the contradictory in such a case.

Moreover, where *the object of the act specifies the act*, as here when we
believe God to be what he essentially is—namely, JEHOVAH, infinitely good,
gracious, just, and so forth—God cannot, in a manner fitting and congruous with
his wise, best, and most just manner of acting and willing, command a creature
to believe that GOD is not GOD. This is not absurd. Indeed, GOD’s fittingness
must necessarily accompany GOD’s volitions, acts, and commands. Thus, if God
commands and wills something in creatures externally, he cannot will or
command it except fittingly, wisely, immutably, optimally, and equitably. In
this way he decreed to create the world: he creates freely, yet only fittingly,
wisely, and immutably. If, while the decree stood, he did not create it, he
would offend against his wisdom, fittingness, and im-*

command because of his own fittingness.]

*—mutability. Thus, if God commanded a creature to believe contradictories
simultaneously true, that command would be unfitting for God with respect to
his manner of acting. But the same does not apply to the first principle, *GOD
exists*. For what God wills, even freely, he also commands freely, and it is not
necessary that he be able not to will and to forbid it, since that would not
accord with the fittingness of the one who is the first truth and the first
lovable one.

For although the acts of believing that God does not exist, is not good, and is
not merciful, and so forth, do not conflict with GOD’s nature—nor would God
simultaneously be God and not God if all human beings and angels should fall
into such impiety and atheism as to believe that God does not exist, as *the
fool says in his heart*, Psalm 14:1—nevertheless, so much moral goodness accrues
to the act from its object, believing that God exists, because of GOD’s
intrinsic dignity, that it would be unfitting in itself for God to command
angels and human beings to believe he is not God.

Fittingness accompanies all GOD’s attributes and all GOD’s actions of
understanding, willing, acting, commanding, forbidding, promising, and
threatening. Fittingness, like wisdom, includes and determines him to do
nothing and command nothing to creatures which, if done or commanded, would be
done or commanded unfittingly and imprudently. Nor is GOD’s freedom thereby
limited by an external created object, but by his own fittingness. Indeed, he
cannot create the world or any creatures external to himself unfittingly or
mutably.

If someone asserts: *We must admit that some truth exists prior to GOD’s good
pleasure, and indeed one which not even GOD can violate; for GOD cannot cause
contradictories simultaneously to be true, and “it is impossible for the same
thing to exist” is true prior to GOD’s decree.* I answer: *It is impossible for
the same thing simultaneously to exist and not exist* is true in the uncreated
being before every decree of GOD, but by an entirely uncreated truth; thus it
does not touch the point in question. In created beings, however, it is not in
the present sense true prior to all God’s good pleasure. For in creating a
being, God created one thing and simultaneously created an identity; and from
his creating a human being it follows consequently that God did not create a
nonhuman. Therefore the created truth, *It is impossible for the same created
being simultaneously to be a being and a nonbeing*, follows the free act of
creating, and this impossibility plainly follows the free creation of the
being.

But if someone should reply: *It is impossible for GOD to create such a
rational creature without logical and rational principles being co-engraved
and co-created in it: in the intellect, principles patterned after the first
truth; and in the will and affections, principles for loving the supreme and
first lovable one. Consequently GOD could not command such a new rational
creature to believe that God is not GOD or to hate the first lovable one.*

I answer: I freely concede everything that follows from this antecedent. For I
believe no created or creatable intellect can, at GOD’s command, be directed
toward that abominable object—namely, that *GOD does not exist or has no
existence*—because the repugnance and impossibility in the object itself is
uncreated and infinite. So execrable a lie and blasphemy cannot terminate a
creatable intellect.

Second, I would not dare penetrate GOD’s unrevealed secrets or determine which
rational creatures, specifically distinct from these, are creatable in the
infinite treasuries of omnipotence and which are not. Of this matter I, an
ignorant little man, have no comprehension. Yet it seems to me there is no
repugnance in GOD’s being able to create a rational appetite specifically
distinct from that in human beings and angels, one which by its own inclination
would tend no more toward love of God than toward non-love of God.

But if it is repugnant for almighty God to create a rational creature of a new
species without co-creating such principles, then I say that God could not have
commanded that new rational creature to hate the first lovable one, and my
principles will remain unshaken. If someone should prove by invincible arguments
that by his absolute omnipotence God cannot create a rational creature
specifically distinct from the creature he created in this order of things,
which by its own nature would not tend toward love of God, I promise to be
teachable. But this is far different from saying that God can create a rational
creature of another species with a co-created and co-engraved principle for
hating God. I would judge the latter, perhaps with greater probability, to
conflict with God’s fittingness and with the congruity of his outward action in
creatures.
