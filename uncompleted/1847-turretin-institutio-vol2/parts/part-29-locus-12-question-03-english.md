QUESTION III.

*Is the covenant of grace conditional, and what conditions have a place
here?*

[Marginal heading: The occasion of the Question.]

I. The Question arose in the preceding century, on the occasion of the
controversy over justification against the Roman Catholics, among the
Evangelicals themselves. Some denied that the promises of the gospel, or of
the covenant of grace, are conditional, while others affirmed it. The former
did so lest the law be confused with the gospel, and the promises of each;
the latter lest the necessity of faith and holiness be relaxed and
libertinism introduced. But it is easy to reconcile the sound intention of
both sides by establishing certain distinctions.

[Printed p. 165 | Internet Archive PDF 193 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

[Marginal heading: The state of the Question.]

II. 1. A *condition* is taken either *antecedently and a priori*, for that
which has the force of a meritorious and impulsive cause for obtaining the
benefits of the covenant and which, when performed, gives a person a right to
the reward; or *concomitantly and consequently a posteriori*, for that which
has the character of a *means and disposition in the subject* required in
those who are in covenant. 2. A *condition* is either *natural*, flowing from
nature’s own powers, or *supernatural* and divine, depending upon grace. 3. A
covenant *promise* is twofold, either *concerning the end* or *concerning the
means*, that is, either concerning salvation or concerning faith and
repentance, because each is a gift of God. 4. The covenant can be regarded
either with respect to its *institution* by God, its *first application* in
the believer, or its perfect *consummation*.

III. With these things established, we say first: if a condition is taken
antecedently and a priori for a meritorious and impulsive cause, and for a
natural condition, it is rightly denied that the covenant of grace is
conditional. For it is purely gratuitous, depending solely upon God’s
εὐδοκίᾳ, *good pleasure*, and upon no merit of man; nor can the right to life
be founded upon any action of ours, but only upon the righteousness of
Christ. But if it is taken consequently and a posteriori for an instrumental
cause receptive of the covenant’s promises, and for a disposition of the
subject who is admitted into the fellowship of the covenant—a disposition
which flows from grace itself—it cannot be denied that the covenant is
conditional.

1. It is proposed with an express condition (John 3:16, 36; Rom. 10:9; Acts
8:37; Mark 16:16, and everywhere else). 2. Unless it were conditional, there
would be no place for threats in the gospel, which cannot be pronounced
except against those who have neglected the prescribed condition. For neglect
of faith and obedience cannot be culpable if they are not required. 3.
Otherwise it would follow that God is bound to man in this covenant, but man
is not bound to God. This is most absurd and contrary to the nature of all
covenants, in which there is a mutual agreement and reciprocal obligation,
because the contracting parties on each side are bound, as between husband
and wife, king and subjects, and so forth.

IV. Second, if the covenant’s *promises* are understood as *concerning the
end*, no one can deny that they are conditional, because they are always made
under the condition of faith and repentance. But if they are taken for
promises *concerning the means*, namely, concerning faith and regeneration or
repentance, it is certain that they cannot be conditional, but are simple and
absolute. Otherwise there would be an infinite regress and the condition of a
condition would always be demanded, which is absurd.

V. Third, if the covenant is regarded with respect to its first *sanction* in
Christ, it has no prior condition, but rests solely upon the grace of God and
the merit of Christ. But if it is considered with respect to its *acceptance
and application* in the believer, it has faith as its condition, which unites
man to Christ and so admits him into the communion of the covenant. If it is
regarded with respect to its *consummation*, however, along with faith the
pursuit of obedience and holiness has the character of a condition and means,
because without it no one will see God.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

VI. Although the covenant of grace is held to be conditional, the promises of
the law and the gospel are not therefore confused. A manifold difference
always remains. 1. In their *matter*: the legal condition is an entire and
perfect obedience to the law (Rom. 10:5), but the evangelical condition is
*faith* (Rom. 10:9; John 3:16), not a perfect faith free from every blemish,
but a living and sincere faith (1 Tim. 1:5; James 2:14). 2. In their *origin*:
the legal condition had to be *natural*, that is, flowing from nature’s own
powers, but the evangelical condition is supernatural, depending upon grace.
The former was only commanded, but was not given or promised; the latter is
commanded, promised, and given (Jer. 31:33; John 6:45; Eph. 2:8). 3. In their
*end*: the legal condition has the character, at least congruously

[Printed p. 166 | Internet Archive PDF 194 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and improperly, of a meritorious cause of the thing promised, namely, life:
*Do this and you shall live*, so that life may be given to him because he has
done it and on account of his own obedience. But the evangelical condition
cannot properly be called the cause of salvation, much less its merit, because
salvation is the mere χάρισμα, *gift*, of God (Rom. 6:23). It is only the
instrument by which the thing promised is apprehended (Acts 26:18; Rom. 5:17),
and without which it cannot be obtained (Heb. 11:6).

VII. If the covenant of grace were said to depend upon an unstable human
condition, its efficacy and immutability would be overthrown. But because the
condition is supernatural and divine, produced by the omnipotent efficacy of
grace, so far is it from being weakened by that condition that, on the
contrary, it is established more and more. This follows from Paul in Rom.
4:16: “It is of faith, that it might be by grace, so that the promise might be
firm to all the seed,” because the covenant rests not upon a condition which
is in us, but upon the mere grace of God, his inviolable faithfulness, and the
infinite merit of Christ.

VIII. When Christ answers the young man who asks what he must do to obtain
life, *Keep the commandments* (Matt. 19:17), he does not intend to intimate
that the same obedience is required in the same manner in both covenants. In
answering the man, he does not act as an interpreter of the covenant of grace,
but of the Mosaic law, so that he might convict that swollen legalist, who
wished to be saved by the law, of his own impotence, and so lead him to the
Mediator.

IX. Although the covenant of grace is proposed under the notion of a
testament, which depends upon no condition—in respect of which all man’s
duties that assume the σχέσιν, *relation*, of conditions are benefits of God
which fall under his promises—this nevertheless does not prevent it also
having a covenantal character, according to which it is called a
*testament-covenant*, as was said before, and contains conditions on man’s
part.

[Marginal heading: Question 2: what conditions are in the covenant of grace.]

X. Second, it is asked here: What conditions have a place in this covenant,
and under what σχέσει, *relation*? Is faith alone to be called the condition
of the covenant of grace, or repentance and the obedience of the new life
along with it? As far as *faith* is concerned, the question is not whether it
is a condition of this covenant, since Scripture so clearly asserts this
(John 3:16; Rom. 1:16, 17; 10:9), but 1. How does it function here? 2. Does it
alone perform these parts?

[Marginal heading: How faith is a condition of the covenant.]

XI. As to the former, when we call faith a condition of this covenant, we
understand it not absolutely and according to its nature and essence, because
it is contained under obedience, since it is commanded by the law just as the
other virtues are, and therefore cannot be contradistinguished from it. Hence
it could not be accepted in place of the righteousness or obedience of the
law, because such a judgment of God would not be according to truth, since
faith is only a very small part of that righteousness. Rather, it must be
regarded *relatively and instrumentally*, insofar as it embraces Christ,
applies him to itself for righteousness, and through him obtains the right to
eternal life.

First, because it is contradistinguished from the works of the law, which
cannot be done if it is taken absolutely, since in that sense it is a work of
the law. Second, because faith can be consistent only when taken
instrumentally: 1. With *God’s grace*, for which nothing is required except
reception, which is faith’s proper action (Rom. 5:17; John 1:12). Hence Paul
says, “It is of faith that it might be by grace.” All the other virtues seem
to give something to God, but faith alone is occupied in receiving: “You have
been saved by grace, through faith” (Eph. 2:8); boasting is excluded through
the law of faith (Rom. 3:27). 2. With the *condition of sinful man*, because
in the first moment of justification nothing is in him besides faith that can
please God. 3. With the *Mediator’s satisfaction and righteousness*, because
faith applies it to itself. 4. With *eternal life* as God’s gift and
inheritance, because faith apprehends it in the gospel (Rom. 3:24, 25; 1 Cor.
1:30; Eph. 1:3, 4; 2:8). 5. With the *promises of the gospel*, which proposes
life to

[Printed p. 167 | Internet Archive PDF 195 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

man not as something to be acquired, as the law does, but as something
already acquired. Third, because Christ is our righteousness (1 Cor. 1:30; 2
Cor. 5:21), in whom we have redemption and life. Therefore, this cannot be
attributed to faith *materially*, insofar as faith is a work, but
*instrumentally*, insofar as it acts as an apprehending hand.

XII. In this covenant faith is opposed to righteousness or obedience itself,
not because they cannot subsist together, since only that faith which is
effective through love or obedience is true faith, but because they cannot
both function *causally*, since the causality of each differs in its whole
kind: the one is meritorious and principal, the other only organic and
instrumental. Righteousness gives; faith receives. Righteousness consists in
the mutual love of God; faith in persuasion concerning the love of God. Hence
what is taken from the one is attributed to the other, not with respect to the
same causality but with respect to the effect, because in the covenant of
grace faith takes the place of works for us. That is, though in a different
kind of cause, it obtains for us what works ought to have effected in the
covenant of nature.

XIII. In order to know further how faith has the character of a condition in
this covenant, it must be understood that faith does this only in relation to
Christ, insofar as it is the means and instrument of our union with Christ
which obtains him for us: “I ascend to my Father and your Father, to my God
and your God” (John 20:17). Thus we cannot be received into God’s covenant
except insofar as we are united to Christ, who is the foundation of the
covenant and the bond of our communion with God.

Since it is of the greatest importance to know whether our faith is truly a
*faith of union*, this can be perceived not so much a priori as a posteriori
from its operations: if, namely, it excites in us a fervent *love* of Christ
(Song 5:10, 16; Phil. 3:8; John 1:14; 21:15), an ardent *desire* to enjoy him
(Pss. 42:1; 84:1, 2; Phil. 1:23), and *separation* from all earthly and carnal
things which impede our union with Christ. Such things include the *love of
sin and the world* (Hos. 4:8; Rom. 6:2; Matt. 10:37; 1 John 2:15, 16; James
4:4)—hence the evangelical merchant sells all things for that most precious
union (Matt. 13:46); Moses prefers the reproach of Christ to the treasures and
honors of Egypt (Heb. 11:25, 26); and the disciples leave all things to follow
Christ (Matt. 19:27)—and *presumption of one’s own righteousness*, because
“the healthy have no need of a physician, but the sick,” and Christ “did not
come to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance” (Matt. 9:12, 13). They
also include *unbelief, impenitence*, and other sins and works of the flesh of
this kind which are ἀσύστατα, *incompatible*, with Christ (Rom. 8:1, 10).

This is especially so if faith produces two acts which immediately and
necessarily follow that union: on Christ’s part, a *sense of his love* through
the *consolation* of the Holy Spirit, who, by sealing our adoption in our
hearts, persuades us that we are children of God, heirs of God, and coheirs
with Christ, that Christ dwells in us, and has been made ours (Rom. 8:15, 16;
1 John 3:24); and on our part, the mutual *consent* of our love through the
pursuit of sanctification. By it, as Christ loves us, we love him in return;
as he gives himself to us, we give ourselves wholly to him, so that we no
longer live for ourselves, but in the faith of the Son of God, who loved us
and gave himself for us (Gal. 2:20; 2 Cor. 5:15). The Bride describes this
movingly in Song 2:16: “My Beloved is mine, and I am his.”

XIV. For this *consent* to be true, it must not be consent *concerning the
future*, such as that of those who daily postpone their conversion, but
*concerning the present*, so that if today we hear Christ’s voice, we do not
harden our heart, but receive the grace offered. It must not *separate* Christ
the Redeemer and Lord, the Surety and Head, the benefits and duties, as is done
by those who gladly embrace Christ’s

[Printed p. 168 | Internet Archive PDF 196 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

merit but are unwilling to be subject to his dominion: they would like to
enjoy the benefits but not be bound to the duties. Rather, it must *join* the
whole Christ with his duties and benefits, so that we receive him not only as
Surety but also as Head, not only as righteousness but also as sanctification,
as the Savior who redeems but also as the Lord who rules and whom we are bound
to obey in all things. It must not be *exceptive and conditional*, like that
of those who consent to Christ and his gospel only up to the point of the
cross and persecution, but withdraw and are offended when these come. Rather,
it must be *total and absolute*, so that without any limitation we follow
Christ in every condition, not only as triumphant and glorious in heaven, but
also as suffering and affixed to the cross on earth, wherever he leads us,
ready both to live and die with him.

Thus our communion with Christ, and with all Christ’s goods, will be true and
saving: communion in “the same Spirit” (Rom. 8:9; 1 Cor. 8:17), “the same
life” (Gal. 2:20), “the same righteousness” (2 Cor. 5:21; Jer. 23:6), and “the
same glory and inheritance” (John 17:21, 24; Rom. 6:8; 8:17). And just as we
receive Christ’s goods in communion with him, so in turn Christ expects from
us *love* (John 21:17; 1 Pet. 1:8) and *subjection* (Eph. 5:24; Matt. 11:29;
Pss. 2:11; 110:3) that are sincere, full, and constant.

XV. Thus it has been demonstrated how faith is a condition in this covenant.
We must now see whether it alone performs this function or whether other
virtues do so with it, especially repentance. This is disputed among the
orthodox themselves, some denying it and others affirming it. We think the
matter can easily be settled by a distinction if we take the different senses
of condition. It can be taken either *broadly and improperly*, for all those
things which man is bound to perform in the covenant of grace, or *strictly
and properly*, for that which has some causality with respect to life and upon
which eternal life in its own way depends not only antecedently but also
causally.

In the latter sense, faith is the sole condition of the covenant, because the
remission of sins, salvation, and eternal life are promised under the
condition of faith alone (John 3:16, 36; Rom. 10:9), nor is there any other
thing that can perform this function, because there is no other thing that
receives Christ and applies his righteousness. But in the former sense,
nothing prevents repentance and the obedience of the new life from being
called a condition, because they are contained among the duties of the
covenant (John 13:17; 2 Cor. 5:17; Rom. 8:13).

XVI. Second, a *condition* either *precedes* the acceptance of the covenant
and has the character of a cause by which we are received into it, or
*follows* it and has the character of a means and way by which we advance to
its consummation. In the former sense, faith alone is the condition of the
covenant, because it alone embraces Christ with his benefits. But in the
latter sense, holiness and obedience can have the character of a condition,
because they are the means and way by which we advance to the full possession
of the covenant’s goods. Even if they have no causality either with respect to
justification or with respect to the eternal life which results from it, they
nevertheless pertain to this covenant in other respects: as *inseparable
companions of true and sincere faith*, because “faith must be effective
through love” (Gal. 5); as *qualities of those who are to be saved* (Matt.
5:8; 25:35, 36; Heb. 12:14); as *fruits of the Spirit* in Christ (Rom. 8:2, 9,
10) and *marks of our conformity* with Christ (Rom. 6:4, 5; Col. 3:1; Eph.
2:4, 5); as *proofs of our gratitude* toward God (Titus 2:14); as
*testimonies of our sonship* (1 John 3:3; Rom. 8:15); and as *duties owed to
God* by a rational creature (Luke 17:10).

XVII. The account of *justification* and of the *covenant* is not the same in
every respect. Faith alone concurs in justification, but besides faith other
virtues are also required for the observance of the covenant. These contribute
not only to accepting the covenant

[Printed p. 169 | Internet Archive PDF 197 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

but also to observing it. For these two things must always be joined: the
acceptance of the covenant and the observance of the covenant accepted. Faith
accepts through the reception of the promises; obedience observes through the
fulfillment of the commandments: *Be holy, because I am holy.* Yet legal and
evangelical obedience are not confused on this account. Legal obedience is
prescribed for meriting life, but evangelical obedience only for possessing
it. The former precedes as the cause of life—*Do this and you shall live*—but
the latter follows as its fruit, not that you may live, but *because you
live*. The former is not admitted unless it is perfect and absolute in every
respect; the latter is admitted even though imperfect, provided it is
sincere. The former is only commanded as man’s duty; the latter is also
promised and given as God’s gift.
