9. Others say that Christ died for all in this sense: that God could forgive
the sins of all under whatever condition seemed good to him; that faith would
be demanded of all in vain unless God the Father could forgive everyone's
sins and Christ's death were sufficient for this; and therefore that he died
for all and each so that all and each would be saved, provided they believed
in Christ. But I believe these things too are false. For these two statements
differ greatly:

[1651 logical printed p. 260 | IA leaf 278 | PDF p. 278]

“eternal life is offered to all and each under the condition of faith,” and
“life is offered to all and each from God's intention to give them life if
they believe.” The former is true, but then the offer of life is nothing other
than a revelation of the divine will by which the connection of faith and
eternal life is proposed. The latter is false. 1. God offers to the reprobate
either life obtained by Christ's death under that condition, or life not so
obtained but to be communicated from God's absolute freedom, apart from
Christ's satisfaction, if they believe. The offer of life is not made in the
former way. For the gospel would then tell the reprobate that, if they believe,
life obtained by Christ's death is offered to them when it was not obtained
by Christ's death. And one who offered them obtained life or remission under
the condition of faith in Christ as its obtainer, though he had not obtained
it, would seem to me impossible to answer. Nor can the latter be said. If
remission and life not obtained through Christ's death were offered to the
reprobate, there would be another name under heaven besides Christ's name by
which men must be saved, which is absurd. 2. The Father gave the Son power to
give life only to those given him by the Father, John 17. But the reprobate
were not given to the Son by the Father. Therefore the Father did not give the
Son power to give eternal life to the reprobate. Consequently, the Son cannot
from his own and the Father's intention offer them life and remission. It
cannot be said that from the Father he acquired the absolute power to forgive
the sins of the reprobate, for he possessed that power in common

[1651 logical printed p. 261 | IA leaf 279 | PDF p. 279]

with the Father from eternity. For by absolute power God can forgive
everyone's sins apart from Christ's satisfaction, as William Twisse proves
solidly, like all his arguments. But if by his death Christ acquired ordinary
and judicial power to forgive the sins of the reprobate, so that notwithstanding
God's justice he could forgive all their transgressions, then I believe Christ
must have made satisfaction for the reprobate's sins. Yet this is false. For
Christ did not bear the persons of the reprobate on the altar of the cross or
satisfy for their sins; otherwise, by the order of justice, they cannot pay the
penalties of their own sins. 3. For the elect alone Christ obtained both the
power of remitting and actual remission, because both arise from the
satisfaction rendered. Perhaps those who think otherwise mean a remote power
founded solely upon the infinite sufficiency of the merit of Christ's death.
But neither can it be said that God's conditional will to confer salvation on
the reprobate is founded upon such a power, because the salvation conditionally
promised in the gospel arises as an effect from its meritorious cause: the
formal and actual satisfaction for sins through Christ's death. It would thus
have to be said that by his death Christ merited life for the reprobate. They
say that because Christ offered God something of infinite value, God can
forgive the reprobate's sins if they believe. I hear an unheard-of gloss. In
whose name did Christ offer anything? In the reprobate's? Then he redeemed
them absolutely,

[1651 logical printed p. 262 | IA leaf 280 | PDF p. 280]

for he paid the price for them. In the elect's? But please explain: because
God paid the price to divine justice in the name of the elect alone, could it
be said that, notwithstanding justice, he could forgive the sins of the
reprobate for whom he did not satisfy? In my madness I do not see how Christ
could forgive the sins of those for whom he did not satisfy God's justice. 4.
Nowhere does Scripture imply that Christ died sufficiently for all and each.
For “for” denotes the intention of the one dying, but in Christ there was no
intention of dying for the reprobate. Nor can it be said in any sense that God
decreed Christ's death to be sufficient for the redemption of all. For that
sufficiency and material causality belong to Christ's death antecedently to
God's decree; by its intrinsic power, apart from every decree, it is of
infinite value. Thus I do not deny that the λύτρον, materially considered, is
sufficient for all and each, but it is not formally an ἀντίλυτρον except
insofar as it is paid in the name of those for whose sins Christ satisfied.
The reprobate are in no way to be numbered among them. Here I would note in
passing the vanity of the Remonstrants' reasoning, who say that we weaken all
the power of Christ's merit toward the reprobate. “The divine will alone,”
they say, “is the cause by which the power of Christ's merit, otherwise
sufficient in and of itself, does not reach them, without any fault of their
own which they could overcome.” Reply. 1. We hold and openly profess that
God's will alone, accepting the satisfaction,

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 8, folio 98.]

[1651 logical printed p. 263 | IA leaf 281 | PDF p. 281]

makes Christ's death formally a λύτρον. For the price of redeeming a captive
is formally a price insofar as it is paid by the payer and accepted by the one
to whom it is paid. Let a price be wholly sufficient: unless it is both paid
in the captive's name and accepted by the one detaining the captive, it is not
formally an ἀντίλυτρον. 2. It is false that God's will alone is the cause why
the power of the merit does not reach the reprobate. It is the sole first and
remote cause, but the reprobate's malicious rejection of Christ is the
proximate cause. By a wondrous association, their obstinate mind most freely
rejects and refuses to embrace what God's absolute will willed should not
belong to them; and because they freely indulge their unbelief, they are most
rightfully punished. 3. We desire them to teach us what Christ merited for the
reprobate. Not faith, not regeneration—for they reject that on p. 95, chapter
8. Presumably he merited that they could believe or not believe, which they
hold arises from the impulse of human choice, if only they had learned to deal
sincerely. 5. Let God propose a universal promise to Judas, Simon Magus, and
the other reprobate: “I intend and decree salvation for you if you believe in
my Son.” That “if” refers either to God's intellect or to God's decreeing will.
If to the intellect, God's knowledge will be doubtful, which is absurd. If to
the will, God's will will hang at a fork in the road upon the condition being
performed or not performed. But God's will, as infinite, depends upon no
external agent and is determined from nowhere but itself.

[1651 logical printed p. 264 | IA leaf 282 | PDF p. 282]

The Remonstrants do not deny this. 6. Speaking strictly and properly, not even
God's will to confer salvation upon the elect is conditional. Much less,
therefore, does he conditionally will it for the reprobate. I prove the
antecedent. The condition of faith does not properly depend upon the elect's
choice, so as to exist or not exist, but upon God's absolute gracious will,
Ephesians 2:1; Philippians 1:25. Thus “I will to give eternal life to the elect
if they believe” is equivalent to “From absolute freedom I will to give life
to the elect if, from that same absolute freedom of mine, I will to give
faith.” What is this but for God to say, “I will to give life to the elect if
I will to give life”? This is not a conditional will when both the thing
conditioned and the condition depend absolutely upon one and the same absolute
will of God. By the same reasoning the creation of the world is conditional,
namely on the hypothesis that he decreed to create. Likewise, if a father
wills to give an inheritance to a son who desires it on the condition that he
wills to give the hundred talents with which he redeems it, what is this but
the father's giving the inheritance on the hypothesis that he wills to give
it? Strictly speaking, no one would call that a conditional will. A potestative
condition toward existence and nonexistence, as jurists say, in no way belongs
to God. The promises in the Word, however, are proposed conditionally

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Confession*, chapter 2, thesis 7.]

[1651 logical printed p. 265 | IA leaf 283 | PDF p. 283]

to engender in us a godly diligence in performing those duties which God
willed to precede the obtaining of salvation. We therefore do not say that God
wills to give life to the reprobate on the hypothesis that he wills to give
faith. The Arminians fight and contend most fiercely with us for conditions
which determine God's will, for they want conditions which depend upon human
choice. Thus they cry that conditional promises of eternal life cannot be
subordinated to the absolute decree by which that good was nevertheless
absolutely destined. This would be true if Scripture contained such conditions
determining God's will as they imagine. Our theologians say that faith is not
a condition moving God's will, yet our salvation is conditional, which is
most true. For God wills no life for us except one having faith as antecedent,
yet God's will is in no way moved by our faith. This, then, is a mystery of
sacred theology: God offers eternal life to the reprobate under the condition
of faith, yet that life was not obtained by Christ's merits, nor did God decree
to give them faith. Nor must it be said that Christ rendered an infinite
satisfaction indefinitely for all and each, insofar as satisfaction was made
to justice for all, so that God is free to forgive or not forgive the sins of
all and each; for

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, writings at the Synod, article 1, pp. 222–223.]

[Marginalia: What kind of faith God requires from the reprobate in the gospel.]

[1651 logical printed p. 266 | IA leaf 284 | PDF p. 284]

Christ satisfied God's justice for certain persons by name and for no others.
But they say that faith is demanded of the reprobate in vain unless Christ
died for them in such a way that God can forgive their sins notwithstanding
justice. I deny the consequence. On the teaching of those who hold this, one
could equally say that faith from the powers of grace merited by the
Mediator's blood is demanded in vain from those for whom the Mediator merited
no such grace. Yet they teach that the grace of believing was in no way
obtained for the reprobate by Christ's merits, and still that faith is
rightfully demanded from them. If God, by demanding faith from the reprobate
in the gospel command, revealed his intention of giving faith, the consequence
would have a greater appearance of truth. But the gospel commands and promises
do not express to the reprobate God's intention that they should obey. They
reveal only that holy connection both between faith and their obligation to
believe, and between faith and eternal life. That connection abstracts from
God's decree and intention concerning the actual effecting of the obedience
commanded, and from every divine intention terminating in the reprobate's
actual obedience. It reveals to the reprobate only God's holy and equitable
complacency in their faith, which God saw fit to reveal to them partly by
commands, partly by promises, and partly by gospel threats. It does not reveal
to the reprobate

[1651 logical printed p. 267 | IA leaf 285 | PDF p. 285]

that it is his intention that they obey. He intends or wills no such thing,
lest in so astonishing a manner the reprobate should so govern matters that
God cannot be said to fail in his intention.

10. But Arminius says that by demanding faith from the reprobate God presents
himself as having appointed Christ to be their Savior, which is false.
Therefore God presents what he does not will. Reply. 1. “Reprobate, believe
and you will be saved” conveys this: “Reprobate, my holy will has joined faith
and salvation, and you are obliged to believe.” But to claim that God thereby
presents his intention and decree that the reprobate believe and be saved
conveys a conspicuous falsehood. 2. Although God did not appoint Christ as the
Savior of all, including the reprobate, he rightfully requires that they rest
with complete trust upon the Savior who is wholly sufficient for all believers.
They are rightfully guilty because they refuse to give themselves to Christ,
although Christ never decreed from eternity to embrace them. What absurdity
is it even to human reason that God invites to the feast very many whom he did
not decree would come, and commands them to embrace Christ as Savior, though
he was never destined for them in God's eternal counsel? God commands Abraham
in his Word to sacrifice Isaac, an act never appointed and willed by God's
decree. Will you say that in his Word he acts the dissembler, saying one thing
and willing another? It does not follow. He wills the obedience of all who are
externally called to please him, although most do not obey. He declares this
and nothing else; God is not bound to express his intention and decree in his
speech. Has any mortal taught Jehovah how to speak? Is he permitted to express
the thoughts of his mind as he wills?

[1651 logical printed p. 268 | IA leaf 286 | PDF p. 286]

11. But the unfortunate ingenuity of Vorstius objects that if the reprobate
believed, it would be in vain. Christ did not die for them; therefore, even
assuming the reprobate's faith, their salvation is impossible, since Christ
merited neither life nor remission for them. Reply. 1. The Arminians do not
dare say that actual remission was obtained for the reprobate, but some
potential remission which is completed by the act of faith, as though our
faith were a collateral part of satisfaction. 2. Further, Christ communicates
the kind of remission he obtained; but he obtained perfect and actual, not
potential, remission—for remission in potency is not remission—Colossians
1:14–15. 3. Being reprobate and believing are ἀσύστατα, incompatible things.
If they believed, their faith would have been decreed; and if faith were
decreed, salvation would also have been decreed. Thus nothing follows from an
impossible supposition. For in God's decree these are reciprocal: “Christ died
for believers,” and “Only those for whom Christ died are believers.” And at
last, in due time, these are reciprocal in reality and coincide in the same
thing.
