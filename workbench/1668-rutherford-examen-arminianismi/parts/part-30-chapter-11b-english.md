For illustration of this disputed question, the following points may also be
noted.

In the year 415 Pelagius, Julian, Faustus, and many others arose, who branded
irresistible grace as Fate. Today four opinions are current. The first is that
of the Dominicans, who acknowledge the insuperable power of grace but teach
that a human being is not converted because he does not will it. So teach
Alvarez, Gumel, Rispolis, the Salamanca and Complutensian Doctors, and Bañez.
The second is that of the Jesuits, who establish congruous calling in: 1.
Persuasion. 2. The adaptation of persuasion to such a disposition that perhaps
the efficacy of conversion is resolved into an afternoon drinking party. 3.
The support of Middle Knowledge, which is a brainsick invention of ill-occupied
wits. The third opinion is that of the Pelagians, Arminians, Socinians, and
Pseudo-Lutherans, who posit resistible grace.

But there are five things here in the will. 1. Illumination of the mind. 2.
Vivification of the affections. 3. The conferral of the power to believe. 4.
The sense and perception of the intellect's persuasions and knockings. They
say that all these are brought about irresistibly by God. 5. Assent and
dissent, about which the question concerns. The Arminians formerly fled with
their Arminius to Jesuitical congruity, *Hague Conference*, page 311; now they
have deserted it.

Yet this controversy did not first arise with Pelagius but is much more
ancient. Even in

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 458 | PDF p. 509]

the time of the Apostles it arose and was stirred up by carnal Romans, Romans
9:19: “If God has mercy on whom he wills and hardens whom he wills, then he is
angry without cause, for who has resisted his will?” Paul does not answer with
the Arminians that one will of God is resistible and another irresistible,
one peremptory and another not, one efficacious and another inefficacious. He
says instead, “No indeed, O man, who are you who answers God back?” and so
forth.

We prove our position against the Arminians by the following arguments.

1. God effects faith in us by that power and might of his by which he raised
Christ from the dead, Ephesians 1:18–19 and Col. 2:12; by his power fulfilling
every good pleasure of his will, 2 Thessalonians 1:11; and by that divine power
according to which he gives us all things that pertain to life and godliness,
2 Peter 1:3.

2. No one resists the Father's drawing, John 6:44, and all who have heard and
learned from the Father come to Christ, verse 45. Therefore, they necessarily
come. For the proposition *κατὰ παντὸς*, “of every case,” is a necessary truth,
and consequently they cannot fail to come.

3. God not only gives the ability to believe but works willing and completing
in us, Philippians 2:13. It has been given to us to believe and suffer,
Philippians 1:29. And he brings it about that we walk in his precepts, Ezekiel
36:27. Therefore, God's grace works so efficaciously

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 459 | PDF p. 510]

that it removes both resistance and the capacity to resist. In the same place,
verse 26: “I will remove the stony heart.” But the stony heart is the habitual
principle of resisting God when he calls.

4. The new heart and new spirit by which we actually walk in God's precepts
stand diametrically opposed to a final and complete power to resist God
calling and to frustrate God's gracious intention to call. For in that case
human beings would have a new heart and never be converted, and thus they
would be converted and not converted.

5. God promises us the removal of resistibility. Deuteronomy 30:6: “Jehovah
will circumcise your heart and the heart of your seed, that you may love the
Lord your God with your whole heart.” Therefore, he removes the hardness and
foreskin of the heart. Ezec. 36:26: “I will give you a new heart and put a new
spirit within you.” But the Jews might object—as the Arminians do today—“It is
possible that, while remaining stony, we resist you, Jehovah, as you give a
new heart.” He answers, “I will remove the stony heart.” Jeremiah 31:33: “I
will put my law in their mind and inscribe it upon their heart.” But it is
possible that we remain untaught and resist you. Therefore, he anticipates
this in verse 34: “They will not, however, each teach his friend and each his
brother, saying, ‘Know Jehovah,’ for as many as there are will know me, from
the least of them to the greatest of them.” Such are the promises in Jeremiah
24:7; Isaiah 44:3; Ezekiel 11:19–20; Zechariah 12:10; and John 14:16.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 460 | PDF p. 511]

6. God the Father promised the Son a willing people, Psalm 110:3; that he
would see his seed, Isaiah 53:10; the ends of the earth for his inheritance,
Psalm 2:8; and dominion from sea to sea and from the river to the ends of the
earth, Psalm 72:8. Therefore, he promises his Son that many are to be converted
to him, and God is truthful in his promises. These people, then, cannot resist
God converting them.

7. If a human being dead in sins contributes nothing toward his regeneration
by which he merits or procures it, and if his conversion is a raising from the
dead and a new creation, then a human being cannot obstruct God calling or
struggle against him regenerating. But the former is true, John 3:3; 5:25; and
Ephesians 2:5.

8. God's act of regenerating would be no stronger than an orator's persuasion
and gentle enticement if it could be overcome by a human being. Nor would God
here be stronger than a Preacher, the Devil, Cicero, or Demosthenes.

9. If this grace can be overcome by a human being, then it is common to the
unregenerate, since it would be given to them but rejected when given. Yet
this grace is proper to the Sons of God. For Romans 8:14 says, “Only the sons
are led by the Spirit of God.” And 1 Corinthians 1:23 says that Christ is the
power of God only to those inwardly called. John 14:17 says, “Only the sons of
God receive the Holy Spirit; the world cannot receive him.” See Joan. 7. §.
37:38; John 4:13; and John 14:17.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 461 | PDF p. 512]

10. God employs his omnipotence in the action of inward grace, and indeed the
kind by which all things are possible to God, Matthew 19:26. By this
omnipotence he converts many from confidence in riches to faith and
repentance. Here he employs a power greater than that which is in the Devil
and sin, 1 John 4:4; 1 John 2:14; John 12:31; and John 14:30. Thus the
conversion of many is inferred from God's will and power, Romans 11:23; Romans
1:16; Jude 24; 1 Peter 1:5; John 10:29; 2 Corinthians 9:8; Romans 15:13; and
16:25.

11. If a human being could resist inward grace proper to the elect, then God's
decrees of election could be rescinded, his counsels changed, and his
intentions frustrated, contrary to the Scriptures, Psalm 33:10; Isaiah
14:26–27; and 46:10–11.

12. If this grace can be overcome, then God would not give repentance and
faith, contrary to the Scriptures, Acts 5:31 and 2 Timothy 2:25, but would only
offer them. Yet faith offered and not received is not God's gift; faith
received would instead be our gift.

13. If this grace can be overcome, then the act of Conversion would be
entrusted to a human being's free Choice, and the efficacy of Christ's merits
and the salvation of the elect intended by God—whether it exists or not—would
depend not on God's grace but on our free Choice. Yet the Scriptures make
Conversion a work of grace, Deuteronomy 30:6; Jeremiah 31:33; Zech. 36:26–27;
Ezekiel 12:10; and John 6:44–45.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 462 | PDF p. 513]

14. If grace can be overcome, God will inwardly call many whom he neither
predestined, justified, nor glorified, contrary to Romans 8:29–30.

15. We pray to God for Conversion in vain if, when all the actions of divine
grace have been put in place, it could happen that we resist and are not
converted. For we would be praying for something God cannot do unless he
overturned liberty. Thus conversion would be non-conversion and disobedience.

16. We would in vain render to God the thanks and glory for Conversion if the
will could hinder it. For God would in that case not be the infallibilizing and
efficacious cause of conversion.

17. A human being would separate and distinguish himself from the unconverted
by his free Choice alone. For the converted person would say, “I did something
by my free Choice, without Christ, and I have something I did not receive from
God's grace—namely, I did not resist God's grace—although another person,
furnished with the same habitual grace, resisted.” Yet the Scriptures ascribe
the glory of our conversion to God as the cause that separates and
distinguishes, Romans 1:8; Ephesians 2:4–5; Colossians 1:12–13; 1 Thessalonians
1:2–3; 1 Timothy 1:15–17; Revelation 1:5–6; and 1 Peter 1:3–4.

18. It would lie within free Choice, not with God, that any people were saved,
that a Church existed in the world, that Christ as Bridegroom had

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 463 | PDF p. 514 | erratum applied: Rex for Res]

a wife, the Head members, and the King subjects, and that the glory of
forgiving mercy was displayed. For every mortal could resist God calling by
free Choice.

19. Augustine would falsely ascribe to God from the Scriptures dominion over
human wills. He would speak wrongly in *On Rebuke and Grace*, chapter 14, and
*On Grace*, chapter 14: “God has the most omnipotent power to incline human
hearts wherever he pleases, and to do what he wills concerning the wills
themselves,” and so forth. “It must not be doubted—human wills cannot resist so
as to prevent him from doing what he wills.”

The Remonstrants in *Synodical Writings*, article 4, page 58, formulate the
question about resistibility as follows: “When God seriously wills that a
human being believe and be converted, could he will not to believe and be
converted?” Or thus: “When God intends the conversion of human beings, does he
employ an efficacy of grace such that the effect of conversion certainly and
necessarily follows, and, once this efficacy is posited, a human being could
not fail to be converted and believe, while without it he could not be
converted and believe?” We reject this state of the Question.

1. They do not have even a syllable about inward grace.

2. They ask whether a human being could fail to be converted when God employs
that efficacy—that is, that efficiency—by which he is necessarily converted.
This is as if they were asking whether, when conversion is posited, conversion
could fail to be posited.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 464 | PDF p. 515]

3. For the Arminians, one intention to convert is peremptory and another
inefficacious; one is absolute and another conditional. Therefore, they
formulate the question confusedly.

4. Since, according to them, God intends by a velleity the conversion of
Barbarians and Indians, and indeed of the damned—as Corvinus says in *Against
Molina*, chapter 31, section 17—they ask whether God employs toward the Indians
who have heard nothing about Christ and toward the damned an efficacy of grace
such that, once it is posited, their conversion necessarily follows. This is a
senseless Question.

Martinez de Ripalda, *On the Supernatural Being*, volume 1, book 2,
disputation 30, section 6, number 28: “If you wish free choice—against the more
recent Thomists—to act immediately *ut quod*, as that which acts, because it
itself is what is immediately rendered knowing or desiring through a
supernatural act, Calvin too speaks thus: who does not know that it comes about
through the will that a human being wills? See, Chemnitz freely admits that it
is the will which properly—in the adverbial sense—wills, and the mind which
properly understands. Nevertheless, the salutary action of acting and willing
does not properly belong to the will and mind but to divine grace, because it
proceeds adequately and immediately not from the human will and mind's own
power but solely from the alien power of divine grace.” Reply: But he imputes
to us that we wish free choice to remain purely passive.

But in stating the Question, we wish Grace

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 465 | PDF p. 516]

and the Father who teaches to have the relation of a twofold cause.

1. Grace is the sole and adequate cause that really draws, inwardly teaches,
and predetermines. Free Choice is acted upon, drawn, and taught. But here there
is no collaterality between Grace and free Choice. Otherwise our will would be
a fellow cause that socially calls, prevenes, and draws, and God's grace would
be the other fellow cause. But the Father alone draws, and the Father alone
adequately makes us hear, John 6:44–45—not the will together with the Father.
Indeed, here we are passive. The Father alone and adequately teaches Peter;
flesh and blood do not teach themselves as a fellow cause, Matthew 16:17. 1.
For then half of Simon's blessedness would have to be credited to flesh and
blood equally with the Father. 2. We would powerfully call ourselves equally
with God, and our own rich mercy would intervene as an active collateral cause
of inward calling. 3. It would lie with free Choice whether we are called or
not.

2. Free Choice thus drawn and moved is a fellow vital cause, but one
subordinate to prevenient grace. He alone preveniently knocks, not merely
arousing morally by prompting and exhortatory admonition, but by a real
influence. Yet we do not open and answer alone. Rather, he who alone calls and
prevenes also follows after, with the same grace working the willing in our
opening and answering.

Martinez de Ripalda, *On the Supernatural Being*,

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 466 | PDF p. 517]

volume 1, disputation 30, part 29: “The Fathers of the Church assert that a
natural or innate faculty for supernatural works belongs to a human being.
Augustine, *On the Gift of Perseverance*, chapter —: ‘From this it is evident
that some people have in their very disposition a naturally divine gift of
understanding by which they are moved toward faith if they either hear words
or behold signs congruous to their minds.’ Note the word ‘naturally’ and also
the phrase ‘congruous to their minds.’ These indicate that the act of believing
itself is congenial to human nature and disposition and is in some respect
natural—not because an adequate faculty is congenital and natural, but at
least because a natural and congenital faculty is incomplete. Likewise,
Augustine in *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 5: ‘To be able to
have faith belongs to nature—to have faith belongs to grace.’ The possibility
is not only passive but also an active power, because we are able to believe,
eliciting the act of faith.”

Reply: We do not deny a remote and certain capacity for grace. But we deny
that this dormant and sleeping capacity is brought into act solely by a
congruous and moral suasive motion—namely, the letter of the Law and
Gospel—without the infusion of a real power and habit. 2. The possibility is
both passive and active. It is passive because it needs a supernatural power
to become more proximate and active. It is not a power of the kind possessed
by stones, pieces of wood, and beasts, as

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 467 | PDF p. 518 | split word joined: Augustinus | erratum applied: οὐ δυνάται]

Augustine says, but is rational and vital, and formally breaks forth into act.
Nor is it the middle power discussed by Augustine in *On the Spirit and the
Letter*, chapter 33, where he doubts and disputes: “Let us first say and
consider whether the fact that free Choice was naturally bestowed by the
Creator is an answer to this question—whether it is that middle power by which
one can either be directed toward faith or be inclined toward unbelief.” Therefore,
this is a remote natural power of believing.

Martinez replies in the same place, number 25: “A purely remote power has no
other force upon an effect than that by which the immediate and proximate
power operates, since it reaches the effect immediately through that power
alone and not through itself. But a purely remote power without the proximate
one is not merely nerveless and weak but utterly nothing. Yet Hugo Victorinus
explains the natural activity of believing by the example of sight eliciting
vision together with light, since it does not possess the power of sight by a
purely remote power.”

Reply: Scripture in John 6:44; Romans 8:7; and 1 Corinthians 2:14 denies
any natural power to come to Christ—that is, to believe—unless a new ability
is infused. Therefore, the natural power is passive under this description.
2. If a power to believe is denied, then it is a remote power. 3. We deny that
a purely remote power is utterly nothing. *οὐ δυνάται*, “he cannot come,” John
6:44, yet he is a subject capable of grace that draws. 4. But it cannot be said
that a remote power does not immediately reach the effect, even if it reaches
it only by grace as the sole intermediary. For heated iron warms with a

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 468 | PDF p. 519]

remote power and also immediately reaches the effect, not through the
intervening fire alone. For it warms immediately through the density of the
matter, by which the powers and degrees of the fire's heat are more firmly
united.

5. Hugo Victorinus's interpretation in *Questions Elucidated in the Epistle to
the Romans*, page 238, is unhappy. He expressly says that “the soul naturally
has the power of meriting.” Yet in that case the eye exists and the faculty of
sight is unimpaired and sound, though set in darkness. Light does not add a
new real power of seeing but presupposes that sight is whole, sound, and
unimpaired, and that even in darkness the power of seeing is proximate. The
Jesuits also concede that a new habit is infused, but only so that acts are
elicited more easily and sweetly. For they hold the natural power to be weak
but not entitatively diminished. 2. As for cooperation, they teach that it is
indifferent and general. 3. They teach only a moral persuasion, which adds no
powers to free Choice.
