ON GOD

Thus far we have treated the first principle of theology, namely, cognition.
Now follows the first principle with respect to the object, concerning God,
before whom nothing can be prior in the order of nature, since he is the
principle of being for all things.

Those who disputed about God were either, first, pagans or people who
professed knowledge of Scripture. The pagans are of three kinds. Some simply
denied that God exists, such as Diagoras, surnamed the Atheist, and Theodorus,
of whom Cicero speaks in book 1 of *The Nature of the Gods*. Some doubted
whether God exists, such as Protagoras. Others affirmed that God exists. Of
these, some maintained that there is only one, as Plato did, and others that
there are many. Of the latter, some said the spirits were incorporeal, while
others said the gods were corporeal; these included dead men and animals in
the number of the gods, as the apostle teaches in Romans 1, chapter and verse
23. The pagans divided the gods into celestial, terrestrial, infernal, marine,
fountain, river, household, La—

[1620 Tome I | col. 171 | BSB frame 00100]

—res, and so forth. The apostle identifies the cause of all these errors in
Romans 1:19, 20, 21, and 23.

Those who professed knowledge of Scripture and disputed about God either held
an orthodox view of God according to Scripture or wrote impiously about God.
The latter either posit many gods or one. Of those who posit many, some posited
two unbegotten, eternal gods who were nevertheless contrary to one another:
one, the cause of all good things, whom they called the supreme good; the
other, the cause of all evil things, whom they called the supreme evil. These
were the Gnostics and Carpocratians immediately after the apostles, and
finally the Manichaeans; then the Cerdonians and Marcionites, who called one
the author and God of the law and the other the author and God of the gospel.
Last came the Valentinians, who named one *Depth* and the other *Silence*.
Others posited three; Augustine speaks of them in tome 3 of *The Christian
Combat*, and they are called Tritheists.

Those who confess one God with us nevertheless err concerning the Trinity. We
will speak of them when we treat the Trinity.

Theology considers two things concerning God: (1) his essence and (2) his
operation. It considers the essence in two ways: first, as one in its unity;
second, in its community, by which it is common to three persons. Operation,
however, is not a work of God outside God or an accident in God, but God
himself operating. Nevertheless, these are distinguished for the sake of
teaching.

Four things concerning God, considered in his unity, must be treated: (1)
whether he exists; (2) that he is one; (3) what he is; and (4) what he is
like.

QUESTION I

Whether God Exists

It ought to be certain that God exists, because one who comes to God must
believe

[1620 Tome I | col. 172 | BSB frame 00100]

that he exists, Hebrews 11:6. That God exists is proved against atheists by
these arguments.

Argument I. All created things and their ordered arrangement and preservation
(Pss. 8, 19, and 104; Rom. 1:19; Acts 17:23 and 14:17), and especially man,
the human soul, and its parts, endowments, and immortality—on account of which
the pagans called the soul a particle of divine breath—teach that there is
some supreme intelligent nature from which all things exist and by which they
are governed.

2. There are things which move and are moved, and things which are moved and
do not move. Therefore, there must be something that moves and is not moved,
which necessarily will be God. Otherwise, there will be an infinite regress
in the subordination of movers and things moved.

3. Experience and the senses teach that the parts of the world come into
being by causation. But a whole whose parts come into being by causation can
be called an effect. The whole world, however, cannot have been effected by
some particular cause, because a particular cause is part of the world, and a
part cannot be called the maker of itself or of the whole. Therefore, the
whole world is from a higher cause, namely, God. Furthermore, there cannot be
an infinite subordination of efficient causes. We must therefore arrive at
one supreme cause, namely, God.

4. That God exists is an innate principle, Romans 1:19, and the nations know
by nature the things of the law, Romans 2:14–15.

5. No nation was so barbarous that, on account of this innate principle that
God exists, it did not fashion a god for itself, Romans 1:21 and Acts 17:23.

6. The terrors of conscience in men who attempt to divest themselves of every
sense of divinity teach that God exists, Isaiah 66:24 and 57:24 and
Deuteronomy 4:24.[^1]

7. Even the atheists’ own disputations about God prove that God exists,
because thinking about God and thinking about something nonexistent are
diametrically opposed.

8. Even the notions—

[1620 Tome I | col. 173 | BSB frame 00101]

—of first principles born with us—such as the distinction between honorable
and shameful things, numbering, understanding consequence in reasoning, and
so forth—cannot exist by chance or from brute nature, but are necessarily
implanted in us by an intelligent cause, which is God, Romans 1:19 and 2:15.

9. The rewards of the good and punishments of the wicked testify that there
is a universal and omnipotent judge of the world, Psalms 9:17 and 58:12.

10. The establishment and preservation of political order, Proverbs 8:15.

11. Impulses and heroic virtues which surpass nature’s common capacity, such
as the invention of arts and sciences, greatness of spirit in performing
deeds, and so forth, Deuteronomy 31:8, Psalm 11, and Judges 14:15.

12. Indications of future things—hence Cicero rightly says in *On Divination*,
"If there is divination, there are gods"—which could not have been furnished
by human insight or from natural causes or signs, Isaiah 41:23 and Ezekiel
12:18.

13. The sense of God’s goodness in his benefits, Acts 14:15 and 17:27.

14. Admirable, extraordinary, and unexpected events which can be accomplished
only by a most powerful nature.

15. The end and usefulness of all things. For since all things have a definite
end, very few things foresee and perceive the end toward which they proceed
and incline, and the usefulness of things does not exist by chance or come
from brute nature, it necessarily follows that these things are ordered by a
most wise and powerful nature, namely, God, who governs each and every thing
in such a way as to direct it to its ends.

16. Most clearly of all, Scripture teaches that God exists, something all the
godly perceive from the Holy Spirit’s renewal, vivification, and consolation.

QUESTION II

That God Is One

[1620 Tome I | col. 174 | BSB frame 00101]

That there is only one God—not one in genus, species, subject, accident, or
causes, but one in number—is taught by these arguments against the
πολυθεΐα, "polytheism," of the nations, the Manichaeans, and the Tritheists.

Argument 1. Scripture says there is only one God, Deuteronomy 6:4, Mark 12:29,
Romans 3:30, Galatians 3:20, Ephesians 4:6, and 1 Timothy 2:5. Therefore.

2. Scripture denies that there are many gods, Deuteronomy 4:35 and 32:39,
Psalm 86:10, Isaiah 37:16 and 44:6, and 1 Corinthians 8:4. Therefore.

3. It befits the supreme majesty to rule without a partner, because the
authority of one who alone governs all things is greater than the authority
of one who governs with another; and *supreme* and *one* are convertible. But
the divine majesty is supreme. Therefore, it rules as one and without a
partner.

4. That which is most perfect can only be one, for he alone is most perfect
who alone is all things. Those who have something particular distributed
among themselves are all imperfect, because no one has the whole, but each
has a part. But God is most perfect, since he is the cause of all goodness and
perfection. Therefore, he is one.

5. If there are many gods, then each is unequal to governing all things, and
thus all would be imperfect. But to be imperfect and to be God are
incompatible. Therefore.

6. Tertullian, in book 1 *Against Marcion*: "Those who want many gods profess
one God in the formulas of speech which they use, as when they say, ‘If God
sees,’ and ‘as God pleases.’"[^2]

7. Lactantius, in book 2 of *The Origin of Error*: "The nations name God when
they swear, when they make a wish, and when they give thanks." Therefore.

8. The wisest among the poets and philosophers acknowledged only one God,
such as Orpheus, Sophocles, Pythagoras, and Plato, who calls the one God τὸ ἕν,
"the One." Therefore.

9. God by nature is infinite, immeasurable, and omnipotent, as will be proved
be—

[1620 Tome I | col. 175 | BSB frame 00102]

—low. But there cannot be many such beings. Therefore.

10. If there are many gods, they must be distinguished from one another by an
essential difference. Thus one will have what another does not, and so they
cannot be most perfect. But one who is not most perfect cannot be God, as will
be proved below. Therefore.

11. Cyprian argues in tract 4, *On the Vanity of Idols*, that each kingdom is
suited to only one king, which he proves by comparisons drawn from men and
animals. But the whole world is God’s one kingdom. Therefore.

12. A multitude of kings must come from one first and be referred back to one
first, for where there is no first there is no order; where there is no order
there is no good; and where there is no good there is no being, because being
and good are convertible. Therefore, it is necessary to posit one God.

QUESTION III

What God Is

Since God is infinite and incomprehensible, he cannot be known by us as he is
in himself, and therefore he cannot be defined.

His nature is nevertheless known in two ways, either in a natural or a
supernatural way. The natural way by which God is known is threefold. The
first is the way of eminence, by which whatever excels among creatures and
has the character of perfection is attributed to God, such as wisdom,
justice, and so forth.

The second is the way of negation, by which we remove from God things that are
imperfect among creatures, as when we say that he is immortal, infinite, and
so forth.

The third is the way of causation, by which we know God as cause from
creatures as effects, as when we know him to be power—

[1620 Tome I | col. 176 | BSB frame 00102]

—ful, good, and so forth.

But this natural knowledge is imperfect and defective in natural men and can
only render a man ἀναπολόγητον, "without excuse," Romans 1:20.

The supernatural way accords with the manner of divine revelation in the
Scriptures. Two things about God are revealed in the Scriptures, his essence
and attributes, and by these what God is like is known; these will be treated
below.

The essence, while it is the supreme and indivisible unity, has in this unity
a real and distinct Trinity of persons, so that neither the unity nor the
Trinity can be separated from the divine nature in an explanation.

God, therefore, considered in his essence, is defined as follows: God is
Jehovah Elohim; or, God is the supremely perfect essence of three persons, the
Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit; or, he is pure act, from whom all things
come and to whom all things return.

The name *essence* occurs in Scripture in Isaiah 28:29, where תושיה comes from
ישה, for which the anomalous תו was used. The Fathers also gathered it from
Scripture and rightly used this name in order to respond more easily to the
cavils of heretics. It is gathered from Exodus 3:14 and Revelation 1:8, where
God is said to be "he who is," or ὁ ὢν, being or existing itself. This signifies
that he is being to himself and exists from himself and through himself.
Therefore, he is being absolutely and consequently can rightly be called
essence. Furthermore, life, goodness, and attributes are ascribed to God in
the abstract. Therefore, he is essence.

Finally, Damascene says in chapter 1 of the *Isagoge*, οὐσία καὶ φύσις καὶ μορφὴ
κατὰ τοὺς ἁγίους πατέρας ταὐτὸν ἐστὶ, "Essence, nature, and form are the
same according to the holy Fathers." Yet φύσις, "nature," occurs in Galatians
1 and 2 Peter 1:4, and μορφὴ, "form," in Philippians 2:6.[^3] Although the
philosophers distinguish these three in the following way: οὐσία signifies bare
essence, which is expressed by genus and difference; φύσις adds essential
properties to the essence, such as being capable of instruction and being
suited to laughing in man, and so forth.

[1620 Tome I | col. 177 | BSB frame 00103]

μορφὴ here adds the accidents by which essence and nature are depicted, such
as having an upright face in man, and so forth. Accordingly, φύσις embraces
οὐσία, while μορφὴ embraces οὐσία and φύσις.

Essence is considered in two ways, either absolutely or with a mode of
existing. Considered absolutely, it is expressed by the name Jehovah;
considered with a mode of existing, it is expressed by the word *Elohim*.

Jehovah, contracted יה, was called by the Hebrews יהוה, God’s ineffable name.
This was not because the word itself could not be pronounced, but because
what this name signifies is ineffable and incomprehensible. In its place they
pronounced אדני, lest this name be profaned. This name is called the
Tetragrammaton, signifying that God has his elect in every region of the
world, whose Jehovah he is.

This name is derived from the root הוה or היה, which signifies *to be*. The
Seventy translated this word ὁ ὢν, "the One who is," as Christ does in
Revelation 1:8. This name therefore signifies the following things.

1. That God is being absolutely, that is, existing from himself, through
himself, and in himself. Hence this name is called essential and proper to
God, and by it he is distinguished from all fictitious gods and created
things, Exodus 13:15, Isaiah 42:8, and Amos 5:8.[^4]

2. That all things come from no other source than God as their author and
preserver, Isaiah 44:24, Romans 11:36, and Acts 17:28.

3. That God is constant and firm in keeping his promises, Exodus 6:3.

Therefore, this essential name belongs to God alone, because it properly
belongs only to him and cannot belong to another—such as an altar, the ark of
the covenant, or Jerusalem, Exodus 17, Jeremiah 3:6, 16, and Ezekiel 48:35—
except by metonymy, because these were signs and symbols of Jehovah’s
presence. Grammarians have observed this. For it is established that among
the Hebrews, names predicated of one do not receive an emphatic ה at the
beginning, a suffix at the end, or a plu—

[1620 Tome I | col. 178 | BSB frame 00103]

—ral number, and are never in the construct state. But this name is of that
kind. Therefore.

From these things it follows:

1. That God is substantial, not accidental, act, and that he is most pure,
free of all potency and passibility, and without a beginning in time, unlike
creatures; that nothing in God is universal, because universals are
potential; that being and acting are the same in God; and that he does all
things through his essence, not through powers diverse from his essence, by
which creatures act.

2. That God has no principle outside himself either for existing or for acting
or causing; instead, he is the first being and the first cause of all things,
and all creatures depend upon this being with respect to their existence and
essence, power of acting, and preservation or government.

3. That God is the most perfect essence, to whom nothing can be added and from
whom nothing can be taken away, since he needs nothing outside himself and
receives nothing from elsewhere, but alone suffices for himself in all things
and has all things from himself and in himself, Genesis 17:1, 28:3, 35:11, and
48:3; Job 42:2; Psalm 16:2; Matthew 19:26; Luke 1:35; 1 Timothy 6:15; James
1:17; and so forth. Indeed, the height and depth of this perfection are so
great that we cannot attain it even in thought, 1 Timothy 6:16 and Psalm
145:3. Therefore, we must speak and think soberly and according to the written
Word.

Objections against the Perfection of the Divine Essence

1. One who makes other things for his own sake is not supremely perfect. But
God made all things for his own sake, Proverbs 16:4. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is true of those who make other things for the sake
of increasing, assisting, or perfecting themselves, but it is false of one who
makes other things for the sake of communicating himself. Thus

[1620 Tome I | col. 179 | BSB frame 00104]

God makes other things for the sake only of communicating himself, because
the good is communicative of itself.

2. One who uses creatures in completing his works is not perfect in every
respect. But God is such. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is true only of one who is compelled by necessity
or powerlessness to use creatures. But God does not use creatures in this way;
he uses them most freely—because he can act without them if he wills—and in
order to show that he is Lord of all. He is therefore called Jehovah of hosts.

3. One to whom internal and external worship must be rendered is not
supremely perfect in himself. But God is such. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is true only of one to whom something is added by
worship. Nothing is added to God by worship, but something is added to us,
and by it the good which ought to be in us is displayed, just as disobedience
does not make God more miserable, but the creature, Psalm 16:2 and 50:12, Job
35:2, and Acts 17:25.

Instance. But worship is said to be given to and taken away from God.
Therefore.

Response. Worship is not said to be given to God as if God in himself needed
it when it is absent or were aided by it when it is present. Rather, it is
because men are bound by justice to render worship, and unless they render it
they fall away from their blessedness, Luke 17:10.

Instance. But glory pertains to the perfection of the one to whom it is
rendered. Therefore.

Response. God’s glory is taken in two ways. (1) It signifies absolutely the
properties and virtues in God. In this sense it always was, is, and will be the
same in God, and can neither be increased nor diminished, John 17:5. (2) It
signifies the acknowledgment and celebration of these virtues, which is not
in God but in creatures. It can become greater or lesser as God is more or
less acknowledged and celebrated. Whether it

[1620 Tome I | col. 180 | BSB frame 00104]

is increased or diminished, it increases or diminishes not God’s perfection
but the creature’s happiness.

Instance. God rejoices in the worship and glory which we render to him.
Therefore.

Response. Joy is not a movement or affection in God as it is in men, but
signifies his approval of our obedience. Nor does this follow: God approves
what we perform from duty for our good; therefore, something is thereby added
to God.

The name *Elohim*, a plural number, comes from אלוה, which is rarely used, and
from אל, that is, *strong*. God is so called because he is omnipotent. Yet
καταχρηστικῶς, "catachrestically," angels and magistrates are called gods
because of power communicated to them, Exodus 22:28 and Psalm 82:2. In
Scripture, however, when this name is ascribed to God, it signifies the three
persons in the divine essence. This name signifies not only what God is like
in himself, but how he is disposed toward us. Therefore, those who maintain
that God is called by this plural name merely for honor, after the human
manner, are in error. More will be said about this name when we treat the
Trinity.
