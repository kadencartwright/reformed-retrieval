VERSE 10. The person who loves money is not satisfied with money, and the
person who loves any abundance will not be satisfied with its yield. This too
is vanity.

The love of agriculture is commended by comparison with something inferior to
it, namely, the pursuit of money. This pursuit is convicted from its effect:
it satisfies no one, however hungry; clothes no one, however naked; and heals
no one suffering from disease. In short, of itself it has no importance for
preserving this life. The late date at which money was invented proves that
its use, though advantageous, is not so necessary. For it is well known that
at first people carried on commerce with one another by a mutual exchange of
services. Aristotle, *Ethics* 5. Finally, because there is a great inequality
between services—for example, building a house and fitting shoes or
clothing—money was invented to establish equality.

This is amplified by a comparison with the greater case. Although a person
may multiply his money through love of it, nevertheless no

benefit from it comes back to him. Even if he were permitted to roll about in
silver and gold, he would gain from it no aid either for obtaining good or for
escaping evil. This ought to break mortals' accursed hunger for gold and
quench their thirst. They persuade themselves that they can obtain everything
with money. It is clear that this opinion once settled in the minds of the
Jews. When the Prophet announced their destruction, the thought immediately
occurred to them that they would deliver themselves from danger with money.
But Isaiah teaches that their opinion was false, because they would have to
deal with people who valued gold and silver as nothing. Isaiah 13:17.

From this it seems possible to deduce that the arts and branches of knowledge
whose use extends most widely and which contribute to preserving every society
of human life must be placed above those which serve the advantage—and
especially the pleasure and delights—of only a few people. If human
occupations were measured by this test, most of those now held in high esteem
would be found worthless, while those now despised would shine more brightly.

Here someone may ask why the patriarchs Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob were
herdsmen rather than farmers. I answer that they lived as sojourners in a
foreign region, where it was easier for them to move livestock from one region
to another than crops. The Prophet pronounces a sentence of vanity upon this
pursuit of agriculture. However much people suppose themselves happy in the
possession of these things, they are empty and vain for acquiring or
establishing happiness. This is confirmed in the next two verses.
