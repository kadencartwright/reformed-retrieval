## QUESTION XI

### ON THE THIRD COMMANDMENT

Do all oaths so bind the conscience that we are constrained to observe them
by an inevitable necessity?

It is distinguished.

[Marginal heading: The question concerning fidelity to an oath is decided in
three propositions.]

I. To the third commandment belongs the important question concerning
*fidelity to an oath*: what the obligation of an oath is and how great it is,
and in which cases we may rescind a pledge that has been given or are bound to
keep it. We comprise its decision in three propositions.

[Marginal heading: Proposition 1. An unlawful oath is invalid.]

II. First: *Every oath that is opposed to the law of God and to legitimate
obedience toward the magistrate, and that concerns either unlawful or
impossible things, does not bind*

[Printed p. 60 | Internet Archive PDF 88 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

*the swearer to observe it.* For an oath ought not to be a bond of iniquity,
and no one ought to be compelled to perpetrate anything wickedly. Here the
apostolic saying must always prevail: *It is better to obey God than human
beings.* To this belongs the familiar saying of Isidore of Seville, canon *In
mal.*, Cause 22, question 4: “With promises wrongly made, break the pledge;
with a shameful vow, change the decree; do not do what you have rashly vowed.”
There is also the saying of the other Isidore, of Pelusium, book 4, letter 96:
ἄμεινον γὰρ ἐν τοῖς ἐναγέσι τὰ κακῶς ἐπαγγελθέντα λύειν καλῶς, ἢ τὸ
δοκεῖν ἐνορκεῖν κακῶς, “In matters that possess the atrocity of a crime, it is
better to rescind well what was promised badly than to appear to keep an oath
badly.”

Hence David’s oath, made when he swore in a fury that he would destroy the
whole house of Nabal with the sword because provisions had been refused, was
unlawful and consequently not binding (1 Sam. 25:22–23). So too was Herod’s
oath promising the head of John the Baptist to a most unchaste woman (Matt.
14:7), and the oath of the Jews who conspired to kill Paul (Acts 23:21). Such
oaths not only do not bind, because an evil thing receives no force from an
oath, but increase guilt if they are observed. For then two sins are
committed: one belongs to the species of the deed considered in itself; the
other is against religion, arising from the abuse of God’s name insofar as an
evil purpose is confirmed by God’s authority. Monastic vows belong to this
kind: as they are badly made, so they are worse observed and lawfully revoked.

III. Defective oaths of this kind are various. First, with respect to their
*matter*, they concern either unlawful or impossible things. Second, with
respect to the *persons*, there are those who cannot swear, such as children,
the insane, fools, the drunk, the delirious, or those who otherwise cannot use
rational judgment. Such also are those who are not their own masters but are
under the authority of others, such as children, servants, women, and so
forth. If they bind themselves by oath to something against the wishes of
those to whom they are subject, they are not bound by law but ought to rescind
it according to the Lord’s command in Numbers 30:4, even if this is done under
the pretext of worship and religion. Thus, when the formal ground of an oath
is removed, the oath ceases on account of the event. This is the case with
those who swore that they would obey some lord or prince who afterward ceases
to be such.

[Marginal heading: Proposition 2. An oath about a lawful and possible matter
binds.]

IV. The second proposition: *Every oath about a lawful and possible matter,
which can be performed without impairing piety, the obedience owed to the
magistrate, and the welfare of one’s neighbor, must be kept without any
subterfuge or deceit, even if it is extorted by force or fear and is joined
with temporal wrong and loss to the swearer, because such an oath is made with
the invocation of the divine name, which must not be profaned.* Its truth
rests upon the explicit words of Scripture. Leviticus 19:11–12 says: “Do not
lie, nor use deceit against your neighbor. Nor swear by my name to any
falsehood.” And Matthew 5:33 says: “You shall not perjure yourself, but you
shall render your oaths to the Lord.” Likewise Psalm 15:4 says: “He who has
sworn to evil”—that is, the evil of loss, which he sees will harm his affairs,
not the evil of an offense—“and does not change will dwell on the mountain of
the Lord.”

Although a wrong that has been done removes the obligation with respect to
the human being who inflicts the wrong, since no right is founded upon a
wrong, the obligation made to God nevertheless remains and cannot be
disregarded without irreverence and injury to God. This is the case of a
person forced by robbers to swear that he will pay the price of his ransom. He
either ought not to swear or ought religiously to observe the oath he has
made, because the oath was made to God, who is not mocked. Second, it was not
made by one who was unwilling without qualification, but is a mixed action in
which there is something voluntary, because between paying the money and
redeeming his life he chooses what is better. Third, the thing promised is
possible and private, and religion and truth must always be preferred to it.

Nor should it be said that robberies and plunder are thus established if the
robber is paid what he extorted by force. This occurs accidentally, for the
traveler who swore

[Printed p. 61 | Internet Archive PDF 89 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

neither assists the robbers nor approves their deed, but redeems his life by
paying what he promised under oath. He can, however, oppose this evil to the
best of his ability by reporting the robbery to the magistrate. Nor is it an
objection if he has also sworn silence to the robber, because this is an oath
about an unlawful thing that bears the appearance of collusion with robbers.
Even if it bore no such appearance at all, it would nevertheless hinder
justice, confirm the wicked in their malice, and give neighbors an occasion
to fall into danger of death, and thus violate piety, justice, and charity in
many ways.

V. This same point is confirmed by the example of the Israelites toward the
Gibeonites. Although they had made a covenant with them ratified by an oath,
having been deceived by their guile as though they were travelers from the
most distant places, when the subterfuge and fraud were afterward discovered
the Israelites did not rescind the oath but fulfilled it in good faith, even
though the Gibeonites belonged to those nations that God had devoted to
extermination. Joshua 9:19 says: “We promised them by oath through Jehovah,
the God of Israel; therefore, we cannot now touch them. Let us do this for
them: let us preserve them alive, so that indignation may not be upon us
because of that oath by which we swore to them.” And lest anyone condemn this
deed as insufficiently approved by God, God testifies by a joyful and
miraculous victory that it was pleasing to him (Josh. 10:13). When Saul, a
hundred years having now elapsed since the covenant, killed some of the
Gibeonites, God testified how greatly this deed displeased him by sending a
famine. This perjury could be expiated only by the hanging of seven of Saul’s
sons (2 Sam. 21:1 and following).

VI. Just as the religious obligation of an oath belongs to the divine forum,
so it is not subject to the human forum. Hence no class or order of human
beings has been granted absolution from an oath, whether wholly by a
dispensation, which is a simple dissolution of the bond, or in part by
commutation through payment, which is the transfer of the bond from one
matter to another.

[Marginal heading: Proposition 3. Sworn faith must be kept even with
heretics.]

VII. The third proposition: *Sworn faith must be kept even with heretics and
unbelievers.* This is opposed to the opinion received among the Roman
Catholics that faith must not be kept with heretics, which was the foundation
of the dreadful massacres and slaughters wickedly carried out against
Protestants both in the preceding century and in our own. The first authors
of this perfidy were the Roman pontiffs themselves, who not only held this
position but also openly professed it and confirmed it by their practice.
First, they did so against excommunicated emperors, kings, and princes—whom
they treat as unbelievers—with whom they neither keep faith themselves nor
will that others should keep it. This was begun by Gregory III against Leo
III the Iconoclast. Second, they did so against unbelievers alien to their
religion, as is clear even from the one example of Władysław, king of Poland,
against Murad the Turk. At the urging of Cardinals Julian and Francis, he
violated the treaty and broke the faith he had pledged. Third, they did so
against alleged heretics, as was done toward Jan Hus and Jerome of Prague at
the Council of Constance. This position is strenuously retained and pressed
by the Jesuits today.

VIII. I acknowledge that some of the Roman Catholics, ashamed of so
disgraceful a dogma, seem to be averse to this impious opinion and even deny
that this is a Roman Catholic dogma. But it is clear, even from the single
decree of the Council of Constance in Session 19, that this is said vainly and
falsely:

“The present holy synod declares that from any safe-conduct granted by the
emperor and other secular princes to heretics or persons accused of
heresy—thinking thus to recall them from their errors—by whatever bond they
may have bound themselves, no prejudice to the catholic faith or
ecclesiastical jurisdiction can or ought to be produced, nor any impediment
furnished, so as to prevent the competent ecclesiastical judge,
notwithstanding the said safe-conduct, from inquiring into the errors of such
persons, otherwise duly proceeding against them, and punishing them as far as
justice shall counsel, if they stubbornly refuse to revoke their errors, even
if

[Printed p. 62 | Internet Archive PDF 90 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

relying upon the safe-conduct, they have come to the place of judgment when
they otherwise would not have come. Nor, when the one making the promise has
otherwise done what lies in him, has he thereby remained bound in any
respect.”

This was also immediately confirmed in practice in Jan Hus and Jerome of
Prague, who were wickedly burned contrary to the pledged faith and the
safe-conduct of Emperor Sigismund. Nor should the Council of Trent in Session
25, in the safe-conduct given to Protestants, have explicitly *derogated for
this occasion* from the Council of Constance and from that maxim that faith
must not be kept with heretics, unless it otherwise acknowledged this to be a
settled dogma of the Roman communion.

Nor is the position of Thomas any different in II-II, question 97, article 1,
where he maintains: “A heretical person must be handed over to the judges,
notwithstanding pledged faith and an oath.” So too Cardinal Toledo in
*Priestly Instruction*, book 1, chapter 13, number 7, says: “Those who were
otherwise subject to the bond of fidelity or of an oath to another are freed
from such a bond if that person has fallen into excommunication.” But all
heretics are excommunicated by that very fact.

This is confirmed by Azorius, *Moral Institutes*, part 1, book 8, chapter 13,
and the *Directory of Inquisitors*, part 3, question 129, where it is taught:
“All those who are bound to someone by any kind of obligation are then
entirely freed when those to whom they were obligated have fallen into
heresy. This penalty,” says the author concerning the loss of the right of
obligations, “produces many effects, and it will not be irksome to recount
some. First, therefore, one with whom a heretic has deposited something will
not be bound to return the thing to the heretic after his heresy becomes
manifest. Again, neither will a Catholic wife be obliged to render the
marital debt to a heretical husband, and so forth. Add to these that the
keepers of fortresses, castles, or cities are not bound to restore them to a
heretical lord or to guard them in his name. Finally, all vassals are freed by
the law itself from every obligation, even the religious obligation of the
oath by which they were held bound to their lords.”

Thus Cardinal Simancas in *On Catholic Institutions*, book 46, article 52:
“The hatred of heretics also pertains to their punishment: faith given to
them is not to be kept, and so forth. Catholics ought to have no commerce and
no peace with heretics. Therefore, faith given to them, even when confirmed
by oath, is in no way to be kept when it is contrary to the public good, the
salvation of souls, and divine and human laws.” To this belong the impious
decrees of the pontiffs freeing subjects from the oath of fidelity toward
princes, whether because of heresy or excommunication. Nor does the mystery
of Jesuitical equivocations and dispensations from oaths aim at anything else
than to deceive people, thus evading every compact and covenant, so
that scarcely anyone can safely contract with them.

How much more upright was the mind of Charles V here. When he was urged to
break the faith pledged to Luther as a heretic, he nobly replied: “Even if
fidelity were exiled from the whole world, it ought nevertheless to remain
spotless with the emperor.” This also is the position of the orthodox, founded
upon the strongest reasons.

IX. First, because keeping faith is an act of truth, justice, and fidelity.
For one who keeps faith performs what he says, which belongs to truth; does
what he promised and to which he is bound, which belongs to fidelity; and
renders to another what is owed to him by promise, which belongs to justice.

X. Second, because it is lawful to pledge faith by oath to unbelievers, and
therefore also to keep faith with them. This is clear from the examples of
Abraham making a covenant with Abimelech (Gen. 21:23–24), Isaac (Gen. 26:26),
Jacob (Gen. 31:53), the spies with Rahab (Josh. 6:22), and the Israelites
toward the Gibeonites (Josh. 9:15). The condition of heretics cannot be worse.

XI. Third, because God most severely avenged such acts of perjury in the sons
of Jacob against the Shechemites (Gen. 34; 49), in Saul because of the
Gibeonites (2 Sam. 21), in Zedekiah against Nebuchadnezzar (2 Chron. 36;
Ezek.

17), in Władysław, king of Poland, against Murad the Turk, and in Rudolf of
Swabia against Emperor Henry IV.

XII. Fourth, because marriages with unbelievers are not to be dissolved
because of unbelief and heresy, nor is sworn faith to be violated because of
it (1 Cor. 7:13–14). Therefore, neither are any other compacts to be violated,
because a religious pledge is involved in both.

XIII. Fifth, because sworn faith must be kept even with enemies by the law of
nature and of nations, as is clear from the illustrious examples of Regulus,
Agesilaus, and others. But unbelievers and heretics no less than enemies
themselves are included under that law.
