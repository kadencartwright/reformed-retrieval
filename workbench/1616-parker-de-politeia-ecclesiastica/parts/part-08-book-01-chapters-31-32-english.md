CHAPTER XXXI.

The Genevan Reformation Is Vindicated from Calumnies.

We must now come to the alteration of the ecclesiastical state, by which, once
Episcopal government had been rejected, an Aristocratic polity was at last
established according to the rule of God’s Word and with the consent of the
whole city. I see three principal objections which Bancroft makes against it.

[1638 control | printed p. 91 | BSB canvas 101 | bsb10507786_00101]

First, he repeatedly presses the resistance of the Senate. I certainly do not
see to what purpose this should be pressed, since the doctrine of the Gospel
itself was introduced everywhere in the world not without enormous resistance
from evil men. Indeed, the dreadful end of the principal Syndics, recorded by
Beza in his *Life of Calvin*, plainly declares that those who resisted the
discipline were evil.

Second, he objects that Calvin used craftiness; Richard Hooker too fastens
remarkable cunning upon him, as if Calvin differed scarcely at all from an
impostor in procuring discipline. Both rely upon the letters which Beza
published, as though it were likely that Calvin was so demented as to proclaim
himself a fool, Ecclesiastes 10:3. Let the letters be read through. What, in the
end, will an impartial reader find in them which does not breathe a plainly
serpentine prudence joined with dovelike simplicity? Would that—oh,
would that—the world might come to know the arts by which the English Bishops
have prolonged the tyranny of their Hierarchy down to the present time. What
Firmilian once answered in a bad cause on behalf of Cyprian, accused as an
impostor by Stephen of Rome, we can now answer more truly in a good cause on
behalf of Calvin: “He forestalled him, so that through lies he might charge
another with those things which he himself deserved to hear.”

Third, Bancroft says that Calvin circumvented the unwary Genevans and, when
nothing was further from their suspicions, imposed an authority equivalent to
Episcopal authority, which he also drew to himself, even though meanwhile he
strenuously professed the complete contrary. Hadrian Saravia too is guilty of
this calumny. “That saying is true,” he says: “‘Though you drive nature out with
a pitchfork, it will always return.’ For it is agreed that Beza is far superior
to his colleagues, and while John Calvin lived he had more authority over his
colleagues than the primate of England today exercises over his fellow Bishops.
I speak of known things, so that it would be shameless to deny what the thing
itself cries out and their writings attest.”

Yet what he calls known is false, and it was shameless to say it, contrary to
what both the thing itself cries out and their writings attest. But suppose it
was so: I hope you will nevertheless confess that a Bishop in England now
exercises greater authority over the other ministers than Calvin and Beza
exercised over their colleagues. “Indeed,” he says elsewhere, “do you wish to
know who has nourished and still nourishes such great agreement among the
pastors at Geneva until now? They have the one man whom they deny and whom they
do not want: a single Bishop. For whatever Master Beza may write or think, he
will never persuade the world that in the consistory, in deliberations, and in
the entire government of the Church he so yields to his other colleagues in the
authority and weight of his opinion, just as in the place of presidency. The
same opinion is held concerning John Calvin of pious memory. Zanchi did not
conceal this either when he expounded his judgment concerning Archbishops and
Bishops. But even where,” he says, “neither those old, good Greek words nor
these new, badly Latin words obtain, there nevertheless tend to be certain
principal men with whom almost all authority rests, and so forth. You see how
the force of nature leads us blind men back to the place from which we flee. Let
Master Beza write what—”

[Marginal references: Bancroft, page 16; Cyprian, Epistle 75; pages 22, 26;
Saravia, *Against Beza*, chapter 3, page 12; chapter 24, page 270.]

[1638 control | printed p. 92 | BSB canvas 102 | bsb10507786_00102]

“—ever he wishes: Geneva, or any other place, will never be able to bear two
Bezas or two Calvins disagreeing in one Church.”

First, a response must be made concerning Zanchi. He never approved the English
Bishop. That Bishop whom Zanchi believed the fathers had established in ancient
times was none other than a German Superintendent, as the sequence of his
discourse itself demonstrates. He said that account must be taken of those
Churches which had Bishops. It is clear from what follows that he did not
understand England to be among them, because, when he described in the next
words the Churches which he wished to be understood, he passed over mention of
England and dwelt only upon German Churches, which have Superintendents.

What is more, even if he acknowledged that these were the ancient Bishops, he
nevertheless saw something in them which he so greatly either missed or
disapproved that he steadfastly refused the Superintendent presidency often
offered to him by the brethren. As a result, he lived, grew old, and died as no
more than an ordinary Doctor. Would that man have approved the English Bishop
when he did not approve the German Superintendent in every respect?

As for his saying that authority at Geneva rested almost with one man, it must
be understood of the authority of presidency. John Calvin presided throughout
his life, and Beza also presided for a long time, namely until Daneus arrived at
Geneva, as George Downham himself relates. Thus the matter stands. The German
Superintendent differed from the Genevan president only in this: the latter was
made changeable, the former unchangeable. But since the Genevan president is
not changed, Zanchi says, who else in effect but a German Superintendent
presides at Geneva?

Second, whatever authority Calvin or Beza had was an authority only of merit,
not of power. We do not begrudge the English Bishops that authority, once the
yoke of power by which we are oppressed has been removed. If they can turn out
to be Calvins and Bezas, let them enjoy the esteem of Calvin and Beza. “Yet I,”
says Saravia, “would rather that Beza had a lawful Bishop than a tyrant for a
colleague.” I shall expose the injustice of this calumny to you by a parallel.

In Sculting’s work, the title of the fourth chapter of the fifth book of
Nicholas Sander’s *On the Visible Monarchy* is this: “That not even a conspiracy
of demons, much less a synagogue of Lutherans and Calvinists, can stand and
flourish without the visible primacy of one pastor or of another kind.” In the
chapter itself, however, he affirms that Luther acted as a plainly Saxon king
and, as it were, a Pope; he says the same of Superintendents and the English
Bishops. Saravia says that Calvin and Beza conduct themselves as Bishops: so
necessary is the episcopate for preserving unity. Sander says that Luther and
the English Bishops bear the image of Papal primacy: so necessary is this
primacy for preserving unity. What difference, I ask, is there here? Therefore
be ashamed to object against Calvin and Beza what, when it is objected against
you, you reckon the height of injustice. For I would dare to say that the
English Bishops come as near to tyranny as Beza and Cal—

[Marginal references: Saravia, *Against Beza*, chapter 3, page 53; book 1,
chapter 8, page 124.]

[1638 control | printed p. 93 | BSB canvas 103 | bsb10507786_00103]

—vin were far from it, so that nothing can be more unjust than this insinuation
of Saravia’s. But to conclude this passage: Hadrian himself elsewhere frees
Calvin and Beza from every suspicion of malicious deceit which Bancroft has
objected against them. Let the reader judge how consistently he does so with
himself. These are his words:

“I believe that, by the permission of divine providence, it happened that men
otherwise very weighty desired the whole order of Bishops to be removed and
judged that it ought to be removed. For it would be better that there were no
Bishops in the Church than that the Church should have wolves for Bishops and
endure those monsters which the Roman Church has brought forth for us. This
error frees from every suspicion of ambition the teachers through whom God
restored the truth of doctrine. No one can accuse them of having been moved by
a zeal for innovation so that they themselves might succeed to the dignities
and wealth of the Bishops. But afterward the innocence and sincere candor of
those who set before themselves no wealth, no honors, and, in short, nothing
besides the restoration of true piety appeared more clearly than light to all
who wished to see,” and so forth.

Thus the Genevan reformation is preserved safe and sheltered, with its
innocence intact.

CHAPTER XXXII.

The Other Reformed Churches Are Vindicated from the Calumny of Craftiness and
Perverse Emulation.

It remains for us to repair the damage which the virulence of detractors has
inflicted upon the reformations of the other Churches. A perverse emulation of
the Genevans is imputed to all of them: had it not existed, France, Scotland,
Belgium, and the other reformed Churches would not have received Presbyterian
discipline. Hence Saravia says: “It seems shameful to them to dissent from their
masters. So great is a master’s authority and so great the reverence for him
that the mere affection of one man persuades more than the writers of all ages.
This prejudice bars the way to truth. It was not without reason that the true
Master forbade us to choose a mortal master here on earth. There is one Master
in heaven; we ought to be taught by him.”

First, let us turn the accusation back. We do not venerate Calvin and Beza as
the Hierarchists venerate Whitgift and Bilson, so that we may answer with
Tertullian: “We have no master but God alone. But those whom you regard as
masters for yourself are men.”

Second, you know that this very thing is hurled by the Papists against the
reformation of doctrine itself. They say that our Churches embraced it because
they were swept away by headlong admiration for Luther, whom, they affirm, we
celebrate as our own Apostle and consider worthy to be preferred even to Paul.
These last things recall for me Bancroft’s own words. Intending to prove that
Genevan discipline flooded into the other Churches because of excessive
admiration for Calvin, he dwells upon a certain Genevan who said that, even if
Paul were about to ascend the pulpit at Geneva, he would nevertheless go to
hear Calvin. Yet these things fall upon doctrine itself, and not upon discipline
alone. This can be demonstrated by the ex—

[Marginal references: Saravia, ibidem; Tertullian, *To Scapula*, chapter 5.]

[1638 control | printed p. 94 | BSB canvas 104 | bsb10507786_00104]

—ample of Hadrian Saravia alone. For why would Gretzer not call him a Calvinist
with respect to doctrine, just as Saravia calls us Calvinists with respect to
discipline?

Third, what Saravia is to Gretzer, we are to Saravia. By what arguments, then,
do you conclude that we are Calvinists or that we have deified the discipline for
his sake? Bancroft adduces as an argument that letters were sent to Master Beza
from every quarter. Saravia himself refutes it: “It does not displease me,” he
says, “that Master Beza was consulted in a most weighty matter.” It does not
displease him? What place, then, must Bancroft be assigned, who for this reason
says that Calvin and Beza were two Patriarchs—indeed, “two fathers of the
Protestants, such as the Pope sells himself to his Papists as being”? Is he not
ashamed of the calumny? For it is ready to hand for us to answer Gretzer in
Saravia’s words: “Neither Calvin, nor Luther, nor Beza is a Patriarch to us.
Know, Ignatian, that the English Church has no master but Christ alone.”

As for Calvin and Beza themselves: do you not yourself confess that Calvin was
an excellent man? And who would forbid consulting an excellent man in a most
weighty matter? But, you will say, he said these things about Calvin with a view
to doctrine, not discipline. Perhaps. But if he was excellent in doctrine, why
not in discipline too? Let us therefore be permitted to turn back that argument
of Tertullian: “O Apollo, not consulted! He gave the testimony of wisdom to a
man who denied that the gods exist.” He is speaking of Socrates; why should we
not say the same of Calvin? These things answer the general calumny by which all
the reformed Churches are defamed.

2. Certain particular charges follow. Nothing particular is objected against
France or Belgium. Concerning England, Bancroft says: “The Puritans consulted
Beza by letters. At his instigation many private assemblies and very many
secret, factious designs followed.” We answer with Tertullian: “This assembly
of Christians would rightly be unlawful if it were equal to unlawful
assemblies; it would rightly be condemned if anyone complained of it under the
heading under which a complaint is made about factions. When have we ever
assembled for anyone’s destruction? Gathered together we are what we are when
dispersed; as a body, what we are as individuals: harming no one and grieving
no one. When upright men, when good men, when pious men come together, it must
not be called a faction but a council.”

As for Bancroft’s remaining calumnies, by which he calls us Hypocrites, people
of the sort of that H. N., serpents, locusts, Edomites, Ashdodites, and
Sanballats—and, finally, those
carnal sectaries who in Jude despise governments and speak evil of those placed
in authority—I answer nothing else than that what he fastens upon us elsewhere
must be turned back upon him: “Their tongues are their own; the Lord will cut
off tongues that speak great things,” Psalm 12.

We must next move over into Scotland, where Bancroft objects, first, that
discipline was established by John Knox in a popular tumult. But do not the
same things, I ask, fall upon doctrine itself? It was once doctrine which, when
the Papists accused it, sustained the charge for the Scottish tumults. Now the
entire burden is imposed upon discipline by the Protestants themselves. Yet it
is freed by the same defense

Tertullian, *Apologeticus*, chapters 39, 45; further references read pages 70,

(for no other is needed) which Jewel constructs for doctrine against Harding,
and Thomas Bilson against Cardinal Allen. Jewel says: “I will say nothing about
the kingdom of Scotland. The kingdoms of the world have various covenants and
constitutions. The nobility and people there neither drew their swords nor
attempted violence against their prince. They sought only the continuance of
undoubted divine truth and the defense of their own lives against your
barbarous and cruel invasions. Among other warning signs, they remembered your
savagery at Vassy, where enormous multitudes of their brethren, while praying
in the church itself, stretching innocent hands to heaven and calling upon the
name of God, were slaughtered without warning. Those nobles learned from Peter:
‘It is better to obey God than men,’ and so forth.”

Bilson, however, imputes all the Scottish tumults to the Papists who resisted
both doctrine and discipline. He ascribes wholly to them both the murder of the
king and the attempt to destroy James in his mother’s womb. Even Bancroft
cannot deny that discipline, no less than doctrine, at last overcame all the
attempts of the rioting Papists. He seems only to intimate some deceit or other
when he says that anyone may see in the Scottish chronicles how discipline was
sanctioned by an Act of Parliament. What is this “how,” or what answer can you
make to so general an accusation? It is enough for us that discipline was
established by a sufficiently lawful means, not only by an Act of Parliament
but also by an oath of all the estates. These are the words of the mandate:

“On the second of March in the year 1581, a mandate of the Royal Majesty was
issued in the house of the Holy Cross, by which all the commissioners, and the
ministers of the Word by name, were commanded—as the King himself and all his
household had subscribed to the aforesaid confession—to require this confession
from everyone in all their parishes and to report those who refused to the
ministers of the Royal household, under a fine of forty florins to be deducted
from their stipends.” The reason why those who refused were to be reported is
added in that mandate: namely, he says, “so that we, with the counsel of our
Senate, may bring such despisers of God and of our laws into order.” This
mandate was undersigned by the royal hand and seal, at the place and time named
above. These too are the words of the oath:

“Promising and solemnly swearing by that great Name of the Lord our God that we
will persevere in the doctrine and discipline of this Church, and that,
according to our calling and ability, we will defend them throughout all the
days of our life, under penalty of all the curses contained in the law and at
the peril of body and soul on the day of that dreadful Judgment of God.”

But King James, says Becanus, declares that he found more good faith in Papists,
indeed in savage robbers, than in Puritans. Yet in the preface to the later
edition the same author openly protests that he did not understand all those
who stood for reformed discipline either in Scotland or in England. But, you
say, in the admonitory preface he calls those who stand for reformation in

England Puritans. All of them? Since in Scotland he understood only certain
factious men, why should he not be judged to have understood only certain men
in England whom he considers fanatics? Certainly the man who envelops everyone
in this censure must hear what was brought from Jerome into canon law: [“Do not
judge anyone before you hear him, because it is not the man who is accused who
is immediately guilty, but the man who is convicted who is criminal.”]

I press the case of holy Chrysostom, who under Emperor Arcadius most piously and
innocently suffered many unjust and many unworthy things. Even if all of us are
criminal, the discipline for which we stand is nevertheless not itself
criminal. Before us, Calvin and Beza and the other reformed Churches embraced
it; the same Royal Majesty, by a particular admonition composed for this
purpose, strikes those Churches from the catalogue of Puritans. Therefore the
immoderation of those in England who from pulpits and professorial chairs most
foully rage against Calvin, Beza, and the other fosterers of the reformed
Churches as though they were Puritans cannot be covered by James’s cloak.

These things answer the third objection commonly brought against imitation of
the reformed Churches: namely, their alleged craftiness or perverse emulation
by which they are said to have imposed Presbyterian discipline.
