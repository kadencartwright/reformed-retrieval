On verses 13 and 14. “In whom you also.”

[Marginalia: Lecture XIV.]

Thus far we have had the first part of this application, in which the Apostle
applied that salvation to the believing Jews. It now remains to explain the
second part of the same application, in which he applies it to the believing
Gentiles—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 89 | Caput I p. 47]

—especially the Ephesians to whom he writes—in these two verses, when he
speaks as follows: “in whom you also,” that is, “you hoped,” as Beza, Piscator,
and others supply it, or rather, “you were admitted into the inheritance”
together with the believing Jews, as seems to me, with Zanchi and certain
others, the preferable word to supply. For since a verb must necessarily be
repeated here from the common construction, as the sequence of the context
itself indicates, and one of these two verbs must be repeated, I would
certainly prefer to supply the former, “to be allotted an inheritance,” rather
than the latter, “to hope.” This is both because it is evident that the former
verb has the principal place in the preceding verses, holding, as it were, the
key to the discourse and guiding its course, and so that it may be all the
more apparent from this that the same thing is applied here to the believing
Gentiles which he attributed above to the believing Jews. The believing
Ephesians are also said to have been admitted into the same inheritance and
thus, in this respect, made equal to the Jews, so that both groups, growing
together in Christ, may have no cause for complaint, but rather equal cause to
give thanks for divine beneficence. The matter comes to the same thing,
however, whichever of those two verbs we supply here.

[Marginalia: Exegesis. Reason for the choice.]

“Having heard the word of truth,” that is, a word not only true but containing
what is preeminently called the truth, namely, “the gospel of your salvation,”
by which eternal salvation was both disclosed to you and particularly applied
to you; “through which” gospel, if this relative is referred to the preceding
noun, or even “through whom” Christ, or “in whom” Christ, if we refer that “in
whom” to Christ, “after you believed”—or, taking the expression as “with
respect to whom,” “when you believed in whom”—“you were sealed,” that is,
confirmed in the faith, “with that Holy Spirit of promise,” namely, the one
who applies to you through faith the gracious promise set before you in the
gospel and seals its truth and certainty in your hearts. This Spirit is the
earnest or pledge of your inheritance, that is, an earnest by which you may
now be made more certain and fully persuaded concerning the full possession
into which you are finally to be brought at the appointed time.

Here we see that in the relative pronoun “who,” which plainly refers to the
noun “Spirit,” account is taken not of the grammatical gender of the word but
of the sense, that is, of the thing signified, since the Holy Spirit is a
person, or even because “the Spirit is God,” as Photius explains the reason.
We have a similar construction in John 16: “When he comes, the Spirit of
truth,” and so forth. Unless we prefer to derive the reason for the change in
gender from the following word “earnest,” which is masculine in Greek. This
accords with Sallust’s expression, “there is a place in the prison which is
called the Tullianum”; Terence’s, “poverty never seemed so heavy a burden to
me”; and finally Cicero’s, “joined dwellings which we call cities.” But the
former reason is more pleasing. “Unto the redemption of the possession”
means, word for word, unto the assertion or vindication of redemption, if we
consider the sense, when we are vindicated or set free into full liberty,
which will take place only on that Last Day. And this is “to the praise of his
glory,” about which enough was said at length above. This, then, is the sense
of the words.

[Marginalia: Recapitulation. Exegesis of the following verse. John 16:13.]

In order that we may grasp the mind of our Apostle in these words more fully
and distinctly—just as we already understand the general aim, which is to
apply the same spiritual and saving benefits, especially that effectual
calling into fellowship in the heavenly inheritance, to the believing
Ephesians as to the Jews—two principal matters in these two verses must be
considered. The first is how the Apostle not only describes this calling of
the Gentiles here but also confirms and demonstrates it with arguments. The
second is the description of the Holy Spirit, whom he had mentioned
immediately before when he called him the Spirit of promise.

[Marginalia: Division.]

As to the first principal matter, which is contained in the earlier verse, we
will again distinguish in it these three members set out in order. First comes
the Ephesians’ calling itself to that same grace of Christ and communion with
him in these words: “in whom,” namely, Christ, “you also,” namely, the
believing Ephesians, either “hoped” or rather, as I observed above, “were
admitted into the inheritance.” Besides the reasons already given, the word
“those who first hoped” cannot be applied to the Gentiles unless it abandons
its prefix “before,” and I do not sufficiently see how that can conveniently
be done. Second comes a description of the instrumental cause by which they
were called to Christ in these words, “having heard the word of truth, that
is, the gospel of your salvation.” These words also contain the first argument
for their calling or allotment, drawn from hearing the word. Third, another
argument for their effectual calling is drawn from the effect of the Holy
Spirit working in their hearts, in the following words: “through which, after
you believed,” or “in whom, when you believed, you were sealed with that Holy
Spirit of promise.” These are the three members to be examined in the earlier
verse.

[Marginalia: Subdivision: 1, 2, 3.]

There is no need for us to linger here over the first of these or to repeat
anything at present. For since the same verb which was used above—“to be
allotted,” that is, to be received or admitted into the inheritance—must
necessarily be repeated here too and applied to believers from among the
Gentiles just as it was above to the Jews, who does not see that whatever was
said there about that gracious calling of the Jews to Christ’s grace and
salvation must also be understood here of the Gentiles? The heavenly Father
willed the Jews to have no prerogative over them except that priority of time,
and this on account of his covenant formerly made with their ancestors, the
fathers and patriarchs, as we taught more fully in its proper place. In all
other matters respecting both salvation and the inheritance itself, as well
as the means and manner of obtaining it, he willed the two groups to be
entirely equal and fellow heirs. For the same Christ, the same kingdom of
heaven, and, as the Lord himself speaks in Matthew 8, the same table in the
kingdom of heaven, at which those who come from east and west will recline
with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; the same faith, the same hope, the same
Spirit, the same gospel, the same seals, the same body, the same head, and the
same communion of saints belong to all, Gentiles and Jews alike.

[Marginalia: Disposal of the first member. Matthew 8:11.]

We note only in passing that the failure to notice that this verb is to be
repeated from the common construction gave both Jerome and Ambrose, as they
expounded this passage, an occasion for error. It gave one of them occasion to
omit the expression “in whom” which follows, since he thought it wholly
redundant. It gave the other occasion to excuse the Apostle at this point for
mentioning Christ very often, superfluously and outside his proper order and
place, because he had been carried away by an excessive—that is, vehement—love
of Christ. We would indeed have to admit that this had happened here if it
were not evident that the Apostle had another intention, namely, to repeat
the same verb used above. But because Jerome did not observe this, he labors
here in vain to excuse the Apostle, when it is rather he himself who needs an
excuse in this respect. Yet in saying this I do not wish to detract anything
from their praises and services to the Christian Church. I have made this
observation only—

[Marginalia: A note in passing.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 90 | Caput I p. 48]

—so that no one, attributing more than is right to those men’s authority in
expounding sacred Scripture, should cling to their footsteps in such a way as
to think it forbidden to depart from them even a fingernail’s breadth.

Let us now proceed to the second member, the instrumental cause by which they
were called to Christ: “having heard the word,” he says, and so forth. Here we
see first that hope, together with faith, arises from hearing the gospel and
follows faith in the order of nature. Next, what is meant in this passage is
not the bare and simple hearing of the body, which can be common even to the
reprobate and unbelieving, but also that of the mind and the inner person,
such as the Lord requires of us in Matthew 13 and very often elsewhere: “He
who has ears to hear, let him hear.” He means the ears of the mind, by which
what is heard outwardly is grasped, understood, approved, and admitted in the
soul. This does not occur except by the efficacy of the Holy Spirit, who
inwardly illuminates the hearers’ minds and opens their hearts, because the
natural person does not perceive or discern by himself the things of the
Spirit of God, even when they have been heard and pressed on him a thousand
times. Hence the Lord is said to have opened the Apostles’ own minds so that
they might understand the Scriptures. How far removed by their own nature
they were from the understanding and true sense of those Scriptures is
sufficiently evident from the gospel history. In it, even though the mystery
of the suffering, death, resurrection, and glorious exaltation of the Lord
Jesus Christ had been confirmed and demonstrated by testimonies of Scripture,
they were scarcely—indeed, hardly at all—able to grasp it with the mind and
receive it by faith. This remained so until Christ himself, by his Spirit, had
opened their ears, fashioned their souls as though anew, and formed them
within by his hidden power, so as to make them capable of these things and fit
to understand them fruitfully.

[Marginalia: Member 2. Matthew 13. Revelation 1:2–3.]

We see these three means of faith awakened in us, and therefore of our
effectual calling—namely, the preaching of the gospel, its external hearing,
and the inward opening of the heart which takes place by the power of the Holy
Spirit—plainly joined by the Evangelist in Acts 16, in the conversion of Lydia
the seller of purple through the preaching of the Apostle Paul. For there we
see that while Paul preached and she listened, the Lord opened her heart so
that she attended to the things spoken by Paul, that is, so that she grasped,
understood, believed, and embraced them. From this, then, it is easy to
construct the first argument for the calling of the Ephesians in this way:
Those who heard the word of truth—that is, who so embraced it when it was
preached to them that it became for them the gospel of salvation and an aroma
of life unto life—were certainly allotted and admitted into the same
inheritance of the children together with us Jews. But you, Ephesians, heard
the word of truth, and so forth, in this way. Therefore, and so forth.

[Marginalia: Acts 16:14. Argument.]

But now let us see how he describes and expresses this gospel. First, he calls
it “the word of truth,” and does so not only by a customary Hebraism for a word
which is truly veracious—indeed, of the highest truth and certainty—because
it proceeded from him who is truth itself and can neither be deceived nor
deceive. From this a tacit argument is elicited for why the Ephesians and
anyone else ought to believe that word: namely, because it is supremely and
most certainly true. But, as is very convincing to me, this phrase also
denotes a certain preeminence, signifying that the gospel contains, above
every other word, even every other true and divine word, such as the law
itself, a certain excellent truth in which alone human salvation and eternal
life consist. For “this is eternal life,” says the Lord himself in John 17,
“that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent.”
Thus later in the same chapter he calls it God’s own truth: “Sanctify them,”
he says, “by your truth; that word of yours is truth,” namely, the word which
the Lord himself, while living among them up to that time, had announced to
them from the Father. And so elsewhere too he commonly calls this same word
simply and absolutely “the truth,” to denote that same preeminence, as, for
example, in John 8: “If you remain in my word, you will truly be my disciples,
and you will know the truth, and the truth will set you free.” Likewise in
John 18: “For this I was born, and for this I came into the world, that I
might bear witness to the truth. Everyone who is of the truth hears my voice.”

[Marginalia: The word of truth. John 17:3, 17. John 8:32. John 18:37.]

Furthermore, this truth is nothing other than that eternal, firm, and constant
purpose of God by which he determined to save not only Jews but also Gentiles,
not through works of the law but through his crucified Son Jesus Christ, and
to enrich them with every spiritual blessing. Although this appears the
height of foolishness in human eyes, before God it is the very highest truth
and supreme wisdom, the only saving and life-giving truth, and therefore the
only one worthy of being called “the truth” preeminently. The word which
manifests it to us—namely, the gospel alone—deserves to be called the word of
truth. So too the Holy Spirit, who illuminates our minds in its knowledge and
seals it in our hearts, is for the same reason called the Spirit of truth,
whom the world did not know and cannot receive. It is therefore no wonder if
the world also rejects the word of truth itself. This seems to be the true
reason for the name. For it seems foreign to the Apostle’s mind and purpose in
this place to oppose truth to the law as containing the shadows and types of
future things whose truth and fulfillment the gospel presents, although this
contrast is true in itself. That antithesis may be observed in John 1, which
affirms that the law was given through Moses but that grace and truth were
provided through Jesus Christ.

[Marginalia: The sum of the truth. Another reason rejected. John 1:17.]

In the second place, by way of explanation, he calls this same doctrine “the
gospel of our salvation,” and certainly for the best reasons. First, because
it is that joyful and favorable announcement of salvation and eternal life,
of which by nature we could never even have dreamed. Second, because it also
discloses the manner of obtaining that salvation and opens the way of
reaching it, namely, Jesus Christ the Mediator, who came into the world to
deliver us from sin, Satan, the coming wrath, and eternal death and to endow
us with righteousness, forgiveness of sins, the grace of adoption, and
eternal life. It does so by setting him before us with the merit of his death
and obedience, and thus by showing us both the end and the means which lead
there, both the destination and the way. Therefore, because of the subject
matter and argument laid beneath it, it is rightly called the gospel of
salvation, since everything handed down in the gospel is directed, as to its
end, toward obtaining that eternal salvation in and through Christ. Third, it
is so called not only because it announces and points out all these things to
us, but because it is the power of God unto salvation, that is, the effectual
instrument of the Holy Spirit which God uses to dispense salvation. For while
all these things are set before us and explained in the gospel, he works faith
and repentance in our hearts through that word. In both respects this word
differs from the teaching of the law: first in the subject matter laid beneath
it, because the law does not—

[Marginalia: The gospel of our salvation: 1, 2, 3.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 91 | Caput I p. 49]

—disclose to us the manner of deliverance or show us the way and exit out of
that native misery; and also in the efficacy of the Holy Spirit, which is not
joined with the ministry of the law as it is with the word of the gospel. Nor
should it be passed over that the Apostle calls it not simply “the gospel of
salvation” but “of your salvation,” thus applying that salvation to the
Gentiles just as to the Jews, and doing so by the benefit not of the law but
of the gospel. When the Ephesians believed that gospel, even though they had
not performed works of the law, he indicates that by this very fact they had
already obtained salvation no less than the believing Jews. But because more
was said above about the excellence of the gospel in verses 8 and 9, let us
now proceed to the third member. It contains that other argument for the
effectual calling of the believing Ephesians, drawn from that sealing by the
Holy Spirit of promise, in this form: Those who have been sealed with the Holy
Spirit of promise have certainly been effectually called and admitted into
the same inheritance with us. But you, Ephesians, when you believed the
gospel, were sealed through that very gospel with the Holy Spirit of promise.
Therefore, and so forth.

[Marginalia: Your. Ephesians 1:8–9. Transition to Member 3.]

Here, as we noted in passing, the expression “in whom” can be expounded in
three ways. First, it may be referred to Christ, and this in two ways. It can
mean “in Christ,” that is, through Christ, because the Father seals us through
Christ the Mediator, with the Holy Spirit as the immediate agent. In this way
it will mean the same as “through whom.” Or “in whom” may stand for “into
whom,” as these prepositions are very often interchanged in our Apostle, so
that it means, “in which Christ, when you believed.” In this case, as is
evident, the expression is referred not to the verb “you were sealed” but to
the nearest participle, “having believed.” Finally, it can be referred to the
gospel, which was mentioned immediately before, with “in which” used in place
of “through which,” so that it means: through which gospel, as an instrument,
you were sealed with the Spirit of promise. This is how Beza translates it.
Let us therefore retain that sense, although the others agree no less well
with the Apostle’s mind and words.

Furthermore, because faith in Christ seems to exist at the same time as this
sealing of believers, which is done by the Holy Spirit of promise—for the
Holy Spirit seals the promise in our hearts by the very faith which he gives
us—some therefore think that the participle should be translated “when you
believed,” that is, at the very moment when you believed, rather than “after
you believed.” Others, even while retaining this earlier exposition, as we
see Beza retain it, think that an answer can be given from the different
stages of the Holy Spirit’s operation in the hearts of believers. For a
twofold work can be observed in the production of faith: first, the
illumination of the mind, which is the beginning of faith, or faith begun;
then, that confirmation by which the truth of the promise is applied to us
and certainly founded and established in our hearts, which they observe the
Apostle to denote here by the name of sealing.

[Marginalia: Removal of a difficulty. As Piscator understands it.]

But in truth, however we finally translate this participle, nothing prevents
us from saying that this form of speech—“having believed, you were
sealed”—designates an order not of time but of nature. For it is evident that
most of the Holy Spirit’s operations in our hearts, and the inward application
of those spiritual benefits, such as faith, hope, adoption, love,
justification, sanctification, and so forth, are distinguished not so much by
an interval of time as by a certain natural order of causes and effects,
antecedents and consequents.

[Marginalia: Judgment.]

Yet it can be shown in another way how faith in Christ precedes, as a cause
precedes an effect, the sealing of which the Apostle speaks, if not in time,
at least in the order of nature. This is done by explaining a little more
distinctly the effect of the Holy Spirit which consists in sealing. For by
the name “sealing” we may understand not so much the first act of faith, so to
speak—that is, its first production—as its further confirmation and progress,
by which it grows and increases in our hearts. But by what means, I ask?
Certainly, especially by the gift of the Spirit of sanctification, that is, by
our regeneration or sanctification itself. As a seal impressed upon our
hearts, bearing and expressing in them the very image of God—for holiness or
regeneration is that very image of God produced in us—it confirms and seals
toward us that promise of life and eternal inheritance in Christ which we
have already embraced by faith. The Apostle himself seems to explain himself
more plainly in this way in 2 Corinthians 3, when he says that, beholding the
glory of the Lord with unveiled face as in a mirror, namely, with the eye of
faith, we are transformed into the same image from glory to glory, as by the
Spirit of the Lord. This seems to mean nothing other than that those who
believe the gospel are sealed by the Holy Spirit, with the image of his own
seal—that is, the image of heavenly light, wisdom, righteousness, love,
goodness, truth, and, to state it in one word, holiness—impressed upon us.

[Marginalia: The true exposition. Note the manner of sealing. 2 Corinthians
3:18.]

For just as a seal impresses its own character, which is essential to it
insofar as it is a seal, upon the sealed object, such as wax, so too the Holy
Spirit of God, by his sanctifying power, impresses and leaves in our souls an
image and some likeness of his own most holy nature. That image is greater or
smaller, clearer or more obscure, according as he wills to be more or less
effectual in us. From the impression of the seal upon the wax and the
production of its character, the instrument itself—whether a contract,
privilege, gift, or some other thing of this sort, to which that seal has been
attached, that is, the wax impressed and sealed with its character—is
confirmed, so to speak ratified, secured, made authentic, and made an
instrument of certain and undoubted credibility. In precisely the same way,
through this impression of holiness by the Holy Spirit, who regenerates and
illuminates us, endows us with righteousness, love, goodness, and holiness,
and makes us in some measure like himself, the very promise of adoption,
already inscribed in our hearts by faith, is confirmed, sealed, ratified, and
made authentic in them. Thus in Acts 15 Peter also proves the effectual
calling of Gentiles as well as Jews from sanctification, asserting that God
“made no distinction between us and them, purifying their hearts by faith.”
It is as if he said that God showed by this very fact that he had called the
Gentiles just as much as the Jews, because he had purified and sanctified
their hearts just as he had ours through faith in Jesus Christ, which that
sanctification follows.

[Marginalia: Comparison. Explanation of the comparison. Acts 15:9.]

But let us linger over this striking comparison, which the Apostle uses not
only here but often elsewhere in this matter of faith and sanctification. For
example, in 2 Corinthians 1: “Moreover, he who confirms us with you in Christ,
and who anointed us, is God, who also sealed us and placed the earnest of the
Spirit in our hearts.” By those gifts and the earnest, about which he speaks
in the following verse, or by the anointing, as he calls it in the same place,
he indicates that we are confirmed in faith. He also speaks in this way in 1
Corinthians 1: “Just as the testimony of Christ was confirmed—”

[Marginalia: Extended explanation. 2 Corinthians 1:21–22. 1 Corinthians
1:6–7.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 92 | Caput I p. 50]

“—among you, so that you lack no gift while waiting for the Lord Jesus Christ
to be revealed.” Likewise below in chapter 4 of this same epistle: “And do not
grieve that Holy Spirit of God, by whom you were sealed for the day of
redemption.” In order, then, to explain this comparison of sealing more
distinctly, four things relevant to our purpose can be considered in
instruments or legal documents secured by an appended seal in this way. First
is the thing written down, that is, the matter or subject contained in the
document, such as a privilege, gift, contract, or something of this kind.
Second is the ink with which it is written. Third are the tablets, paper,
parchment, writing pads, or some other thing of this kind on which it is
written. Last is the seal by which the thing contained in it is confirmed and
sealed.

[Marginalia: Ephesians 4:30. The comparison.]

It is easy to see what corresponds to these four things in the present
matter. The first is the promise itself of adoption and the eternal
inheritance. The second is the Holy Spirit, or his efficacy, by which that
promise is written as though with ink. The third is the hearts of believers
on which, as on tablets, it is written. Last is the gift of the Spirit,
especially sanctification, by which, as though by an appended seal, the same
promise is confirmed and sealed in our hearts. The Apostle himself plainly
declares and confirms this explanation in the same second epistle, 2
Corinthians 3, when he says, “You are manifestly an epistle of Christ
ministered by us”—that is, an epistle in the writing of which he deigned to
use our labor and ministry, as that of his scribes—“written not with ink but
with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone,” as though he
said, “in the way the Mosaic law was formerly written,” “but on fleshy tablets
of the heart.” The Lord himself had promised to write on them this new and
gracious covenant of his, founded on Christ’s blood: “This is the covenant I
will make with the house of Israel after these days, says the Lord: I will put
my law in their mind and write it on their heart; and I will be their God, and
they will be my people.”

[Marginalia: The application. 2 Corinthians 3:3. Jeremiah 31:33.]

But if it is asked how the Holy Spirit writes Christ’s teaching and the gospel
promises on the hearts of believers, the answer is easy from what has already
been said: namely, through faith. For when he awakens in them the faith by
which they apprehend the promise, this writing and saving inscription, so to
speak, about which we are speaking, is accomplished in them.

[Marginalia: Question. Answer.]

It remains for us now to explain why this same Spirit, by whom we are sealed,
is called by the Apostle “the Spirit of promise.” Some, by a customary
Hebraism, take the Spirit of promise to mean the promised Spirit. Conversely,
in Galatians 3—“so that the blessing of Abraham may come to the Gentiles in
Christ Jesus, and so that we may receive through faith the promise of the
Spirit,” word for word, or, as Beza translates it, “that promised
Spirit”—Paul calls him “the promise of the Spirit.” Thus the sense is in fact
the same whether we say “the promise of the Spirit,” as in that passage, or
“the Spirit of promise,” as here, since it always denotes that the Spirit,
long before promised by the prophets, is given to believers, as may be read
in Joel, cited in Acts 2.

Yet this phrase certainly demonstrates something further: namely, the Holy
Spirit’s office or effect in the hearts of believers. That effect is not to
strike them with the law’s fear and terror but to seal the promise of adoption
with his gifts. For just as he is called the Spirit of faith, the Spirit of
wisdom, the Spirit of sanctification, and so forth, because he produces,
nourishes, and increases faith, wisdom, love, and so forth in believers; and
the Spirit of adoption because he confirms the grace of adoption in us and
makes us certain and fully persuaded of it; so too he will be called the
Spirit of promise from that effect of his by which he confirms and seals in us
the promise of the inheritance and grace. For it belongs to the same Spirit
both to give us faith and to seal in us the promise apprehended by faith. In
this way he is opposed to the spirit of slavery, which, from an evil
conscience of sin and a broken law, brings fear rather than full assurance and
certainty of eternal salvation, adoption, and the grace of forgiveness of
sins promised in the gospel. For in Romans 8 the Apostle mentions the Spirit
in two respects: the spirit of slavery unto fear, and the Spirit of adoption,
who testifies together with our spirit that we are God’s children. In reality
this is one and the same Spirit of God, but effectual in us in different ways.
Through the preaching of the law, when he convicts us of sin and awakens
sleeping consciences, he strikes terror because we see that through sin we
have been reduced to slavery to death and divine wrath, like the disposition
of a slave who fears his master’s anger. But through the preaching of the
gospel, when he awakens in us the faith by which we embrace the promise
offered in it, he produces love and filial confidence in our souls, like that
which a child customarily has toward a parent, from the sense and apprehension
of divine love poured out in our hearts. Hence through him we cry, “Abba,
Father.” It is therefore with respect to this effect and operation of his that
he is called here the Spirit of promise.

[Marginalia: The Spirit of promise. Galatians 3:14. Reason for the phrase.
Romans 8:15. Note carefully.]
