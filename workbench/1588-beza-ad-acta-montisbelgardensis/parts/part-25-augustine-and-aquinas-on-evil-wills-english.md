## Part XXV: Augustine and Aquinas on Evil Wills

[Part II | PDF page 182 | logical source scan 181 | printed page 180 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

Come, then, let us add to these the very weighty testimonies of two writers: Augustine, easily chief among the ancient theologians, who treated this question against the Pelagians in many books and passages; and Aquinas, most learned of all the scholastics. Thus Augustine, writing against Julian, explains in its true sense the very word “permission,” which some later corrupted and which we shall discuss shortly. He says in book 3, chapter 5 of *Against Julian*: “What is this claim of yours that when they are said to be delivered to their desires, we must understand them to be abandoned through divine patience, not driven into sins through divine power? As though the same apostle did not place these two together, patience and power, when he says, ‘But if God, willing to show wrath and demonstrate his power, endured with great patience vessels of wrath perfected for destruction.’ Which of these do you say is found in the text, ‘And if the prophet should err and speak, I the Lord have deceived that prophet’: patience or power? Choose whichever you please, or confess both; yet you see that the false prophet’s sin is both sin and the punishment of sin. Will you say here too that the Lord’s statement, ‘I deceived that prophet,’ must be understood to mean, ‘I abandoned him so that, seduced according to his deserts, he might err’? Take it as you please; yet he was punished for sin in such a way that he sinned by prophesying falsely. But—

—consider what the prophet Micaiah saw: the Lord sitting upon his throne and the whole host standing around him on his right and left. The Lord said, ‘Who will deceive Ahab king of Israel, so that he will go up and fall at Ramoth-Gilead?’ One spoke in one way and another in another. Then a spirit came out before the Lord and said, ‘I will deceive him.’ The Lord said to him, ‘By what means?’ He said, ‘I will go out and be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets.’ And the Lord said, ‘You will deceive him and prevail; go out and do so.’ What will you say to this? Certainly the king himself sinned by believing false prophets. Yet this very thing was also the punishment of sin: God judging and God sending an evil angel, so that we might understand more plainly how the Psalm says that he sent the wrath of his indignation through evil angels. Did he do so by erring, or by judging or acting unjustly or rashly? Far be it! But it was not said to him without cause, ‘Your judgments are like the great deep.’ Nor does the apostle cry without cause, ‘O the depth!’”

Augustine says in the same place: “Who is so foolish that when he hears what is sung in the Psalm, ‘Do not deliver me, Lord, to my sinful desire,’ he says that the man prayed that God would not be patient toward him, if God delivers people so that evils occur only by displaying patient goodness while they occur? What, then, do we mean by saying daily, ‘Lead us not into temptation,’ except that we not be delivered to our lusts? For each person is tempted when dragged away and enticed by his own lust. Or perhaps in this prayer we are not asking God that his goodness not be patient toward us? Then we do not invoke his mercy but rather provoke his anger. Who in his right mind could think this—or what madman could even say it? Therefore God delivers people to dishonorable passions so that they do what is unseemly, but he himself fittingly delivers them; the same acts are both sins, punishments of preceding sins, and grounds for future punishments. Thus he delivered Ahab to the lie of false prophets and Rehoboam to false counsel. The one who knows how to work his just judgments not only in human bodies but in hearts themselves does these things in wondrous and ineffable ways. He does not make wills evil, but uses them as he wills, since he cannot will anything unjustly.”

Aquinas, speaking about blinding in I–II, question 79, article 4, sufficiently declares in the following words what he thought about the whole matter: “All evils which God does”—namely evils of punishment—“or permits to be done”—namely evils of fault—“are ordered to some good: sometimes medicinally to the healing of the sinner, in those whom God permits to fall into sin so that they may be humbled and converted, as Augustine says in *On Nature and Grace*, chapters 22 and 35”—namely the predestined, for whom all things work together for good (Romans 8). “Sometimes, however, they are ordered to the condemnation of the blinded, which God ordains for the glory of his justice; sometimes to the good of another who is advancing, namely in God’s just judgments against others; sometimes for the sake of the whole universe, as when he turns the guilt of tyrants to another’s good.”
