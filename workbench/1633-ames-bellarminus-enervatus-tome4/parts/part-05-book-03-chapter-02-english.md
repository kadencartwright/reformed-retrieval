### Chapter II

#### On Sufficient Grace

1. Bellarmine. *The heretics of our time are compelled entirely to reject the
division of grace into sufficient and efficacious grace, since among them
those who are moved by God necessarily act well, while those who are not moved
cannot act well; and by this reasoning every motion of God is efficacious and
none is sufficient.*

Protestant reply. Everything here is false and slanderous. For I. We do not
reject this division entirely, but we reject it in that Pelagian sense in
which some Jesuits customarily explain it. We deny not the distinction, but
the separation, of grace simply sufficient for conversion from efficacious
grace. II. Among us, not all who are moved by God necessarily act well. For we
know many who are moved both outwardly and inwardly to act well, but who
nevertheless resist those motions. III. Among us, even those who are not
actually moved to this or that good often nevertheless possess the power or
capacity to do the good which they do not do.

2. Bellarmine. *Assistance from God that was truly sufficient was present to
some who nevertheless were not in fact converted.*

Protestant reply. An explanation of sufficient grace was necessary at this
point, but Bellarmine seems deliberately to have avoided it as light hostile
to his errors. “Assistance is called sufficient,” Bellarmine says in chapter
2, “when by it God so calls and awakens a man, and is ready to help him by
directing, protecting, and cooperating, that the man thus awakened and called
can truly will to believe, will to be converted, and will to do some good,
even though in fact he does not will to believe or to be converted or to do
that good.” But here there is nothing but darkness. For 1. The question is not
about willing just any good, nor about just any manner of willing to believe
and to be converted, but about believing and being converted themselves. 2.
“The word *can*,” as Medina observes in part 1 of the second part, question
109, article 10, “is equivocal: sometimes it is taken for what can be done by
some power, whether intrinsic or extrinsic; but sometimes for possessing in
oneself a certain positive power for effecting something.” 3. “Some,” among
whom is Bellarmine, “do not rightly understand that God is ready to give
assistance”—and thus to help—Báñez says in part 1, question 23, article 3,
distinction 3, chapter 9. “For they suppose that God in his eternal providence
and predestination is, as far as his own part is concerned, indifferent and as
if awaiting the determination of the inferior cause for producing the effect
of grace. But this is utterly repugnant to divine perfection. The catholic
sense is that God is understood to be ready according to his will—not indeed
his absolute will, which is always fulfilled, but according to either his
antecedent will or his will of sign.” 4. “In common usage,” the same author
observes, “*to give* is customarily equivocal, so that in one way it is taken
as correlative”

[Printed p. 261 | Google Books PA261 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

“with the verb *to receive*, so that reception is inferred from it. In another
way it is taken for offering something to someone by inviting him to receive
it.” There is a similar equivocation in the word *is present*, which
Bellarmine employs, because grace is present in the same way in which it is
given.

We therefore deny that sufficient assistance from God—by which a man can be
converted either by his own intrinsic power or by determining God's
indifferent grace—is received by those who are never in fact converted.

##### Bellarmine's Arguments

3. Bellarmine. Isaiah 5. *“What more ought I to have done for my vineyard that
I did not do? I expected it to produce grapes, and it produced wild grapes.”
The Lord had given them sufficient cultivation, that is, sufficient grace, by
which they could be truly and properly converted; otherwise he could not have
expected good fruit. Yet they did not have efficacious grace, for if they had
had it, they certainly would have produced good fruit.*

Protestant reply. I. No parable ought to be extended beyond its purpose. The
purpose of this parable, however, is to declare as just the judgments of God
to be pronounced against the Jews. Nothing is brought forward or required to
demonstrate the justice of these judgments except the gravity of their sins,
which is heightened by the accumulation of benefits. II. The prophet does not
speak distinctly about those who had sufficient but not efficacious grace,
but about the whole house of Israel and the men of Judah. Among them various
persons had efficacious grace, and many were hardened and blinded, as is
evident from chapter 6, so that at the present time at least they did not have
grace sufficient for conversion. III. It can no more be proved from the words
that sufficient grace was given to all the Jews than that efficacious grace
was. For those who had sufficient but not efficacious grace could, according
to Bellarmine's theology, have replied that God ought also to have added
efficacious grace so that they might produce good fruit. IV. Expectation in
its proper meaning cannot be attributed to God with respect to a thing that
will not occur, unless God's own foreknowledge is denied. V. “What more?”
ought not to be understood without qualification, but within that kind of
benefit which is under discussion; otherwise something more did remain,
namely, to will and to accomplish. VI. To all this must also be added that in
those words the prophet treats the dispensation of God toward the house of
Israel, about which any Jew could judge, as is evident from verse 3. But no Jew
could judge concerning inward grace, whether it had been sufficiently
communicated to all. Nothing, therefore, is more certain than that this
passage, which to many seems to favor universal grace, is plainly being
twisted against its w-*

*ill into that meaning.

4. Bellarmine. Matthew 23:37. *“How often I wished to gather your children,
and you were unwilling!” How did the Lord will to gather the children of
Jerusalem—and will it so that he complains of the unwilling—if he did not help
those whom he knew could not will by themselves, so that they could will?
Therefore they did not lack sufficient grace.*

Protestant reply. I. This passage concerns all the Jews who had opposed
themselves to Christ and his prophets, among whom there were some held fast by
blasphemy against the Holy Spirit, concerning which Christ himself affirms in
Matthew 12:31 that it will not be forgiven. No one will say that sinners of
this kind were endowed with sufficient grace unless, with Pelagius, he calls
nature grace. Bellarmine, in *On the Grace of the First Man*, chapter 6,
distinguishes between grace and nature in this way: “Through the gravest sins
the supernatural gift of grace is lost, but no natural gift is corrupted.” If
there is any sin, or any state of sin, that excludes grace, many Jews were
those who did not have sufficient grace. II. As for Christ's words, they are
rightly explained by Cajetan on this passage: “How often I willed? With the
will of sign, by commanding, warning, and visiting through the prophets, and
finally through myself.” That this is the genuine meaning is evident, first,
from those words, “you who kill the prophets and stone those sent to you”; and
second, from the numerical adverb “how often,” which is joined to the act of
willing: “How often I willed?” For God's inward and absolute will does not
have alternating periods of acting and ceasing, but the preaching of the word
does. Third, the Lord rightly complains about those who are unwilling, even
though they lack grace, because they lack it by their own fault: they
willingly remain in that state and do not even aspire to a better state so far
as they are able.

5. Bellarmine. Acts 7:51. *“You always resist the Holy Spirit.” Good desires
were therefore inspired in them so that they could be converted; for otherwise
how were they resisting the Holy Spirit, and why are they rebuked so sharply?*

Protestant reply. I. By reasoning of this kind some sophist could prove that
the devil himself has sufficient grace, because he too, in his own manner,
always resists the Holy Spirit, Zechariah 13:1. And certainly Stephen speaks
there about plainly diabolical men who were “stiff-necked and uncircumcised in
heart and ears.” II. The manner of resistance understood there is sufficiently
explained by Stephen himself in verses 52–53: “They persecuted those who
foretold Christ; they killed Christ; they did not keep the law they had
received.” They were resisting the Holy Spirit speaking in the law and the
prophets.

6. Bellarmine. Romans 2:4. *“Do you despise the riches of his kindness and
forbearance, and of wrath restrained, not knowing that God's kindness leads
you to repen-*

[Printed p. 263 | Google Books PA263 | GB 1AfNlTSMfpEC]

*tance?” Why would God await repentance from those to whom he had not given
sufficient assistance for conversion? Does he perhaps not know that such
people cannot be converted?*

Protestant reply. I. The word *awaiting*, upon which the objection rests, is
not in the text. II. A reason can be given just as easily why God would await
the repentance of those who cannot repent as of those whom he knows most
certainly will never will to repent. Does he perhaps not know—so that we may
turn Bellarmine's irony back upon himself—whether they are going to will it at
some time or not? III. The apostle indicates only the nature of the thing and
the purpose of the work; that is, that delay of vengeance by its nature
provokes sinners to repentance.

7. Bellarmine. Revelation 3:20. *“I stand at the door and knock.” If those at
whose heart the Lord knocks do not have sufficient assistance to open, why
does the Lord knock? Does he not know that they cannot open?*

Protestant reply. I. God's foreknowledge can be attacked with a similar
sophism: if God knew that these or those persons would never open the door,
why does he knock? He knocks at the heart of the elect so that by knocking he
causes them both to be able to open and to open. He knocks outwardly at the
heart of the reprobate so that by that dispensation, which is common, he may
work in the elect; but sometimes he also knocks inwardly so that the reprobate
may understand the benefit offered, the duty owed, and the gravity of their
sin.

8. Bellarmine. *If no grace is given which is truly sufficient, the first man
would not have fallen by his own fault.*

Protestant reply. The question is solely about grace sufficient for the
conversion of a sinful man; this objection therefore has no bearing on the
matter.

9. Bellarmine. *If no grace is given which is truly sufficient, God will seem
to desert his friends before he is deserted by them.*

Protestant reply. Although no sinner has grace which is sufficient but not
efficacious for conversion—which is the only question under discussion—this
nevertheless can in no way even seem to follow, because unregenerate sinners
are not God's friends.

10. Bellarmine. *If grace of this kind is not given, then no one will be able
truly to say that he did not do as much good as he could; nor will anyone
justly be able to rebuke him for not having cooperated with God's grace as
much as he could and should have.*

Protestant reply. It does not follow, because believers have grace by which
they are able to do more spiritual good than they do; and even unbelievers
themselves are able, in their own manner, to do more good, or less evil, than
they do.
