Vers. 18. Chastise your son while there is hope; and let it not spare him for being put to death (or, take away( [your soul.] [The Latin prints *ad morte* and unmatched parentheses.]

In בנך (*son*) there is a synecdoche for children. Likewise in נפשך (*your soul*.)

The purpose is to rebuke the foolish indulgence of parents.

It is asked in this place, first, why God commands universally that children be rebuked and chastised, when it seemed rather that it should have been said that those who transgress and depart from duty are to be chastised

[1632 witness | PDF p. 413 | printed columns 800-801]

and rebuked. Reply: it cannot be denied that there is a great difference of dispositions among boys and girls, and that most are much more tractable and of a gentler nature than are others, who by their nature are more obstinate and refractory. And therefore parents and teachers who apply the same rule of teaching and correction equally to all are to be blamed. For here the highest equality is inequality; and many, while they think they are scourging vices, are found to be scourgers and executioners of nature. Yet amid this variety of nature and dispositions, it is certain that none are found who, on account of the depravity implanted in their minds and fixed in their nature, do not need chastisement. See Prov. 22.15. For just as no field is so fertile that thorns and thistles—at least some—do not spring up in it, and for that very reason it needs weeding, so the disposition of no boy or girl is so easy and subdued that it does not call for chastisement and the rod.

Then he teaches that this chastisement is to be applied in season and opportunely, that is, early, before the depravity of his mind acquires strength and force through habit and long use, and before, having become more grown, he displays himself as untamed with a certain ferocity of wantonness. For just as a certain time for pruning and cutting back luxuriant shoots must be observed, neglect of which causes trees that have been cut and pruned to perish; and just as a plant, when it is tender, is easily corrected from its crookedness, but, if it has become more grown, is broken more quickly than it is bent to straightness; finally, just as a bullock is docile in bearing the yoke but, after becoming an ox, refuses to go under the yoke: so, unless children are corrected from tender years so that they may be recalled to good conduct, they thereafter become refractory and breakers-out, who will not allow themselves to be restrained and forced into order. Hence it is that Prov. 13.24 not only teaches a parent that the rod is not to be withheld, but further commands him to apply his hand.

Afterward he proves this precept from the danger attached to it. Here the Prophet applies a certain anticipation, whose objection is introduced in the person of parents: Yet because of my mind’s indulgence and my singular love toward my children, I cannot chastise them, especially when they, as suppliants before me and with knees bent, beg off the punishment with the sweetest words. Above all, whose mind would not melt or be broken by his tears or wailing? The answer is supplied by God: but in very truth, unless one has done this, it is utterly over with your son or daughter. For by this indulgence of yours one proceeds straight toward that child’s destruction, either in this life, or in the future, or in both, as we see has often happened. The choice is therefore given you whether you prefer your son to experience your wrath or that of the Magistrate, or God’s fury; your chastisement or destruction by the Magistrate, or by God the supreme judge and avenger, or by both; your rod or their sword and axe. Examples are Absh. and Adoniah, each of whom, corrupted in childhood by the excessive indulgence of their father, perished.

Let this be transferred to Magistrates, who, by establishing impunity, cast men into the certain danger of divine wrath.

V. 19. If one is great in heat, remitting [to him] punishment: if you free him, thereafter you will increase [the punishment].

The purpose is to teach that parents can sometimes remit punishment to children who transgress, but not without indignation declared both by countenance and by words.

In this Proverb an anticipation seems to be applied to the preceding argument, whose objection is: Is no place for pardon left, then, when a fault has been committed? The answer: On the contrary, there is a case in which you should pardon a fault, as on the very first occasion on which someone has transgressed, or if through imprudence,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 414 | printed columns 802-803]

or through the enticements of others rather than deliberate malice, something against duty is committed.

But not without a precaution. The precaution is that he should profess his anger; then that he should employ νουθεσίαν (*admonition*), as the Apostle teaches, Eph. 6.4. Just as this must never be passed over, either in chastisement or in remission of a fault, so it must be applied more copiously and abundantly when the chastisement that takes place by beatings is omitted.

Here, if someone should ask whether it is not permissible sometimes to overlook the offences of children and pass over them as though they had not been noticed—especially when, through continual chastisements, whether by words or beatings, a child develops a callus and is in some measure blunted—the reply is that this is not only permissible but also expedient. Yet this overlooking must especially be applied when nothing is committed against God or against one’s neighbour, but only when offence is given against a certain civil discipline in matters of table, dress, or the combing of hair, and against other rules not dissimilar to domestic εὐνομίᾳ (*good order*).

V. 20. Hearken to counsel, and receive correction, so that you may be wise in your latter end.

The purpose is that true wisdom is begotten and acquired in obedience to commands and in equitable and patient endurance of correction.

Accordingly, there are two things which the Holy Spirit sets forth in this Proverb: 1. an exhortation; 2. a promise made to those who obey the exhortation.

In the exhortation there are two things, that they should

├─ hearken to counsel,
└─ admit correction.

In the former, hearing is included not obscurely in hearkening to counsel. The word of God must therefore be heard, and indeed diligently, with the ear thoroughly applied and inclined to the thing we hear. For from hearing the law comes knowledge, and from knowledge comes consciousness of sin, just as faith is begotten from hearing the promise. Let us therefore remember what Christ says: *He who has an ear, let him hear*, Matt. 13. By this sentence he wishes to teach that the workmanship of the ear was made and formed by God for this reason: that it should serve the hearing of God’s word. But by us it is applied to catching and receiving all things rather than the word of God. And to such a degree do we fill our ears with hearing things that will perish that no place is left for hearing the word of God. Hence it is that we bring to hearing the word ears that are heavy and uncircumcised; in this class are those who are represented by the seed cast onto the paved way, Matt. 13.

But it is not enough if we hear the word unless we further hearken to it, that is, unless we lay it up in the innermost chambers of the mind like a treasure, or hide it like precious seed in the ridge and furrow of our mind, lest the devil, like a bird, carry it away. See Ps. 119, section 2, v. 3, where the use and end is declared, namely, that he may not sin against God. So Isa. 2.3: *He will teach us concerning his ways, and we will walk in them.* Hence those who hear the word and keep it are pronounced blessed, Luke 11.28; and James 1.22: *Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, imposing upon yourselves.*

Then the word *counsel*, by which the word of God is distinguished, must be noted. There are many terms in Scripture, especially Ps. 119, by which the various and differing effects of the divine word are delineated: it is called *ways*, so that we may be taught that one must persist in them; *testimony*, so that we may apply it either to prove or to refute our causes; and *judgements*, to declare that our disputes must be ended and decided by the word. But in this

[1632 witness | PDF p. 415 | printed columns 804-805]

place it is called *counsel*, so that, as often as we are doubtful and hesitant about what way we should enter in transacting our affairs, we may have recourse to God’s word, which is the one oracle. Hence it is that Christ is said to have been given to us by God as Counsellor, Isa. 9; and in Ps. 119 the Scriptures are called the men of David’s counsel and, as it were, a more sacred senate.

Here God’s munificence toward his own must be proclaimed: he grants to all the faithful not only an escort from the Angels, but also a holy senate, so that they may be recognised as Kings not only in title, but as equipped with royal privileges.

But we must diligently guard against the Pontifical error: we must not devise that God’s counsels are optional and that we are bound by no necessity to obey them, and for that reason that those who fulfil them in deed not only merit from God, but further obtain for themselves so great a treasury of merits that they may disburse from it to other needy persons. But they give too little thought to the one from whom this counsel proceeds, namely, God, whose counsel it is impious to disobey. For if that saying is true, εἰ τῶν κρατούντων δέησις ἀναγκαία (*if the entreaty of those who rule is necessary*), much more may this be truly affirmed concerning the counsels of God and Christ. And if it would be proud to reject the counsel of Solomon, the wisest man—indeed, it was dangerous for his son Rehob. not to acquiesce in the counsel of those who were counsellors to his father Solomon—much more is it full of loss to reject God’s counsel. And, that I may go no further, since no true and solid wisdom is acquired except by hearkening to divine counsel, it is plain that this counsel is not optional, unless perhaps we think it is free for us to be fools and unwise.

The second part of the exhortation concerns admitting correction. Here someone may ask what need there is for chastisement when a person not only hears the word attentively but further observes it diligently. What need, you will say, is there to add a spur to one who is running?

Reply: no one’s obedience is so complete in all its parts and proportions that he does not transgress in many things; and for that reason there is need of chastisement. For if, in order to form a certain civil discipline, there is need even in the noblest dispositions that correction sometimes be sprinkled upon the delivery of precepts, how much more do we need chastisement in order to imbibe heavenly discipline, from which we are by our nature much more alien than from petty civil precepts, the seeds of which are born with us?

But this correction proceeds from God either immediately or mediately: immediately, through diseases, shipwrecks, fires, and so forth; mediately, when domestic correction is inflicted by parents, or public correction by the Church or Commonwealth. This latter must be borne calmly even when it is inflicted without cause, because, however unjust men may be, yet God is just, who most justly governs even the actions of unjust men—provided that under the name of punishment something is not inflicted which departs from the duty of a good man. By contrast, we see young people take particular delight in those who flatter them and are ministers of their pleasures.

There follows the promise joined to the exhortation, that he will become wise. The truth of this is proved from Ps.119.s.מ., where David teaches that he became wiser than his enemies, his instructors, and finally old men because he devoted his labour to God’s precepts.

But it must be noted that he says the man will be wise in the end. By this he testifies that this wisdom is not acquired in a short time, but only if someone is an assiduous and constant hearer in the school of Christ. There are many who at first devote strenuous labour to the Gospel and to acquiring this wisdom, and who likewise undergo the discipline of the Church and admit its yoke; yet, because they do not immediately carry away the fruit which they desire and expect, they withdraw from this school of Christ. It ought to be sufficient for them if they

[1632 witness | PDF p. 416 | printed columns 806-807]

are wise at last. How much time do they spend in learning a low and illiberal art? How many spells of heat and cold do they endure? And yet they think scarcely one or two years rightly spent on learning the art of arts. This rebukes those who, after the foundations of the Christian religion have been laid and its first elements grasped, are little concerned about erecting the rest of the building.
