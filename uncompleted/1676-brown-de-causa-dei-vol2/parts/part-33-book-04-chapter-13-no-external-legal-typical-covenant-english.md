# CHAPTER XIII

## The covenant was not merely external, legal, or typical

Some say that, besides the covenant of grace, God made another covenant with
the children of Israel which was entirely *external* and *figurative*. It
consisted of very many outward commandments and of external and carnal
things, such as the tabernacle, sanctuary, and temple, all of them earthly;
priests and high priests according to the flesh, who descended from Levi and
Aaron; and external, carnal offerings, and so forth. Its promises likewise
were external and carnal: namely, promises of earthly goods, longer life, the
possession of the land of Canaan, and things of that kind.

But to avoid every dispute about a word, we offer the following points for
consideration.

1. Although the covenant of grace has always been one and the same in itself,
with respect to

[Printed p. 128 | IA PDF 136 | IA leaf 136 | IA decausadei02brow]

its essence or substance, it has nevertheless varied with respect to certain
incidental features—namely, its various modes of administration. For God saw
fit not always to reveal and explain this covenant of grace in one and the
same way or with the same degree of clarity. It was explained more obscurely
to Adam, somewhat more clearly to Noah, and still more plainly to Abraham.
Afterward it was disclosed more distinctly to Moses and the Israelite people.
Christ himself, the mediator and substance of the covenant, was revealed to
David more fully than before. Other things were added after the Babylonian
captivity to illuminate this covenant further. Finally, the administration
of the covenant which obtained under the New Testament was the clearest and
most illustrious of all. These various modes of revelation or administration
do not in any way vary or alter the substance of the covenant.

2. There is a well-known distinction of the covenant of grace, or testament
(it bears either name under different aspects), into the *Old* and *New
Testaments*. This is gathered from Jeremiah 31:31; 2 Corinthians 3:6; and
Hebrews 8:8, or from its division into first and second in Hebrews 8:7, 13 and
9:1, 15, 18. But this divides one subject according to its various incidental
features; it does not divide a genus into distinct species. The difference is
not substantial or essential but purely incidental, as Rivet rightly notes
and proves in *Synopsis of Purer Theology*, disputation 23, sections 14–15.

3. We may therefore distinguish, both in the Old and in the New Testament,
between the covenant of grace itself and its peculiar manner of
administration. The latter is changeable and has changed several times; the
former has remained immutable and unchanged until now. We can no more call
this different administration of the covenant a distinct covenant under the
Old Testament than under the New. Consequently, we should no more say that
God made two distinct covenants with the Israelite people than that he now
makes two covenants with his people under the gospel. The reasoning is the
same in each case, and not even the slightest difference can be imagined in
this respect.

4. This external covenant which they imagine is either specifically distinct
from the covenant of grace or it is not. If it is, it must be either some
covenant of works or that other species of covenant invented by Cameron. It
was not the covenant of works, because that is incompatible with the covenant
of grace, which this position assumes God made with the Israelites (Romans
4:4–5; 11:6). We therefore cannot say that God made both the covenant of grace
and the covenant of works with them, or that he promised eternal life both on
the condition of faith and on the condition of works. Nor was it a
subservient covenant, for the reasons given above. Indeed, such a subservient
covenant cannot coexist with the covenant of grace itself, since it only
prepares the way for the covenant of grace. If instead they say that this
external covenant is not specifically distinct from the covenant of grace,
why is it called a distinct covenant? Let them show how it differs from that
different administration of the covenant of grace. If this external covenant
is nothing other than that peculiar administration of the covenant of grace,
why is it called a distinct covenant? And what reason is there to multiply
entities in this way without any necessity? Must the covenants also vary
because there are various and distinct promises? Then we will have not only
two but several covenants.

5. We grant that many external and carnal things belonged to that dispensation
of the covenant of grace. Yet it does not follow that God made some external
covenant with them, distinct from the covenant of grace, any more than he did

[Printed p. 129 | IA PDF 137 | IA leaf 137 | IA decausadei02brow]

with us under the gospel. We too have certain external things: namely, the
preaching of the word and the sacraments, which are external. We also have
temporal promises (Matthew 6; 19:29; 1 Timothy 4:8; Luke 12:31; Hebrews
13:5–6).

We have now explained the state of this question sufficiently. There is no
need to refute it with many arguments, since what has already been said makes
sufficiently clear how empty and futile it is and how entirely destitute of
sound reasoning and scriptural testimony.

Nevertheless, let us hear how our opponents try to defend this position. See
the letters written in Dutch by Schlyter, one of the disciples of Jean de
Labadie, pages 57 and following.

He says: *Besides the covenant of grace, God made another covenant specifically
with the children of Israel, one that was wholly external and figurative,
consisting of commandments and other external and carnal things.* But how
does he show that this was a covenant specifically distinct from the covenant
of grace, or anything distinct from the administration of the covenant of
grace instituted for that time? He cites Hebrews 7:16: *He was made a priest,
not according to a law whose commandment was carnal, but according to the
power of an indestructible life.* Yet one could more easily draw water from a
stone than form from this an argument proving what has been denied. Who denies
that many things at that time were typical and figurative? We grant that in
that administration very many external ceremonies were instituted which
signified and foreshadowed something spiritual. But nowhere in Scripture do
we read that these constituted a distinct covenant. Here the Apostle speaks
of the ceremonial law, according to which the ancient priesthood was
instituted by external rites and ceremonies that were transient and
perishable. Nevertheless, as Calvin rightly notes on the passage: *The
priesthood was not carnal in such a way that it was not at the same time
spiritual; but the Apostle here considers only what Aaron had in this respect
that differed from Christ.* And on verse 18 he says: *Moreover, when the Jews
wrongly opposed these things—that is, the ceremonies—to Christ, the Apostle
adapted his language to their opinion and likewise distinguished them from
Christ. But as soon as they are separated from Christ, nothing remains to
them except the weakness of which he speaks.* The same point is made in the
Dutch annotations on the passage before us: namely, that the Apostle speaks
here about these ceremonies according to the common opinion of the Jews, who
sought sanctification in these external things when they ought to have used
them as types prefiguring Christ. Moreover, the law whose commandment is here
called carnal is elsewhere called living (Acts 7:34). But Calvin asks on that
passage: *Why does he call the law a living oracle? This commendation seems
hardly consistent with Paul's words, for Paul calls the law the ministry of
death (2 Corinthians 3:7), says that it works wrath (Romans 4:15), and calls
it the strength of sin (1 Corinthians 15:56). If he means a living oracle in
the sense of one that is effectual and cannot be rendered void by human
contempt, there is no contradiction. But if “living” must be understood
actively as “life-giving,” then the law contains life in itself.—Yet Stephen
looked higher in this passage. He does not speak of bare commandments, but
comprehends the whole doctrine of Moses, which includes the gracious promises
and indeed Christ himself, the only life and salvation of humanity. We must
remember the sort of people with whom Stephen had to deal. They were
ill-directed zealots for the law, clinging only to the dead and deadly
letter, and meanwhile raging against Stephen because he sought Christ in the
law, who truly is its living core (2 Corinthians 3:17). Therefore, reproving
their perverse ignorance, he signifies that something greater and more
excellent is hidden in the law

[Printed p. 130 | IA PDF 138 | IA leaf 138 | IA decausadei02brow]

than they had known up to that point. For because they were carnal, they were
content with the external appearance and sought nothing spiritual in it,
although it was a pedagogue leading to Christ* (Galatians 3:24).

He adds: *Under that covenant all the things were external and carnal which
are spiritual under the New Testament, such as the tabernacle, sanctuary,
temple, priests, offerings, and so forth.* Response. We know that the economy
of the covenant under the New Testament is more spiritual than the
dispensation of the covenant under the Old Testament was. But it does not
follow that there were then two specifically distinct covenants. 2. The
things just mentioned did indeed belong to that economy, which can be called
carnal in comparison with the economy of the New Testament. But it does not
follow that they belonged to a distinct covenant, which was therefore called
carnal and external. 3. Indeed, all these and similar things prefigured and
indicated Christ. In them believers, led by the Spirit of God, looked through
the things to Christ, and therefore they belonged to the covenant of grace.
4. Under the New Testament also there are certain external ordinances which
can be called carnal with reference to Christ, to whom they lead believers.

He continues: *Indeed, the entire people of this covenant was carnal. Those
who were spiritual in the meantime belonged to the covenant of grace. Just as
the spiritual covenant of the New Testament was made with Abraham's children
according to the spirit and faith, so that carnal and external covenant was
made with all Abraham's children according to the flesh.* Response. The
question is whether the regenerate and spiritual people who lived under the
Old Testament were included under this carnal covenant. They say that, insofar
as those people were spiritual, they were under the covenant of grace. But
the question is whether they were in any respect under that external covenant.
If this is granted, then they were under a twofold covenant and under two
covenants joined together. They were considered either in the same way or in
different ways. If they were considered *in the same way*, then insofar as
they were spiritual they were also included under this external covenant. If
they were considered *under different aspects*—namely, insofar as they were
Abraham's seed—then insofar as they were believers they had nothing to do
with that external covenant. Consequently, insofar as they were believers,
they were not bound to perform the external commandments of that covenant or
to expect the external goods promised. Yet the Apostle testifies that those
people were deprived of those external goods and of the land of Canaan itself
because of unbelief. See Hebrews 3 and 4. If believers were in no respect
under that external covenant, they were not bound to observe its external and
carnal commandments or faithfully to expect the goods promised. This is
false, as has just been said. 2. It is also false that all Abraham's
descendants according to the flesh, under whatever form this external
covenant might finally assume, were included in it. Only the Israelites were.
Did the children of Keturah and Esau and their descendants have, by virtue of
this covenant, a right to those external goods—to the land of Canaan, the
temple, sanctuary, high priest, and so forth? 3. We have extensively shown
that even unregenerate people under the New Testament are included under the
covenant of grace with respect to its external administration, and we cannot
take that matter up now.

He tries to prove from Deuteronomy 5:2–3 that the covenant made with the
Israelites at Mount Sinai was distinct from the covenant of grace, because
Moses says that God did not make it with the fathers, with whom the covenant
of grace had nevertheless been made. But we have seen at length that the
covenant made at Horeb or Sinai was the covenant of grace, and that God is
said not to have made this covenant with the fathers with respect to its

[Printed p. 131 | IA PDF 139 | IA leaf 139 | IA decausadei02brow]

circumstances, not the substance of the covenant itself. See the Dutch
annotations on the passage. Moreover, if he wishes the covenant made at Sinai
to have been not the covenant of grace but that external covenant, he must
say that the Decalogue does not belong to the covenant of grace but to that
external, carnal covenant. Consequently, he must join the Antinomians in
asserting that under the gospel we are exempt from obligation to the
Decalogue, because that external covenant has now been completely abolished.

From Jeremiah 31:31–34 he gathers various differences between the Old and New
Testaments. But it cannot be gathered from this that these two testaments
differ with respect to substance and not merely with respect to incidental
features.

He infers, first: [*The new covenant is of an altogether different nature and
has a very different essence and different properties. Indeed, it differs as
much from the old covenant as the soul does from the body, the thing signified
from its sign, and a body from its shadow.*] Response. 1. The Old Testament
did indeed have very many external types and shadows. Yet by them the same
mediator who is mediator of the new covenant or testament was prefigured.
These testaments therefore do not differ substantially or essentially but
only incidentally, because the same mediator and testator is in each. He who
was foreshown in types and shadows in the Old is exhibited in the New. The
things proposed more obscurely and concealed by a veil of types and shadows
in the Old are set forth more clearly and distinctly in the New. 2. That
these two testaments have the same nature and essence is apparent also from
the fact that the very same special promises mentioned in the new testament
itself existed in substance under the old testament also, though not in the
same degrees and other circumstances. For in Genesis 17:7 God promised
Abraham that he would be God to him and his seed. That is surely a pregnant
promise and the sum of everything found in the new testament itself. In
Deuteronomy 26:17–18, Moses says: *Today you have obtained Jehovah's pledge
that he will be your God—while Jehovah has obtained your pledge today that
you will be his peculiar people*, and so forth. What greater thing, I ask, is
stipulated, or could be stipulated, under the New Testament? Likewise
Deuteronomy 29:12–13 says: *To enter into the covenant of Jehovah your God,
with his oath, which Jehovah your God makes with you today, to establish you
today as a people for himself, and that he may be your God, just as he told
you and just as he swore to your fathers Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.* Is this
promise not substantially the same as that of Jeremiah 31:32: *I will be
their God, and they will be my people?* Finally, the promise in Jeremiah
31:32—*I will put my law in their mind and inscribe it upon their heart*—does
not differ substantially from Deuteronomy 30:6: *And Jehovah your God will
circumcise your heart and the heart of your seed, that you may love Jehovah
your God with all your heart and all your soul, that you may live.* It is
therefore sufficiently evident that the new and old testaments, considered
substantially, do not differ as soul and body differ. What? Did the fathers
under the law have nothing but signs, shadows, and external things of that
kind? Let the Socinians see where they are then obliged to surrender.

He further infers: *Under the Old Testament external goods were promised—an
abundance of milk and honey and similar things—but under the new, spiritual
goods.* Response. We have already seen that spiritual things were promised
under the Old Testament also: namely, circumcision, sanctification, and
renewal of the heart. Did it not also promise the remission of sins? It is
true, however, that spiritual goods were promised more sparingly under the
Old Testament and temporal goods more frequently, while under the New
Testament spiritual goods are promised more frequently and temporal goods

[Printed p. 132 | IA PDF 140 | IA leaf 140 | IA decausadei02brow]

more rarely. Nevertheless, the New Testament is not without temporal promises,
as we recently saw from Matthew 6:33; 19:29; 1 Timothy 4:8; and Hebrews 13:5–6.
The Old Testament also had spiritual promises. Among other passages, see
Exodus 19:4–6, compared with 1 Peter 2:7, 9–10; Exodus 20:1 and following;
Leviticus 26:3–5 and following; Deuteronomy 4:13; 5:2, 5–6 and following;
29:10–13; and 30:1–2, 12–14, compared with Romans 10:6–10; Deuteronomy 30:6,
compared with Colossians 2:11–12; Ezekiel 11:19–20; 36:26–27, compared with
Hebrews 11 and Galatians 5:22; Acts 3:25–26; and Galatians 3:13–14. Indeed,
the promise of the land of Canaan itself was in a certain respect spiritual
(Deuteronomy 12:9–13), as was that of the temple (Deuteronomy 12:5; Numbers
7:89; 27:21; Exodus 29:45; 25:22; Leviticus 26:3–4 and following; Psalms 76:2;
132:13–14; 134:13–14).

He also cites 2 Corinthians 3:6–10 and following, and from it concludes: [*The
Old Testament, insofar as it was distinguished from the covenant of grace,
was only the bare letter, the letter that kills. Although a spiritual promise
may be glorious, he adds, it is nevertheless a killing letter unless the
life-giving Spirit is present, and no conversion follows from it.*] He adds:
[*The promises of the New Testament, indeed, the whole gospel insofar as it is
gospel and has the Spirit of God mixed with it, is altogether Spirit and
power; in this it differs from the Old Testament.*] Response. All this does
absolutely nothing to prove his point. When the external ordinances, which
are like the rind, are separated from Christ, who is the kernel of them all,
they are dead and in a certain respect deadly. But it does not follow that
those external ordinances, which are the external administration of the
covenant of grace, constitute a new covenant. Under the gospel it is no less
true than it formerly was under the law that the external administration,
torn away from Christ, is a dead letter. The very proclamation of the gospel
and of the most spiritual promises will accomplish nothing and convert no one
unless the Holy Spirit concurs by his power and works effectually in the
hearts of the hearers. And when the administration of the covenant under the
law had the Spirit of God mixed with it, it was spiritual and effectual to
produce spiritual effects: namely, faith, hope, confidence, and so forth. It
is therefore clear that there was no more an external covenant essentially or
substantially distinct from the covenant of grace under the Old Testament
than there is under the New. What, then? Will he say that the Holy Spirit
always concurs with the promises of the New Testament and actually converts
all hearers? Or will he deny that the Spirit, cooperating with the promises
announced under the law, could accomplish anything, or that he sometimes
effectually worked the grace of conversion or confirmation? Since he cannot
affirm this, he wrongly says that the New Testament is distinguished from the
Old as soul from body, and that the whole gospel is spirit while the whole
Old Testament is the killing letter.

He bids us read chapters 8 and 9 of the Epistle to the Hebrews and derive from
them the difference between the Old and New Testaments. But we openly say
that the Old and New Testaments differ. What we deny is that the old economy
of the covenant was a covenant specifically distinct from the covenant of
grace, or that the Old and New Testaments are specifically distinct. We know
that the external economy of the covenant is distinguished from the covenant
itself as the mode of a thing from the thing itself, and we assert that this
distinction obtains under the New as well as under the Old Testament. It is
no less true now under the gospel than it formerly was that an external
administration

[Printed p. 133 | IA PDF 141 | IA leaf 141 | IA decausadei02brow]

separated from the kernel itself contributes nothing to salvation. The
Apostle in Hebrews 8 and 9 does indeed prove that the administrations of the
covenant under the Old and New Testaments differ greatly, but he neither
proves nor undertakes to prove that there is any essential or specific
difference between the Old and New Testaments.

He continues: [*From the very beginnings of the world God had a spiritual
people, who were called by the covenant of grace and included under it. Yet
it seemed good to him to make another, external covenant and to have a carnal
people, to whom he gave carnal commandments and prescribed carnal worship,
adding carnal promises.*] Response. It is agreed that God had a spiritual
people from the very beginnings of the world. It cannot be denied that he
also had people who were externally in the covenant. For in every age the
covenant of grace was proposed to others besides those whom the Lord
determined to bring to salvation through that covenant: namely, people who
accepted the proposed and stipulated conditions and promised to render
obedience, though not sincerely or with the whole heart. If this is what is
meant by an external covenant, therefore, it had a place from the beginning.
2. We saw above that God made no external covenant specifically distinct
from the covenant of grace or different from its external dispensation. 3.
Under the gospel also there is now a carnal people: that is, a people who are
God's people verbally and with respect to their outward profession, or who
are externally in the covenant of grace. Yet it does not follow that God now
makes an external covenant specifically distinct from the covenant of grace.
4. Was it a carnal commandment to love God with the whole heart and all one's
strength and to obey him in everything? We know that the commandments of the
ceremonial law are called carnal, not simply but in comparison with the
spiritual commandments of the gospel. 5. Their worship also was not simply
carnal; otherwise it would not have been accepted by God, and those who
offered him such worship would neither have been pleasing to him nor properly
obeyed him. It can nevertheless be called carnal by comparison. Yet spiritual
worship was also then offered to God in faith, such as prayers and
thanksgivings. 6. We saw above that the covenant of grace under the New
Testament also has carnal promises and that the old administration was not
without spiritual promises.

What else? Who were included under this external covenant? *All who descended
from Abraham according to the flesh, he says, and who therefore had a right
to the external sacraments, which were seals annexed to this external
covenant.* Response. 1. It is far too loosely said that all Abraham's
descendants without any distinction of persons or times were in this
covenant, since I judge that the descendants of Ishmael, Esau, and the sons
of Keturah eventually stood outside the covenant. 2. Those sacraments were
seals of the covenant of grace, not of any covenant distinct from it. 3.
Abraham's descendants had to be circumcised because they had been received
into the covenant of grace. Hence the uncircumcised male who did not circumcise
the flesh of his foreskin is said to have broken God's covenant (Genesis
17:14). Abraham's descendants had to be circumcised not because they descended
from Abraham as their natural father, but rather because Abraham, as the
covenanted person, was their moral root. Hence proselytes who did not descend
from Abraham according to the flesh, once admitted to the covenant of grace,
had as much right to those seals as others. And not a few in Abraham's own
household—those bought with money or born there, although not descended from
him—were also admitted to the covenant and initiated by circumcision.

[Printed p. 134 | IA PDF 142 | IA leaf 142 | IA decausadei02brow]

He says further: [*This external covenant was also altogether typical,
representing something spiritual in all its parts and therefore serving the
covenant of grace with respect to those who could penetrate with the eyes of
faith to the spiritual kernel.*] Response. We know that there were many types
in that economy. But it is a sheer fiction that we must therefore imagine
some special, typical covenant. 2. That dispensation served the covenant of
grace with respect not only to those already converted but also to those who
were to be converted. 3. If in this way it led those endowed with the eye of
faith to the spiritual covenant of grace itself, how can it be called a
covenant specifically and essentially distinct from the covenant of grace?
4. In the sacraments and other parts of the evangelical administration,
believers endowed with the eyes of faith are enabled to penetrate to Christ.
Does it follow that there is now also a distinct covenant, different from the
covenant of grace?

After these things had been written and prepared for the press, I first saw a
little book by Pierre Yvon, the defender and successor of Jean de Labadie,
entitled *The Essence of the Christian Religion Revealed*, published in 1673.
Glancing hastily through some of its chapters, I noticed that he held the same
opinion as Schurman. But with the press moving swiftly, I have no time to call
everything he says on this subject to a more careful and extended examination.
I will nevertheless make a few observations.

1. On pages 20–22 he acknowledges that blessedness and every good are granted
to those who are truly in covenant, under whatever covenant, citing Isaiah
64:4 and 1 Corinthians 2:9. *And what, he says, did God not give to Abraham,
Isaac, and Jacob? What benefits did he not lavish upon all Israel dwelling in
the land of Canaan?* From this it might at least seem probable that this
Israelite covenant was not a purely external and carnal covenant, especially
since he adds on page 22: *We must conclude, therefore, that the goods of the
divine covenants comprehend every good and that their promises truly embrace
within their scope the present life as well as the future and eternal life.*

2. On page 23 he says: [*In the Israelite covenant God promised many external
goods to the whole Jewish nation and afterward faithfully bestowed them in
accordance with his promises. But he only proposed internal goods to it and
showed it a twofold way in which it should walk if it wished to obtain them.
One was the way of righteousness by works, which Adam had trodden most
unhappily and which through Israel's own fault had become even less passable
for Israel than for Adam. The other was the way of righteousness by faith and
love, which it could enter and successfully pursue only through the mediator.*]
Response. I hope he will not say that this twofold way was proposed so that
the Israelites might obtain the proposed goods through both, since those
ways do not agree well with each other. Nor will he say that the way of
righteousness by works was proposed by God with the intention that spiritual
goods be obtained through it, since the Apostle condemns this in them in
Romans 9 and 10. He must therefore admit that the way of righteousness by
faith was proposed, through which they could obtain the promised goods.
Consequently, that covenant was the covenant of grace, not another covenant
specifically and essentially distinct from it, as he later says.

3. On page 27 he says that *the Sabbath was a sacrament of the old and
external covenant*. We refuted this opinion above in Book III, chapter III.
But let him explain to me how the Sabbath, together with the Passover, which
he wishes to be sacraments of this Israelite covenant, could be instituted
before the covenant itself was made, since, by his own admission on page 264,
sacraments are annexed to promises to confirm them solemnly—and if, namely,

[Printed p. 135 | IA PDF 143 | IA leaf 143 | IA decausadei02brow]

that covenant was essentially distinct from the covenant of grace made with
the fathers. Seals are not normally appended before a covenant is made. What
he later says on pages 154–155 about *the Sabbath of the people of God* is the
same as what Jean de Labadie says, which we refuted in Book II, chapter XVIII.

4. Speaking about sacraments or external signs on page 27, he says: *Those
signs were chiefly proper to all those covenants which did not so much exhibit
the truth and substance of the things themselves as present their shadows.*
Response. But the Adamic covenant exhibited the truth of the things themselves
and nevertheless, by his own admission, had its sacraments. What, then, will
he say about the new covenant under the gospel? Does it present us with mere
shadows because it has its sacraments? Or will he say that there are now no
sacraments properly so called, or that they are not to be used? So indeed it
seems, for he adds in the same place: *But in the covenant of grace Christ
himself is the great sacrament, since he alone is the tree of life and
knowledge, the true Passover, the rest of his people, the heavenly manna, and
that living rock from which, when struck, perennial streams flow. In short,
he is the reality of all the types and the fulfillment of all ceremonies.
Therefore the new covenant and testament—which exhibits the simple, pure, and
complete explanation of the covenant of grace—did not institute these sacred
signs as necessary to itself or ever command its covenanted people to observe
them as such.* Let the reader judge whether this man will reveal to us the
essence of the Christian religion, as he claims, when on this point he joins
the Socinians against the Reformed. This is not the occasion to examine these
things, since we are engaged with something else. I will only say that we
should be less surprised that these people reject the Christian Sabbath when
they also repudiate the sacraments of the New Testament. They do so for
reasons which, if they wish to be consistent, require them also to remove all
external worship and every ordinance of Christ. He attaches the following
five reasons to this statement. 1. Because in these last times of the gospel
and grace, God, who is Spirit and truth, at length wills to be worshiped,
served, and adored in pure spirit and simple truth. 2. Because by his advent
Jesus Christ signified that all figures and external symbols were fulfilled
in himself and were nullified and altogether abolished by his presence and,
especially, by his glorious appearing. 3. Because all these sacred signs and
divine symbols represented nothing other than the graces and gifts of the
Holy Spirit and their complete outpouring, which was to occur in later ages.
Therefore, once that outpouring occurred and the communication of his gifts
was clearly displayed, the signs which prefigured and obscurely indicated it
were undoubtedly no longer necessary. 4. By everyone's agreement, sacraments
are shadows and ceremonies dead in themselves. They therefore ought not to
occupy a necessary place in that economy which is all light, all Spirit, all
truth, and which recognizes as necessarily attached to itself nothing except
what is living and given life by the Holy Spirit. 5. Since under the New
Testament God has fulfilled everything he formerly promised, what great need
is there for signs and symbols to make our minds more certain about the truth
of the divine promises? And since true Christians actually possess God
himself, Christ Jesus and his Spirit, and with them grace and glory and
eternal, divine life, how could symbols of these things detain them, and how
could they be necessary to them? These are his reasons. The second and third,
as anyone can see, do nothing to prove the point. The rest breathe Familism
and open a very wide door to the fanaticism of those who trampled underfoot
all Christ's ordinances. I said in my letter to Anna Maria van Schurman that I
suspected this society cherished something of the kind in its bosom,

and I am now confirmed in that suspicion.

5. On page 55 he proposes four kinds of divine covenants and distinguishes
them with respect to their laws. He says: 1. In the Adamic covenant God
enacted the law of faith and of the Spirit, of love and obedience and total
dependence, and declared it sufficiently to humanity. 2. In the covenant of
grace he instituted the same law in a more divine manner and manifested it to
all the covenanted people. 3. In the Israelite covenant he offered only a
representation of that law, delineating it with a coarser brush. 4. In the new
covenant and testament God fully explained it and, after removing all other
laws, it alone held dominion. So he says. According to him, therefore, faith
in Christ the mediator—which in Scripture is called the *law of faith*—was
required in the Adamic covenant. This is certainly a new theology and savors
little of the essence of the Christian religion. But on the next page he
contradicts himself by distinguishing between the *law of works* and the *law
of faith* and saying that the latter belongs to the *covenant of grace* and
the former to the *covenant of nature*. Then who can wonder that he is
unwilling to call the Israelite covenant the covenant of grace, when here he
distinguishes between the covenant of grace itself and the new covenant or
testament and makes them two kinds of covenant? Elsewhere, on page 157, he
asserts that this new covenant is distinguished in some respect from the
universal covenant of grace extended to all the elect, and he seeks to
establish this distinction. Yet he always speaks about it as though it were
an altogether distinct kind of covenant and promises a separate treatment of
it on page 128. Indeed, on page 127 he tries to confirm this distinction by
several arguments, which are not unlike those by which elsewhere he endeavors
to prove an essential distinction between the covenant of grace and the
Israelite covenant. It will therefore not be useless to review them briefly
here. 1. The prophets always proclaim it as *future*, whereas the universal
covenant of grace permeated all times. 2. The Apostle applies the
establishment of this new covenant to the times of the gospel. 3. It was
extended only to certain elect people, and only Christians properly so called,
distinguished from the ancient believers by a different manner of serving
God, were made its participants. 4. Singular benefits were communicated by
God to these true Christians which did not belong to ancient believers,
especially in their fullness. 5. A peculiar holiness is required from the
members of the New Covenant under the gospel, along with many other things
which other believers were not bound to render to God as they were. I will
say only this about all these points: if these and many similar arguments
cannot prove that the New Testament is essentially distinct from the covenant
of grace, the same or similar arguments will never prove that the Israelite
covenant is essentially distinct from the covenant of grace or from the New
Testament. I leave everyone to judge how accurate a *revelation of the essence
of the Christian religion*, or what sound explanation of it, is to be
expected from someone who mints such distinctions for us.

6. He says further on page 55: *In the Israelite covenant no inward ability
to fulfill that law was granted.* Response. Is that so? Then no one obtained
salvation under that economy. What, will he invent some limbo of the fathers
for us? Did the righteous then live by faith (Hebrews 2:1; 10:38)? What does
he say of David, Solomon, the patriarchs, prophets, judges, and the other
heroes mentioned in Hebrews 11 and elsewhere? Perhaps he will say that they
and others like them shared grace not by virtue of that covenant but by
virtue of the covenant of grace. But that distinction has not yet been
established. It will never be proved from Scripture that the Jews were under
a twofold covenant and possessed inward grace by virtue of the one but not
the other. Bellarmine indeed speaks in this way in *On the Effect of the
Sacraments*, book 2, chapter 16. It is true that the external economy of that
covenant, separated from the promised Messiah received by faith, supplied
nothing inward. The same can truly be said about the gospel when through
gross error it is separated and distinguished from Christ. Would it therefore
be sufficiently sound to say that no inward grace to fulfill God's law is
communicated under the gospel?

7. He says on page 71: *In the Israelite covenant that entire nation was
required not to worship foreign gods, make sacred images for itself, or adore
them; to celebrate feasts, offer sacrifices, observe the Sabbath, not oppress
its neighbor, and not outwardly fulfill its gross desires. Otherwise God
immediately cried out that his covenant had been broken*, and so forth. What,
did this covenant demand nothing besides these things? According to him, it
did not. Anyone who has given this little book a hurried glance can easily
perceive how little he agrees with himself on this point. For when speaking
indiscriminately about all covenants, he explains the duty of covenanted people
very differently in chapter 6: namely, that it is *what a person joined to
God by covenant is bound to render him, both because he is the true God and
because he is humanity's Creator, Preserver, Beginning, End, and supreme Lord,
and finally because, besides all these things, he willed to become humanity's
covenanted God.* He has many other things relating to this in chapter 6 which
I have no time to transcribe in haste. But you will ask whether he says that
nothing else beyond the things above was required from the Israelites. *As
for the other things, he says, they did not flow from that external covenant,
which conferred external goods, but either from the covenant of nature—or
from the simple and external proposal of the covenant of grace.* Yet by
reason of that covenant were the Israelites God's covenanted people? And was
God not their covenanted God? If so, then by his own testimony they were bound
to perform many more things, also by reason of this covenant. Did the
Decalogue belong to this covenant? Undoubtedly, since it is so often called
the covenant itself. But did the Decalogue demand nothing besides those
external things? Whoever says so must have been taught in the school of the
Pharisees, whom Christ refutes in Matthew 5. Why say more? On page 47 he
himself interprets *You shall not covet* in this way: *You shall desire, love,
or seek nothing whatever, whether earthly or heavenly, bodily or spiritual,
inward or outward, for your own sake.* Christ presents us with a far different
sense of the Decalogue when he explains its sum in Matthew 22:37 and following
and Luke 10:27, and Moses demanded the same thing from the Israelites in
God's name in Deuteronomy 6:5. Why say more in so manifest a matter?

8. He says on page 102: *The mediating and, as it were, reconciling power of
this covenant*—that is, the Israelite covenant—*which should have been the
principle and cause for fulfilling that duty, was not grace and love but human
fear and divine terror, promises and threats, exhortations and rebukes,
blessings and curses externally proposed and for the most part earthly and
bodily*, and so forth. Response. We know that the Israelites were under the
rod of a pedagogue and in the state of infancy, and therefore that the state
of Christians under the New Testament is far better with respect to liberty
and freedom of speech. But who besides this man, other Antinomians, and
fanatics of that kind will say that grace and love ought not to have moved the
Israelites to perform their duty? What, did all the upright people who
observed God's commandments from grace, love, and holy fear of God sin against
that covenant? 2. What, I ask, do those words of the second commandment of the
Decalogue signify: *those who love me and keep my commandments*?

[Printed p. 138 | IA PDF 146 | IA leaf 146 | IA decausadei02brow]

Is it not sufficiently assumed here that love of God is the principle of
obedience? Did not the sum of the law of the Decalogue demand that they love
God with the whole heart, and so forth? 3. Did they share in adoption (Romans
9:4)? Were they also heirs (Galatians 4:1, 4)? Did that covenant demand human
fear? See, I ask, Proverbs 29:25; Isaiah 8:12; 51:13; 57:11. 5. Do we now have
nothing to do with the fear of God and trembling? See Psalm 2:12; Hebrews
12:28; 1 Peter 1:17; 2 Corinthians 7:1; Ephesians 5:21; Acts 9:31; 1 Peter
1:17–18; 3:2; Romans 11:20; Matthew 10:28; Luke 12:5. 6. The rest savors of
pure Antinomianism. Are divine promises and threats, exhortations, rebukes,
and so forth now of no value among Christians? Whoever says so will, in my
judgment, be ignorant of the essence of the Christian religion, for scarcely
a chapter can be found in the New Testament from which it could not be
refuted. But if the matter stands this way, what moved our author to explain
at greater length in chapter 11 the dreadful judgments which follow the
violation of covenants? Was it not to deter us all from this sin?

9. This astonishing fabricator of covenants reviews in chapter 11 of his
little book eight various and distinct covenants of God with humanity: *four
universal covenants*, of which we heard above, and four others distinct from
these which he calls *particular* or *singular*: the *Noahic, Abrahamic,
Levitical, and Davidic* covenants. Does he also intend all these to be
distinct from the covenant of grace? Who should then think it strange that he
distinguishes the Israelite covenant from the covenant of grace? But whatever
can be said about the Noahic and Levitical covenants, it is nevertheless
absurd to imagine such a thing about the Abrahamic and Davidic covenants. Do
distinct promises also make distinct covenants? Abraham is called the father
of believers (Romans 4:1, 12, 16). He believed God, and it was imputed to him
for righteousness (Romans 4:3, 9; Galatians 3:6; James 2:23). The Apostle
shows at greater length in Romans 4:13, 16–24 and Galatians 3:8, 16, 18 that
the very promises our author makes peculiar belong to the covenant of grace.
Do Christians under the New Testament share Abraham's blessings (Galatians
3:16, 29)? What will he say about circumcision? Was it a seal of that
particular covenant? Bellarmine indeed speaks in this way in *On the
Sacraments*, book 1, chapter 17. If he thinks that Davidic covenant was
distinct from the covenant of grace, what will he say about the very many
promises made to David and the other testimonies of Scripture which clearly
indicate that the Messiah was revealed to David more fully than before? Let
him consult Psalm 132:2; Romans 4:6–8; Psalm 110; Matthew 22:42–45; Acts 2:34;
Psalm 2:5–7; Hebrews 1:5; Psalm 72:8, 11; Hebrews 5:8; 7:21; Psalm 45:7;
Hebrews 1:8–9; Psalm 40:7–8; Hebrews 10:5–10; Psalms 89:8, 11, 30, 37–38;
2:23; 68:18; Ephesians 4:8–9; Psalm 89:24, 26–27, 34–35; 132:11–12; 2 Samuel
7:14–16; 23:4, with many others.

10. On page 124 he says: *The universal covenant of grace established in
Christ Jesus permeates all the economies.* It is therefore absurd to
distinguish these economies of the covenant of grace essentially from the
covenant of grace itself, as though the mode of a thing had to be essentially
distinguished from the thing itself. Moreover, if the covenant of grace
equally permeates all the economies, it will be no more inconsistent with the
economy of the Old Testament than with that of the New Testament. Thus,
although the economies conflict with one another, neither will conflict with
the covenant of grace itself.

11. On page 125 he says that the Israelite covenant was made with the whole
of Israel, and so forth, and that it was in reality

[Printed p. 139 | IA PDF 147 | IA leaf 147 | IA decausadei02brow]

essentially distinct. Why? *For everyone knows, he says, that a very great
multitude of that people in no way belonged to obtaining the covenant of grace
through Jesus Christ. It is known from the whole New Testament that the elect
themselves received nothing from that covenant which was not external,
ceremonial, typical, legal, and in some measure servile.* Response. Scripture
also testifies that many now in the visible church do not obtain salvation
through Jesus Christ, because many are called but few chosen. They are
nevertheless externally in the covenant of grace, and we must not imagine an
essential distinction between the covenant of grace and its external
administration under the New Testament. We readily grant that the old economy
is distinguished from the covenant of grace itself in the way the new economy
is now distinguished from it. 2. Did the promise *I will be Jehovah to you*,
which is so often presented under that covenant, contain nothing except what
was external, typical, and so forth? Bellarmine indeed speaks in this way in
*On the Difference between the Sacraments of the Old and New Testaments* and
*On the Effect of the Sacraments*, chapter 17. The Reformed hold a very
different view, however, and we showed the contrary above. 3. Since those
external, typical, and ceremonial things foreshadowed and exhibited spiritual
things according to that economy, they much more clearly prove that the
covenant was the covenant of grace administered in that way.

12. In the same place and on page 126, he says: *With respect to others, this
covenant was imperfect and shadowy.* But this is false, because whatever can
be said about the external economy of the covenant must not immediately be
applied to the covenant itself. The New Testament has its own external
ceremonies, so to speak, and external sacraments which exhibit and represent
Christ. Must the New Testament therefore be called a ceremonial and shadowy
covenant? Or will he say with Bellarmine that the sacraments of the Old and
New Testaments differ in this respect: that the former only signified, while
the latter effect and produce grace? He will not deny that God established
that covenant out of love, but he says that *God's justice and hatred of sin
were its principal cause*. In this he openly contradicts Exodus 19:5;
Deuteronomy 7:6–8; 33:3; Jeremiah 31:3; and Malachi 1:3. He adds: *It is clear
that those who were enclosed only under the Old Testament never attained
God's friendship, did not obtain communion with him, and were not made
participants in his blessedness.* Response. These claims are true if he means
special, saving friendship and communion. But nothing follows from this,
because even today those who are enclosed only under the external economy of
the covenant—that is, are admitted to the sacraments and share the other
external ecclesiastical privileges—do not obtain that special communion and
happiness.

13. In chapter 14 he proposes many properties of the Israelite covenant, by
which he ultimately proves nothing. From them he will never conclude that the
covenant was essentially distinct from the covenant of grace. *Its first
property, he says, is that it was transacted with Israel immediately after
the exodus from Egypt.* Response. Yet it was substantially the same as the
Abrahamic covenant, as we proved above, and this circumstance of time does not
alter its essence and substance. 2. *That covenant, he says, took up all the
external and temporal promises God had given to Abraham and included in his
covenant.* Response. Since he himself acknowledges on page 24 and elsewhere
that temporal promises are included in the covenant of grace, it is evident
that this cannot prove it was not the covenant of grace. 3. *It had the
external proposal of the covenant of works joined to it.* Response. Since
that proposal of the covenant of works only served the covenant of grace, as
explained above, and the same proposal of the covenant of works remains to
some degree under the gospel, it is clear that nothing can be proved from
this.

4. *It was instituted by God to uncover, convict, and reprove sin.* Response.
I believe it was also instituted to expiate sin through the Messiah apprehended
by faith. Are there no means under the New Testament to uncover sin and so
forth, while the covenant of grace remains unchanged? 5. *It manifested the
inability of unregenerate people to do good and their aversion from it.*
Response. Does the gospel not also manifest this? 6. *It displayed before the
eyes the misery, unhappiness, and wickedness of the whole human race not
restored through Christ.* Response. Is knowledge of this mystery entirely
absent under the New Testament? 7. *It abundantly justified God in the
declaration of both his temporal and eternal judgments.* Response. As though
the covenant of grace or the New Testament did not show that God is to be
justified in all his judgments, but condemned! 8. *It embraced absolutely all
the Israelites.* Response. Does it therefore follow that it was not the
covenant of grace? It does not. All the males in Abraham's household were
circumcised. Was circumcision therefore not a seal of the covenant of grace?
Whatever he may think here with his Anabaptists and Separatists, we say that
even now under the New Testament the covenant of grace in its external
administration extends to far more people than the faithful or the elect. 9.
*It distinguished the entire Israelite nation from the rest.* Response. Does
the gospel not now distinguish whole nations from the heathen? What, then, is
peculiar here? *10. Therefore a considerable part of the duty of this
covenant was neither manifested nor even imposed by God upon the nations of
the earth.* Response. Does this not also obtain today? Faith in Christ
crucified is a considerable part of the duty of the evangelical covenant, yet
it is not today manifested to all nations of the earth. Will this covenant
therefore have nothing in common with the covenant of grace? Ah, how meager
these things are!

14. He continues: *10. The old covenant not only separated the Jews from the
heathen, but also distinguished them from Christians.* Response. This is true
with respect to certain circumstances but not with respect to substance. For
we have been made participants of the same root and fatness of the olive tree
from which they were broken off through unbelief (Romans 11:16–17, 20). By
faith we are Abraham's seed and heirs according to the promise (Galatians
3:29), just as they were. We are saved in the same way as they were (Acts
15:11). 11. *It distinguished those who lived under its dispensation from the
holy patriarchs.* Response. Nothing follows. Christians are distinguished
from the patriarchs in certain circumstances and are in a better condition,
but the patriarchs were nevertheless under the covenant of grace. 12. *It
served the covenant of grace in various ways. It gathered the children of God
included in it and preserved them until the manifestation of God's salvation.
It prefigured the mysteries of Jesus Christ, who was slain from the beginning
and foundation of the world; directed the minds of believers to him; often
proposed to all Israelites the sum of the covenant of grace; revealed that
life could be obtained through it alone; and continually drove God's elect
to Christ through its laws, ceremonies, and statutes.* Response. That is to
say: it actually was the covenant of grace, but dispensed differently from
the way it is today. 13. *It foreshadowed the new covenant.* Response. Who
denies that this covenant was outwardly administered through shadows, types,
and so forth which represented spiritual things? Let him prove that the
covenant of grace cannot be administered in this way, or that those who lived
under that economy were not made participants of the spiritual goods promised
in the covenant of grace.

15. From this he draws certain other conclusions about this covenant, and,
as his fourteenth, he says: *This covenant was merely ceremonial, consisting
only of shadows, rites, and sacred signs. It was typical both of the universal
covenant of grace and of the new covenant to be revealed in the last times.*
Response.

[Printed p. 141 | IA PDF 149 | IA leaf 149 | IA decausadei02brow]

We grant that this covenant can be called shadowy with respect to its economy,
but we deny that this can be said of it in itself or with respect to its
substance. We absolutely deny that this covenant consisted only of shadows,
rites, and so forth. Nor does Paul say this in the Epistle to the Hebrews, to
which he sends us. 15. *From this it also appears, he says, that this covenant
had to be only external. And in fact everything in it was only outwardly
proposed and done*, and so forth. Response. Must not everything be done
externally in the external administration of the covenant? There was also an
internal administration, whose fruit the upright received, as he himself
concedes in the same place. But he adds: *All of that flowed from the covenant
of grace, not from this covenant.* This is the same as if someone were now to
say: The law of the covenant is now inscribed externally in Bibles; external
sacred worship is now commanded; and God is covenanted only externally to the
whole visible church. Therefore the gospel is only an external covenant. But
when believers now receive anything inwardly, it flows entirely not from the
gospel but from the covenant of grace. Would this not be speaking very
precisely indeed? We know that Jesus Christ, not Moses, granted them the Spirit
and grace, just as Christ now—not pastors or the external administration of
the word or sacraments—works grace in the elect. We also grant that no one
was justified by the law of Moses or sanctified by the external promulgation
of the commandments. But let him tell me whether anyone is now sanctified by
the external declaration of God's commandments or justified by the law of
the gospel abstracted and separated from Christ. He will not deny that David,
Moses, and the other believers were even then justified by faith in the
promised Messiah and sanctified by his Spirit, and that the Messiah and all
his goods were then typically exhibited under that administration of the
covenant, according to that economy.

16. He further says on page 134, as his sixteenth point, that *this covenant
is called legal.* Response. That economy can in some measure be called legal,
speaking comparatively, but I do not recall where in Scripture the covenant
itself is called legal. Yet let us hear his reasons. *Because, he says, it was
entirely supported by the law and not founded upon the grace of Jesus Christ.*
We deny this as utterly false. *The law of works externally proposed, mostly
commanding its external duties, was the sole foundation of such a covenant.*
We likewise deny this altogether. *The whole ceremonial law belonged to it.*
Very true. Therefore it was entirely legal? We deny the inference. The gospel
also has its ceremonies, or external rites, yet we do not say that the
evangelical covenant is entirely legal. Indeed, the moral law in some way
serves the evangelical covenant today, yet for that reason we do not say that
it must be called legal or essentially distinct from the covenant of grace.

17. As his seventeenth point, he wishes it to be called *literal* by Paul.
Response. But Paul does not speak of the covenant itself considered in itself
and with respect to substance, but of the economy separated from Christ,
which indeed conferred no grace, divine love, or Spirit. 18. He wishes it to
be called *carnal*, *because it proposed carnal commandments, held out carnal
promises, restrained the flesh but not the spirit, uncovered the impurity of
the flesh, and enjoined cleanliness and sanctification*, and so forth.
Response. He argues badly from what is true in a qualified sense to what is
true without qualification. He will not deny that certain carnal commandments
and promises exist now—that is, commandments and promises concerning external
actions relating to the flesh. It is false that the commandments of the Old
Testament did not in any way restrain the spirit or enjoin purity and
sanctification of the spirit. See Deuteronomy 11:13; 13:3; 30:6; Joshua 22:5;
1 Samuel 12:20, 24; Proverbs 3:5; Deuteronomy 29:19; and

[Printed p. 142 | IA PDF 150 | IA leaf 150 | IA decausadei02brow]

17:20; Psalm 24:4; 1 Kings 8:61; Leviticus 19:17; Deuteronomy 4:9; 9:4;
15:9–10; 30:14.

18. As his nineteenth point, he wishes it to be called *servile* and says that
it truly begot its children into slavery. Response. We do not deny that the
Old Testament differs from the New in this respect or that the Apostle
indicates it quite openly, although some commentators deny that the slave
woman Hagar was a type of that Old Testament, which they wish to be a type of
the covenant of nature. Yet notwithstanding this slavery, believers were free
and shared adoption. This could not have been said if that covenant had been
essentially distinct from the covenant of grace. Nor am I pleased with the
foundation he lays here when he says: *Since every law reduces people to some
kind of slavery and chiefly restrains and binds the rebellious, disobedient,
and refractory.* For it will follow from this either that under the gospel we
have no law which restrains and binds the rebellious and disobedient, or that
we still remain constrained in slavery.

19. As his twentieth point, he says: *From this it is apparent that this
covenant was no less an adorable monument of divine rigor, judgment, and
justice than a testimony of benevolence.* Response. As we saw above, he said
that *God's justice and hatred of sin were the chief cause of this covenant*.
But neither this nor the other claim is clear from Scripture. When he tries
to establish this from Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 20, he errs greatly, as shown
elsewhere. Yet we do not deny that under that economy God displayed certain
monuments of his justice and severity. I hope he will not say that even now
under the New Testament God does not display such monuments. What, then, will
he conclude from this? 21. He wishes this covenant to have been *ineffectual*
and useless. Why? *For it removed no impediment keeping the sinner from
approaching God; by its own power it conferred not the least grace upon its
covenanted people; it did not pacify consciences, purify hearts, convert
souls, justify sinners, instill the love of God, or obtain actual and true
obedience.* Response. All these things are perfectly true of that external
economy abstracted from Christ. But nothing can be concluded from this,
because the same can truly be said of the external evangelical economy,
considered purely in itself and abstracted from Christ, its true kernel, as
is acknowledged. *And, he says, it presented God only as judge and never of
itself revealed him as a reconciled supernatural Father, or one to be
reconciled.* Response. If *of itself* excludes Christ here, he makes a true
assertion; if not, it is utterly false. See Isaiah 63:16; 64:8. How often do
we read that their sacrifices, when rightly offered, were pleasing odors
(Exodus 29:25, 41; Leviticus 1:9, 13, 17; 2:12, 29; 3:5, 16; 4:31; 6:15;
8:21, 28), and the same frequently elsewhere. And do we read that God accepted
those who offered rightly through faith? See Hebrews 11:4; Genesis 4:4;
2 Samuel 24:23; Job 42:8; Psalms 20:3; 119:108; Ezekiel 20:40–41; 43:17;
Leviticus 1:4; 21:23, 25; 23:11.

20. Finally, he wishes this covenant to be called *temporary, transitory, and
destined by God from eternity for total abolition and annihilation.* He tries
to prove this throughout chapter 15, but in that labor he sweats in vain. If
he means the external economy of this covenant, I do not know that he will
have anyone but a Jew contradict him. But if he means the substance of the
covenant itself, his arguments prove this not at all, as anyone who glances
through them will see. We therefore pass over them without getting our feet
wet. But on pages 148 and following he says that *the very foundations of
this covenant and the things essential to it have now been taken away*. It
will be worth examining these briefly. *The first foundation of this covenant,
after God's good pleasure, he says, was

[Printed p. 143 | IA PDF 151 | IA leaf 151 | IA decausadei02brow]

natural and carnal descent from Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.* Response. No one
denies that the natural offspring of those patriarchs were externally
admitted into this covenant. But how that admission can be called the
foundation of this covenant is something he must explain to me. By excluding
believers' children from the covenant under the New Testament, he declares
himself an Anabaptist and an opponent of infant baptism. 2. He calls
*circumcision of the flesh* a foundation. But how can this be, since male
children had to be circumcised because they were in the covenant, not the
reverse? It is true that circumcision now ceases. But baptism is instituted in
place of circumcision, and so all baptized people belong to the evangelical
covenant—that is, to its external administration. 3. He says that *deliverance
from Egypt* was another foundation of this covenant. On what reason he relies,
unless it is merely that it preceded the covenant, I do not see.

21. These were the foundations. Let us see how the things essential to it
have also been abolished. There are four: 1. Passover. 2. Sabbath. 3. Law. 4.
The gift of the land of Canaan. As for the law, of which he speaks first, we
freely grant that the ceremonial law is abolished and that the forensic law,
properly so called, is not binding. But these belonged not to the covenant
itself but to its external economy. We saw above in chapter IV the sense in
which the moral law is and is not abrogated, and the sense in which it is and
is not opposed to grace. But by saying without distinction that the law is
abolished and citing the scriptural testimonies abused by the Antinomians, as
we saw in chapter VI, he seems to come very close to the Antinomians. As for
the Passover and the promise of the land of Canaan, we grant that they now
cease, but we deny that the substance of the covenant itself is therefore
abolished. And although the Sabbath ceases now as seventh in order, we will
nevertheless prove that the Sabbath of the Decalogue, or the Sabbath one day
in seven, binds perpetually.

22. In chapter 16 he treats what he calls the essential and manifold
difference between the new and old covenants. We gladly grant that the economy
of the New Testament is distinguished in many ways from the economy of the
Old Testament. But for that reason we cannot say that the covenant under the
New Testament is essentially distinct from the covenant under the Old, nor
can this author's arguments compel us to do so. Let us hear how feeble they
are. 1. *By common consent the new covenant is said to be the same as the
covenant of grace. But the covenant of grace is distinct in its whole essence
from the legal, typical covenant*, and so forth. Response. We simply deny the
last claim. 2. *Although, he says, in our opinion this new covenant is in some
measure distinguished from the covenant of grace, its essence nevertheless
coincides with the essence of the general covenant of grace.* Response. We can
say the same about the old covenant. 3. He cites Jeremiah 31:31 and Hebrews 8,
which were discussed above. 4. *When it is called new, it is essentially
distinguished from the Old, because new things are opposed to old.* Response.
New things are indeed opposed to old, but not always essentially. The new and
old testaments are not opposed in this way, because the testament remains
essentially the same, although with respect to certain circumstances the one
can be called new and the other old and can in that respect be distinguished
from each other.

23. He says, as his fifth point: *The properties of the Old Testament are
opposed to those of the New.* Response. But what if these properties concern
not so much the substance of the covenant itself as its external economy?
What, then, are those properties? *The Old Covenant was established after the
exodus from Egypt*, and so forth; *the New after Christ's death.* Response.
Nothing else follows except that those

[Printed p. 144 | IA PDF 152 | IA leaf 152 | IA decausadei02brow]

economies were distinct with respect to time. *The Old exhibited certain
singular external goods promised to Abraham; the New exhibits universal
internal goods, and external ones in common at their proper time.* Response.
The Old Covenant also exhibited internal and saving goods, as we saw.
Therefore there is no difference in substance. *The Old contained the
proposal, imposition, and renewal of the covenant of works; the New altogether
rejects or absolutely ignores it.* Response. We deny that the covenant of
works was imposed under the Old Testament. It must also be proposed under the
New Testament, to awaken sinners sleeping in both ears so that they may
embrace the offered Christ the Redeemer all the more ardently. Nothing follows
from this, therefore, except a certain difference of degree. *The Old only
outwardly uncovered and reproved sin; the New inwardly reveals, drives out,
and mortifies it.* Response. The Old Testament also led to Christ, who alone
inwardly reveals, drives out, and mortifies sin, and without him the New
Testament cannot do this. There is therefore no difference in substance. *The
Old only uncovered humanity's inability to do good and demonstrated its
inborn inclination from sin toward evil. The New removes the one and
transforms the other.* Response. Believers under the Old Testament also
possessed both these goods, and the New separated from Christ will supply
neither. *The Old displayed the misery and guilt of the human race. The New
proclaims grace poured out upon it and happiness.* Response. Under the Old
also, grace was poured out, though not in that measure, and happiness was
proclaimed, as believers testify. Only a difference of degree, not an
essential difference, follows. *The Old extended to an innumerable multitude
of carnal people. The New embraces no carnal person in its bosom.* Response.
The New Testament also embraces many carnal people in its external
administration: all who are externally called and all who are baptized, along
with their children. *The Old divided Israel from the nations of the earth;
the New principally embraces all their elect.* Response. With respect to its
administration, the New does not embrace all the elect, since many of them
have not yet been called. It also divides the church from the nations of the
earth which are outside. *The Old was partly opposed to the covenant of grace
and partly served it by representing it; the New is the very covenant of
grace fully explained.* Response. The Old Testament was the very covenant of
grace explained more obscurely. There is therefore only a difference of
degree. I deny that the Old Testament considered in itself was opposed to the
covenant of grace or only partially represented it. *The Old was ceremonial
and typical; the New is real and true.* Response. The old economy was indeed
typical and ceremonial, but the covenant itself was real and true, exhibiting
to its covenanted people real and true goods both spiritual and temporal,
although the former more sparingly and obscurely and the latter more fully
and plainly. *The Old was only external; the New is only internal.* Response.
Both claims are denied. *The Old was inscribed on stones; the New on hearts.*
Response. The Old Testament also reached hearts, and under it God's law was
written in believers' hearts by the Spirit. Only a difference of degree
follows. *The Old was called legal from the law which was essential to it and
is called literal and carnal; the New is called evangelical from the gospel,
which takes precedence over law, and is spiritual in every sense, rejecting
the letter and mortifying the flesh.* Response. Let him explain, I ask, how
the law was essential to the Old Testament. The New also has law. The gospel
was also announced under the Old Testament, according to Paul in Galatians
3:8. Whatever it may have been when separated from Christ, in itself it was
not carnal or literal in every sense. We may conclude from this only that
there is some difference of degree which concerns the economies. And the New
Testament itself does not mortify the flesh without Christ. *The Old brought
various kinds of slavery; the New communicates full liberty.* Response. Then
the economies are distinguished. Let that suffice.

[Printed p. 145 | IA PDF 153 | IA leaf 153 | IA decausadei02brow]

*The Old revealed God's rigor, justice, and wrath; the New abundantly
manifests mercy, kindness, and grace.* Response. A difference of degree indeed
follows, for the Old manifested God's mercy and the New also reveals his
wrath and justice. *The Old threatened death; the New proposes life and
abundantly bestows and infuses it.* Response. The New also threatens death,
and the Old proposed life. But neither, separated from Christ, could bestow or
infuse life. *The Old was ineffectual and useless for salvation; the New
accomplishes both.* Response. Neither was effectual or useful for salvation
without Christ, and each, rightly used, is useful and effectual to attain in
its own way the ends intended by God. *The Old was temporary and passing; the
New is immutable.* Response. Therefore the New and Old Testaments are
essentially distinct? It does not follow.

24. In the sixth place he proposes certain differences between the Old and
New from 2 Corinthians 3 to confirm this essential difference, but his effort
is entirely fruitless. For the Apostle expressly and clearly enough shows
that he is not speaking about an essential difference, but rather an
incidental difference of degree. That is, he does not deny that the covenant
had one and the same essence and substance under the Old and New Testaments.
He says that these external dispensations and economies differ in degree with
respect to clarity and that the new economy of the covenant is far preferable
to the old. Hence the comparisons the Apostle makes and the statements which
must be taken and understood not simply but relatively, as the commentators
make evident. Thus the Apostle does not say that the old administration was
not glorious; indeed, he openly acknowledges that it was glorious in verses
7 and 9–12. But he concludes and proves in verses 8–11 that the evangelical
administration is more glorious, abounds in glory, possesses surpassing
glory, and is much more glorious. That Paul asserts no essential difference
between the Old and New Testaments in this epistle is clear even from his
application in 2 Corinthians 6:16–18 and 7:1 of promises originally made to
the old church. He says: *For you are the temple of the living God, as God
said*—that is, in Leviticus 26:12 or Ezekiel 37:26–27—*I will dwell in them
and walk among them; and I will be their God, and they shall be my people.
And I will be a father to you, and you shall be sons and daughters to me, says
the Lord Almighty*—that is, in Jeremiah 31:1 and frequently elsewhere.
*Therefore, since we have these promises*, and so forth. Since these are the
same promises to the church under the Old Testament and the church under the
New, it is manifest that the covenant made with the old church is not
essentially distinct from the covenant made with the church under the gospel.
For promises belong to the essence of the covenant and are supplied by virtue
of it.

25. The same answer can be applied to his other argument drawn from chapters
3 and 4 of the Epistle to the Galatians. Although the Apostle there
distinguishes the old and new economies and continually opposes them to each
other, he nowhere asserts an essential difference between the Old and New
Testaments or says that the old covenant differs essentially and substantially
from the New Covenant. Indeed, he plainly enough indicates the contrary when
he shows that believers were justified under that old economy in the same
way we are justified under the gospel. He proves that justification is now by
faith without works of the law and that there is no other manner of
justification from the fact that no other manner of justification before God
obtained under the Old Covenant. Who does not know that the same manner of
justification asserts a covenant that is the same in essence,

[Printed p. 146 | IA PDF 154 | IA leaf 154 | IA decausadei02brow]

whereas an essentially different manner argues an essentially distinct
covenant? Moreover, there the Apostle teaches that the old dispensation led
people to Christ so that they might be justified by faith (Galatians 3:24).
Wherever, therefore, there is the same Christ, who is the kernel of the
covenant and is sometimes called the covenant itself, the covenant must be
the same in essence and substance. Thus when this author says at the end of
the paragraph that *these things mutually expel each other, and the one is
not admitted unless the other is entirely removed*, and so forth, and from
this proves that these two covenants and testaments were entirely and actually
separate, then, if he means separate in substance and essence, he must say
that no one under the Old Testament was justified by faith, that the law was
against the promises, and that a law was given which could impart life,
contrary to Galatians 3:16–18, 21–22.

26. Nor do the things he finally brings from Paul's Epistle to the Hebrews to
prove this difference require any other answer. He commits the same error
here, applying to the substance and essence of the covenant what was said
about the difference of the economies. He must consequently say that the
ancient fathers under the Old Testament had nothing in common with believers
under the New, even with respect to Christ. Yet in the same epistle, chapters
1 and 2, the Apostle illustrates Christ's glory and proves it from Old
Testament Scriptures, indicating clearly enough that many things about Christ,
his origin, glory, office, and so forth were manifested to those under the
Old Testament. He must also say that Christ was not the pioneer of salvation
or the brother of those under the Old Covenant, contrary to Psalms 18:2;
22:22; Isaiah 8:18; and Hebrews 2:11–13. He must say that Paul very
inappropriately cited from the Old Testament the passages we see him cite in
chapters 3 and 4. Indeed, he must say that the Apostle errs greatly when he
says in chapter 4, verse 2, that the gospel was preached to them; when in
chapter 9 he says that the old covenant was also a testament and was
confirmed as a testament by blood, or death; when in chapter 11 he shows at
length that the fathers under the Old Testament lived by faith and performed
excellent works; and when in chapter 13, verse 8, he says that Jesus Christ is
the same yesterday, today, and forever. For *yesterday*, according to Calvin
on the passage, *embraces the whole period of the Old Testament*. Calvin
continues: *It is clear from this that the Apostle does not discuss Christ's
eternal essence but the knowledge which flourished among the godly in every
age and was the perpetual foundation of the church. Nevertheless, the
manifestation of Christ with respect to external appearance and manner was
different under the law from what we have today. But this does not prevent
the Apostle from saying truly and properly that Christ, to whom believers
look, is always the same.* Thus the differences this author proposes between
the typical and the real priest concern the different economies but do not
touch the substance of the covenant. One is called later and the other
earlier because of the external economies, which differed in time and one of
which was finally to be abolished. For the same reason one is called old and
the other new. But who will prove that a person clothed in a new garment
differs essentially from that same person in an old garment? We grant that the
Old Testament endured only until the time of reformation, and therefore we
say that the economies differ, while the same covenant remains in substance.
We also grant that the new economy is far more excellent and better, as the
Apostle shows. Yet we cannot say that under the law there was no certain and
effectual promise of salvation, or that the fathers were destitute of the
grace of the Spirit. Thus, as Calvin says on Hebrews 8:10, *Paul here makes a
comparison of greater and less, because under Christ's reign the Father
exercised the power of his Spirit much more abundantly and poured out his
mercy upon humanity. This

[Printed p. 147 | IA PDF 155 | IA leaf 155 | IA decausadei02brow]

eminence causes that small portion of grace with which he honored the fathers
under the law not to enter the reckoning.* What, will this author take the
things he adds here absolutely rather than comparatively, so that the fathers
under the law shared in none of them in any degree, when he says: *Under the
New Testament Christ absolves the guilty, justifies the ungodly, sanctifies
the unclean, liberates captives, quiets consciences, and perfects the
covenanted; makes God's law inwardly present and easy for them; unfolds and
confers every divine inheritance; reveals life; bestows immortality; joins
them to God in the Spirit; infuses grace; supplies glory; and imparts
everything formerly promised and proclaimed by God through the prophets?* I
do not believe he will. He must therefore conclude that there is only a
difference of degree which does not touch the essence of the covenant.
Finally, to bring this examination to an end, Paul does not say that the old
covenant *was* a shadow and type of future goods, as this man says, but that
it *had* them—that is, the covenant was formerly administered through
shadows, types, and so forth. Only a difference of degree is proved from this.
Calvin says on Hebrews 10:1: *The Apostle therefore establishes this
difference between the law and the gospel: under the law that which is today
expressed vividly and graphically in distinct colors was only sketched in
rough and initial lines. In this way he again confirms what he said before,
that the law was not idle and its ceremonies were not empty. Although it was
not an image of heavenly things finished, as they say, by the artist's final
hand, that representation, such as it was, was nevertheless very useful to
the fathers, even if our condition is better. It must also be noted that the
same things shown to them from a distance are now placed before our eyes. The
Christ of both is therefore the same, as are the righteousness,
sanctification, and salvation; the dissimilarity lies only in the manner of
painting.* The words of A. Polanus, *On the Law of God*, pages 301 and 311,
should also be noted: [*Although in Scripture the grace of the gospel
sometimes seems to be removed from the law, one must know that Scripture often
ascribes various benefits to the economy of the New Testament—for example,
remission of sins, inscription of the law upon human hearts, outpouring of the
Spirit, knowledge of God, and other things of that kind—as though they had
not occurred at all to God's children in the Old Testament. Yet they did
occur to them, but not in this degree, this measure, and this abundance. We
must therefore judge that in these passages it is not the thing but only the
manner of the thing that is denied.*]

What has been said makes sufficiently clear that this man's epilogue is to be
regarded as worthless. In it he concludes: *Those who do not see or notice the
difference between two things so very remote from each other are utterly blind
and blinder than moles themselves. In the future any who dare either to deny
or oppose this evident truth are to be judged most stiff-necked.* These words
express the genuine spirit of the man and his new society and reek of their
customary pride and self-assurance. Although I am regarded as blinder than
moles themselves, I state plainly that I have not yet been able to see this
essential difference which he invents between the Old and New Testaments,
although I acknowledge very great differences. Indeed, I confidently assert
that these testaments were the same in substance and essence. Lest I seem to
assert this without reason, I will confirm it with several arguments, although
it may already seem abundantly proved from what was said in chapters 9–11 of
this book.

1. The doctrine proposed to the people under the Old Testament by the
prophets was the same in substance as the doctrine of the apostles. Hence the
apostles and others repeatedly confirm their doctrine by testimonies drawn
from the Old Testament: Acts 17:2–3; 7:42; and 28:

[Printed p. 148 | IA PDF 156 | IA leaf 156 | IA decausadei02brow]

23; 8:32, 35; 18:28; Romans 4:3–4, 11; 10:6–14; 16:25–26; and 1 Corinthians
15:3–4. Paul says plainly in Acts 26:22 that he *preaches nothing beyond what
the prophets and Moses foretold would come to pass*. But if the Old Testament
or covenant had been essentially distinct from the New Testament, its doctrine
would also have been essentially different and distinct.

2. Christ himself sends his hearers to the Old Testament Scriptures so that
they may see confirmed what he said about himself. John 5:39 says: *Search
the Scriptures, for you think that in them you have eternal life, and they are
the things which testify about me.* And verses 46–47: *For if you believed
Moses, you would believe me, for he wrote about me*, and so forth. He often
asserts that the Old Testament Scriptures speak about him. See Luke 24:25–27,
44. Therefore, if Old Testament Scripture treated the same principal theme
and the same subject matter as the New Testament treats, the covenants under
those different economies are substantially and essentially the same. For
Scripture and that subject matter follow the nature of the covenant, explaining
it and proposing the duty of the covenanted people.

3. If the New and Old Testaments were two specifically and essentially
distinct covenants, it would be impossible for the prophets and apostles to
constitute one foundation. But the Apostle says that we are built upon the
foundation—not foundations—of the apostles and prophets (Ephesians 2:20). I
know that by the proclamation of the gospel the apostles ministerially laid
this foundation more clearly and brilliantly than the prophets formerly did,
who laid it more covertly and obscurely. Yet it is one and the same
foundation. This could not be so if the Old and New Testaments were
essentially distinct covenants, for the prophets laid the foundation of their
doctrine according to that economy and the apostles according to the new
economy.

4. Christ's words in John 4:36–38 also confirm this: *In this the saying is
true, One sows and another reaps. I sent you to reap that for which you did
not labor. Others labored, and you have entered into their labors.* This makes
clear that there is a difference between the work of the prophets and that of
the apostles with respect to its difficulty, clarity, abundance of fruit, and
the ease and joy of working. Yet as far as the substance of the matter is
concerned, all the work of both reapers and sowers tends to the same end.
The very distinguished Boyd, illustrating this parable in passing on Ephesians
2:20, says: *Meanwhile this always remains firm and fixed: just as the very
thing which had earlier been sown is reaped, so reapers and sowers are in
reality occupied with the same work. Thus this one and only foundation of the
whole church was laid by the prophets as well as the apostles.*

5. If the Old Testament is essentially distinct from the New Testament, the
church which existed under the Old Testament in Acts 7:38 must be distinguished
in the same way from the church which now exists. But the church under the
New and Old Testaments is the same in essence and substance. There is only one
church, or one body, just as there is only one Spirit, one Lord, and one faith
(Ephesians 4:4–5). This one body embraces all the members who depend upon one
head and Lord and are animated by one life-giving Spirit. It therefore
embraces the Old Testament church, which did not live without Christ and the
Spirit of Christ. Hence also that building whose one foundation was laid with
one accord by the prophets and apostles can only be one (Ephesians 2:21). In
the same way, with respect to essentials, it is the same house in which Moses
was faithful

[Printed p. 149 | IA PDF 157 | IA leaf 157 | IA decausadei02brow]

as a servant and over which Christ presided as Lord (Hebrews 3:1–3, 5–6).
Therefore, if the church under the Old Testament acknowledged Christ as its
head, it must be one and the same as the New Testament church, for one head
corresponds to one body. No other head of the church is given besides Christ
(Ephesians 1:22; Colossians 1:18). Consequently, Christ's body is one.

6. When the Apostle says in Romans 11:17 that the Gentiles were grafted in
where the broken branches had been and were made participants of the root and
fatness of the olive tree, and in verses 24–25 that the Jews will be grafted
in again, he does not recognize distinct olive trees or roots. He asserts one
which is essentially the same under every economy: when the natural branches
were grafted in, when after their cutting and breaking off the wild olive
branches were grafted in, and finally when the natural branches will be
grafted in again. The same kingdom that was taken from the Jews was given to
the Gentiles (Matthew 21:43).

7. One building has one chief cornerstone, in whom the entire building,
fittingly joined together, grows to be a holy temple to the Lord (Ephesians
2:20–21). Since, therefore, the church both under the Old and under the New
Testament acknowledges only one chief cornerstone, in whom it grows and is
and was joined together, it must be one and the same in substance, not another
specifically different church. Calvin says on the passage: *He wishes to join
Jews and Gentiles into one spiritual structure. They were like two different
walls, and so he places Christ in the middle at the corner, to unite them
both.*

8. If the gospel was announced under the Old Testament, that covenant cannot
differ in substance from the covenant under the New Testament. But the former
claim is true. The connection of the major premise is clear from the fact
that the gospel is the doctrine of salvation in Christ from the sheer mercy
of God and is thus nothing other than the explanation and proposal of the
covenant of grace. It therefore presupposes the covenant of grace, not a
covenant substantially distinct from the covenant of grace. The minor premise
is certain, for the gospel was announced to Adam in Genesis 3:15, in Noah's
time in 1 Peter 3:19–20, to Abraham in Galatians 3:8, and to the Israelites in
Hebrews 4:2. See also 1 Peter 4:6.

9. As an accessory follows its principal, so the sacraments follow the
covenants themselves, and the covenants are of the same kind as their
sacraments or seals. If, therefore, the Old and New covenants or testaments
are not substantially the same but different, then the sacraments of the Old
and New Testaments must signify and seal substantially different things. In
the same way, the sacraments before and after the fall necessarily seal things
substantially distinct, because the covenants they were instituted to confirm
and corroborate are substantially different. Yet it is false that
substantially distinct things are signified and sealed by the seals of the
Old and New Testaments, according to 1 Corinthians 10:1–4. Calvin says on the
passage: *When he says that the fathers ate the same spiritual food, he first
indicates the power and efficacy of the sacraments; then he shows that the
sacraments of the law finally possessed the power our sacraments have today.*
And a little later: *The scholastic doctrine is well known: that the sacraments
of the Old Law figured grace, whereas ours actually confer it. This passage
is very well suited to refute that error, for Paul testifies that the reality
of the sacrament was exhibited to the ancient people no less than to us.
Therefore the Sorbonnists impiously imagine that the holy fathers under the
law lacked the reality of the signs. I indeed acknowledge that, once Christ
was manifested in the flesh, the efficacy of the signs is known to us more
clearly and abundantly than it was to the fathers. Thus the difference between
us and them is only one of degree, according to more and less, because we
perceive the same things more fully than they did in a lesser manner.*

[Printed p. 150 | IA PDF 158 | IA leaf 158 | IA decausadei02brow]

See further 1 Corinthians 5:7 and Colossians 2:11–12.

10. The apostles exhorted the Jews sincerely to embrace Christ offered in the
gospel, using this argument: they were already under the covenant and the
promises had been made to them. Acts 2:38–39 says: *Repent, and let every one
of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins—for
the promise was made to you and to your children*, and so forth. If this
promise had belonged to a covenant substantially distinct from the new
covenant, the Apostle's argument would have been utterly feeble. Likewise, in
Acts 3:19, 29 he says, *Repent, therefore, and be converted*, and among other
things uses this argument: *You are the children of the prophets and of the
covenant which God made with your fathers, saying*, and so forth. The covenant
whose children they were was therefore not substantially distinct from the
New Covenant now offered to them. Otherwise the contrary would have followed
from the Apostle's argument instead.

11. If sinners under the Old Testament were justified in the same way as we
are under the New, the covenant is substantially one and the same, because
the essence and nature of the covenant consists in its showing the way people
are justified before God and accepted by him. The former claim is true, as
Scripture shows at length when it proves that the fathers were justified by
faith (Romans 4:1–4, 6–8, 17–23; James 2:23). Indeed, from this the Apostle
proves in the same passage that we cannot be justified in any other way. See
also Romans 1:17 and Acts 10:43: *To him all the prophets also bear witness
that everyone who believes in him will receive remission of sins through his
name.* See Romans 3:21–22.

12. Because the Jews in unbelief refused to embrace Christ by faith, they
were cut off and cast out of the covenant. Therefore that covenant was not
substantially distinct from the new covenant. The premise belongs to the
Apostle in Romans 11 and 2 Thessalonians 2. The conclusion is clear from this:
exclusion from a covenant which had nothing in common with the new covenant
would be no punishment. Indeed, liberation from such a covenant would be a
very great benefit. Nor would there be any correspondence between sin and
punishment if the Old Covenant had been substantially different from the New.

13. Believers under the Old Covenant lived by faith just as those under the
New do. The only difference is that under the Old they believed in Christ who
was to come, while under the New they believe in Christ actually exhibited.
No essential or substantial difference follows from this, as is evident.
Believers under both covenants had the very same object both of hope and of
faith. See Hebrews 11 throughout; Habakkuk 2:4; Luke 1:54–55, 68–79; Acts
26:6–7; 28:20. Therefore the covenant is substantially the same.

14. Under the Old Covenant all believers were Abraham's children, his seed,
and participants in the blessings promised to Abraham and his believing seed
(Acts 3:25; Romans 2:28–29; 9:6–8), just as believers are under the New
Covenant (Galatians 3:7, 29; Romans 4:12–13, 16). Therefore both covenants
were one in substance, embracing all Abraham's children, whether Jews or
Gentiles.

15. Both the Old and New Testaments or covenants flow from God's special love,
mercy, grace, and benevolence. This is acknowledged concerning the New; it is
no less clear concerning the Old from Ezekiel 16:2–9, 60–63; Deuteronomy
7:7–8; 1 Samuel 12:22. Hence God's mercy is often mentioned together with the
covenant in Deuteronomy 7:9, 12; 2 Chronicles 6:14; Nehemiah 1:5; 2 Kings
13:23.

16. Indeed,

[Printed p. 151 | IA PDF 159 | IA leaf 159 | IA decausadei02brow]

this can be proved from the fact that both covenants are called a testament
in Hebrews 9:15–18, 20. In each, goods are bestowed absolutely by the testator,
are obtained by hereditary right through the force of the testament, and
come to the heirs and legatees through the death of the testator and principal
heir, Christ. For if the Old Covenant were substantially and essentially
distinct from the New, it could not rightly be called a testament, because
the goods would have been bestowed in a very different way than through a
testament. They would not have been constituted heirs, contrary to Galatians
3:29 and Romans 8:17.

17. The goods granted to the covenanted people of each testament and promised
and exhibited in each covenant are substantially the same. Therefore the
covenants themselves are also substantially the same. Our opponents will not
deny the inference. The premise is clear from the fact that God was the God
of those who lived under the Old Covenant just as he is our God who live
under the New. As we saw above, this one thing embraces every spiritual and
saving good. Compare Leviticus 11:45; 22:33; 25:38; 26:12; Jeremiah 7:23;
11:4; 30:22; Ezekiel 36:28; Genesis 17:8; Exodus 29:45; Jeremiah 24:7; 31:33;
32:38; Ezekiel 11:20; 34:24; 37:23, 27; Zechariah 8:8 with 2 Corinthians 6:16;
Revelation 21:3; John 20:17; Romans 3:29. It is also clear because the gratuitous
remission of sins for Christ the mediator's sake was promised and granted to
the covenanted people of each: Exodus 34:6–7; Micah 7:18; Isaiah 43:25;
1 Kings 8:34, 36; Psalms 51:1–2; 32:5; Isaiah 38:17; Numbers 14:19–20;
Jeremiah 33:8. Both shared adoption. No one doubts this concerning believers
under the New Testament, and it is evident concerning the Israelites from
Exodus 4:22; Isaiah 63:16; and Romans 9:4. They were heirs, although under
guardians and trustees (Galatians 4:1). In short, everything relating to
salvation was promised: Psalms 22:26; Hebrews 9:15; Psalms 16:6; 31:19;
Isaiah 64:4. Finally, the same Spirit, who is one according to Ephesians 4:4,
was promised in Psalms 51:11 and 116:10, compared with 2 Corinthians 4:13.
Calvin in *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 10, demonstrates clearly that spiritual
goods were promised to the Israelites. The Apostle plainly proves in Hebrews
11:13–16 that they aspired to heavenly things.

18. Each covenant exhibited the same mediator, in whom and through whom
salvation and redemption were to be obtained and in whom all promises were
yes and amen. He is the seed of the woman promised to Adam and the one in whom
all nations were to be blessed, who was promised to Abraham. He is also the
one the Israelites tempted in the wilderness and whom believers embraced by
faith both in sacrifices and in sacraments. For no other name under heaven is
given among humanity by which we must be saved (Acts 4:12). See Acts 15:9, 11;
John 8:58; Genesis 22:18; Job 19:25–27; Hebrews 9:15; 13:8; 1 Corinthians
10:4.

19. Why say more? Covenants are essentially one and the same when they flow
from one source—God's special love, benevolence, and mercy in Christ; have the
same essential conditions; exhibit the same special promises; promise the
same inheritance; propose the same price of redemption; require the same
duties from their covenanted people; propose the same object of faith to
everyone, namely Jesus Christ; are confirmed by sacraments and seals which
seal the same things; are made with people in the same condition of misery
and inability; tend toward the same principal end, namely the glory of God's
grace in Christ Jesus; and finally are confirmed by the same Spirit. Such are
the covenants of the Old and New Testaments. The major premise is certain, and
