Let us now proceed to the fourth member of this passage, namely, the end of
our allotment, or effectual calling to fellowship in that heavenly
inheritance. The Apostle sets it out in the following verse in this way: “that
we should be for the praise of his glory.” By the glory of God we may
understand that excellence of God which shines out especially in his mercy.
For God wishes to be celebrated above all in this goodness and kindness of his
toward his creatures. The Apostle therefore rightly distinguishes praise from
glory here, because the divine glory properly resides in God himself and not
in any creature. For what else is God’s glory but that infinite totality of
all the divine attributes, by which God himself, the best and greatest,
infinitely surpasses everything else that can be spoken or conceived in
goodness, wisdom, power, holiness, justice, mercy, self-sufficiency,
blessedness, and, finally, perfection of every kind? This glory is indeed in
God himself and in no creature; it is proper and essential to God himself, I
say. Creatures, however, praise and admire this glory as it is in part
manifested to them in God’s works, especially in that work of human redemption
and the ineffable grace by which, through his Son Jesus Christ and in him, he
so richly endowed the elect. They extol and proclaim the excellence of that
divine grace.

[Marginalia: Lecture XIII. Member 4. The glory of God. First sense.]

The distinction between God’s glory and every kind of glory that can belong
to human beings must be noted. A person’s glory resides in the one who
glorifies him and pays him honor, rather than in the person who receives this
glory from elsewhere; but God’s glory properly resides in God himself. Or,
what comes to almost the same thing, by a Hebrew metonymy “the glory of God”
can be taken for the glorious God himself, just as we commonly say “the royal
majesty” for the king himself. For the Hebrews customarily use “the glory of
the Lord” in paraphrase for God himself, full of glory and majesty. Thus in
Exodus 16 the glory of the Lord is said to have appeared to the Israelites in
the cloud itself; that is, the glorious God himself displayed his power by
some visible testimony and notable proof of glory. So also in Psalm 29: “In
his temple,” says the Psalmist, “the Lord declares all his glory”; that is, he
gloriously manifests himself. So in Romans 1: “They exchanged the glory of the
incorruptible God for a fashioned image of corruptible man, and of birds,
four-footed animals, and creeping things”; that is, in place of the glorious
God they substituted creatures for themselves to worship and adore. The
Apostle speaks in the same way about the Father in chapter 6 of the same
epistle, saying that Christ was raised from the dead “through the glory of the
Father”; that is, by the power and strength of the glorious Father. Likewise
he speaks in this way of Christ himself in 2 Corinthians 4: “lest the light of
the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God, should shine upon
them”; that is, the gospel of the glorious Christ himself.

[Marginalia: A note in passing. Second sense. Exodus 16:12. Psalm 29:9.
Romans 1:23. Romans 6:4. 2 Corinthians 4:4.]

Furthermore, we are said to have been admitted into the inheritance so that
we should be for the praise of God’s glory in two ways. First, in a passive
sense, so to speak, because God has magnificently displayed his glory in
calling, adopting, and sanctifying us in this way; in freely bestowing on us
wisdom, righteousness, peace, joy, love, faith, hope, patience, and the other
gifts of his Spirit; and finally in glorifying us, transferring us from the
lowest, most abject, and most wretched condition into so blessed and glorious
an inheritance. Here in us, as in a most abundant harvest, subject, and
occasion of his glorious praise, God has set his magnificent grace and his
beneficence—which can never be sufficiently wondered at and admired—before
the eyes of human beings and angels. He did so in order that this grace of his
toward us, so immense and glorious, might be praised and celebrated by all. Do
we not see the princes of this age regard it as great praise for themselves
that in their court and retinue they have the bravest commanders, the wisest
counselors, the noblest governors, invincible soldiers, and men outstanding
and flourishing in learning and virtue, resources, birth, wealth, and beauty,
even though they certainly did not make them such and could not have done so?
How much more excellent, then, is that praise of the divine excellence! For
his infinite mercy and gratuitous goodness alone has made us—once so wretched
and despised, slaves of death, Satan, sin, and hell—his children in his
glorious Son Jesus Christ, enriched and endowed with every heavenly blessing,
and heirs of the heavenly kingdom. This, then, is the passive sense of this
expression.

[Marginalia: Further insistence. Passive sense. An unequal comparison.]

Second, it is also taken in an active sense: that we should be for the praise
of his glory, that is, so that throughout our life we may glorify him by
newness of life, holiness, thankful hearts, and the voice of praise,
confession, and thanksgiving. For our God wishes not only to be glorified in
us, as in a harvest and subject of his praise, but also by us ourselves.
Otherwise his grace and mercy will never be celebrated in us, but we will
instead become to everyone a proof of wrath, severity, and justice, unless we
ourselves strive, by voice and life, work and example, to glorify so merciful
and beneficent a Father and to advance the kingdom of Christ his Son
throughout our lives. And we must do this seriously and sincerely, not
casually, sluggishly, or perfunctorily. This is the end of our calling, common
to Jews and Gentiles alike, although the Apostle applies it here only to
believing Jews in accordance with the purpose of his argument. We observed
above that he applies these spiritual benefits first to the Jews and then to
the Gentiles.

[Marginalia: Active sense. Transition to Member 5.]

Let us therefore see, in the last place, how he designates and describes the
believing Jews here. For this was the fifth member of our preceding division:
“those who first hoped,” and so forth. In this we have a striking proof of how
valuable a faithful and skillful translation of words is for attaining the
true sense of sacred Scripture and the mind of the Holy Spirit speaking in it.
Such a translation cannot be made unless the original force—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 86 | Caput I p. 44]

—and genuine sense of the word have first been carefully weighed. For when
this participle is rendered as Beza, our most faithful, learned, and precise
translator, translated it—“we who first hoped”—who does not see that the
sense is plain and transparent? The Apostle is plainly speaking of the Jews
who were called to the knowledge of Christ before the Gentiles, from whom the
Christian Church began, and through whom the word of the Lord, that is, the
gospel, was carried to the nations and spread among all peoples from Zion and
Jerusalem as far as the ends and boundaries of the world, like lines extended
in every direction from the center to the circumference. But if you render
this participle with the Old Translator as “we hoped beforehand” or “earlier,”
you will make the Apostle’s sense obscure and his meaning ambiguous.

[Marginalia: The true interpretation. Isaiah 2:2, etc.]

This explains the astonishing diversity among the ancients in interpreting
this passage. It is truly surprising that Jerome looked for a knot in a
bulrush here, as the saying goes. First, he refers these words to that eternal
foreknowledge of God by which, he says, we are said to have hoped in Christ
from the time when we were elected, predestined, and blessed in the heavenly
places, as though we had existed before the foundation of the world so that
we could hope in Christ before we were elected and appointed in him, and had
been elected and appointed on account of that hope so that in the present age
we might be called into the inheritance of God’s kingdom. Since he reported
this opinion on another’s judgment rather than his own—far be it from us to
think that such an impious delusion entered Jerome’s mind as the true sense of
this passage, for he had perhaps read it in Origen, the father of dreams of
this kind—he gives another interpretation which must be strongly condemned
and is altogether heterodox. “Another,” he says, “not maintaining this
doctrine, that we existed and hoped in Christ before we lived in this body,
will transfer its meaning to that point so as to say: at the coming of the
Savior, when every knee will bow at his name, and so forth, some will be
subjected willingly and others by necessity; those who had hoped in him before
the presence of his majesty will be for the praise of his glory and will be
called ‘those who hoped beforehand,’ while those who are found believing by
necessity, at the time when neither the devil nor his angels will be able to
deny him as he reigns, will indeed be hoping, but not for the praise of his
glory.”

[Marginalia: Jerome’s first interpretation. Second interpretation.]

See how that man, wandering as far from the mark as heaven from earth, is
carried from one extreme—eternity preceding the beginning of the world—to the
other extreme, the consummation of the ages, leaving behind the entire
intermediate span in which the truth of the matter must nevertheless be
sought. Theophylact refers this preposition to the future outcome of the
matter, that is, the fulfillment of our hope, as if the Apostle said: “We who
hoped in Christ before we obtained the fulfillment of our hope,” since it does
not yet appear but will be consummated at Christ’s glorious coming. Finally,
Ambrose interprets it to mean that the Apostles hoped in the Christ who was to
come, and therefore refers the word “before” to Christ’s coming. It is as
though the Apostle said, “We who hoped in him before he came.” He seems to
restrict this opinion to the Apostles alone when he writes as follows: “The
dispensation of preaching was decreed by God for those from among the Jews who
believed in Christ; hence no one from the Gentiles was elected to the
apostolate. For it was fitting that preachers should be chosen from those who
had hoped beforehand for the salvation promised to them in Christ.” Thus they
interpret this word of the hope of the elect of Israel in the Christ who was
to come, by which they hoped in him before he came into the world. The Apostle
calls this “the hope of Israel” in Acts 28: “Because of the hope of Israel I
am bound with this chain.”

[Marginalia: Criticism. Theophylact. Ambrose. The hope of Israel. Acts 26:6
and 28:20.]

Although these latter two interpretations wander far less from the mark than
Jerome’s, it is nevertheless clear even from the following verse that in this
passage the Apostle considered neither the fulfillment of our hope, which we
await only in the kingdom of glory, nor the hope of the believing Jews in the
Christ who was still to come before he was manifested in our flesh. Rather,
he meant to compare those Jews who had been called to faith in and knowledge
of Christ with the Gentiles who were afterward, through their ministry,
admitted and received into the same inheritance of God’s children. His point
was to designate not a prerogative of merit or worthiness—for from the
beginning he has so often insisted that this benefit is entirely gratuitous,
and above, by the very word “allotment,” has excluded every merit—but a
priority of time, so to speak. He does not therefore mean that the Jews were
granted admission into the inheritance of God’s children earlier on account
of preceding merits or works, because they had earlier hoped in the Christ who
was to come. Far be it from us to ascribe to the Apostle this impious and
absurd opinion, which so openly conflicts with the rest of his teaching,
especially throughout this entire epistle. For it is evident that by the
expression “hoping in Christ” he means faith or trust in Jesus Christ, which
hope necessarily accompanies. Thus there is a tacit argument here for the
effectual calling of the believing Jews into the heavenly inheritance, drawn
from their faith or trust in Christ. It is as though the Apostle argued in
this way: “Those who believed first were the first to be admitted into the
inheritance. But we Jews first believed in Christ and hoped in him, when he
had now been disclosed and revealed to us through the gospel preached after
his coming. Therefore we too were the first to be allotted.” Yet since there
was nothing in them for which they should be called before others, it
nevertheless seemed good to God, from mere grace, to call everyone into
fellowship in the same inheritance in such an order that he admitted the Jews
to that grace first.

[Marginalia: Why the Jews came first.]

Hence in Romans 15 the Apostle affirms that Christ himself became a minister
of the circumcision; that is, that he preached the grace of his gospel first
of all to the Jews and offered them salvation and eternal life. Indeed, he not
only did this himself but also commanded his Apostles to do the same, as can
be read in Matthew 10. Paul and Barnabas expressly indicate in Acts 13 that
the same order had to be observed: “It was necessary that the word of God
should first be set before you. But since you reject it and judge yourselves
unworthy of eternal life, behold, we turn to the Gentiles.” Elsewhere too,
when distinguishing Jews and Gentiles, the Apostle customarily puts the Jews
first, as in Romans 2, 1 Corinthians 1, and so forth. What, then, was the cause
of this necessity, that Christ himself had to become a minister of the
circumcision and that the gospel had to be preached to the Jews first? The
cause of this Jewish prerogative must certainly be sought not in them but in
God himself. The Apostle himself points to this cause in that same Romans 15,
when he says that Christ became a minister of the circumcision for the truth
of God, in order to confirm the promises to the fathers—that is, the promises
made to the fathers. This appears to be the necessity which Paul expressed in
Acts: because that gratuitous covenant had been struck with their fathers,
the promises of the Messiah who was to come in his own time, the redeemer of
the Church, had been made to them; the oracles of God had been entrusted to
them; divine worship had been instituted among them; the Church of God had
been—

[Marginalia: Romans 15:8. Acts 13:46. Romans 2:9–10. 1 Corinthians 1:24.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 87 | Caput I p. 45]

—preserved among them; and the law, containing the shadow and types of future
good things, had been delivered to them, and so forth. It therefore follows
that the promises had first and before all else to be fulfilled to them; that
Christ, who was to be born from their nation, had first to be presented and
offered to them; and that in him the forgiveness of sins, righteousness,
redemption, life, and every grace had to be offered to them before the
Apostles turned to the Gentiles. In this way the truth of God and the
certainty of those promises which he had long ago made to their ancestors and
later repeated so often through his Prophets before Christ’s coming into the
world would remain firm and unshaken. This is the true interpretation of this
passage. It remains for us, as is our custom, to gather from it in the last
place some doctrine for our use and observation.

First, then, let us observe that our Apostle, not content with a general
explanation of those spiritual benefits, afterward descends to their
particular application. The things which he had attributed above to all the
faithful without distinction he applies first to the Jews called to faith in
Christ and then also to the Gentiles, specifically the Ephesians. What, I ask,
was the use of this, and what fruit did it have in the Church? We may certainly
gather it from the same chapter of Acts just cited. When the Apostles turned
from the Jews to the Gentiles and applied specifically to them the message of
salvation, that is, the joyful proclamation of the gospel, it is said that
when the Gentiles heard these things, “they rejoiced and glorified the word of
the Lord, and as many as had been appointed to eternal life believed.” From
this it is clear that the more particular this teaching of the gospel is, the
more consolation and joy it brings with it. And if it is applied not only to
some whole people and nation, but to individual persons, it will undoubtedly
bring so much more consolation and the fruit of joy.

[Marginalia: First observation. Acts 13:48.]

For this reason the same Apostle, in 1 Timothy 1, after setting forth in
general that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners and applying
that general doctrine particularly to himself by adding, “of whom I am the
first,” from which that conclusion full of consolation spontaneously flowed,
“Therefore he came to save me by name,” immediately shows in the following
verse the joy of his heart born from this particular application. From his
inmost affection he breaks out into that notable doxology and magnificent
thanksgiving to his God. And lest the Roman theologians object that this was a
special privilege of Paul, by which he could indeed be persuaded of his own
salvation through Christ by a special revelation, while the things he
professes concerning himself should not therefore be taken as an example for
us, as though they could be applied to us, who are in no way comparable to
him, he seems to have answered their objection almost expressly in the same
passage when he adds, “that I might be an example,” he says, “to those who
would believe in him for eternal life.” From this statement anyone who is
willing to see can perceive that this very thing was God’s purpose in Paul’s
calling and conversion: that others who would later believe in Christ should
flee to Christ by his example and apply the same grace and salvation to
themselves particularly and by name, as they looked upon this striking example
of our Apostle and this brilliant proof of divine mercy displayed toward him.

[Marginalia: 1 Timothy 1:15. Anticipated objection. 1 Timothy 1:16.]

If it were not both lawful and necessary for salvation to apply to individual
believers this grace of Christ offered to us in the gospel, what
need would there have been for those seals of the gratuitous covenant attached
to the gospel? Their use does not consist merely in bare inspection, or in
some other act common to all, but in a particular application and examination
by which those sacramental signs are distributed to individuals and applied
one by one, for no other end than that the certainty of the promises toward
individual believers may be applied and sealed by the power and efficacy of
the Holy Spirit in the hearts of individuals. Therefore, we rightly turn away
from and abhor the impious and blasphemous opinion of all who condemn and
reject this particular application of Christ’s spiritual benefits, and thus
of Christ himself, to individual believers, as a doctrine full of pride and
arrogance and contrary to Christian modesty. For by taking away this
particular application, they remove from our midst the very soul and life of
saving faith. They abandon the wretched souls of sinful human beings to
struggle perpetually among the shoals and sands of doubt; deprive and defraud
them of all true peace and solid joy in the Holy Spirit; take away that firm
anchor of hope; and overthrow the foundation of solid consolation in both
life and death. Thus, after nursing and deceiving the consciences of wretched
people with their impious trifles and superstitious inventions of human
traditions, and indeed lulling them for a time in the deep sleep of false
security, they finally awaken and torment them in wretched ways. They show
them no saving harbor anywhere in which they can stand in safety and cast the
firm anchor of saving faith. Instead, amid the breeze of hope shining down
from heaven, they allow them to be dashed upon the infamous rocks of despair
and finally, as far as it lies in their power, to break apart and be swallowed
up in grief. They therefore deserve to hear us call them true and utter
frustrators and sedaters of souls, as well as their torturers and
executioners.

[Marginalia: A noteworthy argument. Corollaries.]

Second, because the Apostle, while mentioning here the allotment of the
believing Jews, still returns to the fountain of the divine counsel and so
often presses the point of predestination from God’s purpose, we perceive a
twofold, indeed a threefold, use of the doctrine of God’s special providence
and the eternal election of the saints. First, it serves to exclude all our
works and merits. This is why we see our works and God’s purpose placed in
direct opposition to one another in Scripture. Romans 9 says, “So that God’s
purpose according to his election”—that is, not from works—“might stand, not
from works, but from him who calls.” Second Timothy 1 says, “Who called us not
according to our works, but according to his purpose and grace.” This
opposition has a place not only in election itself but also in its execution.
For just as in that eternal predestination no merit of ours and no regard for
our works intervened as a cause of the divine decree, but only his purpose
from his good pleasure, so also in the execution of that decree—that is, in
our being effectually called into the inheritance of the saints—no merit of
ours likewise intervenes. Instead, we are freely called from God’s purpose,
according to which we had been freely elected from eternity. In this respect
there is a difference between the execution of election and reprobation. For
in the latter sin always intervenes, by which all who are vessels of wrath
fitted for destruction deserve eternal punishment from God’s justice.

[Marginalia: Second observation. Use 1. Romans 9:11. 2 Timothy 1:9. The
difference between election and reprobation.]

2. This same doctrine serves to restrain every curious inquiry of the human
mind, lest we labor beyond what is fitting over the causes of God’s works and
deeds—

[Marginalia: Use 2.]

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 88 | Caput I p. 46 (through Lecture XIII close)]

—something very familiar to us when we follow the guidance of our corrupt and
perverse nature. For we would like to measure divine justice by the foot and
standard of our own conceptions. When we do not immediately perceive in his
unsearchable ways and judgments the equity that our own reason would dictate
to us, we can scarcely keep ourselves from protesting and murmuring against
God’s secret counsels. This is evident in the parable of the laborers hired by
the master of the house and sent into the vineyard in Matthew 20. But whenever
God’s purpose comes to mind, our mind ought to rest entirely and completely in
it and demand no further reason for things in which no equity otherwise
appears to us. We have a notable example of this in the apostolic council in
Acts 15. After much dispute and many arguments exchanged back and forth
concerning the calling of the Gentiles and their observance of the ancient
legal rites and commandments belonging to the instruction of the Israelite
people—some thought this yoke should be imposed on the Gentiles, while others
not only considered this unjust, absurd, and useless, but proved it by the
strongest arguments—James finally added this statement of the greatest
weight: “Known to God from all eternity are all his works.” By these words he
shows how great a reverence we ought to have for that eternal purpose of God,
both concerning the calling of the Gentiles and all other events that belong
to his secret providence. Setting aside all the curiosity of our flesh, we
must rest in it with submissive minds, fretting neither about this thing nor
that, nor curiously inquiring why he who is indebted to no one wished to call
these rather than those, or these before the others. Instead, we must take
care that we ourselves proceed without stumbling along the path marked out
and designated for us in his Word until we finally reach the goal set before
us.

[Marginalia: Matthew 20:1, etc. Acts 15:18. A very important note.]

Finally, although many things in life seem to happen to us by chance and at
random, and we see many people admitted into this inheritance of God’s
children by chance rather than by design—while, for example, they are occupied
with one thing, intending another, and even fiercely persecuting the gospel
itself, they happen, as it seems, to encounter people through whose work God
determined effectually to call them—we must nevertheless always acknowledge
in everything, and especially in the ways and means which divine providence
used to draw us onto the path of salvation, his eternal decree. Nothing in
reality happened either to us or to others randomly or by blind fortune, but
all these things came from his immutable purpose.

[Marginalia: Use 3.]

In the third place, when the Apostle describes God as “the one who effects all
things from the counsel of his will,” we learn from this not only how
effectual God’s counsel is, since divine providence always executes it in its
own time—not as an idle spectator of events, but as the decreer and director
of everything we see happening in the world, and its executor insofar as
these things possess the character of good—but we also gather a most
effective argument for restraining every outcry and murmur of our rebellious
nature whenever we see many things happen to us or our people not only beyond
our hope but even contrary to our will and desires. The God who works all
things, directs, procures, and executes everything, acts not only freely and
according to his supreme right, but wisely in everything he does. He decrees
nothing in the world except with supreme counsel and prudence. If we do not
rest in this with obedient minds, what else do we display but a disapproved
mind within ourselves?

[Marginalia: Observation 3.]

Finally, since he has destined us for the praise of his glory—in both the
senses I explained above—and has produced and set forth in us so rich and
fruitful a harvest of his praise, it will certainly be our part to serve his
glorious praise with the greatest zeal and so let our light shine before
people, according to our Savior’s command in Matthew 5, “so that, seeing our
good works, they may glorify our Father who is in heaven concerning us and on
our account.” Otherwise, if we lead a profane life conformed to this age and
our own desires, then, as far as it lies in us, we will certainly bring
reproach and dishonor to God rather than praise to his glory.

[Marginalia: Observation 4. Matthew 5:16.]

Let every Christian mind consider how great and how detestable a crime it is
to stain God’s glory, as it were, with the shame and disgrace of our sins. I
mean the glory of God, which God himself holds and has held from eternity so
dear, as the most precious of all things, that he rightly set it forth as the
supreme end of all his works, including human redemption in and through
Christ. Yet we seem to treat it as a matter of indifference, though it ought
to be so dear and precious to us throughout our lives that we should neither
conceive, contrive, or desire anything in our mind, nor express anything in
speech, commit anything to writing, or undertake and carry out anything in
deed, unless the praise of the divine glory is always set before our eyes as
the target at which we continually aim. We should pass our lives with the
disposition that we are prepared to undergo even the worst extremities before
we violate in word or deed the glory of such immeasurable grace—that is,
before we stain it with even the smallest blemish of sin.

[Marginalia: Emphatic amplification. Use 4.]

This serious meditation will be a very effective restraint upon every sin and
license, and a spur and stimulus to holiness, for devout minds kindled by
love, fear, and zeal for God. We should reflect among ourselves that every
evil must be avoided not only because it conflicts with the rectitude of
divine justice—that is, with his eternal law—but especially for this reason:
that in some measure it brings disgrace and dishonor upon the divine glory,
particularly when committed by those whom he has honored with his knowledge
through the glorious light of the gospel. The same cannot be said equally of
the sins and profane life of unbelievers. Conversely, we must pursue every
Christian virtue and holiness of life not only because it conforms to God’s
law and will, but especially because it serves to display the glory of his
grace. In this way he attains his own end in us, whom he placed and appointed
for this very purpose, and whom he has given life, understanding, judgment,
knowledge, and finally the saving light of the gospel, to this very end: that
we may exist and serve for the praise of his glory in Jesus Christ. To him,
with the Father and the Holy Spirit, be all honor and glory forever. Amen.
