On verse 8 and following: “which he caused to abound.”

[Marginalia: Lecture IX. Transition.]

There follows now in the next three verses the third part of the same
induction. In it the Apostle considers and sets forth those same spiritual
benefits—which God the Father both prepared for us from eternity and procured
for us in time through Christ’s death and blood, as we have heard thus
far—insofar as they are actually communicated or dispensed to us, so that we
become actual participants in them. This takes place first of all through
effectual calling. It is the first stage of the heavenly and saving blessings
insofar as they are actually applied to us and conferred by God’s grace. In
calling us, God the Father discloses his will to us, especially his will to
save us in and through Christ. He does so both by preparing us through the law
to recognize our misery and by publishing and setting forth in the gospel the
gracious covenant ratified by the precious blood of Christ the Mediator. The
publication of that new and gracious covenant is nothing other than the
preaching of the gospel, in which, in turn, the nature of external calling
consists.

[Marginalia: Calling is twofold.]

For it is agreed that calling is twofold. One is external, which in truth is
nothing other than the preaching of the gospel, whether it is made to
believers or unbelievers. The other is internal and consists in the inward
illumination of the mind, effectual knowledge, and that saving attraction and
persuasion of the heart. Through it, while the Holy Spirit works effectually
within, believers respond from the heart to that external call and embrace
the offered light of the gospel. This effectual calling of which we are
speaking embraces both of the aforesaid parts: the external preaching of the
gospel and the internal action of the Holy Spirit in illuminating the minds
and drawing the hearts of the faithful.

[Marginalia: Proposition.]

The Apostle treats this in the three verses now before us. I mean that first
stage in the dispensation of our salvation by which those saving benefits,
namely Christ crucified together with righteousness and eternal life, are
applied and actually communicated to us through effectual calling. The
Apostle accordingly embraces both of the aforesaid parts of this calling in
these verses, and does so with the utmost gravity, richness, and majesty of
language, in order to commend all the more to the faithful the greatness of
the divine benefit and grace in the revelation of the gospel, although the
Christian faith might appear mere folly and madness to the unsound judgment
of unbelieving people.

[Marginalia: Exegetical division. 1. Faith. 2. The preaching of the gospel.
3. The exposition of the gospel.]

Although in the work of effectual calling the inward illumination of the Holy
Spirit is customarily joined with the preaching of the gospel itself—since
the Holy Spirit uses the latter to produce faith in our hearts—so that it
seems exceedingly difficult to distinguish these two parts of effectual
calling in the Apostle’s words (for both the Wisdom and Understanding with
which God is said to have caused abundance toward us, and the revelation of
the mystery of the divine will, can be understood of internal illumination as
well as external preaching of the gospel, as is evident to anyone who
considers the matter), nevertheless, for the sake of a more precise account,
we shall refer this first verse, verse 8, to internal calling, by which God is
pleased to awaken faith in our souls through the efficacy of his Spirit. We
shall understand the next verse, verse 9, as concerning external calling,
which contains the instrumental cause of faith, namely the revelation of the
gospel. He explains that mystery of the gospel more clearly in the following
verse, verse 10. Let us therefore consider these three members in order.

[Marginalia: Exegesis.]

First, let us consider how magnificently the Apostle describes faith produced
in our hearts by the power of the Holy Spirit, beginning from its first part,
namely the illumination of the mind. He connects this part with the preceding
one in this way: “which he caused to abound,” and so forth—that is, the rich
grace which “he caused to abound” (namely God the Father, to whom we referred
the pronoun “his” placed immediately before) “toward us” (that is, us who have
been effectually called and gifted with faith in Jesus Christ, and not only
chosen from eternity for life and predestined for adoption as sons, but also
lavishly heaped up in time with every spiritual blessing), “in all wisdom and
understanding,” or prudence, as others take the word *phronēseōs*. That is,
with wisdom of every kind—namely spiritual and heavenly wisdom, as he
indicates this wisdom in Colossians 1 both by an epithet and by distinction,
in order to show what kind of wisdom he is discussing. It is not that carnal
and worldly wisdom in which the wise of this age and the Gentile philosophers
outside Christ glory and take pride; nor that in which those haughty and
arrogant Pharisees and other hypocrites of that kind once trusted; nor,
finally, the “knowledge falsely so called” which he names in 1 Timothy 6, in
comparison with which the saving mystery of Christ seemed contemptible both
to unbelieving and conceited Jews and Gentiles. It was of this wisdom that he
quoted in 1 Corinthians 1 what was written in Isaiah 29: “I will destroy the
wisdom of the wise and remove the understanding of the understanding.” It is
instead wisdom that is spiritual, wholly directed toward eternal salvation,
and wholly leading to that heavenly life.

[Marginalia: Colossians 1:9. 1 Timothy 6:20. 1 Corinthians 1:19. Isaiah
29:14.]

Since the verb *eperisseusen* must be referred to God himself and not to
*charitos*, so that we avoid the evident break in construction on which the
sentence in the next verse would otherwise be left hanging, it should be
noted in passing that the verb is used transitively here, as if it had been
put in the Hebrew Hiphil conjugation. It is as if he had said, “which he
caused to abound toward us.” We see the Apostle use the same form of
construction with the same verb in 2 Corinthians 9: *dynatos de ho theos
pasan charin perisseusai eis hymas*—“But God is able to cause all grace to
abound toward you.” Here the verb, which is otherwise absolute and
intransitive, is taken actively in a Hiphil sense. There are some who assign
it a reflexive action, as if the reflexive pronoun *heauton* were understood,
in this fashion: “God abounded toward us,” that is, “he poured himself out
lavishly upon us.” This arrives at almost the same result as the preceding
interpretation, but the former explanation is simpler.

[Marginalia: Subdivision.]

Three things, therefore, present themselves for treatment in this eighth
clause, in which the Apostle sets before us and describes saving faith from
the internal illumination of the Holy Spirit. First is the benefit itself;
second, the quality and greatness of that benefit; third, how it is conferred
upon and dispensed to us.

[Marginalia: Wisdom and Understanding.]

As to the first, this gift of faith, or internal illumination by the Holy
Spirit which takes place through effectual calling, is designated by the
names Wisdom and Understanding. By the name Wisdom everyone without dispute
understands that knowledge which concerns God, Christ the Mediator, God’s
will toward us, and the other mysteries of our salvation revealed in God’s
word, especially by the preaching of the gospel. The Apostle himself will
teach us this in the next verse. Indeed, even by the testimony of the Gentile
philosophers themselves, Wisdom is said principally to concern knowledge of
the most excellent things, that is, of God himself and divine things—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 70 | Caput I p. 28]

—so to what knowledge will this name apply sooner or more properly than to
the knowledge which the Son of God himself, who is in the bosom of his Father,
has explained and revealed to us in the gospel?

[Marginalia: *Phronēsis*.]

There is not the same agreement among everyone about the word *phronēseōs*.
Some interpret it as Understanding, in precisely the same sense as the term
*synesin* which the Apostle uses in Colossians 1; Beza is among them. Others
explain it as prudence, as does the old common interpreter and not a few of
the more recent interpreters. These explain Prudence as that Christian virtue
by which we rightly employ our knowledge of God and divine things and turn it
to saving use in life, rather than abusing it toward vice, negligence, or
carnal security, but instead applying ourselves to holy work and actions.
For Christian doctrine is not only contemplative but also practical. It
therefore requires not only wisdom, which is a *theōrētikos* disposition of
the mind and a purely contemplative virtue, but also prudence, a practical
and moral virtue directed toward action and occupied in correcting and
shaping life and conduct.

[Marginalia: Bernard.]

They therefore rightly hold that the Apostle joined *phronēsin* to Wisdom,
because, as Bernard once said, only that person rightly hears the word of God
who turns words into deeds—that is, one who is not content with bare
contemplation but orders his life according to the prescription of the
divine Word.

[Marginalia: Correction. Distinction by objects.]

But because these interpreters do not distinguish Prudence from Wisdom
precisely enough with respect to the objects of each, for the sake of a
clearer account we shall try to illustrate their position in such a way that,
while retaining with Dr. Beza the term Understanding, we nevertheless
understand almost the same thing as they do. Not everything disclosed to us
in the divine Word is of the same kind, nor is it revealed for the same end.
Some things are set before us only to be known and believed, while others are
also to be done, imitated, and expressed in life and conduct. It is evident
that the things of the former kind turn within and are contained by the
bounds and limits of contemplation, while things of the latter kind descend
to practice and action.

[Marginalia: Application.]

Just as, therefore, I remember noting above that there are two parts of
Christian doctrine—one concerning faith or things to be believed and the
other concerning good works or things to be done—so also, in accordance with
the diversity of these objects, I would distinguish from one another these
two virtues, calling them Wisdom and Prudence, or *phronēsin*, or, if you
prefer, *synesin* and Understanding. I would refer Wisdom to things of the
former kind, which are set before us to be believed and known, and
Understanding or *synesin* to things of the latter kind, which concern life
and conduct, action, and the duties of a Christian person.

[Marginalia: *Ethics* 6, chapter 10. Common definition.]

Aristotle himself proceeds in this manner. He was the most expert evaluator
of what terms of this kind mean and signify in their proper usage in his own
language among natural men. He weighs them not on the common balance but on a
goldsmith’s scale, especially in book 6 of the *Nicomachean Ethics*. He finds
no great difference between *phronēsin* and *synesin*, since he holds that
both deal with the same object, namely actions which lead to practical
happiness. *Euboulia*, that is, the capacity to deliberate rightly, also
deals with them. But *phronēsin* is more a *dynamin epitaktikēn*, that is, a
power which arranges, orders, and commands, while *synesin* appears more a
*dynamis kritikē*, that is, the faculty by which we judge right from wrong
and are able to discern and distinguish harmful things from beneficial ones.
Both must concur in ordering life rightly: both that discerning virtue by
which we judge and distinguish right and wrong, and the virtue which rightly
orders and arranges our life so that it agrees with its proposed end. Thus,
in its own way, the common definition of prudence can apply both to
*phronēsis* and to *synesis*: it is an *aretē tō logistikō*, a virtue of the
practical intellect, *paraskeuastikē tōn pros tēn eudaimonian
synteinontōn*, that is, one which prepares the means conducive to happiness.

[Marginalia: Examples. Correction.]

What those who explain *phronēsin* here as prudence said above—namely, that
through it we adapt our knowledge of those heavenly things to use, that is,
to holiness of life—must therefore be understood rightly and accepted in a
sound sense, specifically of knowledge of those heavenly things which
properly concern life and conduct. Otherwise, for example, I do not see how
we could put to practical use the mysteries of this divine Wisdom, such as
the most holy Trinity, Christ’s incarnation and the hypostatic union of his
two natures, the decree of eternal Election, and other such things. I admit
that saving knowledge of these things can spur me to live well and holily,
for example when I meditate and contemplate God’s eternal decree or the
future Judgment. In the same way love and reverence grow from the knowledge
of God and Christ, and a new zeal for obedience and holiness grows—who would
deny it? But this is nothing other than to say that holiness of heart and
affections and zeal for good works are both born and nourished from faith,
and consequently that divine Wisdom is the mother and parent of Prudence or
this spiritual Understanding and of the practical virtues which depend upon
it, such as Love, Piety, Temperance, Patience, Humility, Vigilance, Sobriety,
and so forth.

Nevertheless, these virtues—Wisdom, I mean, and Christian Prudence or this
spiritual Understanding—remain distinct in their own bounds and objects.
Understanding knows, judges, orders, and arranges things to be done, and
directs and applies all its knowledge to action. Wisdom, however, is content
to contemplate divine things and does not further direct or extend the
knowledge of those matters to action. Such are the mysteries brought forward
above as examples, which are set before us in God’s word not as things to be
done, performed, or imitated, but as things to be known, contemplated, and
believed. Yet, as we have already acknowledged, we by no means deny that from
the knowledge and saving faith by which we believe and apprehend the truth of
those matters there can be born, nourished, and increased trust in God’s
promises, love of God and neighbor, filial fear and reverence of God, hatred
of sin, zeal for righteousness and holiness, and other practical virtues of
this kind pertaining to that spiritual Prudence. The Apostle has thus
described for us that saving gift of faith arising from the inward
illumination of the Holy Spirit which makes us wise and understanding, or
prudent, in these divine matters.

[Marginalia: Concerning the second member, that is, the greatness of grace.]

As for the second point, the measure or greatness of this gift is denoted by
the Apostle in two expressions: both by the verb “to abound” and by the added
mark of universality, when he calls it “all wisdom and understanding,” that
is, wisdom and understanding of every kind and in every form concerning
spiritual matters pertaining to salvation. By these forms of speech he
suggests that in the revelation of the gospel the faithful lacked nothing
which could be required for true and saving Wisdom. For although he had
imparted to the fathers and prophets—

[BSB bsb11059161 | scan 71 | Caput I p. 29 (through Lecture IX close)]

—under the Old Testament some of this same wisdom “in many parts and in many
ways,” he did so more sparingly and piece by piece, insofar as he knew what
suited the character of those times and judged expedient for the capacity of
that people. But upon the faithful living under the grace of Jesus Christ he
as it were poured out all that heavenly wisdom all together and at once in
the gospel through Jesus Christ and his Apostles.

[Marginalia: Exposition.]

In order to understand this more correctly, it must first be maintained that
in that first fountain and wellspring of all good there is wisdom wholly
infinite and inexhaustible: in God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—in that
most holy and glorious Trinity, I say. Next, the eternal Son of God,
personally united with Christ’s human nature, communicated to the nature he
assumed wisdom which is indeed finite in itself. That nature is not and
cannot be capable of that eternal and infinite wisdom which is essential to
the Godhead itself. For how can something finite and created contain within
itself something infinite, uncreated, and incommunicable to a creature, but
as proper to the Creator himself as it is proper for him to be God from
eternity, and as his own Godhead and essence are proper to him? Yet he
communicated wisdom of such a kind as far surpasses the wisdom of all created
things, whether heavenly or earthly. Hence it is said even of Christ as man
in John 3 that “the Spirit was given to him without measure,” and in
Colossians 2:3 that even in him as man “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom
and knowledge,” although I am aware that some explain that passage
differently.

[Marginalia: John 3:34. Colossians 2:3. Accommodation.]

Finally, Christ the Mediator, the *Theanthrōpos* united to us, imparts wisdom
to us just as he does the other spiritual benefits. It is not as great as the
wisdom he has in himself, but it is wisdom abundantly sufficient for us to
have that saving knowledge of God and of the divine will. It not only far
surpasses all the wisdom which our blind nature can possess, even in the most
resourceful and acute natural men, but also possesses that heavenly light in
a far greater degree and abundance than the wisdom which once illuminated
those ancient fathers who lived under the former Tabernacle. This is the
wisdom which the Apostle means in this passage. Although it is perfect and
whole in itself, complete in all its parts, nevertheless we, who are not yet
capable of its entire perfection because remnants of our natural darkness
remain, must acknowledge that it is still imperfect in us. For “we know only
in part and still prophesy only in part,” as 1 Corinthians 13 says. It will
finally be perfected plainly and fully in the life to come.

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 13:9. Points to be noted.]

No one, therefore, should imagine from this abundant supply of every kind of
saving wisdom with which Christ the Most High and Most Great has been pleased
to illuminate us in his gospel that an absolute perfection, complete in
every part, exists in this life. For the Apostle has in view partly the ages
which have passed, in which that divine wisdom was more sparing and
restricted even among the faithful, still meager, obscure, and wrapped in the
shadows of the law by comparison with that noonday sun of the gospel. He also
has in view the condition of the present life and adapts his discourse to
this state of ours in a mortal body. His purpose is to show that in this
mortal life we should neither seek nor expect wisdom greater than that which
has been revealed and offered to us in Christ’s gospel. For that wisdom is
abundantly sufficient to disclose fully to us the divine will to save us in
Christ, to understand the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven as far as the
capacity of this life and body permits, to contemplate, embrace, and love
Christ by faith, and finally to attain salvation perfectly.

We infer this clearly enough from these two arguments. First, at the beginning
of the First Epistle to the Corinthians Paul said that the Corinthians were
richly endowed in Christ in every respect, in all utterance and all
knowledge, so that they lacked no gift. He immediately added, “as you wait for
our Lord Jesus Christ to be revealed.” It is as if he were saying that he
understands the perfection of the gifts not as absolute in every respect, but
as suited to the present condition in which we walk by faith, live by faith
and not by sight, and are blessed and saved in hope, not in the full act of
possession. We are furnished with that abundance of spiritual goods which
suffices as provisions for the traveler, not as a triumph for the victor.
Second, later in this very chapter he formulates prayers for the Ephesians to
this end: that they be endowed by God the Father with the Spirit of revelation
and wisdom, and so forth—that is, that day by day they advance more and more
in knowledge of the same saving mysteries. There would be no place for such
wishes and prayers if there were no room for progress, but there is no room
for progress where absolute and consummate perfection already reigns.

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 1:5, 7. Concerning the third member, namely the way
in which that grace is conferred upon us.]

Now, to touch upon that third member, the manner in which that abundance of
spiritual wisdom and understanding flowed toward us is designated by the
relative pronoun “which.” We have already said that this pronoun refers to
that same rich grace which was mentioned near the end of the immediately
preceding verse. Since it is in reality the same as that glorious grace to
whose praise he said above that we were adopted as sons through Christ Jesus,
I cannot here understand it, as some do, as the grace of calling itself,
whether external or internal. Rather, I understand the antecedent cause of
that calling: that same love and gracious goodwill of God with which he
regards us in Christ and has regarded us from eternity. For it is the fountain
and origin of all those benefits which are freely conferred on us in Christ.
Upon this grace depend our Election itself and Predestination to eternal life.
From this very grace flowed the immense price of our Redemption—I mean
Christ’s blood. From this same grace the price paid for us is imputed to us.
By it we are reckoned as sons and accepted in the one uniquely Beloved. By
this same grace we have been consecrated in that effectual calling with all
spiritual wisdom and understanding.

This, then, is the fruitful parent and fountain of every good,
Whose unpurchased streams flow toward us without end,
Sent down through Christ the head.

Let this suffice concerning the first part of this benefit embraced in this
clause: namely internal calling, or saving faith awakened within us by the
Holy Spirit as he illuminates our minds.
