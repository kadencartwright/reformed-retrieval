## Part XXXVII: “Make Yourself Predestined”

[Part II | PDF page 203 | logical source scan 202 | printed page 201 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

Finally, concerning the statement, “If you are not predestined, make yourself predestined,” who does not wonder that Andreae could produce what he knows was not taken from the genuine Augustine but drawn from Pelagius’s most impure pools and still today most shamelessly offered us to drink by the Semi-Pelagians? Who does not see that it renders everyone’s predestination uncertain until the final breath and thus entirely overthrows the one foundation of our hope [ἐλπιδοφορία]? When will anyone determine that he has caused himself to become predestined from not being predestined? Presumably after he has fulfilled the law—that is, after he has dreamed he fulfilled it. Since faith rests upon God’s grace alone in Christ and is given only to those predestined and called according to purpose (Romans 8:28; Ephesians 1:5), how can faith be the efficient cause of predestination when it is predestination’s effect? Moreover, if by acting we obtain predestination, it will certainly be given according to either foreseen or present merits. The apostle, on the contrary, testifies that we were elected and called not because we already were or would be holy, but so that we might become holy; and election belongs not to one who wills or runs but to God who has mercy, as the ancient Augustine observes a thousand times against Pelagius.

And I ask, what will it mean not to be predestined? It will mean to occupy a certain middle condition suspended from human will and works, since it lies within the person’s discretion either to be predestined or not. Let all who employ with Andreae as true this opinion—which is certainly not Augustinian but was powerfully opposed by Augustine even in entire books—therefore openly desert to the Pelagians. But if one wishes to soften the statement and recall it to the standard of orthodox faith defended by Augustine, it will not oppose us at all. For it must then be referred not to God himself but to us, and not to the decree conceived in God’s bosom from eternity but to our perception and acknowledgment of it as revealed to us in the gospel. Thus Peter warns us to make our election and calling sure—not in God, who rests upon himself alone, but in us as we grow in faith and bring our sanctification to holiness in the fear of the Lord (2 Corinthians 7:1), according to 1 John 3:10: “By this the children of God and the children of the devil are manifest: whoever does not practice righteousness is not from God, and whoever does not love his brother.”

Have we taught that the certainty of our predestination—

— should be sought in God’s hidden decree rather than in God’s Word, which sounds in our ears through human ministry, knocks on and opens our heart through the Holy Spirit, and indeed insinuates itself into it? Nor do we say one must descend from predestination to the intermediate causes. On the contrary, especially in the gravest temptations, the godly must ascend from effects—namely sanctification or God’s fruits—to faith; from faith to justification; from justification to calling according to purpose; and finally from this to the eternal and immutable purpose of predestination. From this the sure hope of our glorification, which never confounds—indeed, an immovable bearing of hope [ἐλπιδοφορία]—is to be cherished in our souls, following Paul’s example from Romans 8:31 to the end. But discussing the doctrine of predestination itself is one thing; discussing its use and application is another. Those who confuse these entirely distinct questions cast great darkness upon this treatment.
