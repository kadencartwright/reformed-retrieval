## Part XXIX: The Absolute Decree and Reprobation

[Part II | PDF page 189 | logical source scan 188 | printed page 187 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

If Andreae calls “absolute” a decree to whose execution no causes are subordinated, so that the reprobate are condemned not because God finds in them just causes of condemnation but simply because he pleases, then this man fabricates for himself a monster which we have never dreamed of. But if he thus names that eternal decree to whose execution intermediate causes inhering in the reprobate themselves are subordinated, and whose cause is God’s will alone, then he not only fights the apostle, who asserts that very will in clear and plain words, and indeed a will which cannot be resisted, but certainly seems not very far removed from that reprobate mind—

[Part II | PDF page 190 | logical source scan 189 | printed page 188]

—into which those who stubbornly resist truth finally fall. Who is more insane than one who seeks something higher than God’s eternal will, by which he created all things as, when, and of what kind he willed and governs them, ordered by that same will? Speaking about this, our Augustine, whom I cited above, says in book 1, chapter 2 of *On Genesis against the Manichees*: “When it is asked why God did so, the answer must be, ‘Because he willed it.’ If you proceed to ask why he willed it, you seek something greater and higher than God’s will, which cannot be found.” And in Letter 209 to Boniface: “Although God did not make sins, who but God created the very natures which in themselves are undoubtedly good, yet in which through the discretion of the will the vices of sins would occur, and among the evil such vices as deserved eternal punishment? Why, except because he willed? But why he willed—O man, who are you to answer back to God?”

A certain scholastic of no mean standing writes on this matter: “Just as God did not predestine his elect because he foresaw that they would obey, but predestined them so that they would obey and believe from his gratuitous movement, so he reprobated the rest not because he foresaw they would be rebellious, but reprobated them by not willing to have mercy upon and draw them in the way he knew suitable for their obedience.” And again: “No one is predestined because he will obey; rather, he will obey because he is predestined to obedience. Likewise, although disobedience and rebellion are meritorious causes of eternal punishment, they are nevertheless not meritorious causes for which God permitted or permits the will of the disobedient and rebellious to fall into the faults of their own disobedience and rebellion.”

How the statement that God wills no one to perish must be understood Augustine declares in book 1, question 2 to Simplicianus: “Humans and sinners are the same persons for him, but they are humans by creation and sinners by their own will. God loves them as humans and hates them as sinners. God therefore does not hate Esau the human but Esau the sinner. Although God hates the sin of both Jacob and Esau, he does not hate it in the same way, since by pardon he takes away Jacob’s sin, as he judges must be done by those unsearchable judgments.” And in the same place: “Thus a judge hates theft in a person but does not hate the person’s being sent to prison; the thief does the former, the judge the latter. So God does not hate what he does when from the mass of the impious he makes vessels of destruction—that is, the work of his ordering—but the work of those perishing under deserved punishment, in which those on whom he has mercy may find an occasion of salvation.”

Finally, who among us has ever denied that contempt for the grace offered in Christ and for his Word is the cause of those rebels’ destruction? Yet unless explained by the distinction from Augustine recalled shortly before, it is not merely false but ridiculous to say that God does not will the destruction of those who scorn the gospel. And I ask, what is the point of all this? As I have pressed a thousand times, the cause of condemnation, which is known and plain, is one thing; the cause of the eternal destination to condemna—

—tion is another. One who scrutinizes the depth of that will seeks what cannot be found; its depth must be adored, not scrutinized. Lest Andreae complain that I have improperly extended the term “predestination” to the decree of reprobation against the opinion of some ancients and moderns—from whom I nevertheless differ nothing at all in substance—he should know this: although the causes of election and reprobation in the execution of the decree differ greatly, which those who applied the word “predestination” only to one member of this decree intended to signify, Augustine, who always diligently observed that distinction, nevertheless did not hesitate to write in chapter 100 of the *Enchiridion to Lawrence*—as the same immediacy of contraries [ἀμεσότης] which I discussed before also requires—that God justly predestined the evil to punishment and kindly predestined the good to salvation. In book 15, chapter 1 of *The City of God*, he writes that there are two societies of human beings, one predestined to reign eternally with God and the other to undergo eternal punishment with the devil. Andreae should also remember that Fulgentius rightly denied that sins are predestined and that death is from God, preserving the distinction we gave in its place between what God rightly does even through evil instruments and what they themselves cause; nevertheless Fulgentius titled the first of his books to Monimus *On God’s Double Predestination*, one of the good to glory and the other of the evil to punishment.
