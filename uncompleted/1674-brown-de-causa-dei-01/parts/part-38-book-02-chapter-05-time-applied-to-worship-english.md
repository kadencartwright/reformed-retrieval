CHAPTER V

Some General Principles of Time Applied to Religion or Divine Worship

Although a prudent reader may understand well enough without prompting what
use all the things said about time in the preceding chapter have or may have,
and how much they serve to explain and confirm our principal question about
the Sabbath, it will nevertheless be worthwhile to apply them also to religion
or divine worship, so that everything may be made as clear as possible.

*Time* applied to religion or divine worship is called either *common* or
*solemn*. *Common* time is that which necessarily accompanies religious acts
no less than other acts. *Solemn* time, however, is that which religious acts
more properly accompany, or which requires religious actions to be performed
in it.

This distinction is not in every respect the same as the preceding distinction
of time in relation to all actions, namely, into *indeterminate and determinate
time*. For although every indeterminate time is common, and every solemn time
is determinate, there is nevertheless in religion a certain determinate time
which should be called common rather than solemn: namely, that which is
determined solely by the religious act itself and is a mere natural and
physical circumstance of the action. It is considered physically, not morally
or ethically, and thus it is only common time. Therefore, by common time we
understand every time which is not solemn; and we mean by solemn time only
that which is antecedently determined by God or by human beings, either for
worship in general or for exercising some part of worship.

That common and indeterminate time is commanded by no special divine precept;
indeed, properly speaking, it cannot fall under a commandment of God. The
reason is that it is ridiculous to command that which necessarily accompanies
an action whether we will it or not. Common time is of this sort, for once the
action has been commanded,

[1674 logical printed p. 256 | IA leaf 274 | PDF p. 274]

this time too is commanded, and therefore no particular precept is needed.
Moreover, it is a contradiction in terms to say that time is commanded by God
and yet remains indeterminate. For a command always removes indeterminacy; once
the command is given, indeterminate time becomes determinate and solemn.

Therefore, when the adversaries deny that the Fourth Commandment commands any
determinate time in any respect—whether as to *how long*, *how often*, or
*when*—they are really saying that no time at all is commanded in that precept.
For indeterminate time falls under no commandment, since, as was said, it is
enjoined along with the commanded action and necessarily accompanies it.

Thus, although they sometimes acknowledge that there is a time for worship
which is *solemn, appointed, fixed, regulated, and sufficient*, they
nevertheless maintain that this time is nothing other than indeterminate time.
For they maintain that place and time are equally moral, and that this time is
a mere adjunct of worship and a mere circumstance, no more due to worship than
to any other action. But if this is true, such time will be no more commanded
in the Fourth Commandment than in any other commandment. For every command
which enjoins some duty upon human beings necessarily also enjoins the time in
which that duty is to be performed. For example, the Fifth Commandment
commands us to honor our parents and to do what they prescribe must be done;
but this can be done only in some time. It therefore necessarily—that is,
naturally—has time annexed to it, though indeterminate time. Hence it is clear
that this time is just as moral and commanded in the Fifth Commandment as the
Sabbath enjoined in the Fourth Commandment is moral according to the
adversaries. This is really nothing other than saying that neither is moral,
but that each is a mere physical circumstance commanded together with the
action which it accompanies.

Thomas indeed speaks in this way, *Summa theologiae* 2a2ae, question 122,
article 4, reply to objection 1: *The commandment concerning the sanctification
of the Sabbath, understood literally, is partly moral and partly ceremonial.
It is moral insofar as a person sets aside some time of his life to attend to
divine things. For there is in a person a natural inclination that some time be
assigned to each necessary thing, as to bodily refreshment, sleep, and other
things of this sort. Hence time is also assigned to spiritual refreshment,
because the human mind is refreshed in God, according to the dictate of
natural reason; and thus having some time assigned for attending to divine
things falls under the moral commandment.*

But 1. How can it be moral that we assign some time to divine things, when that
time necessarily accompanies the sacred action itself, whether we will it or
not, without any assignment? 2. I should like to know why this assignment of
time is commanded in the Fourth Commandment any more than in any other
commandment which enjoins a sacred action. 3. On this account it will also be
moral for a person to assign some place to divine worship, because worship
must occur in some place. 4. The morality of this commandment will therefore
be satisfied if we assign one day or one hour to divine worship in our entire
lifetime. Who does not see how absurd this is? 5. Since a person has no
stronger natural inclination to assign time to spiritual refreshment than to
bodily refreshment—indeed, a weaker one—it seems no more moral, if not less so,
that time be assigned to spiritual things than to

[1674 logical printed p. 257 | IA leaf 275 | PDF p. 275]

bodily things. 6. Therefore no time at all is determined by God in the Fourth
Commandment.

Hence it is clear that if the Fourth Commandment morally enjoins any time, the
time it enjoins must be determinate, not indeterminate. For indeterminate time
is no more commanded in this precept than in any other, as was said. And when
the adversaries deny that solemn or determinate time is morally enjoined in
this commandment, they actually deny that this commandment makes any special
command concerning time.

*Solemn and determinate religious time*, therefore, is that which necessarily
requires religious acts to be performed in it. Thus, by whomever and in
whatever manner it is determined, it necessarily requires sacred duties to be
performed in it according to the aspects determined. For example, when someone
determines the duration of solemn time, or *how long*, this requires sacred
duties to be performed throughout that whole determinate time—say, a day or an
hour—whether one duty or several, once or more often. That is, throughout that
whole time we must either pray, meditate, read the Scriptures, hear sermons,
and so forth, or perform all or some of these in turn, once or repeatedly.
Similarly, when *frequency*, or *how often*, is determined, it is required that
as often as that time recurs—say, once every week or three times every day—we
give ourselves wholly to the foregoing or similar exercises. Finally, when the
season itself, the opportunity, or *when*, is determined—say, the first or last
day of the week, or the sixth or tenth hour of the day—it is then required that
we attend to sacred things precisely on that day or at that hour, whether the
worship then required is indefinite or definite, as when the law required the
Passover to be celebrated precisely on the fourteenth day of the first month.

Every antecedently determined time, therefore, is solemn and more than common,
even if the determination concerns only one aspect and is loose. Insofar as the
determination extends to more or fewer aspects, and is looser or stricter, the
solemnity of the time is increased or decreased accordingly. Thus the
determination of two aspects makes time more solemn than the determination of
one, and the determination of all makes time more solemn still than the
determination of two. Likewise, strict determination, whether of one, several,
or all the aspects, causes greater solemnity than loose determination.
Conclusive determination makes time more solemn still than initial
determination. Finally, exclusive determination, when it can be admitted
either generally or in particular, makes time most solemn of all.

It should further be noted that, just as indeterminate and common time
naturally adheres to religious actions because those duties cannot be
performed except in time, so sacred duties, at least indeterminate ones,
accompany solemn time, because solemn time cannot be spent religiously except
by performing some religious duties. Thus, theologically, acts of divine
worship are as necessary for spending a solemn day religiously as common time
is physically necessary for doing any work. Consequently, once a legitimate
determination has been made—and every determination made by God is such—we
would sin if we did not perform religious duties in proportion to the
determination of time made, whether only with respect to *continuation*, only
with respect to *frequent recurrence*,

[1674 logical printed p. 258 | IA leaf 276 | PDF p. 276]

with respect to the *season itself and when*, or with respect to all these
aspects.

Nevertheless, when a dispensation concerning certain necessary and, so to
speak, interrupting duties has been granted by divine authority, either when
the determination was made or afterward, those necessary and most urgent
things may be done without any scruple or violation of solemn time. The
determination made remains firm and always retains its force with respect to
everything else. For just as the determination made does not prevent a
dispensation from being given for doing certain most urgent things which
cannot be omitted without serious inconvenience, so this dispensation, which
has place only insofar as those necessary and unomissible things press upon us,
does not remove the force of the determination with respect to the remainder.
Its force remains unshaken, just as if no dispensation at all had been granted.

Furthermore—to say something also about indeterminate time—in accordance with
what was said above, it must be maintained that a continuous quantity of time,
even though indeterminate but applied to religion by a person's own choice, is
of great benefit to religion, that is, it is highly useful for advancing God's
glory and the good of our souls, if it is longer; and, conversely, is no small
hindrance if it is shorter. For without doubt the person who assigns a longer
time to sacred things celebrates God's glory more and advances his own
salvation more, other things being equal, than one who assigns a shorter time
to that business. The same must be said of *frequency*, for it is certain that
the person who spends time in divine exercises more often glorifies God more
than one who does so more rarely. The same must be said of both these aspects
of time jointly. For without doubt the person who assigns a longer time, and
does so more often, to divine exercises advances much more than one who
adheres to these duties more rarely and for a shorter time. He advances his
knowledge of divine things more; fixes their memory more firmly in his mind;
moves his affections more toward God and divine things; subdues corruptions
more; and thereby advances mortification and vivification more. He increases
his spiritual consolation, because in those exercises he cultivates a closer,
longer, and more frequent communion with God.

Moreover, the person who is occupied both longer at a time and more frequently
in sacred exercises shows that he pursues God and God's glory, and his own
salvation, with a more ardent love than one who does the contrary. For the
person who willingly spends more time on sacred things, and does so more often,
undoubtedly loves those exercises. Conversely, the person who, though hindered
by no obstacle, willingly spends little or no time on this exercise and is
occupied in God's worship as rarely as possible does not love them.

Similarly, we may conclude that the person who gives himself more often to
religious exercises and persists in them longer advances much more in piety,
other things being equal. Conversely, the person who devotes a shorter time to
these exercises and engages in them less frequently advances less.

From this it is clear that the continuous *duration* of time, or its *length
and frequency*, even though indeterminate, is of the greatest importance even
in religious actions, and that they are circumstances which are, by their
nature, so to speak, substantial.

But as for *when*, or the indeterminate beginning of time, the matter stands
very differently. For from this aspect of time, indeterminate and considered
in itself, with no account taken of any accompanying circumstances, no benefit

[1674 logical printed p. 259 | IA leaf 277 | PDF p. 277]

arises for religion. God's glory or our salvation is advanced no more by this
aspect of time, nor can a person's affection toward religion be demonstrated
from it. For someone may undertake a religious work at this hour rather than
another either from affection, because he judges this hour especially
opportune, or, conversely, from no affection, but rather because he wishes to
be relieved of this burden so that he may apply himself to other things sooner,
which he judges can conveniently be done if he undertakes God's worship at
this hour and not another. Nor can we infer from this how little or how much a
person advances in religion and true piety, as is clear.

There may nevertheless be certain accompanying circumstances on account of
which this aspect of time will be of greater importance for advancing piety.
For example, this is so if an occasion is selected in which a person is better
disposed to perform sacred duties, in which he will have more assistance at
hand from elsewhere, in which a special opportunity will be given to perform
some particular religious act, and in which the *continuation and frequency*
of time, or *how long and how often*, can be impeded less. The beginning, or
the *when* of time, will then also promote the performance of religious
exercises if it is taken at a point when we are more fit and disposed to
perform sacred duties, when greater assistance is to be expected from
elsewhere, when there will be an opportunity to perform some particular
religious work, and when no obstacle will be placed in the way of either the
length or the frequency of the time. Conversely, those exercises and piety
will be impeded if the beginning is taken at a point when we are less fit and
disposed to perform sacred duties, when less assistance is to be expected from
elsewhere, when an opportunity to perform some particular religious work will
be lost, and when some obstacle will be placed in the way both of the length
and of the frequency of the time.
