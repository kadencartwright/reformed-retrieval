The summary is a comparison of riches with other things in order to lower the
value of riches.

The parts are the comparison of riches with reputation and with death.

VERSE 1. A good name is better for a person than the best ointment; but the
day of death is better than the day on which he is born.

After Solomon had considered riches in themselves and in their own nature, he
at last lowers them by making a comparison with other things, so as to bring
back to moderation the judgment of those who think too magnificently of them.
For God, who knows all things, has fully perceived the insane love with which
mortals are desperately enamored of riches, and for that very reason, as in
the most stubborn diseases, he applies by far the most powerful medicine.

By the ointment to which he prefers reputation, therefore, Solomon designates
every kind of wealth by a synecdoche of the part, since ointment holds the
first place among riches. This is plainly established from the history of
King Hezekiah, Isaiah 39, where excellent ointment is numbered among the most
precious things that Hezekiah kept in his treasury and is grouped together
with silver, gold, and spices. This is also confirmed by Solomon, Proverbs
22:1, where reputation, or good favor, is preferred to gold and silver—that
is, to the most excellent riches.

Nor should just any name and reputation be understood when reputation is
compared with riches. For the reputation obtained from riches is more fleeting
than riches themselves; the same must be understood of reputation acquired
from the summit of honor. I therefore understand reputation in this passage
to mean that which is gained by illustrious deeds performed in war or peace
and from which some benefit has flowed to the commonwealth. Although this
reputation is shaken in many ways because it is variable and inconstant, it
is judged preferable to riches. For those who are ennobled by illustrious
deeds are exposed to the envy of many, who look upon this splendor with
resentful eyes. Moreover, great fickleness exists among people: it causes
them, once they have changed their opinion, soon to condemn what today they
approve and what most people do.

Then, among the very people who became renowned and celebrated because of
illustrious deeds, the course of conduct is not always the same and even.
Whatever renown they gained by the greatest deeds in youth, they lose as
their age declines through idleness or some other blemish. This is all the
more so inasmuch as a little foolishness makes a man great in wisdom and glory
stink, just as a dead fly makes precious ointment stink. Ecclesiastes 9:21.

We must therefore be stirred to pursue a good reputation rather than either
riches or pleasure. This does not mean that we should hope to be so well
spoken of that no one disparages us or contrives a slander. It is enough if
our detractors are convicted by the testimony of their own

conscience and we are sustained by the testimony of a good conscience. It
follows from this that those who injure an honorable reputation sin more
gravely than those who are led to the cross for stripping us of our goods.

But if reputation, which is fleeting and unstable, is to be preferred to
riches, it follows that riches are still more uncertain and unstable. Yet a
rather difficult question arises here: how is it fitting that reputation,
which belongs to the ninth commandment, should be preferred to riches, which
are included under the eighth commandment? For just as each commandment comes
earlier in order, so it makes the good included within its scope more
excellent, or the evil forbidden in it worse. If, therefore, it is a greater
evil to take someone's riches than to violate his reputation and name, it
follows that riches are to be preferred to reputation in goodness.

I reply that not every good under one and the same commandment is preferred to
each and every good included under the commandment that comes later in order.
Rather, the greatest goods must be compared with the greatest, intermediate
goods with intermediate, and the lowest with the lowest. The same principle
holds for the things condemned as wrong. For example, a reputation arising
from riches is not to be preferred to riches, nor a reputation for strength
or speed to those physical powers themselves. Thus far concerning the former
part. The excellence of death over riches must be treated next.

Here a new doubt springs up: why does Solomon prefer the day of death to the
day of birth? For to demonstrate the excellence of death over riches, it
might seem that he ought to say that death is more excellent than riches, not
what is actually said—that the day of death should be chosen over the day of
birth. Yet in this form of expression the Prophet has not departed at all
from his proposed aim. Rather, as frequently happens in Scripture, he pursues
the very point he proposes together with the beneficial effect of another
excellent doctrine.

First, it can be established from the following that he adheres to his
proposed subject: since death, which cuts off every use of riches from human
beings, is more excellent than birth, which supplies the beginning and entry
to the use of riches, it follows that death must be preferred to riches. At
the same time an outstanding lesson is also formed, that death must be
preferred to birth.

Whether this proposition should be understood indiscriminately of the death
of all people, both good and evil, is difficult to explain. There is no doubt
at all about the death of the pious and righteous, which is followed by the
highest blessedness and prepares a passage to eternal life—or rather, is
itself the passage to life. But the death of the wicked, which is accompanied
by the second death and hell itself, is not free from doubt. Yet when one
weighs the matter in equal scales, this too will be clear: the death of the
wicked must be preferred to their birth.

For as regards the present life, birth lays open an approach to many miseries.
At birth, like sailors casting off, we are carried onto the deep, while death
imposes an end and a barrier and leads us from the deep, as it were, into
port. By the former we descend into the valley of misery; by the latter we
ascend the mountain of rest and tranquillity. As regards the future life, it
is truly said of all the wicked what Christ affirmed of Judas the traitor:
it would have been good for them if they had never been born. As regards
their death, although that second death follows immediately, death is still a
lesser evil than the prolongation of life. For as they extend their days in
this life, drawing out the thread of impiety, they bring a more severe
judgment upon themselves. Thus it may truly be said of them that the best
thing would have been not to be born, and the next best to die as quickly as
possible.

Conversely, the birth of the pious is blessed because it lays open for them
the way to their calling, but death is far more blessed because it sends them
into possession of eternal life. This should not be understood as though we
agreed with the opinion of the Thracians,
