[Marginal heading: 4. From the inseparability of the gift of the Son and the
gift of the Holy Spirit.]

XXIV. Fourth, from the *inseparability of the gift of the Son and the gift of
the Holy Spirit*. Since these two most excellent gifts were given to us by God
for salvation and are repeatedly joined in Scripture as cause and effect (John
16:7; Gal. 4:4, 6; Rom. 8:9; 1 John 3:24), they must have the same extent and
proceed together. The Son was not given to acquire salvation for any but those
to whom the Spirit is given to apply it, nor can any reason be given why the
gift of the Son should extend more widely than the gift of the Spirit. But it
is agreed that the Spirit is not given to everyone, only to the elect.
Therefore, the Son was not to present himself as Surety for any others, so
that there might be harmony between the work of the Son and that of the Holy
Spirit. Here also belongs

[Printed p. 409 | Internet Archive PDF 437 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

Paul’s argument, which infers the conferral of all saving benefits from the
gift of Christ: “He who did not spare his own Son, but handed him over for us
all, how will he not also freely give us all things with him?” (Rom. 8:32).
The reasoning is from the greater to the lesser: he who gives the Son, which
is undoubtedly greater, how would he not give faith and all other saving
gifts, which are lesser? This is all the more so because, as we are about to
prove, by handing himself over for us Christ merited all these gifts together
with salvation. We must therefore conclude either that the reprobate are given
all things with Christ, if he died for them, or, if these are not given—as
everyone agrees—then Christ was not handed over for them either. The knot
cannot be untied by saying this concerns a special handing over made for
believers. Besides gratuitously assuming that there is a universal handing
over of Christ, as stated above, faith is a fruit of his death and so cannot be
placed before it as an antecedent condition. Add that according to the order
accepted by the learned men, the decree of Christ’s death precedes the decree
to give faith. It therefore cannot be conceived how in the same single and
most simple act he could have been handed over both for everyone and for only
some.

[Marginal heading: 5. From Christ’s supreme love.]

XXV. Fifth, from *Christ’s supreme love*. Christ loved those for whom he died
so greatly that no greater love can be given. “No one,” Christ says, “has
greater love than to lay down his life for his friends” (John 15:13). Paul
likewise commends this love as something altogether wonderful and unheard of
among people: “Hardly anyone will die for a righteous man, though perhaps
someone might dare to die for a good man; but God commends his love in that
while we were sinners, Christ died for us” (Rom. 5:7–8). Yet this cannot be
said of each and every person, since everyone agrees that Peter was loved by
Christ more than Judas. Nor can we conceive how he could be said to have loved
supremely those whom, as a severe Judge, he had already handed over to Satan
to be tormented eternally, or was going to hand over by an irrevocable decree.
It cannot be said that this concerns external acts of love. Besides the fact
that external acts presuppose internal ones, if Christ performed for each and
every person this external act than which no greater can be given, it follows
that Christ can furnish nothing more for the elect than for the reprobate—an
obvious absurdity. Nor does Christ’s loving some of the elect more than others
with respect to the Spirit’s internal gifts, whose diversity is required for
the perfection of his mystical body, prevent laying down his life for both
from being the highest degree of love with which he pursued them.

[Marginal heading: 6. From the nature of suretyship.]

XXVI. Sixth, the same is inferred from the *nature of Christ’s suretyship*.
As stated above and soundly proved by our theologians against the Socinians,
this entails Christ’s substitution in our place: he died not only for our
benefit but in our place. By his suretyship he transferred to himself the
whole debt of those whose person he sustained, took it away from them, and
completely erased it as his own, no less than if those sinners themselves had
rendered the payment. It is therefore inconceivable how those for whom he died
and satisfied in this manner could still be subjected to the eternal curse and
again obligated to bear their deserved punishments. Yet this must be said if
he died for any but the elect, though it openly conflicts with God’s good faith
and justice. If by this suretyship the debt was transferred to Christ and
erased, everyone sees that it was so taken from the original debtors that its
payment can no longer be required from them, but they remain free and
discharged from every future punishment. Here belong the passages asserting
that our sins were so transferred to Christ that the chastisement of our peace

was placed upon him and healing came to us from his wounds (Isa. 53:5–6), and
that he was made sin and a curse for us so that we might be the righteousness
and blessing of God in him (2 Cor. 5:21; Gal. 3:13).

[Marginal heading: 7. Because he procures salvation only for those to whom he
applies it.]

XXVII. Seventh, Christ died only for those for whom he procured salvation and
to whom he applies what was procured. But he procures and applies salvation
only for the elect. Therefore, he died for them alone. The major premise rests
on two foundations: 1. God appointed Christ’s death for us to procure
salvation. 2. Procurement cannot be separated from application, because the
end of procurement can be nothing other than application, and something that
would never be applied would be procured in vain. It follows that if salvation
was procured for everyone, it must likewise be applied to everyone; and if it
is not applied to everyone but only to the elect, it was not procured for
everyone either, but only for the elect. It is answered to no purpose: 1.
Christ’s death was appointed not so much to procure salvation as to remove the
obstacle of justice that stood in the way of God’s considering our salvation.
For it would follow that Christ acquired for us not redemption and salvation
so much as only the possibility of salvation and the Father’s power to
establish a new covenant of grace with people. This was long ago condemned in
the Remonstrants as insulting to the power of Christ’s cross and the efficacy
of his suretyship. How, then, could he be said to have given himself as an
ἀντίλυτρον for us, to obtain eternal salvation for us, to redeem us from all
iniquity, to give us eternal life, and other such things, which establish that
Christ procured for us not merely the possibility of salvation but salvation
itself?

XXVIII. 2. It is no better to say: “Christ procured redemption for everyone
with the end that it be applied to everyone, provided they did not reject it.”
This cannot be said with respect to the countless people to whom Christ was
never offered or even known by name. Moreover, Christ would thus have proposed
to himself a futile end concerning something that not only would not occur,
but could not occur without his gift, which nevertheless he did not will to
bestow upon them. It would be as though Christ’s counsel were: “I will to
procure redemption for everyone so that it may be applied to everyone,
provided they believe. Yet I will neither reveal this redemption to countless
people nor give countless others to whom it is revealed the condition without
which it can never be applied to them. That is, I will something to occur
which I not only know will not and cannot occur, but which I also do not will
to occur, because I do not will to communicate what depends on me alone and
without which it can never occur.” If this is unbecoming to a wise person, how
much less to the supremely wise and good Christ? Even if the failure of this
application occurs not by Christ’s fault but incidentally through people’s
wickedness and unbelief, it does not cease to be insulting to Christ, as if he
either did not foresee it or could not prevent it, with the result that the
procurement is in vain and does not attain its end. The answer that it is not
in vain does not remove the absurdity. They say that although it lacks success,
it was not done rashly and without purpose, because however people conduct
themselves, Christ attained by his death what he chiefly regarded: pardon and
salvation are now prepared for every person, provided he believes and repents,
whereas the inexorable rigor of divine justice formerly prevented this. Yet
because application can be the only end of procurement, what fails to attain
this end cannot but be done rashly. Christ was not to die merely so that pardon
and salvation might be prepared for people under a condition they cannot
fulfill, but so that they would actually obtain pardon and salvation.

XXIX. This is still more strongly confirmed by the *mode of procurement*. If
it extends to each and every person, it is either absolute or conditional. It
cannot be the former,

[Printed p. 411 | Internet Archive PDF 439 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

because then each and every person would undoubtedly be saved; nor can it be
the latter. 1. Conditional procurement would not properly be procurement, but
only a bare possibility of procurement if the condition were fulfilled. 2.
Either Christ acquired that condition for everyone or only for some. If he
acquired it for everyone, then everyone must actually share in salvation,
because such a condition could only be acquired absolutely and without a
condition, and thus conferred upon everyone. There is no condition of a
condition; otherwise the matter would proceed to infinity. Nor can any
condition be given which would not coincide with the thing to be acquired. If
he acquired it only for some, the procurement is not full and sufficient,
since it lacks its most necessary part. Further, it would be empty and
illusory, made under a condition that cannot be fulfilled and never will be,
which God not only foresees will not occur but which he who alone can give it
has himself decreed not to give. Finally, Christ is thus imagined to have had a
twofold intention in satisfying: first a conditional intention for everyone,
and then an absolute intention for the elect. This can never be proved from
Scripture or reconciled with the one most simple decree concerning Christ’s
death.
