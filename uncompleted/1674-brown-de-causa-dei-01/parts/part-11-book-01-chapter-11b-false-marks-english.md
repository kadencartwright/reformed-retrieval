Third, in order to escape the force of this objection—that the adversaries
readily resort to blind nature as their guide and to a glowworm, so that by its
dim light they may see what their own duty is—they devise another indication
of *Moral Laws*, namely: “Only that is a Moral Precept which is ratified in the
Gospel, confirmed, and declared to be such.” Thus Dr. *White* in the place
cited above.

But: 1. If the authors of this assertion understand a general Ratification,
they will prove nothing against the Sabbath. For when the whole Decalogue, or
the whole Moral Law, is ratified in the Gospel, the commandment concerning the
Sabbath is also confirmed, unless the adversaries show us a peremptory
exception of the Sabbath.

Next: 2. If they understand a particular Ratification, so that the meaning is
that nothing is Moral unless it is specifically and expressly ratified in the
Gospel, then many Judicial laws in which there was nothing ceremonial or
anything peculiar to the Jews will not now be moral, because no mention of
them occurs in the New Testament. Next, is marriage within all the degrees
prohibited both directly and by consequence in Leviticus 18 and 20 expressly
prohibited? Further, even those who now propose this characteristic of the
Moral Law acknowledge that some proportion of time is commanded in the fourth
Precept and is moral. In what part of the Gospel, however, do they read that
this particular proportion, whatever it may be, is confirmed and ratified?

Moreover: 3. When Christ and the Apostles make mention of laws of the Old
Testament and, as the adversaries would have it, confirm them, for the most
part nothing of this kind appears. There is no such ratification which
supposes that the law in question had lost all force of binding and now
receives it again, or would lose it unless it were confirmed or enacted anew.
For in the same passages it is apparent that Laws of the Old Testament are
mentioned as laws remaining in force. Hence they very often confirm their
teaching by those laws, as may be seen in Matthew 19, where Christ confirms his
judgment concerning rash divorce by the text of Genesis 2. We also see the
same thing done very often by the Apostles.

Consequently: 4. From this it is apparent that the Old Testament is part of
our canon and binds us just as it did the Jews to perform or avoid the things
commanded or prohibited there, unless it is otherwise evident that those Old
Testament precepts have been abrogated.

And: 5. This assertion would favor the *Anabaptists* and *Antinomians*, who
assert that we have been freed from obligation to the Moral Law, so that it is
no longer a rule of practice or a standard of faith and morals, or any part of
our standard.

Does not: 6. On this reasoning the authority of the Legislator who prescribed
a Law for the Church under the Old Testament get taken away, if a law once
given to the Church now binds no longer, even though it is nowhere evident
that it has been abrogated or has lost its force of binding?

For: 7. It can be asked, what is the reason for this change? Or how does it
come about that this law does not now bind us? Is it because it was enacted
only for the Jews? Yet those who deny that the Law, insofar as it was enacted
by *Moses*, now binds Christians nevertheless concede that the same law binds
us by force of its matter. Later we will prove that the Moral law, even
insofar as it was enacted by *Moses*, binds us to render obedience. Is it
because it was to endure only until the coming of Christ? How, I ask, will
this be proved? And how, then, would it differ from the ceremonial law?

But: 8. If this law was abrogated at Christ’s coming, it would follow that the
Gospel

[1674 logical printed p. 129 | IA leaf 147 | PDF p. 147]

Church, for many years after Christ’s death, was destitute of every moral law
except the pure Natural law, and had no canon of Scripture to regulate its
practice either toward God or toward its neighbor until, namely, the complete
New Testament had been written. But this is absurd.

Fourth, others intimate that “only those things should be called Moral which,
even if they had been commanded by no will, would nevertheless be established
by their own nature and would have to be observed from natural reason.” For
they say that *Aristotle*—from whom this term first took its origin when he
called his teaching concerning morals περὶ τῶν ἠθῶν, “concerning morals,”
ἠθικὴν, “ethical”—so distinguishes the
Natural from the Legal that it corresponds precisely to the distinction
between Moral and Positive Right itself. And they say that the scholastics
borrowed the term from him in the same sense. To this end they cite, among
others, *Vásquez*, part 2 on question 99, article 5, who says: “I indeed think
that what Cajetan teaches is true: that those precepts of the old Law which
are called moral were such that, even if they had been commanded by no will,
they would nevertheless be established by their own nature.” They also cite
*Medina* on the same place, who says: “Moral Precepts are those which in
themselves pertain to good morals. Hence, even if they had never been
established by any Law, they must be observed from natural reason and are
therefore said to pertain to the law of nature.” See Dr. Burmann’s *Apology*,
page 12.

But what does “would be established” mean? Would they be equally morally good
as they now are? We demonstrated above in chapter 3 that this cannot be. Or
would human beings be bound to do and avoid the same things which they are now
bound to do or avoid by the expressed will of God? How, I ask, can this be?
Can anyone be bound to perform some duty and not be bound through any law? When
something must be observed from Natural Reason, is it commanded by no will of
God? Is the Law of Nature no law and no precept of the divine will? These
things seem strange and scarcely compatible. But perhaps by “some Law” or
“will” they understand that precept or law insofar as it had been promulgated
by *Moses*. If so, however, they speak quite improperly and ambiguously and say
nothing to the point. See what we said just above in response to the first
mark.

Fifth, there are some among the adversaries who commonly say that “Moral
things are distinguished from Positive things” (for they do not wish any
positive things to be called moral, the contrary of which we will demonstrate
later) “in this: that the latter are called decrees, while the former are the
sort of things which, if a person does them, he will live in them. They bring
forward Ezekiel 20:11, 13, and 21 for this purpose, and thus declare that by
Moral Precepts they understand only precepts which enjoin those things which,
if a person does them, he will live in them.” See Dr. *Burmann* in the place
cited above, page 31.

It is not opportune at present to call to the anvil everything which must be
examined here. This will be done more suitably later in its own place. Here it
will suffice to note these few things.

First, I would like to know whether they think that these words used by
*Ezekiel* are anything other than a mere repetition of the words of *Moses* in
Leviticus 18:5.

Next, do they think that in these words God proposes the formula of the
covenant of works to the end that anyone should expect life and eternal
salvation according to the tenor of the covenant of works, and in no other
way? If so, it must then be said that God made a covenant of works, and no
other, with the Israelites at Mount *Sinai*. Yet this is utterly false, and
they themselves deny it.

Further: 3. It would follow from this that nothing is now Moral except that by
which life

[1674 logical printed p. 130 | IA leaf 148 | PDF p. 148]

can be attained. But we, just as the Israelites formerly, cannot be justified
by good works or attain eternal life, not even if we were perfectly to perform
all the duties
of the moral law—which is nevertheless impossible. Therefore we will now have
no Moral Law as a rule of faith and morals.

Moreover: 4. If they understand the formula of the covenant of works here,
what shall we say about faith in Christ the Mediator? The adversaries
themselves do not want the precept of faith to be a decree. For when the very
celebrated *Essenius* said in his *Vindications*, page 36, “Does he think that
life cannot be obtained through things which belong to God’s free good
pleasure? What, then, of the Gospel and that saving grace of God through faith
in Christ? Is it not from God’s free good pleasure? Is life not obtained in
this way?” Dr. *Burmann*, in his *Apology*, pages 30–31, answered as follows:
“What do I hear? Is the precept of faith a δόγμα, ‘decree’? Because it was, of
course, God’s εὐδοκία, ‘good pleasure,’ to hand Christ over for us. Indeed, it
is a matter of eternal truth that, once a mediator has been given, the sinner
is saved by faith in him. It was just and necessary, and God could command
nothing else,” and so forth. This is not the place to call these things to
examination. It is sufficient that we see the adversary here vehemently
affirming that faith in the Mediator is not a decree but must be numbered
among the things which, if a person does them, he will live in them. But does
faith in the Mediator pertain to the covenant of works? Was *Adam* before the
fall bound to believe in the Mediator? Then the fall existed before the fall,
which is false and absurd. Nevertheless, on page 32 the adversary himself
affirms that those precepts, “which, if a person does them, he will live in
them,” pertain to the covenant of works.

Moreover: 5. If these words are taken as the Adversary wishes them to be
taken—namely, insofar as they express the sum or formula of the covenant of
works—then among the things “which, if a person does them, he will live in
them” must be numbered some things which flow from God’s pure good pleasure.
For not eating from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil was among the
things which, if a person did them, he would live in them, as will be evident
later. Yet this prohibition did not pertain to the Moral Law and flowed from
nothing other than God’s pure good pleasure; it must be numbered among the
δόγματα, “decrees,” if we must speak with the adversaries.

Moreover: 6. The things which “if a person does them, he will live in them”
are called חקה and משפטים in both Leviticus 18:5 and Ezekiel 20:11, 13, 16,
19, 21, and 24. Yet by these words Moral Precepts alone are not understood,
for חקה or חקים does not signify Moral Precepts when it is joined with משפטים
or other synonyms, as here, but rather Ceremonial precepts, as the Rabbis note.
Isaac Casaubon observes this in *Exercises against Baronius*, exercise 16,
section 43, saying: “The most learned among the Rabbis observe that the
difference between מצות and חקים is that the reason for the former is evident,
as, for example, worshiping God—but they say the latter are statutes of things
whose reason is known to God alone, as circumcision and similar things.” And
it is well known that the Rabbis call the Law of Nature משפטים and מצות without
an addition, because it binds perpetually and is recognized by the Light of
Nature. Hence they call it מצות הדעת, “Precepts of Knowledge,” and because the
rectitude of this law is manifest it is called דברים המישרים, “words of
rectitude.” But the adversary says that they are mistaken: “For precepts,” he
says, “of every kind are called חקים ומשפטים, which is usually translated
‘statutes and judgments’; see Ezekiel 5:25 and 20:11, 13, 16, 19, 21, 24, 25;
Malachi 4:4. For the same precepts are called חקים, ‘definitions,’ ὁρισμοί,
because they have a defined measure” (for חק properly signifies a defined and
fixed measure), “so that they admit neither addition nor subtraction; and
משפטים, ‘Judgments,’ because they provide a canon and rule of

[1674 logical printed p. 131 | IA leaf 149 | PDF p. 149]

judgment and have a judgment of justification or condemnation annexed to
them. Thus these terms are not peculiar to the Ceremonial and Judicial Law, as
many think, but also belong to the Moral Law—indeed, to every law.” Response:
1. That etymological reasoning does not prove that these words denote the
Moral Law more than the Ceremonial and Judicial Law. For nothing could be
added to or subtracted from the Ceremonies according to human judgment
either. And indeed people were justified more through the Ceremonies properly
employed—that is, by faith in Christ foreshadowed by those types, according to
God’s prescription and the end of that institution—than through Moral
precepts. 2. Even if we granted that all these words, when placed separately,
signify every or any Law, how will the adversary prove to us that when these
words are placed together or coupled with one another they signify only one
Law or one kind of Law, or that חקים joined with another term does not signify
the Ceremonial Law? He cites Ezekiel 20:11 and following, but the question is
already being raised concerning this very passage. He further cites Ezekiel
5:25. But there is a typographical error here, and I do not know where to look
for that passage. In verse 15 of that chapter there is indeed the word שפטים,
which, just as it differs from משפטים, signifies no law at all there but
matters of a judicial kind. As for Malachi 4:4, it is certain that the
Ceremonial and Judicial law is denoted there by those words, because they are
distinguished from the law, תורה, of which mention is also made there. Thus
the prophet specifically commemorates all the laws enacted through *Moses*:
the *Moral* laws, which the word “law” denotes, and the *Ceremonial* and
*Judicial* laws. 3. Above we cited many passages in which the Holy Spirit
designated distinct kinds of laws by these distinct words. We also confess
that sometimes every law of *Moses* is denoted by two words, and it is possible
that every law may be signified by one of them placed by itself. But we ask for
an instance where these Two words placed together denote only the Moral law,
to the exclusion of both the Ceremonial and Judicial laws. See also Leviticus
18:4 compared with verse 3, that I may add no more.

Therefore, whether by חקים ומשפטים we understand the Moral and Ceremonial Law,
the Moral and Judicial Law, the Ceremonial and Judicial Law, or all of them,
the result will be the same. For among the things “which, if a person does
them, he will live in them” there will always be numbered some things which are
not Moral, or which depend on the divine will. *Beza*, on Ephesians 2:15 and
Colossians 2:14, notes that the word δόγματα—by which our adversaries
understand things which flow from God’s mere good pleasure—corresponds to the
Hebrew word חקים. Hence, according to *Beza*, it is evident that the word חקים
does not denote the Moral Law but those laws which the adversaries wish to be
called δόγματα. And from this it follows by good consequence that some decrees
must be numbered among the things which, if a person does them, he will live in
them.

But further: 7. Why is the Sabbath not to be numbered among these things
“which, if a person does them, he will live in them,” since observance of the
Sabbath also pertains to the covenant of works and was given formerly to
*Adam* in innocence, as we will demonstrate in its place? “The end for which
the Sabbath was given,” Dr. *Burmann* says in his *Apology*, pages 31–32, “is
different from the end of the precepts which a person does,” and so forth.
“For these were given so that those who walked in them would be holy; the
Sabbath was given as a sign that God sanctified them. And thus it would be a
type of rest to be given at some point, when God had accomplished in Christ
what was necessary for sanctifying God himself who sanctifies them. Therefore
the Sabbath differs from those precepts, since even the precepts which, if a
person does them, he will live in them pertain to the covenant of works,
according to the Apostle, while the Sabbaths are

[1674 logical printed p. 132 | IA leaf 150 | PDF p. 150]

signs of sanctification, which is not from the covenant of works, since it is
the sanctification of impure people. How can these precepts, in which a person
lives, and the signs of such sanctification be the same?” Response: 1. If the
Sabbath is not to be numbered among the things in which a person lives because
it is a sign of sanctification and sanctification is of impure people, then
faith in Christ will not be numbered among these things either, because faith
connotes sin no less—indeed, more—insofar as it is the condition of our
justification than the Sabbath connotes impurity insofar as it is a sign of
sanctification. 2. Then, as it seems, the meaning of their words, “he will live
in them,” is that a person walking in them will be holy. But will a person who
sanctifies or observes the Sabbath not also be holy, no less than one who
abstains from homicide, fornication, theft, and so forth? Next, the words “so
that those who walked in them would be holy” imply that they would not be holy
unless they walked in them, and that these precepts were given to this end,
that they might be or become holy by walking in them. Thus they suppose the
people already to be impure. Hence it follows that these precepts pertain to
the covenant of works no more than observance of the Sabbath does. For he will
not say that the meaning of these words is, “These things were given to the
end that those walking in them might remain holy,” as if they were already
endowed with full holiness, as *Adam* was. Since, therefore, these things also
presuppose impurity, how, I ask, will they pertain to the covenant of works
according to our adversary’s teaching? 3. He says the Sabbath is a type because
it is called a “sign” here, as if every sign were a type. Does he think faith
or the mutual love of Christians and similar things are a type? 4. He says the
Sabbath was given so that it would be “a type of rest to be given at some
point.” Yet the text says it was given as a sign by which it would be known
that God sanctified them, כי אני יהוה מקדשכם, “for I am Jehovah who sanctifies
you,” even then. Therefore it speaks of the sign of a present thing, not of a
type of a future thing. This is clearer from verse 20: “Sanctify my Sabbaths,
that they may be a sign between me and you, that it may be known that I,
Jehovah, am your God.” What then? Was he only going to be their God in the
future? Were the Sabbaths signs or a type that Jehovah would at some point be
their God in the future? What sober person would say this? 5. He says that it
is a type of rest. But does the text speak of rest? Does the text say that the
Sabbath is even a “sign of rest,” much less a “type of rest”? Does the text, I
ask, say the Sabbath is a “type of rest to be given”? In what clause, I ask,
will we find these things stated? 6. But when will that rest be given? “When,”
he says, “God had accomplished in Christ what was necessary for sanctifying
them—indeed, for sanctifying God himself who sanctifies them.” Therefore this
rest was not to be given before Christ, at least before he was born. But what
rest, then, was to be given to those who had died long before Christ’s coming?
Shall we invent some limbo of the fathers from which faithful Jews were at
last to be freed when Christ was born? 7. But what was that necessary thing to
be accomplished in Christ for sanctifying Jews who had died long before
Christ was born? Are dead people capable of sanctification? 8. And what is
this: “indeed, for sanctifying God”? Was the Sabbath instituted as a type of
the Sanctification of God himself—and of a future sanctification, not a present
one? 9. As for the question, “How can these precepts, in which a person lives,
and signs of such sanctification be the same?” it will not be easy to answer
before he explains to us what he understands by such sanctification. But it is
easy to demonstrate and perceive how those precepts, in which a person walking
will be holy, can be a sign and indication that God is the God of people of
this kind or their sanctifier.

[1674 logical printed p. 133 | IA leaf 151 | PDF p. 151]

The same author says in the same work, page 31, that the precept concerning
the Sabbath cannot be among the precepts “which, if a person does them, he
will live in them.” And he adds this reason: “For if it were among them, the
Sabbath would not have been given only at last to the Israelites. Yet it was
given neither to Noah, nor Abraham, nor Jacob, nor to anyone thereafter; and
they were not ἱερόσυλοι, ‘sacrilegious,’ as the fathers constantly say against
the Jews. See Tertullian, book *Against the Jews*, chapter 2. Let him teach,”
he says, “that in time past the just observed the Sabbath and in this way were
made friends of God.” Yet he himself introduces “Adam not observing the
Sabbath in Paradise; likewise Abel, Noah, Enoch, Melchizedek, and Abraham not
observing the Sabbath.” This indeed is true, because nothing else can be
proved from the Scriptures. Response: 1. We assert, and will prove later, that
the precept concerning the Sabbath was also given to *Noah, Abraham, and
Jacob*, because it had been given to *Adam*. Thus it is rightly referred to
the things “which, if a person does them, he will live in them”—that is, to
Moral matters, to speak with the adversary. 2. Whatever the fathers contending
against the Jews may say about observance of the Sabbath, they do not say that
the words of Genesis 2:3 do not contain the institution of the Sabbath. Indeed,
the adversaries cite none of them who explains that passage differently from
us or even explains his own mind concerning that passage. But if those Fathers
had held the same opinion as our adversaries, the shortest way of refuting the
Jews would have been to demonstrate that this passage of Genesis says nothing
about the Sabbath’s first institution. For so long as they left this untouched,
nothing else can be concluded from their statements except that those godly
Patriarchs were imperfect and sinners and neglected their duty. It is therefore
far more probable that they spoke only of the manner of sanctifying the Sabbath
received among the Jews. Indeed, *Tertullian* himself, book 4 *Against Marcion*,
chapter 12, says that “Christ makes the Sabbath day, holy from the beginning by
the Father’s blessing, holier by his own blessing.” Thus he plainly testifies
that the Sabbath day had been made holy from the beginning by the Father’s
blessing. Indeed, *Theodoret* also, whom the adversary cites for himself on
Ezekiel 20, in his *Questions on Genesis*—Walæus cites this in his
*Dissertation on the Fourth Precept*, chapter 3, page 44 and following, where
he also adduces others—testifies that the Sabbath was instituted from the
beginning. He says that by the leisure of the seventh Day God taught that
creation was complete and had also enclosed the entire cycle of days in seven
days. He further adds: “Then, lest anyone think that the seventh day was
maimed and superfluous because God did no work on it, just as some are
accustomed to reckon idle days inferior, lest anything of this kind should be
thought about the seventh day he added,” and God blessed the seventh day and
sanctified it, “by which he demonstrated that he did not consider that day
useless for creating, but established it as suited to rest.” More will be said
about this objection later.

He further urges that the Sabbath is here distinguished from those precepts
“which, if a person does them, he will live in them,” because “Ezekiel says,
‘and also my Sabbaths,’ וגם את. This pronoun-particle denotes accession and the
conjunction of distinct things, although it quite plainly intimates that both
were given in the same discourse and in almost the same tenor, namely, in the
Decalogue.” Response: It is conceded that this particle signifies accession,
since *Buxtorf*, from Rabbi *Jehoshua*, says, כל גם לרבות, “every גם comes to
augment”; and this can be proved by very many instances. But it is not so
plain that it always denotes the conjunction of distinct things, as is evident
from Genesis 30:15; 27:33, 45; 50:18; 1 Samuel 1:6; Psalm 14:3; and especially
Exodus 10:25–26, וגם מקננו, “and also our livestock,” which are not distinct
from the victims and burnt offerings mentioned above in verse 25. 2. The
adversary supposes that the

[1674 logical printed p. 134 | IA leaf 152 | PDF p. 152]

Prophet here speaks only about the moral law, or the Decalogue, the
improbability of which is evident from what was said before. But: 3. Let it be
so. Then the precept concerning the Sabbath, which was given in the same
discourse and in almost the same tenor, namely in the Decalogue, will be one
of the things “which, if a person does them, he will live in them.” Thus this
will not be a conjunction of distinct genera of precepts, but only of
different precepts belonging to the same kind of law. Is not the name of a
genus often joined by a conjunction to the name of a species, and are not all
the other things of that genus then understood by the name of the genus,
apart from the species expressly named?

He proceeds and says that he is not ignorant of this: “But,” he says, “a
person who applies it to this passage asks to be granted that the Sabbaths are
comprehended under the precepts ‘which, if a person does them, he will live in
them,’ or else he is obstinate in demanding proof that it is not so. And he
does this in a passage where God is acting so as to distinguish precepts from
precepts.” Response: A person who demanded this would demand only what is
equitable. Nevertheless, we do not so much demand this here as demonstrate
that the adversaries cannot prove the contrary from this passage. For if the
Sabbath as a species is comprehended under the term “precept” as a genus, who
does not see that what the adversary says we demand here is entirely true? We
say that גם, “also,” by no means proves that the contrary is true. 2. We do not
yet see it proved that God is acting here so as to distinguish precepts from
precepts. Rather, he is acting to aggravate the sins of that people, who
despised God’s singular kindness and gracious dispensation toward them. They
held in contempt and violated the laws and statutes God had given them to
regulate their actions both as to morals and as to worship, so that by
rendering obedience to them they might be free from plagues and possess life
from God’s gratuitous gift. They even despised those statutes which openly
showed that Jehovah was their God, that he alone was the God who sanctified
them, and that rendering obedience to him was true holiness.

Let us nevertheless hear the Adversary’s proof. “For why otherwise,” he says,
“would he so often inculcate concerning certain precepts that the person who
did them would live in them, unless for their διάκρισιν, ‘distinction’? Verses
11, 12, 13, 16, 19, 20, 21, and 24.” Response: He does not so often inculcate
concerning any precepts that the person who did them would live in them,
although these verses are cited. It is said only three times at most. It is
true that when he speaks of statutes and judgments generally, he says that
they were things in which the person who did them would live; and concerning
the Sabbaths specifically he also adds that they would be a sign between God
and them, so that it might be known that God sanctified them and that Jehovah
was their God. This does not prove that the Sabbaths are totally distinguished
from those judgments and statutes, but that they have this peculiarity in
addition: that they were signs. It should also be noted that when he speaks of
the profanation of the Sabbaths, he adds this by way of explanation in verse
16: “that their heart followed their dung-gods.” So also verse 24. It is as if
by profanation of the Sabbaths he understood, or included within it, unlawful
worship, especially idolatry. This also seems plainly established by comparing
verses 18–20. “For I said,” he states, “to their children in the wilderness,
Do not walk in the statutes of your fathers.” What does he oppose to this?
“Walk in my statutes,” verse 19. Next he said, “Do not observe their laws.”
What did he require in their place? “Observe my laws and do them.” Third and
finally he said, “Do not pollute yourselves with their dung-gods.” What does he
now require in opposition to this in verse 20? “Sanctify my Sabbaths,” and so
forth. Thus by sanctification of the Sabbaths he understands, at least in part,
divine worship, which was performed especially on Sabbath days; and the
adversary will concede that at least some part of this worship was Moral.
Further,

[1674 logical printed p. 135 | IA leaf 153 | PDF p. 153]

when God through the Prophet recounts the plagues corresponding to their sins
which had been inflicted, he says in verse 25: “And I also gave them statutes
that were not good and laws by which they would not live.” But what does he
answer to the profanation of the Sabbaths? “Defiling them,” he says, “with
their own gifts, in that they made everything opening the womb pass through,
that I might make them desolate.” That is, he handed them over to their own
ways so that they would embrace the most abominable form of worship, even the
one which would make them desolate. And it should also be noted here in
passing that no one will say that this impurity, or most impure idolatry, is
not to be numbered among those statutes which “were not good and by which they
would not live.” Thus what was said above is explained, namely, that the
Sabbaths must also be referred to statutes and judgments, as a species to a
genus.

This can further be established from the fact that they are said to have
rebelled against God because of both the profanation of the Sabbath and their
contempt for God’s statutes, verses 13 and 21. And on account of both this and
that sin God said he would pour out his burning anger upon them in the
wilderness itself to consume them, verses 13 and 21; that with uplifted hand
he had sworn to them in the wilderness that he would not bring them into the
land which he had given, verse 15; and that he would scatter them among the
Nations and disperse them through the Regions, verse 23. But the adversary
says on page 30: “What is the consequence? Disobedience to that precept is
death; therefore obedience to it is life. Evil works merit death; therefore
good works merit life.” In a word: “We die from disobedience to a positive
precept, because the act of disobedience is transgression. But ἀδικία,
‘injustice,’ condemns. And where there is ἀδικία, there God and his goodness,
justice, and the way of justice are neglected. A person therefore dies for
violating a ceremonial law, not because of the object of that law itself and
its violation, but because of disobedience and violation of the divine
Majesty. Yet an act agreeing with a positive law does not therefore justify.”
Response: Is this consequence as good in decrees, as the adversaries like to
speak, as it is in moral matters: Disobedience to that precept is death;
therefore obedience to it is life? And is this consequence, Evil works merit
death; therefore good works merit life, equally false in moral matters as it
is in others? Where, then, is the difference? 2. We die from disobedience to a
moral precept no less than to a positive precept, because the act of
disobedience is a transgression; ἀδικία, “injustice,” condemns, and where there
is ἀδικία, there God, his goodness, justice, and the way of justice are
neglected. Where, then, is the difference? 3. A person dies, he says, for
violating a ceremonial law, not because of the object of that law and its
violation, but because of disobedience and violation of the divine Majesty.
But what does this mean: “not because of the violation of the object, but
because of disobedience”? Does someone who violates moral precepts die because
of the violation of the object and not because of disobedience? Would
*Abraham* have been guilty if he had sacrificed his son because he violated
the object? Is sin even in moral matters a transgression of law, ἀνομία,
“lawlessness,” and ἀδικία, “injustice”? Would it be a damning sin not to honor
Magistrates, to kill a person, to contract marriage within the now-prohibited
degrees, to marry several wives, and similar things, if God had prohibited
them by no written or innate law? If it were a damning sin to violate a moral
object even if there were no prohibition whatsoever, then it seems no command
of God could render this licit. Thus *Abraham* would have sinned in slaying his
son, and the Israelites sinned in carrying away the goods of the Egyptians,
notwithstanding the fact that God commanded both. See what we said above in
chapter 3. 4. He says that an act agreeing with a positive law therefore does
not justify. But does he believe that an act agreeing

[1674 logical printed p. 136 | IA leaf 154 | PDF p. 154]

with the moral law justifies? Then we are justified by works. And as far as
the covenant of works is concerned, *Adam* was justified as much by an act
agreeing with the positive law—namely, by not eating from the tree of the
knowledge of good and evil—as by an act agreeing with the moral law. 5. Will
the Adversary deny that God would not have punished the Israelites as we read
that he did because of the profanation of the Sabbaths if they had not
violated the Sabbaths? I do not think he will deny this. What then? Is it not
plain that they would equally have been justified by obedience to the precept
concerning the Sabbaths and by obedience to a moral precept? That is, would
they not equally have been delivered from the plagues God sent on account of
the violated law by obedience rendered to the command concerning the Sabbath
and by obedience to the other precepts? Where, then, is the difference? And I
hope that in this passage he will acknowledge that no other justification is
understood on account of obedience to moral precepts.

That the Sabbath is to be numbered among the things which, if a person does
them, he will live in them in this way is evident from other passages of
Scripture. See Isaiah 56:4–7: “For thus Jehovah speaks concerning the Eunuchs
who” (note well) “observe my Sabbaths, choose that in which I delight, and take
hold of my covenant: I will also give them within my house and within my walls
a place and a name better than sons and daughters; I will give each of them an
everlasting name which will not be cut off. As for the foreigners who join
themselves to Jehovah to minister to him and love the name of Jehovah, that
they may be his servants, everyone who” (note well) “observes the Sabbath so
as not to profane it, and those who take hold of my covenant: I will bring them
also to my holy mountain and make them joyful in my house of Prayer,” and so
forth. So also chapter 58:13–14: “If you turn your foot away from the Sabbath,
so that you do not do that in which you delight on my holy day, and call the
Sabbath a delight and the holy day of Jehovah honorable; and honor it, not
following your own ways, not doing that in which you delight, or speaking a
word; then you will delight yourself in Jehovah; and I will cause you to ride
upon the high places of the earth and cause you to enjoy the inheritance of
Jacob your Father, for the mouth of Jehovah has spoken.”

In answer to what we say—that abstaining from the tree of the knowledge of
good and evil belonged to the class of things which, if a person did them, he
would live in them, yet was a matter of good pleasure and not something moral
according to the adversary’s opinion—he responds on page 45: “Who revealed
that the human being would have lived through obedience to that precept, which
Scripture says nowhere? Rather, he would have lived through the image of God
and its preservation, which is contained in love, just as its expression is in
moral works which would then have had a place, when by creation the human
being was constituted under the covenant of works, as is well known.”
Response: 1. The one who revealed to us that the human being would die through
transgression of that precept revealed that he would live through obedience
to it. For Theologians teach that in that penal threat the opposite promise of
life is comprehended by synecdoche, because God had made the covenant of works
with *Adam*, by reason of which covenant life had been promised to obedience,
just as death was threatened to disobedience. Nor do I think the adversary
will say that the human being would have lived through moral works even if no
covenant had been made with him. For although it is true that the human being
would have been bound to obey God even if God had entered no covenant with
him, nevertheless *Adam* could not have expected as a reward the life promised
in the covenant on account of his obedience unless God had seen fit, by his
wondrous condescension, to make a covenant with him and in that covenant to
promise such a reward for obedience. (And even if

[1674 logical printed p. 137 | IA leaf 155 | PDF p. 155]

“the primeval human being,” *Apollonius* says in *On the Law of God*, chapter
3, pages 58–59, “had observed the natural law most perfectly, nevertheless God,
the human being’s creator, was not bound to promise or give him the reward of
eternal life. For the reward of eternal life had to be given to the primeval
human being not by ἀξίᾳ, ‘the worth,’ or dignity of works, but only by ἀξιώσει,
‘esteem,’ and divine condescension, rewarding beyond what is deserved, and by
a free covenant or liberal agreement. For by the first benefit of creation the
creature has nothing of its own which it does not owe to God the creator, and
therefore at the first moment of its nature it owes him all obedience. It is
also evident from the Scriptures” that the master does not thank that servant
who has done the things commanded; “and therefore, when a human being has done
all the things commanded him, he must say, ‘I am an unprofitable servant, for
I have done what I was obliged to do,’” Luke 17:9. “Likewise, ‘Who first gave
to him, and it will be repaid to him?’” Romans 11:35. “Likewise, when the Lord
promises and bestows a reward without proportion or valuation between the
work and the reward, he does what he wills with his own,” Matthew 20:25.
“Likewise, ‘If you are just, what do you give him? Or what does he receive from
your hand?’” Job 35:4. “My goodness does not extend to you,” Psalm 16:2.
“Consequently, every promise of life and reward is founded on the gratuitous
and liberal beneficence of God, by which the first benefit of creation is
augmented by the new benefit of that covenant obligation whereby God binds
himself to give the reward by covenant.” See further page 60. Moreover, the
*tree of life* is commonly regarded as a Symbol or Sacrament annexed to the
covenant, by which the promised reward—and thus what was due to obedience by
promise—was confirmed; or by which it was signified that God would grant
eternal life to human beings, if they persisted in obedience, as certainly as
he granted them the fruit of that tree for use. Part of this obedience was
abstinence from the forbidden fruit. And it is sufficiently well known that,
when this precept had been violated, God did not wish the human being to take
from the tree of life, Genesis 3:22, because, once the precept had been
violated, he had lost every right to life and therefore ought not to have
partaken of the sacrament of life.

He further adds: “Did that precept concerning this tree contribute to the
image of God?” Response: Some explanation was needed here so that what was
said could be better understood. For I do not sufficiently grasp what “to
contribute to the image of God” means or how a precept contributes to the
image of God. But it can be grasped how obedience rendered to the command
flows from the image of God in the human being, and it can be understood how
that image renders people ready and eager to obey God in all things. Nor can
it be denied that obedience rendered to that command was no less a fruit of
the image of God implanted in *Adam* than obedience rendered to any other
command would have been.

But he asks further: “Would it have remained in the future life, where life
will be at its fullest? It must have, if one was to live in it.” Response: From
this it is evident that, according to our adversary’s mind, nothing is to be
referred to the things which, if a person does them, he will live in them,
unless it will remain in the future life. Consequently, nothing can be called
moral which will not remain in the future life. And indeed, if this is how the
matter stands, it is no wonder that he denies that the precept concerning the
tree of the knowledge of good and evil is moral, and likewise the precept
concerning the Sabbath. For by this reasoning he will not only eliminate many
precepts of the second table but will also cast not a few of the first table
outside the number of moral precepts. For, to offer only a few instances, the
*fifth* precept concerning the mutual duties of Parents and Children, Masters
and Servants, and Magistrates and Subjects will have no place in the future
life; neither will the *sixth*, since everyone will be immortal; nor the
*seventh*, insofar as it concerns conjugal duties, for there will be no
marriage there,

[1674 logical printed p. 138 | IA leaf 156 | PDF p. 156]

as Christ testifies in Matthew 22:30. Nor will the *eighth*, for *mine and
yours* will have no place there, and consequently neither will the *tenth*.
Nor will the *ninth*, at least insofar as it concerns testimony to be given
before a Magistrate. Likewise, as regards the first table, there will be no
Vow there, no Oath, no Hope, no invocation, no Grief on account of offenses,
no faith in Christ, and similar things. Yet the adversary himself will not
deny that these things are now moral. Let him see, therefore, how he removes
the contradiction.

He responds secondly, saying: “Let us nevertheless say that *Adam* would have
lived through the precept concerning that tree, but not by force of that
precept and his obedience to it; rather, he would have lived by it as an
indication or proof of the moral and general obedience which by his very
nature he owed his Creator. For, as is well known, this precept was a Symbol
of the general obedience which the human being owed his creator, so that
although this precept was carnal as to the outward act, it was nevertheless
spiritual in its power. For obedience to it involved a constant love of God
which no temptations could overcome, and thus this one positive precept could
not have been fulfilled without obedience to the remaining natural precepts.
And from this came life to the human being, not from this positive precept.”
Response: Although this precept was added to test *Adam’s* obedience and to be
an indication of the general obedience which by his very nature he owed his
creator, how does it follow from this that *Adam* would not have lived by
force of that precept and his obedience to it, especially since he would have
lived by that precept? We know that he would not have lived by obedience to
that precept alone, for he was bound to observe the whole natural law. We know
that obedience to this precept involved a constant love of God which no
temptations could overcome, just as love of neighbor includes love of God. We
know that this one positive precept could not have been fulfilled without
obedience to the remaining natural precepts. But we do not know, nor do we see
it proved, that it follows from this that *Adam* would not have lived by force
of this precept and his obedience to it in the manner in which he would have
lived by force of some other natural precept, especially since this precept
was a Symbol of the general obedience which the human being owed his creator.

He further cites the words of *Tertullian*, book *Against the Jews*, chapter 2,
which do not injure our position in any way but rather confirm our opinion and
condemn what the Adversary said above. Tertullian says that this law—namely,
the law concerning the fruit of the tree, and so forth—would have been
sufficient for them if it had been kept, that all the precepts afterwards
given through *Moses* lay hidden within it, and that it was the matrix of all
precepts. Nor does *Ames* say anything against us in *The Marrow*, book 1,
chapter 10, section 29, whom he later cites, as is evident.

Third, he responds: “Hence also from transgression of that precept came the
general dominion of death. We saw above how weak an argument from the penalty
of disobedience to a contrary promise of life for obedience otherwise is.”
Response: We know that the general dominion of death came from transgression
of that precept, and what we answered above to those statements can also be
seen. We know that no good work of a human being, strictly speaking, merits
eternal life. We abominate that assertion of *Bellarmine*, who says that “a
good work without a covenant and without acceptance possesses a proportion to
eternal life.” But we have not yet read that *Adam* would not have received
the reward of life on account of obedience rendered to all God’s commands,
just as he would receive death on account of disobedience. Nor have we yet
learned that, once a legal covenant or

[1674 logical printed p. 139 | IA leaf 157 | PDF p. 157]

covenant of works is posited, in which God had promised life to obedience and
threatened death to disobedience, the argument from the penalty of disobedience
to the contrary promise of life for obedience is so weak. And even if it is
conceded that this argument is weak now in the corrupted state, it does not
therefore follow that it was weak in the state of integrity, in which the
reward was given from a modified debt—namely, through God’s free covenant and
agreement. And he himself had confessed above that “Do this and you will live”
was the tenor of the covenant of works. Enough has now been said about this
mark of morality.

Sixth, Dr. *Burmann*, in the place cited above, page 4, proposes another mark
of morality in these words: “Properly speaking, that is Moral which flows from
the image of God either concreated in the human being before the fall or
restored by grace after it; and thus that which results from the nature and
attributes of God and from the human being’s natural state under God. Whatever
agrees with and flows from these attributes of God and the human being is
properly morally good; but whatever is opposed to them is morally evil. Yet
whatever cannot be brought back to that image of God and does not flow from
it is positive. Although it too is fitting for God and the human being once it
has been prescribed to the latter by God, nevertheless it does not so flow
from the attributes of God and the human being that it is known of itself and
can be deduced from them, since it depends only on God’s positive will and
command.” Concerning this explanation of a moral precept, I would like to note
the following things.

1. Since the discussion here concerns a Moral Precept, I want to know how any
divine precept—for we are speaking here of divine precepts—can be said to flow
from the image of God concreated in the human being. Does this suppose that
something created—namely, that created or concreated image of God—is the
primary source of all divine moral laws? And does it not follow from this that
God is not the immediate cause of those Laws? Every law properly flows from
the Legislator, whose act it is; but that image of God is not the human being’s
legislator. Therefore I cannot understand how the moral law can be said to
flow from the image of God concreated or restored in the human being, although
I understand well enough how a human being endowed with that image knows and
perceives the divine law prescribed to him; how, by reason of this image, he
renders obedience to divine laws of every kind or is made fit to comply with
them; and, finally, how some law of God the legislator can be known objectively
from this image.

2. When “restored by grace” is added, I would like to know whether this is to
be understood of a restoration effected by force of the covenant of grace, in
which not everyone participates but only those endowed with saving grace; or
of a restoration or new inscription common to everyone, whether within or
outside the Church, and whether endowed with true grace or destitute of it. If
the *Latter* is meant, it will be the same as what *Flacius Illyricus* once
asserted: namely, that those common notions of right and wrong, and the
dictates of natural light, are not now in us by nature but were newly infused
into the human being by God after the fall. Yet this found favor with few, for
Theologians commonly say that these are remnants of the image of God left in
the human being after the fall. But I do not think the adversary holds this,
since he expressly intimated that even those sparks flow from the covenant of
grace, and thus that even all Gentiles participate in the goods procured by
Christ and exhibited in the covenant of grace. This must not be said; and in
his own *Apology*, page 5, he says that the human being always retains the
remnants of the image of God in the very nature of his mind.

[1674 logical printed p. 140 | IA leaf 158 | PDF p. 158]

I therefore judge it more probable that this is taken in the *former* sense.
But then who will not agree that it follows that we must not resort to the
natural light of the Gentiles in order to render a judgment from it concerning
natural or moral law? Indeed, who does not see how uncertain this rule is,
since not all who are renewed by grace perceive the same things and even the
best see only in part, 1 Corinthians 13:9? And indeed, if we had to stand here
by the opinion of the majority who are renewed by grace, the precept
concerning the Sabbath would without doubt have to be numbered among moral
precepts.

3. When he adds “and thus that which results from the nature and attributes
of God,” and so forth, he seems to intimate, *first*, that every divine moral
law both flows from the image of God concreated, renewed, or restored, and
from the nature and attributes of God; and, *next*, that the Moral law results
from the nature and attributes of God and so forth because it flows from the
image of God concreated or restored. But are these things so reciprocal or
necessarily connected that nothing can pertain to the image of God in the
human being unless it results from God’s nature and attributes, and everything
which results from God’s nature and attributes pertains to this image?

4. When it is said that what flows from the image of God results from God’s
nature and attributes, it must be asked whether everything of this kind
results from God’s nature and attributes necessarily or freely. That is, does
it so result that it cannot fail to result and cannot result in a manner
different from the way it does, or could God have constituted matters
otherwise? If the *Latter* is said, many things can be said to pertain to the
image of God which also depend on God’s free good pleasure. If the *Former* is
said, it must restrict moral matters far too narrowly. For in that case
nothing will be moral unless it would imply a contradiction for it not to
exist or to be otherwise than it really is, and thus God will give us every
moral law by necessity of nature. Consult what was said above in chapter 3.

But 5, he also adds, “and from the human being’s natural state under God,”
from which it is evident that not all moral matters flow or result from the
nature and attributes of God alone, to the exclusion of every other
consideration. But it is then asked whether this conjunction, “and,” is taken
here copulatively, or disjunctively, so that it denotes the same thing as “or.”
If the *Former* is said, then nothing will be a moral precept unless it flows
both from the nature and attributes of God and from the human being’s natural
state under God. But it will be difficult enough to demonstrate this origin
of all moral precepts which the adversaries themselves also acknowledge as
such. Yet he seems to take it in the *latter* manner when, in the *Apology*,
page 5, he distinguishes between *divine natural right* and *human natural
right*: that is, between the things which pertain immediately to the image of
God and immediately rest upon the divine nature and attributes, and thus are
as eternal, immutable, and necessary as God himself and his attributes—such as
the moral matters of the first table concerning worshiping God alone, and
doing so in a spiritual and holy manner—and the things which are moral
mediately, since they rest on human nature. The latter are no more necessary
with respect to God than it was necessary for God to establish human nature,
which was free for God to do, since his nature and attributes did not require
him to create human nature. Thus, he says, that human natural right is to this
extent not as necessary or eternal as divine natural right. Yet once human
nature and its creation in the image of God are supposed, and once the nature
of things and their order have been constituted, God could not order these
things except in a manner fitting his image, holiness, and attributes. From
this emanated all the justice, purity, and truth contained in the precepts of
the second table. These things

[1674 logical printed p. 141 | IA leaf 159 | PDF p. 159]

can indeed be admitted as sound, although what follows requires further
explanation, namely: “Which also, once the constitution of things is supposed,
therefore flows from the image of God, or, what is the same here, is deduced
from and becomes known by natural reason. Thus human natural right rests upon
human nature and can be deduced from it just as much as divine natural right
rests upon the divine nature and attributes and agrees with them. Therefore,
whatever does not involve necessary agreement or opposition to the divine or
human nature cannot be called morally good or evil.” For: 1. It is doubtful
what he understands by Natural Reason, from which these things are deduced and
become known. For if primeval Natural Reason as it was in *Adam* before the
fall is understood, it is certain that other things could have been deduced
and become known from that reason than those which are now evident to us. But
if he understands Reason now corrupted, many things can agree with or oppose
human nature which this Reason cannot perceive as such until it is at least
restored by grace. 2. It follows from this that nothing is moral unless it has
such a necessary agreement with human nature that its contrary or
contradictory would destroy human nature itself. But who does not know how
many things now commanded in the second table of the law are such that, even
if they had not been commanded, or even if the contrary had perhaps been
commanded, human nature would in no respect be destroyed? If God had
instituted that all external goods were held in common, would this simply be
opposed to human nature? Or if he had permitted marriage to be contracted
within the degrees now prohibited, would human nature therefore have been
destroyed? The same can be said about not a few other things.

6. It indeed follows from the human being’s natural state under God that the
human being is bound to obey God in all things. But who could infer from this
that no precepts positive from God are moral for the Human being? For from
this state of the human being it follows that the human being is bound to obey
God even in matters which have no necessary connection with human nature
itself. Indeed, the spirituality and purity of obedience often shine most
brightly in matters of this kind.

7. He wishes this natural state of the human being to be the same as the
human being’s attributes, for he proceeds: “Whatever agrees with and flows
from the attributes of God and the human being is properly morally good,” and
so forth. Therefore this natural state comprehends nothing other than the
human being’s attributes. Since, then, by “attributes” he here understands the
human being’s essential attributes, it follows that nothing pertains to the
human being’s natural state unless it necessarily agrees with the human
essence itself, or with its essential attributes. Therefore abstinence from
the tree of the knowledge of good and evil did not agree with the human
being’s natural state, because it did not necessarily flow from the human
being’s essential attributes. Therefore faith in Christ will not be a moral
virtue, because it does not necessarily agree with the human being’s essential
attributes. Thus it does not agree with the natural state of the fallen human
being either, for this state can also be called a natural state.

8. I would gladly concede that every moral matter possesses agreement and
congruity with human nature even before an actual prescription. But unless
this agreement is necessary and flows from the human being’s own attributes
so necessarily that it is known of itself and can be deduced from them—as he
says later—the adversary does not wish to regard it as moral. Let him therefore
teach how faith in the mediator—which, as we heard, he nevertheless
acknowledged as a moral virtue—flows from the human being’s attributes so
necessarily that it is known of itself and can be deduced from them.

[1674 logical printed p. 142 | IA leaf 160 | PDF p. 160]

9. He adds: “Whatever cannot be brought back to that image of God and does
not flow from it is positive.” Previously he had said that only what flows
from the image of God is moral; but here he intimates that some other things
are also moral—namely, those which, although they do not flow from that image,
can nevertheless be brought back to it. But, I ask, what are those things
which can be brought back to this image? Are they not the things which possess
an agreement with it, though not a necessary one? If not, how are they
distinguished from the things which so flow from that image that they are
known of themselves and can be deduced from it? And I would like him to
explain what it means to be brought back to that image, and what or what sort
of things are brought back to this image in that manner but nevertheless do
not flow from it, much less are so known of themselves and capable of being
deduced from it. Perhaps the difference will then appear much smaller than it
now does.

10. The sum, as it seems, of this learned man’s discourse concerning the
nature of morality tends to exclude everything which depends on God’s positive
will and command. For he explains morality in such a way that he wishes
nothing to be moral which can be called positive, and nothing to be positive
which can be called moral. But we will demonstrate that at least some things
which are called positive are truly moral, since the marks of true morality
which will now be proposed and explained in the next chapter belong to them.

What we have said is abundantly confirmed by Dr. *Hoornbeeck*, *Practical
Theology*, book 9, chapter 20, page 345, in these words: “Hence it follows that
those moral matters do not everywhere possess the same and equal evidence.
Some are more certain and less variable than others. Some could not change
their morality even through God, such as the things which have a place in
God’s own nature—for example, love, justice, and holiness. Others depend on a
divine constitution and are commonly called positive. These do not exist in
God’s nature in the same way, yet God willed them to be equitable and just for
everyone. To the first genus of moral matters, or the one which comprehends
duty toward God, belong all the things said to be institutions of universal,
not Jewish, worship: namely, that God is worshiped in this or that manner and
ought to be worshiped by everyone, although there is nothing in God’s nature
which would have prevented him from omitting the moral manner now ordained or
instituting another, if it had seemed good to him. To the second and third
genera—namely, duties toward ourselves and our neighbor—belong those things
which the nature either of a human being individually or of society
collectively requires, because God has now ordered it in this way and has
given to each those precepts of morals which are indeed equitable and just in
themselves for everyone now, but which God could nevertheless vary and did
sometimes vary without in any way denying his own nature. This is impossible
in the case of things which inhere in his own nature. For he cannot fail to
reckon love, justice, and holiness as good, because they are formally in God’s
own nature. And in the same way among human beings, things which belong to
love, justice, and holiness cannot fail to be good and honorable. But other
things which are not founded on God’s nature but on a divine constitution,
although agreeing with his nature or with human nature in matters of the
second table, belong to another order and degree. For example, divine worship
also consists in the reading and meditation of Holy Scripture, although the
Church once existed without it and divine worship can be had without it; that
God wishes to be worshiped in an assembly and not only individually; that he
wishes to be worshiped through Sacraments, fasts, vows, and even oaths. For
these things are individually and collectively moral; add also religious
Sabbaths, that is, that a person abstains from food or from his labor on
account of divine worship. These things do not indeed agree most closely with
God’s nature or his worship, and yet they both can and ought to be performed
by everyone for this end. Nevertheless, no one fails to see that greater parts
of the divine will and constitution intervene here, that they depend more
upon it, and that they can be varied greatly

[1674 logical printed p. 143 | IA leaf 161 | PDF p. 161 | ends before CAP. XII]

by divine judgment either in the matter or in the manner than can the good
things of the former genus which we said were in God’s nature antecedently to

things that there is not one single character of moral precepts, and that not
all are natural in the same way, although they can also be called natural
insofar as they both agree morally with nature and are deduced from it in its
integrity.”
