VERSE 7. Since oppression drives a wise person to madness, and a gift destroys
the mind.

Having enumerated the benefits that the living receive from death, Solomon
explains in this verse the benefit that the dead themselves receive. It
consists in the dead being free and released from two evils to which the
living are subject. For it ordinarily happens that mortals who live here are
either oppressed and crushed by the power of others, or by their own power
oppress and crush others. This

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA263 | printed pp. 263–264 | begins with complete redit after divided re- / dit on p. 262; ends on p. 263 immediately before the Vers. 8–9 lemma block]

is precisely the point of the statement that minds are infected by the
corruption of gifts. Gifts are ordinarily conferred upon those who hold supremacy and possess
power and authority. All people without exception are in one condition or the
other: they are either subject to the power of others and therefore exposed
to the danger of oppression, or they hold supremacy and are thereby equipped
and armed to oppress others. The dead alone are placed beyond the reach of
both evils.

Here it is worth observing that being infected by the corruption of gifts is
accounted just as much among evils as being overwhelmed by the power and
tyranny of others. This is contrary to the common opinion of many, who do not
hesitate to declare that they would rather howl with the wolves than stand in
the place of lambs devoured by wolves. Yet if we wish to assess the matter as
it truly is, it is far better to suffer injury than to inflict it.

Solomon further amplifies the condition of the dead by saying that even a
wise person, when burdened with injuries, is driven to madness. For impatience
and anguish of mind are a kind of madness that oppression brings by its own
force and nature, unless a person, imbued and equipped with piety, flees to God
as a refuge and asylum. In the same way the corruption of gifts blinds even
the eyes of a wise person, as is stated in Moses.

From this it is also established why death ought not to be disagreeable and
unpleasant to us: by its intervention we are released from every evil both of
punishment and of guilt. In the oppression by which someone is oppressed,
every kind of affliction and misery is comprehended; and in the acceptance of
gifts, by which the mind is corrupted, every kind of guilt is comprehended by
synecdoche.
