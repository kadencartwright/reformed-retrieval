BOOK ONE OF THE SYSTEM OF CHRISTIAN THEOLOGY,

which is

ON THE PRINCIPLES OF THEOLOGY.

CHAPTER ONE

On the word or name *theology*: whence it derives its origin, what it means,
in how many senses it is taken, and what false theology is and how many kinds
there are.

As I prepare to write a *System of Christian Theology*, I first pray with my
whole heart that OUR LORD JESUS CHRIST—who has been made for us by God the
Father wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and redemption, and in whom all
the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden—may sanctify us by his Holy
Spirit and lead us into all truth, to the glory of his divine grace and our
eternal salvation. Amen.

[Marginalia: 1 Corinthians 1:30; Colossians 2:3.]

Now in every course of instruction that proceeds from right reason, the words
for the things to be treated must first be explained and then the things
signified by those words. For, πρὸς τὸ ὀρθῶς διδάσκειν δεῖ πρῶτον ἐξετάζειν τὰ
ὀνόματα—that is, “to teach correctly, one must first examine the names”—as
Aristotle advises. And as Clement of Alexandria says in book 6 of the
*Stromata*, p. 293 of the Commelin edition, δύο εἰσὶν ἰδέαι τῆς ἀληθείας, τά τε
ὀνόματα καὶ τὰ πράγματα, that is, “there are two forms of truth: names and
things.” We too will therefore deal first with the word or name *theology* and
then at last with the thing itself.

Concerning the word and name *theology*, we make the following determinations.

THEOLOGY is a word Greek in origin but granted Latin citizenship, as many
other words have been.

It was first used by the Gentiles. For the most ancient writers of Greece, as
Lactantius says in chapter 11 of his book *On the Wrath of God*, were called
*theologians* because they treated the gods and their worship, and their field
of knowledge was called *theology*. It was thereafter also adopted by
Christians, just as the apostle John is called “the Theologian” in the title of
the Apocalypse; and the Greek and Latin Fathers of the Church retained the
word *theology*.

It signifies either a discourse belonging to God himself—that is, one which
proceeds from God—as θεοπροπία does in books 1 and 16 of the *Iliad*. Likewise,
θεοπρόπιον is an oracle or prophecy which God declares, and θεοσημεία is a sign
which God presents. Or it signifies a discourse *about God*, or, as Augustine
speaks in book 8, chapter 1 of *The City of God*, an account or discourse about
divinity. In the same way, physiology signifies discourse about nature;
astrology, discourse about the stars; meteorology, discourse about meteors;
and cosmology, discourse about the world. In this work of ours it is taken in
the latter sense, namely, for discourse about God.

Moreover, *theology* is predicated ὁμωνύμως, or homonymously, for it is either
true or false. We shall touch briefly on false theology and afterward explain
true theology at greater length and by design.

False theology is a false opinion about God, his will, and his works.

This is called *theology* equivocally. Hence it is also called *opinion-based
theology*, since it is a mere opinion conceived by a mind whose judgment is
corrupt and which embraces the false as true. Yet should it be called theology
at all, rather than *matæology*—vain discourse? Certainly, a vain and wandering opinion
about the Deity is ignorance of God. Galatians 4:8: “Indeed, when you did not
know God, you served those that by nature are not gods.” Ephesians 2:12: “You,
I say, were at that time without Christ, alienated from the commonwealth of
Israel and strangers with respect to the covenants of promise, having no hope
and being without God in the world.” Hence those who introduce human opinions
or fables into divine matters should be called *matæologians*—vain talkers—or
anything else at all, rather than theologians.

False theology belongs both to the ancient Gentiles and to others who err
about divine matters.

The theology of the ancient Gentiles had two parts: one about the gods, to
which the general name *theology* was given, and another about demons. About

[1615 printed locator A]

both, and against both, Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea, treats copiously in the
first six books of the *Preparation for the Gospel*.

The part which treated the gods was twofold: one popular and the other refined.

Popular Gentile theology belonged to the undifferentiated common people. It
settled upon the imperfect principles of our nature and did not rise higher by
reasoning.

Refined Gentile theology was that which, by the efforts of those who were
regarded as learned and wise, was added to popular theology and seemed to treat
divine matters more exactly. Yet through error in reasoning or a presumption
of wisdom it flowed from the corrupt principles and

[1615 printed locator B]

notions of corrupted nature, and issued in various mutually discordant and
false conclusions and precepts about God, his nature, works, and
worship.

This refined theology is threefold: μυθική, φυσική, πολιτική—fabulous,
physical, and political—as Augustine teaches from Varro in book 6, chapter 5
and following, and in book 8, chapter 1 of *The City of God*; and Eusebius in
the preface to book 4 of the *Preparation for the Gospel*.

Fabulous theology belonged to the poets and served theatrical pleasure. Hence
it is also called *theatrical theology*, and by some *historical theology*.

[1615 printed locator C]

Physical theology belonged to the philosophers and treated God and other
divine matters by investigating natural things. They call it *natural
theology*, and also *philosophical*, *mystical*, and *allegorical theology*.

Political or civil theology belonged to the citizens in each city, especially
the priests and governors. It was first introduced by the custom of individual
cities and then publicly approved and received by their laws, in order to
stabilize their shared society through the authority of religion. The same is
called *urban theology*. Neither any poet nor any philosopher dared utter a
word against it unless he wished, as an impious person, to be

[1615 printed locator D]

punished by the law of his country, as happened to Socrates at Athens.

These three therefore differed in the end for which they recounted the gods
and their deeds.

The theology that treated demons is of two kinds, μαγεία and θεουργία, magic
and theurgy.

Μαγεία, which was also called γοητεία, was that which treated the appeasing
and warding off of evil demons.

Θεουργία, which was also called τελεστική, was that which falsely promised the
purification of souls by adjuring good demons, who were enticed by invocation
and invited by sacrifices. Hence the Gentiles believed that through theurgic
pursuits the soul was made fit

[1615 printed locator E]

to receive angelic spirits and to see God. Porphyry the Platonist was devoted
to such theurgy. See Augustine, *The City of God*, book 10, chapters 9 and 10.

If anyone with an abundance of leisure wishes to know fully the theology—or,
rather, the *matæology* or vain discourse—of others who err concerning divine matters, such
as blasphemous Jews, Muslims, pseudo-Christians, and heretics, let him seek it
elsewhere. In this *System* we shall touch throughout upon those controversies
and, according to the prescription of God's word, treat correctly those whose
knowledge is especially necessary for us in the present time.
