QUESTION XXX.

Is ecclesiastical power occupied with dogmas of faith and with creeds and
confessions of faith? We affirm.

I. We have said that there are three parts of ecclesiastical power: first,
dogmatic power, which is occupied with dogmas of faith; second, ordering
power, which is occupied with ordinances, or constitutions and canons that
serve good order; and third, critical or judicial power, which concerns the
exercise of discipline. The key of knowledge can be referred to the first,
the key of government and direction to the second, and the key of jurisdiction
and correction to the third.

[Marginalia: On power concerning dogmas negatively.]

II. The first part, or power concerning dogmas of faith, is treated here.
Concerning it we say negatively that it does not consist in: (1) The church’s
conferring authority upon the Word, either in itself or in relation to us. On
the contrary, all the church’s authority depends upon and rests upon the Word
of God (Eph. 2:20), as was proved in its place when we disputed about
Scripture. (2) Its establishing new dogmas of faith, because just as nothing
ought to be taken away from Scripture, so nothing ought to be added to it; and
whoever wishes to be wise beyond what is written or to preach a gospel beyond
what has been preached is subject to anathema (Gal. 1:8). (3) Its interpreting
Scripture sovereignly and infallibly according to its pleasure, because the
supreme right of infallible interpretation can be attributed to none other
than the Holy Spirit himself speaking in the Scriptures, who is his own best
interpreter in and through the Scriptures (1 Pet. 4:11), and who therefore
commands every private interpretation to be gone (2 Pet. 1:20). (4) Its being
the supreme judge of controversies, because this supreme power belongs only
to Christ, the infallible teacher, and can no more be transferred to another
than can his prophetic office. The Holy Spirit sends us to Scripture as judge
and wills that every spirit and the doctrines preached by them be tested by
it (Isa. 8:20; John 5:39; 1 Thess. 5:20–21; 1 John 4:1; Heb. 4:12), as was
proved at length in Part I concerning the judge of controversies.

[Marginalia: And positively: 1. In preserving Scripture; and 2. in
interpreting it.]

III. Positively, however, it consists in: (1) Preserving Scripture as a sacred
deposit entrusted to the church (Rom. 3:2). (2) Interpreting and defending it,
so that its legitimate sense may be sought and disclosed from Scripture itself
and defended against the sophisms of those in error. In this sense the church
is called by Paul “the pillar and ground of the truth” (1 Tim. 3:15). In this
sense the church is the guardian, herald, and defender of Scripture. But
because these things pertain not so much to power as to duty and office, (3)
this power must properly be considered in the judgment which the church ought
to make concerning doctrine and in the creeds and confessions which it ought
to compose for preserving doctrine and as a bond of ecclesiastical communion.

[Marginalia: 3. In judgment.]

IV. This judgment in matters of faith is not merely a private judgment of
discernment, which belongs to every believer, nor is it supreme, infallible,
and unaccountable, which belongs to God alone. Rather, it is a public judgment
joined with authority, yet subordinate and bound to the Word of God. Although
the faithful are bound to acquiesce in it with respect to external order so
long as the foundation of salvation is not assailed, nevertheless with respect
to conscience it

[1847 vol. III printed p. 240 | IA leaf 274 | PDF p. 274]

binds no further than it is found to conform to the infallible canon of Holy
Scripture.

[Marginalia: 4. Concerning creeds and confessions of faith.]

V. The establishment of public creeds and confessions pertains to this power.
These are customarily produced by synods and presbyteries to preserve unity
and agreement in the faith and to reject errors. Although they do not have the
same authority as Scripture, since they must be examined by Scripture and
corrected from it, they nevertheless have their own notable weight and ought
to be held in high esteem by the pious. This is both because they contain the
summary and foundation of Christian doctrine and are like a fence against
errors and corruptions that can infect religion, and because they are bonds of
ecclesiastical communion and formulas of agreement which testify to unity of
faith and the agreement of churches with one another and distinguish them
from unbelievers as badges or watchwords distinguish soldiers from enemies.

VI. Such was formerly the Apostles’ Creed, composed at the beginning of
Christianity—not because it was composed as a rule of faith by the apostles
themselves, with each contributing one article as his share, as some have
held, but because it was collected from the apostles’ writings as a summary of
the doctrine they handed down. Furthermore, from comparing the ancient
writers we gather that this creed was twofold. One was briefer in exposition,
older in use, and greater in authority. It was received with the institution
of baptism, used together with the administration of baptism from the time of
the apostles, and perpetually connected with it. It was immediately divine in
origin and therefore self-authenticating and authoritative—a rule, norm, and
principle of faith which contained the faith of the Trinity from Matthew
28:19. The other was more extensive and is the one we now have, comprised of
twelve articles. The fathers must be understood of the former and briefer
creed, not the latter, when they say that the rule of faith was instituted by
Christ when he sent the apostles into the world to teach “that the nations
were to be baptized in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit”; when
Tertullian says that “we received the rule of truth through baptism” (*On
Prescription*, chs. 14 and 20); and when Irenæus says that “we have the
confession of faith through the very tradition of baptism” (book 1, ch. 2).
As for the latter creed, although we do not deny that it is divine in matter,
since it was taken from Holy Scripture, nevertheless in form it is only a
human writing and therefore has only human authority, not divine and
self-authenticating authority. This creed was followed by others composed by
various persons at various times, such as the Nicene, Constantinopolitan,
Athanasian, and Chalcedonian creeds. The Constantinopolitan is entirely the
same as the Nicene except that these words were added in the article on the
Holy Spirit against Macedonius: “And in the Holy Spirit, the Lord and giver of
life, who proceeds from the Father and the Son, who with the Father and the Son
together is worshiped and glorified.”

VII. Confessions, as distinct from creeds, are fuller explanations not only of
those creeds, or dogmas of faith, but also of the doctrine of morals and thus
of the whole of religion, written according to the need of one or more
churches. Many of this kind have been written from the beginning of
Christianity and in these most recent times in various kingdoms and nations,
so that by them, as by authentic records, what was taught in the churches
might be made manifest against slanders and people’s unjust prejudices. Such
are the Augsburg Confession of 1530, presented to Emperor Charles V at
councils by several German princes; the Bohemian Confession, also called the
Waldensian, approved by Luther and Melanchthon in 1532 and presented by the
free barons and other nobles of the Kingdom of Bohemia to His Most Serene
Majesty King Ferdinand in 1535; the Helvetic Confession of 1536, first made in
a briefer form at Basel and later in a fuller form in 1566; the Gallic
Confession of 1559, established at Paris in the first national synod; the
English Confession of 1562; the Scottish Confession of 1569; the Belgic
Confession, first written in French in 1561 and in 1579

[1847 vol. III printed p. 241 | IA leaf 275 | PDF p. 275]

confirmed in the Synod of Belgium; the Polish Confession printed at Debrecen
in 1570; and many others of that kind.

VIII. Two things can be asked concerning these confessions: first, their
necessity, and then their authority. With respect to necessity, we say it is
not absolute, as though the church could not be without them, for there was a
time when it lacked them and was content with the ecumenical creeds alone, or
even without them, with the formula of Scripture alone. Rather, their
necessity is hypothetical, given the divine command and the condition of the
church from the time when heresies, danger of contagion, adversaries’
slanders, and internal dissensions in religion began to infest it. They were
needed so that the necessity and justice of our separation from the church
might be evident; so that there might be formulas of agreement and a bond of
salutary union by which all the pious might be joined into one body; and so
that every division, dangerous disagreement, and schism by which the truth
and unity of the church are torn might be forestalled.

IX. Their authority ought indeed to be great among the pious in the churches,
but it nevertheless stands below the authority of Scripture. Scripture is the
rule; the confessions are what is ruled. Scripture alone is
self-authenticating and is divine and infallible with respect to both words
and things; the confessions, although divine in their subject matter, are
human writings in their words and manner of treatment. Faith is owed to
Scripture immediately and absolutely. Judgment, however, is owed to the
confessions, and only through the mediation of that judgment is faith owed to
them if they agree with the Word. Scripture is the constant and immutable
canon of faith. The confessions, however, are subject to revision and fresh
examination, in which it is permissible not only to explain and enlarge them
but also, if any fault should be found in them, to correct them and reform them
according to the norm of the Word. Hence it is clear that those err by excess
who hold such confessions as the norm of truth itself and equate them with the
Word of God, since they are at most secondary norms—not norms of truth, but of
the doctrine received in a particular church—because from them it is possible
to perceive and judge what agrees with the church’s doctrine and what differs
from it.

X. Therefore their true authority consists in their being binding upon those
who are subject to them in the forum of external communion, because they were
written by churches or in the name of churches, to which every member is
subject in external communion (1 Cor. 14:32). Hence, if some persons think
they observe something in them worthy of correction, they ought to undertake
nothing rashly, disorderly, or out of season so as violently to tear apart
their mother’s inward parts, as schismatics do. Rather, they should commend
their difficulties to their church and either prefer its public judgment to
their own private judgment or withdraw from its communion if their conscience
cannot acquiesce in its judgment. Thus confessions cannot bind in the internal
forum of conscience except insofar as they are found to agree with the Word of
God, which alone has the power to bind conscience.

XI. Those therefore err by defect who acknowledge no authority, or very
little authority, in confessions. Such are neutrals and Libertines, who, in
order to consult their private interests, profess nothing certain and
determinate. Instead, they fly a middle course amid the conflicts of those who
contradict one another, wavering and accommodating themselves to the winds of
fortune, and turn their sails at their command. Thus, if they have any
religion, one could fittingly call it a monthly faith, indeed a daily or hourly
faith. Such also are the heterodox and heretics, who, when they see themselves
restrained by such formulas as by a bridle so that they cannot spread their
errors, try in every way to undermine their authority, whether openly or
secretly and by tunneling beneath them. So it was done by the Arminians, who
more than once in their *Considerations on the Belgic Confession and
Catechism* slanderously charged us with attributing canonical authority equal
to Scripture to these formulas when they were read and explained in a public
assembly, as though they were regarded as the very Word of God. But the vanity
of that accusation is clear from the distinction already given between the
confessions

[1847 vol. III printed p. 242 | IA leaf 276 | PDF p. 276]

and the Word of God. Nor is the purpose for which they said this obscure:
namely, by this means to undermine the public confession and, after gradually
abolishing these formulas, to introduce new ones devised by themselves, but
deceptive and in bad faith.

XII. What we say about public confessions we wish to be understood also of
formulas of consensus which the churches have customarily produced from time
to time to restrain the itch of innovators and counter emerging errors that
can corrupt the purity of the faith and sow seeds of sacred dissensions, so
that the “pattern of sound words” and “form of doctrine” handed down to us may
be preserved unimpaired. This was often successfully accomplished in the
ancient church and in the preceding century, and has more than once been
renewed in our own by pious and prudent counsel to forestall dangerous
novelties that could infect the simplicity of the faith and disturb the peace
of the church. This was seen in various Gallic synods concerning various
heads of doctrine; in the Synod of Dort in the Netherlands concerning the
five-article controversies; and in Switzerland and here a few years ago
concerning the imputation of the first sin, the universality of grace, and
certain connected heads, for the confirmation of the doctrine received and
constantly believed among us.
