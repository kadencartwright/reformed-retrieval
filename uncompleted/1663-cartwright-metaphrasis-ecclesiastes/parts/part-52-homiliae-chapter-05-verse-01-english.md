CHAPTER V.

VERSE 1. Watch both your feet when you enter the very house of God, and do so
ready to hear rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools; for they do not
perceive that they are doing evil.

VERSE 2. Do not be hasty with your mouth, and do not let your mind hurry to
bring forth anything before God himself. For God himself is in heaven, but you
are upon the earth; therefore let your words be few.

VERSE 3. For just as a dream comes forth from much occupation, so the voice of
a fool comes from a multitude of words.

VERSE 4. When you have vowed a vow to God, do not delay to pay it, for he does
not delight in fools. Pay what you have vowed.

VERSE 5. It is better that you do not vow than that you vow and do not pay.

VERSE 6. Do not permit your mouth to bind your flesh by sin, and do not say
before that angel that this was an error. Why should God himself grow angry
because of your voice and destroy the work of your hands?

VERSE 7. For as vanities inhere in a multitude of dreams, so they inhere in
many words; but revere God himself.

VERSE 8. If you see the oppression of the poor and the overturning of right
and justice in the province itself, do not marvel at that purpose; for one on
high watches over another on high, and indeed the Most High is over them.

The summary is that, once preparation and precaution for the worship of God
have been made, precepts for true and lawful worship are handed down.

The parts are preparation for the worship of God and the precepts of lawful
worship.

He comes to the second part of the disjunctive axiom, which is that the
felicity of this life is contained in the fear of God and in the true worship
of God, since one is not permitted to find that felicity in the affairs and
occupations under the sun. He pursues this treatment of the fear of God, like
the treatment of the vanity of all things under the sun, intermittently and
at intervals. Here he begins this treatment. It should be noted that true
felicity is placed in divine worship rather than in justice toward one's
neighbor, contrary to the common people and the papists, who make almsgiving
the prow and stern of religion.

The first part, concerning preparation for the worship of God, is contained
in the first part of the first verse. It is described by the accompanying
caution to be exercised by the man who determines to enter the house of God,
that is, the synagogues and especially the temple: he must not dare to carry
his foot rashly and heedlessly into God's house. God formerly foreshadowed this
preparation by commanding the people to wash their clothes and abstain from
their wives before they approached the law to be set forth by God. Exodus 19.
He likewise commanded the removal of shoes before we approach to contemplate
the marvelous works of God. Exodus 3; Joshua 5.

It would be discourteous and uncivil to enter a prince's court or palace with
filthy, muddy, and unwashed feet, or to present oneself at the prince's table
with unwashed hands. How much less should anyone tread even God's vestibule or
share his table unless cleansing has gone before? Moreover, since men do not
go out in public unless they are decently and elegantly adorned, lest they be
seen as filthy and held in contempt, how much more must we beware that we do
not present ourselves filthy and foul before God?

Someone may ask here how we should prepare ourselves so that we may be judged
fit and suitable for divine worship. I answer that we must first embrace the
pursuit of godliness and justice. By that very act, whatever impiety or
injustice men entertain in their minds or practice with their hands must be
driven far away;

for the gate of God's house is the gate of justice, and only the just ought to
enter it. Psalm 118.

Hence Jacob, when he was about to set out for Bethlehem for the sake of divine
worship, first purged his house of idols and commanded the members of his
household to wash their clothes. Genesis 35:2–3. By this ceremony, as has been
said, he intimated purity of soul. To the same point belongs David's statement
that he would wash his hands in innocence so that he might afterward approach
God's altar and go around it. Psalm 26.

Not only unlawful things, but also those things which at their proper place
and time we are commanded to do, must be set aside—such as thought and care
about our occupations. To this is added that a person should establish his
mind in obedience to God, so that without exception he receives whatever God
pronounces through his ministers. Acts 10:33; Isaiah 2:3. Therefore, whenever
we come into God's house, let us examine ourselves by these standards and this
balance.

Thus far he has treated the first part, preparation for the worship of God.
The other part, concerning worship itself, follows. In it the principal place
is assigned to attentive and diligent hearing of the divine Word. For although
prayers are sent before the hearing of the Word, by which useful and fruitful
hearing is summoned and begged down from heaven, nevertheless, within the
whole worship of God, hearing the Word bears the palm. It is therefore placed
above both the sacrifices commanded under the law and prayer itself.

First, its excellence is magnified by a comparison with the lesser thing,
namely sacrifices. It presents no conflict that in this passage Solomon
prefers hearing to the sacrifices of fools, as if the sacrifices of the godly
were to be preferred to hearing the Word. In 1 Samuel 15 pure obedience is
preferred to sacrifices; moreover, mercy toward one's neighbor is preferred
to sacrifice, much more the hearing of the divine Word. Matthew 15. Therefore,
by “the sacrifice of fools” he means every sacrifice of those who either pass
over the hearing of the Word and eagerly offer victims to God, or are more
anxious about offering them than about hearing the Word.

Since by the name of sacrifice he understands every kind of ceremony, it is
easy to gather how no lawful worship is found among the papists. Entirely
occupied with ceremonies, and ceremonies invented by themselves and their own
brains, they have driven the preaching of the Word—and thereby the hearing of
it—from the church. Let us too beware of deceiving ourselves. We must not
imagine that we have splendidly discharged our duty in offering worship to God
merely by kneeling, uncovering our heads, and presenting ourselves before the
pulpit and the Lord's Supper.

The common circumstance in all divine worship is that we should be near, that
is, eager and cheerful in offering worship. What is expressly set forth for
hearing the Word must be applied to all parts of worship: prayers, vows, and
the reception of the sacraments. He describes their foolishness from its
effect, whereby they do not know that they are doing evil. Indeed, in the vain
hope of offering worship acceptable to God, they flatter themselves. Those
who knowingly and deliberately commit evil are to be considered mad rather
than foolish. Knowledge is therefore required in divine worship; where it is
lacking, every ardor or zeal, or what people call “good intention,” is empty
and vain.
