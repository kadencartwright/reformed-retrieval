CHAPTER XXV.

The Third Defect in England Is That It Has Not Purged Away All Roman Leaven.

The third defect follows. It consists in England’s failure to purge away all
Roman leaven. The Holy Spirit himself calls the Church’s reformation from the
Papacy a kind of resurrection, Revelation 20:5. Therefore, just as Christ when
he rose cast off every grave-cloth, so no reformation is approved by God unless
it rises from Papist filth in such a way that it sends every remnant of the
Roman apostasy far away. How can this have been done in the English Church?

Hadrian Saravia, who holds discipline to be immutable, asserted that the divine
Episcopal government by Bishops has remained to this day in the Roman Church.
Thus the Roman Church is a true Church of God, and its entire government is
approved by God, with the sole exception of the Pope’s own Monarchy. John
Whitgift, by contrast, holds discipline to be mutable. Yet, while maintaining
that the Church’s polity is indifferent, he says that the abuses of Roman
government should be corrected, but argues at length that the government itself,
although he grants that it can be changed, should not advantageously be changed.
“This,” he says, “is Cartwright’s second rotten foundation: that those things
which have been turned to abuse in the Papacy are not to be retained.”

What then of Jerome Zanchi, who, writing to Lord Knollys about reforming the
Church, lays down this rule? “There are things which, although they are not evil
in their own nature and long had a not unprofitable place in the Church, have
nevertheless afterward been turned by long abuse into superstitions and
idolatries. Stubbornly refusing either to abolish these things completely after
the example of Hezekiah and Josiah, or at least to correct and purge them after
the example of other godly kings so that every occasion of idolatry and
superstition is removed, can in no way be approved.”

Yet England has shown precisely this stubbornness by not removing the occasions
of Papist superstition. But lest they seek a hiding place here, Stephen, himself
once a Roman Bishop, judged that everything turned to abuse must be entirely
abolished after Hezekiah’s example: “Hezekiah destroyed the high places, broke
the images, cut down the groves, and shattered the bronze serpent which Moses
had made. By this a principle of great authority must be observed in the Church:
if some of our predecessors”

“and forebears did things which could then be without fault, but which were

But, I reply, even if they can have a good—that is, useful—use, which I doubt,

cut off such abuses, we are not even permitted to use those things in the
Church, as Tertullian himself judges: “There is no room for abuse when there
ceases to be a legitimate use. How can anyone abuse a thing when he has nothing
which he may use? The Apostle does not permit us to abuse; he more readily
teaches us not to use.”

When Lord Beza objected that the name of Archbishop was first invented by the
Pope, Hadrian Saravia replied: “Nothing is more foolish than the conclusion which
some draw: Antichrist invented this, therefore it is evil—unless they add or
intend that it is contrary to God’s Word.” But since, by your own confession,
ecclesiastical government is prescribed in God’s Word, will not a title for the
chief rulers be contrary to God’s Word precisely because it is not prescribed in
the Word? England’s government by Archbishops and Bishops therefore sins not
only by lacking every foundation in God’s Word, but also by being built upon an
alien foundation: namely, by having borrowed a matter of discipline from
Christ’s enemy, Antichrist himself.

Tertullian speaks thus of the shamefulness of this crime: “How wicked it is to
overlay God’s work with Satan’s devices! Our servants borrow nothing from our
enemies; soldiers desire nothing from their Emperor’s foe. For to request
anything for use from the adversary in whose hand it lies is transgression.”
Again: “Even if we still and always had to seek, where ought we to seek? Among
heretics, where everything is alien and opposed to our truth, whom we are
forbidden to approach? What servant hopes for provisions from a stranger, not
to say his lord’s enemy? What soldier desires bounties and pay from kings who
are not allies, not to say from enemies, unless he is plainly a deserter,
turncoat, and rebel? No one can be built up by the source from which he is
destroyed. No one is enlightened by the source from which he is darkened. Let
us therefore seek from our own, from our own people, and from our own property,
and only that which can come into question without harm to the rule of faith.”

CHAPTER XXVI.

The Fourth Defect: England Corrupts Discipline by Conforming It to Civil Polity.

The fourth defect follows. It consists in England’s not shrinking from corrupting
discipline by conforming it to civil polity. Cartwright had maintained that civil
government should be reformed according to the Church’s government rather than
the Church’s government according to civil government. Whitgift seems to attack
this as Anabaptistic and destructive of every Monarchy such as now flourishes in
England. George Downham cites Whitgift in this sense and states his own position
on this matter as follows: “The Puritans differ so greatly from the King’s
judgment that, when the King judges that the Church can be conformed to the
commonwealth, they say that civil government ought to be adjusted to
ecclesiastical government. They teach that to shape the Church to civil polity
is the same as if someone wished to fit his hall to its hangings instead of doing
what he ought, fitting the hangings to the hall.”

No one, therefore, should be too harsh toward Cornelius Scultingius, who
interprets Whitgift in this sense. “What is still more absurd,” he says, “the
Anglo-Calvinists wish ecclesiastical polity to be shaped and arranged according
to the commonwealth’s polity.” The absurdity of this position seems indeed to
have troubled Whitgift’s own conscience. For although the Cartwrightian position
which he attacks is, “Ecclesiastical government must not be conformed to civil
government”—the position he is said to defend—Whitgift, who ought either to have
denied that he defended this or to have proved the contrary, turns his discourse
elsewhere. He argues only that the Magistrate may lawfully alter discipline
according to the Church’s condition.

Thus he writes: “Since the Magistrate is the Church’s Supreme Governor, it is not
reasonable that Churches be governed otherwise than as he judges expedient for
their present condition.” Again: “That order must be preserved among ministers
which is most suitable to the Church’s condition.” Again: “The Church cannot be
governed under a Magistrate as under a tyrant, nor in an entire kingdom as in
one city. Whoever does not admit diversity of government corresponding to so
great a diversity in the Church does not build but confounds.”

Whether or not Whitgift’s conscience prevented him from openly declaring his
position about conforming the Church to the commonwealth, this is nevertheless
certain and clear from his words: the cloak of expediency covers that conformity
in England which produced a corresponding monarchical government among Bishops,
following the pattern of the Monarchy flourishing in England. Against this
pretext of expediency Whitaker writes: “Bellarmine argues that the dispute
between the Roman and Carthaginian Churches once consisted only in this: Rome
thought the Pope’s primacy expedient, while Carthage thought it inexpedient. But
we must not suppose,” Whitaker replies, “that the Cartha—”

2. But let us proceed point by point. First, these two governments subsist
separately and apart from one another. Neither depends upon the other; each draws
the origin of its authority immediately from God. We may therefore argue with
the most learned Field: “No man has the power to alter anything except the one in
whom it inheres in an eminent degree and from whom it is originally derived.”
Ecclesiastical government, then, insofar as it is ecclesiastical, neither
inheres in the Magistrate nor originally depends upon him. It therefore cannot
be altered by him or bent to civil government.

Second, each government is superior to the other within its own kind. Since
they thus possess equal dignity, each being supreme in its own kind, how can
either have the right to alter the other or conform it to itself? But if the
superiority of the kinds themselves is asked about, ecclesiastical government
is far superior to civil government. The commonwealth should therefore be
adjusted to the Church rather than God’s Church to the civil commonwealth. Field
proves this from Thomas Waldensis: “Spiritual and civil power are each superior
in matters pertaining to itself, and each is independent, although spiritual
power is absolutely more perfect and excellent than civil power.” Gregory
Nazianzen, Chrysostom, Peter of Blois, and Hugh of Saint Victor similarly hold
that the Church’s government is superior to civil government by reason of its
object and effects—indeed, as much superior as the soul surpasses the body and
spiritual life surpasses earthly life. Therefore, if any conformity had a place
here, it would be just for civil government, the less excellent, to be fitted to
the more excellent government of the Church, not the latter to the former.

named the pagan priests. By naming the beast—that is, the civil empire—it is
plain that he included a certain political and governmental resemblance. Third,
Matthew Sutcliffe understands this passage of the image of polity. These are
his words: “The Pope did not succeed Peter in preaching but Constantine upon the
imperial throne, giving life to the beast which seemed dead and setting up at
Rome an image of the old empire.” More on this passage elsewhere.

Tenth, there are men who openly cut the throat of any conformity of
ecclesiastical polity to civil polity. John of Constantinople sought the primacy
under the pretext of the imperial majesty residing at Constantinople. He
therefore argued that the primacy was owed to the Bishop of Constantinople
because the Church ought to follow the commonwealth. Thus John’s episcopate at
Constantinople rested upon the same foundation on which Whitgift’s English one
rests today. Therefore the ancient refutation of Leo and Gelasius applies not
only against John but also against Whitgift. Leo writes: “There is one principle
for secular affairs and another for divine affairs.” Gelasius writes: “The power
of a secular kingdom is one thing; the distribution of ecclesiastical honors is
another.”

Constantine similarly told the Bishops: “You are appointed by God as Bishops of
those things which must be done within the Church; I, however, as Bishop of
those things which are outside it.” Eusebius, *Life of Constantine*, book 4,
chapter 34. “By these words,” says Carleton, “Constantine so distinguished
ecclesiastical and political power that the former concerned only inward
matters, the latter only outward ones.” Since the Magistrate is outside the
Church in this sense, he has no power to transform the Church, much less to
conform its inward government to his outward one. Ambrose held the same
judgment: the Magistrate has no power over sacred things by which he might alter
them. If this is true, he will not be permitted to alter discipline either.
Ambrose’s words to Valentinian are: “Do not burden yourself, Emperor, by thinking
that you have some imperial right in divine affairs. The jurisdiction of
political duties has been entrusted to you, not sacred ones.”

Cornelius Scultingius speaks specifically of the English Hierarchists’
conformity: “We must observe,” he writes, “that the Anglo-Calvinists vigorously
maintain against the Puritans that the Church can establish ecclesiastical
offices according to the diversity of times and places—but very absurdly. For
they wish each province to assume the form of polity”

“which seems most suitable to its godly Magistrate and the condition of the
province itself.” But why, you ask, is Scultingius brought onto the stage? As
though he had not already been on the Hierarchical stage—you strengthen the
English Hierarchy’s side. Hadrian Saravia is no gentler than Scultingius. He
maintains that a definite and immutable form of ecclesiastical government was
commanded by Christ himself. Thus he argues against the conformity to civil
government of which we are speaking: “God never gave any nation a definite and
perpetual form of government which could not be changed according to the
diversity of place and time. But the government of which we speak has a
different character. Since it proceeded immediately from God, men cannot and
need not alter it according to their own judgment. Divine Wisdom so adjusted it
that it opposes no form of civil government. When an entire commonwealth becomes
a Church, the government indeed receives some change, but one which does not
alter its nature.”

Again: “Although ecclesiastical government, once the Gospel has been received,
must be bent somewhat toward the condition of the commonwealth—so far as God’s
Word permits—care must nevertheless be taken that its substance is not altered.
Heavenly doctrine is universal and of such a kind that men can receive it. It
must therefore not be bent to human customs like a Lesbian rule; men must
instead be brought back to that fixed and immovable rule.” Saravia argues here
for the immutability of episcopal government, but what he says is nevertheless
true. Otherwise episcopal government would necessarily fall in every
commonwealth which is either Aristocratic or Democratic. Thus this fourth
defect in the English reformation of discipline—the conformity of ecclesiastical
polity to civil polity—is both forbidden by clear Scriptures and refuted by the
Hierarchists themselves.

CHAPTER XXVII.

The Fifth Defect in England: It Has Not Yet Applied a Remedy to the Source of Its
Evils.

The fifth defect follows. It consists in the fact that no remedy has yet been
applied to the very source of all the evils in the English Church. Peter Martyr
rightly advises that, in reforming the Church, Elisha’s example must be imitated
and the spring itself healed before everything else, since every contagion and
stain flows from it. Writing in defense of English discipline, Lous complains
that the Church’s condition always slides toward the worse and that anyone can
see everything rush toward the worse. Those who habitually proclaim the English
Church’s glorious condition with full cheeks must look to this themselves.

Why should we not glorify God for proving his prophecy true in both respects?
In these boasters we see fulfilled what is foretold of England’s Angel in
Revelation 3:17: “You say, I am rich and need nothing.” In this most just
complaint, however, we see fulfilled what is said of the same Angel: “You are
wretched and”

[Marginal references: Peter Martyr, *On 2 Kings*; Lous, *Ecclesiastical
Complaint*, page 1.]

[1638 control | printed p. 74 | BSB canvas 84 | bsb10507786_00084]

“miserable.” Lous adds especially that the Church lies as though crushed by
ruins and lies in such a condition that it can find neither hope of aid nor
comfort in misery. He adds that this has happened not so much from outward
enemies as from domestic and inward ones. Just as I especially agree with him
on this point, I say that for this very reason reformation must be urged more
vehemently and begun more quickly.

This is what Giovanni Pico della Mirandola once wrote to Emperor Maximilian in
1500: “The Church is shaken by outward enemies and torn by inward ones. The
sheepfold surrounded and consecrated by Jesus Christ’s blood has suffered much
worse, and suffers daily, from wolves ravaging beneath sheep’s skins than from
those in their own skins. Act now, therefore, excellent Caesar. Show yourself a
faithful minister to Christ, king of all kings, who is now about to free his
sheep both from enemies and from treacherous shepherds.”

What then, you ask? Will you say that some of our own people have done the
Church more harm than the Papists themselves? If you do not believe me, at least
believe your own Lous. Then explain why wolves covered in sheep’s skin are not
more dangerous than those held to be open enemies, as Christ once warned in
Matthew 7:15. Answer Cyprian too, who taught that a pretended enemy does more
harm than an open foe. These are his words: “An enemy must be feared and guarded
against more when he creeps in secretly. This is the devil’s cunning: to deceive
the unwary under the very title of the Christian name.” A tragic poet gives the
reason in a similar case: “Hatreds openly professed lose the opportunity for
vengeance.” In the same way, openly professed enemies lose the means of doing
harm.

Since this is so, everyone must wake and carefully inquire who those wolves in
England are who, covered in sheep’s skin, are thought to be pillars of the
Church but nevertheless undermine and overthrow it. I have not mentioned
pillars carelessly. Reason itself and the attested experience of every age prove
that they are pastors, not laypeople. Hugh of Saint-Cher attempts to show from
Daniel 3 where the evils of his age arose: “Iniquity went forth from the elders.”
Nicolas Oresme discusses the miseries of his age before Urban V and traces their
source to the passage in Lamentations 4: “Because of the iniquity of its priests.”
Gianfrancesco Pico delivered an elegant oration for reformation before Leo X at
the Fifth Lateran Council. After searching for the source of every evil, he says
among other things: “John Chrysostom judges that every evil comes from the
temple, and Jerome writes that he found no one except priests who led peoples
astray.”

Chrysostom’s words are: “Just as every good proceeds from the temple, so every
evil also proceeds from the temple.” Jerome’s are: “Searching the ancient
histories, I can find no others who split the Church and led peoples away from
the Lord’s house except those who were appointed priests and Prophets by God,
that is, watchmen. They are therefore turned into a twisted snare, setting a
stumbling block in every place.” We therefore depart from the experience of
every age if we trace the English Church’s evils to any source other than the
priests themselves. Since these are either Hierarchists or Reformers, whom the
common people call Puritans, we must next in—

—quire which of these two kinds of priests must bear the whole blame.

2. George Downham here thrusts himself forward as judge. “It is most certain,”
he writes, “that contempt for Bishops, if not the cause of every evil among us”
—which Chrysostom nevertheless seems to affirm in his second homily on
2 Timothy—“is at any rate the cause of the greatest evil.” But Chrysostom, I
reply, never knew an English Bishop. The episcopate was one thing then and is
another now, to say nothing of the fact that we have never despised any Bishops
in Chrysostom’s sense. You commit the fallacy of accident. For Chrysostom treats
contempt for Bishops as the cause of evil not when their superiority over the
other ministers is attacked from God’s Word, but when their authority over the
Lord’s flock—teaching right things, cultivating unity, and watching over purity
of conduct—is brought into contempt by ungodly and rebellious men.

Thus Saint Chrysostom’s censure brands us with no mark. What you call our present
contempt for Bishops is really nothing other than the application of that
medicine, that eye salve and gold—that is, that reformation of discipline—which
the Holy Spirit himself says in Revelation 3:18 is the readiest and most
effective cure for the English Church’s disease, blindness, and poverty. We can
truly testify of England, still laboring under a Babylonian disease, what
Jeremiah once said of Babylon itself: [“We wished to heal Babylon, but she did
not wish to be healed”], Jeremiah 51:9. She does not even recognize her disease;
indeed, she is so far from recognizing it that she also complains of those who
have presumed in any way to touch her wounds. Let the one who has ears hear our
ready defense from Jerome: “Let them not be angry with us for expounding these
things. Let them pray to the Lord and act earnestly, lest they deserve to be
among the priests who violate the Lord’s holy things.”

What other fault have we ever had than setting forth what the polity of the
Church prescribed in the Word is and how foully those priests who have departed
from it violate the Lord’s holy things? We certainly inflicted no wounds. We
wished to heal those inflicted by the Hierarchists. Thus we cannot be the cause
of every evil, as Downham slanders us, unless perhaps to heal is to wound. How
perversely, then, things are done in England, where no reformation of this kind
is considered except, as the common saying goes, that the Reformers themselves
should be rooted out! Ought it to have been done in this way?

When Emperor Sigismund urged reformation, the Bishops reportedly told him:
“We must begin with the Friars Minor.” “No,” Sigismund replied, “we must begin
not with the Minors but with the Majors.” In England, however, the Reformers are
both few and Minors. What if there were something among them which required
reformation? As long as the Bishop-Majors are not reformed and the very source
of the evils remains unhealed, the evils will never cease to flow. Sigismund saw
that the Church’s evil flowed not so much from outward as from inward enemies,
and among the latter not so much from laypeople as from priests. So too he saw
that among the priests the heads of the evils are those

who hold the head and citadel of affairs. What age or country, after all, has not

experience? Thus the Canon says: “Disease begins to invade the whole body if the

Writing about the Church’s reformation, Durand of Mende says that it must begin
with the Church’s Prelates and with the Roman curia itself, by whom the whole
people are infected. Wenceslaus, lamenting the misery of his age, traces it to

you see in the head you can discern my corruption and pain in the other

This is found in Lord Mornay, who also reports that Robert Gallus was shown in a

emaciated head, as though it were wooden. In the same author, Liutbert,
Archbishop of Mainz, traces the source of all evil to the fact that “the disease
in the head gradually pours through the members.” Pope Adrian VI similarly
writes: “You will say that we frankly confess that God permits this persecution
to be inflicted upon his Church because of men’s sins, especially those of
priests and Prelates of the Church. The Scriptures cry out that the people’s
sins are derived from the priests’ sins. For this reason”—as Chrysostom says—

the temple so that he might first chastise the priests’ sins, like a good doctor
who heals a disease from its root. Nor is it surprising if sickness descended
from the head to the members, from the Supreme Pontiffs to the other, lower
Prelates. So far as we are concerned in this matter, you will promise that we
will make every effort so that this Curia, from which perhaps this whole evil
proceeded, may first be reformed; so that just as corruption flowed from it
into all inferiors, health and reformation may also flow from it.”

What follows? The Scriptures cry out that every evil comes from the highest
priests. Do the Hierarchists not hear? Reason says that disease in the head
ruins the whole body; the Hierarchists wish every evil to have arisen from the
feet. The experience of every age testifies that their Prelates are the source
of all evil; the Hierarchists protest that the opposite happened in England.
Even the Pope himself frankly confesses that his curia is the cause of all evil;
the English Hierarchists now have harder foreheads.

But lest all the blame be transferred to mere abuses, let Christ himself be
heard as judge. He transfers all the blame for that entire lukewarmness to
England’s corrupt government. Unless it is corrected more quickly, England’s
Angel will have to expect him as avenger whom until now, through the Puritans,
as they are called, it has had both as a gentle physician and a benevolent
admonisher—and rightly so. Would so many evils rage in England and consume the
Church’s innermost organs if the saving government of Jesus Christ had prevailed
there as it does in the other Churches? This is the fifth defect of the English
reformation: the very source of all its evils is not reformed.
