CHAPTER VII. On the comparison of councils with the Holy Scriptures.

The sixth question is whether the Pontificians ascribe greater authority to
councils than to Scripture. Bellarmine denies it; we affirm it. If this
question were understood of councils considered by and in themselves, then
this charge should by no means be brought against those Pontificians who agree
with Bellarmine, for he ascribes no special authority at all to a council by
itself, but ascribes it all to the Pope. But since a council is understood as
confirmed by the Pope, and so it is the Pope’s authority rather than the
council’s which he brings into this comparison with the Scriptures, and since
other Pontificians ascribe similar authority to a council by itself, it is
therefore worthwhile to hear what reason Bellarmine can give why he should not
be charged with this blasphemy.

THESIS I.

1. Bellarmine. *If some Catholics sometimes say that Scripture depends upon
the Church or upon a council, they do not mean with respect to its authority
and in itself, but with respect to its interpretation and to us.*

Protestants. I. Not *some* Catholic Pontificians, but *all* say this (without
the *if*); nor does it slip from them *sometimes*, but they hold it
deliberately and defend it carefully—namely, that *Scripture depends upon the
Church*. This minimizing and concealment of a known fact is a confession of
shamefulness, attended by some shame over it. II. Bellarmine’s distinction
between Scripture *with respect to authority* and *with respect to us* is a
futile distinction within one and the same thing, intended to deceive the
unskilled. For authority in matters of this kind is nothing other than that
power to produce faith and bring forth assent which is derived from the person
of the witness or author into his testimony. Therefore the authority of every
witness or testimony has reference to those to whom it is presented and from
whom it requires belief or assent. Consequently, *Scripture with respect to
authority* differs neither in reality nor in any rational respect from
*Scripture with respect to us*. Bellarmine spreads precisely the same darkness
over the same truth by another distinction, the exact twin of this one, in
book 3, chapter 14, when he teaches that *the truth of faith, with respect to
us, rests upon the Church’s authority*, as though the truth of faith with
respect to us were either a different truth or had a different

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n246 | printed p. 29]

cause and basis than it has in itself. Yet there he clearly shows that the
Church is ranked by him above Scripture in authority with respect to us, and
therefore that *with respect to authority* and *with respect to us* are
absurdly opposed to each other here. III. According to Bellarmine, the
interpretation of Scripture is twofold, *On the Word of God*, book 3, chapter
10: *one after the manner of a teacher and another after the manner of a
judge*. But the interpretation which is after the manner of a judge pertains
to councils and the Pope and *requires authority which must necessarily be
followed*, in the same place. Therefore *with respect to authority* and *with
respect to interpretation* are also absurdly opposed to each other in this
petty distinction. IV. There is no authority in Scripture itself except with
respect to its explanation of God’s truth and will. Therefore, if the Church
surpasses Scripture in authority of interpretation, then the Church is greater
in every kind of authority. V. Since, according to the Pontificians,
*Scripture is chiefly in the heart of the Church; Báñez, on I, question 1,
article 8, page 52; and has no authority with respect to us except because of
the Church (although the Church possesses her authority apart from Scripture);
and cannot interpret itself in any way, but must be explained by the Church’s
authority; and since, in the heart of that same Church, there are many
traditions besides Scripture which have precisely the same authority as
Scripture itself; and that Church also has the power to make many new laws
which bind human consciences no less than Scripture itself* once declared by
the Church—since, I say, each and every one of these are accepted teachings
and principles of the Pontificians, vigorously defended by Bellarmine himself,
it ought not to be called into doubt that among the Pontificians, in various
ways and by many degrees, the authority of the Church—that is, of a council
confirmed by the Pope, that is, of the Pope himself confirming the
council—is greater than that of Holy Scripture. This is also the conclusion
supported and fortified with great effort by Stapleton, *Relecture on the
Principles of the Doctrine of Faith*, controversy 4, question 5: *Therefore
the authority of the Church and of Scripture is at least equal.*

2. Bellarmine. *There is a manifold difference between Holy Scripture and the
decrees of councils, from which it is understood that Scripture is preferred
to councils.*

Protestants. There are differences enough from which all the godly understand
that the Scriptures must be preferred to traditions, decrees of councils,
rescripts of Pontiffs, and the consensus of the Fathers. But no distinction
can show that the present-day Pontificians do not ascribe at least the same
authority to all those other uncertain rules, and also to councils confirmed
by the Pope, as they grant to the Scriptures.

3. Bellarmine. *The first difference is that Scripture is the word of God
immediately revealed. Councils, however, neither possess nor write immediate
revelations; they only declare what is the Word of God.*

Protestants. I. Immediate and mediated revelation make no difference in
authority, provided that on both sides there is the same certainty

[1633 witness | Tome II | IA scan n247 | printed p. 30]

that the word of God is declared, and nothing besides the word of God. For
authority adheres to the word: if its author is equally certain here and
there, its authority is equal. The authority of one who receives a prince’s
edict immediately from his mouth is no greater than that of one who declares
the same edict to others after receiving it mediately but certainly, with the
same power of jurisdiction. Indeed, the authority of one who cannot err,
though he has no immediate revelation, ought to be greater than that of one
who is secure from error only insofar as he is directed immediately. II. In
many cases, as Bellarmine says in this same place, *God did not reveal new and
unknown things to the sacred writers, but only inspired them immediately and
moved them to write the things they had seen or heard, and directed them so
that they would not err in any way.* Yet Bellarmine claims for the Pope and
for a council confirmed by him, in general decrees of faith and morals, this
infallible assistance and direction so that it cannot err in any way. III. At
least with respect to traditions, the Pontificians customarily equate the
Church’s authority with Scripture, because the Church gives them equal honor
with Scripture even though they are commended only by the Church’s authority.
This is Stapleton’s argument, *Relecture*, controversy 4, question 5. IV. The
authority of a judge is greater than that of a scribe, or of a teacher even
when he writes what was immediately dictated. But most of the sacred writers,
at least, were only scribes and teachers, while the Pope with his council
wishes to be a praetorian judge.

4. Bellarmine. *The sacred writers did not have to labor much in producing
their books, but the Fathers in councils must seek out the matter itself—that
is, investigate conclusions—by disputing, reading, and thinking.*

Protestants. I. The necessity of labor does not diminish authority. The Pope’s
authority when he defines and declares in general that Holy Scripture is the
rule of faith is not less than when he defines something from the Scriptures;
yet labor is required in the latter instance and not in the former. II. Many
Pontificians maintain that *although the Pope with his council has not
exercised sufficient diligence, God nevertheless will not permit him to
define what is false*. Báñez, on II-II, question 1, article 10, pages 63–64.
III. If what the other Pontificians affirm is true, *that the Pope and a
council cannot omit sufficient diligence when they prepare to define matters
of faith*—Báñez, in the same place—then the necessity of diligence, which is
present just as certainly in every case as revelation itself is present to the
sacred writers, likewise makes no difference in authority. IV. It is plain
that the Pope with his Council of Trent framed various decrees of faith
without an examination of this kind going before, as in the approval of the
Vulgate version.

5. Bellarmine. *No error at all can be in the Scriptures. Councils can err in
particular judgments.*

Protestants. Our accusation has been made concerning universal judgments.

6. Bellarmine. *In Scripture, not only the meaning but also each and every word
pertains to faith. But in councils the greater part of the proceedings does
not pertain to faith: not the disputations which precede, nor the reasons which
are advanced, nor the explanations and illustrations which are offered, but
only the bare decrees themselves which are proposed as matters of faith—and
even these not with respect to their words, but only their meaning.*

Protestants. I. We therefore compare with Scripture those bare decrees with
respect to their meaning, and we say that the Pontificians either prefer them
to Scripture or at least equate them with it. II. If the disputations and
reasons from which the decrees are investigated and deduced as conclusions do
not pertain to faith, then neither do the decrees themselves, insofar as they
are concluded from those reasons. For a conclusion is the effect of the
reasons serving as premises and cannot exceed the power of its causes. Canus
rightly says in book 5, chapter 5: *The Church’s decrees cannot be certain and
firm unless they rest upon certain and firm principles and foundations.
Therefore, if even one of those things upon which the Church’s judgment
depends is uncertain, the Church’s decree cannot be certain, whether the
question is speculative or practical. For the conclusion follows the weaker
part.* III. If a council can adduce inept reasons to confirm its decrees, then
it can err in interpreting and explaining the Scriptures, at least in relation
to another conclusion. If it can interpret Scripture badly, then it can also
hand down outright an erroneous rule of faith. For the application of
Scripture to prove this or that conclusion always presupposes an interpretation
of it which agrees with that conclusion. A syllogistic judgment concerning a
conclusion always requires beforehand an axiomatic judgment concerning the
premises. IV. If a council can err in the reasons for its decrees, then the
Council of Trent also errs in the decree confirmed by an anathema when it
pronounces anathema upon anyone who says that the Catholic Church was *not led
by just reasons* to establish that the laity should commune under the species
of bread alone.

7. Bellarmine. *Scripture does not need the Pontiff’s approval in order to be
authentic, but only in order that its authority may become known; councils,
however, are not ratified unless they are confirmed by the Pontiff.*

Protestants. I. The question was instituted concerning councils already
confirmed by the Pontiff, not councils yet to be confirmed. II. For the faith
of the Church, it comes to the same thing whether Scripture is said to possess
no authority, or to possess none which can become known to the Church without
the Pope.
