VERSE 23. I tested all those things by wisdom. When I said, “I am wise,” wisdom
itself was far from me.

VERSE 24. Whatever has always been is exceedingly far away, and exceedingly

VERSE 25. I turned myself—and did so from my heart—to know and to explore and

the folly of every madness.

VERSE 26. But I found something more bitter than death, namely, that woman, in
whose mind are nets and snares, and in whose hands are bonds. The person who
appears good before God himself escapes her, but the sinner is caught by her.

VERSE 27. “See, I found this,” says Ecclesiastes, “by seeking once and again to
attain the reasoning.”

VERSE 28. The more my soul seeks the reasoning, it nevertheless does not
attain it. I may indeed attain the reasoning of one man out of every thousand,
but I do not attain that of a woman furnished with all these things.

VERSE 29. Only see: I found this, that God himself made humankind upright, but
they have sought out very many reasonings.

Concerning Solomon’s intense and ardent zeal in seeking and investigating
wisdom, and the fruit of that zeal.

There are two parts: 1. Solomon’s fervent and eager zeal in seeking wisdom. 2.
The fruit and effect of that zeal.

Taking up a new beginning, Solomon treats other vanities, ones more difficult
to discover, and places the first three verses before this treatment as a
preface.

VERSE 23. I tested all those things by wisdom. When I said, “I am wise,” wisdom
itself was far from me.

He describes his zeal for wisdom by the accompanying time when he applied his
mind to its investigation. By this he may more readily demonstrate the vanity
of his pursuit: although he was devoted to the pursuit of wisdom at the time
when the fear of God flourished in him, he nevertheless did not attain it. For
if, when he pursued wisdom after his defection, he had not mastered it, the
ἀδυναμία would have been ascribed to the defection rather than to the weakness
of our depraved nature. From this it follows that a great aid for attaining
wisdom is placed and established in the true fear of God—not only for that
wisdom which alone is saving, but also for human and political wisdom.

For Daniel and his companions, imbued with the fear of God, far surpassed all
their colleagues and fellow students from other nations in knowledge of
languages, political learning, the hidden knowledge of the Chaldeans, and
every kind of humane literature, Daniel 1. Likewise, among God’s people, those
who drank in this fear of God more fully surpassed in political learning those
who tasted it more sparingly and thinly. Hence David dares to profess in Psalm
119 that he had surpassed both his teachers and those older than himself in
wisdom, for the very reason that he had been an exceedingly diligent student
of the divine Law.

Nevertheless, it has gained acceptance with very many that those wholly
devoted to the study of divine worship are never politically prudent and
never cultivated in the liberal sciences—as though God were not beneficent to
the good, as though he were more restricted toward his own people than toward
his enemies, or as though the truth and faithfulness of his Word had failed,
by which he promised that no good thing would be lacking to those who fear
him. But although he has not furnished these people so abundantly with these
gifts, he has enriched them with other things according to his πολυποικίλη
wisdom.

The fact that, in Solomon’s judgment, the discovery and investigation of
wisdom was difficult—even though he was fortified by God’s most true and
certain promise, and surpassed in keenness of intellect and capacity of
understanding all mortals who had ever lived or would live—should teach us not
to trust too much in ourselves or claim too much for ourselves. We are many
parasangs inferior to him in investigating matters. If that horseman could
not attain wisdom by pursuing it, what shall we foot soldiers achieve in this
kind of pursuit?

VERSE 23. I tested all those things by wisdom. When I said, “I am wise,” wisdom
itself was far from me.

VERSE 24. Whatever has always been is exceedingly far away, and exceedingly
deep; who can find it out?

He assigns the cause of this incomprehensibility: the supreme difficulty of
wisdom, which is illustrated by a comparison with physical things. He compares wisdom
to physical things separated by such great distances that, whether because of
their remoteness or depth, no one can reach or penetrate them. On this see Job
38:1. Our feet are far too leaden to attain wisdom by pursuing it; our hands
are too short to grasp it; and our understanding is not deep enough to
penetrate its depth.

From this let us learn that our knowledge is only begun and is imperfect. For
there are many things we entirely do not know, and we do not perceive or
comprehend even the things we know without something in those very things
escaping us, 1 Corinthians 8 and likewise 13. Let this instruct us in humility
and submission of mind, so that we affirm nothing proudly, arrogantly,
rashly, or precipitately. Therefore, when some opinion of ourselves and of our
knowledge enters our mind, let there immediately come to mind—in order to beat
down that pride of mind—our ignorance of very many other things, and our
defective, mutilated, and, so to speak, superficial knowledge of the very
matter about which we boast.

Let this meditation further stir us to desire the future life, where a
one-day-old child knows more than a person who lived nine hundred years here
on earth, and more than Solomon himself knew at the summit of his earthly
wisdom. For here on earth we are all infants; we all see in a riddle and as in
a mirror. Only then shall we perceive all things clearly and accurately, when
we have been released from this prison-house of the body and have migrated to
heaven. By nature’s guidance we all desire knowledge and an understanding of
things. Yet how few are possessed by a desire to go to the heavenly Academy,
where the most perfect knowledge of all human and divine things will be
communicated at once in proportion to our nature.

VERSE 25. I turned myself—and did so from my heart—to know and to explore and
seek wisdom and its reasoning; likewise, to know the wickedness of folly and
the folly of every madness.

From the preceding difficulty of attaining knowledge, someone might suppose
that Solomon, broken and deterred by a certain weariness, had lost heart and
abandoned the study of wisdom. For who would weary himself by running after
something that he cannot attain by pursuing it? Yet, on the contrary, we see
Solomon act so that he not only did not abandon the study of wisdom, but
wholly applied his mind to investigating it. This should shame those who, by
invoking the difficulty and roughness of knowing sacred things, bear a mind
alienated from them. What these people think repellent ought to add a spur, so
that with greater exertion or,

rather, struggle of mind, they may apply themselves to knowing those things
more than they did before.

In finding metals, especially in gold mines, although infinite difficulties,
immense expenses, and many dangers occur, we nevertheless labor tirelessly,
encouraged by the hope of obtaining gold—even when we are uncertain of
obtaining it. The proverb says, “Hunger breaks through stone walls.” Therefore,
if we are pressed by hunger for the divine Word, as is our duty, we will
overcome every obstacle. Armed, then, with God’s promises for overcoming those
difficulties by which the road to wisdom is barred, we shall be guilty of
shameful idleness if we are frightened away from the study of this wisdom by
the idea of difficulty rather than by difficulty itself.

By saying that he wished to know not only wisdom but its reasoning, Solomon
teaches how we ought to conduct ourselves in the knowledge of things,
especially sacred things: namely, we should grasp the causes and reasons of
things. For knowledge is then established and, as it were, rooted, when it is
perceived together with its underlying and supporting causes. To this belongs
what is said in 1 Peter 4, namely, that we must give a reason for the hope
within us.

In order to comprehend wisdom more readily, he directed his mind toward
knowledge of folly, because knowledge of one contrary brings great assistance
in knowing the other. On this see what was written at chapter 1:18. It is
therefore not foreign to piety to search out and know the nature, character,
and ways of impiety and wickedness, especially since sacred Scripture sets
before us an anatomy of impiety and wickedness. Hence it also happens that
when an unbeliever enters an assembly of the faithful and hears the sermon,
his heart is disclosed and he is convicted of the sin that had been hidden, 1
Corinthians 14.

This must nevertheless be understood so that the mind is first imbued with
knowledge of virtue and piety before it turns itself to studying folly and
corrupt morals; thus it may be fortified by knowledge of piety and uprightness
as by an antidote against the poison and contagion of vices. Accordingly, I do
not approve the judgment of those who, under the title of usefulness in
knowing evil, propose that tender young minds should first read those authors
whose language is indeed verbally pure but whose subject and argument are
filthy and contaminated. Nor is it safe even for those most firmly established
and most advanced in piety to stir that Camarina of corrupt morals unless they
are well armed after calling forth God’s protection by prayer. If the soul
were equally inclined toward piety and impiety as though in a balance, it
would still be fitting to drink in piety first—much more when the whole soul,
to its full extent, is inclined toward wickedness. So much for the first part.

VERSE 26. But I found something more bitter than death, namely, that woman, in
whose mind are nets and snares, and in whose hands are bonds. The person who
appears good before God himself escapes her, but the sinner is caught by her.

There follows the effects and fruit of the investigation. First, as is
customary for the Hebrews, he begins with what was stated later, concerning
folly and madness. Thus he says that he found the harmfulness of a prostitute,
whom he describes by the bitterness attached to her. He sets this bitterness
forth to make her an object of flight and detestation, for by nature we shrink
from bitter things. This statement seems to conflict with Solomon’s words in
Proverbs 5:3, where her lips are said to drip a honeycomb and her palate to be
smoother than oil. But it must be understood that Solomon speaks there from
the perspective of the lustful, not because the thing is so. For in the same
place it follows that she is bitter as wormwood. And since wormwood is a
health-giving herb, lest it appear to

possess any healing quality, he adds that she is sharp as a two-edged sword,
which is lethal and deadly.

Here, however, he amplifies and exaggerates her bitterness by comparison with
lesser things: she is not merely deadly and lethal, but more bitter than death
itself. Thus, if a person were offered the choice either to die or to join
himself to a prostitute, Solomon would wish him to choose death instead. We
see everyone shrink from a plague merely because it carries the danger
of death, although most people are either not infected by it or at least do
not die from that contagion. How much more ought one to bear a mind that
shrinks from a prostitute, who brings death and destruction often in this
life and always in the future life. And what is more bitter than death except
hell? Even if someone, led by repentance, is drawn out of that pit, the grief
and sorrow joined with repentance, and God’s scourge by which he chastises his
own even when they repent, sting more than death itself. See the examples of
David and Magdalene.

Solomon describes the danger she brings, first from its cause: her soul, the
resourceful inventor and exceedingly clever artificer of evils and devices,
frauds and snares. These frauds are illustrated by comparison with like
things—snares and nets—where he compares her to hunters, fishers, and fowlers,
Proverbs 6:26. For she is no less eager to destroy people than they are to
destroy wild beasts, fish, and birds, and she brings destruction upon people
no less than they do upon beasts. Indeed, she brings a far heavier
destruction. They take away only a momentary life, but she also deprives them
of eternal life. See Proverbs 6:26, where she is said to hunt a person’s
precious soul.

Let us therefore not be like foolish beasts and incautious birds that put and
thrust their necks into her snares and nets. Otherwise we shall be more foolish
than the birds when they see the net, for a net is spread in vain for them
once they see the snares and nets, Proverbs 1. We, therefore,
who are taught by God’s Word and see the frauds and snares—the nets, as it
were, that the prostitute has set for our destruction—will be found more
foolish than brute animals themselves if we allow ourselves to be circumvented
by her frauds.

She is further described from her effects: namely, she holds captives and
prisoners in her hands. She presents herself as regarding them as friends and
as those dearest to her, while in reality she regards them as conquered men
and captives, celebrates triumphs over them as prisoners taken in war, and
desires everyone to know that she exercises dominion over them. See the
example of Delilah, Judges 16. With this agrees what Solomon affirms in
Proverbs 7:22 about the foolish youth who, enticed by a prostitute, goes like
an ox to the slaughter and as though to the stocks destined for the
chastisement of fools.

Thus, first, when he has been ensnared by her, the free and freeborn man
becomes a servant and slave; the honored man becomes infamous, Proverbs
5:9–10; the rich man becomes poor, or rather a beggar, Proverbs 6:26; and she
takes away from the healthy man a feeble and exhausted body, Proverbs 5:11.

Next, the person who is safe from her snares is indicated by his character:
he is the one who is good before God—that is, whom God approves and in whom he
delights. In Proverbs 3, 4, and 5, he declares the means of avoiding her: a
person must apply his mind to God’s Word. Therefore, no goodness of nature or
character, no civil and disciplined education, no literature, and no human
cleverness can free anyone from these nets and hooks unless he is loved by God
and, further, imbued with the precepts of the divine Word.

This is also illustrated by its contrary: namely, that the sinner is held
ensnared by her enticements and flatteries, Proverbs 22:14. The cause of this
ensnarement is indicated: he is hated by God and held by him in detestation.
This does not mean that all whom God detests and hates fall into this pit, for
then, with everyone thrown into confusion and disorder, there would be no
commonwealth or appearance of a commonwealth. For very many

wicked people, eager or thirsting for honors, are not led by any desire for
this shameful lust, because it is opposed and hostile to attaining the honors
they desire. Fornication must therefore be avoided not only because of the
harms already stated, but also lest, once committed, it gather testimony of
divine wrath and hatred against oneself—and so that one may instead obtain a
judgment of divine love toward oneself.

VERSE 27. “See, I found this,” says Ecclesiastes, “by seeking once and again to
attain the reasoning.”

The efficient cause of the discovery last mentioned is assigned: persistence
in seeking it, because he investigated again and again the matter that did not
occur to him when he first inquired. For wisdom wishes to be courted by her
students like an honorable lady full of dignity. A suitor who suffers one
rejection and departs without accomplishing his purpose is regarded as
unworthy to obtain his desire. Knowledge of the greatest matters is a certain
noble lady who does not immediately grant the opportunity of possessing her.

Let this keep us in the study of sacred literature, so that we do not allow
our study and ardor to be immediately beaten back if some difficult passage
occurs that we cannot penetrate with the keenness of our intellect and
understanding. Nevertheless, the purpose of this verse is to deliver to the
pious, as something known, the undoubted certainty of the teaching contained
in the preceding verse, so that they may guard themselves against that plague
the more diligently.

VERSE 28. The more my soul seeks the reasoning, it nevertheless does not
attain it. I may indeed attain the reasoning of one man out of every thousand,
but I do not attain that of a woman furnished with all these things.

This is another thing accompanying his investigation of folly: namely, the
difficulty of discovering the reasonings and deceitful ways of acting in that
lost woman of whom he spoke. In Proverbs 7 Solomon calls her “guarded in
heart”—that is, she conceals what she is thinking so that you cannot fish it
out either from her face or from her words. And in chapter 5, verse 6, he says
that we cannot weigh the path of her life or know her tracks because of her
utmost mobility and changeableness; see Proverbs 30:18–20.

He confirms this by comparison with the lesser, because it is easier to
explore a wicked man’s design and reasoning than a woman’s. For women who
depart from the right way are more unrestrained and less able than men to keep
themselves from crime. Hence it also happens that when women join bands of
male robbers they are ordinarily fiercer and more cruel than the men. Ahab is
marked out among all men for signal impiety, to such a degree that the Holy
Spirit notes that he had wholly surrendered and devoted himself to impiety.
Yet his wife Jezebel was much more wicked than he. Herod was renowned for
impiety, but Herodias surpassed him in crime. In our own age and memory there
have been women who easily surpassed the men of their generation in every
crime.

This should admonish women to conduct themselves modestly and, conscious of
their weakness, to take care not to trust themselves and their own judgment,
but to entrust themselves to be ruled and governed by those governors under
whom they are placed. Above all, let them earnestly pray God that, taking pity
on their weakness, he may gird them with the strength by which they may be
kept in their duty.

VERSE 28. The more my soul seeks the reasoning, it nevertheless does not
attain it. I may indeed attain the reasoning of one man out of every thousand,
but I do not attain that of a woman furnished with all these things.

VERSE 29. Only see: I found this, that God himself made humankind upright, but
they have sought out very many reasonings.

Although he could not separately investigate with precision the depths of
folly and

wickedness in individual people, he nevertheless notes in general that its
fountain and origin lies in the person himself. He confirms this by a
disjunctive syllogism. It comes either from God, who created him, or from the
person himself; but it does not come from God; therefore it comes from the
person. He proves that it does not come from God by the accompanying
uprightness with which God created the person.

After describing the boundless wickedness of humankind, and especially of a
wicked woman, Solomon employs a prolepsis so that he does not appear thereby
to bring human nature or the female sex into hatred; here he anticipates what
could have been objected. Preparing our attention for what he is about to say,
as a matter of great importance, he admonishes us that it is greatly in our
interest to have this teaching well known and established. He therefore
teaches that God made the human being—both male and female—upright, for under
this one term אדם both the male and female sex are comprehended, Genesis 1.
This is precisely what Moses says when he writes that the human being was made
and formed in the image and likeness of God.

Although this form of expression is elegant, it nevertheless appears to
contain some difficulty within itself, which is resolved by the expression
Solomon used here. For, according to the Prophet, there is nothing in the
whole universe of things that is like God or expresses him in even the least
part, Isaiah 46. If someone should now insist that an image of God can be
erected, fashioned, or painted—since the human being bears God’s image, and
thus one may fashion a human appearance for him—the answer from Solomon is
ready and prepared. A human being is like God not in the substance of body or
soul, but only because God imparted to the human being a certain spark of
uprightness derived from his flaming and burning fire, or a drop drawn from
his own sea. Moreover, by affirming that האדם—Adam with the definite article
*he*—was created upright, he teaches that the first-formed man is meant.

It is no contradiction that he next says “they” sought very many reasonings,
since Adam and Eve are two people comprehended under this name “Adam.” Further,
it can also be said, not ineptly, of all Adam’s posterity that it sought very
many reasonings. By imputation, the things Adam and Eve committed in the fall
are ours, since we were together with them and were enclosed in their loins,
as Levi was once in Abraham’s loins, Hebrews 7:9–10.

They are said to have sought reasonings that, without their thinking of them,
were devised and offered by the ancient serpent. By this Solomon wished to
teach that those frauds of Satan were eagerly seized by them. He says there
were very many reasonings, although the history testifies that they were
enticed by only two considerations: namely, that eating the forbidden fruit
would benefit body and mind together. It must be understood that these two
things comprehend many considerations. The promise that their eyes would be
opened and that they would be made like God contains many considerations: they
would be like God in happiness in every respect, no less wise, powerful,
honored, and complete in every virtue than he. As to the body, they persuaded
themselves that the fruit would be delightful and sweet.

If we wished to pursue all the reasonings of people descended from Adam, it
would be easier to begin speaking than to find an end. First, they try to shut
their own eyes so that they may persuade themselves that things are not sins
which ought rightly to be regarded not as slight offenses but as atrocious
crimes. They cover greed with the name “frugality”; luxury with the name
“fellowship and mutual sharing”; drunkenness and reveling with “cheerfulness”;
and fraud and deceit with the honorable title “prudence and caution.” They say
that thoughts, however wicked, are free and pay no tax, and that words are
nothing but wind and therefore

no account should be taken of them.

Even deeds committed against the law do not lack defenses: they were committed
through imprudence and incaution; they intended no evil in themselves or in
their mind; they do nothing that has not been done before, that is not now done
commonly and by the greatest part of humankind. The worn-out maxim here is
that we must do what most people do, so that as few people as possible will
regard us as monsters. Even in matters they do not deny to be crimes and
outrages, they are encouraged to commit them because God is merciful; because
they will afterward grieve over their deeds and be led to repentance; and by
six hundred other things of this kind. They say that ministers sound out such
dreadful threats as caution and terror rather than according to the truth of
the matter.

VERSE 29. Only see: I found this, that God himself made humankind upright, but
they have sought out very many reasonings.

The substance of Solomon’s statement in this verse is that people themselves
are the authors of their own wickedness and, through wickedness, of calamity
and unhappiness. See Lamentations 3:40–42 and Job 5:6–7. This must be diligently
noted, because people customarily divert blame from themselves onto someone or
something else. See the example of Adam and Eve, Genesis 3. Yet although we are
ingenious in this practice, it is astonishing how frequently that expression
is heard by which blame and punishment together are laid upon fortune and
chance as upon an empty idol. Sometimes they are laid upon fellow creatures,
as Balaam transferred the error clinging to himself onto his donkey. Sometimes
they are laid upon lifeless created things, as the Syrians attributed their
defeat by the Israelites to the mountains. Sometimes people lay them upon
their companions, as did the prostitute who had lain upon and smothered her
own infant, and sometimes upon those most closely joined to themselves, as in
Adam.

If someone is so openly convicted that he cannot deny the matter, then blame
is laid upon nature. God, who is the author of nature, is thereby indirectly
censured, instead of the person saying that corrupt and perverse nature was
the cause. Finally, rather than consent to make themselves defendants, they
rise up against God himself, blessed forever, and try to make him a partner in
their crime—but in vain. Although they employ the thorny little arguments of
their utterly vain intellect to cast the blame for their sin upon God, or at
least to transfer some portion of it to him, nevertheless, when God judges, he
will prevail in the case and carry off the prize, though all his slanderers
burst apart.

Thus, first, they bring a charge against him because he decreed and willed
Adam’s fall before all ages, without whose will it would not have occurred.
From this they infer that God is the cause of Adam’s fall and therefore of the
misery the fall introduced. But that inference and conclusion is not just. God
is not the cause of the fall; Satan and Adam’s will are. When Adam’s will began
to be tempted and tried, it did not summon divine aid by invoking God’s name.
God had imparted strength to him by which he could have overcome the
temptation if he had wished. God did not bend Adam’s will, much less impel it
to sin; he did not infuse malice; he did not remove or withdraw the good of
integrity he had imparted to him. He simply did not confirm him in the grace
bestowed, a thing to which he was neither liable nor bound.

Indeed, after this fall God conferred a greater benefit upon all the faithful
than he did at creation. For he gave Adam the ability to live if he so willed;
he gave us the will so that we might have the ability. To Adam he gave a
happiness from which he could fall; to us, grace and blessedness that we
cannot lose.

Next, in the manner of giants, they wage war with God because he predestined
one person to life and another to eternal death. But, O man, who are you to
contend with God? Will the earthen vessel dispute with the potter? Indeed, let
one potsherd dispute with another potsherd, but not with its own

potter. If a beast were to say to God, “Why did you fashion this one as a man
but me as a beast?” you would immediately answer, “Who are you, O beast?” and
so forth. And you too are a beast—or something more brutish than a beast—if
you compare yourself with God.

They continue by accusing God of injustice because he offers grace that no
one can accept once free choice has been removed. But the person fell into
this ἀδυναμία through his own fault. A servant sent to carry out his master’s
business becomes drunk by drinking and returns home without completing the
business. His master takes up the scourge with which to punish him, but the
servant pleads his ἀδυναμία arising from drunkenness. Do you think he should
rightly be exempted from punishment, or rather bound to a double punishment:
one because his master’s business was not completed, the other because he
became drunk? Solomon teaches that all these evasions are of no weight,
because a person is the author of both his own sin and his own destruction.
