# CHAPTER XIX

## Whether GOD is thus the principal agent while devils and wicked people sin,
so that he nevertheless remains immune from every stain, while the instruments
alone are defiled by the guilt of sin

Fourth, we argue as follows.

[Marginal heading: Argument IV.]

*Whoever uses human beings and devils in the very act of sinning as his
instruments in order to attain the ends he intends, to execute his judgments,
to avenge sins, and to humble the saints, wills that sin occur while he permits
it.*

*But GOD uses human beings and devils in the very act of sinning as his
instruments in order to attain the ends he intends, to execute his judgments,
to avenge sins, and to humble the saints.*

*Therefore GOD wills that sin occur while he permits it.*

[Marginal summary: The first distinction of the Evangelicals—between sin in
itself and sin insofar as it is a means of declaring God's glory—is defended.]

The major premise is proved. 1. Everyone who uses an instrument for a certain
end wills the motion and use of the instrument insofar as it is conducive to
that end. One who uses a saw, sword, or rod wills the motion of the saw, sword,
or rod—namely, when it splits wood, strikes an enemy, or beats a servant. 2.
Thomas puts it this way: “Nature's own operation is also an operation of divine
power, just as the operation of an instrument is through the power of the
principal agent.”

[Marginal citation: Thomas, *On Power*, question 3, article 7, m. 3.]

The minor premise is proved because sinning creatures are taken up by God as
the first agent: (1) as he punishes the sins of devils and human beings through
other sins as just penalties—through the Chaldeans who led Judah away captive,
he punishes idolatry; or (2) as GOD chastens—through Absalom's defiling his
father's bed, he chastens David for adultery and murder; or (3) as GOD tests
his servants—through the Sabaeans' robbery and plundering, he tests Job's trust
and hope in God, as well as his patience; or (4) as GOD humbles his own—through
Peter's fall, he drives out Peter's empty and swollen self-confidence and
makes him humbler, as Augustine says; or (5) as he redeems the world through
the unjust killing of the Savior; or (6) through persecutions he makes his own
more obedient to GOD'S commands.-

[Marginal summary: In punishing sinners, punishments are instruments taken up
by God to punish sins by means of sins.]

[Marginal citation: Augustine, *City of God*, book 14, chapter 13.]

[Chapter XIX; printed page 248; PDF page 308; BSB canvas 300]

Psalm 119:71. 2. Hence the wicked, while they sin by oppressing and devastating
GOD'S church, are called “the rod and staff of God's fury” (a), “the hired
razor” (b), “Babylon, the golden cup in God's hand” (c), “God's net” (d),
“fishers and hunters” (e), “God's sword” (f), “God's hand” (g), and “God's
hammer” (h). Indeed, while they serve themselves through the lust for
domination, they nevertheless render service to GOD, the principal agent who
executes his judgments (i). Wicked Nebuchadnezzar, while he tyrannically rages
against the church, is called “God's servant” (k). Zwingli likewise says: “Nor
does he injure the instrument, since all things are more truly his than any
craftsman's instruments are his, and a craftsman does his instruments no
injury if he now turns the file into a hammer and, conversely, the hammer into
a file” (l). Ferrariensis likewise says: “God uses every will for his own end”
(m). Driedo says that he “uses the sinner for his own work” (n). Lyra says that
he “uses the Assyrian as a rod” (o). Indeed, Bellarmine himself says: “God uses
the wickedness of Satan and a human being to punish one who deserves such a
penalty” (p). And the subtle Jesuit Didacus Ruiz says clearly: “The principal
cause is that which preconceives and preordains the action of the instrument
insofar as it is an instrument, and prescribes its measure and the other
circumstances for it; but although sinning devils and human beings are
principal causes with respect to their wickedness, nevertheless with respect
to inflicting grievous evils they are God's instruments.” Thus Jerome speaks
of the Assyrian: “O most foolish of mortals, do you think this wisdom of yours
to be God's, and do you attribute his command to your own strength?”

Hosea 7:12. (e) Jeremiah 16:16; Job 10:16; Habakkuk 1:14–15; Lamentations

*Against the Gentiles*, book 3, chapter 70. (n) Driedo, *On Concord*, chapter

no. 11; Jerome on Isaiah 4.]

[Marginal citation: First response. Guillaume Gibieuf, *On the Liberty of God
and the Creature*.]

The adversaries' first response to this argument is that “God wills the use,
not the origin, of sin.” Others commonly put it this way: “GOD does not act
through the instrument while it sins; nor does GOD will that the instrument's
sin exist. Therefore GOD does not will that it arise and be committed. But on
the supposition that the sin has already arisen and been committed, GOD wills
that it yield to his own glory and be for the sinner a grievous evil—penal and
chastening.”

[Marginal summary: Refutation 1 of the evasion of those who say that God wills
the use, but in no way the origin, of sin.]

I answer: 1. This is asserted unjustly: after the Sabaeans' robbery and
Shimei's abuse have been committed, GOD wills and intends-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 249; PDF page 309; BSB canvas 301]

that the former be a chastening evil for Job and the latter be penal and
ignominious for David. For no wise person intends something as a punishment for
a guilty person if it is already penal for that person before the intention.
But Shimei's abuse is penal and ignominious for David before every intention of
GOD, “because,” they say, “GOD neither intends nor wills the origin of sin, but
only its use.”

2. All GOD'S threats and many of his promises in this matter will be
ridiculous. For just as God is free to threaten evil and promise good, so God
is free to inflict the thing he threatens or to furnish the thing he promises
(for GOD'S immutability does not overthrow freedom). Yet this escape removes
GOD'S freedom in very many instances, because, on the supposition that
Absalom's incest is already dishonorable to David, God cannot with any true
freedom fail to will that it be dishonorable to David. God therefore speaks as
follows: “It is contrary to my holiness and repugnant to my nature to will that
the Chaldeans lead my people away into exile. But on the supposition that they
lead them away against my will, I threaten them with that abominable deportation
as the just vengeance for my people's apostasy and idolatry.” Likewise: “It is
repugnant to my nature to will the incestuous defilement of David's bed by
Absalom; yet on the supposition that it occurs, I threaten that defilement as
just compensation for Uriah's bed defiled by David.” Likewise: “I neither will
nor decree the unjust destruction of the Chaldeans by the Medes and Persians,
who are drawn by the lust for domination alone. But on the supposition that
this destruction exists as a consolation to my people, I promise that
destruction as a solace for my people.”

But it is too well known from Scripture to admit of ignorance that God freely
wills what he threatens and promises; 2. that God makes good his threats and
promises and fulfills them; and 3. that it is utterly absurd for GOD to
threaten as a punishment what is already penal for those whom he threatens
before that threat, or to promise as a good what comes to those to whom the
promise is made before the promise has been given. Nor can they point here to
some little interval of time between the sin and the penal character adhering
to it. For this is incomprehensible (ἀκατάληπτον).

4. They do not explain what they understand here by the use of sin. The use of
sin here is often the punishment or chastening of a person through the very act
of sinning. But while savage assassins kill CHRIST, in that act of killing God
redeems the world; while Absalom commits incest, in that very act God chastens
David. For here the action of GOD, the principal agent, and of Absalom was one
and the same—Absalom-

[Marginal references: Isaiah 21:16–17; Isaiah 14:24; 1 Kings 15:29–30; 1 Kings
22:38; Ezekiel 12:25; Jeremiah 44:29.]

[Chapter XIX; printed page 250; PDF page 310; BSB canvas 302]

who was the instrument. It was, I say, one and the same action really,
physically, or materially, although it was not the same morally, as I shall
teach presently. God therefore does not merely on the supposition that such an
action of Absalom exists will it to be penal for David and declarative of the
glory of justice. For God is the principal agent in that action and the
principal cause judging and punishing David by such an incestuous action, just
as he had previously threatened in 2 Samuel 12:10–12.

[Marginal heading: Second response, that of the Doctors of Salamanca. Not the
act, but the lawless act, is something penal.]

2. The Doctors of Salamanca answer: “Sin *qua* sin is not David's chastening;
rather, the entitative act of incest is David's chastening. Consequently it by
no means follows from this that GOD wills sin as a punishment, but only that he
wills the entitative act as a punishment.”

I answer: The entitative act involved in incest is not in itself a punishment,
but an entitatively good act and morally indifferent. For Shimei's act of
speaking was not a chastening evil for David; only his abusive act of speaking
was. Nor was just any removal of Job's goods, considered simply, Job's
chastening. Rather, it was the removal made through robbery, contrary to every
human and divine right, by people from whom the man had deserved nothing evil.
That removal, I say, tested Job's faith and patience. For if Job's goods had
been removed with his consent and freely given away, there would have been no
occasion to exercise faith and patience.

[Marginal citation: Third response. Stapleton, volume 2, *On the Cause of Sin*,
book 2, chapter 11.]

3. Stapleton answers: “Satan and wicked human beings, in the act of sinning, do
not execute GOD'S just judgments, but only their own wickedness. Consequently,
sinners in the act of sinning are not subject to GOD here as instruments.”

[Marginal summary: In the same act in which the wicked sin, they serve GOD'S
just decree.]

I answer: Stapleton sets in opposition things that ought not to be opposed and
actually says that Satan and wicked human beings, while they sin, “do not
execute GOD'S just judgments, but do execute GOD'S just judgments.” For in the
very fact that they fulfill their wickedness by sinning, in that same act they
execute GOD'S judgments. While the Sabaeans plunder Job, in that act of
plundering they serve GOD'S most holy decree to test Job. While the false
prophets seduce Ahab and entice him into a war forbidden by God, so that he may
there be removed from the scene because of his idolatry and seizure of
Naboth's vineyard, in that act of seduction they serve GOD'S just decree, by
which he had predestined the wicked king to the death he had most justly
deserved. And thus those who violate “the will of GOD,” as Augustine says,
“fall into the just will of GOD.” 2. In the same place Stapleton contradicts
himself when he says that “God directs evils to good ends, either to execute
his judgments or to execute his power-

[Marginal citations: Augustine; Stapleton, in the same work, chapter 3.]

[Chapter XIX; printed page 251; PDF page 311; BSB canvas 303]

or to exercise and display it.” I admit that the wicked in the act of sinning
are not GOD'S formal, rational instruments, endowed with formal knowledge of
GOD'S decrees; rather, they are as it were seized upon beyond their expectation
and design.

[Marginal citation: Fourth response. Bellarmine, *On the Loss of Grace*, book
2, etc.; the Remonstrants' writing at the synod, article 1, pages 14–16.]

4. Bellarmine and the Remonstrants at the Synod press the following retort
here: “If devils and wicked human beings who sin are instruments subordinated
to GOD as the principal agent in the act of sinning, then all sins must be
ascribed to GOD as the first, proper, and principal cause, but to the human
beings and devils who sin only as organic causes which act only in the power of
the principal cause and as causes less properly so called.

“But the former is asserted by Zwingli, Calvin, Beza, and others.

“Therefore all sins must be ascribed to GOD as their principal and proper
cause, but to the human beings and devils who sin only as instruments,” etc.

They base the rationale of the major premise on this topical maxim: “An action
and effect is more properly and principally the action and effect of the
principal cause than of the instrumental cause. Thus skillful writing is to be
ascribed more properly to the skilled writer than to the pen; homicide is to
be imputed to the person moving the sword rather than to the sword. For the
principal, foremost, and first causal influence is from the principal agent,
but from the instrumental cause only secondarily, by participation in the
power and its derivation from the principal cause.” They prove the minor
premise: “Because our theologians,” they say, “teach that the wicked Assyrians
and Chaldeans, in the act of devastating the church, are subordinated to GOD as
a rod, hammer, or net in a person's hand. The use and movement of a rod or
hammer is attributed to the people who hold it in their hand and use it.”

[Marginal summary: The maxim concerning the extent to which an instrument's
action is to be attributed more to the principal cause than to the instrumental
cause is more fully unraveled and qualified.]

This is almost the only argument in which they seem to triumph and sacrifice
to their Muses. Since it is a knot worthy of one who can untie it, let us test
how formidable it is. Meanwhile, let the following distinctions be noted. I
shall explain them in what follows against Becanus, Bellarmine, Stapleton, and
others.

1. Distinction. Three things in this maxim—“the action and effect belong more
to the principal than to the instrumental cause”—must be distinguished: 1.
what the *cause* is; 2. what the *action and effect* are; and 3. what the
*instrument* is.

2. Distinction. “One cause is homogeneous; another is heterogeneous.”

3. “One cause is physical and real; another moral.”

4. “It is one thing to attribute the effect to the principal cause concretely;
it is another to attribute that same effect-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 252; PDF page 312; BSB canvas 304]

to it with respect to the particular aspect involved in the concrete whole.”

5. “One instrument is properly so called; another is called an instrument
improperly and metaphorically.”

6. “One cause is subject to law; another cause is above law.”

7. “One thing is the whole aggregate and concrete work of sin; another is sin
itself formally.”

8. “One instrument acts freely; another is brute and irrational (ἄλογον).”

[Marginal heading: First restriction of this maxim: the action or effect
belongs more properly, or more, to the principal than to the instrumental
cause.]

The whole rationale for untying this difficulty depends on a correct
explanation of the maxim, “The action and effect belong more properly to the
principal cause than to the instrumental cause.” The maxim holds for
homogeneous causes, not heterogeneous ones. Causes are homogeneous here when
the principal and instrumental causes are both either positive, both
privative, both physical, or both moral. But they are heterogeneous when the
one—namely, the principal cause—is positive and physical or quasi-physical,
while the other—namely, the instrumental cause—is privative and moral; or when
one is subject to law and the other is above law.

Hence, 1. the maxim holds for homogeneous causes. “As the word of God
sanctifies as an instrument, therefore the Holy Spirit sanctifies more properly
and principally through the word of God.” Likewise: “Timothy saves himself and
others by preaching. Therefore God saves more properly and principally through
Timothy.” Likewise: “The false prophet seduces Ahab. Therefore the devil more
properly and principally seduces Ahab through the false prophet as an
instrument.” All these inferences are valid according to the foregoing maxim,
because the principal and instrumental causes are homogeneous. In the first
two examples, both causes are positive; in the last, both are privative causes.
But an inference does not hold for heterogeneous causes, as in this example:

“The executioner, as the instrument of a just judge, drives the guilty person
to the cross from a zeal for vengeance.

“Therefore the just judge more properly and principally drives the guilty
person to the cross from a zeal for vengeance.”

Likewise:

“A sword that kills a person as an instrument is not a culpable cause of the
homicide.

“Therefore the murderer moving the sword is no more a culpable cause of the
homicide.”

Neither inference holds, because the causes are heterogeneous. In the former-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 253; PDF page 313; BSB canvas 305]

example, the principal cause is positive, moral, consonant with law, and just;
but the instrumental cause is privative and immorally discordant with law. In
the latter example, the principal cause is privative, subject to law, and
moral; but the latter cause—namely, the instrumental one—is physical, positive,
and subject to no law. For the murderer is a moral and privative cause subject
to law, while the sword is a physical and positive cause subject to no law.

Likewise, in this example of the adversaries, God and the sinning devils and
human beings are heterogeneous causes. God is a positive cause, not subject to
the law given to human beings, and therefore a quasi-physical cause. I say
“quasi-physical,” because, since God is a just judge, he is a moral cause in an
unqualified sense; for GOD'S good pleasure, regulated by infinite wisdom, is
here the principle of the act. Yet he is a quasi-physical cause because GOD
acts here as no more subject to moral law than any natural cause. God punishes
David through Absalom's incest, forbidden by no law from punishing him in that
way; but Absalom commits incest, prohibited by the law of the Decalogue's
seventh commandment. Thus the maxim, “The action of sin belongs more properly
and principally to GOD than to Absalom,” does not hold here. I have thus
distinguished the causes; I now distinguish the actions and effects.

[Marginal heading: Second restriction.]

2. The maxim holds only where the action of the principal cause is morally
altogether the same as the instrument's action. For example:

“Arius's disciple thinks heretically about the Son of God.

“Therefore Arius himself more properly and principally thinks heretically
about the Son of GOD.”

Likewise:

“Doeg, who kills Jehovah's priests as Saul's instrument, is a murderer.

“Therefore King Saul himself, who ordered him to kill them, is more properly
and principally a murderer.”

Both inferences are valid because the moral action of both causes—the principal
and the organic—is the same. But the maxim does not hold when the action of the
principal cause and that of the organic cause is indeed the same materially and
physically, but not morally. Suppose a father and his little son, two servants
subject to Titius as master, roll a stone. The father, the principal mover,
rolls it at the master's command; the little son, his helper, rolls it although
the master Titius has forbidden him. Hence the following inference does not
hold:

“The rolling-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 254; PDF page 314; BSB canvas 306]

of the stone performed by the son, the instrumental helper of his father, is
wrongful, for he rolls the stone although forbidden by the master.

“Therefore the same rolling performed by the father, who rolls it at command,
is more wrongful.”

For although the action of the father and his little son—namely, the same
rolling of the stone—is physically and materially the same, it is nevertheless
not morally the same. The father's rolling is lawful and right because the
master commands it, whereas the rolling of the little son, his father's
instrument, is unlawful because it occurs while the master forbids and
prohibits it.

So too in the present matter: GOD takes away Job's goods through the Sabaeans;
the action of GOD and of the Sabaeans is physically and really the same. The
same sheep and oxen that the Sabaeans took away, God took away through the
Sabaeans; the same servants killed by the Sabaeans in the same physical action
were killed by God. For if there had been two numerically distinct physical
actions, Job would have been despoiled twice—by the Sabaeans in one physical
action and by GOD in another—and Job's servants would have been killed twice,
once by the Sabaeans and once by GOD, which is ridiculous.

[Marginal summary: The action of God and of the Sabaeans in taking Job's goods
was physically, but not morally, the same.]

But although GOD'S and the Sabaeans' action is physically the same, it is not
morally the same. GOD'S action in taking away Job's goods was morally equitable
and just in all its causes. With respect to the efficient principle, GOD'S good
pleasure in this taking was utterly free and most just. With respect to the
matter, God took from Job gifts that were not another's, but his own and the
Creator's property. With respect to the form, no law forbids God to take his
own things from Job. With respect to the end, he took these goods to exercise
his servant's faith and patience and to manifest the glory of grace and of his
supreme liberty.

But this same physical action of the Sabaeans, which was one with GOD'S action,
was morally wicked and unjust in relation to GOD'S law. It was wicked with
respect to its efficient cause because it flowed from a mind at once avaricious
and murderous. It was wicked with respect to its matter because they forcibly
took another's goods, not their own, against the owner's will. With respect to
its form it was wicked because they were forbidden by the Decalogue's eighth
commandment to steal another's property. With respect to its end it was wicked
because they despoiled an innocent man to enrich themselves.

Thus, when the action of the principal agent and the organic cause is not both
morally and physically the same, it is false that “the organic cause's action
is to be ascribed more properly and principally to the principal cause.”
Indeed, it is not to be ascribed to the principal cause at all. For the
Schoolmen rightly teach that “an act's specification with respect to moral
being does not arise from the real act's physical substance, but from diverse
moral circumstances,” because-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 255; PDF page 315; BSB canvas 307]

the same physical action can be right or unjust according to the difference
between agents subject to law and those released from law. Thus an executioner
acts justly when, by the judge's sentence, he fastens a guilty person to the
gallows; but another person who intrudes upon the executioner's office acts
unjustly if he secretly strangles in prison that same guilty person condemned
by the judge's sentence.

[Marginal heading: Third restriction.]

3. I have shown that there is a broad distinction between causes, and also
between actions; now, in the third place, I distinguish here between
instruments. For 1. I distinguish between an instrument properly so called and
one called an instrument metaphorically. 2. I distinguish between a *pure
instrument*, an instrument without qualification, and one that is not pure but
has something of its own mixed in which it does not borrow from the principal
agent. 3. I distinguish between a *rational instrument* and an *irrational
instrument* (ἄλογον).

Concerning the first and second, the most learned Dr. Pareus rightly says:
“Nor, when Scripture calls Assyria a rod and a saw in GOD'S hand, does it
understand this properly. For GOD does not properly have a hand, nor was
Assyria properly a rod. It speaks metaphorically, because through Assyria he
would punish his people, just as a master beats a wicked servant with a rod.”

[Marginal citation: Pareus, *Correction of Bellarmine*, on *The Loss of Grace*,
book 2, chapter 4, lecture 42.]

Sinning human beings and devils are such instruments through which God executes
his judgments in their crimes—punishing the wicked, chastening and correcting,
and testing his servants. But they are not pure *instruments*; they are not
formal *instruments*, because they have something of their own which they do
not have from the principal agent. Indeed, they have something of principal
causality and something of their own mixed in beyond or above the character of
a pure *instrument*, which they do not have from GOD. Therefore the maxim does
not hold in this form: “Whatever is attributed to a metaphorical, rational,
and impure instrument—or to one that is not simply an instrument—is also to be
attributed to the principal cause.” Hence the maxim must be corrected as
follows: “What belongs to an instrument *qua* instrument is attributed more
properly to the principal cause.”

[Marginal summary: The defect that is peculiar to the instrument is not to be
attributed to the principal cause.]

Thomas rightly explains it this way: “It must be said that the act of a
servant”—an instrument—“insofar as it proceeds from the servant's will, is not
the master's act, but is so only insofar as it proceeds from the master's
command.”

[Marginal references: Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I–II, question 21, article 6,
reply 3; Cumel, I–II, question 47, article 2, reply 2.]

Hence the following inference does not hold:

“A horse driven by its rider's spurs limps.

“Therefore the rider himself more properly and principally limps.”

[Marginal explanation: One who drives a lame person so that he advances is the
cause of his advancing, and yet is not the cause of his advance being a limp.]

For the horse is not the rider's pure instrument, but an instrument that has
something of its own in the limping motion, in which it is not subordinated to
the rider as a mere instrument. Namely, it has from itself, and not-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 256; PDF page 316; BSB canvas 308]

from the rider, the dislocated leg that is the adequate and principal cause of
the limp. Nor is the following a good inference:

“A squeaky pen on absorbent paper produces malformed writing.

“Therefore the elegant writer more principally produces the malformed
writing.”

[Marginal citation: Pareus, in the place cited.]

For, as Pareus rightly notes in the same place, the instrument's defect must
not be ascribed to the principal agent. Consequently, the instrument's action
in the concrete—or the whole aggregate and concrete work consisting of
wickedness and penal character—is attributed to GOD, the principal agent, but
not according to every aspect involved in the concrete whole.

[Marginal summary: The whole action in the concrete is not attributed to the
principal and instrumental causes according to every aspect involved in the
concrete whole.]

A craftsman moves a saw back and forth, while the saw moves by gnawing. The same
act is attributed to the saw and the craftsman, but not according to every
aspect peculiar to the saw. Sawing by gnawing is from the saw alone, but sawing
according to the back-and-forth action is from the craftsman alone.

The concrete taking of Job's goods is attributed both to GOD as the principal
agent and to the Sabaeans as GOD'S instruments, but not to each cause according
to every aspect involved in the concrete and aggregate whole of the taking of
Job's goods. The aspect of wickedness and the aspect of penalty or chastening
constitute one total concrete whole. The wickedness in that taking—or the
wicked taking—is from the Sabaeans not as pure instruments. For the Sabaeans,
as principal causes, take Job's goods from their own sheer robbery and
plundering, and this action as such is not to be ascribed to GOD. But the same
taking of Job's goods, insofar as it chastens Job and inflicts upon him a penal
evil—that is, a grievous evil harmful to an innocent man in the category of
damaging and disadvantageous evil, but not in the category of dishonorable and
morally unjust evil—is more properly and principally GOD'S action. Yet it
belongs to the Sabaeans, not as pure instruments, when they inflict the harmful
evil of chastening on GOD'S holy servant. Thus sinners in the act of sinning
are subordinated to God, who executes his judgments in such an act, as
instruments considered materially and physically, or as impure instruments;
but not as formal, moral, and pure instruments.

This is proved, 1. as follows. Those instruments which do not receive the moral
impulse of wickedness from God, the principal agent, are not instruments
subordinated to GOD in the act of sinning insofar as it is such an act. But
devils and wicked human beings who sin receive no moral impulse from GOD, the
principal agent, in the act of sinning. If they did, God would indeed be the
principal author of sin.

[Marginal summary: A distinction among instruments, some of which receive the
whole impulse from the principal cause, while others do not.]

2. In pure instruments, which are instruments only and instruments without
qualification, there is no principal causality, but only-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 257; PDF page 317; BSB canvas 309]

a merely organic causality flowing from the power of the principal agent. But
devils and wicked human beings are principal causes in the act of sinning—and
indeed the adequate and total causes of sin—except that in the act of inflicting
penal evil upon those whom God punishes or chastens through them in that act,
they are instrumental causes. Hence human beings in the act of sinning are not
subordinated to God in this act *qua* such in the way a rod is subordinated to
the one lifting it or a sword to one striking with it. In a sword or rod there
is no motion except what is merely organic, because they are pure instruments:
they have no principal motion, but only an organic motion in the power of the
principal mover. But sinning human beings and devils are truly principal causes
of sins.

God therefore takes service to himself here from the wicked. But of what kind?
Certainly the creature's deviation is subordinated materially, not formally,
to God the Judge. Someone attacks an enemy with a sword, with the mind and
fixed design of killing him, but meanwhile opens a ripe abscess with the sword.
Here this enemy materially serves the life and health of the very person he had
resolved to kill. Isaiah speaks similarly: “The Assyrian does not propose it to
himself in this way, nor does his mind think this way; rather, it is in his mind
to destroy.” Yet in the same place he says that GOD “accomplishes all his work
through the Assyrian.”

[Marginal reference: Isaiah 10:7, 12.]

Through sinning *instruments* GOD accomplishes his whole concrete work in Zion
by afflicting, chastening, humbling, and exercising the faith and patience of
his own. But he does not accomplish the whole of the work, or the whole work
according to all the aspects involved and connoted in the concrete whole. And
although God does not make evil wills, as Augustine rightly says, he
nevertheless does more than merely use sins already committed.

[Marginal citation: Augustine, *On the Predestination of the Saints*.]

I admit that it is human beings who sin, but it is not human beings who, by
sinning, formally redeem the human race. Augustine rightly says: “That they
themselves sin is from them; that by sinning they accomplish this or that is
from the power of GOD, who divides the darkness from the light as he sees fit.”

[Marginal citation: Augustine, in the same place.]

Calvin likewise says: “Furthermore, whenever it pleased him to take vengeance
upon the people's transgressions, how does he fulfill his work? So that you may
see that the efficacy of acting belonged to him, while they supplied only the
service.”

[Marginal citation: Calvin, *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 4, section 4.]

I wish “service” here to be understood as pure and formal with respect to the
aspects involved in the concrete whole—that is, with respect to GOD as he
punishes, chastens, and tests his own. But with respect to the work in the
concrete, it is impure and material service. The spoliation of Job insofar as
it is morally evil belongs to the Sabaeans themselves, and indeed its principal
efficiency belongs to them. But the spoliation insofar as it tests Job-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 258; PDF page 318; BSB canvas 310]

belongs to God. For its judicial and principal efficiency belongs to holy
providence, which imposes a new form, as it were—namely, the chastening and
testing of his servant—upon its rude and unformed matter, namely, their unjust
spoliation. For in preordaining that the sin should exist—that is, that the
spoliation of Job should exist—God ordained by the same decree that this
spoliation should be penal and chastening for Job.

[Marginal heading: Second distinction. Some instruments are unknowing and
passive; others are active and knowing.]

But Calvin, who uses the word *compel* here—for this can be objected—does not
intend sinners in the act of sinning to be subordinated to GOD as *formal
instruments* properly so called. For he says that “enemies are driven by secret
providence where GOD sees fit”; likewise, that “by his secret impulse they are
aroused to destroy GOD'S people and to render services, as if compelled by a
bridle.” Zwingli, with a harsher turn of phrase, says that “human beings are
compelled by GOD to crimes.” And Beza says: “Through those instruments, God
acts so that he does not merely permit them to act. Nor does he merely govern
the outcome, but he also arouses, drives, moves, and directs them; and indeed,
what is greatest of all, he even creates them so that through those agents he
may do what he has resolved.”

These statements are easily resolved by a twofold distinction. There is a
*compulsion properly so called*, by which force is inflicted upon free choice.
Our theologians do not speak of such compulsion. There is also an *impulsive
and improperly so-called compulsion*, which is compatible with freedom and
entails modal necessity, conditioned necessity in a certain respect, and a
necessity of the consequence, but does not entail a pure, unqualified, absolute
necessity or a necessity of the consequent.

[Marginal references: Calvin, commentary on Psalm 5; commentary on Isaiah 10:5;
*Institutes*, book 1, chapter 17, q. 1; book 2, chapter 4, q. 2; Zwingli, *On
Providence*, chapter 6, an oration on providence delivered at Marburg in 1529
in the hall of Philip, Landgrave of Hesse; Beza, *Response to Castellio*, at
aphorism 22.]

2. I add what Dr. Pareus observes: “God drives, turns, directs, moves, compels,
arouses, and even creates human beings for evildoing insofar as those deeds are
punishments, judgments, chastenings, and means serving GOD'S design—not insofar
as they are crimes and sins. He moves the robber,” says Zwingli, “to kill
another.” “He moves him,” says Pareus, “because he kills another only when
moved by GOD, by reason of the secret judgment in which GOD purposes to
transfer the slain man to heaven and drive the robber to the cross.”

[Marginal references: Pareus on Bellarmine, *The Loss of Grace*, book 2,
chapter 3, lecture 40; Zwingli, *On Providence*, chapter 6; Pareus, in the same
place.]

3. “Some free instruments are active, others passive.” They are active when
they act at the direction and command of a superior and know and intend to obey
that superior. Thus Doeg was King Saul's instrument in killing JEHOVAH'S
priests. They are passive when, although utterly free in the material work—for
example, in perpetrating the sin—and conducting themselves all too actively,
nevertheless with respect to that most eminent act of *providence-performing*,
if I may speak so, and of secret provi-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 259; PDF page 319; BSB canvas 311]

dence, as Calvin says, the wicked—while doing something else, blind, unknowing,
virtually unwilling, reluctant, and unaware—fall into the execution of GOD'S
secret will and decree. Thus they are quasi-*passive instruments*. Joseph's
brothers who sold him were such *instruments* for exalting Joseph, as were the
killers of our Savior; in this way they supplied organic service for executing
GOD'S decree concerning the redemption of the human race. GOD'S prophet speaks
this way concerning Zion's oppressors: “They do not know Jehovah's thoughts,
nor do they understand his counsel.”

[Marginal reference: Micah 4:12.]

The reverend Theodore Beza must be understood in this way when he says that
“God arouses, drives, moves, directs, and indeed—what is greatest of all—even
creates, so that through those agents”—blind, unknowing, and quasi-passive—“he
may do what he has resolved.” Hence, when an instrument is a free agent and yet
virtually unwilling, doing something else and unaware that by transgressing it
accomplishes this or that which the principal agent has resolved; and when it
is at the same time an agent subject to law and morally obligated not to act,
while the principal agent is not subject to that same law: then, in this case,
the action of the instrument that is thus obligated and yet acts contrary to
the agent's duty and obligation is not more properly and principally the action
of the principal agent who is not subject to law than it is the instrument's.
For contrary relations—namely, obligation and non-obligation—establish contrary
moral specifications in the principal agent and the instrument.

[Marginal summary: The action of an instrument that is held by no obligation
is not to be attributed to the principal cause, which is released from the bond
of every obligation.]

I call free *instruments* quasi-passive here because they are as ignorant of
GOD'S supreme decree as a rod or sword, which is utterly ignorant of the work
and goal of the person moving the sword or rod. And although it had been
revealed to Judas the traitor that he would be the man through whom the Son of
GOD would be betrayed, and although he had betrayed the Savior with the design
and intention of executing such a decree of GOD, nevertheless he was obligated
by GOD'S approving will not to betray the Savior. Therefore, even in this case,
Judas would no more be owed thanks for fulfilling the decree than a sword is
owed the praise for a victory.

The adversaries do not consider that one and the same work, in relation to the
principal agent obligated to act and in relation to the *instrument* obligated
not to act, is both due and not due, whenever one sin is the punishment of
another sin. The profound Schoolman Thomas Bradwardine expresses it this way:
“Just as when a sinner is struck by someone to whom it does not belong to
strike him, because the former ought to be struck and the latter ought not to
strike, the act ought to be-

[Marginal citation: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 32.]

[Chapter XIX; printed page 260; PDF page 320; BSB canvas 312]

and ought not to be; and therefore it cannot be denied to be right and not
right.” David most justly deserved from GOD the disgrace involved in the
violation of his concubines, and that violation was a just punishment of divine
justice, since he had violated the only wife of the innocent man Uriah after
her husband and protector had been slaughtered. Was this not a due, just, and
right work of GOD? Yet on Absalom's part that violation was unjust and not due.
The ungrateful monster of a man ought not to have defiled the bed at once of
his father, his prince, and the Lord's anointed. Is not the same concrete work
both due and just, a work of exact justice, and also undue and unjust, a work
of injustice?

Thus, whenever the concrete result (ἀποτέλεσμα) and effect is formed and, as it
were, composed in the concrete from what is morally due and morally undue, the
instrument's effect is not attributed to the principal agent, and the
principal agent's effect is not attributed to the instrument.

[Marginal heading: Fourth restriction.]

This is the maxim's fourth restriction, because neither the principal agent's
justice nor any share of praise is due to the instrument as instrumental cause.
No praise for GOD'S justice in the defilement of David's bed is due to Absalom;
nor is the instrument's defect due to the principal agent, who does not act
here contrary to duty and the legal obligation that binds the deviating
instrument. For Vorstius, Arminius, Bellarmine, and the Jesuits do not deny that
it was most just for David's bed to be defiled by incest. We therefore deny in
this case the maxim that “the instrument's effect is more principally and
properly to be attributed to the principal cause.”

For when the principal cause and the *instrument* are not strictly subordinated
but instead opposed through contrary moral specifications, the one cause—namely,
the principal—performs the concrete work in accordance with duty and contrary
to no obligation in moral matters, not the reverse; but the other—namely, the
organic cause—performs that same concrete work contrary to duty and contrary
to legal obligation. And thus, in moral consideration, they approach more
nearly the nature of partial and opposed causes than the nature of subordinated
causes.

[Marginal summary: The wicked are instruments serving GOD'S decree not in moral
matters—not instruments properly so called, but physical and improper ones.]

But these things are so clear that they ought to seem obscure only to someone
who willfully blinds himself in the Lord's judgments. And let me assert that
the wicked, when in sinning they serve GOD'S decree, are *physical, not moral,
instruments* subordinated to God. For although in the act of sinning they are
moral and indeed principal agents, nevertheless inasmuch as by sinning they
accomplish this or that—namely, as instruments they execute the work of the
Lord's just judgment—they-

[Chapter XIX; printed page 261 (upper portion); PDF page 321; BSB canvas 313]

are not moral but physical instruments. My rationale is this: Every formally
moral instrument in a just and good work has some organic activity in that
good work, and thus some praise as an instrument. But Absalom, in executing
that just judgment of GOD, has no praise—not even the smallest share of
praise—but the greatest blame in this good work of GOD. Accordingly, as such
he is only a physical, not a moral, instrument—an instrument not properly so
called.
