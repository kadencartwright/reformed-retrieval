# CHAPTER XXVI

## It is proved against Dr. Walaeus and Dr. Strangius that the fourth precept is not partly moral and partly ceremonial

Here those preliminary points found in Chapter XXII of this book must be
recalled. And this further point must be added: namely, that we ought to
distinguish between the thing commanded by a precept and the things added,
whether as arguments and motives or as explanations of the command. For when
we speak of some precept and inquire what its nature is, we understand the
substance of the command, or the thing itself enjoined by the command, not the
remaining things added either for the sake of explanation or as arguments.
For these do not pertain to the essence of the command. The command would
have been whole and complete even if they had not been added, just as we see
certain other commands proposed without qualification, such as the Sixth,
Seventh, Eighth, and so forth. They are complete although nothing is attached
either as an explanation or as a reason and motive.

The thing itself commanded by this fourth precept is the observance of a
Solemn Time instituted by God. This time is first proposed indefinitely when
it is called the Sabbath, or a sacred time of rest consecrated to God. Then it
is proposed definitely, both with respect to its duration, or how long, when
it is called a day, and with respect to its recurrence, or how often, when it
is called the seventh, or one out of seven. The observance of this time is
first intimated in the word Sabbath, which denotes a sacred time, or a time
consecrated to God and separated from daily and common works. Thus a cessation
from daily works necessarily follows, as nature itself dictates in the
festivals and holidays of the Gentiles, upon a Sabbath, which connotes rest.
But afterward it is proposed more explicitly when this is added: *that you
sanctify it—you shall do no work in it.* The substance of the fourth precept
is therefore this: *Sanctify every seventh day to God; or let one whole day in
the weekly cycle be a Sabbath for you.* It will be proved afterward that this
is the substance of this precept; here it is presupposed.

The question, therefore, is whether this substance of the precept is partly
Ceremonial and partly Moral, not whether something is contained in the body
of the command that is partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. Nor is the question
whether something that can be called Ceremonial or mutable is commanded
consequently by this precept, in respect of which this precept could be called
Ceremonial or mutable. The reason is that ceremonial worship was consequently
commanded in the second precept, and in this respect we can say that that
precept was ceremonial. But when it is asked whether that precept was Moral,
Ceremonial, or partly Moral and partly Ceremonial, we speak about what is
directly and primarily commanded in that precept. Thus the question here
concerns the substance of the precept itself.

We say, therefore, that the substance of this precept is not partly Moral and
partly Ceremonial, and consequently that this precept is not partly Moral and
partly Ceremonial. There is no need for us to prove this at length now, since
from the

[Printed p. 278 | IA PDF 284 | IA leaf 284 | IA decausadei02brow]

arguments by which we have already proved that this precept is Moral, it is
sufficiently manifest; since the thing commanded in each of the words or
precepts of the Decalogue is pure and simple, not mixed or composite; and,
finally, since the Moral and the Ceremonial are so distinguished by their own
proper concepts that they cannot be joined and compounded into one.

It will be sufficient to vindicate certain arguments proposed above from the
exceptions of the most illustrious Walaeus, who treats this subject at length
in his *Dissertation on the fourth precept*, and of Dr. Strangius, who follows
Dr. Walaeus's opinion.

In Chapter 3 of that Dissertation he examines the arguments of those who
acknowledge nothing Ceremonial in this fourth precept. But first he premises a
distinction both of the word *Moral* and of the word *Ceremonial*. He says that
Ceremonial is taken [Either for what is ceremonial, figurative, and typical,
which has a definite relation to Christ our Redeemer, as the sacrifices of
the Old Testament had; or for any sacred rite instituted by God for observing
right order and decorum in the Church. Certain things of this kind concerning
the circumstances of external worship were also ordained by the Apostles in
the New Testament, either in perpetuity or only for a time.] Yet he does not
indicate to us in which sense he takes the word Ceremonial here, which it was
necessary to do in order to explain this question properly. It is certain
that in this question the word cannot be taken in both senses. For the same
thing cannot distinctly be conceived both as a mere circumstance of worship
and as something figurative and typical. What has the character of a type is
not a mere external circumstance of worship. Nor, properly speaking, does it
make no difference in which sense it is taken here, since he himself admits
here that certain circumstances of worship are to be observed in perpetuity
and can in some manner be called moral because they are immutable. But from
the things he has in Chapter 2, which are to be examined afterward, it is
evident that he takes the word Ceremonial here in the former sense, unless he
thinks the sacraments of the Old Testament were mere external circumstances
of worship.

Then, with *Scotus, book 3, distinction 37, question 1*, he distinguishes the
Moral [Into the Moral that belongs to the law of nature strictly taken and the
Moral that belongs to the law of nature broadly taken. He says that the very
first practical principles, known from their terms alone to every intellect
that apprehends them, are of the former kind, as are the things evidently and
necessarily deduced from those Principles. Of the latter kind are the things
very consonant with that law of nature and with those practical Principles or
the conclusions necessarily deduced from them, which are called positive law
by others. Precepts of the former kind are absolutely immutable. Those of the
latter kind, because they depend in large part upon the will of the one
commanding, can admit variation according to the various circumstances,
although they are not always changed. Moral matters of the former kind are so
engraved upon nature that they cannot be abolished unless nature itself is
altogether abolished. Those of the latter kind, unless they are cultivated
and confirmed by right instruction, can pass into oblivion without much
difficulty. But when instruction and recollection are added, they are
generally retained because they are found to agree exceedingly well with the
Principles themselves impressed upon the whole of human nature.] We gladly
admit this distinction.

Having premised these distinctions, he proceeds to examine the arguments. He
proposes the first in this way: [Their first and indeed most powerful argument
is taken from the fact that this fourth precept is contained in the Decalogue
no less

[Printed p. 279 | IA PDF 285 | IA leaf 285 | IA decausadei02brow]

than the other nine precepts, was pronounced by God himself on Mount Horeb,
and was inscribed by God's finger on tablets of stone. Hence in Exodus 34:18,
Deuteronomy 4:13 and 10:14, they are called ten words by Moses himself. From
this they argue as follows. All those precepts contained in the Decalogue are
moral. But this precept is contained in the Decalogue. Therefore it is moral.]
See also Dr. Strangius, *On the Sabbath and the Lord's Day*, page 593.

He does not deny the major premise of this syllogism, for he used this same
argument against the *Socinians and Anabaptists, chapter 2 of his
Dissertation, page 12*, in these words: [The fourth precept is part of the
Decalogue, in which nothing merely ceremonial is commanded, since the law of
the Decalogue is a moral and eternal law. In the same way, Deuteronomy 10:4
asserts that the same law was inscribed by God on two tablets and comprises
ten words, not merely nine. To this external inscription the Apostle Paul
opposes inscription by the Holy Spirit in hearts of flesh, 2 Corinthians
3:3, 7. That internal inscription contains the new man, who is created
according to God in true righteousness and holiness. Accordingly, that
inscription by the Holy Spirit comprises the same internal righteousness and
holiness which the law of the Decalogue indeed prescribed externally in those
ten words but could not inscribe upon the heart, because it lacked the Holy
Spirit's efficacy. Hence that whole law is called the ministry of death and
condemnation, but the Gospel the ministry of the Spirit and righteousness, in
verses 8 and 9 of the same passage. Furthermore, the morality and perpetuity
of this whole law is demonstrated from the summary of the law which Christ
proposes in Matthew 22:37: *you shall love your God with all your heart*, and
so forth, *and your neighbor as yourself*. This is the summary of the whole
moral law, from which no one could exclude the fourth precept except
absurdly, just as no one could exclude it from the curse attached to the law,
Galatians 3: *cursed is the one who has not continued*, and so forth. Add to
these what the Apostle James asserts in James 2:10: *whoever shall keep the
whole law*, and so forth. The Apostle applies this pronouncement to the
Christians to whom he writes and whom he calls *brothers*. Its truth cannot
stand unless it also has a place under the New Testament concerning
transgression of the fourth precept. Otherwise, if nothing moral were in this
precept, the one who did not keep it would not be guilty of violating the law
but would rather be a right observer of that law and a true champion of
Christian liberty.]

How, then, does he respond to this argument? [We must answer here with a
distinction (*he says*): namely, all those precepts are moral either simply
and absolutely, or only in a certain respect, insofar as the commanded matter
itself is considered, which we will indicate more precisely afterward; but
not insofar as all the circumstances and ends of the precept are considered.]
*Response.* If the commanded matter itself is moral, the precept itself will
also be moral, most properly moral, and nothing but moral. For what else is a
precept but a command concerning such matter? As for the circumstances and
ends of the command, they are adventitious, do not enter into the substance
of the precept, and therefore do not affect or change it in any respect. The
precept itself can be called partly Ceremonial in respect of circumstances
and ends of this kind only very improperly. What follows confirms this, when
he adds these things by way of explanation.

*Thus in the first part of the first precept it is said: I am the Lord your
God, who brought you out of Egypt, the house of bondage. These words contain
not so much a kind of preface, as*

[Printed p. 280 | IA PDF 286 | IA leaf 286 | IA decausadei02brow]

*some suppose, but the affirmative part of this first precept, so that at the
outset we may know who is to be regarded by us as the one and true God. Yet in
that part of the precept something is contained which pertained peculiarly to
the Jews and which cannot be applied to the rest of the faithful except
typically.* *Response.* It cannot be proved by any reasoning, even plausible,
that these words contain the affirmative part of the first precept. For when
the affirmative of a precept is proposed, it is always expressed in the form
of a precept; but the form of a precept does not appear here. See Chapter 1.
2. Let us grant that these words contain the affirmative of the first
precept. Does any circumstance or end of the precept appear here from which
this precept can with any propriety be called partly ceremonial? A particular
benefit granted to the Jews is indeed commemorated, but that benefit is
properly neither a circumstance nor an end of the precept. 3. Let us grant
this also; nevertheless, it will by no means follow from it that the precept
itself should properly be called partly Ceremonial. For just as that
particular benefit does not enter into the substance of the precept or make
up any part of it, neither is it anything ceremonial. 4. It is no wonder if
the fourth precept is called partly Ceremonial when the very first precept of
the Decalogue is called partly Ceremonial for such a trifling reason. But who
does not see how improperly this is said, and how, notwithstanding this, the
precept can be called simply Moral? What further does he add by way of
explanation?

[Thus also (*he says*) a promise is added in the fifth precept which has
particular regard to the Israelites, namely, concerning the blessing of God
to be obtained in the promised land. The Apostle Paul transfers it from the
hypothesis to the thesis in Ephesians 6:2, 3, namely, *that it may be well with
them and that they may be long-lived upon the earth.* Therefore, since the
first and fifth precepts, which are merely moral if you consider their
matter, are accommodated by God himself to the peculiar state of the
Israelites, nothing prevents the fourth precept from comprising something
common and simply moral and, in turn, something typical that pertained
peculiarly to the Israelites' present state.] *Response.* That promise
attached to the fifth precept is distinguished from the precept itself. And
our question concerns the precept itself, not adjuncts of this kind. 2. Let it
be said that the Fourth precept is of the same nature as the First or Fifth,
and this controversy will be at an end. 3. If all the precepts that were
accommodated in some manner to the state of the Israelites are to be called
partly moral and partly ceremonial, then all the precepts of the Decalogue
will also enjoy this designation, and no purely moral precept at all will be
found. There is no valid inference from certain substituted adjuncts to the
primary thing commanded. And Dr. Strangius accomplishes nothing when he
vindicates Walaeus by saying that Walaeus said this only to illustrate the
response, not for the sake of the argument. For the argument concerned the
Substance of the precept, and the response must speak about the same thing;
otherwise it will be empty.

He further says: [Indeed, the very men who contradict us here nevertheless
acknowledge that this fourth precept does not have the same nature and
morality as the rest, since they concede that the day commanded in it is
mutable. Yet this cannot be said of what is moral strictly taken.] *Response
1.* All precepts which are not in every respect of the same nature as the
First or Third are not therefore to be called partly Ceremonial. 2. No one I
know who denies that this precept is in any respect

[Printed p. 281 | IA PDF 287 | IA leaf 287 | IA decausadei02brow]

Ceremonial says that it does not have the same morality as the rest or that
the day commanded in it is mutable, unless perhaps they understand the day to
be mutable in respect of God's absolute power, in which respect other moral
things can also be called mutable. Or if by a mutable day they understand
precisely the seventh day, I do not see how they can defend themselves unless
they say that everything which is not properly and strictly typical is moral.
But I do not say that.

What else? [Then (*he says*), since they concede that this moral matter which
concerns designating time for divine worship is not moral in the same degree
as those things which belong to the law of nature strictly taken, but is only
positive-moral, and therefore can undergo change by God's ordination—if this
is true, as is conceded by the most learned among them, I see no reason why
they do not also acknowledge, with the whole Christian Church, that something
in this precept is ceremonial and typical in the same way.] *Response 1.* Did
he not himself above present us, from Scotus, with a distinction of the Moral
into the moral that belongs to the law of nature strictly taken and the moral
that belongs to the law of nature broadly taken? And did he not say that these
latter moral matters can undergo change? But did Scotus or anyone else say
that all those moral matters which can be called positive in relation to
others which belong to the law of nature strictly taken, and which are very
consonant with the law of nature and with practical principles or conclusions
necessarily deduced from them, are only partly Moral and partly Ceremonial? 2.
The positive and the ceremonial and typical differ greatly, as he will
acknowledge; otherwise he must say that this precept is wholly Ceremonial, and
not partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. For he will not say that only what
belongs to the law of nature strictly taken is properly moral. Nor will he say
that the fourth precept contains something of that kind, or something that
pertains to the first practical principles known from their terms alone to
every intellect that apprehends them. Hence it is evident that there is a
solid reason why those who hold this precept to be moral-positive do not
acknowledge in the same manner that something in it is ceremonial and
typical: namely, because the positive-moral differs greatly from the
ceremonial and typical. The *former* is not always changed; the *latter* is
instituted in order that it must finally be changed. The former has the
greatest affinity with the law of nature; the latter has very little. The
former binds everyone at every time; the latter binds only the Church under a
particular administration. 3. It has not yet been established that the whole
Christian Church acknowledged something Ceremonial and typical in this
precept.

[We assert only this here (*thus he concludes his response to this argument*):
namely, that this precept is moral insofar as it concerns each and every human
being, but ceremonial and typical insofar as it was restricted to the
Israelites alone and the state of the Church of the Old Testament.]
*Response.* The same can be said of the whole moral law, which nevertheless
would be called partly Ceremonial only improperly. Thus our *first* argument
has been vindicated; the *second* follows.

[The second argument (*he says*) by which they endeavor to prove that nothing
in this precept is ceremonial is taken from the ends and reasons contained in
the precept itself. For since the reasons and ends of the precept are only
moral, they conclude from this that the precept itself is only moral. The
reasons for the precept are the rest of human beings and animals, the example
of God, who rested from his works on the seventh day, and then the
sanctification of the day itself.] This is how

[Printed p. 282 | IA PDF 288 | IA leaf 288 | IA decausadei02brow]

he proposes our argument, in which an incomplete enumeration of the reasons
should be observed. For others are at hand, such as the concession of six
days for performing all our work. And I do not know whether that rest of the
animals should properly be called an end of this precept. But let this pass;
let us see what he answers.

[But first (*he says*) it is denied that a moral reason added to a precept
makes that precept itself merely Moral, since in Leviticus 11 we see various
ceremonial precepts concerning the distinction of foods and legal uncleanness
proposed by God, to which a moral reason drawn from God's example and nature
is nevertheless appended in verse 44: *For I am Jehovah your God, that you may
sanctify yourselves and be holy, because I am holy.*] So also Dr. Strangius in
the place cited above. *Response.* Indeed, a bare argument drawn from a reason
attached to some precept, without the requisite restrictions, would not
please me, since I know that a ceremonial precept can be urged by a more
general moral reason. Yet I judge that this argument can be framed *ad
hominem*: that precept is not partly Ceremonial all of whose reasons and ends
inserted into the precept are moral, and not partly Moral and partly
Ceremonial. But the fourth precept is such a precept. Therefore, and so forth.
He cannot deny the major premise, for on page 13 he argues as follows against
the Anabaptists and Socinians: [The same thing said by us is proved from the
very words, reasons, and end of the precept. Among these many are not
Ceremonial but Moral, such as the *working of six days*, which is conceded by
this precept and cannot be referred to ceremony alone. Therefore, neither is
the *exemption of the seventh day* to be referred to ceremony alone.] But if
it follows from this that a precept is partly Moral when many of its reasons
and ends are moral, why does it not also follow that a precept is wholly moral
when all its reasons and ends expressed in the precept itself are moral?
Certainly if one moral reason added to a precept renders that precept moral to
that extent—which is nevertheless hardly certain—many moral reasons will do
so much more. Furthermore, Dr. Strangius admits that as the reason is, so also
is the conclusion primarily deduced from it; that the reasons added to the
fourth precept always bind for as long as the precept stands; and,
consequently, that because of the reasons of the precept it is equitable for
us to sanctify one day of the week. This is sufficient for us, who say that
the fourth precept directly and primarily commands us to observe one day of
the week determined by God, not the seventh from creation, as he thinks.

But let us hear the reasons, *namely* (*Dr. Walaeus says*) *because some moral
end is regarded even in the ceremonies themselves. As for what could be urged
here, that God indeed says, “Abstain from foods and other legal uncleannesses,
because I am holy,” but not “because I abstain from uncleannesses of this
kind,” while it is said in the fourth precept, “Rest, because I rested”—that, I
say, does little for the matter, since in the former example God's moral
holiness is proposed as an example of ceremonial holiness.* *Response 1.* If
the ceremonial law should not be called partly Moral even though some moral
end is regarded in it, why, I ask, should the moral law be called partly
Ceremonial because some ceremonial end was perhaps added? 2. It was shown
long ago in Book I, Chapter III, that God's holiness is not properly proposed
there as an example. Furthermore, 3. God's holiness is not proposed there as
a peculiar, proper, internal reason

[Printed p. 283 | IA PDF 289 | IA leaf 289 | IA decausadei02brow]

upon which the command itself rests in a special manner, as God's example is
proposed in the fourth precept. The reasoning, therefore, is not parallel.

[Then (*he says*) the matter is manifest, as we saw before from Hebrews 4,
that God's rest from his works was not only an example from which the
Israelites might understand that they had to rest from servile works on that
day, but also a sacramental type by which they might know that in all their
works they should look toward eternal rest, even from the first institution
of the Sabbath. Hence that seventh day alone is numbered without an evening
in Genesis 1, as Augustine, Epistle 119, chapter 9, and elsewhere frequently,
and other ancients rightly observed.] *Response.* If God's rest was a
sacramental type, then the Sabbath will not be the sacramental type here, for
God's rest differs from the Sabbath. 2. It is novel and, I think, unheard of
until now to call an action or rest of God a sacramental type. Let a similar
sacramental type be produced, whether under the New or Old Testament, before
the fall or after the fall. 3. Does he think that seventh day was not a day
consisting of twenty-four hours, or a night-and-day, just as the others
preceding it were? If not, how could the Sabbath of the seventh day be urged
from this example? If it was, what is intended by these statements, *that they
might know that in all their works they should look toward eternal rest, even
from the first institution of the Sabbath, hence that seventh day alone is
numbered without an evening?* For these intimate that a weekly Sabbath was
not instituted, but a perpetual Sabbath, and that God did not sanctify the
seventh day but all the time following the first six days. And if he
understands this, how will he prove from this fourth precept that it is moral
for one day in the weekly cycle to be consecrated to God? 4. The seventh day
is indeed numbered without an evening, yet it was a day, not a year, an age,
or all the following time. And “seventh” expresses a relation to the six
preceding days, and it was a division of time of the same length as the
preceding ones; otherwise it would be called neither a day nor the seventh
day. 5. Because it is numbered without an evening, did it therefore possess
the character of a sacramental type? What is the logic of that inference? 6.
If that seventh day possessed the character of a sacramental type, did God's
rest from his works also possess it? Or if that seventh day did not possess
the character of a sacramental type, but God's rest alone did, what difference
does it make whether that seventh day was numbered without an evening or not?

*Indeed* (*he says further*), *this very example of God openly refutes their
opinion. For if the sole meaning of the precept were this, as they wish,
“Remember to sanctify one day out of seven to Jehovah,” then the example of
God resting from his works would not fit here, because God rested on none of
the other six days but only on that seventh day which followed those six
days. Since, therefore, God adduces in this precept his example of resting, it
is an irrefutable argument that God did not immediately command according to
the letter of this precept that they sanctify one day out of seven to God, but
that they observe this seventh day, which he calls the Sabbath. Yet we do not
deny that the former is rightly gathered from the force and analogy of this
precept, as we will show in the following chapter.* *Response 1.* This example
of God not only does not openly refute our opinion but also confirms it. For
although God did not rest on any of the other six days but only on that
seventh day, he nevertheless rested on one out of seven, for he did not
establish a cycle of more days. Thus that irrefutable argument does not yet
appear. But more on this afterward. And unless this were the meaning of this
precept, I do not see

[Printed p. 284 | IA PDF 290 | IA leaf 290 | IA decausadei02brow]

how the point he wishes to gather from it could be gathered from the force of
this precept.

[Let us add (*he says*) that the reasons and ends of this precept are not to
be judged from the words of the precept alone, as they appear in Exodus 20,
but also from other passages of Scripture where other and typical ends are
ascribed to the Sabbath, as we proved in the preceding chapter.] *Response.*
Some judge this demand to be inequitable because these reasons were not
pronounced by God himself or inscribed on the tablets of stone, but pertain
only to Moses's commentary. What those other and typical ends are, however,
we will see in the following chapter.

[And it can be proved further (*he says*) from Deuteronomy 5:19, 20, where
those ten precepts are repeated by Moses and the reason from the example of
God resting is passed over, while another is added that peculiarly regarded
the Israelites and in its own manner was also typical—*& remember that you
were a slave*, and so forth.] *Response 1.* Although the example of God
resting is passed over here, elsewhere it alone is recalled, as in Exodus
31:17. 2. A reason peculiarly regarding the Israelites and attached to the
command does not render the command similarly peculiar to them, much less
typical. Before this reason existed, this precept had its force, for it was
instituted from the beginning, as he himself admits. Therefore, this reason
does not concern the first institution of the Sabbath but its peculiar
observance.

[Therefore, since (*thus he concludes his response to this argument*) it was
also among the ends of this precept that they should remember that on this day
they had obtained rest from Egyptian bondage, and since that liberation
looked toward our future redemption through Christ from the bondage of Satan
and sin, 1 Corinthians 10:1, it is therefore evidently gathered that there was
also some typical character in the Sabbath which was added to the first
institution of the Sabbath.] *Response 1.* If this typical character was only
added to the first institution of the Sabbath, the fourth precept cannot on
that account properly be called partly Ceremonial any more than the whole
moral law can. 2. It certainly has not yet been established that the
Israelites were delivered from Egyptian bondage on the Sabbath day. And even
if this were established, it is still not evident that it was also among the
ends of this precept that they should remember that on that day they obtained
rest from Egyptian bondage. Where do we read this in the Scriptures? God
wanted them to remember that they had been slaves in the land of Egypt and
that God had delivered them from it, and on that account more readily to grant
their male and female servants the ability to rest on that day. But every
reason is not an end, much less is a reason pressing one part of the precept
the end of the whole precept.

Now follows the third argument, which he says is numbered among the principal
ones. He proposes it in this form: [What was instituted in Paradise itself
before the fall could not be ceremonial and typical, since all types looked
toward Christ the Redeemer. But before the fall there was not yet need for
redemption, as is agreed among the Reformed.] He does not deny the assumed
premise, which is understood, but confirms it as far as page 54. How, then,
does he respond to the argument? [He asserts that the Sabbath was Ceremonial
because it was among the positive sacramental laws in the state of innocence.
For just as the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and likewise the tree
of life were sacraments of that first state, so also the rest and
sanctification of the Sabbath was added as a third sacrament, or sacramental
type of eternal rest from animal activities, as Ursinus rightly observes below,
which is evidently proved from Hebrews 4, as we showed before.] *Response 1.*
That cannot

[Printed p. 285 | IA PDF 291 | IA leaf 291 | IA decausadei02brow]

properly be called Ceremonial which can be referred to neither of the kinds
of the Ceremonial that he himself set out at the beginning of this third
chapter. But the Sabbath will be such if it is to be referred to positive
sacramental laws in the state of innocence. For *first*, it is not something
Ceremonial, figurative, and typical which has a definite relation to Christ
our Redeemer, since there was no need for such a ceremonial thing in that
state of innocence. Then, it is not a sacred rite instituted by God for
observing right order and decorum in the Church, because sacraments denote
something far different from what pertains to mere right order and decorum.
2. I judge that the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and
evil are improperly called sacraments. At least, they were not sacraments such
as are employed under the different administrations of the new covenant; and
it is certain that their sacramental character perished with that state.
Hence it follows that if the Sabbath was a sacrament of that kind, it perished
immediately at the fall and was not renewed before the time of Moses. He
himself proves the contrary in this same chapter when he proves that the
Sabbath was observed even by the Patriarchs. 3. He will not say that the
Patriarchs observed those two other sacraments; yet he concedes and proves
that they observed the Sabbath. Therefore, the Sabbath is distinguished from
those sacraments. 4. I would not deny that the Sabbath in the state of
innocence could possess the character of a pledge of eternal rest in God. But
Scripture nowhere says that it was a sacrament or a sacramental type of
eternal rest from animal activities. And in Hebrews 4 the Apostle speaks of a
far different rest from that rest from animal activities, as was said
elsewhere.

He proceeds: [Then, although the Sabbath ought not to have been considered
before the fall as a type looking toward Christ, it is nevertheless consonant
with the truth, and truly asserted by great writers of the Reformed Church,
that this relation was also afterward added to it by God's institution and
that its observance was therefore more restricted.] So also Dr. Strangius in
the place cited above, page 546. *Response 1.* What was a sacramental type
before the fall and afterward was a type looking toward Christ appears to be
merely Ceremonial, and not partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. 2. Of what
thing the Sabbath was afterward a type has not yet been clearly indicated. It
is not denied that the Sabbath and its observance could have some ceremonial
character attached to it; but not every Ceremony is a type. 3. Although a
typical character was also attached to the Jewish Sabbath, what does this do
against the Decalogical Sabbath? It does not follow that the fourth precept
itself must therefore be called partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. The
external Jewish worship commanded in the second precept had many ceremonies
mixed with it; yet the second precept itself is not therefore to be called
partly Moral and partly Ceremonial. Similarly, ceremonies were attached to
many moral duties; yet it does not follow from this that the moral precepts
which command those duties are partly Ceremonial.

[Just as we see occurred with the rainbow, likewise with primogeniture, the
kingdom from the tribe of Judah, and similar natural or positive-moral things
which afterward, by God's particular appointment, became types either of
God's temporal benefits or of Christ's kingdom and his Spiritual benefits.]
*Response.* Yet it does not follow from this that the Rainbow, Primogeniture,
the Kingdom of Judah,

or similar natural or positive-moral things, considered in themselves, are
partly Ceremonial. Much less does it follow that God's first creation of the
Rainbow and first constitution of Primogeniture and the Kingdom, and of
similar natural things, is partly Ceremonial. Therefore, neither does it
follow from this that the fourth precept is partly Ceremonial and Typical.

[Many ceremonial things (*he says further*) obtained among the Patriarchs
which were nevertheless more strictly defined after the law and had a more
evident explanation of their ceremonial character attached, as we see
occurred with sacrifices, the distinction of foods, circumcision, and other
types of the Old Testament.] *Response.* All this is conceded, but nothing
follows from it against us.

[And to this (*he proceeds*) must certainly be referred those passages
previously adduced from Exodus 31:13, Ezekiel 20:10, 11, and Nehemiah 9:14
concerning this renewal of the Sabbath.] *Response.* What can be proved from
those passages has already been seen elsewhere. These passages do not speak
of the fourth precept, which is distinguished from the Jewish Sabbath.

[And this is all the more consonant with the truth (*he says finally*) because
the mystery of Paradise and the tree of life, although by God's first
institution it could not signify the redemption to be accomplished through
Christ, was nevertheless afterward applied by the Holy Spirit to shadowing
forth that same thing, as can be seen in Revelation 22:2, and so forth.]
*Response.* Can whatever is said about Christ and his benefits by way of
similitude and allusion also be called a type or ceremony properly so called?
Christ is called *the sun of righteousness, a bundle of myrrh, a cluster of
henna in the plantations of Engedi, the rose of Sharon, and the Lily of the
valleys.* See Song of Songs 2:1; 1:13, 14; and 5:13 to the end.

These are the principal arguments for our opinion which he brings forward to
refute. We do not linger over other arguments to which he responds something,
for far more effective ones are available to us. Consult those presented
above for the Moral character of the fourth precept, which also prove that
that precept is not partly Ceremonial and only partly Moral.

It now remains for us to see how the Celebrated Man proves that this precept
is partly Ceremonial.

From what has been said it is evident what should be answered to the statements
of the most illustrious Rivetus, who holds the same opinion as Dr. Walaeus as
far as this question is concerned. For he has the same things as Dr. Walaeus,
and understands them all of the designation, as they call it, of the seventh
day, which we do not contend is Moral; and we say that it is commanded only
consequently in the fourth precept of the Decalogue. And by ceremonial he
understands an external
rite pertaining to ecclesiastical polity and the circumstances of divine
worship, or anything positive, or everything which is not merely natural;
that is, good or evil by its own nature.
