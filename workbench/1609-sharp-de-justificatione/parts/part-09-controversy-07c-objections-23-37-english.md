[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 116 | image 15575088 | printed p. 114]

**Objection 23.** If faith alone justifies because it justifies relationally,
then faith cannot be in the soul without righteousness also being there, since
relatives are posited together. But righteousness cannot exist without love, 1
John 2:9. Therefore faith alone, separated from love, does not justify.

**Response.** 1. The whole argument is conceded with respect to existence.

2. Love and righteousness exist together, yet the righteousness which
justifies is not love. Faith and righteousness are relatives, but the
righteousness in this relation is not inherent righteousness; it is Christ’s.
Just as the relation and imputation by which our sins were imputed to Christ
was efficacious, so the imputation of Christ’s righteousness to us is no less
efficacious. The foundation of this relation is Christ’s righteousness and its
term is the believing person. Between these two comes God’s ordination—namely,
justification, by which Christ’s merit is imputed to the believer.

**Objection 24.** If faith separated from the remaining virtues justifies, it
could then justify in the company of the vices contrary to those virtues. But
where the contrary vices are found—hatred of God and neighbor, and so
forth—there is no righteousness, unless a wicked person is righteous.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 117 | image 15575089 | printed p. 115]

Therefore faith alone, separated from the remaining virtues, does not justify.

**Response.** 1. The foundation of the proposition is false—namely, that the
faith which justifies is separated from the remaining virtues with respect to
existence.

2. If that separation concerns the act of justification, then the connection
is false. This does not follow: the virtues are separated from faith in the
act of justification; therefore faith can justify in the company of vices.

3. With respect to existence, vices are present in a justified person together
with justifying faith, although they are not in their full strength or without
conflict. The minor premise is therefore false. For contraries can be together
in the same subject in diminished degrees; and so in the justified both
virtues and vices exist, not in intense but in diminished degrees.

**Objection 25.** Scripture does not say that faith alone justifies. Therefore
faith alone does not justify.

**Response.** 1. The antecedent is false, for this is expressly stated in Mark
5:36 and Luke 9:50. 2. It is enough if an equivalent expression is found, such
as: we are justified by grace, without works, without the law, not from
ourselves, and not from our own righteousness, and so forth, as in Romans
3:21, 24, 27–28; Galatians 2:16; Ephesians 2:8–9; Titus 3:5; and 1 John 1:7.
Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Objection 26.** That which is denied in Scripture, either in its own terms
or through a synonym, is not true. But the synonymous expression that we are
justified by faith alone is denied in Scripture, James 2:24, when it says that
we are not justified by faith only. Therefore it is not true that we are
justified by faith alone.

**Response.** This is an equivocation arising from a double ambiguity, one in
the word *faith* and the other in *justification*. For

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 118 | image 15575090 | printed p. 116]

in the passage from James the negation does not concern justifying faith, but
a feigned and dead faith, against which he argues there. The conclusion,
however, speaks about a living and efficaciously justifying faith. Moreover,
in the cited passage *to justify* means to declare righteous, as it does
elsewhere in Matthew 10:27, Romans 3:4, and in the cited passage. 2. Abraham was
justified by the work of offering his son—that is, he was declared righteous—
although he had previously been made and pronounced righteous before God by
the righteousness of faith, Genesis 15:16 and Romans 4:2. In the conclusion,
however, *justification* means making and pronouncing someone righteous before
God. Thus, if *justification* in the minor premise is taken for justification
before God, then the word *faith* means dead faith; and if *faith* means
justifying faith, then the equivocation lies in the word *justify*.

**Objection 27.** That which does not exclude every law and all works does not
justify alone. But the doctrine of justification does not exclude every law
and all works, because it admits the law of faith—that is, faith, which is a
work—Romans 3:27. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. I deny the minor premise. The doctrine of justification
excludes all works, insofar as they are works, from causing justification. It
admits faith not insofar as faith is a work, but as the instrument which
apprehends and applies Christ’s merit.

2. The law of faith is falsely interpreted as faith itself; it signifies the
doctrine of faith—that is, the gospel.

**Further instance.** If justification by faith excludes faith insofar as it
is a work, then to be justified by faith will be the same as saying that one
is justified without faith.

**Response.** I deny the inference, because justification does not exclude
faith simply, but only insofar as it is a work, but not

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 119 | image 15575091 | printed p. 117]

insofar as it is the instrument which apprehends and applies Christ’s merit.
In that respect it includes faith. This therefore commits the fallacy of
proceeding from a qualified statement to an unqualified one: faith is excluded
insofar as it is a work; therefore it is excluded altogether.

**Further instance.** Faith does not justify insofar as it apprehends, because
if it did, it would justify either insofar as this apprehension is in the
intellect or insofar as it is in the will. It does not do so in the former
respect, because intellectual apprehension is common to the godly and the
ungodly, who can apprehend the mysteries of faith through apprehensive
knowledge; and faith consists more in judgment than in apprehension. Nor does
it do so in the latter respect, because this apprehension would be trust, while
to trust is not to apprehend but to adhere firmly to a thing apprehended.
Therefore faith does not justify insofar as it apprehends.

**Response.** 1. Faith is not apprehension, but an instrument that apprehends;
and it justifies insofar as it apprehends by means of the intellect.
Apprehension is the cognition of a thing as it is represented, which occurs
through faith. But this does not follow: apprehension is common to the godly
and the ungodly; therefore it is not present in the faith of the godly. For
this knowledge is common to historical, temporary, and justifying faith, but
in the faith of the godly apprehension is followed by application, which is
not present in the faith of the ungodly.

2. It is falsely asserted that faith, which is in the intellect, consists only
in judgment and not in apprehension. For the intellect judges only things
already apprehended to be true—not because they all become true through the
intellect’s judgment, but because the things believed are true, and it
therefore judges them to be true.

3. Apprehension, properly speaking, is not in the will; hence there is no such
disjunction. The will acquiesces in and adheres to things apprehended and
judged by the intellect.

**Further instance.** But although there is apprehension in faith, the thing

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 120 | image 15575092 | printed p. 118]

apprehended is not apprehended in such a way that it is actually possessed and
inheres in us. It is only in the mind as an object apprehended by the action
of the intellect and will. Love and joy apprehend in this same manner. For
just as faith justifies because it apprehends righteousness, when I believe
myself to be righteous through Christ, so love justifies when I love and
rejoice that I am righteous through Christ.

**Response.** 1. It is falsely supposed that we possess nothing unless it
inheres in us. An owner possesses his possessions, but they do not inhere in
him. In the same way we possess Christ together with his benefits, which
inhere in Christ but are efficaciously communicated to us through faith.

2. The thing apprehended by faith is not merely and nakedly in the mind and
will as an object. It is there in a certain special manner, and the thing
apprehended is applied.

3. Love and joy apprehend in a different manner from faith; and even if they
apprehended in the same manner, they still would not justify. The mind’s
apprehension alone does not justify, but application joined with apprehension
does so, and this application occurs through faith alone.

4. Finally, it is false that love and joy justify when we love and rejoice
that we are righteous through Christ, because love does not act as a cause in
justification in any manner, not even then. Rather, if the acts of love and joy
follow the justification which occurs through faith, they declare that it has
been accomplished.

**Further instance.** Faith does not justify alone because it applies. If it
justified in this manner, it alone would apply; but it does not apply alone.
The sacraments apply the promises and make them ours. Therefore faith alone
does not justify as an instrument which applies. The antecedent is proved from
Titus 3:5 and Ephesians 5:26.

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 121 | image 15575093 | printed p. 119]

**Response.** 1. It is false that the sacraments apply. Rather, they exhibit
the things to be applied and seal them as things to be applied, as signs and
seals. Nor does this exclude the Word and sacraments. In justification,
however, these are instrumental causes which outwardly exhibit and seal the
promises. This exhibition is not the application which occurs by faith alone.

**Objection 28.** That which unites more also justifies more. Love unites more
than faith, because faith is confidence. Love therefore justifies more, and
consequently faith alone does not justify.

**Response.** 1. I deny the minor premise. Faith is the greatest bond and the
foundation of the other virtues.

2. This does not follow: faith is trust or confidence; therefore it does not
unite more than love. On the contrary, because it is trust, it unites more.

**Objection 29.** Scripture says that we are justified through faith and from
faith. Therefore faith is not merely the apprehension of righteousness, but
has the efficacy of a cause and justifies by its own merit and dignity.

**Response.** 1. We concede the antecedent. Scripture does not, however, say
that we are justified *dia pistin*, on account of faith—that is, by the merit
or dignity of faith—but instrumentally through faith. 2. We concede the first
part of the consequent: faith is not apprehension, since apprehension is the
action of faith; and faith also justifies after the manner of a cause, though
not a meritorious but an instrumental cause. 3. The final part of the
consequent does not follow.

**Further instance.** The following proves that it does follow. When the
apostle says that a person is justified through faith and not through works
and the law, he attributes to faith what he denies to works and the law. But
when he says that a person is not justified through or from works, what he
denies to works is efficiency and merit, not relational apprehension.
Therefore what he attributes to faith when he says that we are justified by

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 122 | image 15575094 | printed p. 120]

or from faith is efficiency and merit in justification, not relational
apprehension.

**Response.** I distinguish the major premise. When the apostle says that we
are justified through faith and not through works, does he attribute to faith
properly and by itself, insofar as it is a quality, what he denies to works?
This is false. Or does he do so insofar as faith is taken correlatively and,
by metalepsis, we understand in the expression *through faith* the object of
faith together with faith? This is true. When the apostle denies that we are
justified through works and the law, Bellarmine rightly interprets him,
against Bellarmine’s own position, as excluding all efficiency, efficacy, or
merit of works in justifying. But when the apostle says that we are justified
through or from faith, he does not attribute to faith alone everything which
he took away from the law and works, but to faith together with its
correlative. Taken legally—that is, with respect to faith’s merit or dignity—
the proposition that we are justified through or from faith is blasphemous.

**Further instance.** But where *through* or *from* is used, a cause is
signified, as in Hebrews 11:33: through faith the saints conquered kingdoms;
and Abraham is said to have been moved through faith to obey God. To obey by
faith is nothing other than for faith to be a cause. Therefore to be justified
by or through faith means that faith is the cause of justification.

**Response.** 1. We concede that faith is a cause of justification, but only
an instrumental cause.

2. When *through* or *from* is used, a cause is signified, but not always the
same kind. Sometimes it is material, sometimes principally efficient,
sometimes instrumental, and sometimes formal. The following does not follow:
these particles signify a cause; therefore they signify a formal or material
cause.

**Objection 30.** Faith is called righteousness in Romans 4:5:

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 123 | image 15575095 | printed p. 121]

“To the one who believes, faith is reckoned as righteousness.” Faith therefore
justifies not because it apprehends righteousness, but because it is itself
righteousness.

**Response.** 1. I deny the inference. Although faith, insofar as it is a
virtue in us, is a part of inherent righteousness, it does not in this respect
justify us before God, either by itself or together with love. 2. The
antecedent is false. In that passage faith is not called righteousness or said
to be reckoned as righteousness, but to be imputed unto righteousness, because
and insofar as it apprehends Christ together with his benefits.

**Objection 31.** From 1 Corinthians 3:11. The foundation in that passage is
faith. But a foundation is only the beginning and a part of the building.
Therefore faith is only the beginning of justification, and consequently we
are not justified by faith alone.

**Response.** The proposition is false. According to the apostle’s own
testimony, the foundation in that passage is Christ; the passage does not
speak about faith and justification.

**Objection 32.** From Acts 15:9. Faith purifies hearts because it frees a
person from the defilements of error, which it does not so much efficiently as
formally, as light drives away darkness. But purity of heart is righteousness.
Therefore we are justified by faith as formal righteousness.

**Response to the major.** Faith purifies hearts because it frees from error,
not primarily and principally but secondarily; it does so primarily when it
apprehends and applies Christ’s merit. Properly speaking, however, *to purify*
in that passage does not mean to infuse new qualities, but to wash away sins,
as in 1 John 1:7. Secondarily, faith illumines and purifies from error. This
purity in us is righteousness, but not the righteousness by which we are
justified before God; it is the consequence and effect of justification which
is called sanctification.

**Objection 33.** That which merits forgiveness of sins is the thing by which
we are justified, not as an

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 124 | image 15575096 | printed p. 122]

instrument but by its own efficacy and merit. But faith merits forgiveness of
sins, Luke 7:50 and Matthew 15:28. Therefore the conclusion follows.

**Response.** I deny the minor premise. Faith saves the woman not by the
efficacy of its own merit, but by applying Christ’s merit.

**Objection 34.** From Mark 7:29. The demon went out on account of the woman’s
statement; therefore it went out on account of the merit of her faith.

**Response.** I deny the inference. Christ is said to have cast out the demon
because she spoke, not on account of the merit of her words or faith, but on
account of the words in the sense that by them she attested that she had the
faith by which she believed in Christ. This therefore commits the fallacy of
treating what is not a cause as a cause.

**Objection 35.** From Romans 4:20. Abraham’s faith was reckoned unto
righteousness because by believing he gave glory to God. Faith was therefore
pleasing to God by the act through which God was glorified, and consequently
God justified him by the merit of his faith.

**Response.** The antecedent quotes the apostle falsely, for he does not say
that faith was reckoned, but that it was imputed; nor does he say that faith
was reckoned to be righteousness, as if faith were Abraham’s righteousness.
Rather, faith was imputed to him unto righteousness—not faith itself taken
absolutely, but correlatively, because by this faith he apprehended the promise
or the thing promised, that is, Christ.

**Objection 36.** From Hebrews 11. The thing by which the saints pleased God is
the thing by whose merit and worth they are justified. But they pleased God by
faith, because without faith it is impossible to please God. Therefore they
are justified by faith on account of its merit or efficacy.

**Response.** 1. The major premise, taken without qualification, is false; it
is true only if restricted to Christ’s merit.

2. The phrase *by which they pleased God* must be distinguished. If it means
as an instrument, the major is false; if it means as a meritorious cause, it
is true. By faith,

[1609 e-rara witness | canvas 125 | image 15575097 | printed p. 123]

however, they pleased God as by an instrument.

**Objection 37.** If faith alone justified, it alone would save. But faith does
not save alone, because salvation is attributed to works. Therefore the
conclusion follows.

**Response.** 1. The minor premise is false. Salvation is not attributed to
the merit or efficacy of works; rather, the thing signified is inferred from
an indubitable sign. 2. Salvation is not attributed to faith as a meritorious
cause, but because faith apprehends Christ, on account of whom we are justified
and saved. Therefore one who believes is said to be destined for salvation.
