# On Three Questions

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
[`latin.txt`](latin.txt). The printed-page markers correspond to the 1871
*Calvini Opera* witness.

**[CO X/1, p. 262]**

I would have been more diligent in answering, dearest brother, had I not been
held back by a difficulty that seemed to me connected with what you

**[CO X/1, p. 263]**

ask of me in your letter. You ask, then, whether it is lawful for ministers to
stipulate and receive some return from a sum of money. If I say categorically
that it is not lawful, my answer may be judged harsher than is proper, and
there will be no lack of points that can be urged against it. I therefore do
not dare to affirm that this is unlawful. Yet when I consider how exposed the
practice is to slanders and scandals, and how widely most people extend what
might have been employed under a moderate condition, so as to permit
themselves to exceed the lawful limit in this matter, I would much rather stop
short than suppose the issue should be disposed of once for all by a
categorical answer. The safest course, plainly, would be to abstain from
contracts and the pursuit of returns of this kind. Jeremiah does not testify
about himself without reason: “I have not lent to them at interest, nor have
they lent to me at interest; everyone curses me” (Jeremiah 15:10).

Therefore, when a minister of the Word can refrain from this kind of gain, he
provides well for himself. Yet because that form of gain is more tolerable
than if he were personally to engage in commerce or practice some trade that
would draw him away from his office, I do not see why the act as such should
deserve condemnation. Meanwhile I would wish this moderation to be applied:
he should not stipulate that a fixed return or precise amount be paid to him.
Rather, let him place the money with an upright man in such a way that he rests
content with that man's good faith and integrity, and consequently is content
with an equitable and honest return from the money in proportion to God's
blessing upon the person employing it.

As for requiring an oath in the consistory, the matter must be handled
prudently so as to counter the detractions and whisperings of malicious
people. When necessary, therefore, it will be appropriate to put those who
have been summoned under oath and to place them, as it were, before God's
tribunal, since he himself presides over such an assembly. Yet every manner of
proceeding that has the appearance of civil jurisdiction, or is scarcely
different from it, must be carefully avoided.

Finally, I do not see what difficulty prevents those who preside over ordinary
and civil jurisdiction from being admitted to the consistory, provided they
are not present there in the name or capacity of that jurisdiction, but the
distinction is always observed between the two entirely separate offices,
namely the civil and the ecclesiastical. Otherwise no principle allows people
who hold magistracy to be excluded from spiritual oversight and government.
The point is that, when suitable men have been elected to that office, they
must take care not to confuse with the power of the sword what must by its
nature remain distinct from it.

This is how it seemed good to answer your requests. The brothers whom I
specifically consulted about these matters also approved it, lest these
answers seem to have proceeded from my judgment alone. Farewell. 10 January
1560.

## Translation notes

1. *Fructus*, *reditus*, and *quaestus* overlap but are not identical: they
   denote a return or yield, a regular revenue, and gain or profit-seeking. The
   translation varies the English terms accordingly.
2. *Pecuniae exercitor* is the person putting the capital to work, not
   necessarily a banker in the modern institutional sense.
3. *Adiurare* can mean solemnly adjuring a person or placing that person under
   oath. The immediate subject is *iurisiurandi exactio*, the requirement of an
   oath, so the latter force controls the translation.
4. The warning against the *species iurisdictionis* distinguishes the
   consistory's ecclesiastical proceeding from ordinary civil jurisdiction;
   it does not forbid civil officeholders from serving there in a separately
   ecclesiastical capacity.
