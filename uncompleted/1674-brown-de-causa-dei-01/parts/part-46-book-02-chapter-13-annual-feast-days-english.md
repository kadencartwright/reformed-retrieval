CHAPTER XIII

Whether the Church Is Permitted to Institute Annual Feast Days

To make it even clearer how false is what our adversaries suppose and assert—
namely, that the church or the rulers of the church possess the power to
institute principal, solemn, and necessary time—we will here show, by way of
superabundance, that they do not possess the power to institute annual feast
days, much less this principal and solemn time.

The feasts once instituted by God were very different from the Sabbath (as the
celebrated Voetius testifies in *On the Sabbath and Feasts, Selected
Disputations*, part 3, p. 1231), namely, for the following reasons. 1. Scripture
testifies to this (Leviticus 23:4, 37–38; Numbers 28:10–11, 16). 2. The Sabbath
was prescribed in the Moral Law of the Decalogue, but there was not even the
slightest mention of the other ceremonial feasts any more than of the other
ceremonies. 3. The Sabbath was instituted before the fall (Genesis 2:2) and
observed by the people of God before the ceremonial law was given (Exodus
16:23). But the feasts were at last instituted by God through Moses, and before
the fall there could have been no use for them, because no foreshadowing of
Christ the mediator was then required. 4. The Sabbath could and ought to be
observed everywhere—at home and abroad, in the wilderness, in captivity, and in
exile. But the feasts could be observed only at Jerusalem or at the place God
had chosen so that his name might dwell there (Deuteronomy 15:16). 5. The
unclean were excluded from Passover and the other feasts (Numbers 9), yet they
were bound to keep the Sabbath. 6. The feasts were instituted to commemorate a
particular benefit and to foreshadow a particular mystery, but not the Sabbath,
which was instituted for celebrating the worship of God in general—that is,
with such a specific relation. 7. Consequently, they were bound to a particular

[1674 logical printed p. 302 | IA leaf 320 | PDF p. 320]

worship and particular sacrifices, and vice versa (Numbers 28–29). But the
Sabbath was not; there was only a doubling of the daily sacrifice on it
(Numbers 28). 8. The Sabbath could not be postponed or transferred to another
day, but the feasts could (2 Chronicles 30:2–3, 13, with Numbers 9:10–11). David
Calderwood adds another difference in *The Altar of Damascus*, p. 667: it was
not lawful to fast on a feast day, because fasting and mourning do not accord
with the festivity of a feast day (Nehemiah 8:10). So it is said of Passover in
Exodus 12:14, *You shall celebrate the day with festivity*, תחגו אתו. So in
Numbers 29:15, חגג signifies *he went around, leapt, danced*, and hence, by
metonymy, *he kept a feast day*. Joy was commanded on feast days (Deuteronomy
16:14–15), and the Sabbath is never called חג. But it was lawful to fast on the
Sabbath day.

Feast days are likewise distinguished from the Lord's Day, which succeeds the
Sabbath. Nevertheless, our argument against the church's power to institute
Sabbaths will proceed, because it appears to be less to institute a good,
joyful, or feast day than a weekly Sabbath, on which we may be joyful and may
also fast as circumstances require.

According to Voetius in the place cited above, p. 1229, *feast* is taken: 1. In
the *widest* sense, for every solemn and public observance of time, whether in
the Old or New Testament, ordinary or extraordinary, sacred and ecclesiastical
or profane and political, and instituted either by human beings or immediately
by God himself. In this sense the feast of expiation is called a feast. Any
extraordinary days suddenly appointed upon some present occasion for fasting
or thanksgiving for some singular benefit can be called feasts, as can the
Sabbath day sanctified for ordinary rest. Annual days instituted by human
beings also enjoy this name, and even the civil observance of days, such as the
birthdays of kings and parents, and so forth (Hosea 7:5). 2. Broadly, for the
sacred observance of years and days, whether ordinary or extraordinary and
incidental. 3. More strictly, for ordinary and divinely instituted sacred
times, such as in the Old Testament the two Sabbaths of years, the one weekly
Sabbath, the new moons, and the five annual solemnities. 4. Most strictly, for
feasts properly so called, which the celebrated man defines as follows in the
same work, p. 1230:

*They are solemn days recurring at a fixed time and consecrated by God's
institution to divine worship, so that the people might sanctify them by
resting, or repose, joy, their proper worship, and the commemoration of a
particular divine benefit, to signify and foreshadow spiritual things in
Christ.* Not unlike this is Piscator's definition on Galatians 4:9–10, where he
says that *a feast is a public and solemn ceremony commanded by God, to be
undertaken at a definite time of year with singular joy, in order to give thanks
to God for some singular benefit bestowed upon his people.*

The question is not whether such feast days are now to be observed, but whether
the church is permitted to institute them. And if the church is not permitted
to institute them, one may deservedly doubt whether it is lawful to observe
them. It is certain that the church cannot now institute any days to signify
and foreshadow spiritual things in Christ, since types can now have no place.
It is also presupposed that the days about which the present question is
instituted were not instituted by God, for if this were conceded, it would be
pointless to ask whether the church was permitted to institute them.

The question, rather, is whether the church is permitted to institute such days
as recur annually at a fixed time—and are thus distinguished from extraordinary
and incidental

[1674 logical printed p. 303 | IA leaf 321 | PDF p. 321]

solemnities—and are consecrated to divine worship, to be spent in cessation or
rest, with joy signified also by external signs, gestures, and actions, and with
the commemoration of some particular benefit bestowed by God. They wish these
to be the elements that constitute a feast. Some add the signification and
representation of mysteries accomplished on that day, and the sanctity of that
day above others because it is a part of divine worship and ought to be
celebrated on account of the mystery. It is true that certain hierarchical
writers, especially Durell in *Vindication of the Church of England*, chapter
13, p. 105, distinguish between days observed by the papists and days celebrated
in the Church of England. They say that the Roman Church celebrates feasts from
an opinion of worship, necessity, and merit, but the English Church does so
freely, only for the sake of order, decorum, and greater edification.
Nevertheless, he acknowledges in the same chapter that those days are rightly
called, and ought to be called, *holy days*, and that this is because of the holy
exercises of piety to which they are assigned and which the church takes care
to have performed on them. It is well known, and they acknowledge, that rest or
cessation from servile works is commanded on those days. Ordinary practice
makes it sufficiently plain that these days are equated with the Lord's Day and
even preferred to it by some. The writings of other hierarchical authors also
make sufficiently clear that these days were instituted for a very different
reason than order and decorum, whatever he may say, and we will later prove this
from their *idolatry*.

Furthermore, the question is not whether the church possesses the power to
define and determine certain times for holding meetings and public exercises on
any day of the week, as edification and convenience require. This is merely a
circumstance of time, whose determination has been left to the prudence of
ecclesiastical leaders. They are bound to promote the edification of the church
and to take care that everything is done *in order, decently, and for
edification*. God requires the dispensers of the divine Word to preach the Word
of the gospel in season and out of season. Fixed and definite times are
therefore required at which the members of the church may gather to hear the
Word and participate in the sacraments; otherwise no one would know when to
meet. And in order that individuals who intend to attend those sermons may
prepare themselves, free themselves from other worldly cares, and set them
aside for so long, it is necessary that those times be fixed and conveniently
determined, not always uncertain. But no one will say that those times or hours
are holy, or that the people are bound to rest at that time. Nor will anyone say
that these times and no others are determined for any other reason than mere
convenience, and in no way because of any signification, representation, or
commemoration of some mystery. When the same convenience requires, the
constituted time can immediately be changed and another, more convenient one
determined, even almost every week.

Nor is the question whether any particular church possesses the right to
institute extraordinary days of fasting or eucharistic days as circumstances
require. These extraordinary times do not continually recur at fixed times,
but are instituted as circumstances require and immediately cease. God has
commanded in general that such days be instituted, has determined in his Word
the duration of such times on each occasion, and by his providence and his
extraordinary works—whether of judgment and wrath or of mercy—declares the
suitable season for celebrating such days. By his providence and dispensation
he gives, as it were, the command that at this or that time a day of fasting and

[1674 logical printed p. 304 | IA leaf 322 | PDF p. 322]

prayer or of thanksgiving be instituted. But nothing like this can be shown
concerning the other feast days.

Nor is the proper question here whether it is lawful to institute solemn annual
political days, such as the days of Purim, as we will see. A discussion of this
question would do nothing for our present purpose, since we are not now speaking
of such days.

Nor is the question here whether it is lawful to dedicate days to true or
supposed saints or to angels, as is done in the papacy. Our theologians refute
this abundantly when they condemn it in the papists. Here we speak only about
the constitution of a day, not about such dedication or consecration, which is
a far greater evil. We therefore need not employ special arguments here against
certain feast days dedicated to creatures, since if we prove that it is
unlawful to constitute annual days, we will also prove abundantly that those
days dedicated to angels and saints are to be condemned.

The question, therefore, is whether the church, whether universal or
particular, is permitted to institute feast days recurring at a fixed time each
year—that is, days constituted by the following elements: commemoration of a
particular benefit or religious mystery, cessation from secular or profane works
and occupations, and festivity and joy. We deny this for the following reasons.

*First argument.* If any church is permitted to institute such a day to be
celebrated once each year, it can also institute such a day to be celebrated
each month. And if each month, why not each week? If one each week, why not two,
three, or four? No one may say that the church would not be edified in this way
and that the rulers of the church are bound to do everything for edification.
For I will ask: How is it that such a constitution would not make for
edification? Is it because it would be without foundation? But let us suppose
that there are enough benefits and mysteries to be commemorated on all those
days. This is not impossible, provided that one grants the faculty of invention
which it is not difficult to prove that the papal church and hierarchical
writers have exercised until now. What response could then be given? Is it
because the institution of so many days would be a burden? But how will this be
proved except from some rule of the liberty granted to the church? Yet even the
constitution of annual days removes this liberty. It is therefore no less to be
condemned, because greater and lesser do not change the species.

*Second argument.* The power which they wish to belong to the church for
instituting such feasts is either limited or unlimited and absolute. If the
latter is said, the church can institute as many days as it wishes, and our
preceding argument is confirmed. The church will be the absolute lord of time.
It can command as it wishes; its will will stand in place of reason. But no sane
and orthodox theologian will ascribe such a power to the church—a power which
hardly all the petty popes have dared to ascribe to their church's fictitious
head. If the former is said, the church must proceed according to some rule
prescribed in God's Word, for ecclesiastical power can be limited by no other
rule. We therefore ask them to show us what this rule is by which such a
determination of feasts is to be made and according to which we may judge the
institution that has been made. Will they affirm that by any precept or example
for imitation

[1674 logical printed p. 305 | IA leaf 323 | PDF p. 323]

the rule can be outlined? No precept can be alleged. Nor can they reasonably
assert that God's example of
instituting such days under the Old Testament ought to be taken as a rule,
since there is no inference from divine institution to human institution. No
sane person will say that the church can do such a thing because God once did
so.

*Third argument.* Nothing can now belong to any church which did not once
belong to the apostolic church. But the power to institute such feast days did
not once belong to the apostolic church. Therefore, and so forth. The *major*
premise cannot be denied, because our Lord granted no church a greater power
than he granted that primitive church, or those rulers who were sent
immediately by him and furnished with full and sufficient power to institute,
gather, and govern the church. As for the *minor*, its truth is clear from the
fact that neither that church nor the apostles appear to have instituted such
days. No one will say that they failed to put into action a power granted by
Christ or were not faithful stewards. If someone *replies* that no occasion for
instituting such days then existed, *I answer*: 1. They would at least have
wished to hand down something in writing to indicate that they possessed such a
power and that the church could exercise such power when an occasion arose. Yet
we see nothing of the kind. 2. There then existed an occasion for at least some
of the feasts which we know to have now been instituted—those, for example,
which concern Christ himself, and also the angels. Yet they did not institute
the days which we now have instituted for that reason. 3. Since this occasion
appears no less necessary to the adversaries, and is the reason for which other
days have been instituted, it is certain that today's church possesses no power
to institute such days on that occasion, just as it has no power to institute
other days on similar occasions, since the apostolic church did nothing of the
kind.

*Fourth argument.* The apostles either instituted such days or did not. If they
did not, by what right can today's church institute them? From where does it
have this power? Did it receive any such thing in Christ's commands? In that
case, either the institution of such days is unnecessary to the church, or the
apostles were not furnished with power to institute necessary things, or they
failed to prove faithful in discharging their office. It cannot be said that
they were not faithful or were not furnished with sufficient power, for this
would be the same as saying that Christ was not faithful in furnishing his
house. It therefore follows that such days were by no means necessary. And if
they were not necessary then, how can they now be called necessary? If order
and decorum now demand that such days be instituted, why would the same reason
not have demanded the same thing in the apostles' time? If the apostles did
institute such days, they instituted either the same days now observed or other
similar days. If the same, why is this not shown and demonstrated by invincible
arguments, so that we can observe them with a good conscience? If other similar
days, let them teach which those were and at the same time tell us why they are
not observed today. Let them also teach by what authority the church can
correct the apostles' deed, abrogate their institutions, forge new ones, and
thrust them upon others.

*Fifth argument.* It is unlawful to add anything to God's laws or to give new
precepts concerning the worship of God (Deuteronomy 4:2; 12:32; Proverbs 30:6).
*There is nothing obscure in these words*, says Calvin in *Institutes*, book 4,
chapter 10, section 17, *nothing hidden,*

[1674 logical printed p. 306 | IA leaf 324 | PDF p. 324]

*nothing ambiguous. The whole church is
forbidden to add anything to or take anything away from the Word of God when
the Lord's worship and saving precepts are at issue.* But the adversaries
object that this was said only about the law, which was followed by the
prophecies and the whole administration of the gospel. Calvin answers this in
the same place: *I certainly acknowledge this*, he says, *and at the same time
add that they are complements of the law rather than additions or defections.
But if the Lord, after obscuring Moses' ministry, so to speak, with very many
wrappings, nevertheless suffered nothing to be added or taken away until he
administered clearer doctrine through his servants the prophets and finally
through his beloved Son, why should we not think that we are much more strictly
forbidden to add anything to the law, the Prophets, the Psalms, or the gospel?
The Lord has not degenerated from himself, for long ago he declared that nothing
offends him so greatly as being worshiped by human inventions*, and so forth.
Our theologians have likewise confirmed this abundantly against the papists
when disputing about their dogmatic traditions. But to institute and prescribe
such days is to add to God's institutions and precepts, which is plainly
unlawful. That instituting such days adds to God's institutions is manifest. God
either instituted such days under the New Testament or he did not. If he
instituted days, but not these same ones, who does not see that institutions are
added to institutions and precepts to God's precepts? If he did not institute
them, it was either because he *could not* or because he *would not*. The former
cannot be said. If the latter is said, it is evident that he did not wish such
days to be instituted under the New Testament. Human precepts and institutions
are therefore added to divine ones, and more institutions are imposed upon the
disciples' necks than God wished to impose. Indeed, the things he did not wish
to be imposed are imposed, for what he did not wish to be imposed, he did not
wish to be imposed. In vain will they say that only *corrupting additions, not
perfecting ones*, are prohibited, and that their institutions are of the latter
kind. As our theologians answer the papists well, it is blasphemous to say that
God's institutions are imperfect and must be perfected by human beings. All
such allegedly perfecting additions are in reality corrupting ones, because
whatever is added to what is perfect corrupts it and renders it imperfect.

*Sixth argument.* Although the apostle speaks several times about the
observance of days and testifies and clearly asserts that the Jewish days have
been abrogated, he nowhere speaks about observing any other days except the
Lord's Day. Nor does he say anything from which it can with any probability be
inferred that he wished other days to be instituted in place of those
abrogated. He nowhere says that these or those days are to be observed—these
new, ceremonial, and legal days. *Indeed*, says Didoclavius in *The Altar of
Damascus*, p. 701, *he does not even speak of any mystical day, or even one of
moral use, except the first Sabbath, which John calls the Lord's Day, that is,
the day of Christ. Nor is it sufficiently distinctly known that the day on
which he was caught up in the Spirit, if there had then been other days
dedicated to Christ, he would have said: I was caught up on the day of Christ's
passion, nativity, or resurrection, not: I was caught up in the Spirit on
Christ's Day, or the Lord's Day, which is the same thing, if there had been many
days of Christ or others besides the Lord's Day.* It is certainly astonishing
that he never said anything about instituting or observing other days when the
occasion to say something about it was so often offered. But if someone should
say that the church possesses the power to institute such days, do they not
charge the apostle with neglect of office? Will they not say that he and all
the other apostles, and even our Lord Jesus, were imperfect teachers of the
church and by no means revealed the whole will of God to us? If someone
*insists* that this argument has equal force against instituting any time for
celebrating ecclesiastical assemblies and holding sermons, since the apostles

[1674 logical printed p. 307 | IA leaf 325 | PDF p. 325]

did not institute such times, *I answer* that there is a very different account
of certain hours, changeable each week, fixed for holding sermons, in which
nothing is considered except the convenience that depends upon various and
changeable circumstances, and feast days instituted for an entirely different
end. Moreover, when the apostles commanded the Word of God to be preached, they
showed clearly enough that the church has the power to designate the time and
place in which this could be done conveniently and for edification, and they
confirmed it by their own example. Indeed, nature itself demands this. But can
it be shown that the apostles either observed such days or said in general,
*Celebrate days; institute feast days*? If they had said any such thing, we
would concede that it necessarily follows that the church can institute such
days. But since this cannot be demonstrated, it is evident that the church can
neither institute feast days in general nor these or others in particular. Some
cite Schoockius from Pareus's commentary on the Epistle to the Romans, doubt 4,
chapter 14, and the apostle's precept, *Let all things be done decently and in
order in the church. And God is not the author of confusion but of peace* (1
Corinthians 14:40, 33), and *I rejoice to see your order* (Colossians 2:5). *I
answer*: I freely grant that order and decorum are necessary to the church. But
I steadfastly deny that order and decorum cannot exist without feast days and
that there would be mere confusion in ecclesiastical assemblies. Indeed, this
single fact proves it utterly false: by the adversaries' own admission, no such
feasts were instituted in the apostolic church, yet order and decorum
nevertheless flourished there. Who will show us that such feast days were
instituted in the Colossian church, with respect to which the apostle said this
in chapter 2, verse 5? The cited passages make sufficiently clear that the
apostle earnestly desired everything to be done in order and decently. Why,
then, did he not institute feast days to avoid confusion? Furthermore, why does
order or decorum demand that five be instituted rather than fifty? Indeed, if
order and decorum demanded such days, the more that were instituted, the
greater the order and decorum would be. Why does decorum demand that these in
particular, which have now been instituted, be instituted rather than other
similar ones?

*Seventh argument.* Indeed, the apostle not only did not institute such days to
be observed, but he also condemns their observance in Galatians 4 and Colossians
2. *Days are observed*, says Calderwood in the place cited above, p. 702, *not
only when they are instituted in memory of some mystery or as a sign, but also
when they are esteemed holier or more sacred than other days, either because of
some ancient custom now abrogated or because of a mystery accomplished on that
day.* Some adversaries say that such days are not now observed as if they were
sacred, holy, and mystical, but for the sake of order and polity. Didoclavius,
or David Calderwood, asks in the place cited above why there is not one rule for
celebrating them all. Why is the same liberty not permitted on a feast day as
on the weekdays when sermons are held? Why do some churches dedicate two or
three annual days to commemorating the nativity or resurrection, while others
celebrate more, yet all fewer than the different mysteries or articles of
religion demand? Why do they follow the moon in observing Easter and the day of
the month at Christmas? And why do they not also observe Christmas on the
Lord's Day as they do Easter? Why do they follow the civil year on the Lord's
Day, the sacred year at Easter and Pentecost, and the first month of the sacred
year? Bellarmine himself confirms this in *On the Worship of the Saints*,
chapter 10: *It is certain*, he says, *that these days, namely*

[1674 logical printed p. 308 | IA leaf 326 | PDF p. 326]

*Christmas, Pentecost, the Passion, the Resurrection, and the Ascension, are not observed
for the sake of order and polity but for the sake of signification. For if they
were observed only for order, they would have been constituted rashly. What
kind of order is this: that there is not one intervening day between the Passion
and Resurrection, while there are ten between the Ascension and Pentecost? That
there are more than six months between Pentecost and Christmas? Again, if the
feasts are celebrated only for the sake of order, why do we follow the moon in
finding movable feasts? Again, why do they have the names Easter, Ascension, and
so forth, if they signify nothing sacred?* It is therefore abundantly clear
that such days are not instituted for the sake of order, whatever Durell may
say in his *Vindication*.

*Eighth argument.* Paul calls those who observed such days above others the
weaker in Romans 14. *They esteemed day above day*, says Didoclavius in the
place cited above, p. 702, *who thought that days once instituted by God, though
abrogated, were nevertheless still to be observed and that honor was to be
rendered to them above other days. The apostles tolerated that esteem and
distinction of days for a time because the synagogue was to be buried with
honor. In another way they esteem day above day and think days are to be
observed, whether with festivity or fasting, because some sacred mystery was
accomplished on that day.* But who will now say that the church possesses the
power to institute such days and thus not only teach but also urge that some
days be esteemed above others, when the gospel has now been sufficiently
promulgated and it is confessed that all days instituted by God himself among
the Jews have been wholly abolished? No one may say that this argument has
equal force against the Sabbath and against feast days. For we will later show
in its proper place that this text is by no means to be understood of the
weekly Sabbath, or the Lord's Day; indeed, it cannot be so understood. *We
esteem the Lord's Day above other days*, says the named author in the place
cited above, pp. 705–706, *not because of any mystery accomplished on that day
or signification of a future thing, but only because by divine authority and
for the sake of polity and order, through a positive determination of Christ or
the apostles, it was separated from the other days of the week to take the
place of the old Sabbath. That is, it was separated to preserve the external
worship of God, both public, lest a disorderly assembly of the people diminish
faith in Christ, and private, so that everyone might be bound to devote
themselves to meditation and pious exercises on a fixed day; and to this end,
lest even on a country estate masters should have the power to draw servants
away from both public and private worship of God, much less claim for themselves
the liberty to profane it.*

*Ninth argument.* The church may not deprive us of the Christian liberty Christ
procured for us. Christ redeemed us from the Mosaic pedagogy, from those weak
and beggarly elements which were only a shadow of things to come. But if the
church possessed the power to institute such days, it could deprive us of our
liberty. This must not be conceded. The adversaries reply in vain that the
apostle there condemns only Jewish feasts, not Christian ones. For although he
speaks directly and immediately about Jewish feasts, he consequently condemns
all those days by whose observance and institution our Christian liberty is
taken away. The apostle's arguments conclude no less firmly against feast days
invented by human beings than against Jewish ones instituted by God himself—
indeed, in part more strongly. For those days are a burden, all the more because
they were instituted only by human beings. It is a far greater and heavier
burden to be oppressed by merely human institutions than by divine ones. Those
days were part of the Jewish pedagogy and weak elements. It is therefore
inconsistent with the state of our liberty

[1674 logical printed p. 309 | IA leaf 327 | PDF p. 327]

to constitute similar elements and prescribe them for Christians to observe.
God instructed his ancient church by
such rudiments; afterward, in the time of the gospel, he poured out the Holy
Spirit in full measure. See Beza on Galatians 4:3. Indeed, in Colossians 2:20,
22, the apostle does not wish us to be burdened with *rites*. And what else, I
ask, can those feast days be called? Nor does he wish us to be subject to
prescriptions derived from human precepts and doctrines. It is sufficiently
evident that even now some of those Jewish days are observed, at least
materially—namely, *Easter* and *Pentecost*. But perhaps they will say that they
observe neither these nor any others in the Jewish manner, and that the apostle
condemns only such an observance. *I answer:* The apostle condemns not only the
observance of Jewish days and the Jewish observance of them, but also a Jewish
or quasi-Jewish observance of any days whatsoever, says Didoclavius in the place
cited above, p. 686. For the Galatians did not observe days in the same manner
as the Jews; otherwise they would have denied that Christ came in the flesh or
was sacrificed for us. Nor does the apostle condemn them simply because they
observed those days with the same spirit and for the same reason as the Jews,
or because they thought themselves bound by force of the Mosaic law, but simply
because they observed days, months, and so forth.

*Tenth argument.* The ancient church was not permitted to institute feast days;
therefore neither is the New Testament church. The consequence is clear because
the constitution of such days would accord more with the state of the Old
Testament church than the New. Therefore, if God had granted this power to any
church, he would have granted it to the Jewish church. Two things are objected
against the *antecedent*. *First*, that the Jews were once permitted to
institute the days of *Purim*. The most learned Calderwood answers this
objection in various ways in the place cited above, p. 681 and following. 1.
The days of Purim are never called in Scripture *the feast of Purim*, nor are
they given the nomenclature by which feasts instituted by God are named. Their
appointed time is not called *mogned*. Although *mogned* sometimes has a
broader scope and signifies not only solemnities of feasts but any appointed
time, the word used is *zemin*, a definite or appointed time. Nor are they
called Sabbaths. Schoockius replies in *Exercise* 9, or 11 in the second
edition, saying that *those days are named in the same way as the day of
firstfruits in Numbers 28:26, where the same word occurs in the Hebrew text.*
*I answer:* זמן is not read in the cited text. Mercerus affirms under that word
that it is read only in the books of captivity—Ezra, Nehemiah, and Esther—and
once in Ecclesiastes. See the passages cited by him and Buxtorf's concordances.
Schoockius replies further: *The same verb*, he says, *which in Esther 9:22 is
used of the celebration of the days of Purim is used in Ezra 6:16 of the feast
of Passover. This is a sure argument that, as far as the reason for its first
institution is concerned, this feast was equivalent to the feast of Passover.*
*I answer:* He does not indicate what verb is used in both passages, nor does
any verb appear in the text except the general עשה, *he did*, from which nothing
can be concluded. But if the feast of Purim were equivalent to Passover, then
those days were feasts appointed and destined for celebrating God's benefits
and promoting and declaring his glory, and therefore to be religiously and
perpetually observed by the Jews. For to say such a feast would doubtless have
been to say the feast of Passover. But then, by Schoockius's own testimony, they
ought to have been instituted by God alone, who is the sole legislator and who
therefore—not only so, but these are Schoockius's own words in section 1 of the
second edition—execrates every human commandment in Matthew 15:9 as
ἐθελοθρησκείαν, or voluntarily assumed worship (Colossians 2:20, 23). Therefore
those days ought to have been and were instituted by God, not by Mordecai,

[1674 logical printed p. 310 | IA leaf 328 | PDF p. 328]

whom he maintains was not a prophet but only a man of principal rank, and who
did nothing here by the impulse of the Spirit but relied only upon political
power. He therefore argues badly when he attempts to prove from this feast,
equated with Passover, that the church can, in memory of certain benefits of
God, appoint certain commemorative days for sacred assemblies and exercises,
to be observed freely for a time and for the sake of order, as he speaks. For
this can no more be proved from this feast than from Passover and the other
feasts instituted by God himself, if, with respect to its first institution,
this feast was equivalent to Passover. This is further confirmed from the
second edition, where he answers Reynesius's words, *It was therefore to be
perpetually observed as part of divine worship*, as follows: *Certainly, as
long as Christ, who observed this feast, had not supplied its fulfillment, it
likewise ought to have been held in veneration.* Hence, he concludes, human
beings could institute feasts whose fulfillment was Christ. For he adds, *and
thus far it was equivalent to Passover in its end, but not (note well) in its
institution, since it was not instituted by God himself*—in which he also
contradicts his preceding statements.

Didoclavius continues: *But you will say they nevertheless rested on the
fourteenth and fifteenth days. They rested, to be sure, but from their enemies.
Those at Susa rested on the fifteenth day; those in the other provinces rested
from their enemies on the fourteenth. For the inhabitants of Susa were fewer
and could not destroy their enemies in one day. Thus, although they killed five
hundred men on the thirteenth, they did not yet rest from their enemies, and on
the fourteenth they killed three hundred men. They then rested from their
enemies on the fifteenth because none remained. The context gives us this
interpretation. Esther 9:16 expressly says that those remaining in their
province rested from their enemies, and verse 22 says that, in accordance with
the days on which they rested from their enemies, Mordecai instituted that they
should annually celebrate those days with feasting and joy. You see how
Mordecai himself interprets their rest on those first days of Purim: resting
from their enemies. Nothing is instituted concerning rest to be observed
thereafter. It therefore cannot be extorted by any torture that they ceased
from all servile work, either at the beginning or by a commanded cessation
thereafter. Furthermore, the Holy Spirit here uses a different word to express
this resting than* שבת, *the word ordinarily used to signify vacation or
cessation from work on a feast day. Here he uses* נוח, *they rested, whose root
is* נוח. Schoockius replies: נוח *and* שבת *are synonyms, and although Sabbath
descends from the latter root, the former also, with all its derivatives, is
transferred to signify any rest, and specifically Sabbath rest, as is evident
if Exodus 20:11 and Genesis 2:3 are compared. I answer:* 1. These are not
synonyms in every respect. For נוח, as Edward Leigh has it in *Sacred
Criticism*, signifies the rest of one who does absolutely nothing, while שבת
signifies cessation from work in which one was previously engaged. The force
of Didoclavius's argument lies in this: the Holy Spirit does not here use the
word that ordinarily signifies cessation from work on a feast day, but another
which very rarely denotes it. Therefore, although נוח occurs in Exodus 20:11,
where another word proper to a feast is also read, the learned man's argument
is not weakened. It rests on the fact that, when speaking of Purim, the Holy
Spirit uses only the common word and never the special one used elsewhere when
speaking of feasts properly so called.

[1674 logical printed p. 311 | IA leaf 329 | PDF p. 329]

*Didoclavius therefore rightly concludes: The Holy Spirit did not depart in
vain from the solemn terms for feasts, but did so to teach that no feast
properly so called is instituted here—that is, no sacred day with cessation
from every work or a peculiar worship in the temple or synagogues—even though
it was decreed to be observed after they should return to their homeland.*

Calderwood continues: *Nothing, therefore*, he says, *is decreed except that
they celebrate those days with joy, feasting, and merriment, both by sending
portions to one another and giving gifts to the poor* (verses 19, 22). Thus
chapter 9 says that in every province and city, wherever the king's command and
decree reached, the Jews had joy, gladness, feasting, and merriment. *Does this
make a holy day or a feast properly so called, which is the principal matter in
controversy? The adversaries cannot prove even a simple word from this passage,
much less a feast constituted by the three elements described above.
Hospinianus therefore did not write rashly when he said: On the feast of Purim
labor was not forbidden. They were free to labor insofar as feasting and
merriment did not hinder them. They are called days of feasting and gladness,
and the pagan Jews are said to have celebrated the fourteenth of Adar with
gladness, feasting, and merriment—Hebrew, a good or pleasant day. Elias Levita,
the German Thesbite, as Drusius reports in his annotations on Esther, says that
appointed holidays and solemn feasts called royal are commonly called good
days because they are joyful and pleasant, like wedding days. We thus see that
Purim consisted only of civil or political days of joy and feasting, memorials
of their fasting and crying, as Esther 9:31 says, or, as verse 22 says, because
their sorrow was turned into joy and their mourning into merriment. Glad at the
unexpected transformation of extreme danger into complete tranquility, they
ceased slaughtering the citizens who had plotted against them and gave
themselves to banquets and merriment.* Schoockius replies: *That the Jews
celebrated this day annually as a feast is evident from 2 Maccabees 15:37,
where it is called* ἡ Μαρδοχαϊκὴ ἡμέρα. *I answer:* That apocryphal author does
call it *a feast day*, but what trust is to be placed in him without the
approving testimony of Scripture? The apocryphal text does not say that the
Jews celebrated it as a feast properly so called. It is true, as Didoclavius
says, that the Jews had a special liturgy on those days. But we do not read that
Mordecai instituted it. The same Didoclavius says that the ridiculous rites of
that liturgy show it was instituted by drunkards rather than the sober.
According to Schindler under *Phur*, whenever Haman is mentioned they beat the
synagogue benches with fists and hammers as if striking his head. Schoockius
further replies: *Although the Jews do not appear to have been forbidden to
cease from their works that day, the Talmudists nevertheless thought it better
to abstain, as Buxtorf testifies in chapter 24 of* The Jewish Synagogue. *I
answer:* What the Talmudists said matters little; we are not bound to value
their writings above Scripture. Nor do they say that cessation from servile
work was commanded by Mordecai, but only that they thought abstention better.
We know that the Jews later perverted not a few of God's statutes by their
superstitions. Finally Schoockius replies: *If the passage is rightly examined,
Esther 9:21–22 appears to mention rest from enemies as the cause which ought to
move the Jews to rest, as on a feast day. I answer:* It mentions rest from
enemies as the cause that ought to move the Jews to celebrate those days with
joy, feasting, merriment, and sending portions to one another, but not as the
cause that ought

[1674 logical printed p. 312 | IA leaf 330 | PDF p. 330]

to move them to rest from every work as on a feast day. This is asserted
gratuitously, and nothing follows from it.

Didoclavius adds: *But they ought*, he says, *to have ceased from all work, not
only on those first days of joy—which we do not deny can be done on an
extraordinary day of thanksgiving—but also afterward, and to have sanctified
those days with pious religious exercises. Yet from Mordecai's authority it
does not follow that bishops can do the same by their ordinary power. He was
regarded as a prophet and author of Esther. Moreover, Mordecai was one of the
120 who instituted the Great Synagogue, among whom were the prophets Zechariah,
Daniel, Ezra, Malachi, and so forth. Whatever the nature of Purim, extraordinary
gifts of the Holy Spirit still flourished in the Jewish church. Furnished with
them, they could institute many things not to be drawn into imitation.*

Schoockius replies again. 1. *It is gratuitously said that Mordecai was a
prophet, and Sixtus of Siena's authority alone is not enough for us to believe
it. I answer:* Sixtus says in book 1, p. 20, *Most Latin authors relate that
this volume was written in Hebrew by Mordecai.* The statement therefore does
not rest on his authority alone unless we hold him a liar. And if Mordecai was
God's amanuensis in composing that book, he was an extraordinary and holy man
of God, moved by the Holy Spirit, as 2 Peter 1:21 testifies. Sixtus adds: *The
words in chapter 9, verses 20 and 23, also appear to favor this, as do all the
deeds contained in the epistle—that is, this book. Mordecai wrote everything
from the beginning of the book to the words at the beginning of chapter 10.*
Reply 2: *Mordecai is nowhere commended as a prophet, but only as a man of
principal dignity, who also returned from the Babylonian captivity* (Ezra 2:2;
Nehemiah 7:7), *and whatever he did here appears to have been done in reliance
upon political power. I answer:* Although he is nowhere expressly commended as
a prophet, it is sufficient that he was an extraordinary man and God's
amanuensis, as Ezra and others were who are not commended as prophets. 2.
Whatever appears to Schoockius, it is not evident that Mordecai did whatever he
did here in reliance upon political power. Reply 3: *Drusius never raved so as
to maintain that all who belonged to the Great Synagogue were prophets,
although some prophets might have been its members. I answer:* Nor did D.
Calderwood say that Drusius asserted this. He had said: *Moreover, Mordecai was
one of the 120 who constituted the Great Synagogue, among whom were the prophets
Zechariah, Daniel, Ezra, Malachi, and so forth. They constituted the canon, as
the common opinion holds, says Drusius, and were called the later elders, of
whom Simeon the Just was the last*, and so forth. He said only that Mordecai was
one of that assembly and a member of that council, concerning whose authority
he directed us to Buxtorf's *Masoretic Commentary*, chapter 10, in its
historical section. It is abundantly clear that he was not of ordinary
condition but a man of more than human dignity.

They further object to the *feast of dedication*, instituted by Judas Maccabeus
in 1 Maccabees 4, which our Lord honored by his presence in John 10 and thereby
approved. *I answer:* Some maintain that this feast was lawfully instituted by
a prophet through extraordinary revelation and singular warning, so the
example has no bearing on the matter. So Junius and Sutclivius against
Bellarmine. But others judge more probably that it was not legitimately
instituted, because after the first dedication of the temple under Solomon (1
Kings 8) and the second after

[1674 logical printed p. 313 | IA leaf 331 | PDF p. 331]

the captivity, there was no annual remembrance of even one day with a feast's
solemnity. Who will say that this eight-day celebration was instituted by the
same Spirit who directed Solomon and the returning captives? Those who built
the temple and supplied its utensils instituted no annual remembrance, while
this man introduced an annual remembrance and eight-day feast merely because of
the altar and certain restored things. Neither Hezekiah after Ahaz profaned the
temple, Josiah after the pollution of Manasseh and Amon, nor Zerubbabel, Ezra,
and Nehemiah after it was rebuilt instituted an annual celebration of the
dedication. Many corruptions had crept in during that age and superstition had
prevailed. It is therefore no wonder that the Pharisees, who wished to be
regarded as Israel's wise men and were commonly so called, as Drusius notes on
Mark 2:18 and 12:10, instituted such a feast. This is further clear from its
reported cause: *When they entered the temple and found no clean oil in the
sanctuary except a small flask containing no more than would light the temple
lamps for one day, they lit the lamps from that oil in order. They lasted eight
days, until they had crushed olives and pressed clean oil. The wise men of that
age therefore decreed that the eight days beginning on the twenty-fifth of
Kislev should each year be days of joy and praise, and that each night lamps
should be lit in the entrances of each house to declare this miracle. How
ridiculous were the feasts instituted by Israel's wise men*, adds Didoclavius,
p. 685, *since they instituted one at every slightest occasion, such as when
they crowned an ancient rite, custom, or ministry in the temple or synagogue
with a feast. From the custom of bringing wood into God's house at appointed
times from year to year to burn upon the altar* (Nehemiah 10:35), *they
instituted the feast called Xylophoria. They had other fixed observances, such
as the Tekuphoth. We thus see by whom the annual celebration of the dedication
was introduced and in what times—a more corrupt age, when human traditions and
commands gained strength and ecclesiastical and political government was
confused.* As for John 10, nothing there proves that Christ approved this
feast. The place and time are noted—where and when the question about the
Messiah was raised: when the dedication was held at Jerusalem, it was winter,
and Christ walked in the temple in Solomon's porch. Will anyone say that the
apostle observed the days of unleavened bread at Philippi or the Jewish fast on
the day of expiation merely because Acts 20:6 says he sailed from Philippi
after the days of unleavened bread and Acts 27:9 says he warned his companions
when the fast had passed and sailing was dangerous? Can it be demonstrated that
Christ deliberately went there to celebrate that feast of dedication?

*Eleventh argument.* The anti-Sabbatarian adversaries say that the Decalogue's
fourth precept, concerning celebrating one day in the weekly cycle, was
ceremonial and no more binds us now than other precepts about annual days. They
therefore cannot deny that all annual days belong to the Jewish pedagogy. Hence
I infer that no one may now institute such days and prescribe another
ceremonial law by instituting them. It is well known that the ceremonial

[1674 logical printed p. 314 | IA leaf 332 | PDF p. 332]

law has been abrogated in whole and in part. Who, then, will think that any
church possesses power to
thrust some species of ceremonial law upon Christians? Does this accord with
the gospel state? Does the apostle distinguish between the state of the ancient
church and that of the new Christian church? Does he call those annual days
weak and beggarly elements of the world? Does he say clearly enough that those
elements conflict with the gospel church's state? Why, then, do human beings
wish to reintroduce an infantile and abolished state? They may not say that the
church can now institute such days with an end and intention different from
their former institution. For at the least their observance will be mixed,
partly formal and partly material: they will be observed with an intention of
worship in general. For this reason we condemn circumcision among the
Ethiopians. Although they do not use it as a ceremony indicating the Christ to
come, they use it as something religious and with an intention of worship in
general. When Cajetan minimizes this usage, saying they preserve it not in a
legal spirit but only with a certain intention of imitating Christ, others
answer as follows. One circumcised in imitation of Christ either regards Christ
as undergoing circumcision as a sacrament instituted by God, and thus falls
into Jewish superstition; or regards Christ only as marked by such a wound or
scar, which is implausible and would be vain superstition; or regards conformity
with Christ in suffering that pain, which is likewise improbable, since an
infant has no sense of such conformity, nor may adults pierce their hands or
feet to conform themselves to Christ in pain and suffering—which would also be
vain superstition. If this distinction is admitted as a universal remedy, the
entire Mosaic law, every rite, sacrament, and observance, as well as days of
this kind, can easily be restored. But God does not now wish the Christian
church to be taught by the weak and beggarly elements used by the church while
it remained in a servile state under guardians. As the annual feasts instituted
by God were elements and rudiments by which he taught the Jewish people, so if
he had instituted other days for the same use, they too would have been
elements and rudiments. What else, then, will days instituted by human beings
be? Does such teaching suit the Christian church? Tilenus speaks in vain when
he says in his *Exhortation*: *Nor are all the members of the adult New
Testament church so firm and robust that by far the greatest part does not
always still consist of nursing infants, gradually to be instructed in these
mysteries*, and so forth. Although there are still many lambs, infants, and
nursing children, the apostle compares the ancient church's state with the new,
and its body with the new church's body. It is certain that God now wishes the
uninstructed to be taught otherwise than through such beggarly elements.

*Twelfth argument.* The fourth precept's words, *Six days you shall work*, and
so forth, supply another argument. If these words contain a precept, an
interpretation which does not displease Zanchi on the fourth commandment—*The
opinion of those who think that “Six days you shall work” contains a precept for
avoiding idle leisure does not displease me*, he says, *so that everyone should
work with his hands to sustain his own life and his neighbor's. Thus God appears
here to have instituted for his people what they call the active and
contemplative life and to have prescribed all their time for both kinds of
life*—then it is certain

[1674 logical printed p. 315 | IA leaf 333 | PDF p. 333]

that the church lacks power to command cessation from every servile work for
several whole days. But if these words contain only a permission, as others
maintain, who will say the church possesses the power to deprive anyone of this
God-given liberty? Who dares sanctify a day God did not sanctify? Since God
distinguished one day from the other six and sanctified it by separating it for
sacred uses, and permitted human beings to use the others for conducting life's
business, the church clearly has no power to appoint unsanctified days to uses
which make labor on them unlawful. Our argument follows no less evidently if
the words mean: Six days have been granted to you for all your works, so that
without impediment you may sanctify every seventh day as Sabbath. Those who
institute other days place the impediment God wished removed and thus hinder
Sabbath sanctification.

The adversaries make many readily answered objections. 1. *Objection:* God
granted human beings dominion over the birds, fish, and beasts, yet princes may
grant the liberty of fowling, fishing, and hunting to some and take it from
others. *I answer:* This dominion is granted to humanity in general, not to each
person over all birds, fish, and beasts, for then all private ownership and
possession would be removed. But the liberty of working six days has been
granted to everyone individually. The analogy therefore fails.

*Objection 2.* Everything God permits and places within our liberty is subject
to positive human determinations and commands. *I answer:* Many kinds of things
permitted by God cannot be prohibited by human beings. For example, they cannot
prohibit this or that man from marrying, although it remains within his power
to marry or not. Every human determination in permitted matters must follow
Scripture's general rules. If the church determined that we should have two or
three Sabbaths every week, would this accord with those rules? What if it
enjoined rest every day? Would that determination be lawful? If not, human
beings plainly cannot always prohibit what God permits.

*Objection 3.* A prince can command cessation from economic works and enjoin
military reviews and similar things. *I answer:* Public works concerning the
community's good are themselves works to be done on those six days. Thus, when
cessation from private work is commanded for the kingdom's public business and
the city's common advantage, holidays are not commanded, much less feast days,
nor is the permission simply removed. Although the magistrate can command
cessation from some private work—which cannot be done absolutely and without
regard to the common good—it does not follow that he can command cessation from
all work. Even granting this, it would not follow that he can command a feast
day or religious cessation.

*Objection 4.* This reasoning condemns fixed sermon times on weekdays. *I
answer:* By no means. An hour or two is not a whole day. Those fixed times are
not enjoined as if labor during them were never lawful. People are not compelled
by punishments and threats to cease from work, but all who conveniently can
attend sermons when they can are to be exhorted and

[1674 logical printed p. 316 | IA leaf 334 | PDF p. 334]

admonished. The time is not appointed for religious exercises so that secular
business may not be conducted then. That would in reality make the time
relatively holy, which we deny human beings can do. And when God commands
sermons in season and out of season, does he not command that some times be
established for that end?

*Objection 5.* This argument also condemns sudden extraordinary holidays of
fasting and thanksgiving. *I answer:* It would if the law of nature and God's
Word had not commanded us by prayer and fasting to deprecate guilt and implore
God's grace when evils threaten, and to thank him for a benefit or deliverance.
See Joel 1:14; 2:15; Zechariah 8:19; 7:3. But we have no command to institute
an ordinary annual feast. We are commanded to remember God's benefits, recall
them often, and proclaim the gospel, but nowhere to do this on a fixed annual
day or institute a particular annually recurring day for that end.

*Objection 6.* A private person can vow to sanctify a day or observe festive
time notwithstanding this permission; therefore the church can enjoin a day's
sanctification upon others. *I answer:* The consequence fails. Whatever may be
the matter of a vow is not therefore the matter of a law, for a person can
promise God internal acts about which human beings can make no law. Moreover,
*A person cannot bind himself by a vow*, says Didoclavius, p. 675, *to sanctify
a day or observe a feast, because it is unlawful to sanctify any day even
privately. To consecrate a day is to separate it from all profane exercises for
religious exercises, specifically for commemorating a benefit or mystery, or to
impose a sacred signification upon it. To appoint time for religious exercises
so that secular business may not be conducted then is to make it holy—not by
nature, for the papists themselves do not profess this, nor is a day sanctified
by God holier by nature, otherwise the Sabbath could not have changed to the
Lord's Day—but by use. Neither the church nor any individual can do this where
the Lord has not established sanctification by his authority, as in an
extraordinary fast or thanksgiving. It is one thing when a private person
appoints an hour or day for private worship with liberty to postpone it when a
legitimate occasion of his calling intervenes, or devotes it to private worship
while feeling free to pass it over and choose another. It is another so to
appoint and worship that nothing unholy or unreligious may be done during that
hour or day. This would be to sanctify, or make holy by use. The former remains
free to the private person, who uses time as though not using it. But when a
superior power imposes observance, the liberty he would have had under a private
designation is removed. It therefore does not follow: A private person can, for
order, designate the hour or day most convenient for private exercises, always
free to pass it over and choose another; therefore a superior can impose an
inviolable law and establish an hour and day for both public and private
exercises,*

[1674 logical printed p. 317 | IA leaf 335 | PDF p. 335]

*which he is not free to pass over and replace.* *He insists:* A private person
can separate something from his goods and dedicate it to the church; therefore
he can also dedicate some of the days God permits him for his work. *I answer:*
Time, says Didoclavius, p. 677, is not a private good but belongs to things
common by natural right, like air, flowing water, the sea, and shore, whose use
is equally common to all. One is therefore not as free to surrender the common
use of time as a private thing, because of mutual human commerce. If everyone
at will renounced the use of time, no order could be preserved in commerce. He
further answers: Grant that time and private goods are alike; dedicating private
goods and sanctifying them are still different. To sanctify a day or portion is
to exempt it from every profane use, so that it cannot be used even for the most
honorable end outside worship. No one can sanctify anything this way. No place
or building is so dedicated to worship that outside public worship it cannot
serve an honorable civil use, and all worship there can be freely omitted. Not
everything the church does to promote worship is sacred and holy. Because the
church consists of people, it shares other political assemblies' need for aids
and instruments for public actions. A temple's use is therefore merely
political and civil notwithstanding dedication. Dedication is common to other
places: one can separate land, dedicate it to the city's public use, build a
town hall, and give it to the community. *He insists:* But things dedicated to
sacred uses are called sacred; hence a sacred house, plate, and cup. The same
author answers on p. 679 that these are not sacred even if dedicated to that
use. The same table can serve love feasts and common meals as the sacred supper.
If someone said, Here is the sacred plate, there the sacred cup, and over there
the holy table, I would say his mind already dwells in Jerusalem's temple or a
pagan or papal shrine and that he speaks according to their usage. Furthermore,
*to assign, dedicate, and sanctify* are different. To dedicate is to renounce
dominion. Thus a private person dedicates land or a building to the church by
transferring its ownership. The church accepts and freely uses either it or
another in its place, and may never use it at all. Convenience, not dedication,
moves the church to frequent the place. If time were numbered among private
goods, it would be movable, not immovable; even if called immovable,
immovability would not remove the nature of assignment or designation. But if
the church wished to dedicate to God a building given to it—that is, resign
dominion over it to God—it would do so either for no use or only for worship.
If the *former*, the dedication would be ridiculous.

[1674 logical printed p. 318 | IA leaf 336 | PDF p. 336]

What need has God of buildings? If only for worship, the church could no longer
perform even honorable profane work there because God would own the building;
indeed, it could not neglect worship there. This is to bind itself as a slave
to a place and sanctify what God alone can sanctify. The same applies to church
utensils. Thus freely designating a day or part is one thing, dedicating it
another, sanctifying it another. The church designates a weekday or part for
weekly sermons but does not dedicate it, for it can use that portion as profane,
neglect it, and choose another. *He insists:* Feast days are only of this kind.
The same author answers: *If they say this, I would wish them a modest forehead.
Do they not openly profess the dedication of days? Do they not say those days
are to be sanctified by holy convocations, and outside their public exercises
compel rest from every honorable work lest time consecrated to God be profaned?
If not for this end, then for what other?*

*Thirteenth argument.* God has granted neither the church nor any creature power
to institute divine worship or a part of it. Therefore he has not granted power
to institute feast days. The *consequence* is clear because feasts are a part
of worship and religion, containing and promoting piety and devotion according
to Scripture, the adversaries' hypotheses, and common opinion and practice. The
church can determine a circumstance of worship so all is done orderly and
decently, but cannot institute a substantial part. Circumstances are infinite,
variable, and common to every external action, sacred or secular. All such
actions require decorum and order, which have the same force in all actions.
These circumstances could not be commanded in Scripture because they are
infinite and always changeable. But no one will say feasts could not have been
most particularly designated in God's Word. The adversaries may not deny
esteeming these days as part of worship. Papists openly profess it;
hierarchical writers assert as much; and common practice testifies to it,
since people observe them more scrupulously than the Lord's Day. Nor does it
help to say the church can change one feast to another. While the dedication
remains, that day is holy, just as the seventh day was while its institution
remained.

*Fourteenth argument.* The power to consecrate and sanctify a day belongs to
God alone. As Zanchi says on the fourth commandment, p. 82 in my edition, it
belongs properly to God to choose a person or thing from profane things,
consecrate it to himself, and thereby sanctify it, just as God alone justifies.
All the orthodox teach this against the papists. See the Leiden *Synopsis*,
disputation 21, thesis 7; Polanus, *Syntagma*, book 6, chapter 51; Fenner,
*Theology*, book 6, chapter 3; Perkins on Galatians 4; Willet's *Synopsis*. Our
theologians abundantly demonstrate that nothing is to be instituted or
sanctified in worship without God's Word. He alone can consecrate a thing or
person—appoint it to a particular sacred use so its status is entirely changed
and relative sanctity imposed, so it may be used only in worship

[1674 logical printed p. 319 | IA leaf 337 | PDF p. 337]

of God and for spiritual worship. God alone, therefore, possesses the power to
consecrate, separate, and sanctify a day and make it holier than others. Thus
God alone possesses the power to institute feast days which are regarded as
holier than others—not in themselves, perhaps, but relatively and in use.

*Fifteenth argument* is the one derived by the celebrated Voetius in *On the
Sabbath and Feasts*, p. 1303, from harmful effects. *They carry more
superstition than devotion with them*, he says; *they savor of syncretism with
pagans and Jews, at least have the appearance of evil, and burden the church
with useless perplexities, scruples, dissensions, and contentions which hinder
the exercises and spiritual progress of devotion.* And, as he says later in
argument 7, the newer and later the ages—and the more corrupt and remote from
the first apostolic age—the greater the abundance of feasts that inundated
them. This can be seen in Hospinianus, *On Feasts*, and Chemnitz, *Examination
of the Council of Trent*, part 4, *On Feasts*. Again, as he says there in
argument 8, their first origin was partly the people's ignorance and
κακοζηλίᾳ, and partly, to speak most gently, the private zeal of certain
pastors—not the first councils, whether universal or particular, nor a common
judgment of several eminent churches. Had that been the case, their origin
would not be so ἄδηλος, or uncertain. The procuring cause of their origin was
the pagan feasts, for which reason they ought to have been especially suspect.
For although those imprudent men pretended to flee every appearance of Judaism,
they willingly borrowed or imitated the appearance of paganism, whether the
same or modified—as the papists everywhere acknowledge. Does Hospinianus not
show this concerning particular feasts?

*Sixteenth argument.* The church has not been granted the power to prescribe
and enjoin commands like those of the Decalogue. But if the church possessed
the power to institute a feast, it could give commands like the precepts of the
Decalogue. Who will say this? The foundation of the argument is that our
adversaries maintain that the fourth commandment is ceremonial and mutable. It
is certain that the command enjoins dedicating and spending some day on God and
sacred things. Since such a thing was commanded in the Decalogue, it seems
immodest and arrogant to say that the church can give similar commands. If so,
why do we not have twenty or one hundred commands instead of ten? And if the
church possesses the power to institute feast days, does it not possess the
power to give and enjoin Decalogical commands or their equals?

*Seventeenth argument*, which is argument 5 in the celebrated Voetius, is this.
Feasts are said to have been instituted and are principally observed so that,
not in the common way along with all other days but in a particular way, there
might be a grateful commemoration—for example, of the mystery of Christ's
incarnation or conception; Epiphany, or Christ's baptism and the magi's arrival;
his death and burial; his circumcision; and so forth—as the work of that day on
its own day. Indeed, in the papists' account they were instituted to signify
and represent mysteries and to be parts of divine worship. But the days of
Christ's benefits and mysteries, Mary's conception and purification, the
martyrdom of saints, and so forth are for the most part unknown and uncertain.
In this respect the feasts are lies and their teachers teachers of lies, no
less than papal images. We can add this: If God had wished

[1674 logical printed p. 320 | IA leaf 338 | PDF p. 338]

the various mysteries of Christianity to be celebrated by various annual
feasts, he would not have concealed the day of the Nativity, Circumcision, and
so forth, so that each day's work might be celebrated on its day. If the works
of a day, as the adversaries dream, had raised and consecrated the days
themselves to greater dignity, they would have consecrated the true and certain
days, not false and supposed ones. If God wished us to celebrate a day's work
on its own day, he would at least have made known the days of the year when such
works occurred. Since he chose not to indicate this, it is evident that he did
not wish such days observed for that end. The celebrated Voetius abundantly
shows in his dispute on the subject how uncertain it is in what season of the
year, much less what day, Christ was born. We are therefore uncertain about
this feast and every feast dependent upon its calculation, to say nothing of
many others. Schoockius does not weaken this by asking us to prove from
Scripture or ecclesiastical antiquity the major proposition: *The devout
remembrance of individual divine benefits ought to be celebrated on the exact
day they occurred.* This is the common doctrine of all who observe such days
and the foundation of those who institute them at such times. They suppose the
work of the day ought to be done and celebrated on its day; otherwise the
observance would not be rational. In civil days instituted by human beings,
respect is always had to a benefit occurring that day, and its remembrance is
given on the precise day. This appears in Purim, the Dedication, Passover, some
other feasts once instituted by God, and birthdays instituted by human beings.
Otherwise, different churches could celebrate the same feast at very different
times: one could celebrate Christ's nativity in December, another in May, and a
third in March. One church could celebrate his birth on the same day another
celebrates his death and another his resurrection. Schoockius does not see why
a time defined by the agreement of several churches united in doctrine should
be called into doubt at one church's discretion. Yet if the institution of such
days is free, one church's determination cannot bind all or prejudice their
liberty. Distinct particular churches could therefore celebrate Christ's
nativity on every day of the entire year. Who would not judge that absurd?

*Eighteenth argument* is Voetius's argument 4. Things with no end are nothing.
But New Testament feasts have no end of the sort they allege. Therefore. The
*major* consequence holds because in such relations the end gives the thing its
being and is, as it were, its form. The *minor is proved* because the end would
be a mystical signification and representation of this or that divine benefit,
and its commemoration with particular joy and thanksgiving by a particular
manner of divine worship and duty affixed to the solemnity. See what follows.
Schoockius in section 20 opposes three things from D. Zanchi. The first is:
*If feasts were therefore instituted among the Jews to commemorate those
benefits, why did Christ's church not also rightly institute the feasts we have
recounted, so that on those days solemn remembrance might be celebrated of the
benefits we received from Christ and the illustrious works he performed for
us, of which we have certain histories in the Evangelists?* *I answer:* The
reason is not

[1674 logical printed p. 321 | IA leaf 339 | PDF p. 339]

the same. God saw fit to teach the Old Testament church through such externals
directing it to the coming Christ, but wished the New Testament church free
from that entire ceremonial yoke. 2. The memory of those benefits can be
preserved otherwise than by feasts; otherwise God would have instituted feasts
for us. 3. God instituted the Lord's Day, on which all these benefits can be
sufficiently commemorated. Since we have no other instituted day for this end,
God plainly judged other days entirely useless, and we ought not be wiser than
God. The second argument is: *Since the honorable, as Cicero defines it, is what
is worthy of praise even if it is not actually praised, it follows that it is
honorable to celebrate the feasts celebrated by the ancient church. It is
praiseworthy to follow antiquity in matters not intrinsically evil but
indifferent, for antiquity must be judged not to have acted without cause,
especially in constituting feast days.* *I answer:* We judge it more praiseworthy
to follow an older antiquity—the apostolic church—which also acted for a better
reason, especially by not instituting feasts. 2. We deny that instituting feasts
is indifferent. 3. The papists argue similarly for their corruptions. 4. It is
not evident that the ancient church had a good reason for instituting feasts.
The third is drawn from utility: *It cannot be said how useful it is for the
people to celebrate feast days on which histories of Christ's deeds for our
salvation are recited and explained. How many never read anything themselves or
hear Scripture explained except when they come to church? Another benefit is
that weaker believers are not as offended by us as they would be if we utterly
despised such days, as though we did not care what Christ did for us.* *I
answer:* 1. The histories of Christ's deeds for our salvation can be recited
and explained at times other than fixed annual days, and ought to be recited
and celebrated much more often than once a year. 2. Those who never read or
hear Scripture explained except at church need to hear these things more often
than annually. 3. Those who think people who do not observe such days care
little about Christ's deeds are weak indeed; that weakness must be treated by
something other than performing what is not indifferent but evil.

*Nineteenth argument.* The most highly regarded Reformers wished and desired
all such feast days abolished, and other celebrated theologians judge them
useless and harmful. Luther said in his book to the German nobility that *the
conclusion is that all feasts should be abolished, retaining only the Lord's
Day.* In *On Good Works* he says, *Would that among Christians there were no
feast except the Lord's Day.* Calvin writes to Menso Poppius in 1559: *It is
indeed desirable that few feast days besides the Lord's Day be retained. For as
long as remnants of superstition remain, human beings will always be too prone
to this vice. Today the greater part seeks to favor the papacy by holidays.* In
a 1551 letter to Haller he says: *Moreover, since some of your people have been
seriously offended by the feast days abrogated here*, and shortly afterward,
*Before I ever entered the city there were absolutely no holidays except the
Lord's Day. Those celebrated among you were accepted by the same plebiscite by
which Farellus and I were expelled, and were extorted more by the violence of
wicked men than decreed in legitimate order.* In a letter to the minister at
Büren: *Before*

[1674 logical printed p. 322 | IA leaf 340 | PDF p. 340]

—he says—*I first entered this city, all feast days except the Lord's Day had been
abrogated. Farellus and Viret judged this useful. I acquiesced in the accepted
custom. This new province of Bern followed the same policy at first. N. arose
and fought no less tumultuously for feast days than Victor of Rome once fought
for his Easter.* The adversaries object that his letter to Haller, column 136,
says: *I nevertheless wish it attested that, had the choice been referred to
me, I would not have spoken in favor of what is now constituted.* And that he
says in the letter to the minister at Büren: *When I heard that those days had
been abrogated by plebiscite, the matter was so unexpected that I was almost
stunned. Certainly, had my opinion been asked, I say only that I would scarcely
have dared so to decree.* The answer is easy. He spoke so not because he favored
those days or regretted their abolition, but because the opposition was so
great that he saw he would accomplish nothing. He therefore testifies that he
was inclined to foster peace and concord as far as possible and, if asked,
would have given this judgment lest the gospel's course be impeded. His other
words sufficiently show this, rather than the alleged cause. In the letter to
Haller: *Since some of your people have been seriously offended by the feast
days abrogated here, it is credible that many hateful rumors are likewise being
spread there. Thus I conclude that not only the malicious but also the ignorant
ascribe the whole affair to me. Now, to remove what he expected would be
uniquely cast against him by both,* he adds, *I can solemnly testify that the
matter was transacted without my knowledge or even my desire.* It is wrongly
inferred that they were abolished against his will. He acknowledges the matter
was transacted *without his knowledge*, so he was not its author—indeed,
without his desiring it *under the circumstances*, as the sequel explains:
*Before I ever entered the city there were absolutely no holidays except the
Lord's Day. Those celebrated among you were accepted by the same plebiscite by
which Farellus and I were expelled, and were extorted more tumultuously by the
violence of wicked men than decreed in legitimate order.* It is therefore
beyond doubt that Calvin did not want those holidays, tumultuously restored
from banishment, received and permanently retained. Why, then, did he not oppose
them more effectively? Hear what follows: *Since my recall I have sought this
compromise, that Christ's nativity be celebrated in your manner*, and so forth.
Because everything boiled so violently, he pursued peace. *Meanwhile there
were*, he says, *certain stubborn people who perversely dissented from the
common custom. Since variation would not be tolerable in a rightly constituted
church and would create suspicion among outsiders that the citizens did not
agree, I exhorted the senate to remove this disagreement by a suitable remedy.*
The cause of his moderation is plain: fostering peace among citizens. A little
later he adds: *As a new guest, I heard about the abrogation. Would that N. had
once acted less ambitiously. Feast days had been abolished in this whole
province. He contended no less stubbornly against all French-speaking pastors
that these four return from banishment than if the church's salvation were at
stake. You would have said Victor was fighting the Easterners for his Easter.*
This shows that Calvin unexpectedly learned of the abrogation of all feasts and
had rested before the change because

[1674 logical printed p. 323 | IA leaf 341 | PDF p. 341]

he saw no way to remedy the evil. But did he regret their abolition? The sequel
teaches: *It was enough for me*,
he says, *briefly to indicate the source of this sudden change among us.
Although I was neither its adviser nor instigator, I do not (note well) regret
that it happened.* He ends: *There is no reason for people to be so exasperated
if we use our liberty as the church's edification requires, just as it is fair
that our custom should not prejudice others.* He clearly defends what was done.
His letter to the minister at Büren confirms it: *After our expulsion, four
feast days and other rites were instituted. Upon returning I remained
peacefully at rest, except that I could not conceal that Christ's circumcision
was perversely celebrated while the day of his death was passed over. I
nevertheless restrained myself and checked those who cried for those days to
be wholly removed. Those who had first embraced the gospel resented the later
innovation so greatly that they sometimes did not spare me because I appeared
too lukewarm. It once came even to swords. With immoderate heat prevailing on
both sides, we chose a middle way: the holiday would be kept in the morning
with shops closed, and after lunch people would return to ordinary work. This
was established nine years ago, yet even this did not quiet the conflict, for
the variation of closed and open shops displayed an ugly dissension. Since no
end or means could be found, last year I went to the council and asked the
senate to devise by its prudence a way to preserve better agreement among the
people. I did not specifically mention abrogation.* Thus nothing but zeal for
peace and concord moved this excellent servant of God to remain quiet, scarcely
daring to recommend abolition of all those days. But does he condemn the
abolition or urge renewed observance? By no means. He ends: *You see in the
whole historical context that nothing new was introduced, but a church custom
which had grown by bad example was recalled, though this happened contrary to
my expectation. Nor was this the only church retaining no holidays except the
seventh day. The same had once been instituted at Strasbourg. Upon arriving I
ought not disturb a well-composed order. Its present recall, as from banishment,
does not seem to me so great a cause of offense.* I have repeated and defended
this at greater length to stop Durell's mouth.

Bucer likewise, on Matthew 12, p. 318, desires every holiday but the Lord's Day
abolished if we wish to follow the apostles' example. These are his words: *I
would desire all other holidays, whether commended in the name of the Virgin
Mother of God, Christ, or the saints, universally abolished. First, it is
evident they rest upon no Word of God: where in the apostolic writings is
anything about Christ's nativity, Epiphany, and the like? I readily believe the
ancients introduced them with zeal for God to drive out pagan holidays, as one
nail drives another. But who will say that zeal was according to knowledge,
when it lacked every word and example of Scripture and followed mere human
reason?* The Lutherans objected to the Reformed that some Zwinglians had
abolished solemn festivities, observing only the Lord's Day. See Alting,
*Elenctic Theology on the Augsburg Confession*, article 15, p. 93.

Similarly Daneau, *Christian Ethics*, book 2, chapter 10: *There is no necessity
for us to observe the same days today. There is no necessity or even benefit in
honoring or observing them. It appears how great a superstition was afterward*

[1674 logical printed p. 324 | IA leaf 342 | PDF p. 342]

*introduced and how great a multitude of feast days, so that it is better for them to be
entirely removed.* Ludovicus Capellus in the Saumur disputations *On Feast
Days*, thesis 18, says: *Perhaps it would have been more prudent, useful, and
advantageous to the Christian church not to admit that distinction and peculiar
observance of days. As the outcome proves, from that initially harmless and
even not useless custom later arose superstition and idolatry, which have
occupied and pervaded the entire papal church for many centuries. The churches
that retained the primitive use of several feast days do not appear sufficiently
safe from this leaven. Other churches, lest they contract contagion from that
custom, preferred to abolish it completely in their reformation—not because
they simply condemned it as evil, but to guard against its danger. For in
religion, when people depart even slightly from God's prescription and wish or
think something lawful for them, every safe thing is to be feared. Experience
proves from what small and imperceptible beginnings the papal church made an
astonishing advance into idolatry and horrible superstition. It therefore seems
better to lack some useful but less necessary good than, by using it, to fall
into grave danger of some great evil.*

The primitive Reformed churches of Belgium, according to Voetius in the place
cited above, p. 1308, abstained from the very name *feast*. They translated the
Palatine Catechism's words in question 103, *especially on feast days*, as
*Insonderheyt op den Sabbathdagh des Heeren*—*especially on the Sabbath of the
Lord*. The Dordrecht constitutions of 1578 say: *It would be desirable to retain
in the churches the liberty granted by God to work six days and to keep only
the Lord's Day as a holiday.*

Rivet, on the fourth commandment, while excusing the Church of England's
observance of apostolic days, adds: *Although we desire this also corrected
among them because of the danger of idolatry, so that, as has been done in other
observances after the example of Hezekiah abolishing the bronze serpent because
of some people's abuse, they also drive out those feasts still polluted by so
many superstitions and idolatries in the papal kingdom.*

The same could be confirmed by many other testimonies, but these suffice.

*Twentieth argument.* The contrary opinion rests on weak arguments. It would be
long to examine every adversarial objection here, but we will consider a few.

*Objection 1.* 1 Corinthians 5:7–8. *I answer:* 1. How will this prove that the
apostle speaks about Easter or any annual feast? He alludes to that annual
feast, but not to the one evening when the lamb was sacrificed; rather, to all
seven days when they ate unleavened bread. Just as after the lamb was killed the
Jews could not have or eat leaven at home for all seven days, so he wished
Christians, made partakers of Christ sacrificed on the cross, to spend their
whole life in purity and sincerity and keep the church free from the leaven of
wickedness and malice and from scandals and scandalous people. The papists
Bellarmine and Gretser themselves are ashamed to cite this for annual Easter.
*The sense is*, says Cornelius à Lapide, *Christ was sacrificed for us as our
Passover, redeemed us, and initiated for us the solemnity of unleavened bread.
Thus after this Passover, after Christ's sacrifice and redemption, we keep this
spiritual feast of unleavened bread, so that we may be unleavened and pure and
therefore eat unleavened bread—that is, enjoy purity of life*

[1674 logical printed p. 325 | IA leaf 343 | PDF p. 343]

*throughout the week of our life, that is, its entire course—for when seven days
have revolved, all our time runs its course, and seven is thus the symbol of
universality—and so we keep the memory of Christ's redemption, our Passover,
with pure and worthy conduct, sacrifices, and praises.* See the common orthodox
commentators on the passage. Finally, even if it spoke about annual Easter,
this objection does not prove that the church can institute feast days at will.

*Objection 2.* Psalm 118:24. *I answer:* This more strongly proves that the
Lord's Day recurring every week is to be observed, since on it Christ rose,
ascended, was crowned and inaugurated, and it was observed by the apostles and
apostolic church. But annual Easter's observance is disputed. Those celebrating
Passover on the fourteenth day of the month say John the apostle handed down
that custom; inhabitants of Rome and other western regions allege Peter and
Paul as authors. Neither group produces anything written about it. Whitaker
therefore concludes in *On Scripture*, question 6, chapter 9, that annual Easter
was adopted by local custom rather than any other cause. Socrates, *History*,
book 5, chapter 22, confirms it: *Neither the Savior nor the apostles commanded
its observance by any law. As many other things were introduced in various
places by custom, so the feast of Passover seems to me to have had private
observance among individual peoples through custom, because no apostle gave a
law about it.* Even granting the adversaries' desired inference, it would not
follow that the church can institute feast days at will.

*Objection 3.* Feast days should be instituted to shake the people from their
usual torpor, impress upon them a sense of God's benefits, and inculcate the
history more effectively. You will say this should be done daily; indeed. Yet a
faithful pastor must consider not only what ought to be done but what can be
done and what the weakness of human nature, especially among the uneducated,
can bear. Moreover, the greatness and dignity of the things done by Christ in
many ways on earth is such that all cannot be delivered and explained at once
in one day or assembly. *I answer:* Though they cannot all be delivered at
once, what prevents their being taught in parts on Lord's Days? Why appoint and
consecrate a particular day, recurring only once a year, for recalling one
benefit? Why command memorial feasts in honor of mysteries? Was this hidden from
Christ and the apostles? Why did they command or institute nothing of the kind?
*How anxious they are*, says Didoclavius, p. 698, *for the memory of Christ's
benefits, who nevertheless oppress Christ's bride the church, proscribe faithful
pastors, and scatter flocks. The memory is to be recalled not only of the
benefits they celebrate on feasts, but of all—and not by memorial feasts.
Whenever the gospel is preached, sacraments administered, Scripture read, prayer
and meditation offered, we recall religious mysteries and benefits acquired
through Christ. The Lord gave us no memorial signs or instruments except the
Word and sacraments.* And on p. 699: *This pretext of*

[1674 logical printed p. 326 | IA leaf 344 | PDF p. 344]

*recalling the memory of redemption's mysteries or Christ's benefits opened the way to
superstition and idolatry. Hence the image and sign of the cross and other
images. God gave signs and types to the Jewish people under the law's pedagogy
to instruct the understanding, musical instruments to move the affections, and
other things to aid memory; but we must retain not a literal memory of the law
through external signs but a spiritual one in the mind*, says Jerome to
Colanthia. *He insists:* It is useful, even necessary, that these benefits be
set before the people once a year. *I answer:* This should be done not once but
more often. Special days should not be prescribed for commemorating this or
that benefit only once a year. And how does this establish consecrating days
according to a particular solar or lunar revolution? Why should these benefits
rather than others be recalled at this precise time? *He insists:* God's
wondrous works raised the days on which they occurred above others. *I answer:*
So Bellarmine says: *Christ by his birth consecrated the place—the manger; by
his death, the cross; by rising, the tomb from which he came. Why did he not
also consecrate the time—the days on which he was born, suffered, or returned
to us after defeating death? Why is the place where the Lord rose called holy
above others, but not the day on which he rose?* But why these rather than the
traitor's lips, the wicked executioners' hands, the scoundrels' spittle, the
places in which he ate, walked, and slept, or the time in which he rose every
day? A work or action does not consecrate place or time; divine institution
does.

*Objection 4.* A private person may choose one hour above another for private
exercises because a mystery occurred then. Therefore the church may prescribe
such time. *I answer:* Although the consequence does not hold, the antecedent
is denied. A private person may designate an hour for private exercises for
order, but may not dedicate, consecrate, or sanctify it, nor esteem one hour
above another because of a mystery.

*Objection 5.* The church has power to determine indifferent things. *I
answer:* How is it proved that feast days are indifferent? *Not everything*,
says Voetius, *On Feasts*, p. 1332, *that is alleged to be indifferent always
is and remains purely indifferent—for example, images, altars, and candles in
churches, where they ought and can be removed; likewise crosses in baptism and
kneeling at the Lord's table, which, while papal idolatry still lives, have in
some places been retained and thrust upon people not without the appearance of
symbolic conformity and scandal.*

*Objection 6.* Feast days appear founded on natural right and light. God works
in time, not equally, but doing greater things on one day than another, thus
marking and distinguishing the times of his works, sanctifying and making them
illustrious, just as places. *I answer:* Then the institution of such days under
the old covenant belonged not to ceremonial law but natural and moral law.
Concerning Hooker's argument the celebrated Voetius could not bring himself to
answer seriously and merely replied with Capellus's words: *The man's reasoning
is indeed subtle and clever, but far-fetched and hardly solid, not to say futile
and empty. It savors more of human wisdom and philosophical cleverness than of
Christian prudence from divine Scripture.*

[1674 logical printed p. 327 | IA leaf 345 | PDF p. 345]

I add only this: If natural right requires us to celebrate every day on which
God did greater and more numerous things, we will celebrate almost every day of
the year; and the words of the fourth commandment, *Six days you shall work*,
will conflict with this natural law.

*Objection 7.* The Jewish feasts contained something moral and natural and
common to other nations: they set apart some time for public worship and solemn
testification of gratitude for benefits received. *I answer:* By this argument
the papists can defend almost all their institutions—not only feast days but
canonical hours and the dedication and consecration of places and utensils. 2.
Then the substance of Jewish feasts remains, with only the circumstance of a
definite time changed. But why did God himself not make this change? Why did the
apostles not intimate it by word or practice? What then becomes of the moral
command, *Six days you shall work*?

*Objection 8.* Gratitude requires public benefits to be publicly acknowledged
and testified by public thanksgiving, which cannot conveniently occur unless
fixed meeting days are prescribed. *I answer:* Then every day must be a
holiday, for who can enumerate all God's benefits, and which should not be
gratefully commemorated? How does the institution of annual days follow? Can
God's benefits be gratefully commemorated and our gratitude testified on days
extraordinarily instituted for that end, as was customary in some places? *He
insists:* Memory of past benefits is more effective if they are recalled at the
same time they were first bestowed, for the return of the same day directs the
mind's attention to consider the benefit. *I answer:* Experience by no means
confirms this; it rather shows that the commemoration finally vanishes into mere
form. On this reasoning almost every day must be a holiday. *Human authority*,
says D. Hoornbeeck in *Practical Theology*, book 9, chapter 21, p. 375, *may as
circumstances require consecrate some time to divine worship, such as a day of
fasting or thanksgiving, for God has granted this. It may prescribe occasional
days, but not fixed, perpetual, and universal days always observed each week.
For God reserved to himself the prescription of worship, its perpetual mode,
and its time in the second and fourth commandments. Nor may the God-given
liberty of working six days be perpetually taken away from everyone. Nor could
so much loss and injury be inflicted upon people's affairs—sometimes poor
people's—without grave injustice, as is now done by the necessary rest of whole
days every week, except by divine command. It should rightly be regarded as
harsh to deprive people by perpetual edict of so much time that labor becomes
unlawful under severe punishment for those who must live by labor and provide
food for themselves and their families with their hands, and who have so much
less property the less they work, unless God commands and wills such rest for
his worship and both the magistrates' laws and the church's admonitions rest
upon that divine will.*
