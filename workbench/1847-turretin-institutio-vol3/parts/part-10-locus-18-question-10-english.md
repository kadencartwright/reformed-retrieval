QUESTION TEN

Where was our church before Luther and Zwingli, and by what means was it
preserved?

I. From what was said in the preceding question about the obscuring of the
church, it is easy to answer the proposed question. For if the church can
sometimes be so obscured and hidden that it is nowhere conspicuous on earth—a
fact our opponents themselves do not deny concerning the times of
Antichrist—why can we not say that our church indeed existed, but was hidden
and obscure? Yet because they repeatedly assail us with this question in order
to prove that our church did not then exist at all, since it cannot be shown
where it was, and therefore that it is novel and newly born, we must see how
such a demand should be answered.

II. Before everything else, however, the following must be observed. 1. By
“our church,” or the Reformed church, we do not mean the body distinguished
from other assemblies by that express title and designation. We mean the body
that was the same as our present church by kinship with and profession of the
same doctrine in the articles of faith and by true ecclesiastical communion.

2. True and catholic doctrine must here be distinguished from discipline,
ceremonies from dogmas, and, within dogmas, the questions and tenets of the
schools or the private opinions of teachers from the necessary articles of
faith to be embraced by the common faith of the churches. Again, among the
things the church prescribes, some are of divine law and absolutely necessary
to faith and salvation; in these the essence of Christianity consists. Others
are of positive law and are indeed useful, yet nothing prevents their being
changed and refashioned with the passage of time.

[Marginalia: The absurdity of the demand is shown.]

III. Before we answer the question, the absurdity and unfairness of the demand
must be noted. First, it presupposes a false hypothesis—the opponents’ “first
falsehood.” Once that is denied and overturned, everything resting upon it
must likewise fall: namely, that the true church ought always to be conspicuous
in splendor in the world. Since the preceding question proved this utterly
false, and since it is evident that there are Elijah-like times in which the
church lies hidden and is withdrawn from human sight, we are asked in vain to
show where our church was. It could lie hidden without splendor and eminence.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 51 | IA leaf 85 | PDF p. 85]

IV. Second, they argue wrongly from ignorance of a thing to its nonexistence,
as though a thing must not exist because it is unknown. But the truth of a
thing must be measured from the thing itself, not from our knowledge or
ignorance. Who doubts that something can exist that is hidden from me and
others, either because it is concealed and secret or because we have not
diligently attended to investigating it? This inference once deceived Elijah.
Because he did not know where the church of his time was or who its teachers
were, he thought that he alone remained, since the others were unknown to him
(1 Kings 19:10; Romans 11:4). But the Lord showed that this reasoning was
faulty when he said that “he had left for himself seven thousand who had not
bowed their knees to Baal.” Therefore, if the church of Israel could lie so
hidden that Elijah did not know it, who will wonder that the same thing
happened in the time of our ancestors?

V. Third, they also reason faultily from ignorance of a place to the
nonexistence of a thing. Although the location of a thing may be unknown to
me, it does not follow that the thing itself does not exist at all. Although I
do not know where treasures are in the sea, one must not therefore deny that
they are there. Thus it is one thing to know that the true church exists and
another to know where it is. I can be persuaded of the former even if I have
nothing certain about the latter.

VI. Fourth, the question is one of history, not of faith, and therefore its
solution is not necessary for salvation. It is enough for a private Christian
to know and be persuaded that the assembly in whose fellowship he lives is a
true church, because in it he has what fully suffices for salvation and
nothing that obstructs it, even though he knows nothing about where the church
was in past times. Although it is a most pleasing spectacle to behold the face
of the whole church in every age, no human being can do so; it does not concern
our faith and salvation; these things can be grasped only by those most skilled
in history and are known by historical faith alone; and no dispute about this
will be instituted on judgment day so that our salvation might be endangered
by ignorance of it.

Imagine a man carried off in childhood to farthest Thule, swept elsewhere by
the violence of a storm, yet acquainted with prayer and true religion though
entirely ignorant of history. Is he therefore less capable of salvation
because he does not know where the church existed before his time? Imagine a
city plundered by pirates, its fathers, mothers, and older people carried into
slavery, and only infants surviving. From books and the traces of their
instruction they retain the same piety. Who would therefore exclude them from
salvation, although they know nothing about this question?

VII. It is no more important for us to know where our church was in the age of
the fathers than where it was during the Decian persecution. Though it then
seemed utterly extinct, it nevertheless revived from the ashes. This must be
held as certain: the church existed and revived. God knows its location; he is
the one who marks out the camp of his church and provides its places and
lodgings. When people are absent, he sends ravens; when bread fails, he feeds
by his word. He knows those who are his, marked with his sacred seal.

This can be said all the more properly because we know with certainty that
such times and evils were going to come upon and prevail in the Christian
church as would stain the Christian name with foul apostasy and all but
extinguish it. For this reason the book of Revelation was written and left to
posterity for the church’s singular consolation, so that amid such darkness
of affairs we might have a sure guiding star.

VIII. Fifth, the unfairness of the demand is also clearly evident in this:
while our opponents deceitfully corrupt the writings of the fathers, attempt
to erase whatever integrity remains, and, as far as lies in them, extinguish
all memory of antiquity, these most wicked people wish to turn against us as
weapons of truth the very things they burned, erased, and expunged because we
cannot produce them. Those who persecuted the godly with sword and flame now
receive them with mockery, just like the

[1847 vol. III printed p. 52 | IA leaf 86 | PDF p. 86]

Jews who struck Christ with rods and said, “Prophesy who struck you.” Thus
they want us to prophesy and render an account of a bygone age.

IX. Sixth, if our opponents want us to answer the proposed question, they in
turn are bound to answer us: Where was their church in the time of Christ and
the apostles, when nothing about transubstantiation and the sacrifice of the
Mass, the invocation of saints and worship of images, purgatory and
indulgences, the pope and his authority, and the other theoretical and
practical errors had yet gained strength among believers? Let them show us
where that magnificent apparatus of temples and ceremonies then was which has
corrupted the whole of Christianity and turned divine worship into a stage
play. Where was the papacy during the first six centuries of purer, or at
least less impure, Christianity? Although various errors had already begun to
creep into the church, it is certain that far more and more serious ones
sprang up or gained strength in subsequent centuries: the worship of images,
transubstantiation, communion under one kind, the pope’s infallibility and
monarchy, the use of a foreign language in sacred rites, and so forth. Let
them show where the church was in that tenth century which Baronius calls the
Iron Age, when more than fifty pontiffs succeeding one another were, according
to Genebrard, not so much apostolic as apostate and apostatical.

[Marginalia: The question regards 1. Faith.]

X. Yet lest we seem to evade the question as though it were insoluble for us,
we can answer directly that the question assumes a fourfold respect, or
regards four things: 1. the doctrine and faith of the elect who constitute the
church; 2. their persons and condition; 3. the place where they were; and 4.
the form of government under which they lived and the outward worship they
possessed.

As to the first, if Christian faith is at issue, we say that the substance of
the things to be believed and done for salvation was always in Scripture, the
Apostles’ Creed, the law of God, and the Lord’s Prayer, sealed by the
sacraments. By a singular providence God always willed to preserve this in the
church to sustain the elect, although that doctrine was at times mixed with
various errors. Here the substance of faith must be carefully distinguished
from the accidental corruptions in both doctrine and worship. God knew how to
preserve the elect amid these in the purity necessary for salvation down to
the age of the Reformation.

[Marginalia: 2. Persons.]

XI. Second, if the persons themselves are at issue—that is, the church
properly so called, insofar as it denotes Christ’s mystical body constituted
of the elect alone—we say that it subsisted in all the elect who lived from
the time of the apostles, who in every age believed in Christ according to
the truth of the publicly preached gospel, and who separated the substance of
saving doctrine in the public ministry from the errors that arose from time
to time. We are no more bound to show these people to our opponents than
Elijah was bound to show the seven thousand who had not bowed the knee to
Baal, although they truly existed in Israel.

XII. The condition of this church was miserable and afflicted. It was obscure
and deprived of splendor, foreshadowed by the woman fleeing into the
wilderness (Revelation 12). It was small in number and reduced to a great
fewness, signified by the two witnesses (Revelation 11:3), because very few
stood against the multitude of those in error. It was also partly corrupt,
sharing something of the errors of the papacy, although it always retained the
foundation of salvation and therefore withdrew from the papacy—if not
positively, then negatively; if not in place, then in faith and religion—by
disapproving its errors and opposing them as far as it was permitted.

[Marginalia: 3. Place.]

XIII. Third, with respect to place, the question can be understood in two
ways. It can be understood definitely, of a certain and constant seat of the
church, such as Rome, and a continued succession of bishops or pastors there,
as the papists would wish. But this is wrong. 1. The church of the New
Testament is not bound to any certain

[1847 vol. III printed p. 53 | IA leaf 87 | PDF p. 87]

place as it was in the Old Testament. It is itinerant and migrates from place
to place, as is evident from the example of the Asian churches treated in
Revelation 2–3 and of very many others that perished. 2. It is not bound to
possess that succession, since it either fails completely together with the
churches themselves or changes into a succession of wolves who tear apart the
flock (Acts 20:29) or impiously lord it over the flock (2 Thessalonians
2:3–4). Or the question can be understood indefinitely of a seat and dwelling
in general, which the church has variously received, now here and now there,
according to God’s dispensation.

XIV. In this sense we say that before Luther and Zwingli the true church
already existed in various assemblies separated from the Roman church. It is
certain that not a few such bodies existed in both East and West. The
self-will and tyranny of the Roman bishop in the corruption of doctrine, the
depraving of worship, and the alteration of the church’s order and government
so displeased them that they not only never wished to submit to him but did
not hesitate openly to oppose and reject that church. It is known that the
Waldenses and Albigenses especially were such people.

As long as they were supported by the authority of princes or relied upon
their own numbers and could hold public assemblies, they rendered the church
visible and conspicuous, as in Bohemia, certain parts of England, the southern
provinces of France, Aquitaine, Occitania, and the Narbonne region up to the
Alps. But afterward princes, incited by the Roman bishop’s fury and arts and
bewitched by blind zeal, persecuted these godly believers with arms to gratify
him. They battered them with various disasters, treated them most cruelly,
drove them from their cities and native homes, and attempted to destroy them
with sword and flame. Then the outward face of that part of the church was so
obscured that it seemed to have failed entirely.

Meanwhile, historical records testify that remnants of it still subsisted,
though scattered and dispersed in various places, especially in the Pyrenean
forest and in the valleys at the foot of the Alps. This fact alone would
suffice to refute our opponents’ unjust accusation that we cannot show where
our church was before Luther, since it is evident that those purer Christians,
who held the same faith as we do in essentials, lived in those various places
and had their own separate, though less visible, assemblies. And since their
secession from the Roman church is nowhere mentioned by their historians, it
is reasonably inferred that they never had communion with it but always lived
separated from it and preserved pure and inviolate the truth handed down to
them through the preaching of the gospel.

XV. We further add that our church existed within the papacy itself, insofar
as God always preserved for himself in the midst of Babylon a remnant
according to the election of grace: true believers who groaned under that
captivity and longed for spiritual deliverance. They are said to have existed
within the papacy not with respect to communion with it, for they disapproved
and rejected its errors and superstitions, but with respect to their residence
and lodging. They lived in the midst of the papal church, not together but
scattered through kingdoms, provinces, cities, and families, in which God
preserved his people in a wonderful manner.

[Marginalia: The church is proved to have existed before the Reformation. 1.
From passages of Scripture.]

XVI. This is proved in more than one way. 1. From various passages of
Scripture, such as 2 Thessalonians 2:4, where the son of perdition and man of
sin is said to sit in the temple of God—that is, to exercise dominion over the
church, which is frequently distinguished by this name. The church must
therefore have existed there, oppressed and captive. In Revelation 7:3 the
angels are forbidden to harm the earth until God’s servants have been sealed
on their foreheads. This intimates that his godly worshipers, who constantly
adhere to him during the apostasy, are marked with God’s seal, denoting their
sure protection by God’s providence, by which they are exempted from impending
calamities.

[1847 vol. III printed p. 54 | IA leaf 88 | PDF p. 88]

This alludes either to the custom of kings, who seal with their seal what they
do not wish to be touched (Daniel 6:17); the custom of shepherds, who mark
their sheep to distinguish them from others; or the typical markings of the
Old Testament, by which exemption from some disaster and distinction from
others involved in it were secured, such as the Israelites from the Egyptians
(Exodus 12:13), Rahab from those who perished (Joshua 2:18), and those
groaning among God’s people from the rest who were devoted to destruction
(Ezekiel 9:4). This is clear evidence that amid the corruption of the world
faithful elect people always remain, whom God protects under his seal and
guardianship.

To the same point belongs what is said in Revelation 11:1–2 about measuring
the altar, the temple, and those worshiping in it, while the outer court is
excluded and the city is given to be trampled by the nations. This intimates
that God always preserves the sanctuary and church and the true believers who
worship him with a pure heart within the city—that is, within the visible
church—which is profaned and polluted by uncircumcised Gentiles, that is, the
impious and unbelieving. The addition in verse 3 about two witnesses
prophesying—that is, a few defenders of divine truth who had to lament the
church’s foul corruption with continual complaints and who would confirm the
purity of evangelical faith and worship against papal errors and
superstitions, by words and works as well as their own blood—and what follows
in chapter 12 about the woman nourished in the wilderness, about angels—that
is, evangelical heralds—in chapter 14, and especially about God’s people
being commanded to leave Babylon in chapter 18:4, could not be said unless
God’s people, that is, the true church, would still exist in the midst of
Babylon.

[Marginalia: 2. From the testimony of history.]

XVII. 2. From the testimony of history, which exhibits many worshipers of God
and witnesses of the truth who from time to time opposed papal errors and bore
witness to the truth. For if there were many people who by words, deeds, and
writings assailed the Roman curia for its errors, idolatries, and tyranny, and
who on that account were treated in most shameful ways and exposed to the
cruelest persecutions, there is no doubt that they constituted the true church
we seek. The catalogues of the Magdeburg historians, Illyricus, Plessis, Foxe,
and others prove that there were innumerable such people. They relate the
names and writings of those who lamented the church’s condition under and
from the papacy as utterly deplorable and who desired and counseled
reformation. Ussher’s *Succession and State of the Churches in the West*,
chapter 6, and Voetius’s *Desperate Cause of the Papacy*, book 3, section 2,
should also be consulted. There Voetius proves the succession of true faith
and of the church under the New Testament through every age down to the
Reformation and supplies various examples of godly people who groaned and
protested against papal errors.

[Marginalia: 3. From similar examples.]

XVIII. 3. From similar examples of the church under both the Old and New
Testaments. Under the Old Testament there was: 1. the church in Egypt after
the death of Joseph and his brothers (Ezekiel 16); 2. the Israelite church
under Elijah, who complained that he alone was left though God had reserved
seven thousand for himself (1 Kings 19:18); 3. the church in Samaria after the
ten tribes were carried into Assyria (2 Chronicles 30; 34); and 4. the Jewish
church in the Babylonian captivity, with which the state of the church under
Antichrist’s tyranny is compared (Revelation 17–18). Under the New Testament,
at the time of Christ’s birth the church was not to be sought among the
scribes, Pharisees, and chief priests, who gloried in the church’s name but
were enemies of God and Christ, but among the few godly and faithful people
waiting for the redemption of Israel. In Paul’s time the Jewish church was
reduced to a few elect people with Paul while the rest remained hardened and
unbelieving (Romans 11:1, 5). What wonder, then, if we similarly say that the
true church existed amid spiritual Babylon and Egypt, stripped of its
insignia and outward ornaments?

[1847 vol. III printed p. 55 | IA leaf 89 | PDF p. 89]

[Marginalia: 4. From the conformity of our church with the ancient church.]

XIX. 4. From the conformity of our church with the primitive apostolic
church. Since no one can deny that Christ and the apostles instituted the
primitive church, neither can it be denied that our church already existed in
that church if ours conforms to it in both faith and worship. That conformity
is easily proved if we proceed to the examination of doctrine, as we have so
often requested. From it, it will be evident that we receive nothing to be
believed or done which Christ and the apostles did not hand down and that
everything we reject has no foundation in their writings but is “beyond what
is written” and beyond what was proclaimed as gospel (Galatians 1:8).

Nor should it be said that the ancient church cannot be called the same as
ours because it did not reject the papacy as we have done. 1. Faith and
religion in themselves and essentially are not negative but positive. The
denial or rejection of error is accidental, and positive faith suffices for
the church’s essential unity. 2. If it did not reject the papacy explicitly
because the papacy had not yet arisen, it rejected it implicitly and
virtually by teaching things incompatible with it. In this way Scripture is
said, by positing truth, to reject errors that had not yet arisen but would
arise only with the passage of time.

[Marginalia: 5. From the confession of the opponents.]

XX. 5. From the confession of the opponents, who cannot deny that before
Luther and Zwingli there were innumerable people who professed our faith and
publicly protested against papal errors. See what Thuanus in books 6 and 27;
Æneas Sylvius, or Pius II, in *History of Bohemia*, chapter 35; Poplinerus in
*History of France*, book 1; Carolus Molinæus in *On the Monarchy of France*,
page 56; and many others relate about the Waldenses, Albigenses, Hussites,
Wycliffites, Lollards, and Picards. They trace these groups’ origin to long
before the Reformation. Eck cannot deny this in *Commonplaces*, chapter 28,
when he says that Luther did nothing other than recall heresies long before
condemned in the Waldenses, Albigenses, Hussites, and others. Cochlaeus in
book 5 acknowledges that the Lutherans received their doctrine from the
Hussites.

Nor should the testimony of Reynerius be omitted. Three hundred years ago he
was an inquisitor against heretics concerning the Vallenses, commonly called
Waldenses, and bears witness to their antiquity and integrity in the book he
wrote about them, edited by Gretser and reprinted in the *Library of the
Fathers*, tome 4, part 2. “Among all the sects,” he says, “that still exist or
have existed, none was more dangerous to the church than the sect of the
Leonists,” or the Poor of Lyon, that is, the Waldenses, against whom the
treatise is expressly inscribed. “This is so for three reasons. First, it is
the most enduring of all, for some say it has lasted from the time of
Sylvester and others from the time of the apostles. Second, it was the most
widespread, for there is almost no land in which this sect has not been
received. Third, although all other sects induce horror in their hearers by
the enormity of their blasphemies against God, this one has a great appearance
of piety, because they live justly before people, believe all things well
concerning God, and observe all the articles contained in the Creed; they
blaspheme only the Roman church and the clergy, etc.”

Claude de Seyssel, archbishop of Turin, relates in the book he wrote against
the Waldenses that “the Waldensian sect took its origin from a certain Leo, a
most religious man, in the time of Constantine the Great, the first Christian
emperor. Detesting Sylvester’s avarice and Constantine’s excessive
liberality, he preferred to follow poverty in simplicity of faith rather than
remain with Sylvester and be corrupted by a rich benefice. All who thought
rightly about the faith adhered to him.” That their religion and faith
conformed to ours is gathered not only from the cited passages of Thuanus and
Æneas Sylvius, in which the chief heads of their faith are related, but also
from their ancient confessions and writings reported by John

[1847 vol. III printed p. 56 | IA leaf 90 | PDF p. 90]

Paul Perrin in his *History of the Waldenses* and, a few years ago, by Jean
Léger, pastor there, in his *History of Those Churches*, chapters 3, 4, and
following, where their antiquity and faith are carefully treated.

[Marginalia: 4. The form of government.]

XXI. Thus it has been shown where our church was before Luther with respect
to place or seat. If the question’s fourth respect is now the form of
government and outward worship that presently obtains among us, since it is
certain that it succeeded the form that had gained strength in the Roman
church—as reformation succeeds corruption and healing disease, reducing
everything to the beginning, that is, to the institution of Christ and the
apostles—we readily concede that it did not exist before the Reformation,
since it was restored through the Reformation itself. But this does not hinder
the church’s perpetual subsistence, just as the healing of a lethal disease
that afflicted a person in old age does not prevent him from being the same
person in substance as he was before. Indeed, it necessarily presupposes that
subsistence, since reformation necessarily demands a preexisting subject to
be reformed.

XXII. Although the church’s visible ministry always subsisted from the time
of the apostles, it cannot be said to have subsisted in purity, but with
various corruptions that so deformed its face that it could scarcely be
recognized. Hence God is said to restore it as if by resurrection (Ezekiel 37;
Revelation 20). For this reason reformations had to be instituted at various
times to purge it of its filth and restore it to the former brightness of
apostolic times.

XXIII. If someone further asks what pastors the church possessed in those
times, the answer is ready. Inwardly, the Holy Spirit always taught his church
according to Christ’s promise (John 16:13). Outwardly, its teachers were the
very men who taught publicly, namely, the teachers of the Roman church.
Although they mixed many serious and deadly errors with the purer doctrine of
the gospel, nevertheless, because they still professed to retain the
foundations of religion and salvation, true believers could separate purer
and sound doctrine from what was deadly and wholesome food from poison, just
as sheep in pastures filled with both poisonous and wholesome plants know how
to select the wholesome and abstain from the harmful. This was especially so
because in those times those errors and false forms of worship had not passed
into laws, had not yet been sanctioned by public authority, and had not been
thrust upon consciences under penalty of anathema, as happened afterward.
People were therefore free to admit or reject them.

XXIV. If someone goes on to ask how they could receive truth from false
teachers and seducers, such as we say the Roman teachers were, we answer that
those teachers can be considered under a twofold relation: either insofar as
they presented to their hearers the gospel and the doctrine of faith contained
in it, or insofar as they mixed in the leaven of their own corruptions and
traditions. In the former sense they can be called true teachers because they
handed down what they had received from Christ. In the latter they are rightly
called seducers, in just the way that the scribes and Pharisees were both true
teachers and seducers. They were true teachers when, sitting in Moses’ chair,
they so handed down his doctrine (Matthew 23:2) that the people were bound to
do what they commanded. But they were seducers when, sitting in their own
chair, they presented their traditions and inventions, from whose leaven
Christ therefore wished his people to beware. In the former sense they were
to be heard, but in the latter they were to be shunned.

Thus in the Christian church, the Roman church’s teachers could be regarded
as true teachers and were to be heard insofar as they presented God’s word
and the doctrine of salvation. But when they added their lie to this truth,
their traditions to God’s word, and their leaven to this spiritual bread,
then they sat in their own chair and were no longer to be heard. Nor were they
heard by

[1847 vol. III printed p. 57 | IA leaf 91 | PDF p. 91]

the faithful, who as rational sheep knew and followed Christ’s voice but
separated the leaven from wholesome food so that, leaving the former aside,
they might feed upon the latter.

[Marginalia: By what means the church was preserved before the Reformation.]

XXV. From this it can now be clearly gathered by what means that church was
preserved, which is the second part of the proposed question. Although knowing
this seems to be of no great necessity—indeed, even if we could not explain
how God preserved his elect under the papacy—the thing itself, established by
so many arguments, ought not to be called into doubt. We should be certainly
persuaded that God, who can perform innumerable things beyond what we are able
to think, possessed a thousand ways of preserving the church.

Yet we can also partly satisfy the inquiry if we say that the church was
preserved and propagated through every age by the same means through which it
was gathered and founded from the beginning: namely, by the efficacy of the
Holy Spirit and the ministry of the word, according to Isaiah’s oracle
(59:21). For the Spirit, the teacher of truth given to the church to remain
with believers forever and to lead them into all truth, was always more or
less effective in true believers for preserving faith and piety through the
word of God. Although veiled in the Latin language and as if locked up, that
word was nevertheless read and understood from time to time by many. Indeed,
various translations of it were not lacking, which were read by not a few,
though not indiscriminately by everyone. Moreover, by God’s providence the
chief heads of religion, contained in the Apostles’ Creed, the Decalogue, the
Lord’s Prayer, and the sacraments, were always preserved there, although
corrupted in various ways. From these people could have what had to be
believed and done for salvation.

XXVI. The ordinary public ministry that obtained in the papacy contributed
not a little to this. Although it was corrupted in many ways by various
deadly errors added to sound doctrine, nevertheless, because it still retained
and presented those chief heads of Christianity, God did not cease to
accomplish his work through these defective instruments. By his grace he
provided that Christ’s rational sheep, endowed with the spirit of discernment,
separated truth from falsehood and solid, wholesome food from the poison mixed
with it, as was said above.

This could happen more easily because the corruptions then prevailing had not
been introduced by way of open opposition to and rejection of the Christian
faith and religion handed down by the apostles, but by way of joining and
mixing falsehood with truth and superstitions and self-chosen worship with the
pure worship of God. Therefore, it was not difficult for believers endowed
with the gift of the Holy Spirit to retain the good while rejecting the evil
and to embrace truth alone while repudiating falsehood. Indeed, because papal
doctrine was composed of articles contradictory in themselves—partly handed
down by God in the word and partly devised by people, such as the sacrifice
of the cross and the sacrifice of the Mass, the worship of God and the
worship of saints and images, the merits of Christ and human merits—the
retention of truth entailed the rejection of error. For example, the precept
that Christ’s most perfect merit and righteousness must be embraced for
justification excluded human merits and satisfactions by that very fact. The
command to worship the one God proscribed the worship of saints and images.
The doctrine of the sacrifice of the cross, the true ransom of souls,
overthrew the fiction of the propitiatory sacrifice of the Mass. Thus the
hearts of the hearers were often holier than the mouths of the priests and
teachers.

XXVII. To this public ministry, exercised in public and solemn assemblies,
was added a private ministry conducted privately. It occurred both in
colleges and monasteries, of which notable examples appear in Luther, Zwingli,
Bucer, Martyr, Oecolampadius, and others who learned religion in cloisters and
monasteries, whether by reading Scripture, the purer fathers, and

other writings of that kind in which rays of truth shone, or by instruction

XXVIII. Besides the ordinary ministry, God also provided for his people by an
extraordinary one. From time to time he raised up faithful witnesses of the
truth to oppose growing errors and restore the doctrine of faith to its
purity. Just as he once raised up prophets among the people to rebuke false
worship and strenuously oppose rampant idolatry in both the kingdom of Judah
and the kingdom of Israel, so by God’s providence vigorous defenders of the
truth arose in various places in Christendom and resolutely resisted emerging
corruptions. This was done by Charlemagne and the fathers of the Council of
Frankfurt in the cause of image worship, and by Bertram the Presbyter under
Charles the Bald and later by Berengar in the cause of Christ’s bodily
presence and transubstantiation.

Such people are signified in Revelation 11:3 by the two witnesses, the two
olive trees, and the two lampstands, since through them God sustained the
light of truth and the sacred anointing, or gifts of the Spirit, in believers’
hearts. In chapter 14 they are represented by an angel flying through heaven
and bringing the eternal gospel to the earth (verse 6), and by the others in
that chapter who proclaim Babylon’s fall (verses 8–9). These symbolically
depict God’s faithful servants and evangelical heralds, who thundered against
Antichrist in sermons and writings and exhorted believers to shake off his
tyrannical yoke and claim evangelical liberty. Such were Waldo in France,
Wycliffe in England, John Hus and Jerome of Prague in Bohemia, Martyr in Italy,
Luther in Germany, and very many others.

XXIX. Finally, to this was added the wonderful dispensation of divine
providence. Although to chastise human impiety God willed to permit his church
over time to degenerate from apostolic purity and fall into deadly errors, he
nevertheless did not will those errors immediately to reach their height and
prevail so widely that the necessity of receiving them was imposed upon
everyone. Rather, he willed to restrain them so that they gained strength
gradually and imperceptibly. They were first introduced by private teachers,
proposed and variously debated in the schools, or received by the people from
mere custom before being sanctioned by public authority and passing into laws
that bound the conscience and against which it was not permitted to think or
even mutter, making the evil utterly incurable. Therefore, since papal errors
had not yet passed into laws or been thrust upon consciences under penalty of
anathema, as afterward occurred through the Council of Trent, it can readily
be understood how believers could keep themselves free from them.

XXX. It was no more difficult for the godly to be preserved under the papacy
than for the seven thousand in Elijah’s time, the faithful who lived in the
corrupt Jewish church at the time of Christ’s advent, and the orthodox under
the corrupt ministry of the Arians. Just as negative separation could suffice
for those people through their rejection of errors and abstinence from the
prevailing superstition and idolatry, so it was sufficient for our people to
separate negatively from the papacy until a way and opportunity were given
them to separate positively.

XXXI. Although the church that existed within the papacy before the
Reformation did not have among its articles of faith justification through
faith alone, the rejection of all sensible sacrifices except Christ’s
sacrifice, and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 59 | IA leaf 93 | PDF p. 93]

the repudiation of image worship, the invocation of saints, and the other
articles disputed between us, it does not follow that believers lacked in the
received doctrine of that time the nourishment necessary for salvation. Such
articles are not positive and affirmative, containing the things to be
believed and done in which the essence of faith and religion consists. They
are negative and exclude errors to be rejected, and thus do not pertain to
building faith. Just as no one would count among kinds of nourishment the
care of avoiding poisons that could bring death, so positive articles properly
work salvation, while negative ones only remove things that can harm
salvation.

XXXII. Although it is impossible to work out one’s salvation under a Socinian
ministry because it formally and directly destroys the foundation of
Christianity by denying the mystery of the Trinity, Christ’s deity, and the
truth of his satisfaction, it must not likewise be said to have been
impossible to live under a papal ministry, though corrupted in various ways.
It did not formally destroy the foundation by denying and opposing it, but
added to it other things incompatible with it. Serious and deadly sin occurs
in both ministries, but far more dangerously in the Socinian, because it sins
by subtracting fundamental truths. The other sins by adding and mixing deadly
errors with the foundation. Who does not see that it is far easier to reject
what is excessive by separating truth from falsehood than to supply not only
what is lacking but also what a ministry rejects and opposes?

XXXIII. It is rightly said in different respects that the church existed in
the papacy before the Reformation and that the papacy existed in the church.
The church was in the papacy with respect to its outward form, insofar as it
lay hidden beneath that deformed and corrupt surface. The papacy was in the
church because it adhered to it as an accidental growth that corrupted its
subject. Thus the church that is in the papacy must be carefully distinguished
from the papacy itself. The papacy was indeed in the church but was not the
church itself; it was only an ulcer and gangrene consuming it. The godly who
lived under the papacy remained in that assembly with respect to location and
seat but not with respect to conviction and faith. They belonged to the church
as Christian, but not as papal and antichristian. Those who separated
separated only from the papacy, not from the church.

XXXIV. Although the outward face of the church with respect to polity,
outward rites, and form of government was different in the church preceding
the Reformation from the Reformed church as it exists today, it does not
follow that the former church differed from ours in essentials. Those
circumstantial and accidental things neither establish nor destroy the
church’s essential truth and unity; nor does outward communion or diversity
in them make the church identical or diverse.

XXXV. Things pertaining to the church’s essence must not be confused with
those concerning its state and outward form. The former is immutable and
always endures the same; the latter can be changed in various ways. The
church’s essence consists in its being one body composed of many believers
united together in communion and worship of the true God under the one Christ,
their mediator and head. If this is posited, the church is posited, and if it
is removed, the church is removed. Without it the church cannot exist, and
with it the church cannot fail to exist.

The church’s state, according to which it can be changed, consists in all the
things depending upon the differing arrangements of times, places, and
persons. For example, possessing Christ’s bodily presence, having apostles
and evangelists as its pastors, and enjoying the gift of miracles constituted
the state of the primitive Christian church, but that state was changed in
subsequent ages. Having distinguished pastors eminent in zeal, knowledge, and
piety; being tranquil and conspicuous, without persecution, schism, or error:
this is a state in which the church existed and can exist,

[1847 vol. III printed p. 60 | IA leaf 94 | PDF p. 94]

but not always, everywhere, or with respect to all the persons of whom it is
composed. It exists so only sometimes, somewhere, and with respect to certain
persons. Thus multitude, outward splendor, peace and freedom of profession,
the visibility of assemblies, purity of ministry, and the form of government
and discipline pertain not to the church’s essence but to its state. The
church can be deprived of them wholly or in part and yet not fail entirely.
Therefore, although our church could be changed in various ways with respect
to these things, it nevertheless always remained the same in essence. Nor can
novelty in this respect be charged against it unless it is proved—which will
never happen—that our fellowship and faith differ essentially from the one
Christ and the apostles founded from the beginning and which has endured to
the present time, and that it lacks something necessary to constitute the
church.

XXXVI. Although before the Reformation a corrupt ministry without open
separation could suffice the godly for salvation, it could not therefore
suffice for us, because the times and circumstances are different. Those were
still times of captivity, in which God had not yet opened the way to
separation, and although the errors were serious and dangerous, they had not
yet reached their height or passed into laws so as to have to be received by
an inescapable necessity and under penalty of anathema. But once the evil
became utterly incurable, intolerable errors were sanctioned under penalty of
anathema, and God raised the banner of the gospel everywhere to shake off
Antichrist’s yoke and call believers to liberty, positive separation became
necessary. Provision had to be made for the consciences of present and future
believers, and the church had to be reformed from its filth so that its public
faith and worship, rites, and sacred things might be purged of their former
corruptions.
