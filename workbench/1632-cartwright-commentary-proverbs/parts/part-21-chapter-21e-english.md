Verse 13. He who stops his ear at the cry of the poor will himself also cry out, and will not be answered.

In אטם (*obthurans*, stopping) there is a metonymy of the subject for ἀπαθείᾳ, insensibility toward another’s misery, which follows upon stopping the ears. And in זעקת (*clamore*, cry) there is a metaphor by which an earnest and serious request is adorned with vocal exertion.

The purpose is, by deterring from mercilessness, to exhort to kindness and συμπαθεῖν, sympathy with another’s evil.

The form of speech which the Holy Spirit uses to signify inhumanity does not lack emphasis. For since he designates those devoid of compassion by the stopping of the ears, he teaches how like a monster it is not to be moved by the perception of another’s evil. For it is just as if, while the poor and afflicted man was crying out, we inserted our fingers into our ears, lest the afflicted man’s complaint reach our ears. Hence it is noted as a kind of portent that the Jews held their ears shut, with hands placed against their heads, lest they hear Stephen’s saving doctrine, Acts 7.57. And certainly it would be preferable for us to have been born deaf than, having received from God the faculty of hearing, not to use its aid and assistance for compassion and the other duties of charity. Nor can what is said by Christ the Savior be ineptly or inconveniently taken as bearing upon this: he who has ears to hear, let him hear. For mercilessness is wholly inborn in us, and to shake it off we need not only the sense of the eyes but also that of the ears; and we need not only that they address us, but that they importunately batter us with their tears and cries.

From this inhumanity, however,

[1632 witness | PDF p. 468 | printed columns 920-921]

one is deterred by an accompanying disadvantage which arises from it—namely, that the same measure will be measured to them which they have measured to others.

First, when they themselves are pressed by calamity and cry out to God, they will not be heard. For there is no one, even among the most needy and beggars, who needs your aid as greatly as you need God’s. The things asked of you are pennies; those which you seek from God are talents. See, therefore, how improvidently and foolishly you act when, by denying your aid to a poor brother, you obstruct and shut off your way to obtaining God’s help. James 2.13. You play the deaf man when the poor man fills your ears with cries—for though understanding, you act as if you did not understand—and God in turn will turn his back upon you when you cry out to him. This threat need not be feared in repelling those who contract poverty through idleness and inertia.

Here it should be noted in passing how most people deceive themselves: they determine in their minds that the wicked, who have consumed the whole course of their lives in evil deeds, are for that reason saved along with them, because, when death loomed and calamity pressed them, they called upon God. See Prov. 1.27, 28, 29.

Next, by God’s just judgment it comes about that those who neglected the poor and oppressed themselves become poor or are pressed by anguish and dangers. But God, who according to his will rules, bends, bends back, softens, and hardens human hearts, will in his justice bring it about that those who did not show themselves merciful to the afflicted obtain no mercy from others when they themselves are pressed. Thus those who forgive others’ offenses are fortified by God’s promises not only of obtaining forgiveness of sins from God, but further of obtaining it from people themselves, if in any matter they have alienated those people’s minds from themselves, Matt. 6; Luke 6.37, 38.

Verse 14. A gift in secret beats back anger, and a present in the bosom beats back vehement wrath.

In יכפה (*retundit*, beats back) there appears to be a metaphor transferred from corporeal things, which are bent back and turned back, to incorporeal things. In עזה (*vehementem*, vehement) there is likewise a metaphor of the same kind. There is also a metaphor in בחק (*in sinu*, in the bosom) for that which is in secret.

The purpose is to show how great is the power of a gift, whether in its lawful or unlawful use, for obtaining that which we desire.

The seed of every desire is implanted in us by nature, but some desires bear fruit in us more powerfully, or more weakly, than others. Among them, the desire for money and riches holds first place. For the greatest part of humanity is thoroughly imbued with it and is swept sideways by it, because they use it as a drudge and Minister for pleasures, honors, drunkenness, and so forth. Dan. 3.19.

There is great power in the disturbance of anger, by which not only the body’s temperament but also the soul’s repose and tranquility are so disordered that it is thought, not without cause, not only to deprive a person of memory and speech, but also to be a kind of fury and madness, especially if someone grows exceedingly heated. For those driven headlong by anger rush upon those by whom they are provoked, just as a wild beast assails people with blind violence.

But from this comparison with the lesser, the Prophet magnifies the desire for money, since anger, which otherwise appears irreconcilable, is broken down and appeased by a gift. See the example of Esau, whom Jacob, when he was raging in anger, appeased by a gift; likewise that of David, 1 Sam. 25; 2 Kings 18.14, 15.

Hence it is evident what cause there is why, although the seed of every wickedness in all wicked men has been by no means subdued by the Spirit of God or

[1632 witness | PDF p. 469 | printed columns 922-923]

weakened in its own strength, it does not bear fruit and does not spew out and belch forth its venom to overturn the Commonwealth through the confusion and disturbance of all things. First, certain vices are contrary to one another, so that one who is devoted to one cannot practice the other. Next—as is evident from this Proverb—among those which do not thus destroy one another by their greatest mutual conflict, one remains planted higher than another in the seat of the soul and clings there, so that what has less strength in the wicked gives place to the weaker affection; just as, when two most savage tyrants rage against the Commonwealth, it is freed by the victory of one of them who is more powerful and better furnished with strength over the weaker.

When he says “a present in secret” or “given in the bosom,” he appears to indicate that this Proverb is to be understood of those gifts by which justice is perverted, as by bribes. For honorary gifts, New Year’s gifts, and friends’ congratulatory gifts delight rather in the light and in everyone’s eyes, since everyone desires that his being loved, courted, honored, and esteemed among others should become known to all. But those who pronounce judgment and have been enticed by a gift to pervert it diligently take care that others do not learn that they have justice for sale. Hence, if someone were to approach them openly and with others privy to it by means of corrupting gifts, they would immediately feign the greatest hatred against them on that account. This is also confirmed by the word שחד, which is very rarely taken in a good sense. By this Proverb, therefore, we are taught that the anger of judges kindled against crimes committed is often quenched by an accepted gift, just as fire is quenched by water poured upon it.

Only that gift which is bestowed upon the poor ought to be given secretly, away from others, because by this giving the poor person’s modesty and shame are chiefly cared for. But this proverb does not appear to be understood of such gifts, because it rarely happens that rich people, fearing the anger of the poor, endeavor to appease them with gifts—unless perhaps we wish to refer this anger to God, who by beneficence toward the afflicted turns his anger away from sinners. In this sense it has a great connection with the preceding Proverb.
