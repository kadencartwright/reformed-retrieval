## QUESTION V

### Why in Matthew 1:8 are three kings—Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah—omitted from Christ’s genealogy between Joram and Uzziah?

There are no chronological questions in the New Testament more difficult than those concerning Christ’s genealogy, which both now and formerly have been raised in great number by many people. We treated some of them above as they presented themselves to us in the chronology of the Old Testament: first, when it is asked from Matthew 1:5 how Boaz could have been Salmon’s son when more than three hundred years elapsed from Salmon’s birth to Boaz’s death. Second, when a second Cainan is inserted between Arphaxad and Shelah at Luke 3:36, why does he appear nowhere in the Old Testament? Third, why at Matthew 1:9 is Ahaz said to have begotten Hezekiah, when Ahaz does not appear to have been more than eleven years older than his son Hezekiah? Fourth, at Matthew 1:11, why is Josiah said to have begotten Jeconiah and his brothers when Jeconiah was born not from Josiah but from Jehoiakim? Fifth, at Matthew 1:12 Jeconiah is said to have begotten Shealtiel, and yet the prophet Jeremiah makes Jeconiah barren and childless. We dealt with all these in the previous book: the first in chapter 6, question 4; the second in chapter 4, question 1; the third there in question 19; the fourth there in question 28; and the fifth there in question 29. Several more still necessarily remain to be discussed.

What Paul says in 1 Timothy 1:4 and Titus 3:9 must not be understood of Christ’s genealogy as described by the evangelists. That genealogy is part of God-breathed Scripture, which cannot be vain and useless or itself produce foolish and fabulous questions. Rather, it necessarily serves true edification against the enemies of the Gospel, demonstrating visibly Christ’s true humanity and, in accordance with the promises, the Messiah’s most manifest descent from Abraham, Judah, and David. In Paul’s days there were indeed endless disputes among the Jews about the genealogies of many people. The apostle strictly forbade these, knowing very well that after the Messiah’s birth, and with the perpetual exile of the Jews from the land of Canaan imminent, those genealogies would no longer serve any use or good. Nevertheless, knowledge of Christ’s line and ancestry was absolutely necessary to confirm the faith of the elect concerning both Christ’s person and his offices. Modest and sober questions about it are not only useful and edifying but absolutely necessary against the wicked dishonesty of men.

The Manichaeans deleted that entire account of Christ’s genealogy in Matthew and Luke because it irrefutably proved the Lord’s human nature against their heresy.

Porphyry, Julian, and other pagans and apostates—the fiercest enemies of the Christian religion—attacked all Scripture, especially by means of the contradictions they boasted that they had observed in Christ’s genealogy. To blunt the slanders of these witless men, it is absolutely necessary to discuss the questions and doubts perversely raised against this sacred genealogy.

The Manichaeans objected that both Isaiah and Paul declare Christ’s genealogy impossible to explain, because Isaiah 53:8 says, “Who will recount his generation?” and at Hebrews 7:3 Christ is described as “without genealogy.” The easy answer is that these statements must be understood of Christ’s divine generation, not his human one. Although Christ’s eternal generation according to his divine nature is ineffable, his human line and descent from David, indeed from Abraham and from Adam, was not impossible to recount. Other answers are also given to this objection, but for brevity’s sake we rest content with what has been said.

With these preliminaries, we approach the difficulty proposed in the title: how is it that three kings are omitted from the line in Matthew 1:8 and a leap is made from Joram to Uzziah as though from father to son? There are two difficulties here. The first is how Joram, a great-great-grandfather, begot Uzziah, his great-great-grandson. The other is why those three are cast out of Christ’s line. Spanhemius professes that the former is easy to solve. Indeed, the common answer of both the fathers and more recent theologians from every party is sufficiently true: remote descendants are rightly called sons and daughters of their great ancestors and forebears from the remotest antiquity. In John the Jews call themselves children of Abraham, and Christ calls the infirm woman a daughter of Abraham. In Luke 1 Elizabeth is said to be one of Aaron’s daughters, and all mortals are often called children of Adam. I would only like it observed here that Spanhemius appears voluntarily to concede, contrary to his statements elsewhere, that those who did not immediately beget someone and were not immediate fathers are rightly called fathers and said to beget.

—sius, Grotius, Hammond, and others—unless perhaps this is the prudence of those learned men, a practice followed by altogether too many commentators: to raise none of the matters that readers especially desire to see discussed.

Second, some declare this question so arduous that they affirm no satisfactory answer can be given to it. This is the view of Paraeus, Faber Stapulensis, and almost Spanhemius himself. It can be debated under which of these two opinions Chrysostom ought to be placed. At the beginning of Homily 4 on Matthew 1 he expressly raises the difficulty before us but refuses to solve it, and one might reasonably doubt whether this was because of its ease or its difficulty. The reason he gives his audience for not resolving that difficulty is, “lest you grow listless.” The word *anapiptein* has many meanings: it signifies falling, failing in spirit, or losing heart, but more often growing listless, indolent, and negligent. If one takes it in the former sense, Chrysostom refrains from solving the difficulty because of its difficulty, lest he trouble the minds of his hearers by raising matters harder than their understanding could grasp. But if, with the author’s old Latin translator and also the more recent Jesuit Fronton du Duc, one takes it in the second sense, the opinion will be the opposite: he refrains from solving it because of its ease, lest by his labor he furnish his hearers too much leisure. He therefore leaves the difficulty unresolved so that they might solve it by their own effort without a teacher.

The third opinion is held by those who do not think the difficulty so easy that it should be passed over and wrapped in silence, nor so difficult that they pronounce it hopeless and insoluble. Following a middle way, they propose such suitable solutions as they can. Some of these posit an error by copyists who, in Matthew’s manuscript, easily let their eyes pass from Ochoziah to the similar name Uzziah and passed over the two names between them.

This solution of the Jesuit Sánchez and others before him is commonly rejected by everyone. Among other objections, whatever omission of the intermediate names exists here must necessarily be ascribed not to the copyists but to Matthew himself, for without this omission the number of fourteen-generation groups manifestly established by the apostle would not hold.

The second answer of others, then, is that the apostle merely ran cursorily and less carefully through this historical material and neglected precision as a matter of little importance. Spanhemius rightly rejects and censures as dangerous this answer of our very learned Bucer, though he does not name its author. Far be every error and every negligence, in a matter of even the slightest importance, from every God-inspired writer.

The third answer of others is that the apostle deliberately omitted the three kings for mystical reasons. Tostado of Ávila and the Jesuit Salmerón reign supreme in these mysteries. Subtly searching out the reason why three groups of fourteen were established, the former holds that the apostle did this so that in the number three he might designate the most holy Trinity; in the number four, the four Gospels; and in the number ten, the Ten Commandments of the law. The latter adds to these inventions and holds that the apostle excluded those three kings from Christ’s ancestry so that they might be types of all apostates, who deny the doctrine of the Trinity and cast away the three theological virtues—faith, hope, and charity—and so are excluded from the catalogue of the faithful and the descendants of father Abraham. We would deservedly laugh with Spanhemius at these expositors, except that although these very learned men rave in those invented mysteries, in the same passage they nevertheless furnish solidly and quite fully the truest reasons for the omission before us, which we shall now set forth in the next opinion.

In the fourth and last place, Jerome, Hilary, the incomplete work on Matthew wrongly ascribed to Chrysostom, and after these fathers the *Glossa ordinaria*, Nicholas of Lyra, Tostado, Cajetan, Calvin, Chemnitz, Junius, Gomarus, Cartwright, Diodati, the English annotators, Wall, Jansenius, Maldonatus, Barradas, Cornelius a Lapide, Lucas of Bruges, and a further crowd of both Protestants and papists, in their commentaries on the passage, teach—and we agree with them—that Matthew passed over these three kings because he had determined to arrange Christ’s ancestors into three groups of fourteen. The second group would have been broken by adding those three and would have become a group of seventeen.

The next question will indicate why the apostle chose those numbers. But assuming that the apostle chose three groups of fourteen, it was absolutely necessary to exclude three of Christ’s true ancestors, so that the second group would not grow too large, and to remove them from that catalogue, even though their memory was preserved faithfully enough in other Scriptures.

Why, however, were the three named men—Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah—deleted rather than others? The cause was not some particular sin of their own, for others equally sinful are retained in the series: Manasseh, Amon, Ahaz, and others. Rather, it was God’s particular curse upon Ahab’s descendants. Athaliah, Joram’s wife and Jehoshaphat’s daughter-in-law, was the daughter of Ahab and Jezebel. We need not raise with Maldonatus and Salmerón a supposedly difficult question as to why Athaliah is sometimes called Ahab’s daughter and sometimes Omri’s. These things are easily reconciled. Omri was Ahab’s father and the root and origin of his family. Consequently, his descendants and posterity, of either sex and however distant, are rightly called his sons and daughters according to a sufficiently common Scriptural expression.

Athaliah’s son was Ahaziah, her grandson Joash, and her great-grandson Amaziah. God’s blessing extends to—

—the thousandth generation, but his curse only to the third or fourth. Through Elijah’s mouth the Lord pronounced a great curse upon the utterly wicked Ahab. Jehu executed it at God’s command by destroying all Ahab’s male offspring; for this service he obtained from God the throne of Israel for his descendants to the fourth generation. God willed to mark with this disgrace those kings of Judah who remained from Ahab’s daughter, so that they would not stand in Christ’s line to the fourth generation. Joram himself was not excluded, because although his affinity with Ahab was a guilt attaching to him and to his father Jehoshaphat, he himself was not born from Ahab’s accursed blood. Nor were the great-great-grandson Uzziah and his descendants excluded, although they descended from Ahab, because God’s curse is not accustomed to proceed beyond the third or fourth generation.

Here both Protestant and papist scholars commonly lash the utterly abandoned audacity of that most wicked impostor Annius of Viterbo. Under the guise of Philo the Jew he dared to invent this along with his other lies: that Solomon’s entire lineage, including Jehoshaphat’s, was extinguished in Ahaziah, and that King Joram was born from Nathan, Solomon’s maternal half-brother through their mother Bathsheba. It is grievous that the excellent men Bucer and Gualter followed the frauds of this charlatan here, just as elsewhere very many great men, both from our side and among the papists, embraced his fables before the fraud was detected. I also see that John Calvin supports Bucer and Gualter’s aforesaid opinion in his commentary on the harmony. Yet the Jesuit Maldonatus ought not on that account to have inveighed against him so bitterly. Although Calvin offers some arguments for that opinion, he expressly declares that he holds it only as probable and leaves it undecided. Whatever he thought about it he received from Annius of Viterbo, a papist monk whom Joannes Lucidus and many other papists followed before our writers did. Nor were Annius’s fables attacked and worn away sooner or more sharply by any Jesuit than by Calvin’s followers. In any event Maldonatus ought to have been a kinder critic of others here, since he himself stumbles quite grossly over this same passage of Matthew. Having based the groups of fourteen, after others, upon the emptiest mysteries of numbers, and wishing to show himself more learned in Platonic and Pythagorean philosophy than his colleagues, he also drew this account of that enumeration from his own store. “The fourteenth day,” he says, “is critical in diseases, and therefore in commonwealths the fourteenth generation is critical, after which commonwealths usually change for the better.” Is that so? Is the fourteenth day more critical for physicians than the fifth, seventh, ninth, or various other days? And do crises in diseases signify a change for the better rather than for the worse? Whatever may be true of diseases, do numbers have any influence upon changes in commonwealths? I had thought the Roman Church prohibited the public use of Jean Bodin’s writings on, among other things, this pretext: that in accordance with Pythagorean superstition they taught that the condition of commonwealths depended too much upon the various combinations of numbers in years. Yet whatever force and mystery anyone supposes to be present in the numbers of days or years, Maldonatus is certainly the first and only man, I think, who dreamed of any critical force in the number of generations, in which the years and days are entirely various and uncertain. These Jesuit vanities could have been passed over as ridiculous had he not added to his inventions something else manifestly false: namely, that a commonwealth’s fourteenth generation always changes it for the better by its crisis. But what reasons or examples does he finally bring forward to confirm this paradoxical assertion? His falsehood in the present matter is certainly transparent. It is true that David’s kingdom after the first group of fourteen changed the Hebrews’ condition for the better. But after the second, did the rule of Jeconiah or Shealtiel improve the condition of the Jewish people? And although after the third group of fourteen the Church was elevated by the Messiah’s coming into a far happier condition than before, the Jewish commonwealth after that fourteenth generation was nevertheless utterly ruined and has not been changed for the better by any later group of fourteen.

Enough about the Jesuit’s censure of Calvin. Calvin’s same passage is treated not much more gently—indeed far too bitterly—by the most learned men and his closest friends Gomarus and Spanhemius. Gomarus censures Calvin sharply enough for a mistake that the lynx-eyed Jesuits did not observe: namely, that he places Solomon in Christ’s genealogy; and in this censure he has Spanhemius as a follower. Calvin certainly says repeatedly and most expressly that Christ is Solomon’s son only legally, as successor to the kings of Judah, not naturally; for Calvin teaches that Matthew’s series is Joseph’s legal line, while Luke’s is Mary’s natural one. Yet it seems astonishing to me that Solomon and all the kings who follow him should be excluded entirely from Christ’s ancestry. For what purpose was Matthew’s great labor if two entire groups of fourteen, and no fewer than twenty-eight persons whom he placed distinctly and by name in Christ’s genealogy, must be excluded from it entirely, so that Christ descended from them in no way whatsoever, either legally or naturally? Granted that Matthew’s line is Joseph’s natural line and Luke’s is Mary’s natural line, does it therefore follow that none of all the twenty-eight whom Matthew counts in Christ’s genealogy—and in Joseph’s genealogy only on account of Christ—is in Christ’s ancestry even legally? Are the lines of Joseph and Mary so distant from one another—
