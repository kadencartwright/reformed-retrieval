SECTION FOUR.

ARMINIUS. The instigator was Satan, whose aim was to drive the human being through transgression
into death—not only of the body but also of the soul—and whom the human being could not resist
except through supernatural grace. The manner of temptation was ineluctable for a human being
destitute of supernatural grace.

JUNIUS. The rationale of the third and fourth proofs is the same. The devil could not be resisted
without supernatural grace; therefore the law could not be performed without supernatural grace,
etc. First, though I grant both points concerning the common law, as we distinguished above, yet
concerning that singular law they can deservedly be denied, since it commanded a natural matter
properly and simply placed within the power of nature. For it is as natural not to eat what is evil
by nature or by institution as it is natural to eat what is good. It lay within the human being’s
power not to sin, for it lay within the human being’s power not to eat by the faculty of nature.

But a second distinction must be applied by us to both parts of these arguments, even when taken
concerning God’s common law, with respect to what is called supernatural grace. For just as in
nature the work of providence is threefold—to sustain a thing insofar as it exists, to govern it
insofar as it acts, and to protect it insofar as it is hindered, or to preserve it—so also in the
godly the work of grace is threefold: for it is accustomed to sustain them, govern them, and
protect them. It always sustains, because implanted and common grace is permanent; but it governs
and protects or preserves when and how it wills. For the act of varying grace is singular and free,
because it is assisting grace, not implanted grace. With this distinction posited, we judge these
arguments as follows. The human being was never without implanted and habitual supernatural grace;
he was not without assisting grace except in this singular act, in

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 64 | BSB canvas 76]

which God did not govern and did not protect him, because it was a matter of nature which had to
experience its own measure assigned by God’s infinite wisdom. For God had withdrawn, not indeed
from every [printed: *omnis*], but from the administration of this supernatural grace, from the
time when he had said to the human being, “Concerning the fruit,” etc.; and by agreement he had
entrusted the whole matter, and indeed entrusted it solely, to the human being’s nature. Indeed, by
the law of nature itself he had wholly consigned to the human will the capacity to perform or not
perform obedience in everything that is natural.

But the human being could not resist the devil, and the manner of temptation was ineluctable. This
is denied; for if he could by nature refrain from eating the forbidden fruit, in this he could
resist the devil, and the manner of temptation was not ineluctable. He could refrain from eating,
because the matter was natural in the very simplest sense and had also by agreement been placed
within the human being’s power. But he did not do so—certainly because he was unwilling, but
voluntarily consented to those temptations. On this matter we earlier heard Augustine’s judgment as
well, in connection with Proposition VII.

The rationale for performing the common law is different, because the law both operates in nature
and orders nature to supernatural things; this arrangement cannot be maintained, nor indeed can the
devil be resisted, without supernatural grace for observing that law.

ARMINIUS. I shall say nothing about the last two arguments and your response to them, both because
most things were stated in the preceding arguments and because you grant me that the human being
was not without habitual supernatural grace. From this, therefore, I conclude that the human being
could not have been considered in pure natural endowments by God as he predestined, since the human
being did not exist in pure natural endowments.

But you say, “These arguments do nothing against the opinion which considers the human being
commonly.” I reply that these arguments prove that the human being could not have been considered
commonly, for he could not have been considered in pure natural endowments. Yet when considered in
supernatural endowments he was not reprobated or passed over. For by reprobation or preterition the
human being is left in nature, which can do nothing supernatural or divine, as is stated in your
theses.

Further, by God’s providence those supernatural endowments have a commensurate proportion to
supernatural happiness. Moreover, those for whom God wills supernatural happiness by the
affirmative act of his providence are not persons for whom he can, by the negative act of
preterition, fail to will that same happiness—unless he has considered them as not going to reach
it through supernatural means, but as either going to sin or as having in fact transgressed it by
their free will. Otherwise there would be two contrary acts of God concerning one subject
considered in the same respect and at the same time.

RESPONSE. 1. No one can resist the devil except through supernatural grace. On the contrary, I say
that one can. What then? Are all natural human beings continually carried wherever the devil drives
them? Indeed, it is not probable that all Satan’s temptations are always effective even among his
own. This is especially so since Satan acts upon us in no other way than by persuasion. But is
every persuasion of the devil irresistible without supernatural grace? On the contrary, no
persuasion is irresistible. What do you think of the persuasions of the Holy Spirit to faith and
repentance? Do you not maintain that those persuasions are resistible through nature? Or according
to you will the devil’s persuasions be more powerful than the Holy Spirit’s
persuasions?

2. It cannot at all be denied that refraining from eating lay within the human being’s power by the
faculty of nature. From this it follows, I admit, that not sinning lay within the human being’s
power, namely with this sin located in the act of eating. Yet I judge that it must by no means be
granted that not sinning absolutely lay within the human being’s power by the faculty of nature.
For abstaining from the forbidden fruit could be a sin just as much as not abstaining, if only that
abstinence was not holy—that is, done for God’s sake, with God loved above all things. But I do not
understand how love of God even to the contempt of oneself can be performed apart from supernatural
grace; this Augustine also professed long ago.

“In that singular act God did not govern and did not protect Adam, because it was a matter of
nature.” Thus Junius. But was this singular act only a matter of nature? Did Adam not also perform
other works of nature? In all of them, if God had governed and protected Adam, as you profess,
notwithstanding the fact that they were matters of nature, then he certainly did not fail to
protect him in this singular act because it was a matter of nature. At least, in matters belonging
not so much to nature as to another kind, had God continually protected Adam? Was he keeping
Adam’s mind fixed upon God by loving him above all things? If he had done this, how could it have
happened that Adam stretched out his hand to the forbidden fruit? Consequently, God not only failed
to protect Adam in matters belonging to nature, but also in matters belonging to another and
higher kind. And what if not eating the forbidden fruit was an action of a higher rather than a
natural kind? I admit that abstinence simply so called lay within the human being’s power by the
faculty of nature; but did holy abstinence also lie within the faculty of nature apart from
supernatural grace? But you will say, “Adam sinned by not abstaining.” Yet I say that the proximate
cause of this act arose from a prior duty which had already been neglected. If it had been
performed, Adam would both have abstained and have abstained holily. That duty was to keep his mind
fixed upon God with love above all things. If this had been done, no way would have lain open either
to Satan’s temptations or to the enticements of sin; rather, Adam would immediately have recoiled
at the very first mention and solicitation of a base crime. Adam ought to have performed this,
especially when newly created and well aware of the divine kindness poured out upon him beyond
measure. Who doubts that he could have fixed the consideration of this matter in his mind and, from
that consideration, been stirred to a fitting love of the Lord?

“If by nature he could refrain from eating the forbidden fruit, in this he could resist.” These
things are true, I admit, but they are not full enough. For although Adam had abstained, if he had
not abstained holily, he would by no means have resisted the devil absolutely. And one who does not
holily abstain from what is forbidden is very near to not abstaining at all when temptation
supervenes. But if he had been solicitous to confirm such an inclination of his mind toward God,
which is required for holily doing or omitting whatever he does or omits, he would have abstained,
and abstained holily, even against the devil’s temptations.

Junius had said before that the rationale for performing the common law was different because the
law is related and ordered to grace; but here he proposes its form on a far different rationale,
namely because the law orders nature to supernatural things. If this is because it orders nature
to supernatural happiness, then by equal reasoning was not the particular law prescribed to Adam
also incapable of being observed apart from supernatural grace, since it too would have ordered
human nature to supernatural happiness, provided of course that Adam had obeyed it? Nor is the
common law to be reckoned as ordering human nature to supernatural happiness in any other way.

Arminius did not dare open his mouth against this response of Junius. He merely seizes for the
benefit of his own case Junius’s admission that “the human being was not without habitual
supernatural grace.” From this he concludes that the human being could not have been considered in
pure natural endowments by God as he predestined. But we have already exposed above the invalidity
of this reasoning. In the rest he furnishes himself with material upon which to rush. For these
points do not occur in Junius’s response to Arminius’s last arguments. We, moreover, have already
demonstrated how feebly Arminius weakens that opinion concerning the human being considered by God
in pure natural endowments. He adds, “Yet when considered in supernatural endowments he was not
reprobated or passed over.” But Arminius would not deny that the decree to permit the first sin
presupposes the consideration of the human being as created. And we will readily prove that this
decree belongs commonly to predestination and reprobation, and that in no instant of nature does
the decree to permit sin precede either predestination or reprobation, however their acts are
understood. In no instant of nature is the decree to leave the human being in corrupted nature
subsequent to the decree to permit the human being to fall into sin, whence the result is that
natural endowments are corrupted and supernatural endowments are taken away. For if the permission
of sin were intended by God before

[1649 BSB witness | first body | printed p. 65 | BSB canvas 77]

abandonment in sin, then the permission of sin would have the character of an end and abandonment
in sin would assume the character of a means to that end. For priority and posteriority in
intention rest on no rationale other than that of the end and the means to the end. But it cannot
happen that anyone is abandoned in sin to the end that he be permitted to commit the first sin. So
also in execution, the permission of sin would be posterior to abandonment in sin. What does it
mean for supernatural endowments to have a proportion to supernatural happiness, unless that they
suffice for attaining supernatural happiness provided that the first human being, relying upon
those supernatural endowments, keeps himself from sin? But this does nothing to prevent God from
decreeing that a human being, however fully furnished with supernatural gifts, should fall into
sin and thus be bound by guilt deserving eternal damnation. For those to whom God wills
supernatural happiness, he absolutely cannot fail to will the same happiness in the composite
sense; nor can it happen in the composite sense that those whom God wills to reach that happiness
do not reach it. Consequently, neither can it happen that God ever considered them as not going to
reach it, although Arminius invents both of these things by his own license in disputation. For if
this could happen, there would be two directly contradictory acts in God concerning the same
objects, namely willing happiness for Adam and not willing happiness for Adam. But neither is
furnishing the human being with supernatural gifts in creation the same as willing supernatural
happiness for him, which Arminius seems to suppose by an equal license in speaking. And I think
Arminius did not himself understand how these things differ: willing happiness for someone, and
willing happiness for him by an affirmative act, the latter of which Arminius presents with a
certain emphasis. For what? Could God have willed happiness for someone by a negative or
non-affirmative act? Likewise, that mystery of willing happiness for someone by an act of
providence, as if it were distinct from the act of predestination, was born only in Arminius’s brain
and conceived beyond all sobriety.
