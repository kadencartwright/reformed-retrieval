Verse 28. Kindness and truth will guard the King; and by the same kindness he sustains his throne.

In יצרו (*custodient*, they will guard) there is a metaphor from a bodyguard, or from attendants who stand around the king’s person for his defence. כסאו (*solium*, throne) is a metonymy of the subject for the majesty and dignity which shine most brightly upon the throne.

The purpose is to stir Magistrates to the pursuit of kindness and truth.

It is well known that kingship is a thing sought by people of every kind and rank, and is courted by very many suitors like a beautiful and exceedingly rich, or richly dowered, virgin. Hence the saying, εἰ δὲ ἀδικεῖν, τυραννίδος ἕνεκα δεῖ (*if one must do wrong, it must be for a throne*). Such is human pride and rebellion that people not only invade a kingdom by force, or seize and intercept it by fraud, during an interregnum and while it is, as it were, in its maiden and solitary condition; but they also fly upon it when it has been assigned to rightful heirs and joined as though to a lawful husband. Thus it has been said, not without reason, that every person—even a kitchen-gardener and a porter—carries the spirit of a King within himself. And unless God’s most watchful providence and most powerful hand kept watch for the preservation of Kings, Commonwealths would be weakened and would fall through frequent changes arising from treacherous plots against Kings or from violent hands. But the Holy Spirit, in order to counter this evil, appoints the most faithful guardians both of Kings and of kingdoms; and he promises that, if they are employed, both the King and the kingdom will be delivered from threatening dangers. Solomon assigns these two virtues to Kings as Praetorian soldiers and a bodyguard. It is also customary for Kings to keep their attendants and bodyguard at hand. See also Cant. 3, where sixty valiant men are said to keep watch at night around Solomon’s bed. This is also customary for the Kings of our times, who employ their Pensioners, their Armour-Bearers, and their Praetorian soldiers who surround them. I do not condemn this custom; indeed, I consider it very suitable both for the defence of Kings and also for royal dignity and majesty. Yet I affirm that all these are employed in vain unless there is appointed to them that guard which the Holy Spirit attributes to them for their preservation.

The first virtue is kindness. It is the virtue by which a King bestows benefits upon the deserving, especially the good and godly; Psalm 101. David, while living in exile, promises that he will exercise this virtue as soon as he is established upon the throne. At the same time he teaches the mode and manner in which he will put it into execution: namely, by choosing and selecting godly people both as servants and as counsellors who would be members of his more sacred council. And at 2 Sam. 8.9 it is taught how he executed it: first, by munificence toward the Church, to which he dedicated a very great quantity of gold; then by raising well-mannered Officers to honours; afterward by bestowing benefits upon Jonathan’s descendants; and finally by repaying the goodwill of the King of Ammon toward him when he was a fugitive, by sending an embassy to his son.

The second virtue is truth, which is considered in two ways: either in words or in deeds. How disgraceful vanity in words is for a King, who is mighty in command and power, see what was said earlier upon that Proverb, *Divination is in the lips of the King*, and so forth. Truth in deeds, however, is understood to be that by which he brings it about that the things which display themselves as good are good and wholesome in reality as they are in appearance. In particular, his kindness must not be painted and masked, so that by the appearance of liberality and kindness he deceives those toward whom he professes beneficence and kindness. This happens when

[1632 witness | PDF p. 453 | printed columns 880-881]

δῶρα ἄδωρα (*gifts are no gifts*), as we may remember happened in the case of the Scian horse. A memorable example of this is found in Saul, who first appointed David commander in war and afterward chose him as his son-in-law, with this one design: to take away his life. The rationale is no different for those who take from certain people what they lavish upon others.

We see that this counsel of the Spirit is contrary to human counsels. For people place the greatest defence of the Commonwealth in fortifications, citadels, and the despoiling of subjects, so that they may be broken in spirit and courage.

But when he says that the kingdom, or his throne, is established, he thereby teaches that by the same works of kindness and truth the King’s dignity is secured as well as his safety. The use of these virtues is thus very widely applicable both to securing his life and also to securing the King’s dignity, which consists in the splendour of the kingdom.

If these virtues establish King and kingdom, no one doubts that by the same virtues a household and its head are preserved.

But when he says that the throne is supported by kindness, making no mention of truth, he thereby teaches that kindness contains more power for preserving a kingdom than truth does. For nothing so binds people’s spirits—in which the King’s strength consists—as liberality bestowed especially upon those who are the support and ornament of the Commonwealth.

Verse 29. The ornament of young men is their strength; and the glory of old men is grey hair.

The purpose is to stir these two ages to mutual love for one another and to the cultivation of reciprocal duties.

Like people very readily gather with like; and ἀεὶ κολοιὸς παρὰ κολοιόν (*a jackdaw is always beside a jackdaw*), Aristotle, *Rhetoric to Theodorus*. “Birds of a feather will gather togeather.” For similarity of morals and dispositions wonderfully reconciles people’s spirits to one another; but, on the contrary, dissimilarity of dispositions and morals separates and rends people apart. Since, therefore, the pursuits of the young and the old are so widely different that the things which the latter embrace and love the former avoid and hate, it commonly happens that each holds the other in contempt. Just as the old despise the young for their levity, so the young despise the old for their moroseness. Therefore, so that the Holy Spirit might counter this evil and reconcile these dissenting ages to one another—as Gregory the Presbyter says, οἶνον ἐγκεραννύων γάλακτι (*mixing wine with milk*)—he teaches that within this dissimilarity of morals and dispositions lie just reasons both for old people to embrace the young and for young people to revere the old. Indeed, in the very thing which provides each party an occasion for strife and discord, there is a reason why they should embrace one another. For youth has powers and strength which render young people lovable to the old; and old age itself, with the grey hair which attends it, is a gift of God which the young ought to respect and revere. The old formerly were what the young now are; for the young it is uncertain whether they will ever obtain the benefit of old age, although they seek it. If they do obtain it, with the same fairness by which, once old, they would not wish to be treated as worthless by the young, they ought to honour those who are old now. Likewise, the fact that the old, when young, would have borne it badly to be despised by the old ought to remind them not now to do to those who stand where they once stood what they formerly did not wish to be done to themselves.

And just as God by his most wise and watchful providence causes the poor person and the rich to meet in their station for the mutual good of each, Prov. 22.2, so by God’s most judicious and beneficent governance it has been brought about that the young are mingled with the old for the great good of each. For old people deprived of the young people’s strength and powers cannot defend themselves against the injuries of enemies—whence in this passage they have also received the name

[1632 witness | PDF p. 454 | printed columns 892-893; press sequence jumps from 881 to 892]

of chosen men, because they were enrolled for military service—and the young cannot lack the prudence of the old, by which their weak age is regulated both in war and in peace.

From this, therefore, it follows that young people ought to preserve their powers and strength, which are an ornament to them, and for this reason ought to guard themselves from idleness, luxury, foul lusts, and drunkenness, by which their powers are enervated.

Old people, moreover, must take pains that their grey hair be a most splendid crown to them. This happens, as Solomon teaches at Prov. 16.31, when a person arrives at grey hair in the way of righteousness. Therefore let the young take care that they do not hand over to old age a body enfeebled by luxury, lust, and drunkenness. But if their youth has been spent wantonly, they must strive so much the more, once they have become old, to set themselves as an example of every kind of temperance and continence. For if a youth spent in holiness and integrity is required for venerable grey hair, old age itself ought to be free and far removed from crimes, unless we wish to expose it to everyone as ridiculous and contemptible.

Verse 30. The swellings of a wound are the rubbing-away of evil, and blows penetrating the inward parts of the belly.

In חבורות (*tumices*, swellings, and so forth) there is a synecdoche of the species for every bitter chastisement. In מרק (*defricatio*, rubbing) there is a metaphor from vessels which become infected with some tenacious contamination that clings to them so persistently that it cannot be torn away and washed off except by the application of force through rubbing. In חדרי בטן (*penetralia ventris*, the inward parts of the belly) there is a metonymy of the subject for the heart, which has its seat in the inward parts of the belly.

The purpose, from the exceptional fruit of chastisement, is both to exhort those who are in authority to inflict just punishments and to exhort those who are chastised for their offences to patience.

The wise people of this world have taught that no one can be harmed by himself; Aristotle, *Ethics*. But Scripture, on the contrary, seems to confirm the teaching—however paradoxical to people—that no one can be harmed or suffer loss except by himself. For neither can demons harm us, whatever temptation they cast upon us, unless we grant a place to their suggestions; nor can the world’s enticements harm us unless, overcome by our own desires, we permit ourselves to be entangled by them. And, what is more proper to this passage, no loss inflicted upon us or our possessions and no punishment, however bitter, inflicted upon us either justly or unjustly will harm us, if we are rightly disposed toward those things and prepared with the proper spirit. I will say more, yet nothing that is not most true: all those evils which are either threatened against us or inflicted upon us will turn to our good, unless the fault lies with ourselves. In the case of penalties and chastisements this is excellently confirmed by this Proverb.

This chastisement is described first by its genus, namely wounds; then by its form, when blows leave the wound swollen and it is partly blackish and partly bright in colour—in our language we say “black, & blew”; finally, it is declared by its effect, namely by passing through and penetrating the very spirit with pain and sorrow. By this description he denotes the most bitter and grievous affliction.

No chastisement, however light, is pleasing and acceptable to the flesh. But if it stings and burns sharply, then at last we rage and become angry with those who inflict it, or else in dejection of spirit we surrender ourselves to grief. Indeed, sometimes we are driven toward despair merely by the expectation and fear of the punishment itself. Against these evils Solomon supplies us with a remedy when he sets the fruit of chastisements before our eyes: namely, that by chastisements as by

[1632 witness | PDF p. 455 | printed columns 894-895]

medicine, or as by rubbing, evils or sins will be wiped away. See Heb. 12.11.

Then, the sharper and more bitter the chastisement is, the greater the fruit we will receive from it, provided that we are disposed toward it as we ought to be—that is, that we bear it gently and calmly. For this purpose it will help to consider that sin is so thoroughly implanted and rooted in us that unless it is thoroughly rubbed away by chastisements with some force applied, like a pot to which dregs cling tenaciously and tightly, it cannot be cured. For it is not washed clean by mere washing unless this rubbing is added to it. Hence sin is also said to be thoroughly implanted in us, Heb. 12.1. Therefore let us raise our drooping hands and failing knees whenever afflictions drive us toward faintheartedness—especially if they come immediately from God, who always strikes his own with measure and just weight.

From this those who preside over others, either publicly or privately, are also taught not to withhold due and just chastisements, whose fruit is so exceptional. They must nevertheless employ moderation, and punishment must be applied in proportion to the offence and fault. For blows and beatings ought not always to leave welts embedded in the flesh unless the fault was more serious. And God appointed a limit in the law, so that not even public chastisements should exceed forty blows, however great the crime punished by rods might be. In a household and in private, however, no chastisement extended to the loss of even the least bodily part—not even a single tooth—in the chastisement of the vilest slave; Exod. 21.26–27.

Finally, since this fruit does not lie in the nature of chastisement itself, which incites a person rather to sin more gravely, but arises from the saving grace of God, through the operation of his Spirit effectually producing this fruit, both the offender and the one who applies the chastisement should earnestly ask God in prayer that he would accompany this chastisement with his blessing.
