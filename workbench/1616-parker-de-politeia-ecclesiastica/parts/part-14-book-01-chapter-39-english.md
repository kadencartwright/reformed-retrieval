CHAPTER XXXIX.

Fifth Corollary: Brothers Must Not Be Silent in the Cause of Discipline, Even
If the Bishops Should Connive on That Condition.

The fourth point concerns silent ministers. Concerning them it must be known
that the Bishops are accustomed to connive at silent ministers, especially
those stationed in the countryside—for in prominent places the Bishops would
not bear this so easily—whom they see flourishing either through the favor of
the people or through the patronage of powerful lords. They therefore take the
following course. The ministers will pledge on their part to be silent about
the question of discipline, both in sermons and in public addresses before the
people. Once this condition has been accepted, the Bishops in turn will promise
to abstain from the question for as long as no complaint about these matters is
brought before them. Thus, with no one accusing them, under—

[1638 control | printed p. 128 | BSB canvas 138 | bsb10507786_00138]

—this silence some are permitted to preach, though neither conforming nor
subscribing, and to conduct themselves quite peaceably. This indulgence on the
Bishops’ part is like that which Trajan once showed toward the Christians,
which Tertullian censures in this way: “And yet we find that even inquiry into
us has been forbidden. For when Pliny the Younger was governing the province
and was disturbed by the multitude of Christians, he consulted Emperor Trajan,
to whom Trajan replied that this class of men ought indeed not to be sought
out, but when brought forward ought to be punished. O sentence confounded by
necessity! He says they are not to be sought out as though innocent, yet orders
them to be punished as though guilty. He spares and rages, dissembles and
punishes. Why do you entangle yourself with your own judgment? If you condemn,
why do you not investigate? If you do not investigate, why do you not acquit?
A military watch is assigned throughout all the provinces to track down
robbers, ... and the investigation extends even to their associates and
accomplices. The Christian alone may not be sought out, but he may be brought
forward—as though the investigation would do something different from the
accusation? You therefore condemn a man who has been brought forward, whom no
one wished to be sought out, and who, I think, has not now incurred punishment
because he is guilty, but because, though not to be sought out, he has been
found.”

Thus far Tertullian, by which words he clearly demonstrates both that our
innocence is attested by the Bishops and that the Bishops are entangled by
their own judgment. I therefore will not ask the Bishops where Whitgift’s excuse
should be placed—he said he was bound by the Episcopal oath to remove
nonconforming ministers from office. Nor will I assail the consciences of those
who have borne with certain men notwithstanding any oath, although they seem
to carry a crafty fox beneath the breast in sparing some, as the Pharisees once
did, for the people’s favor while raging against others. This one remonstrance
of Tertullian will be enough: Trajan would not have shown more favor to
Christians than to robbers unless he had been conscious that Christians were
not criminal robbers; likewise the Bishops would not now connive at
nonconforming ministers any more than at criminals unless conscience dictated
that those men were by no means criminals. There therefore appears to be a
certain moral awareness in the conscience which reminds the Bishops of these
men’s uprightness, especially since the canon precisely presses the necessity
of subscription.

2. What, then, must be thought of the English ministers who are accustomed to
redeem themselves from the Bishops’ harassment by a promise of silence? Just
as these ministers do not condemn their associates who suffer in the same
cause—whom they judge to have been drawn into the arena by an inward movement
of the Spirit which they say they do not feel in themselves—so I in turn would
not think that they should rashly be condemned. This is especially so because
I see that by God’s singular providence certain Churches have thereby been
provided for which would otherwise be deprived of the benefit of the Word. For
this reason I will not deal more harshly with them; indeed, I will not even say
much. I will only add briefly certain things to those which I once wrote
elsewhere, so that I may seem to have touched on the chief points rather than
to have instituted a full disputation.

First, then, I say that a great and serious examination of conscience is needed
here, so that the innermost recesses of the heart are searched and every hiding
place explored, because that promise of silence, and the silence itself, seems
to present the appearance of the same denied or trifled-with confession. Let
there be no halting between two opinions, 1 Kings 18:21; no secret Nicodemite
confession, John 3:2; no lukewarmness, Revelation 3:16; no shame at Christ’s
words before an adulterous and crooked generation, Mark 8:38; no fear of
losing, Revelation 21:8; and finally, no love of gaining. For this was the tail
of the dragon by which the stars are accustomed to be cast down from heaven to
earth, Revelation 12:4.

Second, after Christ had reproved the English corruptions in England, Laodicea
in type, he added this epiphonema: “He who has ears to hear, let
him hear,” Revelation 3, last verse. From these words it is readily apparent to
anyone that God’s singular grace has shone upon those whose ears he opened, so
that they might be able both to discern and to flee the corruptions of the
English Church. Since this is so, does not gratitude seem to demand that we use
this grace for the honor of God, by whom it was mercifully given? “That is
true,” you answer, “and we do this in the best way. For we keep ourselves pure
so that we do not communicate in the Church’s corruptions, and we are silent
so that by preaching the Gospel of his Son we may glo—

therefore limps. You use this grace to cleanse yourselves thoroughly—well and
good—but while you are silent concerning discipline, your neighbor is not now
built up. Your preaching of the Gospel is also good, but meanwhile the talent
concerning discipline, which was given for winning and instructing others, is
buried beneath the ground, Matthew 25:18.

Third, you acknowledge that reformed discipline is a part of the Gospel. How
wicked it is to withhold part of a debt from God can be seen in Ananias, Acts
5:2; and how ungrateful it is to God that you should preach one part of the

to them all that I have commanded you,” Jeremiah 1:17. “Teaching them to
observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you,” Matthew 28:20. “How I
declared to you the whole counsel of God and withheld nothing,” Acts 20:20.
“Nothing useful to us” is added, you will say. As though discipline were not
useful, or Paul would not have established discipline at Ephesus, 1 Timothy
5:17—as though it were necessary for him to number it among useful things with
these words! “Yet there are degrees between doctrine and discipline,” you say,
“for the former is more useful.” As though for the sake of the more useful
part, the other, less useful part of the Gospel must either be lost or
betrayed! “But for us to be silent is neither to lose nor to betray it.” It is
nevertheless to withhold it, which Paul judges to be outside one’s duty. To
Gregory the Great it also appeared both to lose and to betray it.

The Bishops’ usurpation over ministers is the same as that of John of
Constantinople. The only difference is that his offended against the whole
Church, while this one offends only against part of the Church. However that
may be, the matter concerned discipline, not doctrine. Yet Gregory says: “To
consent to so wicked a title is nothing other than to lose the faith.” “But our
silence is not consent.” In Gregory’s judgment it certainly is. For when
Emperor Maurice ordered him to be quiet—as even the most pious magistrates are
often more than sufficiently disposed to aid Episcopal ambition—Gregory
defends himself on the ground that he could not be silent because he “could
not consent to so wicked a title.” And he incites others to resist, because
“to be quiet and to consent was to lose the faith.” This is clear from another
passage, where he also forbids bearing it calmly. “If we bear this calmly,” he
says, “we corrupt the faith of the universal Church.” Even if you do not bear
Episcopal jurisdiction calmly, you nevertheless bear it more calmly than the
truth will calmly permit. Tertullian rightly says of the truth: “The truth will
blush at nothing except merely being hidden.” Yet a lamp is hidden beneath a
measure, Matthew 5:15, as is plain from the interpreters, who set the measure
to how far ministers may preach and beyond which they may not proceed. The
same judgment must be made about those who observe the measure.

wicked race of arch-synagogue rulers will not cease until it has reduced us all
to the harshest poverty and slavery. This evil has grown through our discord;
through our discord those scoundrels have appeared. As long as they are safe,
Christians will never be permitted to preserve holy peace, rest from arms, and
cultivate piety among themselves.”

And here I shall restrain myself. Consider, brothers, what I have said, and may
God give you understanding in all things. These matters certainly seem to me
to be such that they should not be rashly scorned unless outweighed by stronger
arguments, of which I know none. I do not say this as though I were ignorant of
those which you are accustomed to bring forward, but because I see that they
are of no weight or moment.
