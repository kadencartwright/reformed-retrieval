With God.

Eternal God, in Jesus Christ the most loving Father alike of mercies and of
lights, grant repentance, we pray, to miserable sinners, so that you may grant
pardon to the penitent. Shine also upon us poor wretches crawling here in
darkness and, when the veil still laid upon our hearts has been taken away,
uncover the wonders of the Law and the mighty works of the Gospel, so that we,
who can do nothing without you, may at last under your auspices both understand
what is true and do what is right, and may finally attain those eternal things
laid up for us in heaven, through Jesus Christ your Son, our only Savior. Amen.

What is proposed for me to discuss at present is found in the Gospel according
to St. John, chapter 17, verse 3, in these words:

“And this is eternal life, that they may know you, the only true God, and Jesus
Christ whom you sent.”

I congratulate first of all, Academic Gentlemen, the most prudent moderators
of the business now in hand. Although by assigning this sweetest passage of
Holy Scripture they have sent me here today to work, they have sent me into a
vineyard, where I may not so much undergo labors as drain cups full of the wine
of consolation. Or, if I may speak somewhat more harshly, although they have
condemned me to the mines, it is nevertheless to a gold mine. For this passage,
among the rest—indeed above the rest—is that field in which the hidden treasure
lies, from which the pearl of great price must be dug. Behold, here everything
is gold and gems. Beside these, to speak with the prophet, “the gold and the
pleasant fine gold flow away”; all other things held most precious are dross,
refuse, and worth nothing. Come in, for here are gods—or rather, behold here
the only true God; behold also Jesus Christ, whom he sent. Behold the saving
knowledge of him; behold, finally, the eternal life that proceeds from it. Thus
Maldonatus could say no more truly that every word of the preceding verse has
its difficulty than Calvin said that every single word here has its own weight.
Weight, did he say? Indeed, what man—even if he were another Atlas—would not
only be struck but utterly overwhelmed by the infinite majesty of the one true
God, the inaccessible light of Jesus Christ the Sun of righteousness (if only
we are outside him), the most splendid rays of the knowledge of him, and,
finally, the eternal weight of glory, while they most blessedly instruct,
console, and meanwhile bless us? Hence what Gerhard said about the whole prayer
of the most blessed Savior recited in this chapter we may most deservedly say
about this verse—or should I say its summit? It is “the summary of heavenly
doctrine, a ready store of life-giving consolation, the Church’s most precious
treasure, and a most effective remedy for every disease.” It is therefore most
worthy of constant reading and meditation, of our sharper investigation now,
and of your attention.

But now a sermon to the people is not to be given; let us do what better suits
the schools.

Since Christ the Lord was our high priest, he willed to fulfill the whole
office of the high priest just as he fulfilled all righteousness. That office
was threefold—namely, to teach, to pray, and to offer—and the Evangelist shows
quite clearly that Christ fully, indeed superabundantly, discharged them all,
as Zanchi and others after him observe.

In the preceding chapters he taught; in the following chapters he offered
himself as a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling savor. Here he prays to the
Father, first for himself, then for the apostles, and finally for the whole
Church, so that this whole chapter can rightly be called the Lord’s Prayer.

It is not, however, the prayer which he himself taught us in Matthew 6, where
he commanded us to seek forgiveness of sins, of which he was entirely free.
Nor is it the prayer which he poured out alone to the Father in the garden in
Matthew 26 and Luke 22, as Rupert thinks. It is rather the prayer which he
offered in the upper room—as is clear from the first verse of the next
chapter—with all the apostles present and listening, with strong crying and
tears, Hebrews 5:7. Some very learned men hold that passage to refer to this
very prayer of the Savior contained in the present chapter.

The first and only thing that, in the first five verses, he asks the Father to
grant him is that the Father would glorify him when he was now about to be most
shamefully disfigured by his passion. He presses this with a twofold argument:

1. So that he in turn may glorify the Father, in the first verse.

2. So that it may conduce to the salvation of the elect—namely, “that he may
give eternal life to those whom the Father has given him,” in the second verse.

The reason for this is given in our verse. The eternal life mentioned in the
words immediately preceding is described more clearly: it consists in the
knowledge of the only true God and of Jesus Christ, whom he sent. Thus, just as
there he most closely joined his own glorification with the Father’s, here he
joins the knowledge of each: “And this is eternal life, that they may know you,
the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent.”

To shed some light on the disputation that follows, let us begin with a very
brief explanation of the words.

First comes the predicate of the proposition: *eternal life*.

Since we are dead in sin, he called it *life*.

And since the natural life we live here is daily rather than lasting—mortal,
perishing, and vanishing—he calls the life of which he now speaks *eternal*,
unto ages of ages, since it will never die away either in this age or in the
age to come.

Interpreters do not agree very well about what this eternal life is. Is it the
eternal life in heaven, as Augustine and other ancients thought, who commonly
prove from here that heavenly blessedness consists in the beatific vision? Or
is it rather the life of grace and faith in this age, as the judgment of many
ancient and

[Marginalia: Matthew 3:15.]

[Marginalia: *On the Three Elohim*, book 4, chapter 10.]

[Marginalia: Gerhard.]

[Marginalia: *Tractates on John* 10.5; *On the Trinity* 1.1.8.]

[1679 printed p. 3 | IA leaf 11]

modern writers approves, because believers are elsewhere said already to have
eternal life, and throughout the prayer that follows Christ speaks as though
he had already given this eternal life to those given him?

For my part, I have always judged the safest manner of interpreting Scripture,
and the one most worthy of its inexhaustible fullness, to be the broadest one
(provided that context and the analogy of faith permit). Let that therefore be
the interpretation here which embraces both lives: the life of glory in
heaven, which is preeminently called eternal, Matthew 25:46; and also this life
of grace on earth, which will never wholly cease here and will one day be
consummated in glory. It is the preliminary exercise and entrance to that
glory, its pledge and firstfruits—or, to speak with Cyril, its root, [obscured
word], and bridesmaid. This is certain: the eternal life of which Christ speaks
in this verse is the same life he had said in the preceding verse that he would
give the elect. He gives both; therefore both are understood here.

In the subject of this proposition the particle *that* occurs first. As Beza
rightly says, it must be taken explicatively, not finally: it denotes the thing
itself, not its end, as in John 15:8, 12.

*That they may know*: First, this is not knowledge implanted in us by nature,
which is obscure and weak where God is concerned and entirely nonexistent
where God in Christ is concerned. Second, it is not knowledge acquired by
natural means or infused by common illumination, since the most wicked people
and even demons obtain this, James 2:19. Rather, in the Hebrew manner—among
whom verbs of sensation and knowledge connote affection and effect—it includes
not bare information in the mind but assent in the will and trust in the
heart. Indeed, it is the same as faith itself, by which we are grafted into
Christ’s body, made here partakers of divine adoption, and finally made heirs
of heaven. Just as we had both kinds of eternal life in the subject of the
proposition, so we need not doubt that the predicate includes both kinds of
knowledge: the inchoate knowledge here through faith, and the intuitive
knowledge to be consummated in the beatific vision of God in heaven. Someone
objects that this vision is indicated by another verb, *to behold*, in verse
24 of this chapter. That does not prevent its being expressed here by this
word *to know*, as in 1 Corinthians 13:12 and often elsewhere.

*You*: First, not only your will, but also your nature. Second, you the Father,
as is clear from verse 1—but is this taken personally? The Socinians contend
so, Zanchi affirms it, and it does not displease me, provided it is explained
rightly: under the name of the Father the whole divine essence, common to all
the persons of the Holy Trinity, must be understood. More on this later.

*You, the only true God*: in opposition to the gods of the Gentiles, which were
both many and false.

*And whom you sent*: first, in the incarnation; second, to fulfill the office
of Mediator. More on this too when we come to the disputation.

*Jesus Christ*: sweetest names, though this is not the proper moment to explain
them.

To draw out the sense of this statement, we should instead observe that in the
second verse Christ had said that authority over all flesh was given to him.
All flesh was commonly divided into Gentiles and Jews. Here, therefore, with
the twofold object of that saving knowledge, he provides the most fitting
medicine for both Gentiles and Jews laboring under their own mortal diseases.
Against the Gentiles’ atheism he sets God; against their polytheism, the only
God,

[Marginalia: John 3:36; 1 John 5:12–13.]

[1679 printed p. 4 | IA leaf 12]

that is, the one God—for Greek *monos* signifies “one” just as *monas*
signifies unity. Against their idolatry, which worshiped worthless, false, and
nonexistent gods—either things that were utterly nothing or at least had
nothing of the true God in them—he sets in opposition *the true God*, whom they
must know and acknowledge if they would partake of eternal life.

As for the Jews, although the Babylonian captivity had at last taught them
better, so that they renounced their ancestral polytheism and idolatry and now
worshiped the only true God after their fashion, they were so far from
believing Jesus Christ either sent by God or the only true Savior and Messiah
that they regarded him as a slave of the Devil, the most wicked of all men. To
apply medicine to this ulcer too, Christ clearly declares that knowledge of
him is as necessary to eternal life as knowledge of the Father.

We have said more than enough about the subject and predicate. Only the copula
*is* remains: to know you, etc., *is* eternal life.

People differ about its sense, but every view can be reduced to this. If the
proposition concerns intuitive knowledge and blessed life in heaven, this
knowledge is formally that eternal life in which blessedness principally
consists. But if knowledge is taken for faith, it is likewise—at least in
part—formally that spiritual life which we already live, since it is its chief
part. It can also partly be called eternal life efficiently, because in its
own kind it is a cause both of the spiritual life that flows from faith here
and of eternal life in heaven, being the way, manner, and means of arriving
there.

To settle everything in a word: as the copula *is* may be applied in different
ways, it says either that eternal life is formally contained in that knowledge,
or that eternal life depends upon it as a cause—either properly efficient or
at least in some way promoting it.

With these prefatory explanations, a field broad enough for the laborer’s
sickle appears. It is clear that the man who called this statement (as we saw
above) a summary of heavenly doctrine was not mistaken. For:

1. If it is called *life*, this says that outside Christ we are utterly dead in
sins, against the papists, Arminians, Socinians, and others.

2. Since it is eternal, whether the life of grace is understood, it asserts
its unfailing perpetuity against the same men.

3. Or if this is the life of glory, it confirms its eternity against certain
recent atheists among us.

4. Since he says it consists in knowledge, it presses the papists, especially
Bellarmine, who thought faith should be defined by ignorance rather than
knowledge.

5. It also gives occasion to ask whether the saints’ eternal blessedness in
heaven consists in the beatific vision of God, or intuitive knowledge, as some
of the fathers seek to deduce from here.

6. If it is knowledge of God, it strikes down atheists.

7. If of the one God, recent tritheists and all polytheists.

8. If of the true God, idolaters.

9. If of Jesus Christ, Jews.

[1679 printed p. 5 | IA leaf 13]

10. Since eternal life is to know not only the true God but also Jesus Christ,
two other matters can be asked and disputed from it:

1. Whether eternal life could be obtained under the first covenant made with
Adam.

2. Whether, now under the covenant of grace, any who are utterly ignorant of
Jesus Christ can be partakers of this eternal life.

Moreover, the words *whom you sent* call for much to be said about the mission
of Christ and of the Holy Spirit.

But I shall now set all these aside. My chief business here is with Arians,
Photinians, and Socinians. As a bee draws honey and a spider poison from the
same flower, so from this verse—which is all honey—the faithful and orthodox
rightly infer that Jesus Christ is the true God together with the Father,
because knowledge of Jesus Christ equally with knowledge of the Father is here
called eternal life. Ancient Arians and recent Socinians, however, strive to
hew from it various devices by which, so far as they can, they would use their
tricks and blasphemies to cast him from his throne and strip him of all the
glory of his deity. They loudly proclaim Christ’s divinity, yet utterly deny
his true deity. Indeed, to deceive his unwary reader more cunningly, Smalcius
laboriously proves in his preliminary disputations that Christ is true God;
and the rest of his flock freely acknowledge him, in their sense, as *true
God*. Yet they do not acknowledge him as God by nature, as the apostle speaks
in Galatians 4:8: not God in his own nature, not eternal, not consubstantial
with the Father, but a made God—which is to say, a truly fictitious one. They
do not call him a mere man, since he is adorned with the most excellent gifts
and offices and exalted far above the common, indeed the highest, lot of other
men: an eminently divine man, or (as they like to speak) a *divine man*. Yet as
regards his nature they fiercely insist that he is only, and thus merely, man.

They try to extort this from various other passages of Holy Scripture dragged
in by the neck, but especially from this one. It was regarded as the Achilles
of the ancient Arians; and Smalcius in his Racovian Catechism and Crellius in
his books *On the One God, the Father*, when with wicked daring and marshaled
ranks of arguments they make war against Christ’s true eternal deity, make
this the leading text and put it first in the line of battle. But what harm can
they do? With what engines drawn from this armory, so well stocked against
themselves? These, namely:

1. That he prays to the Father, in the first verse.

2. That he asks to be glorified by the Father. Both, in their view, savor of
something human and unworthy of God.

3. That in this verse he confesses himself sent by God.

4. And greatest of all, that in the former part—“that they may know you, the
only true God”—he acknowledges the Father alone to be the true God, while in
the latter part—“and Jesus Christ whom you sent”—he openly separates himself
from the Father and utterly renounces any share in the deity proper to the
Father alone.

These are the pipes with which the Socinians support their condemned cause,
which must fall forever; these the battering rams with which they try with
monstrous daring to overthrow the eternal deity of Christ our Lord. But these
weapons are reeds;

[1679 printed p. 6 | IA leaf 14]

though they strike them against our eternal rock, they will never shatter it.

Let us examine first, if you please, the last and chief objection:

Christ asserts that the Father alone is true God and distinguishes himself
from the Father. Therefore he rejects that deity which we falsely ascribe to
him.

I have many answers, though most are not mine. This sweetest text supplies the
most tranquil peace of conscience, yet the blasphemous impudence of ancient
and recent heretics has made it one of the most assailed. It has so exercised
the piety, zeal, and abilities of ancient and recent orthodox writers that
almost nothing remains unsaid. Later authors have consequently done little
more than copy earlier labors. “As cow follows cow, so author follows author,”
as the illustrious Scaliger wittily says in *Evangelical Exercises* 1.3—though
he did precisely that in the chapter where he transferred almost everything
from Chaeus. More recently Arnold borrowed almost word for word from another
everything he has on this text. After so many and such great men, to say more
would be the work of a man industrious in vain. Yet the place and occasion,
and the command of most learned men, require me to say something. The die is
cast: I must attempt either to explain somewhat what others have said or at
least add in a few places my own little extra weight, light though it is.

My first point is directed to the men themselves: if Socinians remain
consistent with themselves and their doctrine, no torture can wring from this
text a difference of essence between Christ and the Father; rather, it exposes
their madness. Nothing is here attributed to the Father as God which they do
not freely and lavishly grant to Christ himself. In their view Christ is “true
God,” the very thing affirmed here of the Father. Therefore the Father is not
the only true God, though they fiercely maintain that he is. Thus they cut
their own throats when they dare fight Christ’s deity.

They say that Christ is true God, which they readily concede, but not the
supreme God, who is meant here: “the true God, or supreme God,” says Crellius’s
paraphrase. Very well. They therefore need another text to establish their
position, one in which the word *supreme* occurs; it is not found here.

It is nevertheless implied, I admit, and I gladly acknowledge that the
emphatic article in “the only true God” suggests it. But I judge the Socinians
unworthy of that concession. On their own reading and construction nothing
emphatic inheres in the article; it is merely relative.

Meanwhile let this stand: whoever is called true God—not tropically or
abusively—and receives truly divine worship, as most Socinians practice and
defend in their worship of Christ, cannot without blasphemy be denied the
majesty of the supreme God.

Leaving the men and their concessions, let us approach the matter itself.

My second foundational point is this: what is most openly asserted in many
other passages of Scripture is not contradicted here,

[Marginalia: A Grotian title cited by Gerhard.]

[Marginalia: See Bisterfeld.]

[1679 printed p. 7 | IA leaf 15]

for the Holy Spirit is perfectly consistent with himself, and the disputes of
Scripture are always friendly—would that ours were too. But the clearest
testimonies of Holy Scripture prove, indeed expressly affirm, that Jesus
Christ is God, true God, and supreme God. “The Word was God,” John 1:1: that
Word who “became flesh,” verse 14, who is Jesus Christ. Behold Christ God!

First John 5:20 says, “We know that the Son of God has come and given us an
understanding, that we may know him who is true; and we are in him who is true,
that is, in Jesus Christ. This is the true God and eternal life.” The same
apostolic writer copies there almost all the words that compose nearly the
whole statement before us. He surely looked back here and interprets these
words. What the Socinians here claim as proper to the Father alone he there
openly attributes to Jesus Christ: that he is true God. Behold, secondly,
Christ the true God!

Behold also the supreme God! Romans 9:5: “Christ, who is God over all, blessed
forever.” I admit that they strain every sinew and turn every way to cast the
darkness of their quibbles over these and other most luminous testimonies to
Christ’s deity—quibbles of which they themselves are now ashamed. Yet these
things are written as with sunbeams, no less in Scripture than in believers’
hearts. Their habitual escapes—“It seems it can be understood otherwise,” and
the like—will never prevent these clearest texts establishing Jesus Christ as
true, supreme God. Unless the Holy Spirit contradicted himself, nothing here
can oppose them.

Indeed, to eyes even a little clear-sighted there is not the slightest
contradiction here, not even its appearance or shadow.

The testimonies cited expressly, clearly, and openly say that Christ is true,
supreme God. Here we have that eternal life is to know you—the Father—as the
only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent. What contradiction, I ask, is
there?

Can it rightly be inferred that the Father is the only true God in such a way
that Christ is excluded from sharing true deity? By no means. It can and must
be understood otherwise.

First, it can be understood otherwise. Let no one blame me for employing as an
answer the escape I just censured in them. In this matter the orthodox have a
greater liberty—indeed right—than heretics. When heretics repeatedly mutter,
with their crafty and protean disposition, that even the most firmly grounded
things, openly delivered in Scripture and believed firmly everywhere and
always by the whole Church, “seem capable of another interpretation,” their
stubbornness is intolerable. In a theologian defending catholic orthodoxy,
however, the same move may be tolerated and praised for checking their
insolence. I therefore say that this can be understood otherwise: the
conjunction *and* is so far from separating that it especially joins, and
admits Jesus Christ into the designation “the only true God.” It does not
follow that Christ’s words by themselves permit the same predicate to be given
to infinitely many other subjects,

[Marginalia: “One God.”]

[Marginalia: See Diodati, Chrysostom, etc.]

[1679 printed p. 8 | IA leaf 16]

as Crellius claims, for the case of Christ differs utterly from that of all
creatures. Crellius admittedly boasts: “It must be proved by some suitable
sacred or profane example that anyone can properly speak this way. They will
not produce an example from Scripture; ordinary people do not speak so. But
Christ spoke in the ordinary way.” With his leave, both Scripture and ordinary
people do speak so. As to ordinary speech, Athanasius’s disputation with Arius
at Nicaea is especially relevant: “If someone says, ‘I acknowledge Constantine
alone as emperor of land and sea, and his son Constantius,’ the son is not
separated from sharing the empire, but rather joined to it.”

This manner of speech is no less common in Holy Scripture. Our theologians have
collected many examples; I offer only two familiar ones. John 8:9 says, “Jesus
was left alone, and the woman standing in the midst.” Though Christ is said to
be left *alone*, the addition “and the woman” means she remained there with
him. Thus *alone* excludes those previously standing by, not the woman.
Likewise here “the only true God” excludes other, alien, false gods, not Jesus
Christ, who is true God together with the Father. The second example, from
Chrysostom’s eightieth homily on John, is 1 Corinthians 9:6: “Or do only
Barnabas and I have no right not to work?” Chrysostom asks, “When he says,
‘Only I and Barnabas,’ does he cast out Barnabas? Not at all.” Just as Paul’s
“only I and Barnabas” does not exclude Barnabas, so when Christ says, “you, the
only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent,” he does not exclude himself but
rather includes himself. Thus we establish the first point: it can be
understood otherwise than the Socinians wish.

Second, it also must be. The grammatical construction permits it; the truth
of the matter and analogy of faith persuade—indeed compel—it. Since Scripture
openly and expressly calls Christ true God, as we proved, the copulative *and*
joins “the only true God” and “Jesus Christ.” It distinguishes but does not
separate: it distinguishes the person, but does not separate the nature or
remove the deity.

This leads directly to examine more closely the orthodox answers to the
Socinian argument. There are many; omitting the rest, I shall present what I
judge the four chief answers and try according to my strength to explain or
confirm them.

The first is that of Tertullian, Athanasius, Nazianzen, Hilary, Ambrose, and
Augustine, and is the same as what we just said. Through the conjunction *and*,
they attribute the three words “only true God” not to the Father alone but also
to the Son. Thus Tertullian—or rather Novatian—in *On the Trinity* 24: “If he
had not wished himself to be understood as God, why did he add, ‘and Jesus
Christ whom you sent,’ unless he wished him also to be taken as God? He joined
him to God so that through this conjunction he might be understood as God, as
he is.” So Athanasius

[Marginalia: Crellius, book 1, chapter 10; ibid.; defense 10.36.]

[Marginalia: 1 Kings 22:20; Matthew 6:47.]

[1679 printed p. 9 | IA leaf 17]

in the passage cited above confronted Arius, “Nor did Arius have anything to
object against the answer.” Nazianzen’s judgment is explicit: “The addition
does not cut off Christ, but joins him to the only true God.” Hilary, Ambrose,
and others have parallel statements. Augustine therefore does not hesitate to
assert this order of the words: “That they may know you, and Jesus Christ whom
you sent, as the only true God.” Here at least I agree with Maldonatus: “I do
not know whether this interpretation can be refuted by those whom it
displeases; for I cannot find how it can.” Neither can I. Gerhard is right that
this coordination cannot prevail against a stubborn adversary, but we need not
heed their stubbornness. Nothing in Scripture is so plain, so written as with
the sun’s rays, that captious minds hardened in unbelief—as many Socinian minds
are—will not make it controversial. Let them clamor as much as they like,
provided they have nothing with which to refute it. Calvin’s contrary point,
that the same question would arise about the Holy Spirit, proves nothing.
Everything need not be said at once. Other texts clearly establish the Holy
Spirit’s deity; no controversy about it arose here, nor any occasion to name
him. Christ prays to the Father and seeks the manifestation of his glory,
which his death was about to diminish. He speaks of eternal life, of which he
alone was mediator. He had no need to mention the Holy Spirit, whose perfect
knowledge had not yet been delivered, but became fuller through his later
descent upon the apostles. Moreover, if “the Father” here means the whole
divine essence common to the several persons, as will appear, the Holy Spirit
is not excluded. This was the fathers’ first answer.

The second, given by Basil, Chrysostom, Theophylact, Nazianzen, and others, is
that *only* does not exclude the Son or Holy Spirit, who have the same nature
and therefore the same deity as the Father, but created things. Augustine says
this, and especially that it excludes the Gentiles’ gods and idols, which
either were nothing, 1 Corinthians 10:20, or worthless—at least infinitely
distant from the infinite majesty of the true God and without the nature of
God the Father, as Brugensis rightly says. Basil says, “Not to separate the
Son, but the falsely named gods”; Chrysostom likewise says “only true” is set
against unreal gods, and Theophylact follows him.

Citing the fathers is fruitless with Socinians, who have abjured the ancient
faith together with the ancient fathers. Let us see, therefore, whether reason
(their Helen) can accomplish anything, and reason sought from Scripture. It
shows that this is the sense of the words:

1. A very similar text, 1 Thessalonians 1:9: “how you turned to God from idols
to serve the living and true God.” As here, *true God* occurs there. If one
asks why God is called true, the reason left unspoken here is openly explained
there: he is opposed to idols, which they abandoned as soon as they turned to
God,

[Marginalia: *Theological Oration* 4.]

[Marginalia: Augustine, *Tractates on John* 105.]

[Marginalia: Brugensis. See also Ussher, *On the Epistles of Ignatius*,
chapter 13, p. 88.]

[1679 printed p. 10 | IA leaf 18]

and thus is called living and true because all their gods were false and most
were dead. Theodoret comments: “Not comparing him with the Son, but with those
who are not gods, he called the One who is living and true—living because they
were dead, and true because they were falsely so called.”

2. A second reason proving the same can be drawn from the aim and context of
the very passage before us. In the preceding verse Christ asserted that
authority over all flesh—not only Jews but Gentiles—had been given to him. The
Jews utterly denied Christ as the sole means to eternal life; the Gentiles
were wholly devoted to worshiping many false gods. Christ provides medicine
for both: for Jews, in acknowledging Jesus Christ whom they had rejected; for
Gentiles, in saving knowledge and worship of the only true God after abandoning
their many false gods, formerly worshiped in marvelous blindness. Even
Crellius admits that this occasioned the words. Their genuine sense therefore
plainly opposes to their *many* gods this *one* and *only* God, and to their
*false* gods this *true* God. Those gods alone, or principally, are excluded;
or, taken most broadly, all creatures and things, and images of them, which are
not true God. The Son is never excluded: he is in the Father, John 14:10; one
with the Father, John 10:30; and God, John 1:1. A person who is one with another
is not for that reason alone. But what prevents the one God from being one
with himself? Father and Son are the very same one God. In person he is called
another, John 5:32, “There is another who testifies about me”; in nature he is
utterly the same God. If he may in some other respect be called *alone*, in the
sense of deity he will never be solitary. That the Father is called the only
true God no more implies that the Son is not true God than calling the Son
“the true light,” John 1:9, implies that the Father is not light or not true
light. Theophylact says well: “Whatever belongs to one nature is common also
to the Father.” What is said of the Son alone is common to the Father. Because
of their shared nature, what is here attributed to the Father alone belongs to
the Son who shares it. Thus here and often elsewhere. God is called “only
wise,” Romans 16:27, but Christ—God’s very Wisdom—is not therefore foolish.
God alone “has immortality,” 1 Timothy 6:16, but Christ is not therefore still
subject to death, since he testifies in Revelation 1:18, “I live, and was dead;
and behold, I am alive forevermore. Amen.” Away with these blasphemous follies!
God forbid, as Theophylact piously says.

This presses the Socinians harder because Smalcius and his fellows concede as
an axiom: “The name *alone* and particles signifying the same, when said of
God, never exclude those subordinated to God.” Good heavens! Can something
that does not exclude those subordinated to God exclude Christ the Son? Though
as man he is subordinated to God, as Son—whatever the Socinians blaspheme—he
was from eternity and will eternally be consubstantial and equally divine with
the Father.

This

[1679 printed p. 11 | IA leaf 19]

is what Oecolampadius meant when he said that under the Father’s name the
whole Trinity is understood here, and I do not differ much. Although I readily
grant, as before, that *you* in this verse means the Father, and that *Father*
in verse 1 is taken personally, as Zanchi and others hold, I understand it so
that the Son and Holy Spirit, who share one nature, essence, and deity, are not
excluded.

The adversaries’ objection—that on this reading Christ would pray to
himself—proves nothing. He does not pray here as God, but either as man, as most
interpreters hold, or rather as our Mediator and high priest, as some sounder
writers judge. Thus, when addressing the Father, he includes all the persons
of the Trinity, who share one nature. In sum, *alone* excludes idols, fictitious
gods, and every creature; it does not exclude Jesus Christ, the Son, or any
person of the Holy Trinity consubstantial with the Father. This was the second
ancient answer, particularly consonant with piety, truth, and the sense of the
passage.

The third answer belongs chiefly to recent writers: the exclusive term *only*
does not qualify the pronoun *you*, yielding “the Father alone is God,” but the
noun *God*, yielding “that alone is true deity which is in the Father.” The Son
is not excluded from it, since he is consubstantial with the Father. Lyra goes
this way.

This answer deserves attention because it belongs not only to scholastics
unskilled in elegant literature and languages, but also to men thoroughly
versed in Greek and Latin idioms. One says: “The sense is not, ‘That they may
know you, who alone are true God,’ but ‘That they may know you, who are that
God who alone is true.’”

The learned Beza says nearly the same: “This sentence can be perverted as
though the Father alone were said to be true God—namely, by construing *only*
backward with a necessarily understood substantive verb. Yet the article
placed before *only* shows it should be connected forward with that word: the
Father is said to be that true God who alone exists, since he is unique and
most singular. The same article and particle must be construed this way in
Romans 16:27 and 1 Timothy 1:17: not ‘you alone to be true God,’ but ‘you to be
the only true God’—that is, you are he who alone is true God, as the Syriac
translator also rendered it.”

I admit Calvin thinks this weak. Yet, with respect to so great a man, it seems
to contain much solidity. “You alone are that true God” and “You are the only
true God” differ greatly.

1. In the manner of assertion: in the former, *alone* belongs to the subject;
in the latter, to the predicate. This is immediately obvious, and the position
of the Greek article between *you* and *only true God* openly indicates it.
Christ does not say, “that they may know only you, the true God,” but “that
they may know you, the only true God.”

2. In meaning: for if the Father personally taken were said alone to be God,

[Marginalia: Maldonatus.]

[1679 printed p. 12 | IA leaf 20]

this would, on the Socinian view, exclude the Son from deity. But when the
Father is called the only true God, it says only that the deity which alone is
true belongs to him. Neither the truth nor this form of assertion excludes
Christ from that deity. Thus in Zechariah 13:7 the Father calls him “my
fellow,” as Piscator admirably translates after Pagninus and says the word
implies Christ’s equality with God the Father. Vatablus similarly renders “the
man equal to me”; the earlier Chaldee interpreter has “a companion like me,”
and the Vulgate “the man who cleaves to me”: according to his deity
consubstantial with the Father; according to his humanity most closely akin
and like him; and through the hypostatic union intimately joined to God.

To return from my digression: I dare affirm that “the Father alone is true
God” and “the Father is the only true God” greatly differ. I do not deny that,
depending on the subject matter and less exact speech, such propositions
sometimes coincide. “You alone are my heir” and “you are my only heir” may
mean the same. But examples of divergence also occur. If a piece of refined
gold is offered and I say, “this alone is refined gold,” it could mean that
this piece contains all refined gold anywhere. But if I say, “this is the only
refined gold,” I mean merely that this piece belongs to the nature and number
of the other pieces that truly are refined gold. Likewise, the Father is here
called the only true God because he has in himself the only true deity, though
it is common to the Son and Holy Spirit, who are one with him.

To finish this third answer, I add only this extra weight. If “the only” refers
backward rather than to what follows:

1. The Greek article would be inserted less suitably. Had the text read “only
you, the true God,” the speech would flow neatly and clearly. The Socinian
construction is awkward and little suited to Greek usage.

2. As the article’s location would be incongruous, so the article itself would
be less emphatic, though Socinians especially want it to denote the *supreme*
God. Referred backward it would be merely relative, containing nothing
emphatic. So much for the third answer.

The fourth and last—and in my judgment the most genuine and most suitable to
this and many other passages, especially in Paul—is that Christ spoke here not
precisely as God but either as man or as our Mediator and high priest. In that
sense he distinguishes, but does not separate, himself from God—or separates
him only in one respect. Regarding the eternal life or blessedness discussed
here, two things must especially be noted: the end in which we finally rest,
and the means by which that end is reached.

[Marginalia: The Hebrew of Zechariah 13:7.]

[Marginalia: See Gataker, *Miscellanies* 2.1.4, p. 302; also Calvin on John
17:3 and Pareus.]

[1679 printed p. 13 | IA leaf 21]

God is the end, according to 1 Corinthians 15:28, where, after Christ delivers
his kingdom to the Father, God is said to be all in all. Christ the Mediator
is the means by which we tend toward and finally reach him, as he testifies in
John 14:6: “I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father
except through me.” Christ plainly distinguishes these two here. The Father
together with the Son and Holy Spirit is the only true God whom we enjoy as
our one end; Jesus Christ as Mediator is the sole means by which we are brought
to that true God and thus to eternal life. It is according to this mediatorial
economy that he distinguishes himself, if at all, from the true God. The
inserted words “whom you sent” make this especially clear, for Christ’s mission
concerns the work of the Mediator for which he was sent. Although true God
inheres in the divine essence, and Christ as Mediator is true God according to
one of his natures, in respect of this mission and his mediatorial office and
work he distinguishes himself from God considered absolutely and simply. This,
I judge, is the genuine sense: “This is eternal life, that they may know you,
the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you sent.” It contains the sum of all
theology: all our blessedness consists in knowing by true faith the only true
God, but through Jesus Christ our sole Mediator.

In exactly the same way Paul often joins and distinguishes Christ from the
Father and God with respect to the mediatorial dispensation: “one God and one
Mediator between God and man, the man Jesus Christ,” 1 Timothy 2:5; “one God,
the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ,” 1 Corinthians 8:6; “the kingdom of
Christ and of God,” Ephesians 5:5; and many similar texts. None proves the Son
is not God. They prove that Christ—God manifested in flesh—is our Mediator and
high priest, who emptied himself for our salvation, took our place, bore and
removed the sins and punishments of us all, and so is the one means and sole
way by which access lies open to the Father, heaven, and eternal life.

This is the sum: eternal life comes to us from the Father through Christ—from
the one true triune God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, through the merits and
obedience of our Mediator Jesus Christ, whom he sent. This seems to me and
others the passage’s genuine sense. “We must therefore believe the true God as
regards faith in divinity, and also believe Jesus Christ as regards faith in
the dispensation,” says Cardinal Toledo. Calvin puts it beautifully: “We must
always remember what person Christ sustains here. For we must not consider
only his eternal deity”—though he establishes it in the next verse by speaking
of the glory he had with the Father before the world existed—“because here he
speaks as God manifested in flesh and in the office of Mediator.” Thus Calvin.
According to this divine economy he distinguishes himself from the Father,
and elsewhere acknowledges the Father greater, John 14:28. He prays to the
Father and credits all things to him, John 10:28–29; says he can do nothing
without him, John 5:19; undertakes his commands, John 5:36; is commanded by
him, John 14:31; taught, John 8:28; sanctified and sent, John 10:36. Many
similar things occur,

[Marginalia: The Father designates the end; the Mediator the means through
whom he is savingly known.]

[Marginalia: Calvin, *Institutes* 1.13.26.]

[1679 printed p. 14 | IA leaf 22]

none implying that as Son he is not the same God with the Father. As our
Mediator, according to their mutual eternal counsel, he undertook and
performed everything belonging to the surety’s office, the manifestation of
God’s glory, and the reconciliation of divine love to us. For this end he
submitted to the Father, always spoke honorably of him and most modestly of
himself, and here, as Maldonatus notes, spoke of himself in the third person.
He refers everything to the glory of the Father—that is, God—and attributes
all teachings and signs to him, as Euthymius says. No one should wonder,
therefore, if he distinguishes himself from the Father to this extent—if he
distinguishes himself at all, which some dislike.

Others, conversely, venture further and wish Christ distinguished from the
Father here not only as Mediator but as the eternal Son of God. They think the
Father personally and as such may by attribution be called the only true God
in relation to the Son and Holy Spirit—not absolutely, but because he is
unbegotten and neither generated by nor proceeds from another person, but is
the fount of deity for the other persons, possessing (as some say) fountain
deity from that spring of Godhead. Maldonatus considers this interpretation
somewhat too bold, perhaps rightly. Gerhard thinks it dangerous that the Father
alone should be called true God merely because of personal order, since this
either really conflicts, or seems to conflict, with the equality of the
persons. For my part, I confess it sufficiently out of place and less exact
according to the analogy of faith. I do not deny Gerhard’s fear that it could
deceive the unlearned and incautious concerning the holy persons’
consubstantiality. I therefore set it aside as at best unnecessary to interpret
this passage and perhaps wholly foreign to it. Christ does not deny that as
the Father’s Son he is true God; he says that as Mediator he was sent by the
Father and is so far distinguished from him. Let us follow Calvin, whose words
will conclude the matter: “Because God, whose living and express image he is,
is known only in Christ’s face, it says that they may know you and Christ whom
you sent.” Elsewhere: “The solution is easy. Christ appearing in human form
designates under the Father’s person God’s power, essence, and deity. Therefore
the one true God is Christ’s Father—that is, the God who long ago promised the
world a Redeemer is one. Yet the unity and truth of deity are found in Christ.
Christ was humbled to raise us on high. Once we arrive there, his divine
majesty comes forth; then he is recognized wholly in that Father and the Father
wholly in him. In sum, whoever separates Christ from the Father’s divinity has
not yet recognized him who alone is true God, but fabricates an alien god.”

We have at last completed that fourfold answer to the Socinian argument
wrenched from this text against Christ’s deity. Since it is their chief and
supposedly Achilles argument, we refuted it at greater length. The rest are
light-armed soldiers, more easily scattered. Three remain which Socinians
think fight Christ’s divinity here:

[Marginalia: Theodore of Heraclea.]

[1679 printed p. 15 | IA leaf 23]

1. That he prays to God the Father.

2. That in the preceding verses he prays to be glorified by the Father. 3.
That here he confesses himself sent by the Father. All this, they think, speaks
of a man or creature and denies him to be God.

But if all is spoken and understood according to the economy, as will shortly
appear, how can it prejudice Christ’s deity?

Let us treat each briefly.

1. He suppliantly prays to the Father. But he does this as man and in the state
of emptying. Because he is man and does what befits man, shall we deny him to
be God when we believe that the God-man is simultaneously true God and true
man? The beloved disciple who reclined at Christ’s breast knew best and
explained what was in him, joining two distinct but inseparable things: “The
Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and yet we beheld his glory, glory as of
the only-begotten from the Father, full of grace and truth,” John 1:14. When he
said *as*, this was according to the flesh and Hebrew idiom; “as a theologian,”
says Chrysostom, he speaks properly of nature. He affirms not one merely like
the Father’s only-begotten but the very only-begotten himself. If Christ prayed
not merely as man but, as I rather think, as our Mediator and high priest, does
this divine economy derogate from divinity? Not at all. The priestly office
included not only praying, but sacrificing and expiating the people’s sins.
Though a mere man could pray, expiating our sins by the sacrifice of his death
and satisfying divine justice requires God. Our one high priest, who prays and
expiates, must therefore be God-man, true man and true God. Human things are
interwoven with divine, and the prayer composed of both, as Cyril aptly says.
As the Mediator has two natures, so the prayer indicates both. Its humble
elements, involving submission to the Father, belong to Christ according to
his human nature; its higher and magnificent elements reveal God. To die is
human; to die for man belongs to the Mediator, who is man and God. Prayer
savors of humanity; meriting what he prays is the clearest sign of deity.
Weeping prayers sounds humble; making them mediatorial, reconciling, and
effectual proclaims God. Though Socinians regard Christ praying to himself as
a paradox, orthodox fathers find no absurdity in the same Christ praying and
granting according to different natures. “What the Son of God received from
the Father in the form of a servant, he also bestowed in the form of God,” says
Leo, Epistle 41. “As man he received by grace from deity what as God he has by
nature; therefore as man he receives from himself as God,” says Cyril,
*Treasury* 8.1. Bernard adds: “He freely seeks to receive what lies in the
seeker’s power. The Son’s request is therefore dispensational, not necessary,
since he gives with the Father whatever he receives from the Father.” His
prayer as man and Mediator therefore detracts nothing from his deity, since as
God-man Mediator he merited what he asks, and as God bestowed it. So much for
the first.

[Marginalia: Cyril on John, book 11, chapter 17.]

[Marginalia: It is wonderful that, being the Son, he receives from himself.]

[1679 printed p. 16 | IA leaf 24]

The second objection was that he prayed to be glorified by the Father. This
presses us less. If prayer does not diminish deity, much less does
glorification—first, being glorified; second, being glorified by the Father.

1. Being glorified is nothing unworthy of God, to whom all glory is deservedly
due: “You are worthy, Lord, to receive glory and honor,” Revelation 4:11. More
particularly, glorification here comprises two things: reality and knowledge,
the true conferral of glory and its manifestation. Christ seeks both, one
according to each nature.

Regarding his human nature he seeks the real conferral of glory. As man he had
been “a worm and not a man, a reproach of men and despised by the people.” With
an ignominious death now near, the remaining spark of glory seemed about to be
utterly extinguished. He prays that his human nature, so despised, might
receive true glory through support in death, resurrection from the dead, and
ascension to heaven. How does this derogate from his essential deity or glory?
The same Christ who was put to death in flesh and made alive by the Spirit, and
who in human nature was the most despised of men in Isaiah 53:3, is in divine
nature most deservedly called “the Lord of glory,” 1 Corinthians 2:8.

Regarding his divine nature he most rightly seeks glorification, not the
conferral of new glory—for no accession can be made to his already infinite
essential glory—but the manifestation of that essential glory. It was veiled
by the incarnation and seemed almost extinguished and buried in death’s
darkness. Yet what is obscured is not immediately lost. This does not happen
to the Father’s glory; why to the Son’s? A person who vindicates the Father’s
glory from blasphemous human insults does not deny him to be God. Nor does
Christ divest himself of Godhead when he seeks the open manifestation of his
formerly obscured divine majesty. Two contrary points should be noted:

1. The depth of Christ’s self-emptying, undergone for us, and the astonishing
emptying of God’s Son: his divine glory was so obscured that it needed
manifestation. How far Christ’s humility descended!

2. The detestable ingratitude of the Socinians. Because the eternal Son of God
became man for us, they wickedly conclude that he is not God in himself;
because for our salvation he willed his supreme divine glory to lie hidden
briefly under the veil of flesh and death, they strive on that very account to
strip him of deity itself. Behold how high the proud insolence of the most
ungrateful men ascends!

Yet it is well, and this can stop their blasphemous mouths forever: although
in verse 1 he asks the Father to glorify him—whence they dare deny he is
God—he seeks glorification with the glory he had with the Father before the
world existed, verse 5. As will appear later, this proclaims him eternal God.

Therefore neither being glorified nor anything in it detracts from his deity
or glory. That was the first point.

2. But

[Marginalia: Brugensis.]

[Marginalia: Psalm 22:6; Isaiah 53.]

[Marginalia: 1 Peter 3:18.]

[1679 printed p. 17 | IA leaf 25]

does his asking to be glorified by the Father sound some inferiority and thus
derogate from his deity?

By no means. In scriptural and ordinary speech, to glorify another is not
restricted to a superior glorifying an inferior; an equal may glorify an
equal, someone himself, indeed an inferior a superior.

The Spirit glorifies the Son, John 16:14; yet even Socinians do not infer that
the Spirit is superior to the Son.

The Son also glorifies the Father. Here, after saying, “Father, glorify your
Son,” he immediately adds “that your Son also may glorify you”; verse 4 again
says, “I have glorified you on earth.” Yet on their view this does not make the
Father inferior to the Son.

Indeed, we who are lowest of all can and must glorify the supreme God: “Let
your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify
your Father who is in heaven,” Matthew 5:16. Yet an earthly man will not
therefore equal, much less surpass, the heavenly Father.

They may say that although the phrase does not compel it, the passage requires
the Father’s glorification of the Son to differ from ours of God. We freely
admit they are not entirely the same, but neither are they wholly unlike.

Insofar as glorification denotes the real conferral of glory, we cannot
glorify God this way. The Father did glorify Christ this way, but as man,
according to which nature we acknowledge him inferior to the Father.

Insofar as glorification is the manifestation of glory, we glorify God, and
the Father glorifies Christ both as man and as God. One who manifests that the
Son is God does not deny that he is God.

We may add that as the Father glorified Christ, so Christ glorified himself;
not, in the supreme sincerity he possessed as man, by seizing honor for
himself, Hebrews 5:5, but by the supreme power he possessed as God’s
only-begotten Son, openly demonstrating himself true God. As the Father raised
him, Ephesians 1:20, so he raised himself, John 10:18. The Father glorified the
Son and the Son himself, because whatever the Father does, the Son likewise
does, John 5:19, proving him the same God with the Father. So much for his
request to be glorified by the Father.

The third and final point is his saying that the Father sent him: “and Jesus
Christ whom you sent.” They claim this proves the Father’s authority over him
and his inferiority to the Father, denying him supreme deity. They use the
same argument against the Holy Spirit’s deity because he too is said to be
sent—by the Father in John 14:26, and Christ in John 15:26.

We need not discuss the Spirit’s deity or mission here. We gladly acknowledge
that the Father sent Jesus Christ, and say this in explanation:

1. With Bonaventure, mission imports manifestation and connotes emanation in
the one sent. Thus, though Father, Son, and Spirit are said to send, only Son
and Spirit are properly said to be sent, since they are the persons produced
and emanating from the Father, the fount of deity.

[1679 printed p. 18 | IA leaf 26]

2. Mission therefore suggests the order of persons. It is fitting that the Son,
the second person, sends the Spirit, the third, just as the first sends both
second and third.

3. It consequently shows distinction of persons. The same person may in
different ways send and be sent, as the will moves and is moved when it moves
itself; so the Spirit may be called sending insofar as he is God and sent
insofar as he is gift. But when the Father is said to send the Son, and the
Son the Spirit, we acknowledge a proper personal difference. Does it also show
difference of essence? Socinians affirm and orthodox writers deny it. Mission
implies substantial difference only when it imports proper dominion, as when
God sends an angel, a master his servant, or Christ the apostles: “As the
Father sent me, so I send you,” John 20:21. Yet *as* denotes some likeness, not
complete agreement. The Father did not send the Son with the same commanding
authority by which Christ sent the apostles. Although the Son sends the
apostles as the Father sent him, it does not follow that he sent no one while
the Father sent him. We recognize in this authority to send, not an essential
lordship proper to the Father over the Son, but the divine economy and order
of persons. As Son of Man and Mediator he is properly called sent. As the
second person who was to be incarnate and undertake the mediatorial office,
mission denotes personal order and difference, but no such distinction of
essence or proper dominion of sender over sent that each cannot be true,
supreme God, whether sent or sending. An equal may send an equal by mutual
agreement: one colleague by compact sends another, and so the Father the Son.
Indeed, *sent* proves rather that whoever is now sent existed before. Christ
was sent by God in his first incarnation; he therefore existed before that
incarnation and is true, eternal God manifested in flesh, according to 1
Timothy 3’s final verse. These are approximately the Socinian objections from
these three verses against Christ’s deity. We can set against them several
things from this chapter that establish it; merely naming them will suffice.

First, that we owe eternal life to him. That expression, on no lower reading,
proclaims him God: “This is God and eternal life,” 1 John 5:20.

2. That we owe this eternal life equally to Christ and the Father, since it
consists equally in knowledge of each, proves him the same God and sharing the
same divine honor with the Father. For those whose power of conferring eternal
life is the same,

[1679 printed p. 19 | IA leaf 27]

3. Eternal life consists in knowledge of Christ; but this knowledge includes
trust, which belongs to God alone.

4. He is preeminently called the Father’s Son—that is, the only-begotten and
essential Son. Scripture repeatedly does not deny him attributes applied to
the Father as peculiar.

5. Authority—that is, commanding authority and armed power—over all flesh is
given him, verse 2. This is proper to God, Numbers 16:22; 27:16; Jeremiah
32:27. The same Father with him is the apostle’s one “who is God over all,
blessed forever,” Romans 9:5. If they object that the authority was *given*,
we easily answer with Cyril: he possesses all things as God and receives all
things as man. As the eternal Son of God he naturally has communicated to him,
in the proper manner, both divine essence and power; as Son of Man and
Mediator, it is freely granted by the Father. Nothing prevents him from having
as God what he receives as man, or having in himself by nature what as Son he
receives personally from the Father. As Son he draws deity from the Father as
its fount, while nevertheless being God of himself.

6. Verse 5 especially presses the adversaries. He asks to be glorified by God
with the glory he had with him before the world existed. This openly
demonstrates that he is God and eternal. Nothing existed before the world’s
beginnings, much less possessed glory, that was not God.

7. Finally, verse 10: “All mine are yours, and yours are mine.” This is not
merely as a steward administers everything belonging to his lord, Genesis
39:9; nor merely as a bride possesses all the bridegroom’s goods and the
bridegroom himself by covenant, Song of Songs 2:16; but as one sharing the same
nature and therefore the same power.

I admit the Socinians ordinarily raise many objections against each point,
and the orthodox offer no fewer arguments to explain and establish them. I now
set all these aside. What has been said sufficiently shows that this sweetest
text is so far from weakening the supreme deity of our Lord Jesus Christ that
it establishes it most firmly.
