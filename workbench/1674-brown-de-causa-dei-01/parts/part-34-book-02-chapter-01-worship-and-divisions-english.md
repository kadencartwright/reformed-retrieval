CHAPTER I

On the Worship of God and Its Division

Various related terms must be explained a little here so that we can more
easily understand the nature of *worship*: namely, *adoration, veneration,
honor, praise, glory, devotion, religion, piety, observance,* and *reverence*.

The Hebrew for *to adore* is שחה or שחח: *he bowed himself, humbled himself; he
was humbled, fell down, prostrated himself*; and in the Hithpael, השתחוה: *he
fell down; he prostrated himself for the sake of reverence or honor, adored,
supplicated*; and, by metonymy, *honored, reverenced* (Judges 7:15). This Hebrew
word, as some note, designates not only an act of the *mind* but also an act of
the *body*, whether performed by inclining the head, bowing the entire body,
bending the knees, sinking down upon the knees, or—what is the highest
honor—falling prostrate. Rabbi David Kimchi, according to Zanchi in his work
*On the Second Precept*, says that this word properly means *to prostrate
oneself upon the ground with the whole body and arms outstretched, than which
no greater symbol of humility and subjection can be found*. The Greek is
προσκυνεῖν. Some hold that this word derives from κυνέω, *I kiss*, in place of
κύω or κύνω, as though it denoted the same thing as *moving the hand to the
mouth*, because among the Greeks veneration was shown by moving the hand to
the mouth. Others hold that the word denotes the same thing as prostrating
oneself entirely at someone’s feet, as a little dog does at its master’s, for
the sake of subjection. See Zanchi at the place cited above. Among eastern
peoples it was customary to reverence and honor those who excelled others by
prostrating the body. Thus the Persians venerated their kings. The Latin word
means the same—Valla holds that it derives from *ad* and *ora*, as does Grotius
on Exodus 20, who does not wish it to be derived from *orare*, that is, *to
pray*. It denotes the same thing as προσκυνεῖν, unless perhaps, as some think,
it has a wider scope than either the Hebrew or the Greek word and so embraces
every kind of adoration.

This *adoration* is either purely *civil and dutiful*, which is given to
creatures (Genesis 18:2; 19:1; 23:12; 27:29; 33:3; 42:6; 43:26; Exodus 18:7;
Numbers 22:31; 1 Samuel 20:41; 25:23; 28:14; Revelation 3:9), or *religious*,
which is owed to God alone (Exodus 20:3; Deuteronomy 5:7; 6:13; 10:20, and
frequently elsewhere), where it denotes religious worship rendered to God on
account of his absolute power and dominion, which those who adore acknowledge
and attest by this act. Although the Hebrew and Greek word

[1674 logical printed p. 236 | IA leaf 254 | PDF p. 254]

more closely denotes an external act of the body, it is nevertheless
transferred from the body to the mind and signifies the mind’s observance,
honor, and subjection. Adoration is therefore distinguished into *external and
internal*, or *bodily and spiritual*. The former is the body’s prostration or
inclination; the latter is the mind’s abasement and humiliation. This
humiliation of the mind which occurs in adoration means that the one whom we
adore is acknowledged by us as our Lord. And, as Zanchi rightly observes at
the place cited above, there can properly be no adoration except from an
inferior toward a superior, and unless we humble ourselves and submit to the
one whom we adore. Consequently, all true adoration proceeds from a knowledge
of our own unworthiness and of the dignity of the one whom we adore. In
religious adoration, therefore, we acknowledge God as Lord of all, who has a
most absolute right over us and all that is ours, both body and soul. And
because he is Lord of the body no less than of the soul, external adoration in
the body and internal adoration of the mind, which is supremely necessary,
are therefore owed to him. Those who wish all adoration to consist in an
external act of the body are therefore mistaken, for the angels themselves
undoubtedly adore God (Isaiah 6:1–3; Hebrews 1:6), as will the spirits of the
just (Revelation 5:8–10).

*Veneration* comes very near to *adoration* and appears to differ from
*reverence* only in degree. Veneration includes fear and reverence, and it has
regard to some majesty or glory shining forth, or supposed to shine forth, in
the one whom we venerate. Hence a *venerable man* is one who is dignified,
σεμνός, whether in words, deeds, bearing, or gait (Titus 2:2; 1 Timothy 3:8,
11), for σεμνά, venerable or honorable things, are those which pertain to
gravity of character.

To render *honor* to someone means that we think highly of him and are well
disposed toward him, whether because of the virtue with which he is endowed,
the dignity to which he has been raised, benefits conferred upon us, or
something of that kind, and that we attest this disposition of our mind by the
due external signs. These signs are *rising before him, inclining the head,
bending the knees, falling at his feet*, or the like. See Genesis 18:8; 1 Kings
2:19; Luke 14:7; Job 32:6. This honor must be rendered to human beings: to
magistrates (Romans 13:7; 1 Peter 2:17), parents (Exodus 20:12; Malachi 1:6;
Matthew 15:4–5; 19:19; Ephesians 6:2), husbands (Esther 1:20), masters (Malachi
1:6; 1 Timothy 6:1), ministers of the church (1 Timothy 5:17), and elders
(Leviticus 19:32). Indeed, in a certain way it must be rendered to *everyone*
(Romans 12:10, compared with Luke 14:8–10; 1 Peter 2:17), and especially to the
godly (Psalm 15:4). But this honor is distinct from that with which we attend
upon God, nor is it permissible to give human beings the honor which we render
to God, as is evident from Acts 10:25; 14:11–13; Revelation 19:10; 22:8. This
is so even if the holiness apparent in the godly is a certain participation in
the divine nature, because it is only a temporal and created participation,
and not the same holiness which shines forth in God.

To *praise* someone is *to magnify him, acknowledge him as superior, pronounce
him blessed, celebrate, commend, exalt him, and speak well of him*. This is
done with words. But we praise God because of his excellence (Exodus 15:11; 2
Samuel 22:4; Psalms 18; 48:2; 96:4).

Formally, *glory* is the effect of honor and is founded upon renown. Sometimes,
as Ravanella has it under the term, it signifies the *opinion, judgment,
approval, and celebration* of a thing or person, and so is taken for excellent
reputation (2 Kings

[1674 logical printed p. 237 | IA leaf 255 | PDF p. 255]

3:10; Psalm 7:6; 2 Corinthians 6:8), and for an honorable commendation (2 Peter
1:17). Hence to give glory to God is to celebrate him with the highest praises
(1 Chronicles 16:28–29; Psalms 29:1–2; 96:7–8).

*Devotion*, Thomas says in *Summa Theologiae* 2a2ae, question 82, article 1, is
named from devoting; hence those are called *devout* who in some manner devote
themselves to God so as to submit themselves totally to him. “Thus,” he says,
“devotion seems to be nothing other than a certain will to surrender oneself
readily to those things which pertain to God’s service.” Hence also, Cajetan
says at this place, in the Latin language those are called devoted to a
superior who are dedicated to him with their will so disposed that they are
ready to act for him. Properly, the term seems to have regard to God and to
those who in a manner occupy God’s place—princes and one’s country—as our
origins. Hence in the church’s usage, devotion is appropriated to those who
are so disposed toward God that they hold themselves ready for God and the
things of God. Devotion has consequently come to signify the act of this kind
of will by which a person presents himself as ready for divine worship.

Whatever the derivation of *religion* may be, whether from *relegere*, as it
seems to Cicero in book 2 of *On the Nature of the Gods*—“those who diligently
reconsidered and, as it were, reread everything which pertained to the worship
of the gods were called religious from rereading, just as the discriminating
are named from discriminating and the intelligent from understanding, for in
these words there is the same force of reading that is present in
‘religious’”—or from *reeligere*, because through religion we ought to choose
again the God whom we had lost through neglect, as Augustine has it in *The
City of God*, book 10, chapter 4; or from *relinquere*, because sacred things,
as if set apart and removed from common use, are regarded as such because of
their holiness; or from *religare*, as Lactantius has it in book 4, chapter 28:
“We are born under this condition, that we render just and due duties to God
who begot us, know him alone, and follow him alone. Bound to God by this bond
of piety, we are tied to him, whence religion itself received its name.” And
indeed, as Hoornbeeck says in *Practical Theology*, book 9, chapter 4, whatever
may be true of these proposed etymologies, they all readily agree with the
nature of religion. For the religious person diligently rereads everything
that pertains to sacred things, and chooses in them both those things and God
himself as what he must follow. He does not think the worship of God should be
regarded as a common and ordinary thing, since by a singular principle he is
bound and joined to it in this manner. Whatever its origin, I say, religion
always has regard to God and entails an ordering toward him, for he is the one
to whom we ought principally to be bound as the unfailing first principle and
toward whom our choice ought continually to be directed as the ultimate end,
as Thomas says in *Summa Theologiae* 2a2ae, question 81, article 1.

Religion therefore means, first, subjection to God as supreme Lord, and then
the rendering of the honor owed to him, whether by invocation and adoration or
by performing any other sacred duty which tends toward God and advances his
honor. For the good toward which religion is ordered is rendering to God the
honor owed to him because of his singular excellence, who transcends
everything in every way (Revelation 5:12–13; Psalm 100:3; Genesis 18:27;
Leviticus 18:2; Luke 2:13). It therefore also connotes *reverence*, arising
from the recognition and sense of divine excellence and of our lowliness in
relation to God (Hebrews 5:7; 12:28; Philippians 2:12).

Religion has two kinds of acts: some proper and immediate, which it elicits
and by which a person is ordered toward God alone, such as sacrificing,
adoring, praying, and the like; and others mediate, which it produces through
the virtues that it commands, ordering them toward divine reverence, because
the virtue to which the end pertains

[1674 logical printed p. 238 | IA leaf 256 | PDF p. 256]

commands the virtues to which the things directed toward the end pertain. In
this respect religion is truly said to be *visiting orphans and widows*, and
so forth (James 1:27). It accordingly requires all duties of both the first and
second tables toward everyone (Luke 1:75; Romans 14:17–18; Titus 2:12; James
1:26; 1 Corinthians 10:31; Colossians 3:17). Nevertheless, properly and
commonly it is distinguished from justice, which commands duties toward human
beings. It includes all those duties which are owed to God and which are
directly concerned with rendering honor to God. Hence Cicero in book 2 of *On
Invention*: “Religion is that which brings care and ceremony to a certain
superior nature which they call divine.” And in book 2 of *On the Nature of the
Gods*: “Religion which is contained in the pious worship of the gods.” And in
book 2 of *On the Laws*: “To undertake the worship of the gods and pure
religion,” and at the same place, “ceremonies and religious observances toward
the gods.” And in book 1 of *On the Nature of the Gods*: “Piety, holiness,
religion—all of which must be rendered purely and chastely to the divine power
of the gods.” Hence, because religion flows immediately from that faith by
which we believe in God as the sufficient and efficient cause of life, it has
God alone as its immediate object. Therefore, whoever renders this honor to a
creature commits idolatry. Nor is sin committed in this way only toward angels
and saints through open invocation, as happens in the papacy, says the highly
distinguished Hoornbeeck in *Practical Theology*, book 9, chapter 4, “but also
when flatterers transform human beings into idols, or when others attend upon
them with excessive worship, or expect certain benefits from patrons by
submitting to them beyond human measure, or expect what they happen to seek
from them too absolutely, which is not without tacit and true idolatry” (1
Corinthians 7:22–23; Matthew 23:8). “It is indeed permissible to call someone
on earth Father and Teacher, but not such absolutely, and to use the same word
with an earthly and heavenly father in seeking some benefit—and to say, ‘Give
me bread.’ But a difference must be maintained in the manner and intention of
asking, so that religious worship is thereby distinguished from civil: namely,
when you ask it absolutely from God alone, but from human beings with that
restriction that you seek only insofar as they are instruments under God
through whom he bestows those things upon you, and not absolute givers. But
when you look to them in such a way that you ascribe to them either the power
or the absolute intention of giving, this is religious worship, and you
worship the creature above the Creator” (Romans 1:25).

“The proper act of religion,” Ames says in *The Marrow*, book 2, chapter 4, §
23 and following, “is rendering honor to God, and is called worship” (Exodus
12:25–26) “and adoration” (Job 22:29). “For worship must in some manner contain
something good for God, otherwise there would be no observance toward him. No intrinsic
good can be added to God, but the extrinsic good is honor—that is, an
attestation of another’s virtue intended to advance his glory or esteem—and
this is all that a creature can render to God. A fitting or worthy estimation
of God, therefore, and the other acts by which that estimation is manifested
constitute, as it were, the proximate matter of religion. And every honorable
human act, insofar as it can be referred to the honor and glory of God, can be
the matter, or material object, of religion. The very same act which, by reason
of subjection to a precept, is called obedience is called religion and worship
by reason of the honor which it renders to God. The proper manner of religious
honor or worship is to subject the soul itself and the lower affections and
acts of the will to another. For with respect to the soul and its inward acts,
a person is not directly and of himself subjected to any creature, although
just as the soul is connected with the body, so the inward act is connected
with the outward as its necessary condition; and religion also commands the
subjection which is owed to a creature as superior. This honor is owed to God
not only according to the fittingness of the thing, in which sense we say that
the things which he grants out of liberality are owed to him, but also
according to the right of the person to whom it is rendered, and by such a
strict right that it exceeds all justice in respect of obligation, although in
respect of

[1674 logical printed p. 239 | IA leaf 257 | PDF p. 257]

equality it falls far short of justice. Every act of worship, therefore, which
either by its nature or condition, by common law and custom, or by the mind and
institution of the one who renders it gives religious honor to another besides
the true God fashions for itself a new and false God, at least to that extent.”

Taken broadly, *piety* embraces every good duty of religion toward anyone,
whether God, human beings, one’s country, or animals. In this sense it agrees
with the notion of justice taken generally and universally (1 Timothy 2:2; 2
Peter 3:11; 1:3; 1 Timothy 5:4). Taken strictly, however, it denotes the
principal virtue of religion, related to God and to the first table, and in
this sense it is distinguished from the other virtues (Titus 2:12; 2 Peter
1:6). It is the virtue toward God which consists in the religious observance
of every duty owed to him; or the virtue by which we rightly worship God and
the religion prescribed by God; or the Christian virtue by which we worship
God immediately, and which embraces adoration, confidence, invocation, and
thanksgiving (Acts 3:12; 1 Timothy 4:7–8; 6:6, 11; 2 Timothy 3:5; 2 Peter
1:6–7; Titus 1:8; 2:12).

*To observe* is sometimes taken to mean celebrating something with fixed and
solemn rites and ceremonies, as in observing days and months, and so forth
(Galatians 4:10). *Observance* is something general, embracing both religion
toward God and justice toward human beings, and so is the same as obedience,
for to observe sometimes also means to perform or fulfill something. See
Ravanella.

*Reverence* is a kind of worship, honor, and observance joined with a certain
fear and singular humility and submission, both of the mind and the body,
which inferiors must render to one who excels in dignity and power or virtue,
having great regard for him, his words and deeds, and everything which pertains
to him, Ravanella says. It is owed especially to God, who is to be supremely
reverenced (Nehemiah 1:5; 4:14; 9:32; Psalm 47:3; Daniel 9:4) and is worthy to
be praised and celebrated with reverence (Psalm 66:3, 5). He is to be
reverenced above all (Psalm 89:8; 1 Chronicles 16:25; Psalm 96:4). Hence
reverence for God is required of us (Psalm 2:11; Malachi 1:6; Hebrews 12:28),
because his name is to be reverenced (Deuteronomy 28:58; Psalm 99:3; Malachi
1:14; Nehemiah 1:11; Psalm 102:16; Isaiah 59:19).

As for the *worship of God*, it has regard in some manner to everything
mentioned above. For *religious adoration* is its principal part. And it
includes *veneration*, for one who worships God rightly must do so with
reverence and godly fear (Hebrews 12:28) and with fear and trembling (Psalm
2:11). Hence to reverence and adore God is to worship him with fear. Thus one
who rightly worships God *honors* him and celebrates him with the humblest and
most submissive gestures, as in 1 Kings 8:22, 54; 2 Chronicles 6:13; 20:5. We
also worship God by *praising* him with words (Psalm 100:2), certainly when we
ascribe glory to him. And when we are occupied in these exercises sincerely,
diligently, and from the affection of our mind, we show the *devotion* of our
mind. The greatest and principal part of our *religion, piety,* and
*observance*, as stated above, consists in this exercise of divine worship,
pure and according to the prescription of God’s word.

According to the celebrated Voetius, *Ecclesiastical Polity*, part 1, book 2,
treatise 1, chapter 3, page 75, “in the Scholastics’ usage” (on *Summa
Theologiae* 2a2ae, question 81, article 1), “*worship* says more than *honor*,
for it is honor with subjection. Therefore we also honor everyone whom we
worship, but not conversely. For example,

[1674 logical printed p. 240 | IA leaf 258 | PDF p. 258]

God honors the angels and the saints who are his friends, but does not worship
them.” According to Bonaventure, *Commentary on the Sentences*, book 3,
distinction 9, article 2, question 3, the worship of God “is taken for an act
directed to God under the aspect either of the end alone, or also of the
object, or, further, of the honorable object; and in sacred writings this
worship is called λατρεία, λατρεύω, and also θρησκεύω, with which θεοσέβεια
agrees.”

*Worship* comes from the verb *colo*, which properly signifies venerating and
adoring. Consequently, Zanchi says in *On Religion and Its Parts*, book 1, page
342, worship properly signifies either an inward or outward action by which we
venerate and serve God. But unless that word *action* is taken more broadly,
worship is restricted too much—namely, if it is said to consist only in actions
properly so called. For other things constitute part of outward divine
worship: the priestly office itself under the Old Testament was a part of
worship. We will later show that a certain solemn time also constitutes part
of worship. The same can be said of the high priest’s garments, the breastplate,
and so forth. These were not instituted for a physical and natural use—that
is, to ward off injury from the air—and so they were not mere adjuncts of
worship. Instead, they had a religious status and were instituted for the
immediate worship of God.

Therefore to worship God is to render homage and give immediate honor to him.
It is done either *inwardly* alone, through *faith, hope, love*, and so forth,
or *inwardly and outwardly* together, through prayer, adoration, confession,
hearing the word, and so forth.

This immediate honor does not consist in something indivisible, but admits of
degrees. That is, although honor is rendered immediately to God in all
worship, honor is nevertheless rendered more immediately to God in some
exercises of worship than in others. For example, when we worship God through
prayers, thanksgivings, and the like, honor is rendered more immediately to
God than when we hear God’s word proclaimed, because other means intervene in
the latter—the pastor proclaiming the word and the Bible. Notwithstanding
these, honor is rendered immediately to God in that exercise of worship when
the manner prescribed by God is observed, because no divine honor or outward
adoration is owed or ought to be rendered to those means.

Since, therefore, we can attest our homage and subjection to God and render
immediate honor to him through things other than outward actions, it is clear
that those other things also pertain to divine worship and are parts of
outward worship.

Nevertheless, the following things are required in every legitimate outward
worship. *First*, the intention of the one acting, without which the outward
action is not to be regarded as worship. For example, if someone repeated
certain words of prayer in order to teach a boy to pray or explained the
meaning of the words to a foreigner, he would not be worshiping God; although,
if he repeated the same words while praying, he would be regarded as a
worshiper. Thus bodily prostration with the intention of honoring God, as in
prayers, is a religious act. Yet the same prostration performed with another
intention, such as picking up a straw or rendering honor to a human being, is
not a religious act. *Second*, what is performed must be commanded by God. For
if this condition is lacking, although the outward action can be regarded as a
religious act and as worship, it will not be legitimate worship but rather
idolatrous, superstitious, and unlawful. *Third*, it is required that the act
by the nature of the thing and the condition of the work be referred to the
honor of God, and that immediately. When I perform duties of justice and
equity toward my neighbor, although I thus honor God mediately, those duties
by the nature of the thing and the condition of the work tend immediately
toward

[1674 logical printed p. 241 | IA leaf 259 | PDF p. 259]

advancing my neighbor’s good. They are therefore not acts of worship properly
so called, although even in performing these duties I ought to keep God’s
honor before my eyes. But when I praise or pray to God, God is worshiped both
by the intention of the one acting and by the intention of the work, and that
act by the intrinsic nature of the thing tends toward God’s honor, even if no
one else is edified by that act.

Legitimate divine worship—for there is no need to speak here of
illegitimate—is commonly distinguished into *natural and instituted*. *The
former is prescribed in the first commandment*, not only insofar as it is
inward but also insofar as it is outward. For, Ames says in *The Marrow*, book
2, chapter 5: “1. All observance is the same inwardly and outwardly; therefore
the same worship, both inward and outward, is contained in the same precept.
2. In each of the precepts that pertain to the second table, inward and
outward obedience are prescribed together in each, according to Christ’s own
interpretation in Matthew 5. Much more, therefore, is this true in the
precepts of the first table, and in the first and principal one among them. 3.
If that distinction were legitimate, and the first precept concerned inward
worship alone and the second outward worship alone, then the first precept
would bind only the inward person and the soul to obedience, and the second
only the outward person and the body, which is contrary to every reason.” The
latter worship, which embraces all the means freely instituted by God for
advancing and exercising natural worship, is prescribed *in the second*
commandment. To *the former* belong faith, hope, love, and similar Christian
virtues, as well as prayers, thanksgivings, and so forth. To *the latter*
belong the hearing, proclamation, and reading of the word, the administration
of the sacraments, religious fasts, the making of vows, and so forth.

Again, worship is distinguished into *solitary and social*. *The former* is
performed by one person alone; *the latter* by several together. The latter
can be subdivided into *fixed and occasional*. *Occasional worship* is that
which is performed not at a fixed time, but as the matter arises or as an
occasion is given by those, namely, who are not fixed members of some
particular society but come together occasionally to worship God. *Fixed
worship* can in turn be subdivided into *domestic, ecclesiastical,* and
*fraternal*. We call that worship *domestic* which is celebrated in a
household, and that *ecclesiastical* which is performed solemnly in an
assembled church. *Fraternal worship* is that which is practiced by those who
perhaps are not members of the same particular church or the same household,
and yet come together at a fixed time to praise God.

*Solitary* worship is also distinguished into *private and solemn*. *Private
solitary* worship is the same as *ejaculatory* or *instantaneous* worship,
which can be performed in an instant or in the midst of other outward actions.
*Solemn solitary* worship is that which is performed more solemnly, with all
other business set aside, by a person withdrawn or called away from other
lawful business with respect both to body and soul.

It is consequently clear that all *social* worship is solemn, but not all
*solitary* worship. Only that solitary worship is solemn which is performed
solemnly, when the person who worships God calls himself away from every other
occupation, presents his whole self to God in both soul and body, and for some
period of time devotes himself to worshiping, celebrating, or invoking God,
and so forth.

Worship is also distinguished in another way into *Levitical* or *legal*,
which had a place under the Old Testament, and *evangelical*, which obtains
today under the New Testament and is called rational and spiritual worship,
relatively—that is, in comparison with legal worship, which in practice was more

[1674 logical printed p. 242 | IA leaf 260 | PDF p. 260]

carnal. See Romans 12:1; 1 Peter 2:5. It is also distinguished into *inward and
outward*, *ordinary and extraordinary*, and *private and public*. But the
earlier distinctions are more useful for our purpose.

Worshiping God is expressed in various ways in the Holy Scriptures: *drawing
near to God* (Hebrews 11:6; 4:16; 10:22); *cleaving to the Lord* (Deuteronomy
4:5; 10:20; 11:22; 13:4; 30:20; Joshua 22:5; 23:8; Jeremiah 13:11; Psalms 63:9;
72:28); *setting one’s heart to seek God* (1 Chronicles 22:19); *setting one’s
face toward God* (Daniel 9:3); *joining oneself to God* (Isaiah 56:3, 6);
*adoring God, acknowledging God* (Isaiah 19:21; 1 Chronicles 28:9; Psalms 9:11;
79:6); *loving God; walking before God* (1 Kings 2:4; 3:6); *walking with God*
(Micah 6:6); *walking in the name of God* (Micah 4:5); *approaching God*
(Isaiah 45:20); *stretching out one’s hands to God; bending the knees before
God* (Isaiah 45:23; Romans 14:11; Ephesians 3:14; Ezra 9:5; Psalm 95:6);
*calling upon God*, or the name of God (Genesis 4:26; 12:8; 13:4; 21:33;
Jeremiah 10:25; Psalm 14:4, and frequently elsewhere); *swearing by the name of
God* (Deuteronomy 6:13; 12:20; Jeremiah 4:2; 12:16; Isaiah 19:18; 45:23);
*observing God* (Zechariah 11:11); *seeking God* (Deuteronomy 12:5; 1 Samuel
13:16; 1 Chronicles 10:14; 15:13; 21:30; 22:19; 2 Chronicles 11:16, and
frequently elsewhere); *reverencing God* or God’s name (Ecclesiastes 5:6;
12:15; Isaiah 25:3; Jeremiah 5:22, 24); and, frequently, *fearing God*.
