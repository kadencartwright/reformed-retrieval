QUESTION VI

Are the attributes rightly distinguished into communicable and
incommunicable? Affirmative.

I. Among the various distinctions of the divine attributes, none occurs more
frequently than that by which they are divided into communicable and
incommunicable. Since this distinction is not equally accepted by everyone, a
few things must be said about it.

[Marginal heading: Communicable and incommunicable attributes.]

II. To understand the distinction, note that communication is twofold: one is
essential and formal through the intrinsic being of the thing; the other is
communication by likeness and analogy, with respect to effects and works. With
respect to the former, we say that all God's properties are equally
incommunicable, since they can no more be communicated than the divine
essence; otherwise they would cease to be properties. But we acknowledge that
the latter can occur when God produces in creatures, especially rational
ones, effects analogous to his properties, such as goodness, justice, wisdom,
and so forth.

III. In this sense, those attributes may rightly be called incommunicable
strictly and in every way which are so proper to God that nothing similar or
analogous to them, and no image or trace of them, can be found in creatures.
Such are the negative attributes, which remove from God every imperfection of
creatures, such as infinity, immensity, and eternity. These are such that
either their absence or their contrary inheres in every creature. Other
attributes, however, are not wrongly called communicable when their likeness
or certain dim traces of them are detected in creatures, and this is by a
simple analogy of name and effects. Such are the affirmative attributes, which
are attributed to God by way of eminence or causality.

IV. The communicable attributes are not predicated of God and creatures
univocally, because there is not one account, as there is in things simply
univocal which agree in name and definition. Nor are they predicated
equivocally, because the account is not completely

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 172 | IA leaf 226 | PDF p. 226]

different, as in things purely equivocal which agree only in name. Rather,
they are predicated analogically, by an analogy both of likeness and of
attribution. The former occurs when things are called by the same name because
of a certain likeness which they have to one another. The latter occurs when
one name is so attributed to several things that it is said of one primarily
and principally, or prior in order, but of the others secondarily and
posterior in order because of their dependence upon that first one. Thus these
attributes are predicated of God essentially and in an altogether singular
mode, that is, infinitely and most perfectly, and therefore even in the
abstract. It is in this sense that God alone is called good, Matt. 19:17—that
is, originally, independently, and essentially. But they are predicated of
creatures only secondarily, accidentally, and by participation.

V. Believers are called partakers of the divine nature, 2 Pet. 1:4, not
univocally through formal participation in the divine essence, but only
analogically through the benefit of regeneration. Regeneration imprints upon
them the marks of holiness and of the justice that most properly belongs to
God when they are renewed according to the image of their Creator, Col. 3:10.
In this way one must understand what is often read in the Fathers, when they
say that the regenerate person is deified (θεοῦσθαι) and made godlike
(θεοειδῆ), conformed to God—not essentially, which is said of the Son alone,
but analogically.

VI. The fact that certain attributes are called communicable and
incommunicable implies no inequality among the divine properties, as though
some were more proper to God than others, because they all equally belong to
him essentially. Rather, this commends God's goodness and glory, because he
willed to express in his creatures a certain likeness and little shadow-image
(ἀποσκιασμάτιον) of his virtues.

VII. That distinction cannot favor the error of those who contend that the
divine properties have been communicated to Christ's human nature, because,
as will be seen in its proper place, one thing is communication *in concreto*,
with respect to the person, which we acknowledge in Christ; another is
communication *in abstracto*, with respect to the nature, which we deny. One
thing is formal and intransitive communication through a transfusion into
Christ's human nature of the same properties which are in God; this we reject.
Another is transitive and effective communication through analogy, which we
maintain occurs in rational creatures with respect to the affirmative
attributes.
