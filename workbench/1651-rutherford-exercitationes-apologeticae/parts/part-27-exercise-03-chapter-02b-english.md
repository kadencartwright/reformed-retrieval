7. For we distinguish efficacious grace from predetermination in this way: the
whole efficacy of grace is situated in this predetermination. We, however, do
not wish

[1651 logical printed p. 412 | IA leaf 430 | PDF p. 430]

grace to be efficacious because of the outcome or the free cooperation of the
will, but both because of the nature of predetermining grace and because of the
intention of God who calls. We prove this from these Scriptures, Deuteronomy
29:4: *You have seen the signs, mighty works, and trials, etc.* Why, then, were
they not converted? Surely if Moses had been of the Arminian faith, it would
have been enough for him to answer briefly, “Because you stiff-necked Jews
refused to obey.” Yet he gives the reason for this deepest mystery not from
them but from God himself: *But* (he says) *God did not give you a heart to
understand.* Corvinus replies: God could not obtain from you that he should give
you a heart to understand, for you were stiff-necked. Reply 1. What could be
more forced? Indeed, he did obtain that he should give it, and, as far as he
could without injury to justice, he acted toward the actual giving, as other
Arminians teach. 2. What a distorted answer! “God did not give to you,” that
is, “you did not receive.” Is this to expound, or rather to posit, *to give in
place of to receive, the related thing in place of its correlate*? “He is not
a father,” that is, “he is not a son.” The Remonstrants reply: *He gave because
he did all those things which were by far the most apt to produce in them an
understanding heart, unless we wish God to complain about himself.* Reply 1.
But tell us what things are by far the most apt to produce an understanding
heart: signs, miracles, and the word? Then he gave Pharaoh a new heart in this
way. 2. Nor are these by far the most apt, because they only rouse externally
and suasively; they do not add new powers to an impaired and weak liberty.
Indeed, internal grace is far more apt, but you wish there to be profound
silence about so great a grace. 3. It does not follow that God complains about
himself, but about the

[Marginalia: *Apology*, chapter 17, folio 165.]

[1651 logical printed p. 413 | IA leaf 431 | PDF p. 431]

Jews, who abused the external means—unless you wish God to be obliged by
justice to give an understanding heart. But by what law? You must teach this.

8. *Isaiah 44:3: I will pour water upon the thirsty, and streams upon the dry
ground; I will pour my Spirit upon your seed, and my blessing upon your
offspring; and they will flourish like grass and like willows beside the
courses of waters.* What is the reason why they flourish in works of holiness,
as is explained in what follows? It is the outpouring of the Holy Spirit upon
them; therefore determining grace and its efficacy are proved. The
Remonstrants reply: *It is certain that temporal blessings are treated in that
passage.* Reply: It is certain that they are blind. “I will pour out my Spirit”
cries something else. One will say, *I am Jehovah; another will call himself
not of Jacob*, that is, he will join himself to the true church. Therefore
spiritual grace is treated there. 2. The Remonstrants reply: *He addresses
upright Israel; therefore grace necessary for conversion is not treated.*
Reply: the inference does not follow. The promise was made to the seed of
Jacob. What then? Is seed not yet born upright? Is an accident prior to its
subject? The same account applies to the grace of conversion and to grace
following conversion. For both undeviatingly determine the will of God's
people to grow like watered plants in good works, *to subscribe with the hand
to Jehovah*, and to believe the gospel, as appears in verse 5. Otherwise God
promises what it does not rest with him to perform. 3. The Remonstrants reply:
Determining grace is not promised, because this grace is promised to upright
Israel,

[Marginalia: *Apology*, folio 182; first reply of the Remonstrants.]

[Marginalia: Second reply of the Remonstrants.]

[Marginalia: Third reply of the Remonstrants.]

[1651 logical printed p. 414 | IA leaf 432 | PDF p. 432]

because at that time Israel lacked it and needed it. But this determining
grace already had a place at that time in upright Israel, for Israel was not
upright without that grace. Reply 1. All these things are lighter than smoke.
The promise is made not only to upright Israel, but also to seed not yet born,
much less upright. 2. Determining grace in a smaller measure was not then
lacking to Israel; therefore no greater determining grace is promised under
the Messiah. This does not follow. The ancient people did not lack faith in
Christ, Hebrews 11. Does it follow that nowhere in the prophets is a greater
and more copious faith promised? Then let the whole Old Testament be taken
away. 4. The Remonstrants reply: *Nor does it help if you say that the calling
of the Gentiles is promised here, for the Gentiles are not the seed of Israel.
Then what would be the reason for the preceding exhortation: Do not fear, my
servant Jacob, for at some time I will also call the Gentiles to faith, say,
after eight hundred years or 100 years? What? Did Israel fear that this would
happen, etc.?* Reply: Are such great masters not ashamed of such trifles? The
Gentiles are the spiritual seed of Israel. Romans 2:29: *But he is a Jew who
is one inwardly, etc.* Romans 9:8: *Not the children of the flesh are children
of God, but the children of the promise are counted as seed.* Indeed, the
Philippians *who worship God by the Spirit are the circumcision*, Philippians
3:3. Have you forgotten the Scriptures? 2. The reason for the exhortation is
most fitting, and it is no small consolation to Jews almost devoured by their
enemies. For in chapter 43:28 God had given Jacob over to cursing and Israel to
reproaches. Thus, on account of the weakness of their faith, they could plainly
lose heart

[Marginalia: Fourth reply of the Remonstrants.]

[1651 logical printed p. 415 | IA leaf 433 | PDF p. 433]

and think that the church of God would almost fail. God comforts the faithful
because their seed would extend its branches beside the courses of waters
through the calling of the Gentiles. Thus Isaiah 54:1, 2, 3, etc.; Galatians
4:27; Isaiah 2:1, 2, 3. I would say that anyone who asserted that the calling
of the Gentiles was no consolation to the Jews had scarcely read the
Scriptures. 5. The Remonstrants reply in their book: *The promise is directed
to the whole people, but that peculiar grace does not belong to the whole
people. Finally, if the promise is said to belong only to certain few from the
Gentiles, specifically elected by God from eternity, what consolation was this
by which God wished to raise Israel up against fear, etc.?* Reply 1. Nothing is
colder than this common refrain. You must say whether, by God's intention and
decree, evangelical promises apply to the reprobate; we deny it. 2. The promise
of determining grace is no small consolation to the elect. We say that there
is no consolation for the reprobate in the Scriptures. What do they have to do
with peace, consolation, and the joy of the Holy Spirit? 3. Say from your
consciences what consolation any sons of Adam receive from the promise of that
grace against all the assaults and temptations of this life, the world, sin,
the devil, every affliction, death, and the terror of hell, when, with that
grace posited, it can happen that they all rush into eternal destruction and
that all temptations end for them in the utmost evil, provided that, as they
freely can, they bend the will to assent to the temptation. Surely they must
seek consolation here in free choice, or in the new goddess Contingency or

[Marginalia: Fifth reply of the Remonstrants.]

[1651 logical printed p. 416 | IA leaf 434 | PDF p. 434]

Fortune, not in God, grace, and providence. Do you wish this to be the
consolation of Christians? It is as Arminius says: *We cannot be cast down from
the Rock unless we willingly desert our station*—as if what must chiefly be
feared were that we should be forced by violence to defect from the faith and,
compelled by temptations, should will to yield. That is to fear that heaven may
fall or that God may not be God. For to will under compulsion is to will and
not to will; that is, we must fear lest we defect if we have defected. Indeed,
the consolation of demons and men is equal, and their guardianship by God lest
they sin is equal, provided only that they are unwilling to sin. Thus demons,
and thus those who have now fallen into sin against the Holy Spirit, are not
deprived of Arminian consolation. I add, for example, that by this account the
damned in hell have consolation in abundance. Doctors, I ask, make very sure
that the damned do not will anything under compulsion—namely, that they do not
despair unless they will to despair!

9. *Deuteronomy 30:9: Jehovah will circumcise your heart and the heart of your
seed, to love the Lord your God with your whole heart.* If to circumcise is not
to cut away the foreskin of the heart and the callus of hardening by which we
resist God, we desire the Arminians to teach us what it is. Reply 1.
Circumcision is promised on the hypothesis that they have turned to God in
verses 1 and 10 and have heard the voice of Jehovah. Answer 1. But circumcision
itself is conversion; therefore conversion is promised on the hypothesis that
they have been converted—that is, “I will circumcise if I have circumcised.”
For in Colossians 2:11, circumcision is the removal of the body of sin. 2. It
says in verse 2, *If you listen to the voice of Jehovah, you*

[Marginalia: *Against Perkins*, p. 758.]

[1651 logical printed p. 417 | IA leaf 435 | PDF p. 435]

*and your children, in all that I command you, with your whole heart and with
your whole soul.* What is lacking here for perfect circumcision? Surely
nothing. Therefore they were circumcised before they were circumcised. 3. If
that antecedent conversion is a free act, which cannot be denied, then, once
one free act is posited, the will is undeviatingly determined to another free
act. Otherwise it can happen that a man fulfills the condition by completing
that antecedent conversion, while God does not circumcise the heart as he
promises, which plainly conflicts with God's truthfulness. Therefore they must
acknowledge determining grace. 2. The Arminians reply that by “heart” the
affections, not the will, are designated. Reply: By “heart” is understood that
by which men obey the voice of Jehovah in all things, verse 2, and by which
they love God above all things so that they may live eternally, which is the
fulfillment of the law. For if the Arminians wish men to render perfect
obedience with mind and affections to the voice of Jehovah, so that they may
live, verse 6, *so that Jehovah may rejoice in doing them good as a beloved
people, as in verse 9*, while the will is excluded, then men obey the voice of
Jehovah under compulsion and are dragged by force to eternal life. Would it
not have been better to keep this silent?

10. *Ezekiel 36:23: I will also give you a new heart, and I will put a new
spirit in your midst.* This disposes of two Arminian doubts. The first is this:
“No indeed, Lord, by your antecedent will you are ready to give a heart of
flesh, provided that we do not oppose a heart of stone to you when you give.”
Reply: No indeed;

[1651 logical printed p. 418 | IA leaf 436 | PDF p. 436]

it will not rest with you to oppose and resist the giver. *For I will take the
heart of stone away from you*, so that you will not wish to resist me as I
give. What could be clearer? 2. The second Arminian doubt: “After you have
implanted in us, Lord, a new heart by which we are able to will well, it can
happen that we do not actually obey, since liberty is not determined to good
acts.” Verse 27 disposes of it in this way: *I will put my Spirit in your midst
and cause you to walk in my commandments.* The Remonstrants and Corvinus reply:
God does not move the will undeviatingly and absolutely, for the people of the
*New Testament*, in whom the promise was fulfilled, remained uncircumcised in
neck and heart, Acts 7. They did not accept the day of salvation, etc. But he
does this only for those whose heart does not walk after abominable things.
Reply 1. The gloss conflicts with the text, since God takes away that very
heart of stone which walks after abominable things. And let them say what the
difference is between a heart of stone, an uncircumcised heart, and a heart
that does not accept the day of salvation. Surely these are the same. Yet God
both took away and did not take away the heart of stone. Nor are the
uncircumcised people of Acts 7 and this people in Ezekiel 36 the same; they
foolishly beg the question. For they ought to prove that the heart of stone and
uncircumcised remained in the same people—that is, in the numerically same men
in whom God promises that he will produce a new heart. They lay this down by
dictate; they do not prove it. 2. A circumcised heart which does not walk after
abominations is the thing promised. It is therefore not the

[Marginalia: The Remonstrants in the synodical writings, article 4, p. 165, and
the *Apology*, folio 164; Corvinus, *Against Molina*, chapter 33, folio 22.]

[1651 logical printed p. 419 | IA leaf 437 | PDF p. 437]

antecedent condition for fulfilling the promise. For one and the same thing is
not both the condition of the thing promised and the thing promised. The thing
promised is future. For God does not now promise the creation of the world or
Noah's deliverance in the ark. But an antecedent condition has being first and
is fulfilled before God causes the promised thing actually to exist. 3. This
promise was made to those who had shed blood in their land, verse 18, *and had
profaned God's name among the Gentiles, verse 20; and verse 32 says: I do not do
these things for your sake, says Jehovah God; let it be known to you. Be
ashamed and confounded, O house of Israel, because of your ways which are not
good.* No indeed (the Arminians say), there is no reason for them to be ashamed
of their former life, for they cast away abominable things before God created
a new heart in them. This matter is surely worthy of praise, not shame.
Accordingly, the Arminians speak thus: “For your sake and for your good ways,
inasmuch as you have cast away abominable things, I do these things—that is, I
implant a new heart and take away the stony one. But in those whose heart walks
after abominable things I do not put a new heart.” What could be more contrary
to the Holy Spirit? 4. God promises that they will be cleansed from all
defilements. Therefore either resisting God as he gives a new heart is no
defilement, or they are cleansed from that hardness. 5. What the Arminians call
the antecedent condition of a new heart, God calls the effect of the new heart.
Verse 31: “Then”—namely, after I have implanted a new heart in you—“you will
remember your evil ways and loathe yourselves,” that is, you will cast away
your detestable things.

[1651 logical printed p. 420 | IA leaf 438 | PDF p. 438]

The Remonstrants' second reply: This promise is made to the whole people, but
the promise of determining grace is made only to the few who are absolutely
elect, not to the whole people, since in Jeremiah 31 and Hebrews 8 it is made
to the house of Israel and Judah. For by each house are designated those in
whom there was an old heart and spirit, in whom the heart was not fleshly but
stony. If the elect are designated by them, then there were none among the
people at that time who had a new and fleshly heart when this promise was made.
But apart from the elect, there were none among the people except the
reprobate. Yet neither the reprobate nor the elect had a fleshly heart, because
otherwise there would have been no need for it to be given to them. Therefore
no one had it. Answer 1. These are mere conjectures, and all false. This
promise is not made to the whole people according to God's intention, but is
set before the visible church and, according to God's intention, is made and
fulfilled in the elect alone. Who could doubt that the house of true Israel
and Judah, circumcised in heart, designates the elect alone? Romans 11:26–27:
*All Israel will be saved.* For in Jeremiah 31:33 the promise is made to those
in whose hearts God was going to engrave the law and whose sins God was going
to forgive, so that he would no longer remember their sins; and in Ezekiel 36
he speaks most plainly of the church of the elect. 2. Further, if it is made to
the whole people according to God's intention, each and every one will be
sprinkled with pure water, will obtain remission of sins, will loathe
themselves because of their sins, and in verse 29 will be freed from all
uncleannesses, and consequently from placing an obstacle before God as he
calls, which is no slight uncleanness. This is

[Marginalia: Second reply of the Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 17, p. 164.
Suarez responds in the same way, book 3, *On the Helps of Divine Grace*,
chapter 15, folio 8.]

[1651 logical printed p. 421 | IA leaf 439 | PDF p. 439]

absurd. 3. It is false that the promise is made simply to those in whom there
was an old and stony heart, so that they were altogether unregenerate. For a
new heart is often promised to those who have a new heart, and this promise
was being made in part to that people then present. For Christ says that it is
given to the one who has, Luke 11:9. God gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask.
I ask whether anyone can ask for the Holy Spirit without faith and therefore
without the Holy Spirit. He cannot. Therefore if God gives the Holy Spirit to
one who has the Holy Spirit, he can promise a new heart to one who has a new
heart. The Remonstrants' second reply: Since this promise is said to take
effect after many days, and therefore only in the times of the Messiah, as the
writer to the Hebrews indicates not obscurely, then no one would have been
converted up to those times. Reply: Nothing could be more childish. The
Remonstrants do not deny that a new heart is produced suasively and resistibly,
and that this is promised in that passage. Therefore, from the Arminian
position it follows that no one was converted up to the times of the Messiah.
For in the times of the Messiah a new heart is given in a suasive manner. Thus
this difficulty presses them. 2. Although this promise would take its fullest
effect in the times of the Messiah, as in Hebrews 8, it does not follow that
God did not fulfill that promise daily in the Jewish church, but more sparingly
according to the measure of grace, whose fullness was to be reserved for the
times of the Messiah. Isaiah 40:4; Luke 3:6; Joel 2:28; Acts 2:16–17. But who
is so ignorant as to assert that there was no Holy Spirit and no new heart in
the saints before the coming of Christ, because a more abundant

[Marginalia: Second reply of the Remonstrants.]

[1651 logical printed p. 422 | IA leaf 440 | PDF p. 440]

outpouring of the Spirit is promised under the Messiah? 3. The Remonstrants
add that from our opposing position it follows that what had always happened
before was promised for the times of the Messiah, for grace which physically
determines always physically determines. Reply: I turn it back upon them. It
follows that grace operating upon a new heart suasively and in a manner open
to deviation is promised, and therefore that what had always happened from the
beginning of the world is promised for the times of the Messiah. For grace
which operates in a manner open to deviation always operates in such a manner.
2. It follows quite well that a greater and more abundant determining grace is
promised, since it is extended to more people—to young men, old men, virgins,
servants, etc., Joel 2:28. Is what had always happened from the beginning of
the world therefore not promised? 4. The Remonstrants reply in folio 165 of the
*Apology*: *Then you must say that all those who lived at that time and were to
die during those times were not only reprobates but also knew—or at least could
have known from the sense of this promise—that each and every one of them had
been eternally reprobated and shut out from the privilege of that grace by a
peremptory decree.* Reply 1. This follows in no way, for many among those who
then lived had a new heart and a new spirit, although the fullest promise was
to be reserved for Christ. For they suppose the falsehood that God gave no one
a new heart before Christ's advent, which is utterly false. 2. From the
Arminian doctrine of middle knowledge it follows that neither more nor fewer
could freely receive a new heart than those who actually receive it. Given

[Marginalia: Third reply of the Remonstrants.]

[Marginalia: Fourth reply of the Remonstrants.]

[1651 logical printed p. 423 | IA leaf 441 | PDF p. 441]

that God willed it most intensely, once the peremptory volitions of men are
posited—whose futurition precedes both God's knowledge and his decree—it
follows that God has been peremptorily excluded from every possibility of
saving Judas or any others who are damned, or of destroying Peter or any
others who are saved, unless he wishes to renounce his justice and
truthfulness. For how can he save Judas, or will peremptorily that Judas be
saved, when the futurition of all Judas's wicked acts is prior both to God's
knowledge and to his decree? This is truly horrifying.

5. The Remonstrants and Corvinus reply that the promise must be understood of
such a mode of operation as was going to possess a singular and wonderful
power for newly kindling and inflaming the hearts of the whole people—although
otherwise very alienated from observing the divine commandments—with love of
God and of the divine laws, and thus for always retaining them in duty and
faith; yet in such a way that the matter, although not probably, could still
turn out otherwise—that is, so that either the heart was not inflamed with that
love, or, once inflamed, grew cold again and returned to its natural
disposition. So they say. Corvinus says that this promise concerns an
accumulation of God's benefits which cannot fail to move them to obedience
unless they have cast off all fear of God, and that the particle ו does not
always designate the event and effect, but often the end and intention.
Ezekiel 24:13: *I cleansed you, and you were not*

[Marginalia: Fifth reply of the Remonstrants; Remonstrants in the synodical
writings, article 4.b, p. 113, and in the *Apology*, folios 165–166; Corvinus,
*Against Molina*, chapter 46, folio 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 424 | IA leaf 442 | PDF p. 442]

*cleansed.* Jeremiah 15:9: *We treated Babylon, and she was not treated.* Thus
false teachers are said to cause people to trust in a lie, Jeremiah 29:31, *and
the Canaanite is said to call the sons of the Israelites after himself*,
Deuteronomy 7:4. Reply 1. They give us a singular manner by which God inflames
hearts to obey, even though the contrary can occur, albeit not probably. Who
knows what “not probably” means? Namely, not with probability—because God moves
the will while ignorant of the future free act, yet by a probable or plausible
conjecture God opines, if the omen does not deceive, that they will be
persuaded. In other words, the Arminians here wish God to construct rhetorical
syllogisms as if he were an ignorant novice in logic. Away to hell with such
blasphemies! 2. “Yet in such a way that the matter could turn out otherwise,”
that is, so that the heart might not be inflamed with that love: thus they
enervate the whole singular and wonderful power, since that power accomplishes
nothing but leaves the heart uninflamed. Therefore, with that promise posited,
God as much does not give a new heart as he gives it. Indeed, if the Arminian
fictions stand, this is as much a most severe threat as a gracious promise,
proceeding as much from justice as from mercy. For if God employs such a
wonderful power in producing the affection of a new heart, from which a stony
heart and a perverse and stubborn resistance made against God as he regenerates
follow just as much as a new heart and new spirit, then God equally threatens
them with a heart of stone as he promises a new heart. Then these promises are
ridiculous and truly hypocritical, and then

[1651 logical printed p. 425 | IA leaf 443 | PDF p. 443]

that statement in verse 32, “I do not do these things for your sake,” must
surely return to this sense: “For your sake and your evil ways I take the stony
heart from you.” Consequently, they are hardened not because of their sins but
because of God's glorious name. The Remonstrants do not dare assert this. But
many gracious benefits are promised here: that God wills to sanctify himself,
verse 23; that peace of conscience and true tranquility are promised, verse
28; sorrow according to God, verse 31; remission of sins, verses 28 and 33;
that their prayers will be heard, verse 37; that the nations will acknowledge
God to be their God, verse 36; and that he predicts that it will certainly be
so in verse 36: *Because* (he says) *I, Jehovah, have spoken, and I will
perform it.* All these things, I say, designate that it will happen most
certainly—not probably and plausibly, but most certainly—that a new heart and a
new spirit will be implanted in them; and if most certainly, then
undeviatingly. For all certainty of divine knowledge supposes a necessary
object. 3. From the Arminian position, how ridiculous and theatrical would
these sweetest consolations be? Namely: “I will deal with you in such a way
that it can happen that my name is not sanctified but profaned; that you are
not cleansed from uncleannesses; that a heart of stone remains in you; that a
new heart is not in you; that you walk not in my commandments but in your evil
ways; that you are not touched by sorrow and loathing for your ill-spent past
life; that you become prey to the Gentiles; and that the land is left to
desolation.” What sort of consolation is this, and how meager! Who could
distinguish between God

[1651 logical printed p. 426 | IA leaf 444 | PDF p. 444]

consoling and thundering judgments, between him graciously and evangelically
promising and brandishing the dire thunderbolts of legal threats? 4. When they
say, “yet in such a way that the matter could turn out otherwise,” they
sufficiently imply that God gives only the power to make oneself a new heart
and the power to walk in God's commandments. But what could be more
contradictory to the text, “*I will cause you to walk, etc.*”? Namely, this is
to explain act by potency and walking by its contradictory, not walking—which
is to turn the Holy Scriptures upside down. But I ask whether God's people may
pray for the new heart promised here. They will not deny it, I think. And then
it follows that the faithful pray for the power to obey, which we heard them
deny above. 5. Corvinus teaches that this is produced by an accumulation of
benefits, and the Remonstrants that it happens by a singular and wonderful
power. Yet here there is nothing about the infusion of supernatural power,
nothing about internal grace. The whole matter is accomplished by benefits
which move through moral persuasion. But when he led the people out of Egypt,
what benefit could be more excellent? When, to deliver them and enrich them
with the spoils of their enemies, he repeatedly transformed nature by very
many miracles, divided the Red Sea, led them with light and fire shining from
heaven, and submerged their enemies, had he given them a new heart and
inscribed the law upon their heart? God plainly denies it, Jeremiah 31:31–32.
Did he not then give Moabites, Ammonites, and Chaldeans a new heart in the same
way? 6. Corvinus says these

[1651 logical printed p. 427 | IA leaf 445 | PDF p. 445]

benefits could have moved them unless they had cast off all fear of God. But
who doubts that those in whom there is a stony heart, as there is in the
unregenerate, have cast off all fear of God? But what is it to cast off all
fear of God? I believe it is nothing other than for men to be so stony that
they are unwilling to be moved to obedience and unwilling to walk in God's
commandments. Corvinus's sophistry therefore comes to this: they could have
failed to cast off all fear of God, and could have been moved to obey, if they
had not cast off all fear of God and had not been unmoved to obedience; they
could have cast away the heart of stone if they had not retained it; and God
could have given them a new heart if they had not refused. What dizziness of a
deranged brain is this? 7. To say that the particle ו designates not the event
but an end from which God can fall short is to say that God is truthful in his
promises provided that men are willing. What could be more absurd? Nor is it
enough if they say these promises are conditional—namely, that God promises to
give a new heart provided they are willing to receive it. But do they receive
a new heart with a heart of stone? It would be monstrous to say. If they
receive a new heart with a new heart, then they have a new heart before they
have it—I speak of men at the first moment of conversion—which is absurd. Or
they receive a new heart by pure nature, which is Pelagianism. Let them give a
fourth alternative. But I ask about the condition required of us, namely, “if
we are willing.” This means that God produces a new willingness in us if he
produces in us

[1651 logical printed p. 428 | IA leaf 446 | PDF p. 446]

a new willingness. 8. The passages of Scripture cited are altogether
heterogeneous, as if God did not produce a new heart in the elect more
efficaciously than Babylon was treated by threats and external benefits, or
with an efficacy equal to that by which the Canaanite and the devil call men
away from God. This strongly smacks of Pelagian grace. 9. I ask whether such a
doubt could ever have entered the mind of God's people: that God would give
them a new heart provided they did not resist, but only if they themselves
wished to have it—as if they had feared that he would give them a new heart
while they resisted and were unwilling. Indeed, in this way he gives a new
heart to the devils whom he endures with great patience, and to all nations.

11. The Remonstrants add in the *Apology*: This promise in the same chapter,
11:17, *I will give you a heart of flesh*, is alleged as the reason why it is
said that they would remove every detestable thing, and yet it is added, “And
as for those whose heart walks after their abominations, I will repay their
way upon their head.” But the threats are ridiculous if a promised efficacy is
maintained which was going to determine their will to remove the abominations.
Answer 1. There is a manifest distinction between the elect in verse 17 and
the reprobate, of whom verse 21 speaks. And that promise is not only the reason
but also the efficacious cause why they remove detestable things. Threats
cohere perfectly well with such efficacy, as do commandments, since those who
lack such efficacy most freely approve the lack of such efficacy—or rather,
they love its absence—and therefore are justly punished. And perfectly well

[1651 logical printed p. 429 | IA leaf 447 | PDF p. 447]

there remains a place for commandments, since the elect most freely approve
such efficacy. Nor is there any conflict in someone being unable not to obey
and yet being commanded to obey. This is clear in Christ, who could not fail
to lay down his life for his sheep and yet testifies that he received from the
Father this command to lay down his life, John 10:17–18; and in Paul, who
obeyed the command, as Acts 26:19 compared with Acts 9:11 and 20 shows. Yet the
Jesuits and Arminians wish that he could not fail to obey, because of the
extraordinary motion by which they teach that he was actuated.

12. *Jeremiah 24:7: I will give them a heart to know me, and they will return
to me with their whole heart. Jeremiah 32:40: I will establish with them an
everlasting covenant; I will not turn away from them, that I may do them good.*
But the Arminians cry that it rests with us to depart from God, since the free
will is not undeviatingly bent by God. It seems to me that the Holy Spirit most
explicitly contradicted them here when he adds, to establish the perpetuity of
the covenant on the part of both contracting parties: *And I will put my fear
in their hearts, that they may not depart from me.* Thus Jeremiah 31:33, 37,
39; Isaiah 54:10; Isaiah 50:20–21. Indeed, also God's covenant established by
an oath, Hebrews 6:13, most clearly demonstrates that this new covenant cannot
fail to be violated unless God so moves and bends the wills of men to obey and
freely persevere in grace that they cannot fail to be moved by him and to be
gently drawn and led to where God wills and intends.

[1651 logical printed p. 430 | IA leaf 448 | PDF p. 448]

They think that they have untied this Herculean knot when they say that the
new covenant is eternal because God has sworn that he will perform the things
promised, provided that we do not fail to cooperate—that is, if we are willing
to obey. But in that way the covenant of works was eternal. For God says in
holiness that whoever does these things will live in them, and he no less
renders to those who perform the law the life promised to their works than he
does to those who fulfill the new covenant. Thus also the covenant of works
with the angels who fell would, in this sense, be eternal. Indeed, in this way
what Jehovah said to Cain in Genesis 4, *If you do well, will you not be
accepted?*, would be an eternal covenant. Yet Cain immediately violated it by
murder and the miserable despair of his conscience. And the reward promised
for not eating the fruit of the forbidden tree would be an eternal covenant.
What could be said more absurdly? But Jeremiah establishes an essential
difference between the covenant made with the fathers at the departure from
Egypt, insofar as it was legal, and this new covenant: the former was broken
by the fathers and therefore was not eternal; but the latter will be eternal,
and therefore can no more be broken by the covenant people than God's natural
covenant with night and day can be broken, or the breadth of heaven and the
depth of the earth can be searched out, Jeremiah 31:34–37.

2. Arminius suggests another escape: *Among other things* (he says), *that
state (Adam's under the covenant of works) did not have a promise of the
remission of sins if Adam happened to sin even once; but the state of the
faithful is made happier by this promise:*

*I will remember their sins no more. Hence it happens that God's faithfulness
is not rendered void even if his covenant people sin, Romans 3. For it is the
covenant of grace and of faith. Yet posit whatever differences you please; you
must always confess that perseverance (and therefore all obedience, for the
account is equal and the same) is voluntary, free, and mutable toward salvation
in each state.* Answer 1. But in Jeremiah 31 God wills that the new covenant be
adorned not only with the blessedness of promised forgiveness, but also with
undeviatingness in that blessed state, just as night and day and the
revolutions of the sun, moon, and stars continue most constantly under an
unmoved and fixed law—indeed, just as the mountains remain most firmly in
their seats, Isaiah 54:10, as the text says and plainly contradicts Arminius.
2. Arminius concedes that the sins of the faithful do not render God's
faithfulness void, but ambiguously and falsely. What? Did God fall from
faithfulness and truthfulness because of Adam's sin? What do you mean, Jacob?
You ought to have said that the sins of the faithful do not render God's
covenant void, for that is what the Apostle means in Romans 3. You do not dare
to say this, for then the new covenant would stand firm for all apostates and
those ending their lives in final impenitence, especially if they fell away
from faith, as you teach. Do you therefore mean by the sins which do not render
God's faithfulness void all sins except impenitence? But why would you except
impenitence? For forgiveness is promised to impenitence and was given to Paul,
Zacchaeus, Mary Magdalene, and the thief who repented on the cross. For in
Ezekiel 36:33, where the same promise occurs,

[1651 logical printed p. 432 | IA leaf 450 | PDF p. 450]

according to the Remonstrants' own *Apology*, cleansing from all iniquities is
promised in that covenant. Therefore either impenitence is not iniquity, which
is an iniquitous thing to say, or the forgiveness of impenitence is certainly
promised. Therefore God must at some point finally grant even the gravest
covenanted apostate repentance undeviatingly, or God's faithfulness will not
remain firm even when the covenant people sin. Yet I do not understand why
forgiveness of sins but not of impenitence should be promised. 3. How does
God's faithfulness remain firm if the covenant people can fall from grace and
perish, no less than those who violated the covenant of works perish? Because,
of course, God keeps faith with those who believe and repent. But in this way
God's faithfulness also remains firm in the covenant of works: God likewise
keeps faith with those who work. 4. If in each covenant there is always that
equal and natural indifference to sinning or not sinning, then the covenant of
works will be as stable as the mountains and as God's natural covenant with
day and night. But Scripture plainly denies this praise of eternity to the
first covenant. Jeremiah 31:32: *Not according to the covenant that I made
with their fathers on the day when I took them by the hand and led them out of
the land of Egypt, which covenant of mine they violated.* Yet the new covenant
can be violated if there is such indifference in the will to sinning or not
sinning. But the stability and perpetuity of this covenant are asserted more
strongly. Jeremiah 32:40: *I will make an everlasting covenant with them. I
will not turn away from them, that I may do them good; and I will put my fear
in their hearts so that*

[1651 logical printed p. 433 | IA leaf 451 | PDF p. 451]

*they may not depart from me.* 5. Arminius inclines to the claim that the legal
covenant is eternal in this respect: after it has once been violated, it
ceases to be an ordinary way of attaining salvation; whereas, even if men sin
against the new covenant, it does not cease to be an ordinary way to salvation,
because it always promises life to those who repent. But even so he does not
satisfy the difficulty. The legal covenant does not cease simply, but only in
a qualified respect, to be a way of attaining salvation—namely, because of the
weakness of the flesh, Romans 8. In this respect the new covenant is no more a
way of salvation for the stiff-necked than the covenant of works is. Indeed,
the covenant of works still says, “*Do this and you will live*,” Romans 10. But
if determining grace is not promised in the new covenant, the covenant is in
no way stable, as Scripture says it is, but is as unstable as the covenant of
works, while it plainly contradicts Scripture.
