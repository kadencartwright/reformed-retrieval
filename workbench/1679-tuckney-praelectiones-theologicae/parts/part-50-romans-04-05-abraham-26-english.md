Aquinas argues chiefly from the fact that imperfection of knowledge belongs
to the nature of faith, since faith is defined as “the substance of things
hoped for and the evidence of things not apparent.” Perfection, however,
belongs to the nature of beatific vision. Things opposed in this way can no
more come together in the same subject than one and the same numerical
thing can remain itself while being transferred from one species to another.

Durandus argues as follows. If acts of sight and faith actually existed
together in the blessed, they would exist either as distinct acts or as one
act. They cannot exist as distinct acts, because in the heavenly homeland
the blessed see clearly what wayfarers believe. Faith rests on the authority
of Scripture and believes it even though it is not evident that Scripture
was inspired by God. The blessed, however, know this very fact through
vision as something self-evident. Therefore they do not properly believe,
which is the function of faith. Consequently faith and vision do not remain
as distinct acts.

Nor do they concur in one and the same act, because faith and vision do not
have the same medium. Faith's medium is the authority of Scripture, which
is not evident. But it is apparent to the blessed through clear vision that
the Scriptures are God-breathed. Or if such a medium of vision were granted
there, since that vision is a precisely supernatural act with respect both
to its cause and its essence, it comes totally and precisely from the divine
essence as its effi-

cient and objective cause, with nothing else concurring. Thus the act of
faith is abolished there not only with respect to its imperfection, but
also with respect to the essence of the act.

As for Aquinas's argument, although he has Estius and several others as
advocates in saying that imperfection belongs to the nature of faith, he
has Durandus as an adversary. Imperfection denotes either the presence of a
defect and privation of due perfection or merely a lesser degree of
perfection.

In the former sense, privation and defect may be an accompanying condition,
but can never belong to the formal nature of any positive act, such as the
act of faith. If in the latter sense imperfection is taken for a lesser
degree of perfection, it will indeed belong to the nature of the act in
which it inheres. In this sense faith is less perfect than vision, but it
does not follow that faith must be banished from heaven. Although everything
there is supreme in its own kind, it does not follow that everything there
will be equal. In different subjects, some possess greater degrees of glory
than others. Even in the same subject there can be greater or lesser glory
in different respects—as the soul's glory is greater than the body's, and
why should the glory of sight not be greater than that of faith? Even if
one vision of God were granted to everyone, nothing prevents the blessed
from knowing all things through vision in God himself and nevertheless
knowing them in their causes by knowledge properly so called. They may
likewise know God himself there at once through immediate vision and through
the mirror of creatures. His deity will shine more brightly in the
creatures there than it does here. Why should they not also know him through
faith insofar as faith is acquiescence in the authority of divine testimony?

As for Aquinas's proof that imperfection belongs to the nature of faith
because it is defined as “the substance of things hoped for and the evidence
of things not apparent,” I answer:

1. Although Jerome, Theodoret, and Theophylact call this a definition of
faith, by “definition” they mean only a description, according to the
judgment not only of our theologians but also of Erasmus, Estius, and others.

2. Just as it is only a description of faith, so it describes faith only
insofar as it is the guide of wayfarers, as the whole following chapter
shows. The same applies to its being called “the substance of things hoped
for” in that description itself.

3. When it is called “the evidence of things not apparent,” the Apostle
does not mean that everything believed is non-apparent, as Estius himself
judges. In that very chapter the Apostle says that through faith we
understand that the world was made by God, although this appears through
demonstration. Non-apparent things are mentioned for two other reasons.
First, they are more properly the object of faith. Evident things are the
object of knowledge and vision, while faith believes both evident and
non-apparent things on account of God's testimony. Second, faith's virtue
and commendation shine more brightly because, even when sight and knowledge
are absent, it nevertheless believes God when he testifies about things not
apparent.

4. Whatever Thomas says about the incompatibility of vision and faith with
respect to the same subject and object, he does not prove that the same
subject cannot possess different acts and ways of knowing the same object
through different media. He himself grants that the same conclusion can
follow from both a demonstra-

[1679 printed p. 267 | IA leaf 275]

tive and a dialectical medium. Consequently the same blessed person can
know the same thing through vision from the object's clear presence,
through knowledge from clear cognition of its cause, and simultaneously
through faith from the sure testimony and authority of God speaking. Sight
does not so absorb everything that a blessed person fails to believe God
precisely as one who speaks and testifies, which belongs to faith. Nor does
it utterly extinguish all the knowledge acquired in this life through
knowledge of causes, which the scholastics grant—indeed affirm—will remain
in the life to come. Someone who reaches a demonstrative conclusion does
not immediately forget the dialectical medium, nor does someone who knows
a thing by sight at once surrender to oblivion the sure persuasion given
him by testimony. The greater light certainly outshines the lesser, but
although it may seem to obscure it, it does not entirely extinguish it.

5. Finally, when Aquinas says that vision and faith are so opposed that
they can no more coexist in the same subject than one and the same numerical
thing can remain itself while being transferred from one species to
another, he errs. We are far from saying either that the acts of sight and
faith are numerically one or that they are opposed species. They agree in
the common genus and substance of knowledge. Aquinas is so far from denying
that they can remain together that he holds it to be most certainly true.
