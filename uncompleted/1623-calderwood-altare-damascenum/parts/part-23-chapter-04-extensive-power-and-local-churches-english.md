On the Bishop’s Extensive Power

Once the princely bishop’s intensive power is removed, his extensive power is
also removed. For one who has no power over a single church cannot extend his
power over several. Nevertheless, in keeping with the purpose of this work, I
will say a few things about the bishop’s extensive power. In Holy Scripture
the word “church” is never used to denote the faithful and Christians of a
nation, province, or diocese, as it is now used when we say the Gallican,
English, or Scottish Church. This form of speech was not dictated in Scripture
by the Holy Spirit. We may indeed speak this way if we do so in the same or a
similar sense in which we speak of the universal and catholic church. But when
we speak of a particular and properly visible church, the term is not used for
the number of believers in a nation, province, or diocese, but for a number of
believers sharing in divine worship and discipline. We do indeed read in Acts
7:38 of the church of the Jews in the singular, but Stephen is speaking there
of the nation of the Jews who were in the wilderness and at that time were
gathered together and encamped around the ark. Indeed, when they entered the
land of Canaan they shared together in divine worship at the three principal
feasts, at which they were bound to go up to the temple, the common place of
public worship. Outside those appointed times the priests and Levites
ministered in the temple not only for the city of Jerusalem but also for the
whole nation, and the devout went up for sacrifices and divine worship as they
were either bound by the law or moved by devotion. That city had several
synagogues, but the temple was common to all who inhabited the country.
Christ does not will to be worshiped in this way in the Christian church, nor
does he will a common place of public worship to be erected in any nation,
province, or diocese in which the faithful are to commune. When Paul speaks of
the Christian Jews themselves, he does not say “the church of the Jews” or
“the Jewish church,” but uses the plural: the churches of Judea, Galatians
1:22; the churches throughout Judea, Acts 9:31. Scripture speaks likewise of
others: the churches of Galatia, Galatians 1:2 and 1 Corinthians 16:1; the
churches of Asia, 1 Corinthians 16:19; the churches of Macedonia, 2
Corinthians 8:1; the churches of Syria and Cilicia, Acts 15:41; and the
churches throughout Galilee and Samaria, Acts 9:31. And when Scripture speaks
of a particular church, it very often also uses the preposition ἐν to denote
the particular place where the members of one church communed, a place which
was neither a province nor a diocese: the church ἐν ἱεροσολύμοις, “in
Jerusalem,” Acts 8:1 and Acts 11:22; ἐν κορίνθῳ, “in Corinth,” 1 Corinthians
1:2 and 2 Corinthians 1:1; ἐν ἀντιοχείᾳ, “in Antioch,” Acts 13:1; ἐν περγάμῳ,
“in Pergamum,” Revelation 2:12; ἐν θυατείροις, “in Thyatira,” Revelation
2:18; ἐν σάρδεσιν, “in Sardis,” Revelation 3:1; and ἐν φιλαδελφείᾳ, “in
Philadelphia,” Revelation 3:7. Therefore, in the age of the apostles churches
did not extend throughout whole dioceses or provinces. For the form of speech
would have been inept if the churches had been

[1623 printed p. 282 | IA leaf 294 | PDF p. 294]

diocesan. Who would say that a diocesan church was in some city, such as
Antioch, London, or York? “Why not?” says Downham, *since the soul is said to
dwell in the head although it is in the whole body.* The soul can be said to
be in the head although it is in the rest of the body because it is something
indivisible and is whole in every part. It is not a quantitative thing whose
one part lies outside another. The faithful of an entire diocese cannot be in
the city. If the faithful cannot be there, the church of the diocese cannot be
said to be in the city. For what is a church if not an aggregate of many
believers? If the church is the soul and the city the head, what, then, will
the body be that is distinct from the soul? Therefore, when Scripture says
that the church was in the city of Jerusalem, Antioch, Thyatira, Corinth, and
so forth, it is clear that the particular churches furnished with ordinary
jurisdiction and the power of discipline were not diocesan or provincial.

At the first beginnings the number of believers in particular churches, even
in large cities, was so small that they could easily and conveniently come
together ἐπὶ τὸ αὐτὸ, “in the same place,” into one assembly and share in
divine worship, Acts 2:44, 46; Acts 5:12; Acts 21:22; 1 Corinthians 5:4; 1
Corinthians 11:17, 18, 20, 23, and so forth. Nor do I think it can be
demonstrated by certain evidence that in the age of the apostles the number of
believers had grown so great that they could not gather into one place and ἐπὶ
τὸ αὐτὸ. But let us grant that, because of their increased number, they met in
several places and could not come together into a single assembly. Even so,
our opponents admit that in the age of the apostles there were no distinct
assemblies—or, as we say in ordinary usage, parishes—but that they met
indiscriminately in various gatherings and that the presbyters ministered and
taught indiscriminately. So long, however, as the number of believers in each
city was not divided into fixed, definite, and settled congregations, even if
several gatherings were held indiscriminately in one city, that number was
designated by the name “church,” not “churches.” Thus we say the Amsterdam
church, not churches, because the pastors preach indiscriminately and the
faithful meet indiscriminately in this or that church building. Likewise, when
Revelation was written, the particular churches of Ephesus, Thyatira, and
Laodicea are not called churches in the plural but church in the singular,
although they themselves conjecture that several but indiscriminate gatherings
were held in those cities when John wrote. For, as Baynes aptly says, it is one
thing when sheep feed in a common pasture even though they do not graze upon
the very same blade of grass, and another when they feed in diverse and
distinct pastures. Moreover, the presbyters not only taught indiscriminately
but also governed in common the church committed to their care, as Jerome
said, and consequently they ought to govern the church in common. Downham says
that this does not follow: presbyters devoted more attentive labor to the
whole flock in common before the church’s indiscriminate gatherings

were arranged into settled congregations and the presbyters attached to
separate parishes; but after each one had been assigned to his own title,
the bishop and the presbyters whom he retained with himself and whose counsel
he employed took upon themselves the government and care of them all. But by
what right? An apostolic right? Downham himself admits that in the age of the
apostles the church of one city was not divided into several distinct
congregations or parishes. Somewhere he excepts the Alexandrian church because
Eusebius says that Mark established churches at Alexandria. Eusebius does not
say that he knows this with certainty; *they say,* he writes, *that Mark was
the first sent into Egypt and the first to establish churches at Alexandria.*
Moreover, Eusebius supports his conjecture by an erroneous interpretation of
Philo, who speaks of Essenes, not Christians. Joseph Scaliger noted this
blunder of Eusebius in his refutation of the triheresy, as we said above. The
reader should also see what we noted above about Eusebius and Mark.

Moreover, when Downham cites Epiphanius, Heresy 69, on the distinct parishes
in the Alexandrian church, he says they had been divided before the age of
Constantine. Nicetas Choniates, when he mentions the distinct parishes of
Alexandria, does not trace them back to Mark or the time of the apostles, and
Filesacus says that he took what he relates from Epiphanius. Nicephorus says
only that by ancient custom one bishop presided over all the presbyters in the
Alexandrian church, while the presbyters themselves governed their own
churches and taught the people gathered in them, as the same Filesacus
observes. Duarenus says that in every city there was a certain college of
presbyters over which the bishop presided and that the canons seem to have
succeeded in their place. *For the presbyters did not yet have separate
churches in which they taught and administered the sacraments. Sozomen
demonstrates this when he says that the custom at Alexandria in his own time
was that, although one bishop stood over all, the presbyters separately held
churches and the people gathered in them celebrated solemnly; Tripartite
History, book 1, chapter 19.* Sozomen’s own words are these: *Arius sends
envoys to Paulinus, bishop of Tyre; Eusebius surnamed Pamphilus, who governed
the church of Caesarea in Palestine; and Patrophilus, bishop of Scythopolis.
He asks that, since he had previously attained the rank of presbyter, he and
his companions be granted power to summon the people who favored him to
gatherings. For the custom at Alexandria was, as it still is, that although
one bishop presided over all, the presbyters nevertheless retained separate
churches and assembled the people committed to them for gatherings.* He says
that it was the custom of Alexandria, not an institution of Mark. Since,
therefore, this exception of Downham’s is groundless, what he often says
elsewhere remains firm: the churches of cities in the age of the apostles were
not divided into distinct parishes and subordinated to one mother and
cathedral church.

Panvinius writes in his little book on the episcopates, titles, and deaconries
of the cardinals: *Moreover, lest the presbyters’ administration be conducted
indiscriminately, the same Evaristus, who was the third Roman pontiff after the
blessed apostle Peter, around the hundredth year after Christ’s birth divided
the titles—or, as we now say, parishes—in the city of Rome among the
presbyters. Thus, separated from one another, each in his own district of the
city, in a title or parish, for the sake of removing confusion, he might
instruct the people in the divine mysteries and administer God’s sacraments to
all who came to him; and he placed individual presbyters in each title. For at
that time this number seemed sufficient because of the fewness of the
believers,* and so forth. But this hermit will not readily persuade anyone
that what he reports is true. Platina, I admit, records the same, but he
follows the pretended Damasus, saying, *as Damasus says.* *But this book*—the
Lives of the Pontiffs—which is attributed to Damasus, *is the work of
Anastasius the Librarian, not Damasus, as is well known,* says Bellarmine. Yet
Anastasius the Librarian, the Roman abbot, flourished around the year 858.
Even if Damasus himself had said it, should he be believed when he was later
and was pontiff near the end of the fourth century? Platina’s Damasus is a
pretender, and yet he says only, *as Damasus says.* He does not dare affirm it
as certain.

Moreover, Onuphrius says, *titles were temples or, as they are now called,
churches—that is, sacred places erected in honor or memory of the saints and
anciently distinguished throughout the districts of the city by the Roman
pontiffs, in which the cure of souls was held by the presbyters residing in
them.* Downham says that the *titles* which Evaristus assigned to the
presbyters *were sacred buildings or places of assembly.* Again, *Evaristus
divided the titles among the individual presbyters—that is, the settled places
of assembly which we call parish churches—to which they were instituted and
from which they were called presbyters of this or that title or parish.* Is it
likely that under the emperor Trajan, who stirred up a persecution against the
church and, when at last he called off the savage persecution and the search
for Christians, nevertheless ordered that those who were denounced should be
punished, so many sacred buildings had been constructed in the city of Rome?
Nor is what the same Onuphrius says about Hyginus credible, namely, that
*not long afterward, moved by the multitude of believers increasing every
day, Hyginus, the Roman pontiff, wished there to be several clerics as well as
presbyters in each title, since these were not sufficient for so many
believers. From this, I believe, the name “cardinal presbyters” arose, so that
a cardinal presbyter was the principal presbyter who presided over the other
presbyters of the same title.* Onuphrius writes about the cardinals to
Alexander Farnese, cardinal deacon; for this reason he traces their origin to
the earliest times he can. But Bellarmine shows that Onuphrius’s *I believe*
about the name “cardinal” is an error. Nor would I believe Onuphrius in other
matters. Platina says that Hyginus *prudently reduced the clergy to order and
distributed them,* but he adds other things: that he *decreed that churches
should not be dedicated without a celebration, nor increased or diminished
without the judgment of the metropolitan or bishop; that timber and the remaining*

*materials gathered for building churches should not be converted to profane

churches built in the time of Hyginus? And was
Hyginus, whom they call the ninth pontiff after Peter, a patriarch who had
metropolitans subject to him and even prescribed laws for them? Others say
that Dionysius, whom they count as the twenty-fifth after Peter, first divided
the parishes in the year 290, because he writes in his second letter to
Severus: *We assigned the individual churches to individual presbyters; we
divided the parishes and cemeteries among them.* Yet the authenticity of the
letters attributed to the Roman pontiffs of that and an earlier age is still
disputed, and the more learned men among the papists themselves doubt it.
However it may be, whether Evaristus, Hyginus, Dionysius, or Marcellus divided
the individual parishes in the city of Rome among individual presbyters, this
was done only after the apostolic age had ended, and it was done in a very
large city, not immediately in every city.

Moreover, we do not condemn dividing larger and more extensive churches into
smaller ones. What we condemn is dividing them in such a way that those
distinct churches are deprived of their own presbytery and the exercise of
discipline, or at least are divided so that the presbyters of the particular
churches are excluded from the common government of the distinct parishes.
For Downham claims that they were divided in such a way that the presbyters
who governed the church in common before the division governed only their own
parish after the division, as though merely in the forum of conscience, while
the bishop and the few presbyters serving as his counselors in the cathedral
and mother church retained common jurisdiction over the distinct parishes in
the external forum. But the question is, by what right can presbyters be
attached to distinct parishes in such a way that they abstain from the common
government of the distinct parishes, and consequently from the external
government of their own parish and from the forum of causes? For according to
the truth of the Lord’s arrangement, as we proved above, the presbyters
governed the church in common. Human law cannot abolish divine law. Just as
all presbyters are equal to one another and none is to be excluded from the
common government of the church, so distinct parishes are equal to one another
in power, and one is not subordinate to another.

Downham says that the apostles established presbyters throughout the cities
so that the entire city might be leavened through their ministry. He grants
that while the whole lump was already leavened but had not been divided into
several loaves, the presbyters governed by common counsel; but once the lump
was divided into several loaves, individual presbyters were placed over their
individual portions, while the bishop and a few counselors exercised
jurisdiction over them all in his cathedral church. But this is precisely the
question: why should one part of the divided lump rule over the others? Was
that part leavened before the others—that is, was that separate part converted
first and did it convert the others? It is not likely that one street or
district of a city was converted before the others. Rather, believers were
converted indiscriminately throughout

[1623 printed p. 286 | IA leaf 298 | PDF p. 298]

various districts and parts of the city; gathered into one assembly, they
gradually converted others in the same way indiscriminately throughout the
parts of the city. Thus, when the whole lump had been leavened, it was not
possible in the division to choose one part which had leavened the others.
Instead, when the whole lump was divided, the believers who had come together
in the first assembly were necessarily divided as well and assigned to their
own titles and distinct parishes, just as the presbyters themselves were.
Moreover, it would not follow that one part was leavened first and leavened
another, and therefore the others ought to be subordinated and subjected to
it. A converted church may honor the church that converted it as prior in
Christ and because of the benefit of its conversion. But Christ granted as
much power to converted churches as to converting churches. For if this
reasoning carried any weight, the church of Jerusalem would have ruled over
all the others—the churches of Antioch, Rome, Alexandria, and so forth. *The
Roman people freely, not under compulsion, received from the Greeks certain
laws called the Twelve Tables, from which the remaining laws of the Roman
people proceeded as from their origin. Yet it is agreed that the Roman people
were subject to the Greeks by no jurisdiction or authority,* says Marsilius
of Padua.

And is this power hereditary as well? What if the converting mother becomes a
prostitute or falls away from the faith, while the converted daughter remains
in the faith? What if a devout mother puts off maternal affection and conducts
herself imperiously enough? But let us return. One part of the lump is not
superior to another or greater in authority. A baker divides the whole lump
into equal loaves. However the baker divides his lump, so Christ wills larger
churches to be divided when they can no longer conveniently coalesce into one
church but labor under their own bulk. Spalatensis says that the entire
Christian multitude, all who are found scattered throughout the whole world,
constitutes one great assembly and a single spiritual commonwealth. *And this
unity is that of one integral whole in a quantitative and homogeneous thing
which has parts that are merely integral but homogeneous, as if you divided
one great stone into several parts and particles, and likewise wood, gold,
earth, water, and so forth. This one church is one great flock whose parts and
particles are not subjective, potential, moral, or metaphysical, but merely
and purely integral; yet the larger and smaller particular churches are
homogeneous and of the same nature. Therefore, the unity of the universal
church militant is not the unity of a genus which is multiplied through
species down to the most specific species, nor unity through essential
subordination, because among the merely integral parts of this great church
there is no essential subordination, no dependence, and consequently no
subjection and no prelacy.* These things can equally be affirmed of particular
churches. A lump divided into several parts is like a great stone divided into
several particles. Before the division the lump is an integral whole; the
distinct parishes are integral but homogeneous parts. Here there is no
essential subordination, no dependence, no subjection, and no prelacy.

In the formal definition of the church by which the church is a church—namely,
in faith and religion—the whole church is one, he says, and by this unity the
whole is homogeneous, for the parts are of the same nature as the whole. Thus,
divided portions of water are water, and many portions of water joined
together make nothing other than a great body of water; likewise, the many
partial churches situated throughout the world constitute one great total
church. And so the partial churches of this lump are participants in one faith
and religion; why, then, not in a common government as well? *Bellarmine is
mistaken,* he says, *when he makes the body of the church one heterogeneous
whole, as a tree is one whole composed of branches and root.* The
hierarchists are certainly mistaken—or rather, they wish to mislead—when they
make the body of a church such as a city or diocese one heterogeneous whole,
as though the cathedral were the mother and the other partial churches her
daughters—or rather, as though the former were the mistress and the latter
maidservants—and, as someone wittily said, as though the cathedral church’s
bell tower swallowed the little towers of the parish churches. The cathedral
rises above the others only as much as cypresses are accustomed to rise among
pliant viburnums, to display the queen’s majesty. But I am now discoursing not so
much about the monstrous construction of towers as about the lord bishop’s
towering power. It was once said that the daughter devoured the mother; now,
conversely, it can be said that the mother has devoured many daughters.

Since, therefore, the partial churches are *kindred sisters,* to use Bede’s
words, *begotten from the same incorruptible seed, raised in the same catholic
family, members of the same head, branches of the same vine, fellow heirs of
the same inheritance, daughters of the mistress Sarah and not of the
maidservant Hagar,* and equal in so many privileges, should they be unequal in
the power of government? The churches, therefore, are equal, their presbyters
are equal, and none are to be excluded from the common government of the
partial churches of one lump. Before the division they were endowed with an
equal share of honor and power. The presbyters of the church attended the
council at Jerusalem, and when Paul went in to James in Acts 21:18, all the
presbyters were present. By what right are they to be shut out after the lump
has been divided? Certainly, if such an institution had prevailed in the
churches in either the second or the third century, I would say that the
divine order was violated imprudently—to say nothing harsher—and, after so
many centuries, was lawfully restored in the Reformed churches but was not
restored in the others, to the churches’ very great injury.
