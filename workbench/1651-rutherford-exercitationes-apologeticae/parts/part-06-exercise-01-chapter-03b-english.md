14. But since this argument incidentally overthrows the Arminian
predestination based on foreseen faith, it will be useful to press the
Arminians' evasions somewhat more closely. Corvinus wishes God's freedom, and
not faith's free obedience, to be the cause of election, because, he says:

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Censure of Molina*, ch. 17, sec. 8, p. 266.]

[1651 logical printed p. 86 | IA leaf 104 | PDF p. 104]

*God determined in general to save believers, and so in general he also
chooses this or that individual believer, insofar as a part, or that which has
the character of a part, thus imitates the whole. For individuals are
comprised under the universal as a part under the whole.* Reply. 1. God's
freedom is indeed established in the general decree by which he determined to
save believers, although he could, had he so willed, have saved workers. But
his freedom is not established in the particular decree by which Peter rather
than Judas is chosen. First, let this syllogism be formed: God chooses all
believers; but Peter believes; therefore God chooses Peter. The conclusion is
in no way true because of the truth of the major premise. Otherwise the
conclusion of the following syllogism would also be true because its major is
true: Every tree is a substance; but Peter is a tree; therefore Peter is a
substance. Even novices barely initiated into the rudiments of dialectic know
that a true major does not make a conclusion true, but that the truth of the
major and of the assumption jointly is the total cause why the conclusion is
true. Therefore the assumption, *But Peter believes*, is neither certain nor
true because God chooses all believers. This consequence is therefore built
on sand: God chooses all believers according to his good pleasure; therefore
according to his good pleasure he chooses Peter. For Peter can fail to
believe, and then he is not elect, even while this remains true, that God has
chosen all believers. 2. In the general decree God exercises no causality
toward the election of Peter rather than Judas. For while the general decree
to save believers remains in force,

[1651 logical printed p. 87 | IA leaf 105 | PDF p. 105]

Judas can be chosen and Peter not chosen. Thus there is no freedom of God in
choosing Peter rather than Judas. Instead, once Peter is going freely to
believe antecedently to God's decree, God will either choose Peter under the
compulsion and necessity of fate, or he will retract the general decree and
cease to be truthful and just. 3. I ask whether God decrees absolutely to save
believers before he foreknows whether any will believe or not, or whether he
does so without knowing whether any will believe. If the latter is said, then
the general decree is foolish and absurd. For in this manner he could have
decreed to deliver the Savior by more than twelve legions of angels. But if
the former is said, and God first foreknows whether some will believe, then
the general decree is not absolute, as Arminius himself maintains, but
conditional. People are foreknown as going to believe before God gives the
means of faith, which reverses the order of the Arminian decrees. But I wish
this to be said only in passing.

[Marginalia: In the decree of the sentence.]

15. Nor does it belong to God's freedom and dominion whether the Assyrians,
Chaldeans, Edomites, Ammonites, and others ravage God's Church or do not ravage
it; whether the Sabeans plunder Job or not; whether the ten tribes defect from
David's house because of Solomon's idolatry; or whether David begets sons from
whom Christ is to be born. For all these things happen most freely and
antecedently to God's decree. Consequently, despite God's dominion, they
cannot fail to happen. Thus these petty theologians bind God to fate while
attempting

[Marginalia: Acts 4:27–28.]

[1651 logical printed p. 88 | IA leaf 106 | PDF p. 106]

to exempt human beings from an invented fate.

16. It conflicts with the infinite perfection of the divine nature. For if
God's will depends on the determination of a contingent cause, God's nature is
not infinitely perfect. But if God does not decree contingencies until they
are future, God's volition will depend upon the operations of the contingent
cause. Indeed, God will thus be determined to act by created beings. Once the
futurition of the contingent effect is posited, he will necessarily be moved
to decree it, for God's will cannot remain indeterminate concerning future
contingents, among which are many good things and supernatural acts of faith,
love, repentance, and so forth.

[Marginalia: Argument 7.]

17. It conflicts with the supreme dignity of the first cause, which consists
in its being the cause of all beings. This position maintains that all future
contingents possess futurition and have passed from the state of possibility
to the state of futurition from eternity before the first cause's decision
and free volition intervened. God's will will therefore not be the first cause
of all things, contrary to the lucid Scriptures, Romans 11:36; Acts 17:28;
Proverbs 16:17; Revelation 4:11. Thus God will not be the first efficient and
the ultimate end of all beings. For if beings possess existence antecedently
to God's free determination and intention, in that priority they exist for no
end and do not serve God's glory, because every means of displaying divine
glory must exist in God's intention. But these things precede God's intention.

[Marginalia: Argument 8.]

18. It also conflicts with God's eternity. For

[1651 logical printed p. 89 | IA leaf 107 | PDF p. 107]

if God's volitions are posterior to created and temporal volitions, they are
not eternal. But if contingencies possess futurition before God's decree, they
are prior to God's volitions.

19. It conflicts with God's immutability. For if God bends the contingent
cause before its own determination and determines it where he wills, as
Scripture says in Proverbs 21:1, then either he decreed before that
determination so to move and bend the contingent cause, or he does something
in time which he did not decree from eternity to do, which makes God mutable.

[Marginalia: Argument 9.]

20. It conflicts with God's justice insofar as it is to be displayed. It
could have happened that all human beings and angels obeyed, provided that
they willed to do so. Thus there would have been no opportunity for God, even
though he willed it, to manifest vindicatory justice. By the same reasoning,
all could have disobeyed, and despite God's wise decree the mercy which God
had irresistibly decreed to reveal could have remained hidden forever.

[Marginalia: Argument 10.]

21. It conflicts with God's grace and gratuitous love. God does not then
regenerate us according to his will, as James teaches in 1:18, nor from his
immense love toward us, as Paul says in Ephesians 2:4, nor according to his
gracious good pleasure, as Christ says in Matthew 11:26, but from debt and
necessity. For antecedently to the act of the gracious will our regeneration
possessed futurition, and God could not prevent what is future from being
future.

[Marginalia: Argument 11.]

[1651 logical printed p. 90 | IA leaf 108 | PDF p. 108]

22. This decree also reverses the gospel. The gospel establishes that
humiliation and saving sorrow precede exaltation and joy. But insofar as these
things often occur freely, it rests with human beings to rejoice before they
humble themselves. Such an order cannot be called God's decree, for it
preceded the decree. Further, the gospel is God's free decree concerning the
production of a new heart in the elect, the hardening of the reprobate, the
deliverance of the Church by free agents, the destruction of Christ's enemies
through bloody wars which occur freely, and the humiliation of the elect
through very many afflictions and temptations which come upon them through
contingent causes, such as the world's hatred. But this decree maintains that
all these things happen antecedently to God's decree—indeed, that the Savior
is killed, the apostles are scourged, the martyrs slain, Antichrist exercises
tyrannical dominion, false Christs deceive and seduce many, the elect
persevere, and hypocrites defect antecedently to God's decree. This makes the
whole gospel a fortuitous event and confluence of things apart from and before
God's wise decree, contrary to Ephesians 3:9 and 1 Corinthians 2:7.

[Marginalia: Argument 12.]

23. This decree removes all the saints' patience in afflictions. For why would
David console himself in the Lord in Psalm 39, saying, *I was silent because
you did it*, if the evil brought upon him by his enemies had come to be
antecedently to God's decree? Why would he seek comfort in God against
Shimei's insults

[Marginalia: Argument 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 91 | IA leaf 109 | PDF p. 109]

if those insults possessed futurition antecedently to God's decree? For when
the saints fled to God, this answer would lie ready at hand for God: “My
children, these evils inflicted by the world and contingent causes must be
borne, not mourned. For they happened before I had decreed anything about
them. Fortune first cast the die from eternity, and I could not but subscribe
to that adverse fortune. Therefore you have no reason to seek comfort for
your afflictions in me.” Would not the godly person's consolation in God be
cold?

[Marginalia: Argument 14.]

24. Nor would faith have any place here. For why would the Church in war hope
for Jehovah's aid against its enemies? Antecedently to God's decree the victory
was already settled and fatally established. Nor against unforeseen and
premature death would the Holy Spirit's statement in Job 14:6 have force: *His
days have been determined, the number of his months is with you; you have
established his limits, which he cannot pass.* Corvinus says, “I deny that
they have been fixed by a merely absolute decree, except insofar as it is
granted that a person dies once and afterward comes judgment. But if you wish
the hour and instant of death to have been so ordained by a precise decree
that it is at the same time precisely decreed that a person will die at such a
moment, in such a manner, and through such means, I deny that the terminus of
life is fixed in that way.” Indeed, he says that prayers for a long life tell
against this, and that it follows from it that God ordains some people to kill
themselves and that medicine is futile. But I reply: 1. Job is not there
discussing merely the necessity of dying, but dying at such a precise time.
Job says that the months and days are so

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, ch. 24, fol. 33.]

[1651 logical printed p. 92 | IA leaf 110 | PDF p. 110]

fixed that a person cannot pass beyond them. The atheistic innovator is not ashamed
to contradict God, as if through prayer and medicine a person could prolong
life beyond the moment prescribed by God. 2. He denies that the limits are
fixed absolutely. You therefore wish the limits to be fixed conditionally.
But give me the condition—namely, on the hypothesis that a lethal illness
overcomes him at such a time. The question then returns whether that lethal
illness invading at that time was ordained absolutely or conditionally. If
absolutely, our position is proved. If conditionally, I ask again for the
condition. At last the sophist will lead us to say that God ordained this
provided that it would be future. Hold your laughter, friends. 3. Scripture
says that God ordained for Christ the manner and the hour of darkness in which
he would be captured, the kind of death, the piercing of his hands and feet,
the agents of his death, his burial, and so forth. God precisely decreed the
manner of Jezebel's death, and the death of Ahab. 4. We pray for Christ's
glorious coming in Revelation 22:20. Yet God appointed beforehand the day of
his coming, Acts 17:31. This sophist argues in no other way than the devil did
with Christ in Matthew 4: “God appointed his angels as your guardians;
therefore cast yourself down from the pinnacle of the temple.” So Corvinus
argues: God absolutely decreed the hour and manner of death; therefore
medicine is futile, prayers empty, and prudent avoidance of dangers vain. 5.
I would hope in vain for a peaceful death and deliverance from sudden death
from Jehovah, if

[1651 logical printed p. 93 | IA leaf 111 | PDF p. 111]

death was settled and fixed with respect to manner, kind, means, place, and
time by nature before Jehovah had determined anything about my death by his
decree. For why would I trust in Jehovah? Even by his omnipotence he cannot
make that which is future antecedently to his will not future.

[Marginalia: Argument 15.]

25. Under this decree prayers, thanksgiving, and all gratitude toward God are
futile. Why would I pray against the scourging of the tongue; the violent
hands of robbers on the road; imprisonment; contagious plagues from contact
with the diseased; an unforeseen death from the collapse of a house; the
premature and bitter death of a wife and children; length of life or the
shortening of one's days; plunder by enemies; houses set on fire; and
infinitely many other evils which happen through the service of contingent
causes? Why would I trouble God with prayers and supplicate him concerning
these things? For all of them possessed a settled and fixed futurition from
eternity antecedently to God's middle knowledge, and much more to God's will.

[Marginalia: Argument 16.]

26. Why, after being plundered by the Sabeans, would Job bless the name of
Jehovah, who gives and takes away? For that plundering possessed the whole of
its future being apart from and before every volition of God. Why would a
soldier, having achieved his aim and distinguished by victories, glory in the
right hand of Jehovah, mighty in war? Psalm 20:7.

[Marginalia: Argument 17.]

27. The Word, promises, and threats are plainly employed by God in vain with
the

[1651 logical printed p. 94 | IA leaf 112 | PDF p. 112]

intention of leading human beings to obedience or of rendering the reprobate
inexcusable through their voluntary obstinacy. For God intends in vain the
obedience of those whose obedience possesses all its being before that
intention. Through the denial of efficacious aid, he intends in vain the
obstinacy of the reprobate as punishment for preceding crimes when God's
volition is in no manner the cause of such punishment. Thus every necessity
for ministry is removed.

[Marginalia: Argument 18.]

28. The Holy Spirit also works in us in vain the saving care and solicitude
for working out salvation and the pursuit and zeal for good works, when that
pursuit and solicitude fatally precede both all of God's foreknowledge and all
his volition. Every action of God upon the mind, will, and affections with the
intention that we should be sanctified is also futile, when our sanctification
is accomplished antecedently to every intention of God. It is just as if God
intended to do what has already been done.

[Marginalia: Argument 19.]

29. Finally, I see no more direct road for establishing atheism, Epicureanism,
and the throne of fortune or contingency than this: the consciences of all
human beings are called away from God and providence and fastened upon causes
operating in an entirely fortuitous whirl. Jesuit-Arminianism will therefore
be Epicureanism.

30. Let the absolute decree therefore stand, and the conditional decree must
fall. But the Englishman Thomas Jackson forges for us a third decree, which he
calls disjunctive, and whose

[1651 logical printed p. 95 | IA leaf 113 | PDF p. 113]

object this jejune theologian makes “the possible.” Thus he says: “The impeccable rule
of eternal equity, which from eternity decrees whatever is possible and
foreknows whatever is future, establishes no statute opposed to its absolute
and eternal power of dominion. It always was, is, and will be equally
indifferent and free to repay everyone according to his works.” Elsewhere he
says, “For every possible antecedent it decreed a corresponding consequent.”
And in part 2, book 2, chapter 17: “There is no decree in God concerning
contingents except a disjunctive one: either it will be or it will not be;
either it will rain throughout tomorrow or it will not rain throughout the
day. Today the sun will either shine at twelve o'clock or it will not shine.”
It is not useless to call these pronouncements of a wild and savage mind to
examination. For 1. Dr. G. Twisse, a man who can never be praised enough,
observes in the *Scholastic Arena*—good God, what a man!—that the object of
the divine decree is not necessary. For God does not decree that he himself
should be infinite, just, and so forth, since he cannot fail to be infinite.
But the object of the disjunctive decree is necessary, because Peter
necessarily either believes or does not believe, since one side of
contradictories is necessarily true and the other false. 2. From this
disjunctive decree many of God's predictions and promises will be openly
false: “It will happen in that day”; “In the last days I will do this”; “I
will give you a new heart”; and Zechariah 12:10, “They will look upon me whom
they pierced, and they will mourn,” and so forth. These can bear an entirely
contrary meaning:

[Marginalia: *On the Divine Essence*, sentence 10, part 2, p. 128; ch. 8,
secs. 2–3.]

[Marginalia: Twisse against Jackson.]

[1651 logical printed p. 96 | IA leaf 114 | PDF p. 114]

“It will not happen in that day”; “I will not give you a new heart”; “They
will not look upon me”; “They will not mourn,” and so forth. “You must
prophesy,” Jeremiah 1. Why? “For I appointed you as a prophet to the nations.”
Indeed, from this appointment it follows instead that you will not prophesy.
Who, then, can decide between God predicting that this will happen and God
predicting that it will not happen? 3. It follows from this that God's will is
the cause of no supernatural acts and of no moral good. Once God's decree has
been posited, all things decreed can fail ever to exist. Indeed, the will of
devils is as much the cause of good as God's will, since once the latter is
posited, a moral good can either exist or not exist. Christ could simply have
become incarnate or not, could have died and redeemed the human race or not;
the Jews could have been led into captivity by the Chaldeans or not, once
God's will concerning the futurition of all these things had been posited.
Indeed, the entire gospel can be either true or false, since many things in
the gospel are fulfilled through the intervention of contingent agents. 4.
Nearly all the arguments against the conditional decree will overthrow this
decree. For I can have no faith or hope in Jehovah against evils which occur
contingently if, once his decree has been posited, they can equally happen or
not happen. What consolation would there be in God for saints almost expiring
under the burden of afflictions if contingent evils can either befall them or
not befall them despite God's most wise decree? Then

[1651 logical printed p. 97 | IA leaf 115 | PDF p. 115]

there is no reason for us to pray to God for the prolongation of life, good
health, the grace of faith and perseverance against temptations, constancy,
or patience in adversity. What else could God answer us here but this? “It
does not rest with me to advance or withhold those things, for either they
happen to you or they do not. Even unwillingly, I subscribe to the fateful
contingencies established apart from any act of my will.”

31. If possibilities are decreed by God, then God decreed to deliver his Son
through twelve legions of angels, for that deliverance was possible. But what
could have prevented that decree's fulfillment? Are the good angels less
obedient to God? They will not say so. Does God himself prevent his decrees
from being fulfilled? In that case the creation of the world, the raising up
of Abraham's children from stones, and the redemption of devils are decrees.
Who would listen to such prodigies? 2. A decree establishes nothing except
what appears most advisable to divine wisdom, and consequently that whose
contrary appears least expedient to God. Otherwise God would act without
counsel. Thus God decreed both that Christ's bones should be broken and that
they should not be broken. 3. What wise earthly judge decrees that guilty
people either be put to death or not put to death? Yet such a decree is
ascribed to God. 4. If God decrees whatever is possible, then he decreed the
sins of human beings and angels, both those which exist and those which are
possible. Will God therefore be the author of sin? And do sins occur by God's
will?

[1651 logical printed p. 98 | IA leaf 116 | PDF p. 116]

5. Even if it were granted that God had decreed absolutely nothing which
could happen—because he freely and not by necessity willed and created all
things outside himself—nevertheless everything which is now possible would
still have been possible. Things are possible in relation to God's
omnipotence, not to God's will. Possible things therefore exist apart from
every decree of God, and they need not be decreed. 6. From his infinite
foreknowledge God sees which things will be future and which will not. Yet by
no wisdom is something decreed to be future which the one decreeing saw would
never be future. For to what end would things never future be decreed? To no
certain end. Certainly the possible is said to be decreed in order to
preserve human freedom. But this seems to incline toward the view that God
does not foreknow all future things, but knows them when they occur—something
possible, so that new knowledge would thus accrue to God. 7. Whatever God
decrees to be future he freely decrees to be future. Therefore, if God had not
decreed possibilities, possibilities would not have been possible, which is a
contradiction. 8. Since the future adds something to the possible, it is asked
whether the future possible is decreed by God or not. If it is, then it is
decreed either absolutely, in which case the disjunctive decree perishes—for
the same thing is not both absolutely future and not future, since these are
contradictories—or conditionally. But under what condition? On the hypothesis
that it will be future. The question then recurs without end whether the
future hypothesis is decreed absolutely or conditionally. If the future is
not decreed at all, then

[1651 logical printed p. 99 | IA leaf 117 | PDF p. 117]

Jackson's first statement, that every possibility is decreed by God, is
false. A thing also begins to be future apart from God's will, and God's
providence is endangered. 9. Every argument of Scripture from God's decree
would be empty and null, for both this and not-this follow from the decree of
a possible thing. 10. The comparison between God's counsel, which cannot be
annulled, and human counsel, which can be annulled, in Psalm 33:10–11, is
foolish. For who calls into question whether God's counsel concerning a thing
which can either not exist or exist—as the possible can—may be annulled? On
this account human counsel would be as fixed as divine counsel, for what human
beings decree either can exist or not exist. 11. We pray to God in vain,
confide in him in vain, and patiently submit our will to him in prosperity or
adversity in vain, if God's will exercises no causality concerning a thing's
futurition or non-futurition, but only concerning its possibility. It would be
as if David prayed to God for the possibility of deliverance from Saul,
Absalom, and other enemies rather than for actual deliverance. Whatever
happens would have to be ascribed to fortune, not providence. Indeed, the
wicked would immediately possess every good thing which they ask from God's
will and would always be heard, for that good was possible through God's will.
Yet God's will could not will its futurition without a signal injury to the
new goddess of contingency.

32. Whoever says that God is and always will be indifferent and free to repay

[1651 logical printed p. 100 | IA leaf 118 | PDF p. 118]

each person according to his works utterly denies the eternity of the divine
decree. Jackson does this here. From eternity God so determined his will to
treat human beings according to their works that he cannot fail so to treat
them unless he wished to annul both promises and threats. Nevertheless, no
one asserts that God is compelled by the decree's force to treat them in this
way. If we speak of natural freedom, God executes his decrees as freely as he
makes them, although by hypothetical necessity he cannot fail to execute them.
But Jackson wishes God to repay human beings according to their works not even
by the hypothetical necessity of the decree. Rather, according to the rise
and extinction of angelic and human volitions, new free volitions arise in
God, since Jackson wishes irrevocable decrees to impede divine freedom.
Indeed, for Jackson, the futurition and actual existence of contingent effects
precede rather than follow God's decrees.

33. If God decreed a corresponding consequent for every possible antecedent,
tell me: what is the consequent of a world not created, or of Christ incarnate
but not crucified? For these are possible. Indeed, in this way God does not
intend his glory through possible means, for through a possible antecedent he
intends his own non-glorification just as much as his glorification. 2. What
wisdom is there in decreeing a consequent for an antecedent which God
foreknows will never be future? Would a mother be wise who prepared clothes
and food for a fetus which she knows with complete certainty has died in the
womb?

[1651 logical printed p. 101 | IA leaf 119 | PDF p. 119]

They reply: “Because the antecedent is possible, God therefore decreed the
consequent; and from many possible antecedents this one can in fact occur, so
it is advisable that a corresponding consequent be prepared for it.” Such
providence belongs to human beings ignorant of future contingents. But God
knew from eternity which possible antecedent would be future and which would
not. 3. A possible consequent is either contingent or necessary to the
possible antecedent. If it is contingent, once the possible antecedent is
posited, the possible consequent may never follow. Therefore God fitted no
consequent to a certain possible antecedent, and his decree is plainly
mistaken. If the consequent is necessary, it necessarily arises either from
the second cause or from the first. If from the second, I ask what necessary
consequent belongs to Judas's betrayal. Is Judas's bringing death upon himself
with a noose from a necessary second cause? If so, Judas did not sin by
committing himself to the noose. If this consequent is necessary from the
first cause, then the necessity of the sin came from God. The same can be said
of Ahithophel's sickness and sorrow after his counsel was rejected, which was
followed by his abominable crime. 4. The things this untutored innovator
dictates tend nowhere else than to make all things occur at fortune's
direction and not be determined by God's decrees—from among the many
possibilities, some to futurition and some to non-futurition. To preserve
fortune, or this invented contingency,

[1651 logical printed p. 102 | IA leaf 120 | PDF p. 120]

he wishes God to leave all things in possibility. He makes God's will entirely
dependent upon the futurition of the possible, as though it blindly fitted
some consequent lest perhaps the antecedent appear empty. 5. What does he
understand by a corresponding consequent? Either he understands a
corresponding reward according to the order of justice, or that which most
nearly follows from the possible antecedent. If the former, he wishes God to
have elected or reprobated no one by name, but by a certain confused decree to
have left life and damnation to everyone as the reward of possible good or
evil acts. This makes human beings elect rather than God. Further, according
to Arminius, many who believe and are justified for a time obtain no reward
according to the order of justice. For they fall from faith at the last moment
of life and obtain the reward of eternal damnation for nearly all their
preceding life. Many wicked people believe and repent at life's final end and
obtain eternal life. I ask, then, is eternal life the possible corresponding
consequent of an antecedent wicked and profligate life? 3. The Arminians do
not deny that sin is the punishment of antecedent sins, and Scripture
expressly teaches it. Thus sin will be a corresponding consequent decreed by
God. 4. Defection and final impenitence are possible for all the elect, and
perseverance in faith is possible for the reprobate. Therefore God decreed
destruction no less than life for the elect, and life no less than destruction
for the reprobate. Thus election is universal,

[Marginalia: The argument proceeds from Jackson's hypothesis.]

[1651 logical printed p. 103 (partial) | IA leaf 121 | PDF p. 121]

which is a contradiction in terms, and reprobation is universal. But if the
corresponding consequent is that which most nearly follows from the possible
antecedent, then for David's innocence God decreed Saul's mind full of malice
and envy as its corresponding consequent; for Christ's sincere preaching of
God's truth, he decreed the monstrous and horrible crucifixion of the Lord of
glory as its corresponding consequent; and for the Chaldeans' cruelty toward
God's Church, he decreed a most flourishing kingdom and an abundance of every
good. Yet no one will call this a corresponding consequent of cruelty. 6.
What God has decreed will stand and occur inevitably, Isaiah 14. But most
consequents corresponding to a possible antecedent are merely possible and
will never be future. Therefore they are not decreed by God. 7. By this
statement Jackson implies that all good or evil things are simply left in the
state of possibility and are not determined by God's will. Whatever good or
evil may befall us from contingent causes, this abolishes the use of prayers,
faith, hope, gratitude, and a mind submissive to God.
