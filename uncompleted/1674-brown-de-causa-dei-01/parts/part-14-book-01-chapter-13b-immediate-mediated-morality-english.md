He further distinguishes the moral into the *Immediate and the Mediated*, and
explains each in axioms 2 and 3. “The morals of the first table,” he says,
“which concern worshiping God alone, and doing so in a spiritual and holy
manner, relate immediately to the image of God. And since God exists
necessarily from himself, this morality, which rests immediately upon the
divine nature and attributes, is likewise as eternal, immutable, and necessary
as God himself and his attributes. For those fundamental truths and forms of
goodness—such as that God is to be loved and worshiped, and so forth—rest upon
God’s own essence and attributes, since they contain the image of God, which is
God represented. Hence God cannot change those essential truths or remove them
by any dispensation, for he would thereby deny himself; 2 Timothy 2:13.” Reply:
1. When he says that the morals of the first table relate immediately to the
image of God, either his meaning is that all the precepts of the first table
are immediately moral, or that only those precepts of the first table which
are moral relate immediately to the image of God. The statement is indeed put
forward so ambiguously that it can convey either of these meanings. If he
intends the *latter*, let him say which precepts he excepts. I know that he will
except the *fourth precept* of the first table. But does this precept relate
neither to immediate nor to mediated morals? Why, then, is it inserted into the
Decalogue, which consists of two tables? If he means the *former*, then the
fourth precept too will be moral. If he says that he refers here only to those
precepts of the first table which concern worshiping God alone, and doing so in
a spiritual and holy manner, I *reply*: when he says this, he does not express
his meaning clearly enough or without all ambiguity, because these words can
be taken either *broadly* or *strictly*. If they are taken *strictly*, the
meaning will be that only those precepts of the first table relate immediately
to the image of God which command that *God alone* is to be worshiped and that
God is to be worshiped *in a spiritual and holy manner*. But in that case he
will erase from the list of morals not only the *fourth* precept but also the
*second*. For the *first* commands that the one God is to be worshiped, and the
*Third* commands that he be worshiped in a spiritual and holy manner. If the
words are taken *more broadly*, so that they embrace everything which concerns
the worship of God and has been prescribed by him, then not only must the
*Second* precept, which commands observance of the lawful manner of worshiping
God prescribed by God himself, be referred here, but also the *fourth*, which
prescribes the solemn and principal time for the public worship of God. 2. I do
not sufficiently understand how the morality of these duties prescribed in the
first table can be said to be as eternal, immutable, and necessary as God
himself and his attributes. For from eternity there were no creatures who
worshiped God alone in a spiritual and holy manner, nor did the Lord by any
necessity of nature create angels or human beings who would so worship him
alone. Therefore, when the adversary says these things, either he implies that
God created rational creatures by a necessity of nature and did so from
eternity—something which he nevertheless denies later—or he ought to have
spoken differently. For it can readily be understood that from eternity God
necessarily and immutably was such that he alone ought to be worshiped, and in
a spiritual and holy manner, by rational creatures actually existing, and how
those actions of rational creatures actually existing can be called moral. But
how the morality of those duties prescribed in the first table can be said to
be as eternal, immutable, and necessary as God himself cannot be understood
with equal ease, unless we suppose that rational creatures existed from
eternity and as necessarily

[1674 logical printed p. 164 | IA leaf 182 | PDF p. 182]

as God himself existed. If he says that this morality presupposes the existence
of rational creatures, and that, once they are posited, this morality is
posited necessarily and immutably, I *reply*: how, then, do these Immediate
Morals differ from Mediated Morals? For he speaks of the latter in this way, as
we will hear later, and on this ground wishes the former to be distinguished
from them. 3. He would have those fundamental truths and forms of goodness,
such as that *God is to be loved and worshiped*, and so forth, rest upon God’s
own essence and attributes, since they contain the image of God, which is God
represented. But the Decalogue and its first table enjoin the human being’s
duty toward God and therefore presuppose the existence of the human being who
is bound to love and worship God. Thus, although by reason of his essence and
attributes God is necessarily and essentially one who ought to be worshiped
and loved by rational creatures that exist, this morality of worshiping and
loving God must not be said to be as eternal, immutable, and necessary as God
himself. For God existed immutably and necessarily from eternity; yet this
morality cannot be said to have existed from eternity, because its subject—the
human action of worshiping and loving God—did not exist from eternity. He must
therefore explain these matters better.

Let us hear what he calls the *Mediated* Moral; he would have it rest upon human
nature. “This morality,” he says, “was no more necessary with respect to God
than it was necessary for God to create human nature, since God was free in
this matter, as his nature and attributes did not require him to create human
nature. Thus human natural right has not been as necessary or eternal as
divine natural right. Yet once human nature and its creation in the image of
God are posited, and once the nature of things and their order are established,
God could not arrange these matters otherwise than in agreement with his
image, holiness, and attributes. From this flowed all the justice, purity, and
truth contained in the precepts of the second table; and therefore, once the
constitution of things is posited, it too flows from the image of God—or, what
amounts here to the same thing, it is deduced and becomes known from natural
reason. Thus human natural right rests upon human nature and can be deduced
from it just as fully as divine natural right rests upon the divine nature and
attributes and upon agreement with them. Therefore, whatever does not involve
a necessary agreement or repugnance with the divine or human nature cannot be
called morally good or evil.” Reply: 1. From this it appears that the precepts
of the second table depend in some measure upon God’s free good pleasure. He
himself acknowledges this on page 6, and therefore he does not wish them to be
absolutely moral. It is well that this Human Natural Right is not as necessary
or eternal as divine natural Right, for then, as it appears, God can change it
or remove it by some dispensation without denying himself. 2. It is most true
that, once human nature and its creation in the image of God are posited, and
once the nature of things and their order are established, God could not
arrange these matters otherwise than in agreement with his image, holiness,
and attributes. Nevertheless, the adversary will not deny that God could, had
he wished, have arranged all created things otherwise than we know he actually
arranged them, while always remaining in agreement with his attributes. And
from this it can be shown no less plainly that the fourth precept belongs to
Human Natural Right than do at least some precepts of the second table. For,
once human nature and its creation in the image of God are posited, and once
the nature of things and their order are established, it is as agreeable to
the image, holiness, and attributes of God that a human being solemnly worship
God on every seventh day—that is, that he devote a seventh portion of time,

[1674 logical printed p. 165 | IA leaf 183 | PDF p. 183]

dedicated to God, to divine exercises—as it is that he obey a magistrate, not
marry a wife within the prohibited degrees, and the like. 3. The question is
not from where the justice, purity, and truth contained in the precepts of the
second table emanated. The adversary will not deny that Justice, Purity, and
Truth are contained even in purely positive precepts. Hence it is clear that
these points contribute nothing to the matter at hand. The question instead
concerns the commands themselves which belong to Human Natural Right: namely,
from where they flow. 4. He says that the justice, purity, and truth contained
in the precepts which are referred to Human Natural Right flow from the image
of God, once, that is, the constitution of things has been posited. But does he
believe that all these things necessarily flow from the image of God once this
supposition, or this constitution, has been posited? They would then always
flow in this way, and thus God would never have permitted the sons of *Adam* to
marry their sisters, the Jews to carry off the goods of the *Egyptians*, or
*Abraham* even to attempt to sacrifice *Isaac*. It further follows that God
could not have instituted a different order of things, permitted marriage to
be celebrated within the prohibited degrees, instituted a community of goods,
or done similar things. 5. He would have the Justice, Purity, and Truth of
these precepts be deduced and become known from natural reason. But either he
means Primeval Natural Reason, pure and uncorrupted, or Reason as it is now,
corrupted and blind. If it is taken *in this latter sense*, then he is wrong to
say that flowing from the image of God is the same thing as being deduced and
becoming known from this corrupt reason. For the image of God, in itself and
insofar as it is the source of Purity and Justice, is, as he himself said
above, God represented; it is something pure and uncorrupted, and things can
flow from it other than those which our Natural Reason, now corrupted, can
deduce from it. If he speaks of uncorrupted Reason, he will never prove that
the Justice, Purity, and Truth even of the *fourth precept*, concerning the
*Sabbath* to be celebrated solemnly and in holiness every seventh day, could
not have been deduced and known from it. 6. When he says that human Natural
Right rests upon human nature just as fully as divine Natural Right rests upon
the divine nature, he implies that human nature would be utterly overthrown
and abolished if these precepts had not been given or had been altered in any
way, just as God’s own essence would be abolished if divine Natural Right were
abolished or altered. But who will say that these precepts have so great a
connection with human nature? Did God say that a human being was not human
when he commanded or permitted the daughters of *Adam* to marry their brothers,
a Jew in a certain case to marry his brother’s widow, or *Abraham* to sacrifice
his son, and similar things? 7. It is not enough for him that what is called
moral should have an agreement with human and divine nature unless it also has
a necessary agreement. But is he able to show this necessary agreement with
human nature in all the precepts of the second table? Let him make the attempt;
I do not see it. 8. The chief error of this learned man is that he thinks all
moral precepts which are reduced to Natural Right for us take their origin
solely from principles of rectitude innate in the human being even in this
corrupted state—for he does not explain himself so clearly here that the
contrary can be evident—and not also from God’s works, from the manner in which
he created all things, and from what, after the creation of all things, he set
before the human being for imitation. We showed above how a human being’s moral
duty arose from God’s works and certain circumstances of creation—a point
which he himself seems later to concede—and how Nature, or sound and

[1674 logical printed p. 166 | IA leaf 184 | PDF p. 184]

uncorrupted human Reason, distinctly perceived this. How these points can be
applied to the precept concerning the Sabbath we will show later in its proper
place.
