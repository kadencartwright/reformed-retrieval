### Chapter II

##### On the monarchy of Peter

The question is: Was Peter established by Christ himself as universal monarch
of the whole Church in Christ's place? The Papists affirm it; we deny it.

##### Bellarmine's arguments

##### Thesis I

Bell.: *This is proved from two passages of the Gospel, in one of which this
monarchy is promised to Peter and in the other bestowed.* Prot. Whatever power
over the Church we read was promised and bestowed by Christ upon his disciples,
we also read was exercised and employed by them themselves, Mark 16:15 and 20.
Therefore Bellarmine betrays his whole case here at the outset when he contends
that this monarchy was promised and given to Peter in two passages, yet cannot
produce a single passage from which he might prove with any appearance of
plausibility that Peter accepted and employed it.

2. Bell., Matthew 16:18–19: *You are Peter, etc. The plain and obvious sense
of these words is that we should understand the primacy of the whole Church to
have been promised to Peter under two metaphors.* Prot. If the sense of these
words expressed under a double metaphor is so obvious and plain, then that
same sense ought to be explained in other passages without metaphors; yet not
even Bellarmine affirms this. 2. If that sense is so plain and obvious, then
the Apostles would have understood these words in the same sense as Bellarmine.
But it is apparent that they did not understand that a primacy of this kind had
been given to Peter, because several times afterward they contended among
themselves over who was first. 3. It is so well known that various Fathers, at
least sometimes, understood these words in a different sense from Bellarmine
that the Papists themselves are not accustomed to

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 152 | printed p. 142 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

deny it. 4. All the old Scholastics, together with their master, understand
them differently when they teach that the power treated here is priestly,
given first to the Apostles and through them to others. See the commentaries
on Lombard, book 4, distinction 18, and Alexander of Hales, part 4, question
20, where at member 6, article 3, he also contends against Bellarmine that the
keys were given in this passage, not promised. It is also known to all that
the sacred faculty of theology at Paris dissents greatly from Bellarmine about
the sense of these words, as do the Councils of Constance and Basel. For they
contend that far less is attributed to Peter in this passage than Bellarmine
wishes, just as, on the other side, the canon lawyers arrogate far more from it
to Peter and the Roman Pontiff than Bellarmine himself grants, as can be seen
in Carerius, *On the Pontiff*. Nor has that Roman judge of the faith yet
settled this dispute. Therefore, the sense proved by Bellarmine is neither
plain and obvious nor common to all Catholics.

3. Bell.: *It is openly deduced from the text that the person who was called
Peter is the rock—not as he was a particular person, but as Pastor and head of
the Church.* Prot. 1. Neither from this text nor from any other can it be
deduced that Peter was head of the Church. Therefore, if he was the rock
inasmuch as he was the head, he was the rock in no respect. 2. We can safely
grant that Peter as an Apostle, together with the other Apostles, was in some
manner a rock and foundation of the Church, one among many, so that Peter's
monarchy is not proved from this but overturned. That is called a rock or
foundation which supports the whole building. The Church is firmly and really
supported by the real power and efficacy of Christ, and also doctrinally by
that settled institution and doctrine by which Christ and his power and
efficacy are declared unto sure faith in him. In the former sense Christ is
the Church's one foundation. In the latter sense, because doctrine concerning
Christ is the foundation, those who declared this doctrine to the Church with
infallible certainty are also called foundations by a metonymy of the cause:
not Peter alone, but the other Apostles, Revelation 20:21, and also the Prophets,
Ephesians 2:20. Thus the three interpretations of this passage which occur
among the ancients agree very well and come together as one:

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 153 | printed p. 143 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Christ is the rock; the confession, faith, or doctrine concerning Christ is
the rock; and Peter himself, as an Apostle of Christ, is also metonymically a
partial rock. Nothing is sown or reaped here for Peter's fictitious monarchy.

4. Bell.: *Peter's faith in relation to Peter is the foundation, and that for
two reasons: 1. Because on account of the merit of his faith Peter obtained
that he should be the foundation of the Church. 2. Because Peter is above all
the foundation of the Church in this respect: since his faith cannot fail, he
ought to sustain all others in the faith.* Prot. 1. This is the same as if
Bellarmine had said, “Faith is the foundation in relation to Peter, and Peter
is the foundation in relation to faith,” which is a circular explanation of a
dizzy dogma. 2. Neither Peter's faith nor Peter can in any sense be called the
foundation of the Church except in relation to Christ, who is its one real
foundation. 3. If faith merited that Peter should be the foundation of the
Church, then it did this—according to Bellarmine's distinction—either as
considered absolutely or in relation to Peter. If in the first manner, then
the ancient Prophets, the Blessed Virgin, Simeon, Zechariah, John the Baptist,
the Apostles, and the other disciples also merited the same thing, for in this
very tenth chapter Bellarmine affirms that all these had already believed
Christ to be the Son of the living God. If in the latter manner, while there
was some singular power of meriting in Peter's person antecedent to his faith,
which could impart so great a power even to its act beyond what the persons of
others can do. 4. If Peter had merited this dignity at this time, he would
also in some manner have received it immediately, which Bellarmine nevertheless
denies. The promise of reward is accustomed to precede merit, not follow it;
and here too it had necessarily to precede, unless Bellarmine says this merit
arose from the bare worthiness of the work or person without any preceding
covenant. 5. The faith of the other Apostles could no more fail than Peter's
faith. Nor did Peter confirm Paul in the faith any more than Paul confirmed
Peter. 6. If the firmness and efficacy of faith for sustaining others is the
reason for being a foundation, then those among

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 154 | printed p. 144 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Peter's successors who did not have such faith were not foundations. Cajetan,
in *On the Primacy of the Roman Church*, chapter 2, therefore favors those
Pontiffs more when he teaches that by these words the office of sustaining the
Church is referred to Peter the man as doing nothing. This can nevertheless
also be Bellarmine's meaning when he says Peter is the foundation because he
ought to sustain, not because he sustains.

5. Bell.: *By the building of the Church upon the rock it is signified that
the government of the whole Church, especially concerning the faith, was
committed to Peter. For it is proper to a foundational rock to govern and
sustain the whole building.* Prot. 1. Thus, according to Bellarmine, the
explanation of this metaphor is: to establish the Church upon Peter is to
establish Peter over the Church. Is this not an excellent proportion in the
similitude? 2. Nowhere in all Scripture does *to build* signify to commit
government, but always to establish or to cause to grow and promote. 3. To
commit this monarchical government to a man, especially concerning the faith,
is to establish a man as lord of the faith, which the Apostle abominates in 2
Corinthians 1:24. 4. It is neither proper nor common to a foundation, as a
foundation, to govern the building. Thomas, in book 3, distinction 23,
question 2, final article, and II-II, question 7, article 7, reply 4, teaches
that only two things are required for the character of a foundation in
spiritual matters, so that it may be spoken of metaphorically by similarity
to a material foundation: *namely, that it be first and precede the other
parts, and that it be connected with the other parts, sustaining the whole
building.* 5. Faith too is the foundation of the Church, and all the Apostles
were foundations; but neither faith nor the Apostles exercise monarchical
government.

6. Bell. 1: *Faith is not properly called the foundation.* Prot. More properly
than Peter. The Church is expressly said to be built upon the faith, Jude 20;
this is not said of Peter.

Bell. 2: *Everything must be understood in a way suited to the natures of the
things. Faith is the foundation as a principle and as an indispensable gift;
but Peter as ruler, for such is the dependence of one man upon another.* Prot.
1. It is not suited to Peter's nature that he should be monarch or lord of the
Church's faith, or even Pastor of each and every believer. 2.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 155 | printed p. 145 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Faith inwardly unites and joins the Church to its foundation, Christ, and thus
comes nearer to the nature of a foundation than an external ruler does. 3.
One man depends upon another not only as a ruler but also as a procreating and
preserving cause, as children depend upon their father. Hence children are
also called the building of their parents, Genesis 16:2 and 30:3, Deuteronomy
25:9, which is also the Hebrew etymology of the word *Ben* from *Bunah*.
Therefore the Church, insofar as it was begotten through Peter's ministry, can
be called his building and can depend upon him as a father, not as a monarch.

Bell. 3: *All the Apostles were foundations of the Church, equally and in
equal measure: 1. Because as the first founders they founded churches equally.
2. Because they equally revealed the doctrine upon which the Church's faith
rests. 3. Because they all governed the universal Church. But with regard to
the manner of government, there was this difference: the others had the
fullness of the most ample power as Apostles, but Peter as ordinary Pastor and
also their head.* Prot. 1. If this was so, then nothing proper to Peter is
signified in this passage by the building upon the rock. For Bellarmine was
just saying that government simply is signified; this singular manner of
governing, which alone he makes proper to Peter, is declared by no word or
syllable of this text. 2. If all the Apostles had *the fullness of the most
ample power over the universal Church*, then they were certainly Peter's
equals with respect to power, for Peter had no greater power. 3. No ordinary
power of any Pastor is greater than Apostolic power, 1 Corinthians 12:28:
*first Apostles.* 4. An extraordinary power given by God is always accustomed
to have greater certainty of direction than an ordinary power, as can be seen
in Moses and Aaron, in the Prophets and priests. By this difference,
therefore, the other Apostles are elevated above Peter. 5. It remains for
Bellarmine to prove that Peter was head of the Apostles by an eminent degree
of power, for we shall not grant this principle to one who begs it.

7. Bell.: *The person to whom it is said, “I shall give you the keys,”* Matthew
16:19, *was Simon son of Jonah.* Prot. We do not deny this, but

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 156 | printed p. 146 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

say that the keys were promised to Simon Peter in such a way that they were at
the same time also promised to the other Apostles and in some manner also to
the whole Church, whose person Peter then bore, as Augustine speaks in
*Tractates on John* 50 and 124.

8. Bell.: *Peter could bear the person of the Church in two ways: either
historically, so that something truly done by him signified something to be
done by the Church, or parabolically, so that although nothing was done by him,
it nevertheless signified something to be done by the Church. But we say that
Peter bore the person of the Church in the former manner, so that he himself
truly and principally received the keys and at the same time also signified
that the Church would afterward receive them in its own manner.* Prot. We
admit the distinction between signification by means of something truly done
and that which is by means of a parable. For a pure type differs from a mixed
type, in which a thing is done absolutely according to its own nature, yet so
that a relation of the sign to something else is added besides. But 1.
Bellarmine badly defines each manner, for something done as a type does not
always signify something to be done afterward by another; it often denotes
something done by another at the same time. Thus in Genesis 17:2 Abraham
entered into a covenant with God and represented his seed, not as entering
afterward into a similar covenant, but as covenanting at the same time in him,
Deuteronomy 29:14–15: *Nor do I make my covenant with you alone.* Nor is there
mere fiction in every pure type, as Bellarmine says. 2. In that historical
type which Bellarmine notes, the person of the one who is signified is not
properly borne. Abraham, having two sons, was a figure of God, yet in this he
did not properly bear the person of God. 3. Therefore a similitude is either
true or fictitious. A true similitude is either merely applied to illustrate
another thing, to which it had no singular relation in itself, or appointed
for that end; and this appointed similitude is properly called a type. An
appointed representation, moreover, either merely represents, so that it does
not perform the same thing which it represents, or represents it by performing
it. By performing it, it either represents a similar thing to be done
afterward by another, or a thing done at the same time by another. In this
latter manner, he who bears the person of the community of which he is a part

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 157 | printed p. 147 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

acts both in his own name and in that of his community, so that in part he
acts, and in part he represents those acting through him at the same time. In
this manner Peter, in receiving the keys, represented the Church, because he
himself truly received them for himself, and at the same time the Church
received them in his person. Similarly, at the end of Matthew, when Christ
said to the Apostles, *I shall be with you*, they received this promise as
pertaining to themselves and at the same time in the name of the Church, which
is signified by the words that follow: *until the consummation of the age.*

9. Bell.: *Peter's person is described precisely. He had said, “You are the
Christ, the Son of the living God.” To him it was said, “Go behind me, Satan;
you are an offense to me.”* Prot. These things prove that the keys were given
to Peter, but not to Peter alone. For that confession was not proper to Peter,
nor was it proper to Peter to deserve the name Satan because of an offense.

10. Bell.: *Peter was not at that time the legate or vicar of the Church. Who
had imposed this province upon him?* Prot. 1. At that time he was equally the
legate or vicar of the Apostles in John 6:68, Acts 2:14, and elsewhere, when he
was accustomed to answer in the name of all. 2. With respect to the confession
he issued, this province was imposed upon him partly by the consent of the
Apostles known to him, and partly by a certain singular fervor and readiness
of the Spirit by which he was impelled to go before others. 3. With respect to
receiving the keys, Christ imposed this person upon him, who also often imposed
that person upon all the Apostles so that they received his promises in the
name of the Church, as in Matthew 28:20. 4. It is most certain that Peter was
not at that time the legate or vicar of the Roman Pontiffs. For who had imposed
this province upon him? We therefore cannot suppose that Peter received the
keys in their name.

11. Bell.: *Peter answered in the name of all, not as some herald but as
prince and head, since the others did not know what answer chiefly to give.*
Prot. 1. The enumeration is not sufficient. Between a herald and a prince
there is a third possibility, namely, a member of the community answering in
the name of all. 2. The others did not lack knowledge of what to answer:

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 158 | printed p. 148 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*for many had already believed Christ to be the Son of the living God: the
Blessed Virgin, Simeon, Zechariah, John the Baptist, the Apostles, and the other
disciples.* These are Bellarmine's words in chapter 10.

12. Bell.: *If Peter had answered in the name of all, he did so either because
the others had entrusted this province to him, or because he knew what they
would answer. Neither is true. Not the first, because he learned this by the
Father's revelation. Not the second, because the revelation was made to him
alone. Moreover, he would have indicated this in some manner, as in John 6,
“We believe,” nor would Christ have approved it without qualification.* Prot.
Indeed, nothing prevents the latter cause from being true, and many things
commend it. For 1. although Peter learned this by revelation, he could at this
time declare that revealed truth by the consent of the others. 2. This
revelation was not made to Peter alone, as appears from Bellarmine's words set
down in the preceding thesis, unless he says that they believed the same thing
without revelation; yet this would in no way have prevented Peter from knowing
what they would answer. 3. From John 6:69 it plainly appears both that the
Apostles had believed this before and that Peter had previously confessed the
same thing in the name of all when he answered, *We believe and know that you
are that Christ, the Son of the living God.* Augustine, *Harmony of the
Gospels*, book 2, chapter 45; Chrysostom, homily 59 on Matthew; Jerome on
Matthew 14; Toletus on John 6; and Jansen in his *Harmony* teach that the
account in John 6 preceded this event in Matthew 16. Reason also establishes
it, because the miracle mentioned in John 6:5–15 is the same as that narrated
in Matthew 14:15–21, and what follows is the same. The sequence of the account
establishes that Peter's confession in Matthew 16 was made some interval after
that miracle, but verse 22 shows that the one contained in John 6 was made the
next day. 4. He did not use the plural number as in John 6 because he did not
explain the act of believing and the persons believing, as there, but only the
truth which they believed. By this ellipsis the other Apostles are no more
excluded than Peter himself, for he no more said *I believe* than *we believe*,
but simply, *You are the Christ.* Martha expressed both in John 11:27: *I
believe that you are that Christ, etc.* Yet she

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 159 | printed p. 149 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

neither believed more nor participated in a higher degree of the life promised
to this faith in verse 26 than her sister Mary did. 5. The same reason can also
be given for why this confession is approved without qualification, without
the exception found in John 6:70—namely, because the persons were not
mentioned in the words of the confession. But it is not necessary that the
same things always be repeated by Christ in the same manner. Nor had the
Apostles forgotten the warning which, as we have shown, preceded, so that they
needed to hear it impressed anew so soon.

*Against this restriction of the keys, as though promised to Peter alone,
there are many arguments of our men, but we shall press only one.*

12. Prot. If they were promised to Peter alone, then they were also given to
Peter alone. But they were given to all the Apostles, not to Peter alone,
Matthew 18:18; John 20:22–23.

Bell. 1: *There is no difficulty concerning the latter passage, for it is
certain that not all the power of the keys is given through those words, but
only the power of order for remitting sins. Matthew 16, however, is not
restricted to sins. Men are bound not only by sins but also by laws.
Furthermore, to retain a sin—that is, to leave a man in his own state—is less
than to bind—that is, to impose a new bond upon him.* Prot. 1. That entire
distinction between all the power of the ministerial keys and the power of
order for retaining and loosing sins is a Papist and recent invention, of
which no traces appear in the whole New Testament or even among the ancient
Fathers. It does not even have the approval of the old Scholastics. Alexander
of Hales, part 4, question 20, member 8: *The keys are given or conferred in
and with the priestly order and cannot be separated from it.* But in question
21, member 1, article 4, so that he can defend the monarchy of bishops, he is
forced to deny that loosing and binding in the judicial forum are acts of the
keys. He similarly pronounces concerning indulgences in question 23, member
3, that *the key is not their whole rationale*. Thus Thomas in the
*Supplement*, question 19, article 5,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 160 | printed p. 150 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

denies that the power of jurisdiction in the forum of causes is *the key of
heaven*. Bonaventure too, in book 4, distinction 18, is forced by the same
reason to distinguish between the power of the keys and the power of the sword
given to Peter, where he nevertheless refutes Bellarmine in express words:
*The power of the keys*, he says, *was promised in Matthew 16 and given in John
20: “Receive the Holy Spirit,” etc.* Scotus on the same passage teaches that
*the commissioned key, which can be entrusted to a man, is given to a priest
in the reception of order.* The whole force of the key is also explained by
its definition in the Master at the same place: *The key is the power of
binding and loosing by which the ecclesiastical judge ought to receive the
worthy into the kingdom and exclude the unworthy from it.* The same Master,
with all his disciples, teaches that this whole power belongs to order. 2.
That exception—that *in John 20, but not Matthew 16, the power is restricted
to sins*—is utterly inept. For all the Scholastics restrict the whole use of
the keys to sin as the obstacle by which heaven is closed to men unless sin is
removed by the power of the keys and heaven thereby opened. Moreover, *whatever
you bind* is plainly restricted also to sins, Matthew 18:18. 3. The general
particle *whatever* is also pressed in vain and ridiculously, for in Matthew
18, where only sins are treated, it could appear to be extended more broadly
still in the plural number, *whatever things you bind*. This can also be
opposed to the clever little distinction of certain Papists who affirm that
the keys of heaven were given to all the Apostles but the keys of the heavens
to the Apostle Peter. 4. Moreover, this is utterly absurd: by *to bind*, which
must necessarily signify there something grievous and fearful to men, to
understand the promulgation of ecclesiastical laws. 5. Nothing more futile can
be said than so to distinguish between *binding* and *retaining* that the
Apostles in John 20 are understood not to have received the power of
excommunicating or doing anything by those words, *Whose sins you retain*, but
only the power of doing nothing and leaving men in their own state. *To retain
sins*, no less than *to bind sinners*, is determined by all the Scholastics to
be one and the same act of the key as closing heaven, just as *to remit*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 161 | printed p. 151 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*sins* and *to loose a sinner* are one and the same act of the key as opening
heaven.

Bell. 2: *Matthew 18:18 gives nothing to the Apostles, but only promises,
explains, and foretells how great a power the Apostles and their successors
would have in their time. But the keys were given to all the Apostles in John
20: the key of jurisdiction in the words, “As the Father sent me, I also send
you”; and the key of order in these, “Receive the Holy Spirit; whose sins you
remit,” etc. Yet they were given as to delegates and with a certain
subjection to Peter, as appears from John 21, “Feed my sheep.”* Prot. 1.
Although before the resurrection the keys were not given to the Apostles to be
exercised in their full force, because before then they had not been fully sent
by Christ, it nevertheless appears that they were in some manner given from
the fact that not only the Twelve but also the other seventy disciples had
previously been sent, Matthew 10; Luke 10. 2. The present question is not when
the keys were given, but whether the same power of the keys was promised to
all in Matthew 18:18 which, according to Bellarmine's opinion, was promised to
Peter alone in Matthew 16. Bellarmine does not deny this. 3. Since what is
promised to all in Matthew 18 is the same as what is promised to Peter in
Matthew 16, the same must also be given to them somewhere. Therefore, either
the same is given in John 20, or let Bellarmine give us some other passage in
which it is given. 4. Bellarmine expressly contradicts his own defense. Until
now he contended that the keys were promised to Peter alone; now he grants
that they were both promised and handed over to all. 5. How absurd and
incoherent are those inventions? *The Apostles were made priests at the Last
Supper; they received the key of jurisdiction in John 20:21, “As the Father
sent me, so I also send you”; but the key of order in John 20:22–23, “Receive
the Holy Spirit; whose sins you remit.”* Were they priests without the keys?
Did they have jurisdiction with priesthood but without order? Is the power of
order communicated after jurisdiction was given? Who ever raved such dreams,
I do not say from among the Fathers, but from the very dregs of the
Scholastics? How much more correctly Thomas on John 20 says: *First the office
is enjoined: “As he sent me”; second, fitness for the office is given them by
the gift of the Holy Spirit; third,*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 162 | printed p. 152 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*the fruit of the gift is set forth: “Whose sins you remit,” etc.* 6. Nothing
can be elicited from the keys, whether promised or given, concerning a
singular delegation of the other Apostles with subjection to Peter. Nothing
of this kind is signified anywhere in Scripture, and finally Bellarmine
himself could not explain what this subjection was; as though it were an
indefinite individual, he calls it *a certain* subjection, but does not know
what kind.

12. Bell.: *All Catholics understand by the keys given the highest power over
the whole Church, and this is proved: 1. From the metaphor of the keys, which
signify ecclesiastical primacy, Isaiah 22, Isaiah 9, Revelation 3; the handing
over of keys at the surrender of a city and when a steward is established in
a house also signifies primacy. 2. From the metaphor of binding and loosing,
because to bind is to command and punish, and to loose is to remit sins and to
dispense in law, vows, oaths, etc.* Prot. 1. If by the highest power
Bellarmine understands monarchical power, what he says about all Catholics is
as plainly false even if he embraces only Papists under that name. For the
master of those Catholics, book 4, distinction 18, defines the keys differently,
so that they are common to all priests and restricted to remitting or retaining
sins. So too Bonaventure, Thomas, Durandus, Scotus, and Alexander of Hales, as
was explained before. So too today the doctors of the Sorbonne, together with
the French Papist Church. 2. The Scholastics are also accustomed expressly to
distinguish among the key of authority, the key of excellence, and the key of
ministry: Bonaventure on book 4, distinction 18; Thomas, part 3, question 64,
article 4; or, as Scotus has it in his *Reportata*, among the principal key,
the commissioned key with preeminence, and the common commissioned key. They
teach that the key given to Peter together with other men was not that of
authority, excellence, or preeminence, but of common ministry. They indeed
apply this to the sacraments, but for no other reason than that they thought
the keys given properly pertained to the sacraments. Therefore, they
understood no monarchy to have been included in the keys. 3. If monarchical
power of this sort is understood by the keys, then all the Apostles were
monarchs, for Bellarmine just now conceded that the same keys were given to
all. 4. Although

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 163 | printed p. 153 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

an ecclesiastical primacy granted to a man is signified neither in Isaiah 22
nor anywhere else in all Scripture, we nevertheless do not deny that keys
sometimes denote primacy and dominion. But it belongs to childish sophistry
to infer from this that they designate monarchy in Matthew 16. If we pleased
to be so clever, we would say thus: A key is *knowledge*, Luke 11:52;
therefore, in receiving the key, Peter received nothing other than knowledge.
5. Someone can ministerially command, punish, and remit sins even though he is
not a monarch. Moreover, the power of dispensing is not the key of heaven but
of hell, as is also that power of commanding which the Roman Pontiff is
accustomed to arrogate to himself.

*Thus far concerning the promise of the keys in Matthew 16. Their delivery in
John 21 follows.*

14. Bell.: *To Peter alone it was said, “Feed my sheep,”* John 21:17. Prot.
These words, *It was said to Peter alone*, can admit a double sense: 1. That
the sense is that the exhortation was not directed at that time to anyone but
Peter. 2. That the sense is that a similar exhortation was never directed to
anyone else, or did not pertain to anyone else as to the thing itself. We
grant the former sense, and nothing follows from it that can in any manner
serve to establish Peter's monarchy. Bellarmine's proofs by which he confirms
that sense are therefore superfluous. In the latter sense Bellarmine will
never prove what he affirms. For 2. As to the thing itself, the same was said
to all the Apostles, Matthew 28:19: *Teach all nations.* For in Scripture,
when *to feed* is applied to ecclesiastical ministry, it signifies the same as
to teach, Jeremiah 3:10: *They shall feed with knowledge and understanding.*
2. Since all the Apostles had also previously been sent as shepherds to feed
the sheep, Matthew 9:36 together with 10:6, and now with ampler commands were
sent to accomplish the same work, Matthew 28; John 20, and afterward also
performed it diligently, as appears from Acts and their Epistles, no one of
sound mind can doubt that they too had been told to feed Christ's sheep. 3.
This pertains

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 164 | printed p. 154 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

also to ordinary pastors or presbyters, Acts 20:28; 1 Peter 5:1.

Bell.: *Others feed particular churches, but Peter the universal Church.
Although these words also pertain properly and principally to Peter alone,
they nevertheless apply in their own manner to all bishops, insofar as they
are called by Peter into a share of his solicitude.* Prot. 1. Therefore, to
feed is not to exercise monarchy, for presbyters are not monarchs. 2. It
pertained to Peter to feed the universal Church, not affirmatively, as though
he supplied necessary food to each and every believer, but negatively, insofar
as he was bound to no particular Church and excluded from none so that he
could not feed it. 3. He did not at the same time feed through others all the
parts of the Church to which he devoted his labor, as a monarch, but
successively and by himself, as a minister. 4. The office of the other
Apostles was equally universal with Peter's. 5. Nowhere in the whole New
Testament is any pastor read to have been called by Peter into a share of his
solicitude. Bellarmine confesses that the Apostles were sent as immediately by
Christ as Peter himself.

15. Bell.: *To feed signifies to provide and procure food for another, and
then to lead, protect, preside over, govern, and chastise. This is apparent
from the verb* ποιμαίνειν *itself, which signifies to feed by governing and
presiding.* Prot. 1. Even if all these things are granted, they nevertheless
do not suffice to establish a monarch unless one person alone does them with
the highest power of dominion. For all these things are also done by ordinary
ministers of particular churches. 2. Pastors who do these things and are
called ποιμένες in Ephesians 4:11 are not monarchs. 3. When it is said of
ecclesiastical ministers, nowhere in all Scripture does *to feed* signify only
to procure food, but also to minister it. 4. Peter was commanded not only
ποιμαίνειν but again and again βόσκειν in John 20:15, 17, which is to minister
food. 5. By this reasoning the Roman Pontiffs have fallen from their monarchy,
because they do not feed.

Bell.: *They exercise pastoral acts by binding, loosing, dispensing, judging,
creating bishops, and preaching through others.* Prot. 1. Therefore, he feeds
with the sword, Micah 5:6, with wormwood and

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 165 | printed p. 155 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

the juice of hemlock, Jeremiah 9:15. 2. Therefore, they do not discharge the
chief office of a shepherd, which is to feed by preaching, Matthew 28:19, and
thus are not the chief shepherds. 3. If the Pontiff preaches through others,
in this he does no more than any little woman can perform.

16. Bell.: *The universal Church, even the Apostles themselves, are commended
to Peter as little sheep in the words, “My sheep.”* Prot. We do not deny that
the universal Church was commended to Peter in the sense previously explained.
It was so by the same reasoning by which the whole world was commended to all
and each of the Apostles, at the end of Matthew: *Teach all nations*; Acts 1:
*to the uttermost part of the earth.* In the same particular Church at Ephesus,
Paul commended *the whole flock* to all and each of the Presbyters, Acts 28:2.
The other Apostles were commended to Peter just as Peter was commended to them,
John 13:34 and 15:12: *Love one another as I have loved you.* Not to him as
Pastor and monarch. Peter fed Paul no more than Paul fed Peter.

Bell.: *Lambs are repeated twice to signify the two peoples, Jewish and
Gentile; and sheep once to signify the Apostles and bishops. Or lambs, little
sheep, and sheep signify beginners, those advancing, and the perfect. Or lambs
signify the people, little sheep the Presbyters, and sheep the bishops.* Prot.
Or rather, sheep, little sheep, and lambs, if the terms had been distinguished
here in this manner—which is false—would have signified exactly the same ones,
as in Matthew 10:16; Luke 10:3; Acts 8:32. Peter himself certainly understood
no other mystery in that threefold repetition of the exhortation to feed than
in that threefold question, *Do you love me?* by which he was affected with a
sorrowful sadness, John 21:17. So far was he from exulting in it as in the
communication of a threefold dominion. But it was fitting that a threefold
confession and also a threefold exhortation be opposed to a threefold denial.
Bell. 2: *The pronoun “my,” without restriction, plainly signifies all the
sheep.* Prot. Although the thing itself can be granted in a sound sense, as
was said, the reasoning is nevertheless invalid. For *my sheep* is taken not
only universally but also particularly, as in Ezekiel 34. If a father about
to die appointed several guardians for his children,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 166 | printed p. 156 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

and said to one, “Take diligent care of my children,” no one would infer from
this that he had received greater power than the other guardians or that the
care of all the children had been committed to him more than to the others.
Bell. 3: *To feed the sheep is to have care of the sheepfold, but there is only
one sheepfold.* Prot. Yet that one sheepfold is cared for by him who, to the
extent of his strength, takes thought for those parts of it which he can
approach. Bell. 4: *To commend some indefinite sheep is not the act of a wise
provider; it is also to commend none, because none can be identified.* Prot.
The sheep are commended in the same manner as the nations at the end of
Matthew. Although the labors were not distributed by those words, the same
Provider who gave this command generally nevertheless attended the Apostles
particularly by his Spirit, who led them into their distinct, as it were,
provinces, Acts 13:2, 4; 16:9; 18:9; and 20:22. Thus those sheep which were
indefinite in the word were made definite by the Spirit attending the word.

17. Although many other things can be opposed to this fictitious elevation of
Peter into an ecclesiastical monarchy, we shall press only one here, which is
drawn from the comparison of Peter and Paul as equals, Galatians 2 and 2
Corinthians 11 and 12. Bell. 1: *Paul's purpose in the Epistle to the Galatians
was not to show that he was not subject to Peter with respect to government,
but that his Gospel was equally divine and received immediately from Christ,
just as Peter's Gospel was.* Prot. 1. Although Paul chiefly regarded the
certainty of his Gospel, yet, employing this as a means, he maintains his own
authority. Otherwise, in the comparison, the mention of *those who are held in
esteem* and *who are considered to be pillars* would have no force, for
immediate revelation pertained equally to all. 2. If Paul was Peter's equal in
immediate revelation, then Peter certainly did not feed Paul or confirm him as
Pastor. 3. If Paul received immediately from God those things pertaining to
doctrine, then also those pertaining to government, since that doctrine is the
rule of government. Could he govern others without error but not himself? 4.
The Apostle says, *They added nothing to me*, Galatians 2:6. Bell.: *Indeed,
something in government.* The Apostle: *I was inferior in nothing*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 167 | printed p. 157 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*to the chief Apostles*, 2 Corinthians 11:5; 12:11. Bell.: *Indeed, in
government.* Are these not splendid little glosses? 5. Bellarmine could not
explain what that subjection with respect to government was. Monarchy does
not consist in the word *government*, but in the thing itself.

Bell. 2: *The right hands of fellowship in verse 9 do not prevent us from
understanding that Peter was greater than Paul in the office of governing*, 1
Kings 23:25. Prot. They plainly prevent it altogether if taken together with
the opposition the text has to verse 6: *They added nothing to me; indeed, on
the contrary, they gave me the right hands of fellowship.* Bell. 3: *“What
they were formerly is of no concern to me,” verse 6, is said in praise: though
they formerly had been ignorant men and very lowly fishermen, they were now
great Apostles.* Prot. 1. Paul was not concerned to excuse himself for deigning
to approach Apostles who had been fishermen, but to meet the objection of
those who extolled the other Apostles in order to depress Paul's authority.
Therefore, he does not deny that he considers the previous private condition
of these men—for to what purpose would that be?—but denies that it matters to
him how long ago they had associated with Christ and similar things. 2.
Whoever reduces that sentence to logic will see its sense to be that, although
those Apostles were considered to be something because of external privileges
of person which had formerly been granted to them and not to Paul, they
nevertheless added nothing to Paul.

Bell. 4: *The division of which Paul speaks in Galatians 2:7, 9 was not of
jurisdiction but of provinces for more convenient preaching: so that Peter
would devote his principal labor to the conversion of the Jews, while Paul,
on the contrary, would chiefly pursue the conversion of the Gentiles.* Prot.
As these things are true, so they have nothing to do with the matter. For 1.
they in no way prevent that comparison between Peter and Paul, by this
division and the *just as* in verse 7, from establishing them as equals. 2.
Bellarmine previously conceded that they were equals in authority to preach.
But if Peter's jurisdiction had been greater and singularly universal, the
Apostles would undoubtedly have warned Paul of the fact, and Paul the churches
in his sermons and Epistles. But they warned Paul

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 168 | printed p. 158 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

only about care for the poor in verse 10, and Paul everywhere pleads the cause
of the poor. Yet descending to the smallest details in his Epistles, he
nowhere says anything about Peter's singular jurisdiction and government.
Bell. 5: *A lesser may reprove a greater, provided that the matter demands it
and due reverence is preserved, just as Paul reproved Peter*, Galatians 2:11.
Prot. 1. *No mortal presumes to reprove the Pope's faults*, distinction 40. 2.
A just cause and due reverence are required also in the reproof of equals. 3.
The singular reverence owed to a monarch does not appear in this reproof. 4.
Paul's purpose in recounting this reproof was to show that he was in no way
inferior to Peter. Thus Cajetan: *It is not enough for Paul to have shown
himself equal to the other foremost Apostles; rather, he shows himself also to
have exhibited himself greater in action than Peter, the highest summit of the
Apostles.*

18. Bell.: *Peter had twenty-eight singular prerogatives: 1. A change of name.
2. That he is always named first among the Apostles. 3. He alone walked upon
the waters. 4. He had a peculiar revelation*, Matthew 16. *5. Perpetual
stability is promised to him and his See*, at the same place. *6. He paid the
tribute with Christ*, Matthew 17. *7. In the miraculous catch of fish he was
the prince. 8. He is commanded to confirm his brothers. 9. He was the first of
the Apostles to see Christ raised from the dead. 10. Christ first washed his
feet. 11. Christ foretold his death to him alone. 12. He presides in the
election of Matthias. 13. He is the first to proclaim the Gospel after the
Holy Spirit was given, unto the conversion of three thousand men. 14. He
performed the first miracle. 15. He condemned the hypocrisy in Ananias and
Sapphira. 16. He passed through all*, Acts 9:32. *17. He first began to preach
to the Gentiles. 18. He was miraculously freed from prison at the Church's
earnest prayers. 19. He speaks first in the Council*, Acts 15. *20. Paul
visited him. 21. He alone was baptized by Christ's own hands. 22. He alone was
ordained bishop by Christ, and the others by him. 23. He was the first to
expose, condemn, and extinguish the heresiarch Simon Magus. 24. He placed his
See at Rome. 25. Christ appeared to him near the end of his life. 26. The
churches which he founded were always regarded as patriarchal. 27. The feast
of his*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 169 | printed p. 159 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*chair is celebrated. 28. In formal letters the name of Peter is added after
the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.* Prot. 1. Bellarmine acted badly
and sophistically by treating prerogatives at such length while explaining to
us by no definition what should be understood by a prerogative. 2. A
prerogative is accustomed to be taken for one person's superiority or
excellence above another. But not every superiority consists in power, nor
does it prove a singular power, except in the manner in which anything is
usually proved from anything. 3. Neither these individual prerogatives
separately nor all of them together either make Peter a monarch or show that
he was one. 4. Paul was inferior to Peter in nothing, and therefore not even
in prerogatives, 2 Corinthians 11 and 12. 5. Many of these prerogatives are
plainly false: 2, 4, 5, 14, 19, 22, and 26. Many are uncertain: 10, 17, 21,
and 24. Some are the emptiest trifles: 6 and 7. Some are common to others with
Peter: 5, 6, 9, 15, and 16. Some are even superstitious: 27 and 28. In the
twenty-fourth, concerning Peter's See placed at Rome, Bellarmine contradicts
himself by a double reasoning. For in chapter 16 he admits that Peter devoted
his chief labor to the conversion of the Jews and on this ground also contends
that Peter's office was more honorable than Paul's, because it chiefly
regarded the Jews. But here he wishes Peter's labor and chair to have been
chiefly fixed at Rome, and notes as a great prerogative and an evident sign of
primacy that he was sent to the capital of the world and queen of cities.

19. Although the Fathers Bellarmine cites in chapter 25—at least those among
them who were more ancient—attribute to Peter a singular primacy of dignity
and order among the Apostles and a primacy of Apostolic power over the Church
in common with the others, they nevertheless acknowledge that no monarchy
over either the Apostles or the Church was given to him.

20. Because we elevate neither James nor Paul in power above Peter, and regard
Peter's sins nowhere except as probable indications that no unrivaled power
had been granted to him, we therefore pass over those final three chapters
which treat these matters.
