# CHAPTER VI (continued)

*Objection 6.* Although believers are bound to perform the thing commanded,
they are nevertheless not bound to do so by virtue of the command. The Spirit
makes them conformable to the law, but they are subject not to the authority
of the law but to that of the Spirit, who is not subject to laws.

*Answer.* If they are not bound to perform the thing commanded by virtue of
the command, there is no command, and all fear of God is banished. For the
fear of God is especially conspicuous in this: that we do such a thing
because he, the supreme Lawgiver, commands it to be done.

2. It will follow from this that believers do not sin when they commit
murder, secretly carry off or openly seize another's goods, violate other
men's wives, and perpetrate similar crimes, because they are in no way bound
by the law which enjoins the contrary; it has or exercises no force over them.
However evil these things are in themselves, they are nevertheless not sins
for believers, because they are not bound to the contrary by the force of the
law. And where there is no binding law, there can be no transgression of law.
*Reply.* But these acts are sins

[Printed p. 61 | IA PDF 69 | IA leaf 69 | IA decausadei02brow]

because they are transgressions of the law of the Spirit, to whom believers
are subject. *Answer.* Since the Spirit is the principle or efficient cause of
our actions, whatever is contrary to, or not according to, his guidance is
not a transgression of a rule or law. For a law is something external and
distinct from an efficient cause, and sin is called ἀνομία, a transgression
of law, which directly and formally sets forth the lawgiver's mind and will
concerning the duty owed. Sin is not, however, resistance or opposition to
the inward guidance and efficient working of the mover, although this is a
very great aggravation of sin.

3. If believers are subject to the Spirit as their sole rule and law,
according to which they must arrange and order all their actions, then they
are bound to acknowledge no higher rule or law, nor ought they to test those
motions of the Spirit by that rule. The apostle would therefore have said in
vain in 1 John 4:1, *“Do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see
whether they are from God.”* It will then also follow that when the Spirit
suspends his act, believers cannot sin whatever they do, for then a rule or
law will be lacking, and where there is no law there is no transgression. It
also follows that this law is not the same in everyone, for the Spirit does
not work equally in everyone or exert his power equally, whether by
illuminating, arousing, moving, or impelling; rather, he uses his freedom and
works as he wills.

4. This objection supposes that the Spirit moves us as people move stones or
inanimate things, which cannot be governed or moved by laws, and consequently
that believers cannot properly sin because they do not act rationally.
Instead, it supposes (which is blasphemous) that the Spirit errs, because he
does not guide and impel a person otherwise, or because he permits that
person to stray. And from this it follows that even the regenerate have no
inward principle or renewed faculty or power for doing any good thing.

5. Acting by virtue of a precept and acting under the Spirit's motion are
subordinate things, and subordinate things do not conflict. For the Spirit
acts efficiently, while the precept moves objectively or as an end, and these
two can readily coexist. A pupil forming letters or characters according to
a model set before him can keep his eyes fixed upon the model and direct
himself to imitate it exactly, even though the teacher meanwhile guides his
hand and effectively contributes to the formation of those letters.

6. We see believers, by virtue of a command, often apply themselves keenly
and diligently to certain works, and for that reason their obedience is
praised in a special way. See Hebrews 11:8. Indeed, this pure obedience tends
especially to display God's glory and commends especially our sincerity,
self-denial, most humble submission of spirit, and ardent love. Indeed,
whatever we do, unless it is done in this way by virtue of a command, will
not be pure and sincere obedience but mere hypocrisy. In that deed or that
endurance we will seek not God's glory but our own.
