Verse 13. Do not withhold correction from a child: when you have struck him with the rod, he will not die.

Verse 14. You yourself will strike with the rod, and you will have rescued his soul from the grave.

In נער, “a child,” there is a synecdoche of the species, for it stands for offspring of either sex. Likewise, שבט, “the rod,” stands for every instrument of moderate chastisement.

The purpose is to exhort superiors, in view of the disadvantages of excessive indulgence, to moderate and prudent chastisement of their inferiors.

Here, as in the earlier proverb, he warns that correction must be applied to absolutely all children, whatever their disposition, character, or upbringing, because there are none, however well disposed or educated, who do not sometimes bear such fruits of the old Adam as must be restrained by chastisement, lest they spread more widely. For since human beings are like colts, Job 11:12, none of which are so tame and gentle that they do not need a trainer to curb their fierceness by putting in a bit and applying a rod or spurs, it follows also that children, although endowed with the mildest disposition, nevertheless sometimes need chastisement.

Although the first part of the verse gives a command concerning correction taken generally, the correction administered by words is nevertheless to be understood in a primary and principal

[1632 witness | PDF p. 518 | printed columns 1020-1021]

sense. For if children were beaten with the rod for every offense, frequent beating would produce a certain callousness that would remove their feeling of the blows and engender contempt for the rod. Accordingly, unless something more serious has been committed, one must begin with rebuke; nor should those who can be amended by words be loaded with blows.

But neither, however obedient children may have been, should the matter always be carried out by mere words; the rod must sometimes be added. For if someone is repeatedly admonished about one and the same thing without blows, he not only casts aside the desire to amend but also hardens and stiffens his neck, Proverbs 29:1. For chastisements resemble medicines, not food. Therefore, just as the same medicines, when used too frequently, pass into the nature of nourishment and thus themselves not only fail to heal but also obstruct the action of other remedies, so rebukes too often repeated become so familiar through long habit, and so easy to administer, that those who ought to have been brought back to good conduct are thereby confirmed still more in their wickedness.

He adduces a reason from an adjunct: that through chastisement the child's life will be preserved, not only the present life but also the eternal. Hence it is clear that parents and all those charged with bringing up children, unless they chastise them, are not only killers of their bodies but also executioners of their souls. Here one is moved to wonder at some parents' cruel love for their children: they hold them so dear that they readily endure their dying an untimely death and being hurled headlong into the dreadful abyss of hell. Such was the love of Eli the priest for his sons, and David's love for Absalom and Adonijah. Not without reason we may suspect that all these were cast into the eternal torments of Gehenna by the most wretched death which we know they suffered.

In verse 14 there is an intensification. For in verse 13 he had warned parents to allow their children to be chastised, contrary to what most do, who commonly agree with teachers or masters that they should not beat their children. Here he commands the parent himself to strike with the rod. For the greater his own authority over his children, and his love for them, the more effective will be the chastisement proceeding from him than that inflicted by another, whose authority with them, at least, is less, and whose love is not equally evident. But most parents, because they themselves are so foolishly indulgent that they can neither inflict blows on their children themselves nor look calmly upon blows inflicted by others, entrust the task of correcting and chastising to others.

Here children must be exhorted to patience in enduring chastisement, especially if they are chastised by their heavenly Father, Hebrews 12:9.

Ecclesiastical discipline is compared to a father's rod, 1 Corinthians 5; and on that account it ought to be most highly commended to us, since to it belongs, in a certain supreme and primary manner, the task of freeing us from the first and second death.

### Translator's notes

The source's *sicures* is retained and understood as “tame,” the sense of *cicures*. Its *factu faciles*, literally “easy to do,” describes rebukes rendered habitual and easy to administer; the following clause gives their counterproductive effect. The printed reference *1. Cor. 5.* is retained. “First and second death” distinguishes bodily death from eternal condemnation. The page break divides *quadam* in the witness; the complete Latin word and corresponding English “sense” are assigned to PDF 518.
