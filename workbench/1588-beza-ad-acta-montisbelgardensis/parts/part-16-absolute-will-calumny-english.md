## Part XVI: Refutation of the Charge Concerning God’s “Absolute Will”

[Part II | PDF page 163 | logical source scan 162 | printed page 161 | begins after preceding unit on shared page]

Theodore Beza’s response to Dr. Andreae’s annotations

The false charge concerning God’s absolute will is refuted.

To 1. Andreae calls that will *absolute* for which foreseen rebellion against offered gospel grace supplies no cause. In this matter he errs most grossly in three ways. First, he mistakenly wills the cause of the decree concerning the condemnation of some—to which the just causes for executing that decree are subordinated—to be one and the same as the cause of condemnation itself. Yet the cause through and because of which God executes his decree must necessarily be subordinated to the decree itself—

[Part II | PDF page 164 | logical source scan 163 | printed page 162]

—for what moves one who decrees to undertake this or that is one thing; that through which he executes his decision is another. Second, we said a little earlier how falsely and unskillfully rejection of gospel grace is established as the cause of the destruction of everyone condemned. The fact itself cries so loudly that even the deafest must hear it: if the condemned could be numbered, tens of thousands who never heard anything about Christ would be found for every one who spurned offered gospel grace.

No one should consider this surprising. Dr. Andreae’s example on page 540 marvels at it and testifies to it as novel and never before heard: namely, that some whom God never loved are condemned or destined for condemnation—meaning by that the love by which God wills everyone to be saved and to come to knowledge of the truth. Yet Moses testifies to us in countless places that peculiar grace was shown to Abraham. Was that because God foresaw that he would believe? Rather, if we believe Joshua 24:2, he was an idolater, and God could foresee nothing upright in him except what he himself would create in him by grace.

Behold David crying, “He has not dealt so with every nation.” Was this because he foresaw that it would believe? Rather, the prophets foretold its unbelief, its obstinacy for the greatest part, and indeed its rejection, which we have now long beheld as present. Nevertheless, election remains because of the fathers—an election which is not of him who wills or runs, but of God, who has mercy upon whom he wills. Behold God himself testifying through another prophet, “Esau I hated.” Was that because of Esau’s foreseen perversity? Rather, says the Apostle—speaking not simply about time, certainly, but about the order of causes—“before he had done anything evil,” indeed while he still lay in his mother’s womb. Finally, behold the same Paul, treating countless peoples rather than merely individual persons and so many repeated ages: “In past ages,” he says, “God permitted all nations to walk in their own ways,” and so forth. How, nevertheless, the cause of destruction inheres in those who perish alone, we will discuss in its place.

Finally, what kind of piety and reverence for the divine majesty is it to think that God did not determine before he created man the purpose and end for which he would create him, but that, foreseeing what man himself would do, he then first derived the cause of his counsel from it and decreed within himself what would follow? Indeed, if we agree with their dogma, must he afterward condemn—either unwilling, with his judgment changed, or certainly willing and unwilling at once—those whom he formerly willed to be saved?

To conclude this point briefly: we teach that God’s decree concerning those to be saved by grace in Christ does not depend upon foreseeing faith and good works, nor that his decree concerning sinners to be condemned by his just judgment depends upon corruption and its rotten fruits, but that both depend upon his will alone. Nevertheless, we never detach this will, or this decree of his, from justice and truly right reason. We believe it always to be most orderly—

—although unsearchable even to angels—and therefore we marvel at and adore it. We acknowledge no other absolute will in God. Therefore, when the matter has come to this point, let us hear Augustine leading us according to Paul’s footsteps and going before us with these words in *Sermons on the Words of the Apostle* 11: “Do you wish to dispute with me? Rather, marvel with me and cry, ‘O depth!’ Let us both agree in trembling, lest we perish in error.” And in Letter 105: “Who,” he says, “created the reprobate”—that is, those destined for just condemnation—“except God? And why, except because he willed?” Therefore it was not, as they will have it, because of foreseeing any vice, since such foreseeing precedes not the decree but its execution. “Why did he will it? O man, who are you to answer back to God?”
