# Response to the Polish Brothers: How Christ Is Mediator, Refuting Stancaro's Error

John Calvin and the pastors and teachers of the Genevan church — Geneva,
9 June 1560

This is a new English translation made directly from the normalized Latin in
`latin.txt` and checked sentence by sentence against the 1870 *Calvini Opera*
facsimile. It includes the complete address, response, blessing, date,
corporate subscription, and list of signatories at CO IX 337–342. CO's title
leaf and editorial apparatus are documented in the README but are not
presented as the signatories' words. Biblical quotations are translated from
the Latin rather than replaced with a modern English Bible version.

**[CO IX, p. 337]**

To the faithful ministers of Christ who profess the pure doctrine of the
gospel, and to the other sincere worshipers of God, our brothers worthy of
honor.

It grieves us deeply, most upright brothers whom we cherish from the heart,
that your churches are being troubled by importunate men. Those churches are
still tender in their first beginnings, and the crop of pure doctrine among
them is still scarcely beyond the blade. All the more, therefore, must we
mourn the death of that distinguished man, Lord John a Lasco. You experienced
him as a vigorous champion of Christ; throughout the whole course of his
life, endowed as he was with uncommon gifts, he devoted to the church, to the
full measure of his strength, service no less fruitful than useful.

But the Lord, who deprived you of so useful an aid, has other means in his
hand by which he may protect sound doctrine, the sincere worship of
godliness, the kingdom of his Son, and the glory of his name. He will never
leave you without his help so long as you serve under Christ's sacred banner,
nor will he ever exercise you with any conflicts without also supplying the
strength to obtain the victory.

He displayed no obscure proof of this grace when, for the refutation of
Stancaro's error, he directed you by the Spirit of wisdom, armed you with his
power, and joined you together in godly agreement. For the invention by which
that man, swollen with pride and excessively eager for novelty, attempted to
undermine the first principles of the orthodox faith is not merely absurd but
deadly. It is certainly a matter for grief that ambition, the mother of
heresies, drove a man who could otherwise have been useful into doing harm.
The arguments he puts forward are so frivolous that it is sufficiently clear
he sought nothing but a reputation for sharp intelligence by disagreeing with
others.

**[CO IX, p. 338]**

They deny that Christ can be called mediator according to his divine nature,
because then he would be inferior to the Father. Our first reply is that the
name *mediator* fits Christ not only from the time he put on flesh, or from the
time he undertook the office of reconciling the human race with God, but that
he had already truly been mediator from the beginning of creation. He was
always the head of the church, held primacy even over the angels, and was the
firstborn of every creature (Ephesians 1:2; Colossians 1:15 and the following
verse, and 2:10).[^1]

From this we conclude not only that he began to discharge the office of
mediator after Adam's fall, but that, insofar as he is the eternal Word of
God, by his grace both angels and human beings were joined to God so that they
might remain upright. It is therefore ignorant to infer from the title
*mediator* that he is less than the Father. These things agree perfectly with
one another: that the only-begotten Son of God was the same God and of one
essence with the Father, and yet was like a middle between God and creatures,
so that from him might flow the life which otherwise lay hidden in God.

We add next that, although after humanity's alienation from God he was
foreordained and at last presented as mediator in another manner—namely, as
the one who would restore the lost human race to life by expiating sins—yet
in this person of the mediator he is the head of angels no less than of human
beings. This may be gathered from the first chapter of Colossians and could
by no means apply to the human nature alone.

It is therefore evident that whoever denies that he is mediator with respect
to his deity exempts the angels from his dominion and thus detracts from his
supreme majesty, before which every knee, both of things in heaven and things
on earth, must bend (Philippians 2:10). For although that sublime name was
given to him in the flesh in which he had emptied himself, Paul nevertheless
includes the whole person beyond any doubt. If the glory and power proper to
God alone resided in him only with respect to his human nature, Isaiah's
declaration would collapse: “As I live, says Jehovah, I will not give my glory
to another”

**[CO IX, p. 339]**

(Isaiah 42:8). Nor could the power of supreme judgment have been transferred
to the human nature without God despoiling himself of his own right. But it is
perfectly consistent for God manifested in the flesh, in the one person of
Christ, to be and to be accounted the judge of the world according to both
natures.

No surer or more solid proof of this point should be sought from anywhere
other than the office of mediator. On this condition he descended from the
Father and undertook this office: that he might have life in himself just as
the Father does (John 5:26), so that we would not have to seek it far away.
It is evident to everyone that this cannot rightly be ascribed to the human
nature. How, then, could one who subsisted below the angelic order fulfill the
functions of mediator? This must necessarily be understood of the human
nature considered in itself.

Furthermore, just as the apostle reasons that Christ had to become a partaker
of our flesh and blood in order to be our brother (Hebrews 2:17), so in turn
we must affirm that he was endowed with the same deity as the Father in order
to be our leader and guide to the Father. This properly belongs to the office
of mediator. Nor could he fulfill its other functions except by his divine
power. For it was not within a human being's power to conquer death and the
devil; nor could a mere man acquire righteousness, bring life, and bestow all
the benefits we receive from him.

The same point pertains to the priesthood, which he could not discharge
without penetrating into the heavenly sanctuary. Therefore, to prove that he
is a legitimate priest, the apostle adduces this testimony: “You are my Son;
today I have begotten you” (Hebrews 1:5 and 5:6; Psalm 2:7). By this he plainly
shows that no one is equal and fit for this office without deity.

The consequence is certain: if Christ is priest because he is the
only-begotten Son of God, and in turn he is not the Son of God without regard
to his deity, then that same deity is necessarily required for the honor of
the priesthood. Accordingly, from the time Adam was alienated from God by his
defection and reconciliation became necessary, Christ was foreordained as
priest so that through him access to God might lie open to the ancient
fathers. And he is therefore called a priest forever: he was not simply
chosen from among human beings, but by the Father's decree assumed a human
nature to expiate sins. Finally, unless he had been declared the Son of God in
power (Romans 1:4), neither should he be regarded as mediator.

If these two things cohere in an indivisible bond—that the Son of God is
mediator, and that it is beyond controversy that Christ is the Son of God
with respect to both natures—then the other point is also established: he is
mediator by reason of his deity no less than by reason of his human nature.

Furthermore, no sane person will deny that he sits at the Father's right hand
insofar as he is God

**[CO IX, p. 340]**

manifested in the flesh, and that this is predicated of the whole person. The
prophet joins these things mutually to one another (Psalm 110:1): that the one
who sits at the Father's right hand was appointed Lord and king, and was
placed over the church as priest.[^2] Nor is it right in this case to omit
Peter's statement that the holy prophets of old spoke by the Spirit of Christ
(1 Peter 1:10), because the passage concerns not only the eternal Word but the
office of mediator. If, before he was clothed with flesh, he governed the
prophets by his Spirit, it follows that deity is comprehended in this same
person.

It is also rightly said that all the actions by which he discharged his work
of reconciling God to us pertained to the whole person, so that they ought not
to be restricted separately to only one nature. Nevertheless, to prevent
this statement from being exposed to quibbles, a distinction will be useful:
certain actions in themselves concern one nature, yet because of the effect
that follows they are common to both. For although dying is proper to the
human nature, if we consider what the apostle means when he says that our
consciences are cleansed by Christ's blood because he offered himself through
the Spirit (Hebrews 9:14), we will not separate the natures in the death
itself. Expiation could not have been accomplished by a mere man unless divine
power had been joined to him.

If the apostle rightly and suitably concludes that Christ is the mediator of
the new testament because he offered himself through the Spirit, it follows
that his death was expiatory because he was the only-begotten Son of God and
the redeemer given to the human race. Thus nothing will prevent each nature
from retaining its own property intact, nor will their communication conflict
with their distinction.[^3]

The absurd consequence with which Stancaro attempts to burden this doctrine
is no consequence at all: namely, that Christ would be less than the Father
if he interceded with him according to his deity. Whenever Christ sustains the
person of mediator, he himself does not hesitate to subject himself to the
Father. This is not because his deity lost its rank while clothed with flesh,
but because he could not interpose himself between us and the Father in any
other way without the Father's glory, in the order of dispensation, rising
above the very person of the mediator.

Nor is Christ's pronouncement that he is less than the Father (John 14:28)
understood of his humanity in such a way that it does not apply to the whole
person. He does not compare his humanity with the Father's deity, but his
present state with the future state, in which the glory of his deity was to
be perfectly manifested.[^4] For under the name of the Father the whole deity
is designated here, the fullness of which dwells in Christ (Colossians 1:19
and 2:9). Thus we must maintain not only the distinction between the persons
of the Father and the Son, but also that wondrous counsel of God by which the
only-begotten Son of God

**[CO IX, p. 341]**

descended to us. In this way the empty subtlety with which Stancaro tried to
cast a fog over the matter is dispelled. He boasts that it is inconsistent
for the Son of God to be mediator according to his deity, because he would be
interceding with himself. Yet although the deity in the Holy Spirit is the
same as that in the Father, Christ is not said to intercede with the Spirit,
but with the Father alone. Here we must regard the economy of the divine
counsel and must not loosen the bridle upon our speculations.

For this reason Christ is said to be about to send the Spirit from his Father
(John 16:7), so that he may raise us by degrees to the Father. Finally, for
this reason the Son will hand the kingdom over to God and the Father on the
last day (1 Corinthians 15:28): because then, immediately, so to speak, the
radiance of the glory of God and the Father will be visible to us—the
radiance which now appears in Christ, his living image.

Would that Stancaro might be moved by these things. This can be hoped for only
when his disposition, raised excessively high by its own vanity, has bent
itself to gentleness and modesty. We trust, however, most upright brothers,
that you have been given satisfaction.

## Translator's notes

[^1]: CO's main text prints “Ephesians 1:2,” although the assertion that Christ
    is head of the church corresponds more directly to Ephesians 1:22. No CO
    apparatus note corrects the transmitted locator, so it is preserved rather
    than silently altered.

[^2]: *Creatum* is rendered “appointed” here. In this predicative context the
    word concerns Christ's appointment as Lord and king in the mediatorial
    office; rendering it “created” without qualification would suggest a claim
    the surrounding argument expressly rejects.

[^3]: “Communication” translates *communicatio*. The immediate subject is the
    attribution of actions and effects to the whole person while each nature
    retains its proper characteristics; the sentence must not be detached from
    that controlling distinction.

[^4]: CO prints the adverb *perfecte*, “perfectly.” Its apparatus reports the
    codex's *erat perfectae*, which would instead yield “the glory of his
    perfect deity.” The translation follows the CO main text.

[^5]: The Roman inclusive date *5 Idus Iunii* is 9 June 1560.

[^6]: The personal names remain in the Latin forms printed by CO so that every
    signature can be checked without uncertain modernization. The final two
    professional titles are elliptical in the Latin: after Cevalerius is
    expressly called *professor*, Beraldus is identified “of the Greek
    language” and Tagautius “of the arts.”
