# Calvin to Heinrich Bullinger, Letter 1211

Geneva, 26 June 1549

This is a fresh translation of the complete Latin letter at CO XIII 305–307.
Calvin comments on the draft of the agreement later known as the *Consensus
Tigurinus* and proposes several additions before publication.

## Translation

### [CO XIII, p. 305]

To the man of outstanding godliness and learning, Master Heinrich Bullinger,
pastor of the church of Zurich, fellow minister, and most esteemed brother.

Greetings. When I returned home, I found no letters from you or from Laelius,
though Laelius's followed not much later. One of the royal envoys delivered
yours to me on 17 June—and opened, at that. He pleaded that he had taken this
liberty by right of our old friendship. Whatever his deed amounted to, I was
not surprised by it. Meanwhile, we must take care that our letters do not fall
into such hands.

I read to the brothers the document that we brought back from you, to the
great rejoicing of everyone. While passing through Lausanne, I had not only
read it aloud to Viret and his colleagues but also allowed them to copy it.
All were affected with no slight joy and vied with one another in giving
thanks to God. Some, however, observed that if it should ever appear in
public, a few things might be missed which, if added, would lend it some
clarity and grace at the same time.

First, where we say that Christ in his flesh is to be regarded as priest,
king, and so forth, and then immediately add, “This is a spiritual
communication,” the transition is certainly too abrupt. The defect could
easily be corrected by inserting this sentence:

> Furthermore, for Christ to exhibit himself to us as such and to bring about
> effects of this kind in us, we must be made one with him and grow together
> into his body. For he diffuses his life into us only by being our head, from
> whom the whole body, compacted and joined through every supporting joint,
> according to the working measured to each member, makes the body's growth.

In these words I know

### [CO XIII, p. 306]

there is nothing that will not please you. Indeed, something of this kind is
altogether necessary to the composition of the argument.

In the sixth chapter, where it reads, “and at the same time for these benefits
once exhibited on the cross, but received by us through faith,” and so forth,
I afterward noticed that the otherwise somewhat rough sentence could be
softened in this way: “and which we receive daily through faith.” The sense
will thus be fuller as well.

After the eighteenth chapter, where we expound figuratively the words of the
Supper, “This is my body; this is my blood,” I thought it would not be
untimely to meet the doubt of certain people. There will undoubtedly be some
good men who will be troubled that no mention is made of the reality where
only the sign is named, especially since throughout the entire document there
is no word about eating the flesh. Just as we cannot be too careful that our
words suggest nothing akin to gross notions, so in turn we must take care
that moderate and right-thinking people find no less than is sufficient. It
would not be difficult to satisfy them if an explanation such as this were
added:

> That Christ feeds our souls through faith, by the power of his Spirit, with
> the eating of his flesh and the drinking of his blood which are figured
> here is not to be understood as though some blending or transfusion of
> substance occurred, but in the sense that we draw life from flesh once
> offered in sacrifice and from blood poured out in expiation.

Here too, I think, nothing is contained that would either displease you, be
liable to appear suspect to others, or finally be exposed to slander. But if
it is omitted, the complaints of many will be heard that the point most
pertinent to the matter was passed over. Nor will there be any lack of people
shouting that this was done craftily. Those who are less unfair and malicious
interpreters will suspect that we deliberately concealed it because there
was disagreement among us. Besides this, a scruple about the manner and
means of eating will remain lodged in many minds. No one will excuse haste,
because everyone will require maturity in so important a matter, and hardly
anyone will be persuaded that we had to hurry so greatly.

I offer these observations to you in the familiarity of friendship, with the
purpose that, while the matter is still open, you may consult with our
brothers as to what is more expedient. Meanwhile, if you decide that nothing
should be altered or added, I promise that there will be no delay on our part
in signing. Although perhaps there is no great need to labor over this at
present, since our Bernese brothers recoil from publication—unless at last,
swayed by the example of others, they come over to a different opinion. The
reasons that hold them back seem to me rather weak. For we do not profess
that there has hitherto been disagreement between us, nor do we give the
wicked an occasion for slander, as though from fear of the present troubles
we were removing odium from ourselves. None of these things will be able to
be said. Rather, by our agreement the spirits of good people will be raised
up to break the wicked. Our constancy

### [CO XIII, p. 307]

and freedom in these turbulent times will have great force. Whoever was
previously persuaded otherwise will understand that our views are godly and
right. Very many who today remain in suspense will have something in which
to rest; those in distant regions who are estranged from you will soon, I
hope, extend their hands. Finally, whatever happens, posterity will possess a
testimony to our faith such as could not be gathered from contentious
disputations.

But I leave this to God's governance, and I commend you, together with your
family and indeed your whole church, to his grace. Farewell, most
distinguished man and brother, greatly esteemed in the Lord. Please greet
Master Theodore Bibliander, Pellican, Gualther, and the other colleagues
carefully in my name. My brothers also send many greetings to you and to
them. Geneva, the sixth day before the Kalends of July, 1549—that is, 26
June.

Your John Calvin.

## Translator's notes

1. The document Calvin brought from Zurich was the developing Eucharistic
   agreement between Zurich and Geneva, published later in 1549 as the
   *Consensus Tigurinus*. The letter is unusually valuable evidence for
   Calvin's proposed revisions before publication.
2. The edition's first apparatus note cross-references letter 1191 for
   Laelius Socinus's earlier communication. Its second tentatively identifies
   the royal envoys as the sieurs de Mesnaige and de Liancourt. Neither note
   is part of Calvin's text.
3. The proposed paragraph about Christ as head adapts Ephesians 4:15–16.
   *Subministratio* is the support or supply furnished through the body's
   joints; the translation keeps the compact bodily metaphor rather than
   importing wording from a modern Bible.
4. In the second proposed insertion, *substantiae* can grammatically modify
   both *commixtio* and *transfusio*. “Blending or transfusion of substance”
   preserves the doctrinal point: communion is real without a physical
   mixture or transfer of Christ's substance.
5. *Data opera nos dissimulasse* means that readers might suspect purposeful
   concealment, not merely an accidental omission. Calvin regards explicit
   treatment of sacramental eating as necessary to prevent that inference.
6. The CO apparatus identifies “Master Theodore” as Theodore Bibliander. It
   also records that one secondary witness omitted his name; the main text
   retained here includes it.
7. The printed closing uses the Roman date *sexto Kalendas Iulias*, six days
   before 1 July by inclusive counting: 26 June. The date is internal to this
   letter, unlike the editorially inferred date of Calvin's adjacent letter
   1212 to Socinus.
