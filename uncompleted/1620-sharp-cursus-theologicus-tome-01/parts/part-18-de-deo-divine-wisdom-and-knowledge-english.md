On the Wisdom or Knowledge of God

Wisdom, or the knowledge of God, is a communicable attribute by which God is
signified to be wise in his essence, knowing and understanding all things
infinitely and most simply, and to be the cause of all wisdom in creatures:
Job 9:4 and 12:16, Psalm 94:9, and Daniel 2:22. He knows most simply because
God’s wisdom is the very essence of God by which he knows, hears, and sees
universals and particulars most distinctly in one most simple act, so that he
knows, hears, and sees all things in his entirety, Psalm 139. He knows
infinitely because he knows neither successively nor by reasoning one thing
from another, but, concluding all at once, beholds all things in one eternal
and immutable act, Hebrews 4:13 and Psalm 139:11. Consequently, neither
foreknowledge nor recollection is properly in God, but only with respect to
us. Finally, he is omniscient, knowing all things without increase or
decrease, ignorant of nothing, learning nothing, and forgetting nothing.

A twofold knowledge is attributed to God:

1. Absolute, by which from eternity he knows all things absolutely, Hebrews
4:13 and Psalm 94:10.

2. Special, by which he not only knows his elect as he knows the remaining
things, but also acknowledges them as his own, discerns them, and loves them;
and this is called the knowledge of approval, Matthew 7:23, 2 Timothy 2:9,[^1]
and Romans 11:2. This will be treated in the question on predestination.

There are, however, two modes of knowing. The first is through sense, but this
does not apply to God. The second is through intellect and is threefold:

1. Opinion, which is neither certain nor evident knowledge and therefore does
not apply to God.

2. Faith, which arises from another’s report and is certain but not evident
knowledge; therefore faith is said to concern things not se—

[1620 Tome I | col. 195 | BSB frame 00114]

—en, Hebrews 11:1. This does not apply to God.

3. Knowledge, which is twofold:

1. Knowledge properly so called, which is cognition of conclusions arising
from a preexistent cognition of principles, causes, and propositions serving
as premises. Thus it is acquired by reasoning, dividing, combining, and
defining. But such knowledge is not in God, since he is pure act, in whom
there is no prior and posterior.

2. *Nous*, which is cognition of first principles and comes closer to the
knowledge of God because it does not arise from some preexistent knowledge.
But God’s knowledge is simpler even than this, because this act of our
cognition does not understand all things at once or perfectly, whereas God
does so most perfectly and in one act. Moreover, all our knowledge differs
from the intellect and mind itself, but in God these are the same.

God’s knowledge is twofold according to the diversity of its subjects. 1. By
it God alone knows himself in himself most perfectly and infinitely, as in 1
Corinthians 2:11, John 1:18, and Matthew 11:27; yet in the divine intellect
the thing comprehended and that which comprehends are not different. 2. By it
he knows all things outside himself in one indivisible act, Genesis 1:31,
Hebrews 4:31,[^2] and James 1:17. He knows not only all things in general, but
also every individual thing and all their causes and circumstances, such as
place, time, and so forth; and he directs all things to definite ends,
Proverbs 16:4 and Romans 11:36. Although in Scripture foreknowledge with
respect to future things and recollection with respect to past things are
attributed to God anthropopathically, he nevertheless beholds each and every
thing in one act; and by this knowledge he knew all things from eternity,
Romans 8:29 and 11:2, 2 Timothy 2:19, 1 Peter 1:2, and Luke 10:20. He also
knows in time everyone’s words, deeds, and outcome of life, Proverbs 15:3 and
16:9, Jeremiah 10:23, and Job 14:5 and 11:11, as well as the inward movements
of tho—

[1620 Tome I | col. 196 | BSB frame 00114]

—ught and will, Jeremiah 11:20, Psalm 7:10, Genesis 7:5, and Revelation 2:23,
whether of the godly, Psalm 33:18, 34:18, and 90:8, Matthew 6:32 and 10:30, or
of the ungodly, Genesis 8:21, Job 5:13, and 1 Corinthians 3:19. He also knows
sins, Genesis 6:5 and Psalms 19:13 and 65:6. In one act he presently and
immutably knows every local and temporal distance and all necessary and
contingent things, Psalm 147:4, Proverbs 15:11, Matthew 6:26 and 28, and
Matthew 10:30.

God’s knowledge is also considered in two ways. 1. As theoretical, insofar as
we consider it distinct from the will. In this respect God’s knowledge is not
the cause of things, because the mere cognition of a future thing does not
give that thing the power to exist.

2. As practical, namely when God’s knowledge is considered as acting through
the will; thus it is the cause of things. Therefore, when Scripture speaks of
the creation and governance of things, it also makes mention of the will.
Finally, this wisdom of God cannot be communicated to any creature; indeed,
it does not belong even to Christ insofar as he is man. Therefore, it is
attributed to God alone, 1 Kings 8:29,[^3] 2 Chronicles 6:30, Isaiah 40:13 and
41:23, Romans 16:27 and 11:33, Psalm 139:6, Job 11:7, and 1 Timothy 6:16.

The uses of the doctrine:

1. God is distinguished from all creatures.

2. Therefore, all our wisdom is from God.

3. Since God knows all things, even the hidden things of the heart, let us
flee hypocrisy and live not only outwardly but also inwardly, as in the sight
of God.

4. Therefore, the ungodly can devise nothing against the faithful without
God’s knowledge, much less execute it; consequently, there is no reason for us
to fear them.

5. Therefore, afflictions are to be borne patiently and a happy outcome is to
be awaited, because they do not happen to us without God’s knowledge.
