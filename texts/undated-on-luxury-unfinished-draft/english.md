# On Luxury (Unfinished Draft)

*A fresh translation of the complete surviving Latin draft at CO X/1
203–206. The text is not a finished treatise: incomplete clauses, working
directions, abbreviations, and `etc.` belong to the witness and are preserved
rather than silently completed. The title “On Luxury” is CO's editorial
title.*

[CO X/1, p. 203]

When I undertake a war not against one or another person but against this age,
I know how much hostility I am preparing for myself among those in whose etc.
Yet one group will perhaps return me thanks and forgive the service once, with
me as its admonisher, it begins to come to its senses. Others will either look
down on me from on high, as those indolent Maecenases are accustomed to do, or
will ridicule me with the witticisms they habitually use.

[Authorial marginal note:] They laugh at what we are saying, as though we
wanted to bring their sumptuous living back to the rusticity of those times.
Why, then, do they use an example of thrift as a cloak for their own greed?

No one bears being censured with greater indignation or listens more
reluctantly than those who cannot defend themselves by reason. It will be
enough for me to have secured the return of some to better conduct, and to have
exposed the wickedness of those among whom neither truth nor honor has any
force. I am confident that I can certainly accomplish something among some who
sin through the fault of their time more than through their own. For they are
led by popular judgment, and they consider honorable that of which they see an
example, or which they have as an example. Truth may perhaps lay its hand upon
these people, I say, who are still teachable and only lightly corrupted, once
it has found an advocate.

These enticements of food which compel even the satiated to eat. Names must be
sought outside nature if we wish to designate these monstrosities of luxury.
We look up to and worship these playthings just as children fear specters in
the dark. And, so that we might be children twice over, we made everything
darkness for ourselves.

Part of the remedy is to know one's own evil. It often happens that vices do
not please even their own originators so much that they dare to praise them.
Now, however, there is a deadly plague when people flatter themselves and seek
a defense for their evils. Chrysostom, Homily 17 on Genesis, near the
beginning.

[CO X/1, p. 204]

They appeal to Joseph's many-colored garment and to Rebekah's earrings and
bracelets. How true is that saying of Cicero:

[Authorial marginal note:] *On Duties*, at fol. 349.

Many imitate the magnificence of Lucius Lucullus's villas, but who imitates
his integrity? In the many-colored garment they show those pageants of their
own to be children's playthings. We do not condemn care; we want a limit to be
applied that is nearer to self-restraint than to luxury. On the first
garments, Genesis 3.

The age has so degenerated from those ancient morals that a chaste man or a
chaste woman is now regarded as a monstrosity of nature. A broken and loose
voice belongs to an ungirt man. Do not suppose the mind to be self-controlled
where speech flows loosely. Yet these effeminate and dissolute men are men in
one thing which it is shameful to name, and of which they themselves rightly
ought to be ashamed.

How much praise for gentleness Maecenas could have borne away, had he not
spoiled that very quality by his own wantonness and indulgences! Amid the
utmost license in everything, he restrained himself from shedding blood, from
acting violently through injustice, and from oppressing anyone with his
power. He was said to have been soft, not mild. Customary things are scorned,
and everything usual is squalid.

[Authorial marginal note:] Seneca, Letter 20.

Why are they so depraved and shameless that they bear being rebuked so long as
they are looked at? They want everything to be so transparent that not even
the eyes of passersby are permitted to skip anything. Do you think it is one
body for which so many garments are prepared? You are mistaken: they belong
to months and days. Seneca, Letter 21.

Refined arrangement is not a masculine ornament. We are worse than boys,
because necklaces bought for little money delight them, while our folly costs
more. Never a vice without a defense. While we live by examples, we are no
longer shaped by reason but led astray by custom. Be ashamed, when you have
cultivated your appearance with meticulous care, to be surpassed by many
animals. That nothing in one's clothing be conspicuous for an ostentatious
display of thrift. See Seneca, C. 1, Letter 5.

[Authorial marginal note:] Seneca, at the end of the letter.

The ancients said this more truly than I would wish: that those who labor so
hard in

[CO X/1, p. 205]

cultivating the body are little concerned about the cultivation of the soul.
These—not enervated by softness, but castrated. It is ridiculous for them to
court popular admiration by great expenditures on garments, buildings, and
dinners, when their dressed-up squalor stands out in those very areas. For
they are miserly toward everyone so that something may remain from which they
can be generous toward themselves—or, rather, prodigal. Some dine frugally so
that they may build luxuriously; others take from the furnishings of their
houses what they devote to the adornment of the body.

We push one another into vices, and since we are by turns the cause of evil to
one another, we put forward custom and the examples of the multitude. A good
many excuse the age, others the place. This is how people live. What was I to
do? I would conspire against public morals. I would declare war on both my
country and my age. I hear the objection, but I answer: does a person amid a
city's plague therefore die any less? Campania broke Hannibal, undefeated in
war.

Funeral pageantry must be discussed, because we prolong our indulgences after
death, and because what we cannot give ourselves we give to a corpse that will
not feel it, as though some pleasure from it were going to return to us. How
rare among them is a person who, if you strip away his clothing or hair, does
not think his own limbs are being torn from him, so that he now seems no longer
the same person to himself? This is the art of slave dealers: to hide defects
with some enticement. The vices of individuals produce public error; then
public error [produces] the vices of individuals. Whoever blushes at a shabby
garment will glory in a costly one.

It now belongs to luxury how many horses each person drives. Yet Cato was
content with one. See Seneca, Letter 88. I shall nevertheless spare their
ears. Individuals, however, will not listen because they do not wish to. If
only I can secure this much once more: that they hear it publicly. They would
rather confound everything human and divine, would rather burst through every
law, than not serve the highest luxury.

So that examples need not be sought far from us. We saw recently in Genuens.
Oh, if poverty might someday be consecrated in churches and in public, so that
one may not afterward be ashamed to admit it in private! See Seneca, fol. 211.

Seneca, *On the Shortness of Life*, p. 374, against capes etc. I shall have
inscribed God upon the crime, and to pursue this passage against custom. If
things went well with human beings, the custom of living ought to have been
sought from the good. Now, when the public consensus of vices is thrown up
against me, what else is being boasted except that we have fallen into a time
in which it is not permitted to act holily and honorably among people already
corrupt?

Against men's long hair. It is the glory of Christ. Do you dare to paint this
over with your cosmetics? Is that not sacrilege? Perhaps this perversity of
luxury led the ancient anchorites to prescribe for themselves a fixed form of
humbler dress, by which they might withdraw from the common desire of all.
Then, as things well restored generally lapse into vice, it came about that
monks sought holiness—or at least a reputation for sanctity—from cowls, capes,
hoods, and trifles of this sort. Those, I say—the

[CO X/1, p. 206]

anchorites—if they lived today, what would they do, or with what antidote
would they fortify themselves, since in that still less-corrupt age they could
no longer bear luxury creeping in? For even with that antidote they did not
adequately protect their posterity. We see how elegantly many monks walk, so
that one would say that the white garment is deliberately an instrument of
luxury.

Let no one say, “I am dressed without wrongdoing,” etc. They all bear the blood
of the poor. For where do those little blessed fellows get their revenues
except from the poor, etc.? But especially those advocates, etc. The prophet
said, “Your hands are full of blood”; but I shall say that the whole body is
infected when it is clothed in such things. They will say, “The example is not
new.” I admit it. Human affairs have never gone so well that better things
pleased the majority. Nevertheless, the luxury of the ancients could be called
thrift in comparison with this wickedness.

Let that dreadful warning which speaks about the days of Noah return to mind.

You would say that some carry perfume shops around with them. A person who
always smells good does not smell good.

Not only all parts of the body, but even the clothes themselves are
adulteresses.

Our dinners serve not the belly but ambition.

One must argue against that saying of Valerius concerning Tubero's dinner:
“Our ancestors wished to be magnificent in public and sparing in private.” If
they had learned private thrift, they would not have aspired to public luxury.
As it is, they bring into public vices conceived at home.

One may see very many who, like Bias, carry all their possessions with them,
and others even more than they possess.

H....um [unresolved]: the use of ornaments used to be careful; now disdain is
also added to increase luxury.

This expression of the pampered—how long, finally?

*Vestiarium* in the neuter—which [masculine *quem*] in Seneca, fol. 354.

When you see our dances, you would say that those cheerful people are going
mad through laughter.

Seneca used “with uncovered head” proverbially for what is “without shame.”

To be admonished so that they may be fairer to us. For easy remedies are given
to vices which they cannot bear.

For some, the summit of their concerns is what they may eat, what they may
drink, and what they may wear.

A few things to be said about the honorable use of a mirror.

Whatever was once called a woman's adornment is now a man's baggage—indeed,
even a soldier's.

Our people's *misotechnia* must be censured.

Augustine, Letter 5: From your abundance actors live luxuriously, while the
poor do not have necessities.

## Translator's notes

1. CO describes this as an unfinished youthful draft in Calvin's autograph.
   The incomplete opening *in quorum etc.*, note-like infinitives and
   gerundives, the abbreviation *Genuens.*, abrupt topic changes, and broken
   syntax are therefore translated as transmitted. Smooth English continuity
   should not be inferred where the Latin does not provide it.
2. The four passages labeled “Authorial marginal note” are words that CO prints
   in its footnotes with `(C.)`, its attribution to Calvin. The bracketed label
   is supplied by this edition; it is not part of the autograph wording.
3. The opening *beneficium ignoscet* is grammatically and semantically
   difficult; “forgive the service” retains the printed verb rather than
   silently changing it to a conjectural “acknowledge.” CO separately calls
   *habent* on p. 203 a doubtful word.
4. At the pp. 204–205 turn, the printed *ex-colendo* is joined and the marker
   placed before “cultivating.” At the pp. 205–206 turn, *ana-choritae* is
   joined and the marker placed before “anchorites.”
5. The main text distinctly prints *Vide Sen. C. 1, ep. 5.* The unidentified
   `C. 1` has not been emended or expanded.
6. CO prints `H....um`, calls the word doubtful, and suggests *Horum (?)* in
   its apparatus. The translation retains the unresolved form rather than
   adopting the conjecture. The following *Vestiarium neutro quem* is itself
   malformed or unfinished working shorthand; the translation exposes the
   mismatch instead of repairing *quem*.
7. The Greek *μισοτεχνία* means hostility or aversion to art/craft. The
   transliteration “misotechnia” preserves its conspicuous code-switching in
   the Latin note.
8. This English was translated afresh from [`latin.txt`](latin.txt) and checked
   against the facsimile. The Beaty–Farley translation was not consulted or
   used for wording.
