#### Our arguments concerning the words of institution

36. Protestant argument. When *Hoc* (“This”) was uttered, it could not have
demonstrated anything other than bread, because at that time the Lord held
bread alone in his hands. This follows from the position of the papists, who
teach that the Lord's body was not in the sacrament when *Hoc* was spoken, but
only when the consecration had finally been completed.

Bellarmine. That *hoc* does not demonstrate precisely the bread but what is
contained under those species. Although this had indeed been bread before the
consecration, by the force of those words it begins to be Christ's body.

Protestant reply. 1. When Bellarmine denies that *Hoc* demonstrates the bread
precisely, he does not deny but tacitly concedes that the bread is demonstrated
in some manner, though not precisely. 2. All the papists concede that when
*Hoc* was uttered nothing was contained under those species besides bread,
since they concede that the conversion does not occur except at the final
instant in which those words are uttered: “For this is my body.” If *Hoc*
therefore demonstrates what is contained, it demonstrates the bread. 3. If by
the force of the words the thing contained begins to be Christ's body, then
those words, “This is my body,” are not true before they have effected that
conversion. But words which are not yet true can effect nothing. Therefore by
the force of those words it does not begin to be Christ's body. 4. “Begins to
be” is stated ambiguously, for the conversion, according to the position of
all the Scholastics, occurs in an instant. Although one might therefore imagine
that it follows after the final instant in which all the words are uttered,
one cannot even imagine that it is beginning when the first particle, *Hoc*,
is uttered. 5. It follows necessarily from Bellarmine's own concessions that
the bread is demonstrated by *Hoc*. For in this very chapter 11 he says: “If
someone points to something with his finger while uttering a demonstrative
pronoun, it seems very absurd to say that the thing present is not
demonstrated by that pronoun. But the Lord takes bread and, holding it out,
says, ‘Take, eat; this is my body.’ He seems to have demonstrated the bread.”
And again: “When *Hoc* is spoken, the substance of Christ's body is not then
present. If therefore the substance of Christ's body is understood, the
demonstration is rendered false. But we say ‘this,’ that is, the substance
under these species.”

37. Protestant argument. Matthew 26 says, “Drink from this, all of you.” The
words “from this” cannot refer to anything except the wine.

Bellarmine. “From this” does not signify “from this wine,” but “from that which
is contained in the cup under the species of wine.”

Protestant reply. The Lord himself, however, testifies in express words that
it was “the fruit of the vine,” Matthew 26:29.

38. Protestant argument. “This cup” demonstrates the wine; therefore “this”

[1632 witness | printed p. 81]

demonstrates the bread.

Bellarmine. “This cup” signifies what is contained in the cup.

Protestant reply. But it was demonstrated above from Bellarmine himself that
when that pronoun was uttered, no substance besides wine was contained in the
cup.

39. Protestant argument. What the Lord took, blessed, and broke, he gave to
his disciples and demonstrated by the pronoun *Hoc* as the thing to be given.
But he took, blessed, and broke bread. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Bellarmine. The Lord took and blessed bread, but he gave bread which was not
ordinary as it had been when he took it, but blessed and changed by the
blessing.

Protestant reply. 1. Ordinary bread and blessed bread have one and the same
substance and differ only in quality, and that quality is not absolute but
relative. This change therefore no more makes one bread be taken and another
given than cooking a fish makes one fish be bought and another eaten, or than
the covenant of marriage brings it about that one woman is courted and another
married. 2. Bellarmine speaks equivocally and sophistically of “bread changed
by the blessing.” For neither Bellarmine nor the other papists acknowledge
that a change or conversion of the bread occurs except, as Bellarmine has just
said, by the force of those words, “This is my body.” But the blessing under
discussion preceded those words: “He took, blessed, broke, gave, and said,
‘Take, eat; this is my body,’” Matthew 26:26.

Bellarmine 2. If someone says, “A certain man struck Peter, killed him, and
buried him,” he does not mean that Peter was buried alive, although he was
alive when struck.

Protestant reply. The rationale is not the same, because killing a man takes
away his life, but that blessing of the bread does not take away the substance
of the bread, not even according to the papists' position.

40. Protestant argument. In this sentence, “This is my body,” *est* (“is”)
cannot signify the same thing as “becomes,” “let it become,” “is
transubstantiated,” or “let it be transubstantiated.” Yet the papists take it
in this sense; otherwise the sentence would be false.

Bellarmine 1. Those words, like any other sentence, do not have their complete
signification except at the final instant, when the final word is uttered. At
that same final instant, however, the effect of the words is brought into
being.

Protestant reply. 1. None of this is relevant unless the verb *est* signifies
effecting, for it must necessarily signify either a body which has been made
or one which is to be made. It does not signify one already made, because the
body is said to be effected by its causal force; nor does it signify one to be
made unless it signifies effecting in the same manner as “becomes,” “let it
become,” and so forth. 2. These words cannot at the same instant both make the
body and signify that it has been made, because by the order of nature one of
these follows the other.

Bellarmine 2. By the order of nature these two precede one another and

[1632 witness | printed p. 82]

follow one another. For insofar as those words are the cause of that
conversion, they precede the conversion; but contrariwise, insofar as the
truth of the proposition depends upon the thing, the conversion precedes the
signification.

Protestant reply. 1. It is no more possible to bring about or conceive that
two absolute things, actions, or effects should by the order of nature precede
and follow one another than that something absolutely prior by nature should
be absolutely posterior by nature with respect to the same thing. 2.
Bellarmine was ashamed to utter in the former part of this distinction what he
is compelled to concede. He therefore did not say, as the thing itself
required, “The signification precedes the conversion,” corresponding to the
statement in the other part, “The conversion precedes the signification.”
Instead, he said, “The words precede.” 3. If the conversion precedes the
signification of the words by which it is effected, then it is effected by
words which signify nothing, in a plainly magical manner.

Bellarmine 3. The words are operative even if *est* is not put in place of
*fiat*. For when God, who cannot lie, says that something is, it must be so;
and therefore, if it was not so before, it must then come to be.

Protestant reply. 1. If God himself said this in the sense that the thing came
to be by the force of the utterance, *est* would necessarily be put in place
of *fiat* (“let it become”) or *esto* (“let it be”). 2. Nowhere do we read that
God used the verb *est* in this manner.

Bellarmine 4. Who imposed a law upon Christ that he must always speak in the
same manner when he did something?

Protestant reply. 1. He imposed upon himself the law that he would always
speak in a manner which could be understood. 2. The papists themselves
imposed a law upon speech concerning a sacrament: namely, that Christ say
nothing improperly, ambiguously, or obscurely which could be twisted to a
foreign sense, as Bellarmine said above in chapter 9.

41. Protestant argument. A sacramental proposition ought to be taken
sacramentally, that is, significatively or tropically.

Bellarmine. In a sacramental proposition which explains the sacrament or its
signification, the predicates are always taken tropically, not properly, as
in Revelation 17, “The seven heads are seven hills”; Matthew 13, “The seed is
the word”; and Genesis 41, “The seven cows are seven years.” But the words
“This is my body” are spoken not to explain the sacrament but to institute or
confect it.

Protestant reply. 1. In the first part of this reply Bellarmine hands our
whole case over to us. 2. What is granted in the first part is not taken away
by the second. For the same words are spoken both to institute and to explain
the sacrament. Thus Genesis 17 says, “This is my covenant: every male among
you shall be circumcised,” and Exodus 12 says, “The lamb is the Passover.” By
those words the sacraments are both instituted and explained. Nor indeed is
it possible for the nature of a sacrament to be declared by the words of
institution—which is nevertheless necessary for a complete institution—yet
for the sacrament itself not to be explained in some manner. 3. No

[1632 witness | printed p. 83]

rationale can be given or imagined why the words “This is my body” should not
be said to explain their own subject just as much as those which Bellarmine
acknowledges to explain and to be taken tropically: “The seed is the word” and
“The seven cows are seven years.”

42. Protestant argument. Unless it had occurred to the apostles that *Corpus*
(“body”) ought to be taken figuratively, they would have been disturbed by so
prodigious a thing.

Bellarmine 1. One reason why the apostles did not marvel or become disturbed
is that they had already been instructed and forewarned.

Protestant reply. They had been disturbed then by a carnal conception of this
kind, and they did not cease to be disturbed until they had been instructed
that those words were to be understood not carnally but spiritually.

Bellarmine 2. Another reason is that the same Christ who delivered the
mysteries illuminated their minds by faith so that they believed simply.

Protestant reply. Those things pertaining to everyone which the apostles,
illuminated by faith, simply believed, they afterward set forth in their
sermons and writings for others to believe. Among these they especially
pressed and explained the things which might seem especially incredible. But
they teach absolutely nothing concerning the real presence of Christ's body
under the species of bread, in the same space in which the bread had
previously been—not even where they especially ought to have taught it, if it
were true, when they discuss Christ's wonderful power, presence, and efficacy.

Bellarmine 3. Many thousands of simple Catholics are not disturbed when they
hear the same thing.

Protestant reply. The cause is either a brutish simplicity rather than a
Christian one, or it is that through implicit faith their consciences are so
ensnared by the one who sits in God's temple as though he were God that they
are prepared to believe that virtues are vices and vices are virtues, if he
says so. See Bellarmine, *On the Pontiff*, Book 4, chapter 5.

43. Protestant argument. The body which is given in the Eucharist under the
species of bread is eaten bodily and ground or chewed by the teeth. But this
is foreign to Christ's natural body. Therefore the conclusion follows.

Bellarmine 1. Grinding is not necessary to the nature of eating.

Protestant reply. For the eating of solid food, chewing is so necessary by
the order of nature that the Scholastics customarily enumerate it among the
parts of eating. Thus Alexander of Hales, Part 4, question 10, member 9,
article 1: “There are three things in eating: chewing, conveyance into the
stomach, and incorporation.” Thus Bonaventure, on Book 4 of the *Sentences*,
distinction 9, question 2: “In eating there is a twofold act belonging to its
completeness, namely chewing and incorporation, as is evident to one who
considers it.”

Bellarmine 2. Christ's body is not eaten absolutely but under the species of
bread, and thus it too is ground insofar as the species themselves are ground
by the teeth.

[1632 witness | printed p. 84]

Protestant reply. 1. If Christ's body is not eaten absolutely in the
Eucharist, then that *hoc* which is taken and eaten absolutely is not properly
Christ's body. 2. Accidental species cannot of themselves be the object of a
real action which divides and corrupts, such as grinding. Chewing is as it
were the first digestion of food; are accidents customarily digested for
nourishment? 3. All inexplicable evasions of this kind, which are wrapped up
in the phrase “under the species of bread,” and so forth, depend upon a
confusion of the disparate significations of this particle *sub* (“under”).
As Alexander of Hales, Part 4, question 19, member 4, article 2, section 3,
observes, it does not always denote “under” in the sense of containment, but
sometimes “under” in the sense of signification, in the manner in which the
thing signified is under the sign.

44. Protestant argument. Similar predications—that is, tropical or
figurative ones—occur everywhere in Scripture concerning similar things.

Bellarmine 1. It is not thereby gathered that the predication in this passage
is such a one.

Protestant reply. It follows, 1. that another sense cannot be concluded from
the bare phrase; and 2. that this sense is exceedingly probable, since it
agrees so well with the manner of speaking customary in Scripture concerning
similar things, on account of a reason which has as much force in this passage
as in the others.

Bellarmine 2. No example is brought forward in which something is predicated
tropically of the pronoun *hoc*.

Protestant reply. 1. The papists cannot bring forward one example in which by
“this is that” one substance is changed into another. 2. We, however, have
given examples involving the pronoun *hoc*. Let this one suffice, Genesis
17:10: “This is the covenant.” The other phrases have the same force: “The
lamb is the Passover,” that is, the passage; and “The rock was Christ.”

Bellarmine 3. The paschal lamb was not tropically called the Passover, for
there was no similarity between the killing of the lamb and the Lord's
passage. Rather, it was properly called the Passover because it was sacrificed
in memory and honor of that passage.

Protestant reply. 1. Not every trope rests upon similarity; only metaphor does
so. The lamb could therefore be tropically called the Passover although it
had no similarity to the passage. 2. The lamb in its appointed use, with all
the preparation and manner required, greatly represented the things
pertaining to that passage, from which it received its designation. 3. What
Bellarmine says involves a contradiction: that the lamb was called “the
passage” in memory of the passage and nevertheless was so called properly,
not tropically.

Bellarmine 4. By the words “The rock was Christ,” 1 Corinthians 10, it is not
signified that the material rock was a figure of Christ, which we do not

[1632 witness | printed p. 85]

deny was a figure of Christ.

Protestant reply. 1. That the rock was a figure of Christ is understood from
no source other than this passage. 2. Bellarmine concedes that there is a
trope in Christ's being called a rock, and yet he affirms that the proposition
“The rock was Christ” is taken properly, not figuratively, because it is
explained by the addition of “spiritual.” But this is nothing other than
granting the thing itself while disputing about its manner.

Bellarmine 5. In every passage brought forward, where there is either a trope
or something obscure, an explanation is always added.

Protestant reply. Those same explanations illuminate all similar phrases and
therefore this very phrase which is in dispute. Besides these, there were many
circumstances in the Lord's Supper by which these words were sufficiently
explained.

Bellarmine 6. In parables, similitudes, visions, and so forth, *est* can be
taken for “signifies” without any trope, because the whole essence of such
things is situated in signifying.

Protestant reply. If this is true, then the same must be said of the bread in
the Supper, because its whole relative essence is situated in signifying and
sealing. But once this is conceded, there is no reason for us to contend
greatly about a trope.

45. Protestant argument. In the words “This is my body,” the whole Christ is
signified by “body”; therefore some trope must necessarily be conceded in
that word.

Bellarmine 1. By the name “body” the body alone is understood, yet the body is
not separated from the blood because of their necessary concomitance.

Protestant reply. If by the name “body” only the body is understood, and by
the name “blood” only the blood, then it is not signified in the sacrament
that Christ suffered anything for us in his soul, reputation, outward goods,
or, finally, in his whole manhood. This is plainly absurd.

Bellarmine. We can admit an *intellectio* here, but there is a great difference
between an *intellectio* and a metaphor or metonymy. Catholics admit an
*intellectio* in the words of John 1, “The Word was made flesh,” but not a
metaphor or metonymy.

Protestant reply. 1. The state of the question is sophistically changed here.
The question now is not whether there is a metaphor or metonymy in the word
“body,” but whether there is no trope there. 2. An *intellectio* occurs where
something is understood which is not expressed. But if this is admitted in
words pertaining to the institution of a sacrament, then Bellarmine's
principle in chapter 9 is certainly finished, where he contends that
sacraments require proper words which are neither ambiguous nor obscure, lest
error arise in their use. 3. An *intellectio* does not exclude a trope but
very often means plainly the same thing. 4. On those very words, “The Word was
made flesh,” Thomas, in his *Catena*, notes from Augustine that “flesh” is
understood to be put for “man,” the whole being understood from a part, by a
figurative manner of speech.

46. Protestant argument. In the words “This cup is the New Testament in my
blood,” a twofold trope must necessarily be admitted: 1. in the word “cup”; 2.
in the word “testament.”

Bellarmine 1. We do not deny that there is a trope in the word “cup,” but it
is explained very clearly in the same place. The trope is also customary and
common, so that it is no less clear than the proper words themselves.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine here overturns two of his chief foundations upon
which he builds his transubstantiation: 1. that there is no trope where the
institution of sacraments is concerned; and 2. that every trope is obscure.
2. He also establishes two things, one of which overturns the other: that this
trope is no less clear than proper words, and yet that it is explained. 3. We
have shown that the trope which we maintain is customary and common in all
the sacraments. It therefore had no need of any particular explanation in
this passage.

Bellarmine 2. There is no trope in the word “testament,” because it is not
taken for the testator's will by which he disposes of an inheritance, but for
the authentic instrument by which the right to the inheritance, and the
inheritance itself, is applied to the heirs. In this manner circumcision in
Genesis 17 is in the former sense called the sign of the covenant and in the
latter sense the covenant itself.

Protestant reply. 1. The name “will” can never be attributed to this or that
instrument of a will according to some fixed rationale except through a
change in the sense or signification of that name. But this change makes a
trope. 2. That variation of interpretation in Genesis 17 is ridiculous:
“Circumcision is the sign of the covenant,” that is, of the will;
“Circumcision is the covenant,” that is, the instrument of the will. For every
sacramental sign is an instrument, and to imagine that in those words there
is one covenant and then another, each properly so called, is to rave against
common sense. 3. A little earlier Bellarmine conceded that the phrase “sign of
the covenant” is an explanation of the other phrase, “the covenant.” He must
therefore concede in the same manner in this passage that “testament” is to be
explained by “sign of the testament.” 4. If the instrument of a testament is
properly called a testament, then this phrase would also be proper and worthy
of approval: “This is the testament of the testament.” 5. There is no
customary trope which could not easily be converted into a non-trope by the
same art.

47. Protestant argument. One purpose of the Eucharist is the remembrance of
Christ giving his body and blood for us: “Do this in remembrance of me.” But
remembrance concerns a thing which is not present. Therefore Christ's body
and blood are not really present.

Bellarmine. We are commanded to receive the Eucharist in remembrance of the
Lord's passion, which is not present but absent—indeed, it does not exist now,
but only took place in the past.

Protestant reply. The sense, therefore, according to Bellarmine as interpreter,
is of this kind: “In remembrance of my bodily passion, make, break, and eat my
natural body, and make and drink my

[1632 witness | printed p. 87]

natural blood.” But a commemoration of this kind conflicts with common sense:
that Christ should be made really and substantially, and eaten, in remembrance
of his passion. For then the instituted means of commemoration would in
reality be the very same thing as the thing commemorated. 2. Christ would at
the same time be the subject and the adjunct of his passion, for a sign which
commemorates by institution is an adjunct of the thing commemorated. Our
memory would be led back-to-front through an insensible thing to his sensible
passion.

48. Protestant argument. That which was in the cup is called “the fruit of
the vine” after the consecration, Matthew 20:29 and Matthew 14:25. The substance
of the wine therefore remains.

Bellarmine 1. It is plainly gathered from Luke that those words pertain to the
cup of wine which the Lord gave to the apostles before the consecration of the
mysteries.

Protestant reply. On the contrary, it is plainly gathered even from Luke
himself, although those words are transposed there, that they pertain to the
mystical and sacramental cup of the Lord's Supper. For in chapter 22, verse
17, the Lord is said to have given thanks over that cup, in which he says the
fruit of the vine afterward remained, in the same manner as he gave thanks
over the bread in verse 19. Bellarmine concedes in chapter 10 that this giving
of thanks is to be understood as the sacramental blessing and consecration.
Indeed, he contends in express words that this very giving of thanks pertains
to the institution of this sacrament: “The Lord,” he says, “is not read to
have given thanks except in John 6, Matthew 15, John 11, and finally in the
institution of this sacrament.” For the giving of thanks of which we read in
Luke 22:17 is not one of the earlier instances. Therefore, by Bellarmine's own
admission, it pertains to the institution of this sacrament.

Bellarmine. Luke joins these words, “I will not drink of the fruit of the
vine,” with these: “I will not eat of this Passover.”

Protestant reply. These words are joined because of the commonality which they
have in what is said—“I will not eat”; “I will not drink”—and because the
paschal and eucharistic wine are substantially identical. But in Matthew
26:29 the demonstrative pronoun in “of this fruit of the vine” necessarily
refers to that which was demonstrated by the same pronoun in the preceding
words: “This is my blood.”

Bellarmine 2. If we were compelled to admit that those words of the Lord, “I
will not drink of the fruit of the vine,” were spoken after the consecration
of the cup, our adversaries would still gain nothing. For those words could
and ought to be referred not to the consecrated cup but to the one drunk
previously.

Protestant reply. From this we necessarily establish that what Christ drank
in his Supper was the fruit of the vine, because those words neither ought nor
can be referred to a cup drunk previously. For

[1632 witness | printed p. 88]

the present time is expressly denoted by Matthew, *ap’ arti*, “from now,” or
“from this time,” and the consecrated cup is demonstrated to the senses as if
by a finger: “of this fruit of the vine.”
