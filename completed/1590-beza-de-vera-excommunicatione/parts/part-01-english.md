## To the Christian reader

Theodore Beza wishes grace and peace from the Lord.

What the beginnings were of the controversy over **the presbytery and
excommunication** which arose in the church of Heidelberg—and which, by God's
kindness and the prudence of the most illustrious Prince, was extinguished
there—and what course it afterward followed, I do not know well enough. Even if
I did know, I have no intention of recounting it, since I have resolved neither
to dig up this fire nor to fight with a dead man, especially one who was my
friend. But because the weapons are now expressly aimed at me, I must briefly
explain what part I had in that dispute, so that everyone may understand that I
entered it neither as a quarrelsome man nor as a meddler in other men's affairs.

I have always honored, with the respect it deserves, the renowned and orthodox
church of Heidelberg, most happily restored by the most illustrious Elector
Frederick III of blessed memory. While Dr. Erastus was alive, I embraced him
with a particular affection. He was, admittedly, a private member of that
church and a physician by profession, but he was also diligently versed in Holy
Scripture, and I knew well enough that he had rendered distinguished service
toward that church's restoration. How much he in turn valued our friendship
before this controversy I learned not only from very many letters written to me
in the warmest terms, but also from the fact that he sent me certain writings
of his not merely to be printed but also to be examined.

When letters from friends informed me that such weighty matters were being
debated there, and not without a certain bitterness, I did everything in my
power through brotherly letters—both to Erastus himself and to my very dear
brother Dr. Caspar Olevianus, then the chief pastor of that church—to have the
whole matter settled calmly and amicably at once. I asked Erastus, once he had
set out his opinion before the most illustrious Prince and the pastors of his
church, men of proven piety and learning, as the duty of a Christian required,
to be content that he had satisfied his conscience. He should then either leave
the entire matter alone or commit its decision to those to whom judgment of it
belonged.

[Scan 8; signature A2v]

Olevianus, whose temperament I knew to be rather vehement and at times nearly
immoderate, I exhorted not merely as a brother but also as my former pupil in
the schools of Lausanne and Geneva. Although in my judgment he was defending a
just cause, he was to guard himself with the greatest care from every excess.

Then, while I was doing this, manuscript theses by Dr. Erastus himself were
brought to me. They were not the theses now published, which I had never seen
before, but were more numerous and—although, as he himself testifies in the
preface, they embraced the same position—certainly differed somewhat in several
places. The friends from whom I received them added that copies were already
circulating and had been sent to the Zurich brethren, who did not disapprove of
them. Those same friends, with the most illustrious Prince's knowledge, asked me
to undertake to inspect and answer them. What, then, ought I to have done? If I
am not greatly mistaken, precisely what I did. I examined the theses and, with
a sincere mind—as God himself is my witness—tested them by the standard of the
divine Word. While preparing a calm refutation, free from every harshness, to be
read and examined first by Erastus and then by my very dear Zurich brethren, I
continued by letter to press the reconciliation I had already begun.

I hoped that after reading my work Erastus might perhaps change his opinion, or
at least remove from it some things that he had even then written in those
theses with altogether excessive bitterness. As for those eminent Zurich
brethren—with whom this church has always been most closely united and, God
willing, always will be—I had always known that to their supreme piety and
learning they joined a mind most devoted to the peace of the churches. I did not
doubt that, even if they perhaps did not entirely approve everything I wrote,
they would nevertheless devote all their labor and utmost zeal to extinguishing
the fire that was beginning to blaze. Nor did that hope disappoint me.

The controversy had already advanced rather far and, as I surmise, Dr. Erastus
had by then written out this refutation of his, only now delivered to me, with a
pen sometimes dipped more in pure bile than in ink. Nevertheless, the most
illustrious Prince had already determined in the churches of his Palatinate
what needed to be done. The Zurich brethren also foresaw in their prudence where
the matter would otherwise end and judged that it should be resolved once for
all by a general assembly and conference rather than by publishing the
arguments of various private men, however learned. They therefore persuaded me
without difficulty not to circulate my writing, which I had never thought of
publishing. The facts show that they obtained the same agreement from Dr.
Erastus himself: although he kept the materials now published in his possession
(as the author of this edition testifies), nothing of them appeared while he
was alive,

[Scan 9; signature A3r]

before the present day. I was therefore filled with extraordinary joy that, by
the authority not only of the most illustrious Prince but also by the counsel
of such great men and the consent of the disputants themselves, the protasis
and rising action of this tragedy had at last reached so peaceful a
denouement. Who but that evil spirit, the author of discord and quarrels even
among brothers, has now disturbed that joy by again stirring a little flame
from the embers of the extinguished fire?

I hear that, to justify this ill-fated edition, they put forward Dr. Erastus's
widow, now the wife of a certain Castelvetro. They say that, when her husband
was dying, she received a commission concerning it, confirmed even by an oath.
Who can readily believe it? Consider a man who, while alive, took no action at
Heidelberg or Basel for many years; who perhaps never inspected his copy from
that time onward, for this printer himself testifies that it lay among his
papers, exposed to moths and bookworms and obscured by repeated erasures; who,
when I visited him at Basel, embraced me so warmly without mentioning this
dispute at all that he promised, God willing, to visit Geneva soon; who had at
his bedside the eminent men and closest friends Dr. Jacob Grynaeus and Dr.
Theodore Zwinger—the former as physician of his soul and the latter also of his
body—yet said not a word to them about this matter; and whom, as reliable
witnesses attest, death itself could scarcely persuade that he suffered from a
fatal disease. Who, I ask, would suppose that, as he breathed out his life and,
as befitted a Christian, already strained upward with his whole heart, he gave
even the slightest thought to stirring this Camarina marsh—much less secretly
demanded an oath from his wife concerning publication of a writing lying among
his papers, with no witness whatever present?

Let us nevertheless grant that he acted thus, though it accords poorly with
his usual constancy. Why do you equivocate here, Castelvetro? Do you dare deny
what you affirmed to more than one person at the last Frankfurt fair, when you
brought this merchandise there somewhat late: that this book was brought to
publication by you yourself, or by another man you hired, compelled by the
importunity of your wife as she pressed both Erastus's commission and the pledge
she had given? Tell us, then, with what conscience you play two roles here. You
are the same printer who, concealing that whole story in the letter prefixed to
this edition, testifies that after a long search and the greatest diligence,
labor, and effort you scarcely found this book among heirs who neglected it and
bought it from those same heirs at a price paid to them. Thus you were both
seller and buyer—or, rather, neither. How will you reconcile this story with
that commission and oath? What was it that advised you to act so: Truth herself,
whose devotion supposedly drove you to this edition and to whose keeping you
will entrust it with the same honesty,

[Scan 10; signature A3v]

or rather those “good and pious men” whose desire, you say, you were unwilling
to deprive of so great a benefit? Were they, Castelvetro, the ones who advised
you to print these things secretly, to put down a fictitious Pesclavium in place
of London or another English city, and to come onstage partly anonymous and
partly suddenly disguised beneath the mask of some unknown Baocius
Soltaceterus? Did you learn those frauds and falsehoods in the school of truth,
and not rather in the school of arrogance and shamelessness?

Why was it necessary to name Beza, whose name Erastus himself nowhere thought
fit to mention? Good man, what besides a wicked purpose and an evil temper
moved you—or the hired writer of that letter—to add an index full of abuse and
slanders? Our present condition prevents me from thinking that the perverse
lust for rule, which either your bile or that of your hired name-maker would not
allow you to omit, refers either to me or to the brothers who are exiles here.
No one, however hostile, could reasonably begrudge us our present condition.
But you should consider whether, in your ingratitude, you intended rather to
strike at others not so far away, whose patience you have long abused.

One more outrage was needed to complete the crime: this anxious champion of
truth had to append several private letters of great men to Erastus's book,
while threatening that he possessed still more. With a greater quantity of
fuel thrown on, the revived fire that certain men are preparing might thus
spread farther and wider. But what is the publication of private letters
written among friends and family except, as someone long ago rightly
complained, to remove companionship from life and conversations with absent
friends? How many things, humorous or serious, are commonly found in letters
that ought never to be published! How many ambiguous and insufficiently
considered thoughts are poured into the bosom of friends which we ourselves
would afterward prefer never to have spoken or written! With what conscience
could you buy these letters, or Dr. Erastus's heirs sell them?

Perhaps more than enough has now been said about this edition. Yet I could not
and ought not pass it over, so that all may recognize the spirit that drives
these enemies of the peace and concord shared by the holy churches, who use
zeal for truth as a pretext, and may therefore guard themselves more prudently
against their snares.

I must now turn to you whose judgment and authority these men have dared use to
decorate their apple of discord. By the churches' own weapons they seek to
pierce, utterly overthrow, or transform into dreadful disorder our churches—the
French, English, Scottish,
Belgian, many of the German, Swiss, Rhaetian, and Savoyard—which are most
closely united to one another by the same bond of faith. Yet I trust their
attempt will fail. You who are now blessed with Jesus Christ, enjoying the
fruit of your labors—

[Scan 11; signature A4r]

spirits of Dr. Heinrich Bullinger and Dr. Rudolf Gualter, who were until
recently lights not only of the Zurich church but of the entire Christian
Church—you who, while journeying with us on earth, labored so well to prevent
this fire from being kindled: if any concern for human affairs now touches you
in heaven, can I suppose that you would approve either the signatures of men
who dare misuse your privately written letters to rekindle this fire, or the
publication of writings which, while alive, you had suppressed for the sake of
peace and common concord? Far be that from us. Among us your holy memory instead
flourishes and always will, in keeping with your very great services to the
whole Christian Church and with our perpetual and inviolable union.

“But,” one of them will say, “they nevertheless approved Dr. Erastus's theses.”
I do not deny that those theses displeased them less than they displeased us. I
also admit that, concerning the use of excommunication and the authority of the
presbytery, they leaned somewhat toward Dr. Erastus's opinion—not because they
condemned these things in themselves, but because they feared their abuse. Who
will grant us that on every individual matter, especially matters on which
controversy is possible without injury to faith and religion, we must all think
precisely alike? Who is so ignorant of sacred history that, with all due respect
to such great men, he does not know that disagreements and even quarrels arose
between Epiphanius and Chrysostom, Theophilus and Nazianzen, Theodoret and Cyril,
partly through emissaries of Satan of this kind and partly because those men too
were human, and that these quarrels advanced to the greatest lengths?

That spirit of discord attempted more than once to produce the same result
between the eminent pastors of Zurich and of this church. Yet by God's kindness
he was so far from succeeding that our mutual union in all things emerged all
the stronger. Those excellent brothers of blessed memory were endowed with
supreme piety and learning, and these very letters testify that nothing
concerned them more than the churches' peace. Who can believe that it ever
entered their minds to approve what our Erastus, prompted surely by bile rather
than reason, dared not merely write but repeatedly assert: namely, that
excommunication and the ecclesiastical presbytery are a purely human invention,
unheard of in Holy Scripture and in the whole Israelite and pure Christian
Church; that they are sheer tyranny, introduced into the Church against the
magistrate by a lust for rule; and, finally, that they are nothing other than
the god Maozim foretold by Daniel? Come, then: let us hear from those very
letters how entirely differently they thought about this same subject. Dr.
Gualter writes to Dathenus in these exact words:

> In February 1536 the leading ministers of all the Swiss churches were
> summoned to a public assembly at Basel. Dr. Capito and Bucer of blessed
> memory, ministers of the Strasbourg church, and delegates of the Neuchâtel
> church were also present. After conferring among themselves long and fully,
> they all at last agreed upon the position set out in our latest confession.

[Scan 12; signature A4v]

The following are the exact words of chapter 18 of that Helvetic Confession.
Not only neighboring churches but even the most distant peoples who profess the
same orthodox truth as we do subscribed to it, and above all this church of
Geneva:

> Because discipline is exceedingly necessary in the Church, and because the
> entire ancient Church used excommunication, and ecclesiastical courts existed
> among the people of God in which prudent and God-fearing men exercised this
> discipline, it is therefore also the ministers' duty to employ this discipline
> for the Church's edification, according to the times and the public condition
> and as necessity requires. In this matter the following rule must always be
> observed: everything in the Church must be done decently and honorably for
> edification, without tyranny or sedition, so that vices are not fostered and
> nothing is done imprudently. The Apostle testifies that power was given to the
> Church not for destruction but for edification. The Lord forbids even the
> angels to root out all the weeds, lest they uproot the wheat with them.

When Dr. Bullinger was asked by Dr. Erastus when people first began to be
excommunicated for offenses in life, he replied:

> I see that this is quite ancient. When the Church lacked a magistrate, it
> quickly devised plans for restraining wicked men, and no remedy seemed more
> suitable to some than excommunication.

So he wrote in another letter to Erastus. Dr. Gualter likewise said:

> There has never been any dispute over excommunication between our Zurich
> church and the beloved church of Geneva. Do not suppose us so mad as to wish
> to subject or reduce everything here to the rigor of the Genevan church. Some
> years ago, when serious disturbances arose in that church and we were asked
> about them, we had an opportunity to force our views on them, or at least to
> commend them. Yet, as was right, we followed counsels of peace. We therefore
> neither wished to hinder the discipline they had begun nor to call it either
> rigid or lax, nor did we wish to offer their adversaries an occasion for
> raging more violently. For just as we claim no dominion over other churches,
> so surely, if some men

[Scan 13; signature A5r]

> in their own churches—whose circumstances are better understood by them than
> by us—exclude certain people from the Supper by means of their discipline,
> without turmoil and complaint and with splendid fruit, we leave that to the
> judgment of the church and to their own good faith.

So he wrote, with his characteristic Christian prudence and fairness. What more
is needed? No one should henceforth thrust at us the authority of Dr. Gualter,
a man who served the Church of God exceedingly well and remained most closely
united to me until his last breath, as though he had straightforwardly and
without qualification approved these theses and their refutation. Let us learn
instead from the original texts of his homilies, recently published, what he
thought about most of the New Testament passages I cited for excommunication
and the presbytery and which Dr. Erastus attacked.

Nothing is pressed more strongly by Dr. Erastus than the claim that **to hand
over to Satan** and **to remove from the midst of the Church** mean execution.
Dr. Gualter says the contrary in Homily 7 on 1 Corinthians 5:3:

> The Apostle determined that this incestuous man should be handed over to
> Satan, that is, cast out of the Church, so that the Corinthians might be freed
> from so great a disgrace and the scandal removed. The reason for this form of
> speech is that Satan reigns outside the Church.

He likewise says in Homily 12 on 1 Timothy 1:20:

> Because Hymenaeus and Alexander had overthrown the faith of others, they were
> handed over to Satan, that is, excluded from fellowship with the faithful.

Dr. Erastus, you contend from 1 Corinthians 5 and Matthew 18 that no presbytery
existed at Corinth—that is, no ecclesiastical assembly to which the incestuous
man could be referred. Our Dr. Gualter says the contrary in Homily 1425:

> That power of handing over to Satan belonged to the whole Church, which
> nevertheless acted through chosen elders, lest confusion result. See 1
> Timothy 5 and also Tertullian, *Apology* 39.

Dr. Erastus, you interpret “destruction” as the final punishment of death. But
Dr. Gualter says:

> Paul commands that the incestuous man be handed to Satan for the destruction
> of the flesh, that is, so that the flesh, subdued and mortified, might give
> place to the spirit and the man might stand in the day of the Lord.

This is what happened with that incestuous man in 2 Corinthians 2:6, where
*epitimia*, a censure or rebuke, is plainly distinguished from being handed over
to Satan. The Corinthian presbytery had been prevented from proceeding that far
by hearing Paul's sentence again and by the censured offender's fierce
repentance, displayed with immense bitterness. Nor did Paul say that this
rebuke was imposed “by everyone,” but “by the majority,” namely by the body of
elders chosen in that church, as the article placed before the phrase also
shows. It is therefore also plain that in Matthew 18:17 “church” means not the
universal assembly but the ecclesiastical council, which was succeeded by the
Christian presbytery,

[Scan 14; signature A5v]

contrary to what that disturber of churches, named by Dr. Erastus on page 70 of
his refutation, supposed. His view is not merely false but ridiculous, unless
we prefer to imagine that the whole church was assembled as often as this
occurred—that is, that it sat almost perpetually for judicial business whenever
someone who had sinned against a brother would not yield to private warnings.

Dr. Erastus takes the “cutting off” in Galatians 5:12 to mean execution. Dr.
Gualter says the contrary in Homily 7 on that epistle:

> Because circumcision so pleased those men that they nullified Christ's merit,
> Paul wishes them to be entirely cut off—that is, from the fellowship of the
> Church.

Dr. Erastus dislikes the allegorical application of unleavened bread and
leaven, although it rests upon apostolic authority. Dr. Gualter says the
contrary in Homily 35:

> Excommunication was lawful exclusion from the fellowship of the Church—and
> consequently from Christ—so that the person who offended it might be
> corrected and saved, and also so that the infection might not spread to more
> people.

And in Homily 36:

> Thus Paul indicates that discipline is necessary lest the infection of sin
> spread. That occurs when impunity is permitted, for the multitude of sinners
> creates hope of impunity.

And in Homily 37:

> By “leaven” the Apostle understands sin and everything connected with it.

From these very statements, as also from the interpretations of ancient and
recent writers—and indeed from the matter itself—I judge it clear enough that
our printer was at least speaking hyperbolically when, supposedly with no
concern for his own advantage, he commended Dr. Erastus's writings by appealing
to the authority of the leading theologians of our age and by saying that they
“were wonderfully approved by learned and pious men free from party spirit.”

I do not in the least deny that those great men, including Dr. Wolfgang
Musculus—whose eminent piety and learning I doubt anyone esteems more highly
than I do—held the following opinion. Excommunication formerly belonged to the
presbytery, and presbyters went so far as exclusion from the Lord's Supper
because the Church did not then have a Christian magistrate. Through his
authority both capital judgments would have been exercised—had this been done
at Corinth, there would have been no need for Paul's sentence about handing
that man to Satan—and other judges would have sufficiently restrained those
who sinned by lesser offenses, without excluding them from the Supper. Since a
Christian magistrate can and ought now to perform both tasks, Christian
churches can now dispense with this severity of discipline. I do not deny, I
say, that they thought this, although even this remains very far from Dr.
Erastus's position. Yet they did so without applying passages of Scripture to
their opinion by novel interpretations devised to suit it, and without wishing
to prejudice those who thought differently. It is clear enough that they
wished to root out the Cathars and Novatians reborn in our age, not far from
Zurich, among those furious Anabaptists, and also to root out papal tyranny;

[Scan 15; signature A6r]

they did not intend thereby to condemn excommunication itself when administered
moderately, lawfully, fruitfully, and in accordance with God's Word.

I have not undertaken to discuss this matter here in its fullest extent. Yet I
did not conceal what I believed while those men were alive, without even the
smallest injury on that account to the supreme and perpetual union of our
minds. Therefore it cannot be right for me to remain silent now. My only purpose
is to set these matters before truly pious and learned theologians, wherever
they now are, to be judged in a Christian spirit.

I ask first whether the ancient people of God did not have their holy
magistrates, and second whether exclusion from the worship of God was not
nevertheless divinely commanded against the unclean and distinctly exercised
by an ecclesiastical council. The first point certainly cannot be denied. Even
if I granted Dr. Erastus that the second applied only to those defiled by
external or ceremonial pollution, nothing contrary to my conclusion could be
inferred from it. For my purpose it is enough to show that, even when there was
a holy magistrate, exclusion from the sacraments had a place among God's
people.

But the many laws concerning sacrifices for sin demonstrate that Erastus is
mistaken here. I mean sin of every kind: committed through error, ignorance of
the law, ignorance of the fact, or an evil conscience. See Leviticus 4:2, 13,
22, and 27; 5:4 and 17; and Numbers 15:22–23. That this extended even to
transgressions of the second table of the law, and that confession of sin
before the priests and expiation through sacrifice were added to civil
penalties, is stated most plainly in Leviticus 6 through verse 7 and Numbers
5:6–8.

Expiation necessarily presupposes impurity, and exclusion from the altar
followed uncleanness. What was that but our greater excommunication? What was
the expressly commanded confession before the priest but an adequate testimony
of repentance? Those unclean only with respect to legal impurities—whom we may
compare with the ancient class of penitents called the Abstainers—were ordered
to be sprinkled with cleansing water. How much less was every unclean person
approaching the altar left to the examination of his private conscience, as
Dr. Erastus maintains and others perhaps press too strongly.

Someone will object: “If this is so, since no one lived who did not daily sin
in some way against the moral commandments, what could follow but that the
altar must glow day and night at every moment with sacrifices for sin?” Not so.
The lawgiver expressly restricted these sacrifices to sins that had been
disclosed and had become known, and therefore were connected with scandal and
judicially established; see Leviticus 4:14, 23, and 28. Since this is so, the
Israelite magistrate did not impede that ecclesiastical discipline. As its
guardian and defender, he sometimes even assisted it with capital punishment
against those who were unruly and incorrigible; see Deuteronomy 17:12. I see no
reason why the same should not be true of

[Scan 16; signature A6v]

a Christian magistrate. And why should the right that existed in the synagogue
under the law not remain valid in the Church under the gospel, by Christ's
authority in Matthew 18:17? Furthermore, who will guarantee any church that its
magistrate will be sufficient to restrain those who, through their disgraceful
life, contaminate the whole Church—not without manifest contagion, as happens
far too often? Must we therefore wait until this leaven corrupts the whole lump?

“They are warned by the pastors,” you will say. But what if one of them, as very
many such men have done, safely despises the private rebukes of an individual
or of the Church itself—that is, of the presbytery—and nevertheless approaches
the Lord's table, thereby binding himself to an even more dreadful judgment of
God? Should a diligent pastor devoted to his flock—or, rather, the presbytery—
be required to allow a wandering little sheep voluntarily to rush headlong to
its destruction through the profanation of sacred things? Paul certainly did
not judge that this should be done. He did not simply remit something of the
sentence of handing over to Satan, though it was his own decree. Rather, he
finally added the chief reason why the censure imposed not “by all” but “by the
majority” should suffice: namely, lest the
truly repentant man, who therefore needed pardon rather than excommunication,
be swallowed up by excessive sorrow. Otherwise Paul would by no means have been
satisfied with either the incestuous man's self-restraint or that of the
Corinthian presbytery.

For freeing a pastor from fault, a diligent and appropriate rebuke of the
sinner suffices; see Ezekiel 18. But it does not suffice for the salvation of a
sinner who needs a sharper remedy—which is one of the chief ends of
excommunication—nor as an example, nor to prevent contagion. There is, in fact,
little difference between people who have no Christian magistrate and people
whose magistrate is Christian in outward profession but in reality forgets the
duties of faith toward the Church and is no purer than the Church's other sick
sheep. Must there be a suspension of action where sin threatens the Church with
the greatest danger?

Finally, it cannot be denied—even if the denial is wrongly made—that external
civil penalties, unless the Spirit of God accompanies them, can achieve no more
than some abstention from sin through fear of punishment. Thus they cut off only
a branch of sin, not its root. The root is cut away solely by the Holy Spirit's
power, through God's Word preached publicly and individually and penetrating
to the division of soul and spirit. It is established that responsibility for
applying this remedy with public authority belongs not to the civil magistrate
but to the ecclesiastical order. In this sense the Zurich church has never
lacked such an order, lawfully chosen partly from pastors and partly from
distinguished citizens, as the prosperous edification of that splendid church
under its own laws demonstrates.

But ecclesiastical misconduct in judgments of this kind may rightly be feared
and guarded against,

[Scan 17; signature A7r]

as may even that tyranny which long ago persecuted the prophets in the
Israelite church and for many centuries cruelly persecuted so many holy men in
the Christian Church, and still persecutes them with utmost fury. The same has
also occurred more than once in synods, even very large ones. What surer and
more suitable remedy, I ask, can be used to avert or restrain this kind of
tyranny or oligarchy than the authority of prudent citizens of especially
proven piety, chosen and joined to the pastors? Even a consistory properly
constituted from all these elements might do an injustice to an accused person.
Nothing therefore prevents an appeal to a synod where synods can be convened,
as very many ancient canons—not to be rejected—provide and as is observed in
churches of larger territories. Where no synod is possible, as in smaller
territories, the Christian magistrate, as guardian and defender of both tables
of the law and also of ecclesiastical government, can nevertheless provide
other remedies for complainants without violating the ecclesiastical order.

So that everyone may know whether the Genevan church exercises this discipline
for edification, with the moderation that is proper and that the circumstances
of this place and time require, I have decided to present its whole form here
briefly and in summary. My purpose is not to force it upon anyone—something that
has never entered our minds—but to let everyone understand whether some people
justly or unjustly accuse us of excessive severity and something resembling
tyranny. Indeed, if our order is compared with the ecclesiastical order of the
French, Scottish, and Belgian churches, we might instead be accused of a
certain slowness.

We therefore have a consistory. The ministers of the Word do not sit there as
the only judges; twice as many others are selected, partly from the lesser and
partly from the greater Council, and not without a prior public proclamation to
the people. Concerning the doctrinal teachings received in the Church, those
who teach otherwise are first warned amicably and fraternally. If they yield,
they are ordered to remain quiet, without any mark of disgrace. If they persist
and a second, sterner warning does no good, they are summoned before the
consistory. If they stubbornly resist it, only then are they barred from the
solemn Lord's Supper as the token of the doctrine from which they dissent, and
the Council is informed of the whole matter. The same ruling is made against
those who reveal their profane mind through open contempt for sacred assemblies.

As for individuals' conduct, calm admonitions are used for hidden faults, as
the Lord prescribes. No one is called before the Church's court because of a
private offense not connected with public scandal, unless stubborn contempt
for private admonitions is added.

[Scan 18; signature A7v]

When the Church warns those who despise correction for their faults and lawful
testimony convicts them, if they respond to reproof with obstinacy instead of
asking pardon, Christ's word in Matthew 18:17 requires them to abstain from the
Lord's Supper until they attest a changed mind. In the case of notorious and
public sins, which the Church cannot conceal, the offender is indeed summoned
before the consistory as an example to others, but is dismissed when he asks
pardon. If, after being rebuked more sternly, he still does not acknowledge his
sin or promise amendment, then, as one persisting in a scandal to the Church,
he is kept from the sacred Supper—the seal of mutual union with Christ and our
neighbor—until he gives a sign of repentance.

In the case of more serious crimes, deserving a penalty more severe than words
alone, after a lawful examination those who have fallen are ordered, with the
reason first made public so that their repentance may be more plainly evident
to everyone, to humble themselves before God and abstain from the Supper for a

## A disputation concerning the presbytery and excommunication, divinely instituted in the Christian Church

*Long ago set against one hundred manuscript theses by the physician Dr.
Thomas Erastus, and now published for the first time.*

Christians have never doubted that the Church absolutely needs discipline—that
is, some definite order—or that its form must be sought from God's Word. Yet
three principal causes explain why people today dispute what that discipline
is, what its parts are, and what its lawful use is. The first is common to every
age: people bear nothing less willingly than the Lord's yoke. The second is
that, since papal tyranny undeniably advanced on this occasion so far as to
overthrow that whole divinely instituted order, many exceedingly learned and
excellent men now recoil so strongly at the very name of excommunication—not
the least important part of that discipline—that they begin to doubt the thing
itself. The third is that those who began restoring the churches in our time
were so intent upon the primary task, the purification of doctrine itself, that
they did not give the other part their fullest consideration.

Another evil soon followed. What would not have been difficult to accomplish at
the beginning has now become not only exceedingly difficult but, to some,
almost impossible. Some, despairing of obtaining it, think nothing in the
present condition should be changed. Some, while seeking moderation, transform
the thing itself into a mere shadow and likeness. Others, moved by reasons of
their own, not only neglect it themselves but do not allow others to attend to
it. Finally, some openly attack as human inventions the two principal parts of
ecclesiastical discipline: excommunication and the authority of the presbytery.
Our present business is with this last group—not in our private name, but

[Scan 24; printed page 2; signature B2v]

in the name of all the churches in which the very form of discipline they
condemn has been restored. We ask them to listen modestly and calmly as we
answer their arguments, without prejudice against those of their brethren who
confine this discipline within narrower bounds, just as we bear without
bitterness their disagreement with us and even with the remotest antiquity,
while religion itself remains secure.

Some say against us that we regard churches lacking excommunication or a
presbytery of this kind as no churches at all. Our perpetual fellowship with
those men and their churches sufficiently testifies how unjust that charge is.
Still more obvious is the slander of those who secretly write that we are
introducing some new tyranny into the churches and wish to force it upon the
rest. It is not so, brothers. We therefore earnestly and urgently ask you not to
listen to lying spirits of this kind. They intend nothing but to use false
accusations against us to open for themselves a way to produce schisms and
hinder the Lord's building.

Those who restored this discipline among us—in the French, Belgian, Scottish,
and other more distant churches as well—are sufficiently vindicated, without
boasting, from every suspicion of ambition, much less of detestable tyranny, by
their many labors endured for religion, the integrity of life maintained to the
end, and finally the martyrdom of many. Their exceptional wisdom and learning
also more than vindicate them among good and learned men from any suspicion of
ignorance. The experience of events has been added to these things. It has so
confirmed us in this discipline that if anyone attempts either to condemn it as
evil in itself or to take it from us, we profess that we can no more abandon it
than we can abandon the defense of doctrine itself. By God's kindness, we have
no fear that any mortal can justly accuse us of approving any abuse of this
discipline.

Those who judge that some parts of this discipline would be not merely useless
but harmful to their churches may certainly use their own judgment. Without
doubt they themselves see what their flocks can bear, and we do not doubt that
brothers of such learning and integrity, whom we also greatly honor in the
Lord, have reasons for their course. Who appointed us judges of another's
flock? Yet we trust that in turn they will believe this about us: we did not
advance farther rashly,

[Scan 25; printed page 3; signature B3r]

nor do we persevere in our practice for trivial reasons. Since matters have now
reached the point that men who overturn our position from its foundations have
confirmed their view in speech and writing, we in turn have determined, as is
proper in a friendly Christian discussion, to defend our discipline with
appropriate and, in our judgment, sure and solid testimonies of Scripture.

We shall follow the very order Dr. Erastus especially approved in certain of
his theses. First, we shall state our position on excommunication. Next, we
shall answer his arguments directed against excommunication itself. Third, we
shall present our reasons and add a refutation of the opposing objections.
Finally, we shall discuss something of the use of excommunication and then
something of the presbytery's institution and authority.

### What we understand by “excommunication”

Dr. Erastus wishes to define the excommunication he attacks as exclusion from
the use of the sacraments, following an examination by the elders, for the
amendment and repentance of life. In this causal definition or description we
especially miss two things. It does not adequately explain either by whose
authority and by whom the sentence is issued, or concerning what matters it is
issued. We therefore prefer the following description:

> Excommunication is a sentence by which a presbytery assembled in the Lord's
> name, after a lawful examination and with the Church's knowledge if necessary,
> declares that a person who has alienated himself from God and has refused to
> hear the Church—that is, the presbytery—is also to be regarded as cast out of
> the Church's external fellowship. This lasts until his attested repentance,
> so far as it ought and can be made known, establishes that he has been
> reconciled to God, either before the whole Church that knew of his
> excommunication or, if the Church was not made aware of it, before the
> presbytery.

In this description we make God himself the author of both the presbytery and
this judgment. The presbytery is only his minister and interpreter. We add that
a lawful examination must precede and, if the gravity of the scandal and the
obstinacy of the convicted sinner require it, that the Church must know of it,
so that we distinguish use from abuse. We further say that excommunication on
earth is consequent upon a rejection previously made in heaven, established
from God's Word and the sinner's hardness. Excommunication on earth is nothing
other than the declaration of another, more hidden judgment made in heaven.
From it one rightly concludes

[Scan 26; printed page 4; signature B3v]

that a person not approved in heaven at that time is unworthy to be counted
among the faithful on earth. This latter declaration made on earth is also
ratified in heaven.

Our description adds another point, showing that this rejection is neither
absolute and unconditional nor perpetual, but conditional and temporary—unless
the sinner should persist in obstinacy to the end. Finally, it says that the
sinner's repentance must be established and satisfaction given either to the
whole Church where the excommunication was published or, if the person was
excommunicated with the presbytery alone as witness, to the presbytery. It
should thus be understood that what Erastus wrote about the presbytery—as though
the reception of excommunicated people depended upon the presbytery's pleasure
and arbitrary choice—does not describe our position.

In its proper place, God willing, we shall demonstrate that this definition is
drawn from God's Word and confirmed by sure testimonies from it. We disapprove
for many reasons that Dr. Erastus mentions “sacraments” too generally when only
the second sacrament, the Lord's Supper, is at issue, and that he emphasizes
exclusion from the Lord's Supper alone. He should not have omitted the principal
matter: the exclusion from Christ's fellowship made in heaven. Unless that
precedes, the earthly sentence too is rightly regarded as void. How far that
exclusion extends—since Christ casts out absolutely none of those whom the
Father has given him, and the decree of election cannot fail—will be explained
in its place.

### Refutation of arguments claiming that excommunication does not include separation from God himself

#### Refutation of Argument I

Since Erastus thinks otherwise, let us weigh the force of his reasons.

> Just as men cannot give salvation to anyone, says Dr. Erastus, so they cannot
> take it from anyone by their sentence. Excommunication therefore cannot be
> defined as cutting off or denying salvation.

Indeed, if one considers independent power, it belongs to God alone to save and
destroy, bind and loose, remit and retain sins, open and shut. Yet who does not
know that these same actions are everywhere attributed to those whose ministry
God uses to perform them, precisely as God's ministers? A subordinate cause
cannot conflict with the primary and proper cause it serves.

Suppose, then, that the presbytery, instituted by God for this very purpose—as
we shall show in its place—after a lawful investigation of the facts makes the
following determination about a drunkard. The Lord declares that drunkards have
no share in God's kingdom, namely,

[Scan 27; printed page 5; signature B4r]

unless they repent. We have established that this man is a drunkard. It follows
that he has no share in God's kingdom unless he repents. If the presbytery
reasons in this way and announces this fact to a convicted person, is this a
human judgment rather than the judgment of God himself?

Suppose every proper attempt to lead such a man to acknowledge his sin has
failed, he remains openly stubborn, and he even mocks those who warn and rebuke
him. The assembly of elders then reasons again: The Lord declares that people
have no share in his kingdom as long as they do not repent. It is therefore
unworthy for such people to be regarded as being in a different condition on
earth, in the assembly of the faithful. It is publicly established that this
man is such a person. Let him therefore also be sent away from the assembly of
the faithful on earth—not so that he may perish, but on this condition: since
his disease can be cured only by applying this cautery, through this very
expulsion let us consult both the wretched man's salvation and that of the rest
of the flock.

Tell me, is this to arrogate dominion over the salvation of souls? Is it not
rather to make known on earth what God has secretly determined in heaven and
what cannot remain unspoken if God's own glory, the man's salvation if he is
curable, and the welfare of the whole Church are to be served?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument II against the definition of excommunication

> Not everyone who was excommunicated was evil, says Dr. Erastus, nor was
> everyone who attempted to participate in the sacraments good. Since it is
> possible that someone may not immediately be admitted to them because he
> cannot satisfy those who preside over discipline, for whatever reason this
> may occur, he does not therefore immediately perish. Just as we cannot affirm
> that all whom we receive into favor will be saved, because many are
> hypocrites, so we ought not deny salvation to all who are excluded, because
> many are wronged.

**Refutation I.** We grant the whole argument and say that it changes the state
of the question. We do not ask whether whatever is decided in any manner at all
by a presbytery is ratified before God. We ask rather whether what a presbytery
does lawfully and from God's Word is ratified in heaven. We affirm this from
Christ's words, which say that what is lawfully bound and loosed on earth is
bound and loosed in heaven.

**Refutation II.** And what, I ask, is the point of this? Excommunication does
not aim at anyone's destruction, but rather at bringing back into the way those
who would otherwise perish.

**Refutation III.** The rhetorical amplification that follows therefore needs
no refutation. Erastus says that even in a presbytery one finds elders full of
pretense, anger, hatred, favoritism, and other human passions.

[Scan 28; printed page 6; signature B4v]

Does the fact that many magistrates have always been full of such passions mean
that the magistrate has no lawful power of life and death? Or, because so many
peddle and adulterate the gospel, does the gospel therefore cease to be God's
power for salvation to everyone who believes?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument III against excommunication's force in heaven

> Only he who knows human hearts, says Dr. Erastus, can cast someone outside the
> Church. Human beings therefore cannot do more than bodily bar people from the
> sacraments.

**Refutation.** We grant the premise and deny that it tells against those who,
as God's ministers, do whatever they do from his Word. By God's grace we are
not so mad or impious as to arrogate to ourselves what belongs to God alone:
knowledge of what lies hidden in the recesses of the heart. Rather, we are
faithful agents of God when we declare bound or loosed on earth those whom he
declares by his Word to be bound or loosed in heaven, and we believe Christ when
he affirms that this proclamation made on earth is ratified in heaven.

If someone asks how we know any particular decree of heaven, we answer as we
just did. As a matter of law, the reasons for losing the heavenly citizenship
are known to us from God's written Word. As a matter of fact, the case is made
clear by adequate testimony. Therefore, whenever the divine voice itself
supplies the major premise of an argument and the minor premise is drawn from
solid testimony concerning the charge and its circumstances, the conclusion
of a presbytery discharging its duty is also solid.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument IV against excommunication's force in heaven

> The apostles, says Dr. Erastus, afflicted certain people with bodily
> penalties and even killed some. Yet because of sins into which people fell,
> they never condemned anyone by casting him out of the Church through
> excommunication so long as he seemed to retain the faith and continued to
> profess rather than revile Christian doctrine.

**Refutation I.** We shall discuss in its place the bodily penalties that the
apostles extraordinarily inflicted upon some by a certain spiritual means. But
we emphatically deny the present assertion that the apostles never
excommunicated anyone because of sins. God willing, in its place we shall have
little difficulty proving the contrary.

For now we ask only whether Peter, after repelling Simon Magus from the laying
on of hands, can be thought willing to admit that same man to the supper of the
Lord's body without manifest repentance. I certainly cannot think so. Peter's
words say otherwise: “You have neither part nor lot in this matter.” A man thus
repelled from the gifts of the Holy Spirit because of simony, and said to have
neither part nor lot in the preaching of the gospel—how could he at last have
been admitted to the Lord's table without testifying repentance?

[Scan 29; printed page 7; signature B5r]

Since Dr. Erastus explicitly excepts people who revile the Christian faith, he
at least concedes that excommunication has a place in their case.

**Refutation II.** Even if we granted that no one is recorded as excommunicated
by the apostles, it would not follow that no one was, since it is sufficiently
established that not every individual act of the apostles was committed to
writing.

**Refutation III.** Even if we granted that no one was ever excommunicated by
the apostles, Erastus's conclusion would not necessarily follow. Many things
are not done which nevertheless can be done if necessity demands it. But far
be it from us to suspect, even in the slightest degree, that the apostles failed
in their duty.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument V against excommunication's force in heaven

> No one can know, says Dr. Erastus, when sinners will return to themselves,
> since some have repented at the very end of life and died in godliness. When
> someone is therefore said to be expelled from the Church, he must be
> understood as cast out of its external, not its internal and spiritual,
> fellowship.

**Refutation.** We grant the premise and deny the conclusion. Human beings
cannot know—unless perhaps extraordinarily informed by God—what God has willed
to reserve to himself alone: to whom and at what moment he will give the spirit
of repentance. Yet that fact in no way impedes the Church's ordinary ministry.
In it, those whom the Lord has called to this office are commanded to feed both
the whole flock and its individual sheep.

Those who feed are not only those who lead hungry sheep to pasture. They also
gently bring wanderers back if the sheep allow it; but if they are stubborn,
they frighten them with warning of danger and even a measured withdrawal of
food and so bring them back into the path. In performing all these tasks they
conduct every detail at the command of the one supreme and true Shepherd, to
whom in constant prayer they commend both themselves and the wandering sheep.
Anyone who wishes may judge that this godly care, prescribed by God himself to
the governors of his flock, is merely external. But we rely upon Christ's
express words, “He who hears you hears me; he who rejects you rejects me.” We
believe the true judgments of true pastors are ratified in heaven just as
certainly as we know that Christ reigns in the ministry of those whom he has
placed here to govern his Church. We judge the lawful decisions of a true
presbytery to be feared no less than if God's eternal Son himself pronounced
them with his own mouth.

[Scan 30; printed page 8; signature B5v]

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument VI against excommunication's force in heaven

> But what, says Dr. Erastus, if such a person dies repentant but could not
> satisfy the presbytery or the Church? It cannot be doubted that the sins of
> those who truly repent are pardoned.

**Refutation.** Someone will infer: “The sentence of excommunication therefore
does not bar a person from the kingdom of heaven.” We neither simply admit nor
deny the inference. The presbytery binds a person on earth neither absolutely
nor perpetually, but conditionally—“unless he repents”—and only until he does
repent. It is one thing to cast someone outside a city without any hope of
restoration, and another to keep him bound within the city but excluded from
the fellowship of the other citizens: he is either to be received again if he
comes to acknowledge himself, or through his fault alone to die an exile.

Although the presbytery alone or the assembly of the whole Church rightly
requires adequate testimony of this repentance, since it neither can nor ought
to judge hidden things, far be it from either to prescribe any law absolutely
and necessarily to God acting inwardly by his own right—whose goodness is
infinite and in whose hand and power all things are—or to the penitent himself.
What should we desire more than that at the final moment of life the Lord
graciously grant a spirit of repentance even to those who showed themselves
stubborn to the very end? Indeed, charity commands us to hope and think this
rather than to think evil of those who have fallen asleep. But this neither
weakens lawful ecclesiastical judgments nor prevents a definite order from
being instituted and preserved in the Church, with God himself confirming it
from heaven. If someone knowingly and willingly despises that order, we do not
see how he can be thought truly to have repented.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument VII against excommunication's force in heaven

> Since no one can be removed from prayer, reading, and hearing the Word, says
> Dr. Erastus, it remains that he be barred only from the sacraments.

**Refutation.** We shall shortly consider whether someone can be excluded all
at once from everything done in the public assembly of the godly. But if we
grant the premise, the conclusion is that excommunication excludes only from
the sacraments—that is, from the Lord's Supper. One cannot be said to be
excluded from baptism if he was never called into the number of the faithful
through baptism, and we acknowledge only two sacraments of the Christian
Church. We understand this exclusion in such a way that one who is prohibited
from the sacraments is at the same time prohibited from the reality of the
Sacrament, that is, from fellowship with Christ,

[Scan 31; printed page 9; signature B6r]

yet conditionally and only until he repents.

### The kinds of ecclesiastical censure

Enough has now been said about the definition of what is properly called
excommunication. We come next to what Dr. Erastus says about the distribution
of ecclesiastical censure into kinds or degrees, according both to our view and
that of the Schoolmen. He says we establish two degrees of excommunication, so
that some are punished more harshly than others: first, a private exclusion
imposed temporarily by the presbytery alone, during which a person must prove
his life and conduct to the presbyters; and second, a public interdiction
applied to people who despise that earlier censure and do not prove their
repentance to the elders.

I answer that he either does not rightly understand our practice in these
matters or does not explain it plainly enough. First, he confuses
excommunication with ecclesiastical censure, the species with its genus.
Second, he does not enumerate all its kinds. Following both reason itself and
the practice of the entire ancient Church, we instead establish three degrees
of this discipline and of ecclesiastical corrections.

In some cases, according to the nature of the offense, we are content merely to
have warned people of their duty and go no farther. Either the scandal is not
so serious or the people concerned show themselves teachable and voluntarily
repentant. Others seem not to know the Christian religion sufficiently—for
catechesis today follows baptism—or the testimony of their repentance is
insufficiently clear or rightly suspect, or it benefits them or the whole
Church that they be penalized in some way. We do not excommunicate these to the
extent of declaring that they have ceased to be members of Christ. But after
warning them of their duty, we nevertheless order them to abstain temporarily
from the Lord's table, because they are not yet sufficiently and seriously
touched by a sense of their sins, and also so that others may fear. This lasts
until they are instructed, if they are insufficiently acquainted with the
mysteries; their repentance is proved, if they are rightly suspect; or it is
seen that a remedy has been applied to the public scandal. These are those whom
the ancient Church called the Abstainers. In its place we shall show that many
similar people, even under a harsher condition, existed under the law's
pedagogy. Finally, those convicted of the gravest crimes, and especially of
untamed stubbornness, who could be cured by no other means, we publicly

[Scan 32; printed page 10; signature B6v]

excommunicate in God's name. That is, we declare that because of their sins
joined with a rebellious mind they have been cast from the Church's fellowship
in heaven, and on earth through God's servants in his name, until they satisfy
the assembly with adequate testimony of true repentance, since they cannot at
the same time be members of Christ and Belial. We do this not with the harshness
of which some unjustly accuse us, but with such moderation that I remember no
more than two people in this church over an entire decade being excommunicated
in this manner—that is, properly excommunicated. We do not use perpetual
anathema or even exclusion from every public assembly of the Church. This order
is observed in our churches with such success—praise be to God—that we have no
reason to regret it.

### “Greater excommunication” also has a command and example in God's Word

I come to the Schoolmen. Although we have no intention of defending their
corruptions, neither do we think truth should be betrayed here. Dr. Erastus
disapproves of the division of excommunication into lesser, defined solely as
exclusion from the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, and greater, which excludes
from every public sacred act. We have no intention of defending the severity
of the ancient canons in every respect, much less of defending in any way the
papal tyranny, than which nothing is more detestable. Nor will we pursue their
most intricate controversies concerning this business.

But we do say that what is called greater excommunication, although we do not
use it, must not be condemned without qualification, because it rests upon the
express Word of God. It is established that under the law the unclean were
barred even from the entrance of the temple until they were purified. Dr.
Erastus objects that this uncleanness was merely external, but he appears to say
nothing that does not tell against himself, as we hope to show more fully
elsewhere.

**Response I.** One of two things must certainly be admitted: either external
pollution established by evidence was more detestable before God than inward
pollution—which is absurd to imagine—or those polluted by the latter were
regarded as more impure than those polluted by the former, provided a judgment
had intervened.

**Response II.** And what, I ask, was the purpose of those sacrifices for both
voluntary and involuntary sins, if not to be public testimonies of repentance?
The Evangelist's express words make plain that because of schisms and obstinacy,
and therefore serious scandals, people were declared expelled from the
synagogue—that is, barred from all sacred assemblies—

[Scan 33; printed page 11; signature B7r]

and it can plainly be proved that Christ did not condemn this practice itself,
but only its abuse; see John 9:22; 12:42; and 16:2.

**Response III.** Even in private offenses that are not otherwise public,
Christ commands that a person who rebels against the Church's judgment be
regarded as a Gentile and tax collector. The nature of the case therefore seems
to require all the more that he intend the same in public crimes joined with
manifest wickedness. No one can deny that Gentiles, the uninitiated, were
forbidden to enter God's temple, much less to participate in prayers and
sacrifices. Everyone also knows that Jews were not permitted even private
social intercourse with the uncircumcised. Had this been tyrannical or
superstitious, Peter would either never have been accused of it by the Jerusalem
church, or, when charged, would have replied with one word that he had done
nothing unlawful. And surely, if Christ—who otherwise carefully abstained from
Pharisaic traditions—had ever had such intercourse with the uncircumcised,
Peter and the whole Jerusalem church would afterward have had no religious
scruple about doing the same.

It follows that, when Christ commands that stubborn people lawfully declared
such by the Church be regarded as Gentiles, they were also bound by what is
called greater excommunication. No better interpreter of Christ should be
sought than Paul. Speaking of stubborn authors of public scandals in the
Church, he forbids even eating with brothers of this sort in name only. However
one twists himself into every shape, he will never prove with probability, much
less necessity, that people with whom association in private life was judged
unlawful were nevertheless regarded as worthy of love feasts, sacred meals,
and assemblies properly concerned with the confession of faith—unless perhaps
we imagine that when the Apostle commanded the rejection of a heretic, namely
one self-condemned, he excepted public sacred assemblies.

Because certain objections of no little weight are advanced against almost
everything said above, let these points stand until we answer those objections.
For now I say that they show the following: greater excommunication, when
rightly used, is so far from being rashly regarded as a tyrannical or
superstitious invention outside God's Word that, on the contrary, express
examples of lesser excommunication are rare in God's Word while examples of
greater excommunication are many.

### Refutation of arguments against greater excommunication

> It is manifest from 1 Corinthians 14, says Dr. Erastus, that no one should be
> barred from public readings and prayers, but that everyone, even unbelievers,
> should be admitted.

[Scan 34; printed page 12; signature B7v]

**Refutation.** From that passage of Paul we acknowledge that unbelievers were
admitted to hear the Word in the Christian assembly. From several passages in
Luke we infer that they were admitted even in the Jewish synagogues. But if the
council had declared anyone expelled from the synagogue, or the presbytery had
handed him over to Satan, we deny that he was admitted—without accompanying
testimony of repentance—to assemblies instituted not for learning Christianity
but for professing and celebrating it.

The reason unbelievers are admitted but these people are not is plain. The
former sin from ignorance, the latter from wickedness. The former need teachers,
the latter correctors. Knowledge is lacking in the former, conscience in the
latter. The former were therefore to be offered the heavenly food of the Word
which they desired; far be it from denying it to those who seek it. From the
latter, however, the food they disdain because of its abundance is withdrawn
until, once removed, they seek what they now despise.

The Apostle himself teaches us this distinction. He permits association with
unbelievers but not with brothers cast out of the Church for depravity of life
and conduct; see 1 Corinthians 10:27 and 5:11. We have expressly said that this
must be understood only of assemblies belonging to the common confession of
faith. In the ancient Church these included not only love feasts, with which
reception of the Eucharist was joined, but also daily prayers customarily held
at fixed hours. At Jerusalem people assembled at the hour of the morning and
evening sacrifice, and Jews scattered in other places likewise observed fixed
hours of prayer. Why should I not believe that those whom the Church removed
from its midst, as Paul speaks, were formerly and rightly excluded from these
public assemblies?

Public invocation of God is no small part of shared agreement in one faith. I
ask, therefore, who could be admitted to the common profession of faith with
the Church except hypocritically and to his own destruction, whether he openly
dissents from the faith itself or, though verbally confessing Christ, is found
in reality to despise Christ, as the presbytery has lawfully judged, and is
therefore handed over to Satan? The epistle of Jude plainly shows that the
apostles instituted this exclusion from love feasts—much less would disorderly
people of this kind have been admitted to common prayers or the sacred Supper—as
we shall say elsewhere; see Jude 12 and 23 and 2 Timothy 2:25. But I think
differently about simply hearing the Word. Since it is established that by this
instrument the Lord wills both to gather outsiders

[Scan 35; printed page 13; signature B8r]

and bring wanderers back into the path, it follows that everyone ready to hear
it should be admitted, so that even those held captive by Satan may escape his
snares; see Acts 16:13.

Exclusion from the public profession of one and the same faith, and also the
prohibition of ordinary association and commerce in civil life, does not, by
the Apostle's own testimony, dissolve marriages or break once for all the bonds
of human society. Indeed, the Apostle teaches that it does not extend so far as
to relieve us of concern to bring the sinner back into the path or of attending
to every opportunity to warn him, so that he may take account both of himself
and of the Church. We should therefore seek out the very person whose company we
avoid, not from any hatred of the man but from desire for his salvation, until,
conquered at least by shame, he strives to acknowledge himself and be reconciled
to God and his brothers; see 2 Thessalonians 3:15. Thus this censure becomes a
most wholesome medicine. It is indeed sharp, but an otherwise untamable disease
of obstinacy has proved curable only by a remedy of this kind.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument II

> The papists themselves, says Dr. Erastus, have conceded that no one can be
> barred from the communion of mind or Spirit, but that a person marked by
> ecclesiastical censure is prohibited only from the Church's external
> fellowship.

**Refutation I.** If this is true, the empty talkers certainly disagree greatly
among themselves even here, since some leave hardly any hope of salvation to
excommunicated people if they die before making satisfaction. But let us leave
them aside.

**Refutation II.** As for the matter itself, the Church's judgment is nothing
other than the proclamation on earth of a sentence decreed in heaven. As we
previously taught, God's Word supplies the universal premise of that sentence
and the facts confirm its particular application. The proclamation made on
earth is ratified in heaven. It follows that those lawfully cast out of the
Church's communion on earth are declared alienated from the sense of God's
grace.

Yet this expulsion is conditional—unless they repent—with the outcome in God's
hand, and it is temporary—only until they repent. Therefore, as regards the
eternal and immovable firmness of election, or God's good pleasure, people
excommunicated by the Church's external voice who belong to the number of the
elect do not cease to be Christ's members, although for a time they are neither
vigorous nor fruitful. In his own time the sap of Christ, to which they always
adhered, will make them live again. To speak more plainly: those lawfully
excommunicated, if they belong to the elect—a fact eventually declared by a
spirit of true repentance—nevertheless remain

[Scan 36; printed page 14; signature B8v]

grafted into the true vine and are therefore not condemned before God as
regards his purpose. Yet as long as they do not repent they are like branches
which for a time behave as though they did not adhere to the vine: through
their own fault Christ's sap does not nourish them for a time, although they
are not to be cut off and burned. Or, if you prefer, they are indeed members of
Christ, but through atrophy they incline toward decay and would soon be
followed by mortification if that life-giving Spirit of Christ did not revive
them again in his own time.

It ought not seem strange that Christ calls people excluded with respect to
present effect and condition who nevertheless have not been amputated like
branches that were never joined to the vine except in external appearance. For
those who are elect are said to be and truly are eternally elect before God,
although in actual effect they are not elect before they are called and
justified. In the same way a drunkard does not cease to be a rational animal,
but his reason is merely obstructed for a time.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument III

> The Schoolmen torture themselves miserably, says Dr. Erastus, trying to show
> that a priest who absolves someone already absolved by God—that is, someone
> who has truly repented—does anything at all. The doctors do not dare attribute
> more to the keys than the power to restrain someone from the Church's external
> fellowship and reception of the sacraments and to admit him to it again.

**Refutation I.** What is it to us that the Schoolmen torture themselves in this
and many other matters? We must derive wisdom from God's Word. Let us therefore
leave those blind guides of the blind. Our position on the matter itself can be
understood from what precedes. Both the nature of the case and Christ's own
express words declare that what is determined on earth by God's command is
ratified in heaven and that this must be understood in both directions—of those
bound and of those absolved.

When an external sentence of the Church gathered in Christ's name, striking
the bodily ears themselves, is added to the inward judgment of conscience, the
wound must grow in those who are not utterly insensible. Conversely, when God
binds up and entirely heals that wound, not only by the Holy Spirit's inward
testimony, which makes our spirit receive consolation and peace in Christ, but
also by the public and audible acclamation of the whole Church while the angels
themselves rejoice, how greatly do you think a conscience recently collapsing
beneath the burden of sorrow is refreshed? “For an ailing soul, a wise word is
a physician,” he says. I in turn would add, “for a soul recovering its health,”
for the same occurs in diseases of the body:

[Scan 37; printed page 15; signature C1r]

a person who feels himself beginning to recover is greatly confirmed when he
also hears the judgment of skilled physicians and from it receives new strength
against the remnants of the disease.

**Refutation II.** Furthermore, shall we think that the Church's public
rejoicing over the safety of a sheep brought back to the flock—the rejoicing
that follows absolution and is, as it were, its appendix—has no weight with God?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument IV

> If being handed over to Satan meant being cast out of the Church, outside
> which there is no salvation, says Dr. Erastus, the Apostle would not have said
> that he wished to hand the Corinthian over to Satan so that his spirit might
> remain safe in the day of the Lord.

**Refutation.** We acknowledge that “Church” sometimes signifies the elect
alone and sometimes everyone who outwardly professes the name of the true God.
We also acknowledge that through an unjust judgment, whether from ignorance or
malice, people may be received into the assembly of believers who do not belong
to it at all. John says of them, “If they had been of us, they would surely have
remained with us.” People may also be cast out of the same assembly without
ceasing to be living members of Christ, as in our own day has happened to very
many, perhaps more than ever before.

Whichever meaning “Church” bears, however, we deny Erastus's conclusion. A
person can be expelled from the very body of citizens and, for a time, be
deprived of the right of citizenship not merely in appearance but in reality,
yet later be restored because he asks pardon. Nor was he expelled for any
purpose other than that this penalty might bring him to acknowledge his sin
and ask forgiveness.

### Refutation of arguments attacking exclusion from the Lord's Supper

Enough has now been said about the definition and division of excommunication,
partly presented and partly criticized by Dr. Erastus. I have resolved to say no
more at present than is needed to refute what does not agree with our position.
I turn to the second part of his theses. In it he undertakes to prove that the
practice of excluding from participation in the Lord's Supper those who
otherwise retain the same faith as we do but have fallen in life and conduct
has neither a divine command nor a divine example. As was proper, he begins his
proof from the books of the old covenant and directs this first argument
against us.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument I against excommunication

> Scripture excludes no one for any committed sin from sacrifices, whether
> propitiatory or thanksgiving sacrifices, from circumcision, or from eating
> the Passover lamb. On the contrary, it warns and exhorts sinners to come for
> the salvation of themselves and their households

[Scan 38; printed page 16; signature C1v]

> more frequently to the sacrifices and be reconciled to God more frequently.
> They command the whole assembly to celebrate Passover and all males to appear
> before God three times each year. They even invite resident aliens among the
> Israelites to Passover. Finally, they require even the unclean to celebrate
> Passover in another month, specifically on the fourteenth day of the second
> month. If the divine law forbids no one to participate in these sacred rites
> but rather compels them to be present, who can believe that the Jewish council
> ruled differently concerning them?

**Refutation.** To move from one extreme to the other while omitting the middle
is not to descend by steps but to rush over a precipice. Under the law the Lord
called, and now through the sacred ministry still calls, sinners of every kind
to the kingdom of heaven. He invites them both to hear his Word and to use the
sacraments. He even testifies that tax collectors and prostitutes go before
the rest. Elsewhere, however, sinners are said not to be heard by God, and Paul
cries out that the wicked have no share in God's kingdom. Christ himself showed
the slandering Jews how these things do not contradict one another when he said
that he calls sinners to repentance. Sinners are therefore called to the
kingdom of heaven, but on the condition that they first repent and cease to be
sinners.

We answer in the same way. Far from barring them, the Lord compelled judicially
established sinners to sacrifices and sacred assemblies, but under the law that
their repentance be attested. “Yet,” someone will say, “Christ did not command
them to approach the Jewish council to attest this repentance.” No more than he
commanded his apostles to approach their synagogues. We are speaking of a
properly constituted Church, of which Christ said, “Tell it to the Church.”
Yet Christ did not utterly reject the Jewish presbytery when judgment of
leprosy was at issue, for he said, “Show yourself to the priest.”

What does the discipline we now defend contain that is not fully consonant with
that ordinance of God? Whom do we not invite to repentance? Whom whose
repentance is established do we not receive with open arms, much less repel
from the Lord's table? Is not the public testimony of repentance we require the
very thing contained in God's Word? How could anyone offer a sacrifice for sin
without publicly confessing that he had sinned? This cannot be understood only
in general of the confession by which the whole Church and every individual
are bound to profess their guilt before God. Those who offered specifically for
a sin necessarily also acknowledged themselves specifically guilty of the sin
at issue.

[Scan 39; printed page 17; signature C2r]

**Objection I.** Someone may perhaps object here to the practice I previously
said we follow. At times people profess repentance, but because their repentance
is rightly suspect or insufficiently expressed, so that longer testing is
needed, or because a public example appears necessary for the Church's
edification, we do not indeed excommunicate them but nevertheless exclude them
from the outward use of the Supper for a definite time. Dr. Erastus himself
supplies us with the best answer to this objection when he admits that the
unclean were prescribed the space of a month before celebrating Passover,
evidently as a public example, although it is probable that many of them were
otherwise ready to attest repentance much sooner. God willing, we shall discuss
this more fully in its place.

**Objection II.** Someone may also object that not all who were externally
unclean, nor all sinners with respect to God's worship, were referred to the
priests or council to be judged there and barred from the temple and sacred
rites. I readily admit that not everyone of this kind was called before the
council. The priests were generally exceedingly negligent. Some people also
easily concealed their sins, as happens far too often. Others voluntarily
acknowledged that they had sinned or were otherwise polluted and approached the
expiations of their own accord, judging themselves. Finally, not everything
improperly said or done between private persons needed to be revealed and
judged by the elders.

But I maintain that the pastors were bound, so far as they could, to warn every
individual sheep that wandered even slightly, as Ezekiel expressly commands.
They were also required to examine and decide from God's Word both hidden
offenses—first privately and then, if the parties were not reconciled, when the
offenses were reported to them—and, much more, public scandals. That is, they
were to sprinkle with cleansing water those convicted of and compliant
concerning legal uncleanness. To sinners—including those who sinned from
ignorance of law or fact when the scandal was reported and proved—they were to
prescribe the sacrifices for sin described in the laws, accompanied by an
express confession. If the people obeyed, they were reconciled; if not, as
unclean they were barred even from the threshold of the temple.

[Scan 40; printed page 18; signature C2v]

That this order was established is clear from what is written about sinners in
Deuteronomy 17:18 and following, likewise Leviticus 4–6 and Numbers 15, and many
other places concerning legal uncleanness and water of expiation. Christ's
speech in Matthew 5:22 also shows that different sacred judges sat according to
whether offenses against a neighbor were lighter or more serious. Christ was
certainly not writing civil statutes—as I trust every fair judge will concede—
but treating matters that properly and directly concern conscience, although
he accommodated his language to the order divinely established under the law.
Nor can “Church” be understood otherwise in Christ's words in Matthew 18:17,
“Tell it to the Church,” unless we dream that either the whole assembly of the
Church or the civil magistrates had to be convened whenever private offenses of
this kind arose without a more serious public scandal.

Finally, no one should object that this whole action was civil and settled by
civil penalties irrelevant to our present dispute. This is so false that, on
the contrary, when conscience had been injured, public ecclesiastical expiation
with confession of sin before the priests was added even to civil judgments and
penalties imposed by the civil magistrate; see Leviticus 6:2 and following and
Numbers 5:6 and following.

The sum of our response is that all people were called and almost compelled to
God's worship and sacred ceremonies, but in such a way that the repentance of
the unclean had to come first. A particular and public testimony of this
repentance was also required from those known to be polluted by sin or legal
uncleanness. Therefore, not only does nothing follow from this argument against
us, but it powerfully confirms our position.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument II against excommunication

> The priests could not bar anyone from Passover, he says, because only a
> definite number celebrated Passover by households. If one house or family did
> not contain enough people to consume the lamb, they joined others to
> themselves until they seemed sufficient. Otherwise it would have been
> necessary to inspect every family throughout Judea, which was scarcely—indeed
> hardly at all—possible.

**Refutation I.** I answer first that those lambs were not slaughtered and
prepared by just anyone throughout Judea but by the Levites at the house of the
Lord, as is clear from the history of Josiah in 2 Chronicles 35:11. Who can
suppose that the Levites would have mixed themselves with the unclean?

**Refutation II.** Furthermore, I admit that

[Scan 41; printed page 19; signature C3r]

pastors were generally required to care for every individual sheep, much more
for entire flocks, as the Lord says to Ezekiel. Yet this particular care was not
necessary in the Passover celebration. First, a solemn sanctification was
proclaimed. Second, those whose purity was not in dispute were lawfully
regarded as clean. The book of Ezra plainly shows that only clean people were
received to celebrate the Passover meal; see Ezra 6:20. And 2 Chronicles 23:19
expressly says that gatekeepers excluded from the temple those judged unclean
“in anything whatever.”

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument III against excommunication

> We never read, says Dr. Erastus, that the prophets excluded the wicked from
> the temple, ceremonies, and sacrifices.

That is because they did not hold the reins of government, and no one was more
hostile to them than the priests and elders of the people.

**Refutation I.** How often those same prophets reproach the priests for caring
about nothing less than their office! How often they charge the people with the
most serious crimes, by which they pollute themselves and the temple! Do they
not openly indicate what they would have done had they presided over the
administration of the Church?

**Refutation II.** Isaiah in particular says that the hands of the wicked are
full of blood, by which he signifies every crime committed against neighbors,
and adds that all their sacred rites are therefore an abomination. He plainly
applies to inward pollution what had been enacted concerning external
uncleanness and clearly demonstrates what the priests should have done in such
a case. David asks to be cleansed and washed and delivered from blood before he
places young bulls upon the altar. He sufficiently proves that people known to
be polluted by some crime were excluded from the altar unless, after publicly
attesting their repentance—as David does in that psalm—they sought pardon
according to the law's prescription.

Those who were required by so solemn a rite to ask pardon for a homicide whose
perpetrator they did not know—would they have admitted a person confessing
another manifest crime, even if not capital, without testimony of repentance?
And when a person who swore rashly was not admitted to the temple without a sin
offering, what do we think was done to someone who knowingly perjured himself?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument IV against excommunication

> The Pharisees, he says, never barred the Sadducees, those most wicked
> heretics, from the temple or the use of ceremonies, nor did they attempt to do
> so.

**Refutation I.** If we deny this, how will his assertion be proved? Because it
is not written? How many things would we deny if that argument were valid!

[Scan 42; printed page 20; signature C3v]

**Refutation II.** Let us nevertheless grant it, since it is also probable. Why
is it strange that hypocrites who otherwise differed on the most serious
matters mutually fostered one another, when revivals of the same sects do the
very same against us today?

**Refutation III.** Even had the Pharisees especially wished to cast out the
Sadducees, or the Sadducees the Pharisees, under what pretext of right could
they have done so? “Pharisee” and “Sadducee” are not names of any civil or
ecclesiastical power but of sects in whose hands—though not theirs alone—the
chief control of ecclesiastical matters often lay.

Finally, suppose someone in the Christian Church today openly denied with the
Sadducees the substance of souls—to say nothing of confessing their
immortality—and the resurrection of bodies, and even defended these doctrines.
Would we regard such a man, lawfully convicted by the presbytery and refusing
to repent, as a Christian? Surely not. Would Dr. Erastus nevertheless admit him
to the Lord's Supper without testimony of repentance? I think not. He would
rather judge that the magistrate's sword should kill him, just as he claims was
extraordinarily done to Alexander and Hymenaeus. But what if the magistrate
himself were a Sadducee or failed in his duty? Would the presbytery rule about
that ungodly man—would that none like him existed anywhere today—differently
than it ruled about the incestuous Corinthian?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument V against excommunication

> The college of priests and the Jewish leaders judged Christ and the apostles
> worthy to be removed even from life itself. Therefore, if they had possessed
> a command to bar such people from their ceremonies, they undoubtedly would
> not have admitted them. Yet they never excluded Christ or the apostles, such
> as Matthew the tax collector or Paul himself, whom they bitterly hated. They
> did, however, rebuke Christ for eating with tax collectors. Consequently,
> people with whom common food was not taken were not excommunicated.

**Refutation I.** On the contrary, John testifies most explicitly in two places
that the Jews had agreed that anyone who confessed Christ should be expelled
from the synagogue, much less that they would spare Christ himself. Dr. Erastus
indeed denies that being cast out of the synagogue was the very thing we call
excommunication, but in its place we shall examine by what right he does so.

**Refutation II.** Let us grant for now that this had nothing to do with
excommunication. Still, besides the fact that Christ and the apostles rightly
despised those most unjust judgments of the Jews, it is certain that the Jews
were wonderfully restrained both by God's secret power and by fear of the
people from actually carrying out what they had decreed.

[Scan 43; printed page 21; signature C4r]

**Refutation III.** As for Matthew the tax collector, I say that there was more
than one class of tax collectors in Judea. Some were Gentiles, usually Roman
equestrians as in the other provinces of the Roman Empire; others were
circumcised and from Judea itself. The Jews bitterly hated both. For the most
part they were at once exceedingly cruel collectors and moneylenders; and the
Jews, bearing the Roman yoke with great resentment, regarded as bastard Jews
those who drew profit from their brothers' slavery. Hence the revolt of Judas
the Galilean and the question whether it was lawful to pay tribute to Caesar.

They utterly abhorred the Gentile collectors as uncircumcised. At times they
avoided contact with the others more from hatred or Pharisaic superstition than
from law, since the law does not condemn either taxes or tax collectors in
themselves; but fear of the Romans prevented them from going farther. They did
not therefore dare bar Matthew or any other Jewish tax collector from the
temple or sacred assemblies on that account. Luke accordingly introduces a
Jewish tax collector praying, though not sacrificing, and standing far off;
see Luke 18:13. Perhaps he stood in the most distant portion of the court to
which even visiting uncircumcised people who possessed some reverence for the
true God were permitted to approach for prayer, as is clear from 1 Kings 8:41.

These are Dr. Erastus's arguments drawn from the Old Testament. With his leave,
they seem to us scarcely to need refutation. Let us now consider those drawn
from the new covenant.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument VI against excommunication

> John the Baptist, says Dr. Erastus, did not reject the Sadducees and Pharisees
> from his baptism, although he knew their conduct so well that he called them
> a brood of vipers. Therefore no one should be barred from our sacraments
> either, especially the Lord's Supper, unless it is established that Christ
> added this to the ancient institution.

**Refutation I.** I answer that John the Baptist had no cause to reject those
who approached. They came to his baptism acknowledging their sins, as Matthew
3:6 expressly says, and had not been excommunicated by others. John himself had
no power to excommunicate them, even if they had deserved it. John did precisely
what belonged to his office, just as we do according to the measure of our
calling: he exhorted everyone who approached to repent, setting before them
God's exceedingly severe judgment from the law while simultaneously offering
in the now-present Christ the free remission of sins.

[Scan 44; printed page 22; signature C4v]

They too did what was fitting: first they confessed their sins and then sought
the token of their sins' free remission. What bearing has this upon the matter
before us?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument VII against excommunication

Seizing on this very point, Dr. Erastus says:

> Just as we argue from circumcision to baptism in order to prove that infants
> should be baptized, so it will be lawful to argue from the Passover lamb to
> the Lord's Supper. Since Christ mentioned no exclusion when instituting his
> Supper, it follows that no one is to be barred from it.

**Refutation I.** I do not deny that it is lawful, following the Apostle's own
example, to argue both from circumcision to baptism and from Passover to the
Lord's Supper—but not rashly and universally, unless we wish infants to be
baptized or admitted to the Supper only on the eighth day.

**Refutation II.** I further maintain that the Lord's Supper succeeded not only
Passover but the whole Feast of Unleavened Bread and indeed all the ancient
sacrifices.

**Refutation III.** Let us therefore see whether the comparison is valid in the
form Dr. Erastus presents. “Vices were not punished by denial of Passover;
therefore they ought not be punished by denial of the Lord's Supper.” I deny the
premise. It is established that the unclean were prohibited from Passover and
prescribed a month's interval in which they were to be regarded as purified.
The comparison therefore necessarily yields an argument entirely opposed to
Erastus: as the unclean were rejected from Passover, so they are to be rejected
from the Supper.

**Refutation IV.** If people were barred from sacred rites because of external
legal uncleanness that could come upon them even unwillingly, such as pollution
from a corpse, much more were they barred because of pollution contracted
through sin. There will be another place to discuss this. Moses specifically
mentions corpse-uncleanness there because that occasion had led him to inquire
of the Lord's mouth, not to exclude other, more serious pollutions. That would
have been utterly absurd, since elsewhere legal pollutions were expiated merely
by sprinkling cleansing water, while sins required confession and a solemn
sacrifice. The practice of this law under Hezekiah, where pollution of every
kind is treated generally, establishes the point; see 2 Chronicles 30:17.

**Refutation V.** It is not strange that Christ, when instituting the Supper,
gave no command concerning the polluted. Erastus's conclusion follows from
this no more than that no penalties are to be established for idolaters,
perjurers, Sabbath-breakers, and other transgressors because the Lord decreed
none when delivering the law written on the two tables. Moreover, by instituting
the Supper

[Scan 45; printed page 23; signature C5r]

only among his disciples, Christ plainly enough showed that its celebration
belongs only to those who profess to be Christ's disciples. I admit that all
who testify that they are such are generally to be regarded as disciples, even
if in reality they are anything but Christians. But I add, first, that this is
understood of those whose hypocrisy still remains hidden from everyone; and
second, that those who have not been convicted as such and condemned as rebels
through the order God established in the Church are regarded as undiscovered.
Likewise, of old, although all the circumcised were generally bound to perform
the sacred rites, those polluted by external uncleanness and those accused of
a sin and condemned in the council were excluded and admitted only after
confession.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument VIII against excommunication

> The arguments that formerly prevailed, and still prevail, against heretics
> who separate themselves from others because of their vices now also prevail
> against those who exclude and condemn others in the communion of the Lord's
> Supper.

**Refutation I.** We certainly grant both points, since one follows from the
other. If the Cathars and Donatists formerly did wrong—or the Anabaptists in our
own time—when they separated themselves from others because of certain vices
and tore the Church, they also did wrong by barring others from their
assemblies, since the two things entirely cohered.

But what is this against us, who condemn that action today no less than the
ancients did? What does it have in common with excommunication: a just and
lawful judgment of the presbytery proclaimed before the Church, by which a
person otherwise incurable and likely to contaminate the whole lump is driven
from the sacred token of union between Christ and the Church for this purpose,
that he may afterward truly be restored? What, I ask, does this judgment, by
which the Church is purified and a rotten member is healed unless he prevents
it, have in common with a voluntary and therefore seditious withdrawal, which
opposes charity and tears the Church, Christ's body? This argument compares
utterly dissimilar things and wrongly says against the light what is rightly
said against darkness. Otherwise the whole ancient Church must be convicted of
madness, for it simultaneously condemned the Donatists and used lawful
excommunication.

**Refutation II.** Why say more? Although no one separates himself from another
without the other also being separated from him, there is often a great
difference between the two acts. Sometimes both parties sin; sometimes only
one.

[Scan 46; printed page 24; signature C5v]

Thus in marriage, if the believer voluntarily separates from the unbeliever and
the unbeliever from the believer, both sin and neither will be free. But if an
unbeliever deserts an unwilling believer who would rather gain to Christ the
very person who deserts him and strives to do so, the believer is not to be
regarded as the deserter but the deserted and, being free from blame, will be
free according to the Apostle's teaching; see 1 Corinthians 7:15.

Likewise, a person who avoids the company of wicked people, whom he would prefer
to be good so that he might associate with them, is not to be condemned. He
does not bar others from himself but, fearing infection, does not join himself
to them, because they first separated themselves from him by their wickedness
and refused to remove the cause of that separation. In the same way sins create
a division between God and us, but we are deserters before we are deserted.
Thus stubborn excommunicated people have no reason to complain of the
presbytery's judgment. By their sins they first separated themselves from the
Church and refuse to obey those striving to bring them back. Thus Paul separated
obedient disciples from those who stubbornly resisted; see Acts 19:9. Thus he
wishes the troublers of the Galatian churches to be cut off by the sword of the
Spirit, surely for salvation unless they prevent it; see Galatians 5:12. Shall
we say Paul sinned, as the Anabaptists who tear the Church do?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument IX against excommunication

> Christ entered the same temple with his disciples, says Dr. Erastus, and used
> the same sacraments as the Pharisees and greatest scoundrels. He was baptized
> by John with the same baptism as they and never excluded them from the
> sacraments.

**Refutation I.** Quite right. Who had prohibited them from the sacred rites?

**Refutation II.** But this separation differs from the preceding. The former
is right: not without groaning the godly separate themselves from those who
have first separated themselves from God and the brothers and cannot otherwise
be joined to them again. But the separation by which some voluntarily abstain
from sacred assemblies and the proper use of the sacraments because of the
vices of others—that is, excommunicate themselves—deserves severe rebuke.

**Refutation III.** First, what happened to that Pharisee often happens to
them: the judge is worse than the person he judges. Even if someone is as
wicked as he is thought to be, what appearance will the Church soon have if
everyone assumes such a judgment for himself?

**Refutation IV.** We must further hold in this matter that one person's evil
conscience does not pollute another's upright conscience. Although evils must
never be done so that good may result, charity nevertheless sometimes commands
that, so far as evil is not indulged,

[Scan 47; printed page 25; signature C6r]

many things be tolerated. Augustine discusses this abundantly against the
Donatists in several places, but especially in the very work Erastus cites, the
third book against Parmenian.

**Refutation V.** As I just said, none of this is against us. It should also be
remembered that when Christ used his supreme power extraordinarily and cast
the sellers and buyers from the temple, he plainly indicated both what he would
have done had the heavenly Father willed him to exercise the Levitical office
and what the priests ought to have done according to the law's prescription.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument X against excommunication

> You except Christ, says Dr. Erastus, as at least having authority over his
> own. Yet he did not bar from the sacraments Judas, a manifest thief and
> traitor who had made an agreement with the priests and whose treachery he
> revealed when the disciples asked, as many suppose. Even if Judas did not
> participate in the Supper, as some maintain, it is at least established that
> he was not prohibited from eating the lamb. It is also established that
> Christ sent Judas to proclaim salvation, gave him authority over unclean
> spirits, and admitted him to all his deliberations and actions, even though
> Christ knew perfectly well who he was and what he did. And who denies, he
> says, that admitting an evil man to the Supper is less than entrusting him
> with the ministry?

**Refutation I.** We are compelled to criticize many things in this sequence of
argument. First, even the most learned dispute the argument's foundation:
whether Judas participated in the Supper at which it was instituted. Since John
writes that Judas left before the meal was concluded, I agree with those who
think Judas departed before the institution of the Supper.

**Refutation II.** Second, the assertion that the crime of treachery was not
hidden because Judas had already agreed upon it with the priests carries no
weight. The very name “treachery” shows that it was a conspiracy known to very
few and especially unknown to Christ's household, as the historical narrative
makes clear.

**Refutation III.** The added claim that Christ revealed it at the Supper is no
stronger. He revealed it to John alone, who did not then disclose it to Peter
or anyone else. This is clear because Scripture expressly says that none of the
others reclining at table knew why Jesus said to Judas as he departed, “What
you do, do quickly.” And what did Christ reveal to the apostles? Not something
already done, but something future: “One of you,” he said, “will betray me.” To
what end did he disclose this? Not so that Judas's crime should then become
known,

[Scan 48; printed page 26; signature C6v]

but so that after the event had taken place, the memory of these words might
confirm the disciples' faith. “I tell you now,” he says, “before it happens, so
that when it has happened you may believe that I am he”; see John 13:19.

Next, as for Judas's theft and perhaps his other crimes, how is it inferred
that the disciples knew them? John's calling him a thief is not to be understood
as though the disciples had ascertained this at that time. Rather, the event
itself afterward disclosed the motive with which Judas had spoken about the
ointment poured out and about care for the poor.

**Refutation IV.** Matthew says that the other disciples were also indignant,
but undoubtedly with a different motive from Judas, though still a misguided
one. These things were certainly well known to Christ: known to him as God and
the knower of hearts, and revealed to him insofar as he was man. But what
bearing does this have on ecclesiastical ministry? That ministry renders such
a judgment as the one now under discussion only about matters established by
ordinary human means, not by extraordinary revelation, and joined with an
offense to the Church. Indeed, even if a minister sees with his own eyes someone
doing something that merits exclusion from the Supper, as a matter of law he
neither ought nor is able to prohibit him authoritatively from the Lord's table
unless the person has been summoned, convicted, and finally lawfully condemned
according to the order established in the Church. Are we surprised, then, that
Christ refused to do this when the crime was still hidden from human beings and
had not yet even been committed—Christ who, I say, did not assume the office of
the still-operative Mosaic discipline?

**Refutation V.** Moreover, charity commands that even manifest sinners first
be instructed, admonished, rebuked, and finally awaited with the greatest
patience rather than excommunicated. Even if we granted that Judas's crimes
were public and that the Levites' office had also been imposed upon Christ,
what else did Christ do by drawing Judas to repentance so patiently than what
we ourselves would do today, while nevertheless preserving ecclesiastical
authority, until a person showed himself incurable in fact?

**Refutation VI.** Nor is Erastus's other insistence relevant, namely, that “it
is a greater thing to admit an evil person to the ministry than to the Supper.”
Would Erastus himself think that someone known to resemble Judas should be
admitted to the ministry, contrary to the Apostle, who requires ministers to
be blameless? Would we tolerate having a thief as steward of our household,
whether known to be one in fact or justly suspected? Christ therefore performed
both acts, not as a minister following the prescribed rule of elec-

[Scan 49; printed page 27; signature C7r]

tion, but as the Son complying with his Father's will so that what the prophets
had foretold of him might be fulfilled. How can these things, then, be used to
subvert the Church's order?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument XI against excommunication

> Christ, says Dr. Erastus, commanded everyone to eat that bread and drink from
> that cup. Therefore he wishes to exclude no one who professes to be his
> disciple.

**Refutation I.** We grant this, so much so that, as I said before, we number
even hypocrites among the disciples as long as they remain entirely concealed
or have not been convicted and condemned by public authority. But suppose a
person has been convicted by lawful judgment of denying Christ by his deeds,
refuses to hear the Church, and after being condemned persists in corrupting
himself and offending the Church. If he nevertheless does not fear to eat the
Lord's bread without evidence of repentance, on what grounds will he be
reckoned among the disciples? We deny that demanding the Lord's Supper and
professing with the voice alone the death of Christ, which the person persists
in profaning by his conduct, constitute sufficiently suitable evidence of
repentance in those who refuse to hear the Church, who may perhaps have deceived
her more than once, and who confess Christ in word but deny him by deeds.

**Refutation II.** Furthermore, who would think that when Christ instituted the
Supper—the token of our mystical incorporation with him—he wished anyone to
approach his table otherwise than worthily? Thus, while inviting those who
approach worthily and yet commanding all to eat, he certainly did not command
the unworthy to approach just as they were. Rather, he exhorted them to prepare
themselves for that feast by repenting, giving sufficient warning, as the
Apostle later interpreted him, that they must not approach in any other
condition. And what else does the presbytery do when, by barring the stubborn
from access lest they destroy themselves and corrupt others, it prepares the
very people whom it excommunicates for worthy access?

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument XII against excommunication

> Yet when the Apostle treats those who considered it no violation of religion
> to eat food sacrificed to idols in an idol's temple, says Dr. Erastus, he
> does not bar them from the Supper. First he rebukes them for paying greater
> regard to their liberty than to charity toward their neighbors. Then he
> presses the matter even more strongly, showing that those who approached the
> table of demons became associates of demons.

**Refutation I.** On the contrary, what could the Apostle have said more
plainly? “You cannot,” he says, “drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of
demons. You cannot partake of the Lord's table and the table of demons.” Why,
I ask, did he name the cup rather than the blood and the Lord's table rather
than the body, unless he meant to join the signs with the things themselves—that
is, the outward action of the Supper with its fruit? He could also have written
here,

[Scan 50; printed page 28; signature C7v]

as he does elsewhere, “What fellowship has Christ with Belial? What fellowship
has light with darkness, or a believer with an unbeliever?” Paul therefore
plainly signifies that those who participate in the sacred rites of idols
cannot approach the Lord's Supper.

**Refutation II.** Someone may object that idolaters can nevertheless
participate in the outward action without inwardly participating in Christ. We
answer that the Apostle is not discussing what someone can do contrary to right
and divine law, but only what can be done lawfully and with a good conscience.
Thus “You cannot” declares precisely the same as “You cannot do so without
offending God,” or “It is unlawful for you.”

**Refutation III.** Who should be surprised that the Apostle writes this? He
says that one ought not even take a private meal with so-called brothers who
have been condemned not only for idolatry but for much lighter crimes and
therefore cast out of the Church.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument XIII against excommunication

> The same Apostle, says Dr. Erastus, speaking about the fathers, says that
> they all ate the same spiritual food and drank the same spiritual drink. Yet
> among them were idolaters, fornicators, tempters, and murmurers whom the Lord
> afterward struck down in the wilderness. They were therefore not barred from
> the sacraments but restrained by bodily punishments.

Dr. Erastus then applies to us those other words of the Apostle, “We all eat
from one bread and drink from the same cup,” and argues:

> Therefore not even such people are to be barred from the sacraments; rather,
> they should be punished by the magistrate. When the magistrate failed to do
> so, the Apostle indicates that many at Corinth fell asleep—that is, were
> removed from this life by the Lord—because they approached the Supper
> unworthily. Yet he mentions no one excluded from the Supper, nor does he
> admonish that anyone ought to have been excluded.

**Refutation I.** I answer first that there was a peculiar reason why everyone,
even the most unworthy, ate the manna and drank from the water of the rock—a
reason that applies neither to the ancients' sacrifices nor to our sacraments.
Those things were spiritual in the sense that they were types of spiritual
things and indeed of Christ himself, while at the same time they were bodily
benefits intended to feed the whole people physically. They are therefore not
to be numbered among things that were sacraments only. Thus no distinction
could or ought to have been maintained there between the good and the evil or
the clean and the unclean. It is established that such a distinction was
maintained in the sacrifices, which were instituted properly not to feed bodies
but to nourish faith and make a common confession. This is true even of the
sacrifices in which the largest portion fell to the offerers for a sacred
feast, not to speak of whole burnt offerings. The comparison is therefore
incongruous, far less should our sacraments have been compared with those
provisions.

**Refutation II.** But let me pass over that distinction, important as it is.

[Scan 51; printed page 29; signature C8r]

Even if many wicked people formerly belonged to those fed by these sacraments,
just as far too many still belong today to those who approach the Lord's table,
does it follow that excommunication had no use? Certainly no more than it would
follow that the civil sword has no lawful use because very many people live
unpunished throughout the cities, covered with every kind of crime; or that no
offering for sin was required under the law because very many approached the
altar with hands full of blood.

**Refutation III.** Moreover, not all evil people are known; many are
hypocrites; and there is great negligence among pastors. Nor should everyone
who is evil be immediately cast out of the Church, since one must begin with
milder remedies. Sometimes too, as Augustine judges, necessity compels us to
tolerate an evil that cannot be corrected more severely without the remedy
becoming worse than the evil itself.

**Refutation IV.** Finally, however diligently you cleanse the Lord's field,
the darnel nevertheless grows from day to day, and the larger party very often
overcomes the better one even in the judgments themselves. But who could
rightly infer from this that one should cease performing one's duty?

> But the Lord punished those people with bodily punishment, Erastus says.

**Refutation V.** We grant this. But because the Lord, either by himself or
through magistrates, punished and continues to punish contempt of himself with
bodily penalties whenever he pleased, does it therefore follow that there was
then, and now is, no place for spiritual censures? Through such censures sinners
are brought to godly sorrow, repentance, and finally salvation, whether bodily
penalties are absent or employed, and whether they are inflicted directly by
God alone or through the magistrate.

> But the Apostle says that no one was restrained by exclusion from the
> sacraments, nor does he admonish that anyone should be so restrained.

**Refutation VI.** As though everything had to be treated in every passage! As
though in the same epistle he had not expressly taught true Christians how to
deal with brothers convicted of denying by deeds what they confessed by mouth,
and what authority the Church has over those guilty of more serious crimes!

**Refutation VII.** Finally, one must also observe that Paul does not accuse
any of the Corinthians there of being guilty of those crimes. He only warns
them, by setting the ancients' example before them, lest they become such
people. Had Dr. Erastus noticed this single fact, he might perhaps have passed
over this argument.

> Yet there were factious people at Corinth, says Dr. Erastus, and there were
> drunkards, but Paul does not mention their exclusion.

**Refutation VIII.** Of course, because while absent he considered that they
could be recalled to their duty by exhortations.

**Refutation IX.** Meanwhile,

[Scan 52; printed page 30; signature C8v]

does he conceal what he will do if they do not repent? “What do you wish?” he
says. “Shall I come to you with a rod, or with charity and a spirit of
gentleness?” The printed marginal citation is 1 Corinthians 5:1, though the
quotation is 1 Corinthians 4:21. The Apostle himself best explains what this
rod is: he commands the Romans to turn away from those who cause divisions and
offenses; see Romans 16:17. Elsewhere he expressly excludes the stubborn—the
“disorderly”—even from common association; see 2 Thessalonians 3:14.

#### Dr. Erastus's Argument XIV against excommunication

> The Apostle commands each person to examine himself and then eat of that
> bread. Therefore, since one who eats unworthily eats judgment not for others
> but for himself, each person ought to scrutinize his own faith and life and
> not abstain from the Supper by another person's judgment.

**Refutation I.** Certainly, because each person is commanded to examine
himself, does it follow that examination by another is excluded when there is
reason to require it? If so, what will finally remain secure either in civil
affairs or in the constitution of the Church? Every good citizen ought carefully
to examine within himself his disposition and abilities if he is called to
some dignity or office. Should an examination therefore be rejected? Each
person ought to consult his conscience as to whether he has lived uprightly.
Are judges therefore not obliged to investigate the conduct of criminals?

No one ought to labor harder at anything than at testing and examining himself
to see whether he is in the faith. The printed marginal citation is 1
Corinthians 13:6, though the passage is 2 Corinthians 13:5. Does it therefore
follow that pastors and presbyters have no role in testing catechumens or
instructing the unlearned? I add the point most relevant to the matter: does it
follow that they have no role in testing the repentance of those who refuse to
hear the Church? The Apostle certainly teaches something very different. He
exhorts the Corinthians to examine themselves, but nevertheless announces that
when present he will use against them the severity and power received from the
Lord unless, of course, he finds them repentant; see 2 Corinthians 13:10. That
certainly could not be done without examining the accused.

Indeed, who needs diligent and exact self-examination more than those who must
think seriously about returning to God's Church and proving their repentance
to the assembly of the brothers and even to the angels themselves? Private
self-examination is so far from preventing the presbytery, or sometimes even
the whole Church, from being assured of the repentance of those convicted that
under the priests both confession of sin and an offering were required for
absolution.

**Refutation II.** No one will be surprised that the Apostle directs everyone
to self-examination without mentioning examination by another if he considers
that this epistle was addressed not to certain excommunicated persons, nor to

[Scan 53; printed page 31; signature D1r — continuation only]

catechumens, but to the Corinthian Church itself. The Apostle was therefore
content in this passage with a general admonition applying universally to all,
since all who profess Christianity are rightly presumed to be true Christians
unless someone has been denounced and convicted of being other than he wishes
to appear.

**Refutation III.** Why say more? If the prescribed self-examination of each
person is sufficient, by what right, on the day before the Lord's Supper in the
German churches, are all who intend to approach the Supper commanded to go to
their pastor and answer when questioned both about their faith and about their
repentance? And how can those who do this complain about our severity, when they
require evidence of repentance from everyone who will approach the Lord's
Supper, while we require it only from those who have been denounced and
lawfully convicted?
