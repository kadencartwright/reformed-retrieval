## Book III. On Confirmation

### Chapter I

Confirmation among the papists is an anointing with chrism on the forehead,
made with the sign of the cross, under this formula of words: “I sign you with
the sign of the cross, and confirm you with the chrism of salvation, in the
name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.” Filincius, *Treatise on
Confirmation*. The first question concerning it, therefore, is whether it is
a true sacrament. The papists affirm that it is; we deny it.

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. Three things are required for the essence of a Christian sacrament
properly so called: 1. A promise of grace. 2. A perceptible sign, together with
a word, which is the means or instrument by which the promise is applied. 3. A
divine command by which its administration is enjoined.

Protestant reply. These things are indeed truly stated by our theologians, but
they are put forward sophistically and ambiguously by Bellarmine. For: 1. The
promise of any grace gratuitously given does not suffice for a sacrament, but
the promise must be of the grace of the saving covenant, of justification,
sanctification, and so forth. 2. The promise of grace is required

[1632 witness | printed p. 60]

to be in some way the effect of the sacrament. Bellarmine, *On the Sacraments*,
Book 1, chapter 14. 3. A perceptible sign, together with a word, must be
definitely determined by God. Bellarmine, *On the Sacraments*, Book 1, chapter
21. 4. The divine command must be not temporary but perpetual—that is, it must
last as long as the religion itself lasts. Bellarmine, *On the Sacraments*,
Book 1, chapter 14. But none of these things belongs to the papists'
Confirmation.

2. Bellarmine. There is indeed a promise, and certainly a most ample one: of
the Spirit the Comforter, John 14; of the Spirit strengthening, John 16; and
of the Spirit making people strong in the confession of the faith, the last
chapter of Luke and Acts 1.

Protestant reply. 1. There is indeed a most ample promise, but it is attached
to no sign. It therefore no more constitutes any particular sacrament than do
the other promises found in the Gospel, which are certainly far more numerous
than the sacraments. 2. According to the papists' position, what is promised to
the apostles in those passages is not the grace of ordinary confirmation, but
that grace which makes people incapable of sinning. Thus Medina, on part 3,
question 27, article 4: “There are three degrees of confirmation: 1. That of
the blessed. 2. That of the Blessed Virgin. 3. That of the apostles, who, after
receiving the grace of the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost, were so
confirmed in goodness that afterward they never sinned mortally.” 3. That
grace of confirmation which the papists attach to the sacrament can both be
promised and bestowed without a sacrament. Thus Alexander of Hales, part 4,
question 9, member 1: “In Matthew 19 the Lord conferred the grace of
confirmation on the little children, though not by sacramental ministry.”

3. Bellarmine. The means or instrument, or perceptible sign, consisting of
things and words by which that promise was applied, was the laying on of a
hand with prayer.

Protestant reply. 1. Neither of these was determined by Christ. Alexander of
Hales takes this as so fully admitted in the place cited that he concludes
from it that the Lord neither instituted nor administered this sacrament as a
sacrament. 2. Bellarmine himself concedes that on the day of Pentecost the
apostles received the promised grace without any means or sacrament. That
promise discussed above was therefore not sacramental, nor was a particular
sacrament necessary for obtaining grace of this kind.

Bellarmine. The others received that same grace through the ministry of the
apostles, by that means.

Protestant reply. 1. It does not appear from Acts 2 that the apostles alone
received the grace of the Spirit on the day of Pentecost. 2. It is clear that
others received the same grace before they were either confirmed or baptized,
Acts 10:44. 3. Alexander of Hales in the place cited and Bonaventure on Book 4,
distinction 7, affirm that the apostles employed neither the matter nor the
form of this sacrament, but confirmed others without the admini—

[1632 witness | printed p. 61]

—stration of the sacrament. 4. Prayer is no more the form of one sacrament
than of another. Moreover, the laying on of hands was a gesture of prayer that
was formerly customarily employed in solemn prayer for others, even outside
the sacraments. 5. The grace of holiness and righteousness obtained by this
means was not the grace which Bellarmine requires to be conferred in a
sacrament. Rather, it was grace gratuitously given in certain extraordinary
gifts, which someone can use not only well but also badly, 1 Corinthians 14.
6. Those gifts were not given to all baptized men, Romans 12 and 1 Corinthians
12, much less to women and infants. 7. The matter and form of the papists'
Confirmation are not the laying on of hands with prayer, but the cross, chrism,
and a formula signifying that their use and authority are handed down.

4. Bellarmine. In place of a command we offer the apostles' execution of that
command.

Protestant reply. 1. According to Bellarmine, this consequence has no force:
the apostles did this or that; therefore, they had a command concerning it.
Indeed, he asserts that the apostles wrote canonical books without a command
concerning the word of God, Book 4, chapter 4. 2. With us, the consequence has
force concerning the apostles and what they did, but not concerning others,
or concerning what the apostles themselves did not do, or what they did not do
according to a common rule.

5. Bellarmine. By the laying on of the apostles' hands, not only gifts of
speaking in tongues and similar gifts were given, for such gifts are not
simply called the Holy Spirit. Rather, through the laying on of hands the Holy
Spirit himself was given, Acts 8.

Protestant reply. 1. Such gifts are expressly called distributions of the Holy
Spirit in Hebrews 2:4, and the reason is given in 1 Corinthians 12:10. 2.
Cajetan replies on Acts 8: “Without doubt they received the Holy Spirit in a
perceptible effect. Through Baptism they had received him in the effects of
inward grace alone. But the statements that follow testify that they received
the Holy Spirit himself in a perceptible effect—namely, in speaking in
tongues—for Simon, who had been a magician, saw that the Holy Spirit was given
through the laying on of hands.”

6. Bellarmine. What the Lord had promised in John 14, 15, and 16 was given
through the laying on of hands.

Protestant reply. Not all of it, as is plainly evident in the apostles, who
received a special grace without the laying on of hands.

7. Bellarmine. The Holy Spirit was given to everyone through the laying on of
hands.

Protestant reply. This cannot be proved. The contrary is clear from the fact
that he was given only through the apostles' hands, and they could not be
present with everyone who had been baptized.

8. Bellarmine. In Hebrews 6 the apostle numbers the laying on of hands together
with Baptism, and so forth.

[1632 witness | printed p. 62]

Protestant reply. Cajetan gives this reason: “Because in the primitive church
that laying on of hands also belonged to the first foundations of faith.” But
it is more likely that the entire ministry is there understood through the
laying on of hands.

9. Bellarmine. The apostles would never have gone down to Samaria to confer
those miraculous gifts, for Philip was performing many miracles quite
sufficiently.

Protestant reply. It was nevertheless necessary for others besides Philip to
be furnished with gifts for building up the church, and the apostles went down
for that purpose.

10. Bellarmine. The efficacy of this laying on of hands was not a unique
privilege in the apostles, because no such command and promise is read to have
been made particularly to them. Rather, the promise was made to the whole
church, and its rationale has no less place among us than among the apostles.

Protestant reply. 1. Scripture indicates clearly enough in Acts 8 that it was
a privilege. But extraordinary privileges granted to the apostles do not
require a particular command and promise to exist in Scripture. 2. The
promise of that grace which depended on the laying on of the apostles' hands
is not general, nor does its cause have a place among those to whom the Gospel
has been sufficiently attested, Hebrews 2:4. It is also sufficiently clear
that the effect which followed the laying on of hands at that time has ceased.

Bellarmine. It has ceased with respect to the visible symbols, not with
respect to invisible grace.

Protestant reply. 1. It has therefore ceased with respect to the laying on of
hands, which was a visible symbol. 2. Every invisible grace necessary for
salvation is sealed in Baptism and the Lord's Supper. The papists'
Confirmation therefore has no use or fruit, either visible or invisible.

### Chapter II. The second question, on the matter and form of the sacrament of Confirmation

Bellarmine 1. The remote matter of this sacrament is oil mixed with balsam and
consecrated by a bishop. Its proximate matter is an anointing with that oil,
applied to the forehead in the form or figure of a cross.

Protestant reply. 1. This matter is so remote from the sacraments of the New
Testament that it is nowhere mentioned in the entire New Testament.

Bellarmine 1. Either this anointing is expressly mentioned in 2 Corinthians
1:21 and 1 John 2:27, or it is certainly alluded to there.

Protestant reply. Rather, those passages expressly discuss spiritual
anointing, which alludes to the anointings of the Old Testament. The anointing
of Christ himself was also of this kind, yet all the scholastics

[1632 witness | printed p. 63]

deny that he received the sacrament of Confirmation.

Bellarmine 2. The ointment of the Old Testament in Exodus 30 was a figure of
chrism.

Protestant reply. 1. Alexander of Hales replies in part 4, question 3, member
2, article 3: “Confirmation and extreme unction had no corresponding
sacraments in the Law.”

Protestant reply. In Acts 8 and 19, where the papists wish examples of
Confirmation to be set forth, there is no mention of chrism but only of the
laying on of hands. Earlier, in chapter 2, Bellarmine also wished the
perceptible sign in the sacrament of Confirmation to be the laying on of
hands.

Bellarmine 1. Certain scholastics say that the apostles administered this
sacrament in varying ways, and this is not wholly improbable.

Protestant reply. Others also, such as Alexander of Hales and Bonaventure, say
that the apostles did not administer this sacrament in any way, and this is
wholly probable. For the Jesuits themselves deny that the simple laying on of
hands employed by the apostles was a sacrament, because it was not a permanent
ceremony. Filincius, *Treatise on Confirmation*; Suárez, on part 3,
disputation 33.

Bellarmine 2. Anointing with chrism and the laying on of a hand are the same
thing, for one who anoints lays on a hand, and the apostles always marked
people with chrism when laying on hands.

Protestant reply. 1. Among the papists, the anointing or smearing with chrism
is performed only with the thumb on the forehead. Thus far, therefore, no
laying on of hands appears, but only the application of one thumb. Later,
indeed, as Bellarmine recounts in chapter 13, prayers are said with the laying
on of a hand. But in the same place Bellarmine expressly testifies that this
is in addition to the essential ceremony of anointing, denying what he says
here. The bishop then strikes the face with a slap or blow using his whole
hand. “He strikes lightly with the hand,” Bellarmine says in the same place.
Yet here too no laying on but an application of a hand appears. 2. It would
seem exceedingly strange if the Scriptures commended to us a sacrament whose
matter they never designated by its own name. But Bellarmine preferred to be
boldly ridiculous in perverting Scripture rather than to imitate the candor
of Alexander of Hales, who argues as follows: “When the Lord instituted any
sacrament, he determined by which element or thing composed from an element
it ought to be performed. But we do not read that the Lord determined anything
about the material in which the sacrament of Confirmation ought to be
performed. The apostles also confirmed by the laying on of hands alone,
without anointing and without the formula of words which the church uses.” He
finally concludes: “This sacrament was instituted at the Council of Meaux with
respect to the formula of words and the material elements.” Part 4, question
9, member 1.

2. Bellarmine. The form of this sacrament consists of these words: “I sign you
with the sign of the cross and confirm you with the chrism of salvation, in
the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit,” according to the sense
and meaning they possess.

[1632 witness | printed p. 64]

Protestant reply. They have the form which pleased its fashioner, perhaps the
Council of Meaux, as Alexander of Hales affirms. But since nowhere in the whole
of Scripture is there any mention either of this form or of the sign of the
cross and chrism of which it consists, it is sufficiently evident that this
sacrament was unknown to the apostles.

Bellarmine 1. In a general way we have a word in Acts 8, where the apostles
are said to have prayed.

Protestant reply. 1. From that general reference someone would more probably
infer that the Lord's Prayer is the form of this sacrament.

Bellarmine 2. In 2 Corinthians 1:21 Paul expressed the principal words: “The
one who confirms us is God and has sealed us.”

Protestant reply. In sound and the number of letters, not in sense. Bellarmine
alone appears to have dreamed that Paul was confirmed by the sign of the
cross, chrism, or a similar sacrament. Yet, awakening a little, in chapter 12
he teaches the contrary: “It is very probable that Paul, like the other
apostles, received confirmation immediately from God, without a human laying
on of hands.”

### Chapter III. The third question, on the effect of the sacrament of Confirmation

Bellarmine. The effect of this sacrament is twofold: 1. It confers grace that
makes one pleasing, and indeed greater grace than Baptism itself with respect
to strengthening the soul against the Devil's assaults, but lesser grace with
respect to the forgiveness of sin, because it does not remit the whole
penalty. 2. The other effect is a character by which we are enrolled in
Christ's army, just as through Baptism we are enrolled in Christ's household.

Protestant reply. 1. Since, according to the papists' position, every fault
and penalty is remitted in Baptism, and grace that makes one pleasing is
conferred, Confirmation—at least for those recently baptized before they have
committed new sins—cannot remit any fault or penalty. Nor can it confer on one
who is not pleasing the grace that makes one pleasing, by which a person is
reconciled to God, because the justified are pleasing to God. Finally, it
cannot confer on one who is already pleasing grace that makes him more
pleasing, because in Baptism Christ is put on, and he makes us supremely
pleasing to God. Bellarmine therefore stated his position about the grace
conferred through Confirmation ambiguously and very sophistically: that it is
grace making one pleasing, lesser than baptismal grace with respect to the
forgiveness of sin, and not remitting the whole penalty. 2. Confirmation is
not required to strengthen the soul, because the Eucharist was instituted to
strengthen the soul, just as bread strengthens the human heart. Nor is a
baptized person capable of any other strengthening than he has in first
regenera—

[1632 witness | printed p. 65]

—tion before he is also capable of the Eucharist. 3. Alexander of Hales
rightly observes in part 4, question 9, member 3, article 2, section 2: “For
baptismal grace to be strengthened and advance toward the goal of its
perfection, the sacramental grace of Confirmation is not necessarily required.
For baptismal grace itself, with the help of unfailing power—that is, divine
power—is, as far as lies in itself, sufficient in some way for its own
strengthening and fortification.” 4. The exaltation of the grace of
Confirmation above the grace of Baptism with respect to strengthening is not
only impious in itself, but is plainly ridiculous in Bellarmine, who teaches
that every penalty, and thus every weakness, is removed in Baptism. 5. The
distinction between enrollment in Christ's household and in Christ's army is
merely verbal. For all who are enrolled in Christ's household are by that very
fact enrolled in that army, which is the common duty of all who belong to the
household of faith.

### Chapter IV. The fourth question, on the minister of this sacrament

Bellarmine. Only a bishop is the proper minister of this sacrament, but a
presbyter can confer this sacrament by dispensation.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine ought to have explained to us whether this is
proper to a bishop by virtue of order or by virtue of jurisdiction. The power
of order is the same in bishops and presbyters. Nor can it be said to be only
incipient in a presbyter with respect to this sacrament, as Bellarmine later
most absurdly imagines, but absolute, perfect, and independent with respect to
Baptism and the Eucharist, which are evidently greater. Nor is there any
greater jurisdiction in confirming than in baptizing. 2. He ought to have
given a reason why administering Confirmation would not belong to a presbyter
just as much as administering Baptism and the Eucharist. 3. He also ought to
have shown from the word of God that the right to dispense concerning offices
of this kind has been granted to human beings, and to which ones. Since these
things cannot be established in any way, what Bellarmine confusedly affirms
appears false in itself.

2. Bellarmine. In Acts 8 Peter and John are sent to give the Holy Spirit,
through the laying on of hands, to those whom Philip had baptized.

Protestant reply. They were not sent to mark with the sign of the cross, to
anoint with chrism, to recite the formula of words, or to administer any
ordinary sacrament, but to perform the miraculous baptism of breath or of the
Spirit. Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 6, member 7.

3. Bellarmine. Confirmation is the completion and perfection of Bap—

[1632 witness | printed p. 66]

—tism. It is therefore fitting for it to be given by the primary minister, for
in all other things the final form is imprinted by the primary agent.

Protestant reply. This argument is compounded from two blasphemies: 1. That
Baptism is imperfect without Confirmation, as if this sacrament had been
devised to remedy the imperfection of Baptism and to strengthen its weakness.
2. That in imprinting the final form, or the grace of the sacrament, a bishop
is not only the proper agent but also the principal and primary agent.

4. Bellarmine. In Confirmation we are enrolled in Christ's army, and
enrollment in an army is the proper work of commanders and emperors.

Protestant reply. 1. In Baptism we are enrolled in Christ's army. From Baptism
the apostle certainly concludes that all Christians ought to present their
members to God in Christ as weapons of righteousness, Romans 6:13. 2. Even the
lowest officers can instrumentally enroll people in an army. 3. Those who turn
bishops into spiritual commanders and emperors place human beings on Christ's
throne.

### Chapter V. Question 5, on the ceremonies of Confirmation

Bellarmine. 1. In the consecration of chrism: 1. Blessings of the oil and
balsam are performed through prayers. 2. They are also performed through the
sign of the cross. 3. The bishop breathes several times over the vessel of
chrism. 4. The chrism, once consecrated, is greeted by the bishop and
presbyters with these words: “Hail, holy chrism.”

Protestant reply. 1. In all these things, apart from the blessings consisting
of prayers, there is absolutely nothing that is not plainly theatrical and
ridiculous, devoid not only of the authority but even of the likeness of Holy
Scripture. 2. Those blessings, moreover, are not merely empty, since they lack
the word, but profane and plainly insulting to the grace of God. For among
other things the bishop prays over the balsam “that God, by granting spiritual
grace to this ointment, would infuse the fullness of sanctification, and that
it would serve for adoption as sons through the Holy Spirit for all who are to
be anointed with it.” See the *Roman Pontifical*.

2. Bellarmine. In Confirmation itself, besides the essential ceremony: 1. A
sponsor presents the candidate. 2. Prayers are said with the laying on of a
hand. 3. Peace is given to the confirmed person. 4. The bishop strikes the
confirmed person lightly with his hand. 5. A band is tied around the forehead.
6. The head is not washed for seven days, and so forth.

Protestant reply. Merely to have recited these things—except for the prayers,
together with their gesture—is more than sufficient to have refuted them.
