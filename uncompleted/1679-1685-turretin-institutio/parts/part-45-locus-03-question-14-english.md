QUESTION XIV

ON GOD’S WILL

Does God will some things necessarily and other things freely? Affirmative.

I. Will necessarily follows God’s intellect, and its object is only the good,
just as the object of intellect is the true. But since the good is either
uncreated and infinite or finite and created, a twofold object is assigned to
the will: the primary object, namely God as the infinite good, and the
secondary object, namely all created things outside God, which have the
character of finite good. God also wills these outside himself, but not in the
same way: he wills himself necessarily through complacency, but all other
things freely by decree. Hence the proposed question arises.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

II. To establish the state of the question, observe first that necessity is
twofold. One kind is absolute: that which simply, in itself, and by its own
nature cannot be otherwise, such as that God is good, just, and so forth. The
other kind is hypothetical: that which is not such in itself and simply that

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 198 | IA leaf 252 | PDF p. 252]

it could not be otherwise, but which, once something is posited, follows
necessarily and cannot be otherwise. For example, if you suppose that God has
predestined Jacob to salvation, Jacob must be saved, namely, upon the
hypothesis of the decree, because otherwise he could have not been
predestined and not been saved. Therefore, when it is asked whether God wills
some things necessarily and others freely, the question does not concern only
hypothetical necessity. For in that sense, once the decree has been posited,
God can no longer fail to will those things that he wills freely. Rather, the
question concerns absolute necessity.

III. Something is called free either by reason of spontaneity or by reason of
indifference. The former is what occurs spontaneously and without coercion;
the latter is what is so constituted that it can occur or not occur. When it
is asked whether God wills some things freely, freedom of spontaneity alone
is not meant. For in this sense he also wills freely—that is, without
coercion—the things that he wills most necessarily. Rather, freedom of
indifference is properly meant: whether he wills those things in such a way
that he could have not willed them.

IV. Third, there are two kinds of things willed. One is principal and has the
character of the ultimate end; the other is secondary and has the character
of means. The will is borne necessarily toward the former, so that it cannot
fail to be borne toward it. It is borne freely and by choice toward the
latter, unless that means has a necessary and inseparable connection with the
ultimate end, so that the end cannot exist without it. For then, by the same
necessity by which we will the ultimate end, we also will that means, just as
someone who wills the preservation of life also wills the taking of food,
without which life cannot be preserved.

V. With this established, I say that God wills himself necessarily, not only
with hypothetical necessity but also with absolute necessity, because he is
the ultimate end and the supreme good, which he cannot fail to will and love.
This is true not only with respect to specification, so that he wills or loves
nothing contrary to himself, but also with respect to exercise, so that he
never ceases to will and love himself, because he cannot fail to will his own
glory or deny himself. But he wills all other things freely. For since
no created thing is necessary in relation to God but is contingent, inasmuch
as he could have been without it, he wills all things in such a way that he
could have not willed them—that is, with freedom not only of spontaneity but
also of indifference.

VI. This freedom of the divine will concerning created things must be
understood absolutely and *a priori*, and with respect to things considered in
themselves, not hypothetically and *a posteriori*, with respect to the decree
itself. For in the latter sense God wills created things necessarily upon the
hypothesis, because, once it is posited that he has willed them, he can no
longer be unwilling to have them, on account of the immutability of his will.
But, speaking absolutely, he wills them freely, because he was induced to
will them initially by no necessity but by sheer freedom, and he could have
refrained from producing them.

VII. This indifference of the divine will is so far from being less fitting
to God’s majesty that it is the greatest evidence of perfection in God, who,
as an independent being, needs nothing outside himself, just as it is the
greatest evidence of imperfection in creatures.

VIII. God wills all created things for himself—not to perfect himself, as
though he needed them, but to communicate himself and to manifest his
goodness and glory in them. Hence, because he could have been without all of
them with no loss to his blessedness, he is said to will them freely. For
since God wills everything outside himself insofar as it is ordered to his
goodness as its end, and God’s goodness is perfect without other things, so
that no perfection accrues to him from them, it follows that God’s willing
things other than himself is not absolutely necessary but necessary only upon
the hypothesis, as Thomas well notes, part 1, question 19, article 3.

IX. Although God wills all things for the sake of his goodness and wills his
goodness necessarily, it does not follow that he wills with the same necessity

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 199 | IA leaf 253 | PDF p. 253]

all the other things that he wills outside himself. For he wills them for the
sake of his goodness, not to enrich it but to pour it forth, and thus in such
a way that he can exist without them.

X. The case of the intellect in knowing is not the same as that of the will in
willing, because the intellect has things in itself and beholds them
according as they exist in itself, whereas the will is borne toward them.
Hence in understanding, things are understood according to the manner of the
recipient, but in willing it is otherwise. The intellect beholds things as
existing, at least in itself, and therefore cannot fail to understand them;
but the will makes them, and accordingly deals freely with them. Thus, just as
all things, insofar as they are in God, have a certain necessity, whereas in
themselves they have contingency, so whatever God knows, he knows by
necessity, but he does not likewise will by necessity whatever he wills.

XI. Although every volition of God is eternal, it must not therefore be
immediately absolutely necessary. For what is necessary in its origin, on the
side of the principle, can be free in its terminus, on the side of the object.

XII. The question of God’s will concerning sin would belong here—how God can
be said to will sin—but it will be treated more suitably later in the topic on
providence.
