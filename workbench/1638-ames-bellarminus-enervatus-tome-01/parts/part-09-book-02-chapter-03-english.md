### Chapter III

##### On Christ the Mediator, book 5

##### Thesis I

###### State of the controversy

1. Bellarmine: *We call him a mediator who interposes himself as a middle
party between some who disagree, or at least are not united, so that he may
bring them back to agreement or unite them by a new covenant. This happens in
two ways: 1. through a certain substantial mediation; 2. through operation. In
the first way Christ can be called Mediator according to his personality, by
which he joins two natures, though not two natures at variance, because
Christ's humanity was never opposed to his divinity. But the question is not
about this mediation. It concerns that by which the innocent Christ reconciled
sinners to the Father.* Prot. 1. The name Mediator, in its proper meaning, is
not the name of a substance but of a duty and office. *Substance* and
operation, therefore, do not constitute two kinds of mediation, but one and
the same thing, just as a whole office is fulfilled by fitness for the
discharge of some duty and by the discharge or execution itself. Substance
considered in itself can be a *middle,* but not a *Mediator,* as Bonaventure
not inaptly distinguishes in his commentary on the Master, book 3, distinction
19. 2. The very assumption of human substance or nature in the form of a
servant was a part of Christ's humiliation and therefore of that operation by
which he discharged the office of mediation, Philippians 2:7–8. 3. Although
human nature as it was in Christ had no enmity with the divine nature, yet as
it exists in us, whose cause Christ was pleading, it was most hostile to God.
4. Nor ought Christ's substantial mediation to be separated from his
operation in this question, nor can it be, because when it is asked according
to which nature Christ is Mediator, the question concerns not only the
operation but also the one operating. And our Mediator had such a substance
for no other end than to discharge through it the parts of a Mediator.

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 127 | printed p. 117 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

2. Bellarmine: *Mediation in operation can take place in four ways: 1. by
discerning and judging the case, as arbitrators do; 2. by reporting the
agreements and conditions from each side, as messengers do; 3. by praying and
supplicating; 4. by paying and making satisfaction. The present question—By
which nature does the office of Mediator belong to Christ?—can be understood
of all these ways.* Prot. The question is understood of all those things which
Christ has done and does, either in our name before God or in God's name among
us, in order to reconcile God to us and us to God. For all these things belong
to Christ's mediation. Bellarmine has nevertheless omitted no small part of
them, namely that which is situated in Christ's kingdom.

3. Bellarmine: *The common position of the Catholics is that the principle
which performed the works of the Mediator was God and man together, but that
the principle by which those works were done, or their formal principle, was
the human nature, not the divine. Calvinists and Lutherans contend that Christ
exercises the office of Mediator according to both natures as a formal
principle.* Prot. 1. The principle by which, or the formal principle by which,
some effect is produced is nothing other than that power of the cause which
brings such an effect into existence. Therefore, if the Roman Catholics deny
that the divine nature was a principle of this kind in Christ's mediation
which procures our salvation, then they make the divine nature idle, exerting
no power in this whole affair. They also deny that principle which is accepted
even among their own Scholastics: *that the restoration of the human race was
accomplished through the God-man, inasmuch as he was able to make satisfaction
as God but ought to make it as man.* They then strip Christ's whole mediation
of divine value and worth. Finally, they openly contradict Scripture, which
expressly affirms that *Christ offered himself to God through the eternal
Spirit,* Hebrews 9:14. 2. We, however, contend that Christ exercises the office
of Mediator according to both natures in such a way that we establish a
communion in the work, but a distinction of power and manner in the natures
which operate. Thus we say that Christ suffered according to the human nature,
but sustained and overcame what he suffered by the power and might of the
divine nature. In the human

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 128 | printed p. 118 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

nature he made satisfaction, but the value, worth, efficacy, and application
of that satisfaction proceeded from the divine nature. In short, all the
Mediator's human operations are exercised by Christ according to the human
nature, and all the divine operations according to the divine nature. This
position is confirmed in large part by that concession of Vásquez in part 3,
disputation 83, chapter 3: *Taken in himself and reduplicatively, it cannot be
said, “This man, insofar as he is man, is Mediator,” or “God, insofar as he is
God,” but “The God-man, insofar as he is the God-man, is Mediator.”*

##### Bellarmine's arguments

4. Bellarmine: *1 Timothy 2:5: One Mediator of God and men, the man Christ
Jesus.* Prot. Nothing else follows from this except that Christ Jesus the
Mediator is a man. Bell. 1. *Why, I ask, did he add “man,” unless to express
the nature according to which Christ is Mediator?* Prot. 1. He expressed the
nature according to which he is Mediator, but not the nature according to
which alone he is Mediator. 2. The reason he mentioned the man Christ was to
warn the faithful not to despair of any class of people, but to pray for them,
because Christ, having been made man, sustained the common person not of this
or that class of people, but of mankind. 3. By similar reasoning someone could
conclude that Christ has the power to forgive sins only according to the human
nature, because in Matthew 9:6 *the Son of Man is said to have power to forgive
sins.* Bell. 2. *The Apostle distinguishes the Mediator from God, for he says,
“one God and one Mediator.”* Prot. The same Apostle distinguishes Christ from
man in Galatians 1:1: *Not through man, but through Jesus Christ.* Yet it does
not follow from this that Christ called Paul only according to the divine
nature. But Christ is rightly distinguished both from mere man and from God
considered apart, insofar as he is the θεάνθρωπος, the God-man.

Bellarmine: *The Fathers deny that Christ is Mediator insofar as he is God.*
Prot. The Fathers intend nothing other than to indicate the necessity of the
personal union in Christ the Mediator. They therefore deny that Christ,
insofar as he is simply God,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 129 | printed p. 119 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

is Mediator, in order to show that he is Mediator insofar as he is God
manifested in the flesh.

6. Bellarmine: *A mediator ought to be a middle party, that is, in some manner
distant from each party at variance. But Christ as God is not distant from God
in any way.* Prot. 1. Bonaventure, previously praised by Bellarmine, admits in
his commentary on the Sentences, book 3, distinction 19, that Christ is a
middle between the human and divine natures insofar as he is one person in two
natures. Bellarmine himself also acknowledges in chapter 1 that Christ is a
middle and Mediator through a substantial mediation, joining two natures
according to his personality, and that the Fathers often speak of this
mediation. 2. That distance which Bellarmine posits—*that of a righteous man,
distant from God insofar as he is man and from man insofar as he is
righteous*—is not even consistent with itself, because there is no proportion
between that distance which on one side is in substance, *insofar as he is
man,* and on the other is in quality, *insofar as he is righteous.* Nor is it
sufficient for mediation, because in the righteousness of one man, as such,
there is not found sufficient value to satisfy God's justice for a world of
unrighteous people.

7. Bellarmine: *Christ is not Mediator according to both natures together,
because in that case he does not differ from God the Son, either in person or
in nature.* Prot. He differs with respect to the economic state in which that
person exists under another form, as the Apostle Paul speaks in Philippians
2:7. Bellarmine admitted this earlier in chapter 1 concerning Christ insofar
as he is the substantial Mediator.

8. Bell.: *If Christ were in some manner Mediator according to the divine
nature, then the Father and Holy Spirit would also be Mediators.* Prot. This
follows no more than the following: if the Son of God is in some manner
incarnate according to the divine nature, then the Father and Holy Spirit are
also incarnate. Acting as Mediator belongs to the divine nature, not as nature
simply, but as existing in such a manner—a manner which does not belong to the
Father or Holy Spirit.

9. Bellarmine: *Sacrificing cannot belong to Christ according to his divinity.*
Prot. 1. This sophistry militates against the express words of Scripture,
Hebrews 9:14: *through the eternal Spirit he offered himself.* 2. Although the
sacrifice was

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 130 | printed p. 120 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

Christ most properly according to the human nature, nevertheless the whole
person was offered in some manner, as Suárez teaches on Thomas, part 3,
question 1, distinction 4. Moreover, he was Priest also according to that
nature with respect to which it was said, *You are my Son,* Hebrews 5:5. An
altar likewise pertains to sacrifice, and its character with respect to
Christ's sacrifice is found nowhere but in Christ's divine nature.

10. Bellarmine: *Confusion and heresy will follow if there is one operation of
both natures as the proper and principal cause.* Prot. No confusion or heresy
follows if two formal principles, according to their distinct powers and
operations, are said to concur by cooperating toward the same work. There is
a great difference between operation and work, and between motion and the
thing made by motion. The divine nature exercised at least that governance
over the work of mediation by which the efficacy and worth of that work were
made to proceed from the Deity.

Bell.: *Efficacy and worth are not some physical or real action, but proceed
from the fact that the sacrifice is a divine person, just as royal majesty
exerts no real influence upon a work done by a king, but adds worth to it.*
Prot. 1. Although efficacy is not a real action, nevertheless in a real action
it always proceeds from a real agent, for it is nothing other than the
completed act of efficient power. 2. Royal majesty cannot exercise a real
operation, but the divine nature can; therefore the example is irrelevant. 3.
Royal majesty, however, adds no worth to a king's physical actions of eating,
drinking, and so forth. 4. Unless the nature to which royal majesty adheres
operates, it adds no worth to any action. So also the divine nature, to which
divine majesty properly belongs, ought to perform something in order that
divine worth may accompany that work.

##### Our arguments

11. Prot. John 10:17–18: *I lay down my life by myself.* Christ says this as
Mediator, but it is not an act of the human nature. Bellarmine: *To lay down
one's life belongs to man, not to God, for it is nothing*

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 131 | printed p. 121 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*other than to die, just as taking up one's life is nothing other than rising
again, although the power to lay it down and take it up at will was given by
God to the man himself.* Prot. 1. Although to lay down one's life in a passive
sense is to die, and to take up one's life in a passive sense is to rise
again, yet if these phrases are taken actively—as they necessarily ought to be
in this passage, where the power of laying down and taking up is treated—then
to lay down one's life is freely to release the soul in death, and to take up
one's life is to raise the dead. But these things do not belong to man. 2. If
this power was given to the human nature, then the human nature had divine
power. For to be able to raise the dead belongs to plainly divine power,
Ephesians 1:20; Philippians 3:21; Matthew 22:29. 3. According to the position
of all the Scholastics, when Christ was dead he was not a man. How then could
Christ as man take up his life or have the power to take it up? Bell.: *Augustine
says that the flesh took up the soul by the power of the one inhabiting the
flesh.* Prot. Therefore that power belonged to the divine person, not to the
human nature. Christ's dead flesh did not even possess a human will, much less
the power to accomplish what it willed. Thus Cajetan and Medina, on Thomas,
part 3, question 15, article 3, and Cajetan and Toletus, on John 10, affirm that
Christ laid down his life and took it up again according to his divinity or
divine hypostasis. Calvin, therefore, is not the sole author of this
interpretation, as Bellarmine wished to suggest by those final words, *Let
Calvin see which authors he follows.* Toletus also illustrates the matter with
an elegant comparison: *Just as a man who has a sword in a sheath draws the
sword when he wishes, and yet holds the sheath with one hand and the sword
with the other, so the divine person through death separated the soul from the
body like a sword from its sheath, yet kept each part united to himself. But
through the resurrection he joined the soul to the body, as though replacing
the sword in its sheath.*

12. Prot. Bellarmine raises as objections against himself passages supposedly
cited by our men, in which the Apostle speaks in 2 Corinthians 5 and
Colossians 1 of reconciliation and peacemaking procured by God in Christ and
through Christ. But Calvin, whom Bellarmine attacks here, does not put forward
those passages. Nevertheless, the statement in 2 Corinthians 5:19,

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 132 | printed p. 122 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

*God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself,* shows that God had some
part in procuring reconciliation. Bell.: *Reconciling does not always belong
to a Mediator. God reconciled not as mediator, but through the Mediator whom
he appointed and sent.* Prot. 1. The reconciliation accomplished by Christ
belonged to the Mediator. 2. God personally existing in Christ reconciled the
world to himself, as Cajetan interprets that passage. Christ as Mediator and
reconciler, therefore, was not only man but also God. 3. The same Apostle
expressly testifies in Acts 20:28 that the Church was redeemed by God's blood.
Christ the God-man, therefore, not only appointed the Mediator, but also
discharged the office of Mediator.

13. Prot. He also objects with Hebrews 4:14, *he passed through the heavens,*
and chapter 7, *without father, without mother.* Although neither of these
passages is found in Calvin, they nevertheless contain more force than can be
evaded by Bellarmine's answer. Bell.: *These rightly prove the person of our
High Priest to be divine. The power by which Christ penetrated the heavens was
from God, but the Word, God, is Priest according to the form of man.* Prot.
Here Bellarmine concedes all that we maintain: that the person of the Mediator
was divine; that the power by which he discharged mediation was divine; and
that this person under the form of man was Mediator. For if by *according to
the form of man* he understands human power, not divine, then he openly
contradicts himself, because at the same time he affirms that the power was
from God, that is, from the divine nature.

14. Bellarmine also surrenders the whole case to us in chapter 7 when evading
the testimonies of the Fathers, where he concedes: 1. *In some of Christ's
works, the actions of the Deity and humanity concurred.* 2. *All Christ's
miracles were partly divine, partly human.* 3. *The substantial mediation not
only of the human but also of the divine nature was necessary so that the
Mediator's works might be of infinite value.* For 1. none of Christ's works
are more divine than those which concern redemption, which is not without
reason held by some to be a greater work than creation itself. 2. He who
acknowledges nothing miraculous in the work of redemption is a miracle—or
rather a monstrosity—of a Christian. 3. If the

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 133 | printed p. 123 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

substantial mediation of both natures was necessary for the worth of the
operation, then it was certainly necessary for operating, for no worth passes
from a nature into an operation unless that nature operates.

15. Prot. Renewing hearts and giving the Holy Spirit are works of the
Mediator, but they belong to God alone. Bell.: *Doing these things
meritoriously is proper to the Mediator, but doing them efficiently and as the
author of the gift does not belong to the Mediator.* Prot. To deny that this
efficiency belongs to Christ the Mediator is nothing other than to deny that
*all power in heaven and on earth was given to Christ,* Matthew 28:18; that
*he gives his own eternal life,* John 10:28 and 17:2; that he *baptizes us
with the Holy Spirit and fire,* Matthew 3:11; that he *sends the Spirit from
the Father,* John 15:26; that he *preserves his own in this world,* Matthew
28:20; and that he *raises them on the last day,* John 6:54. In sum, it is
nothing other than effectively to deny that Christ is Jesus, or Savior.

16. Bellarmine puts forward one other argument from Calvin's letter 352 about
Christ's mediation between God and angels, which, since it was far-fetched and
obscurely proposed, he thought he could burden with unjust suspicions. But in
the meantime he leaves Calvin's first argument untouched, *because Christ as
Mediator is to be believed in;* the second, *because as Mediator he holds
supreme dominion in heaven and on earth;* the third, *because he joins us to
God;* and the fourth, *because he makes us partakers of the divine nature.*
Bellarmine, I say, conceals all these arguments together with various
testimonies of Scripture, and seizes upon that one which Calvin puts forward
not to prove the case, but to explain the name. What is this other than to
fight sophistically by fleeing bravely?

##### Whether Christ also merited anything for himself, chapters 9 and 10

17. Although one need not labor greatly over this question as one of lesser
necessity, we nevertheless determine two things about it with complete
certainty: 1. The position which the Roman Catholics maintain on this subject
is either false or at least uncertain in part and

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 134 | printed p. 124 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

in part self-contradictory. 2. It is a matter of meddlesome rashness to
attribute to Christ, with respect to himself, any merit other than the way or
manner of passing from humiliation to glory.

18. The Roman Catholics teach that from the instant of his conception Christ
was a perfect possessor of the beatific vision and was blessed. The Master,
book 3, distinctions 14 and 26; Thomas, part 3, question 9, article 2; question
15, final article; and question 34, article 4. But according to the doctrine of
the School, it does not belong to one who has attained to merit something for
himself, but only to a wayfarer.

19. They do not explain to us whether he merited primarily for himself and
secondarily for us, or primarily for us and afterward for himself. Nor do they
define whether the merit by which he acquired something for himself was merit
by congruity or by condignity, as they are accustomed to speak, or upon what
agreement or covenant it rested. Nor have they yet shown how one and the same
merit has a proportion both to liberating sinners and to exalting a righteous
man. Finally, they give no suitable reason why or how Christ had the glory of
the body and the exaltation of his name by merit, but not the glory of the
soul.

20. Bellarmine: *Thomas answers in part 3, question 19, article 3, that it is
better for the soul never to lack glory than to merit it; but it was better
for the body to lack glory for a time and acquire it by merit than to lack the
dignity of meriting. For other things being equal, it is better to have
something by merit than without merit.* Prot. 1. What is laid down as the
foundation—that it is better to have something by merit than without
merit—is false. For to have something without the laborious procurement of
merit comes nearer to the mode of perfection found in God. 2. There is one
glory of the whole human nature, which ought to be acquired by one and the
same right, not in one part by merit and in the other part without merit. 3.
If some dignity came to Christ by meriting, then it came chiefly to the soul;
but the soul, according to the Roman Catholic position, was already perfectly
blessed. 4. A blessed soul is not in the state of meriting.

21. In Philippians 2:9, neither *for which reason* nor *and* necessarily proves
anything other than a fitting correspondence

[1638 Amsterdam witness | e-rara canvas 135 | printed p. 125 | ZB Zürich Rp 510]

of reason between Christ's humiliation and his glorification. The parallel
phrase cited by Bellarmine from Matthew 16, *and I say to you, you are Peter,*
is very far from signifying a meritorious cause. For Peter's confession did
not merit that he should be constituted the foundation and Monarch of the
Church—which the Roman Catholics wish to have been Peter's reward—because
then all those who make the same confession would also be admitted to the same
primacy. By a similar argument Bellarmine can conclude that Christ also laid
down a law of meriting for the priests in Matthew 21:24: *If you tell me, I
also will tell you,* because *and* is similarly used there. And in Hebrews
2:9, *because of the endurance of death* signifies that death was the end of
his humiliation, not the cause of his glory.

22. Bellarmine concedes in chapter 10 that *the Son of God did not descend in
order to acquire something for himself,* and he does not deny that all glory
was due to the human nature itself without any labor. Therefore, there appears
to be no other reason why the Roman Catholics contend so vigorously that
Christ merited something for himself than to lay a foundation for their
doctrine of human merits. Yet they do not dare to attribute to Christ that
freedom of will which they maintain is so necessary for a human work to be
meritorious that they are accustomed to demonstrate it from the nature of
merit—namely, the power to act either well or badly.
