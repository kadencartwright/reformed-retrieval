ANIMADVERSION.

Pure natural things formally exclude the corruption of natural things; insofar as they are called
pure, they do not exclude supernatural things by virtue of the phrase; for neither the natural voice
excludes them, nor the pure voice. Indeed, if pure natural things cannot be considered except by the
addition of supernatural things, they would rather include supernatural things than mostly exclude
them. However, if I am not mistaken, the phrase is used exclusively both with respect to
supernatural things and with respect to the corruption of natural things. 2. This distinction of
Junius regarding catachrestically opposed reprobation is not acceptable. If reprobation is opposed
to election, it is necessary that it be opposed to it in all respects. For as one of the opposites
is spoken of in one way, so in many ways the other is spoken of, according to the axiom commonly
used in the schools. Therefore, just as election is defined as the decree of conferring grace and
glory, so the reprobation, if opposed to election, must be called the decree of permitting sin and
inflicting damnation for sin, just as Aquinas once defined reprobation 1. q. 23. art. 3. in the
body. And in my own judgment, the revocation of reprobation usually results more from a spirit of
evasion and concealment, and from guarding oneself from adversaries' attacks, than from openly
presenting the truth and striving courageously to defend it.

REPLY. 1. We have already said regarding the sixth proposition, that predestination is not resolved
except through various acts, among which a threefold consideration of the object occurs according to
Piscator, who explained the sentence thus, which Junius seems to have embraced, much more clearly.
For since predestination encompasses three notable acts according to the same, namely the decree to
create, the decree to permit sin, and finally the decree to choose some from the corrupt mass and
reprobate others; hence it seems evident that there is a place for a threefold consideration of the
object of predestination; namely, that a man not yet created is foreseen in the divine consideration
by the decree to create; a man created and still in pure nature is foreseen by the decree to permit
sin; finally a man both created and corrupted is foreseen by the decree to choose and to reprobate.
According to the aforementioned explanation (as long as it is established that the individual
members are indeed real) it appears that these three sentences are by no means opposed but
consistent; although perhaps this was not so apparent to all and to the hypothetical affirmers of
them. For although the First and Second may have excluded sin from the subject condition in that
inquiry, which pertains to decrees of creation and permitting sin (and who more soberly would not
exclude it?), it does not follow that they excluded sin from the subject or rather the object of
universal predestination as a whole, so that it is not only absolved by the aforementioned decrees,
but is simultaneously completed by the decree from the corrupted mass of choosing some for salvation
and others again to pass over or condemn to destruction. I have already indicated what I consider in
this passage to be alien to sound reasoning, in my sixth proposition, and I have elaborated further
in my response to the examination of Perkins' booklet, with previous digressions related to
predestination that primarily deal with this subject. 2. No one more clearly raises the question
whether sin is caused by election; but only whether the cause of passing over or reprobation must be
stated. Arminius denies this, asserting that sin is not the cause why God passes over some, but
simultaneously demands that it be granted to him that sin might be the cause why God could pass over
some created in his image. In the former statement I desire the speaker's faith, in the latter the
reasoning of the one presenting it. But, I admit, the ambiguous use of passing over gives room for a
freer assertion while simultaneously precluding a distinct explication of truth. We therefore say
that passing over is twofold; For either it is the denial of efficacious grace, by which one is
preserved from sinning; or the denial of efficacious grace, by which one is restored and rises again
from sin. Yet both of these must be distinguished from the decree of passing over. As far as the
former acceptance is concerned, it is false to say that sin is the reason why God can pass over;
indeed that passing over does not follow from sin but rather precedes it. For first of all, sin
prepares this kind of passing over for itself. But the passing over of the latter kind presupposes
sin for itself according to Arminius as the cause of itself, not only regarding the power to pass
over, but also regarding the act; this is most certainly the case concerning reprobation. Indeed,
contrary to this, it seems to me; nor can sin be established as a suitable cause why one is passed
over by God, nor why one does not receive grace that is effective for believing and repenting. For
what reason? Shall disease be considered a suitable cause why one does not receive the healing hand
of a physician? Or a cause may properly be assigned why someone does not extend helping hand, so
that the one who has fallen may rise again? Arminius felt this inconvenience, and therefore
preferred to assign the cause why God might pass over man, rather than why He would pass over.
Unless perhaps he did not even sense this inconvenience, but instituted it under another name,
because certainly he noticed that not all sinners are passed over by God. But no consideration
suffices to impart any sobriety to this opinion. For it is impossible from a man's sin that that
power might accrue to God; even if perhaps a new designation is created. Nor can there be a place
for grace of rising up, unless one has already fallen, nor for grace of repenting unless one has
already sinned. Yet I think Arminians would not deny that it is the same grace by which one
restrains oneself from sin and by which one grieves for committed sin and repents. Certainly they
will never overcome, God can do something today that He could not do yesterday: although at this
point nothing is needed for us to pull the rope of contention; Nor does Arminius seem to have come
here with any other intention than to guard himself from certain impending troubles. 3. Junius
interpreted the opinion of the Tertians in such a way that the consideration of sin was subordinated

fallen, to predestination. Although this reasoning is not presented very distinctly by Junius, it
is, however, very clearly and distinctly presented by Piscator. Namely, because these Tertians

safer to remain within it; of whose number was Calvin himself concerning predestination against.
Pighius. Therefore, when these five selections were considered predestined as a decree of raising

allowing persistence in sin, and finally of inflicting damnation for sin: Hence it is that these

for any other reason than that these acts according to their formal denomination presuppose sin.
Hence it came to pass that they also thought that the decrees of raising some from sin, and leaving

REPLY.

Let us set aside for now the June discussion on philosophy regarding the distinction between nature
and things natural, as currently it is by no means necessary. Others may judge how valid this
distinction is. I see no reason why it would have caused trouble for Arminius at the first instance.
However, I see the Junianist exception—not all that convincing in my judgment—handled, nonetheless,
not unhappily, by divine providence. For this prompted Arminius to defend what he had asserted, and
he pronounced matters that otherwise perhaps he would scarcely have produced. The ancient
scholastics handed down that our Adam the Archparent was founded in grace, a view in my judgment
sound and orthodox; this pertains to natural indifference in acting in any matters indifferent, yet
not indifferent to moral good & evil, rather leaning with a holy inclination toward true good, that
is, to obey God and comply with His admonitions. Indeed, also in our regeneration, while God
inspires us with the holiness of faith and morals, we are said to be renewed in the image of God who
created man. Eph.4.24. Col.3.10. This holiness, although it can be called natural insofar as it is
given at the very beginning to nature, and had it remained intact, would have been hereditary, is
not so much by the power of informing and propagating nature as by the indulgence of God and the
benefit of assisting grace, yet with respect to its origin, it is more probable that it was
supernatural. For otherwise, it would by no means have been very good in its kind from creation,
just as other things were also very good in their kind. And this argument for this truth drawn from
the scriptures themselves is of greater importance than what has hitherto been presented by Junius,
while he struggles against it. Certainly, the body is formed first, before the soul is inspired; And
see whether we should not argue for a stronger consequence for our statement than that which Junius
contends for it, while we establish, just as all the qualities of bodies are simultaneously inherent
when a body is formed: so when the soul is inspired and created, all spiritual qualities, in order
for it to be very good and created by God, are likewise imparted to it. And I beg that the grace of
creation is not considered unnecessary, especially since neither natural emanation nor natural
acquisition takes place. Nor does it follow that these gifts were therefore common to the species or
would pass to descendants through propagation, which only holds true in those things which are the
result of natural emanation. Meanwhile, however, it seems to me more than probable that such things

the consideration of the created human, and the decree of permitting sin, which seems to presuppose
the consideration of the human indeed created but not yet corrupted; finally the decree of choosing
in Christ, which both Junius and Piscator, together with Arminius and not a few others, thought to
presuppose the consideration of the human not only created but also corrupted. Therefore they would
admit in the very act of choosing the person to be considered as a sinner, but not in the two acts
previously mentioned. I, however, contend that neither in the second act is he considered as
created, nor in the third act is he considered as a sinner. For although the permission of sin
presupposes creation, it does not, however, decree permission: just as, although the gift of
salvation presupposes good works, it does not, however, decree their conferment. In the same way,
although separation from a corrupt mass presupposes sin and misery, it does not, however, decree
separation (as they interpret election) presuppose or consider sin or misery. And if it did
presuppose, it would also presuppose the decree of permitting sin; which anyone who says, we have
already sufficiently shown, leads to insurmountable difficulties and absurdities. Therefore I
confess that we are elected in Christ the Redeemer from sin; this is established by God, that we are
redeemed from sin through Christ.

Indeed, sin precedes that redemption, but consideration of sin by no means precedes the decree to
redeem, lest the decree to permit sin thus be placed before the decree to redeem from sin. If this
were said—as it frequently is, through an error not immediately obvious—it would follow that the
permission of sin is prior in God's intention to redemption from sin, and consequently that the
permission of sin has the relation of an end, while redemption from sin assumes the relation of a
means to that end. For only the intention of the end is to be
called prior, and the intention of the means posterior. Hence it would follow that God redeems us
from sin for this end, so that He may allow sin; it would also follow that in the execution the
permission of sin must occupy a subsequent place; as if God were to redeem us from sin before
allowing us to sin, when in truth in both respects the opposite rather obtains. I recognize that a
man is a sinner before restoration; but does it follow that he was also a sinner before the decree
of restoration? Indeed, the field was full of brambles, not only before cleansing, but also before
the human decree of cleansing; nor is it surprising, since all human decrees are temporary, not
divine or eternal. You will say, but in regard to God at least one was a sinner before the decree of
restoration. Yes, I admit, those who are accustomed to dictate are accustomed to establish it
without delay. Certainly, nothing else confirms this except that sin precedes restoration; therefore
consideration of sin precedes (they say) the decree of restoration. This is drawing anything from
anything. In the same way I would reason; good works precede the granting of salvation; therefore
the consideration of good works precedes the decree of conferring salvation.
