Answer: Surely it ought now to seem surprising to no one that he says the
former, namely immediate, power of the Spirit is now nothing, since he scarcely
concedes that the Apostles and Evangelists themselves, incapable of error,
possessed it, and that only in a few things; for he does not know whether the
Holy Spirit used this way so that they might be infallible in interpreting.
But how, then, will it be evident that they were infallible in interpreting,
if not by that extraordinary leading of the Spirit, or by an utterly effectual
inward impulse, infallibly inspiring them and rendering them completely
certain, so that they would not depart from the divine truth revealed or err
in the doctrine which they had received to reveal? If they interpreted the
Old Testament while led in no other than the ordinary way, how will it be
established for us that they were infallible in interpreting it? And if it is
not yet established that the Apostles and other sacred

[Page 109 | ONB canvas 163 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000163]

amanuenses were infallible, what will become of our salvation, since the whole
New Testament is, as it were, a certain interpretation of the Old, and very
many passages of the Old Testament in particular are explained in it? It will
be futile to say that they were infallible in writing. For how could they be
infallible in writing interpretations and not in interpreting, unless he says
that they wrote while out of their minds and did not know what they were
writing, as he says elsewhere? Next, what reason can be given for this
difference? He does indeed say that the reason of necessity was not equal. But
why not? He adds a reason, but one which does not prove what was denied; for
that added reason, if it proves anything, proves only what he said above:
namely, that no extraordinary assistance or impulse of the Holy Spirit was
needed to interpret the writings of Moses and the Prophets, as it had been
needed to receive new doctrines. But what has this to do with the matter in
hand? Does this prove that this extraordinary assistance of the Spirit was
needed for writing, but not for interpreting? Let the Reader now judge whether
Wolzogen agrees with the orthodox, and whether, by saying these things, he does
not undermine our faith. But what of this statement: *They could not believe
many truths with certainty without extraordinary testimony?* Could they have
believed *any* of those truths which were not yet contained in any scriptures
(the truths of which he speaks), or believed them *in any way*, without this
testimony? Wolzogen will not explain this to us, especially since he himself
says that they could rest upon no other support. Next, what do these words
signify: *not even that of reason itself, since very many are above reason*?
If only very many are above reason, then not all are; and those which are not
above reason can rest upon the support of reason, while those which could rest
upon another support can be received as certain without that extraordinary
testimony. I fear that Wolzogen will finally say that those extraordinary and
infallible amanuenses and sacred Interpreters were moved by no extraordinary
impulse of the Spirit. *Fur-*

[Page 110 | ONB canvas 164 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000164]

*-ther, he says that there is a sure conviction concerning interpretation.*
But can an interpretation be infallible without an extraordinary influx of
the Spirit? *[And it has*, he says, *another source by which it is confirmed?*
I concede that such an interpretation can be confirmed. But from where, I ask?
From the Spirit? By no means; for *the grace of the Spirit only causes it to
perceive the things by which it is confirmed.* Therefore that by which it is
confirmed must be sought elsewhere and not from the Spirit. But let us return
to the matter in hand. He holds that the former power of the Spirit is now
nothing and, consequently, that God opens our minds by no inward impulse as a
witness. But did he not by an inward impulse, and also as a witness, open the
heart of Lydia and of others whom he effectually taught divine truths and led
gently and powerfully into the truth, and whom he still teaches and leads even
now? Does he not by an inward impulse, as a witness, affect the minds of the
faithful when, together with their consciences, he testifies that they are the
children of God, and when he fastens the meaning of some passage upon the
heart, impresses, engraves, and inscribes it, and persuades them of it? Yet
(you will say) he does not bring a new argument different from the one which
can be drawn from the things Scripture itself supplies. Answer: Wolzogen does
not require both, for he speaks alternatively, saying, *or brings a new
argument.* But what does he understand by a new argument? A new truth different
from scriptural truth? Then the point is conceded. But no dispute is being
raised about this. Or does he mean a sort of new means, or a testimony distinct
from that which is found in the usage of language and similar things? Then it
is denied; for experience has
sometimes established that God, by a certain inward and effectual power,
explains the meaning of a passage to the faithful or reveals a scriptural
truth, and impresses it upon the soul in such a way that by its power they are
brought back from an error by which they had perhaps previously been seized,
restored to the right way, and confirmed in it if ever they hesitate and
stagger under the force of temptation—and this without exact, or perhaps
without any, prior attention to the words,

[Page 111 | ONB canvas 165 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000165]

syntax, usage of language, manner of speaking, or those rules instituted by
men.

Wolzogen's arguments follow: *[Nor can any boast of those things*, he says,
*except those who are seized by the madness of the Enthusiasts and rave.]*
Answer: Wolzogen is very greatly mistaken. How very far they are from that
Enthusiastic madness: for they read, search, meditate upon, and highly esteem
the Scriptures, which the Enthusiasts do not do. 2. They hold the Scriptures
as the sole norm and rule of faith and morals; the Enthusiasts do not do this.
3. They do not hold all these impulses and inward operations as a governing
rule, but examine them by the Scriptures as by a touchstone. 4. They reject
them as proceeding from the Devil if they say or recommend anything opposed to
the word; those who are seized by the madness of the Enthusiasts and rave do
no such thing. We shall enumerate more differences later.

*[For*, Wolzogen continues, *this opinion must necessarily descend to such a
point that, after all the light which either the rules for interpreting
discourse or reason examining the things themselves could furnish for
obtaining assent has been set aside, the Spirit is believed to pronounce by a
kind of oracle that these words, this span of words, this whole context of the
Discourse are to be understood in this way.]* Answer: This consequence does
not follow; for those who do not disdain those rules of interpretation can be
taught by God as by a kind of oracle. Nor do those who assert that God
sometimes teaches his people otherwise than through these rules say that the
rules should be set aside. Can God, by a certain power which he exercises in
the minds of men, open the meaning of the scriptures, address the faithful,
lead them gently, and persuade them, even when they do not attend exactly to
these rules? There is nothing to fear here as long as Scripture is held as the
norm, although they hear the Spirit's voice and inward speech and await his
oracle, voice, and effectual power: subordinate things do not conflict. What
does he say next?

[Page 112 | ONB canvas 166 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000166]

*[If this way is once opened, religion is done for, and we shall have no other
religion than one which has been thrust upon us by the caprice of malicious
men or by the imagination of the melancholic; for it is easy for anyone to
boast of the Spirit, and no one can refute an obstinate man by any reason.]*
Answer: I do not deny that it is easy for anyone to make the Spirit a pretext.
And if immediate impulses are held as the sole norm of our faith and morals, I
readily concede that all religion is done for. But to what purpose are these
remarks? Are you not speaking about the interpretation of the scriptures? Are
you not therefore disputing against those who acknowledge, read, and search
the Scriptures? What danger is there, then, Wolzogen, so long as all these
immediate motions and impulses of the Spirit are to be examined by the
Scriptures? For then religion will by no means be done for; we shall have one
far different from that which has been thrust upon us by the caprice of
malicious men or by the imagination of the melancholic. What next? *[Therefore*,
he says, *I acknowledge here the Spirit—not the one whom everyone vaunts, but
the one whom I find in Scripture. It is sufficient for there to be something
which, if it is clearly perceived, leaves us unable to doubt the meaning.]*
Answer: If by that spirit which you acknowledge here you understand no other
than him who speaks in and with the scriptures, and through and according to
them, we readily agree; for we acknowledge no other except the Spirit who
instills the scriptural truths themselves and by his voice and operation leads
into all truth. But if by that Spirit whom you say you find in Scripture you
understand only something in that material object, you are very greatly
mistaken: the usage of language, or words and syllables, will be your spirit,
not mine. Indeed, you appear to say that all persuasion depends upon the
explanation and illumination of the object alone; *there is*, you say,
*something which, if it is clearly perceived, leaves us unable to doubt the
meaning.* Therefore, to remove every doubt concerning the meaning, it will be
sufficient if that which is in the object is

[Page 113 | ONB canvas 167 | ABO Z176010803 image 00000167]

clearly perceived. This is Pelagian and Socinian, not orthodox. But in what
follows you explain to us what the Holy Spirit's parts are.
