CHAPTER TWO

Whether true theology exists, what it is, and what its subject is.

After the name *theology* has been explained by a nominal definition,
ὀνοματώδη, and distinguished according to its meanings, the treatment of the
thing itself now

[1615 BSB witness | digital canvas 26 | printed p. 2]

follows. In this treatment it must first be shown whether true theology
exists; next, what it is; then, how many kinds it has and what things follow
from these. This chapter must explain whether true theology exists, what it
is, and what its subject is, around which it is concerned.

THAT TRUE THEOLOGY EXISTS is proved both from testimonies and from arguments.

The testimonies are both divine and human.

The divine testimonies are these. Job 12:13: “With God are wisdom and
strength; counsel and understanding are his.” Romans 11:33: “O the profound
riches both of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God!” Deuteronomy 4:6:

[1615 printed locator B]

“This is your wisdom and your prudence in the sight of the peoples, who, when
they hear all these statutes, will say: ‘Only this great nation is a wise and
prudent people.’” If wisdom concerning divine things exists, theology also
exists. But Scripture testifies that wisdom concerning divine things exists.
Therefore it also testifies that theology exists.

There are two human testimonies. One is general, namely, the universal
agreement of all peoples; for what the universal agreement of all peoples
testifies exists certainly exists. That agreement is known from the writings
of the philosophers, historians, Gentile poets, and oracles, as Justin Martyr
explains at greater length in his

[1615 printed locator C]

*Paraenesis*, or *Exhortation to the Greeks*; Clement of Alexandria throughout
his *Protrepticus to the Greeks* and books of the *Stromata*; Lactantius in his
books of *Divine Institutes against the Gentiles*; Theodoret in his discourses
or books *On the Cure of Greek Affections*; Augustine in his books of *The City
of God*; and others. For even all the Gentiles acknowledged that true theology
exists, although they erred in deciding which one it is.

The other human testimony is special, namely, the Church of God, which
acknowledges that theology exists and congratulates itself on possessing it.

The arguments are these:

I. If God exists, theology must exist. The antecedent is true;

[1615 printed locator D]

therefore the consequent is also true. Proof of the connection: If God is
wise, theology must exist. But if God exists, he is wise. Therefore, if God
exists, theology must exist.

II. If God is the principle of every good in nature, theology must exist. The
former is true; therefore the latter is also true.

III. If God speaks not only singular things but also universals that pertain
to the true knowledge of him, then theology must exist. The antecedent is
true; therefore what follows is also true.

IV. If God acts as God toward all creatures, impressing on some obscure traces
of his majesty and on others a clear image, then

[1615 printed locator E]

theology must exist. The former is true; therefore the latter is also true.

Thus it has been proved that theology exists. What true theology is now
follows.

TRUE THEOLOGY is wisdom concerning divine things.

This is called *theology* univocally. It is called *true* because it is one,
for what is true cannot be more than one; because it belongs to and comes from
him who is truth itself and does not know how to lie; because it makes those
who truly devote themselves to it truthful, when they are led by the Holy
Spirit into all truth and sanctified by truth; and because through τῇ
παναρμονίᾳ, or the sweetest and most wonderful concord of all its parts, which
is the proper mark of truth,

[1615 printed locator F]

it remains perpetually coherent. It is also called θεοσοφία, *theosophy*.

This description has two parts. The first part teaches that true theology is
wisdom. Thus wisdom extends more broadly than theology, for wisdom is the most
perfect knowledge of the highest things, divine and human, and of the causes
under which those things are comprised.

[Marginalia: What wisdom is.]

So it is defined by Clement of Alexandria in book 1 of the *Stromata*, p. 122
of the Greek Commelin edition. Of this wisdom Ephesians 3:10 says: “So that
now, through the Church, that manifold wisdom of God might be made known to the
principalities and powers that are in heaven.”

Moreover, leading scholastics also taught that theology is wisdom, such as
Thomas Aquinas in the first part of the *Summa*, question 1, article 6. John
Duns Scotus, in the prologue to the *Sentences*, question 4, folio 10, column
4, of the Venice edition of 1506, says: “Yet it can be said more properly that
theology in itself is wisdom.” The name *wisdom*, however, is sometimes
understood to mean knowledge of divine things alone, as in 1 Corinthians 2:6:
“We speak wisdom among the perfect, but not the wisdom of this age, nor of the
rulers of this age, who are being brought to nothing.”

The second part of the description teaches what the subject or object of

[1615 printed locator H; the witness divides veræ across the locator.]

true theology is, about which it is concerned. Its subject is divine
things—that is, God and whatever belongs to God, insofar as it is ordered and
referred to God as pertaining to the knowledge and worship of him—namely,
every good arising from God in nature, and the words, actions, and works of
God.

The subject of theology is infinite and unknown to anyone except insofar as it
reveals itself. Hence theology itself infinitely excels all other disciplines,
beyond every analogy.

Theology indeed treats not only God but also angels, human beings, and other
creatures, though not equally. For it treats

[1615 printed locator I]

God principally as its subject, but creatures insofar as they serve to
manifest the subject—that is, insofar as they are ordered and referred to the
right knowledge and worship of GOD. Theology, therefore, is not about angels
or other creatures as its subject, but treats them for the manifestation of
its subject. It is thus about all things that exist with respect to the
relations they have to God. See John Duns Scotus, called the Subtle Doctor, in
the prologue to the *Sentences*, question 3, folio 10, column 2, of the
previously cited Venice edition of 1506.

The same is evident from the principles of theology—that is, Holy Scripture,
whose summary consists of the articles of faith and the precepts of the
Decalogue.

[1615 printed locator K]

The articles of faith concern God the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit;
his household, the holy catholic Church; and God’s benefits toward it.

[1615 printed locator A; the witness divides Decalogus across the column turn.]

The Decalogue commands love for God and love for one’s neighbor on account of
God. Moreover, the subject of the principles and of the whole science is the
same, since the whole science is contained virtually in its principles.

Furthermore, the noblest science has the noblest subject. But theology is the
noblest science. Therefore theology has the noblest subject, namely, God and
whatever belongs to God.
