NINTH CONTROVERSY

The Perfection of Scripture

The term “tradition” is equivocal and has the following meanings.

1. It is taken for every doctrine, whether written or unwritten (2 Thess.
2:15).

2. It is taken for doctrine delivered only by living voice (Matt. 15:2).

3. It is taken for the written word (1 Cor. 15:3; Acts

[1620 Tome I | col. 126 | BSB frame 00077]

6:14).

4. It is taken by the Roman Catholics for written doctrine, but doctrine not
in the sacred Scriptures.

5. It is taken for the action of handing something down. The Fathers sometimes
use the term “tradition” to signify doctrines contained in the apostolic
writings. Sometimes they call something an unwritten tradition when it is not
found in so many words in sacred literature, although, if you consider the
thing itself, it exists in Scripture. And sometimes by “tradition” they
understand not doctrines but ecclesiastical rites, in order to commend
ecclesiastical order more highly.

Bellarmine says in book 4, chapter 3 of *The Word of God* that the controversy
between us and them rests upon three points: they assert that all doctrine
necessary either for faith or for morals is not expressly contained in the
Scriptures. Therefore, besides God’s written word, an unwritten word is
required—that is, divine and apostolic traditions.

He calls those traditions “divine” which Christ handed down to the apostles
and which are nowhere found in divine literature. Of this kind are those that
concern the matter and form of the sacraments. This is indeed true of the
Roman Catholic sacraments, which are found nowhere in Scripture. He calls
those traditions “apostolic” which the apostles instituted not without the
assistance of the Holy Spirit, but which nevertheless do not exist in their
writings, such as the forty-day fast and the fast of the four seasons. Yet the
papists themselves maintain that Lent was instituted by Christ’s authority and
example, and that the time of the four fasts was instituted by Pope Callixtus.
They wish these traditions to have equal authority with Scripture.

We, however, say that we assert that everything required for faith and morals
is contained in the Scriptures and that there is consequently no need for an
unwritten word. But if by “expressly” Bellarmine understands what exists in
Scripture in so many words, he reverses

[1620 Tome I | col. 127 | BSB frame 00078]

the state of the question, because we do not say this. But if by “expressly”
he understands what is deduced from the Scriptures by necessary consequence,
we say that everything is expressly contained in Scripture. Nazianzen
therefore rightly says in book 5 of *Theology*: “Certain things do not exist,
yet are said to be in Scripture, as ‘to sit’ is said, and so forth. Certain
things exist, but are not stated in so many words, such as infant baptism, the
Trinity, and that the Holy Spirit is God, and so forth. They can nevertheless
be concluded necessarily from Scripture. Certain things neither exist nor are
stated, such as all heresies. Finally, certain things both exist and are
stated.”

2. Bellarmine says that they acknowledge apostolic traditions of every
kind—that is, concerning faith, morals, and ecclesiastical decorum.

We, however, [say] that the apostles instituted rites and ordained certain
things for order which are not necessary or commanded, but free. That this is
true is evident from 1 Corinthians 11 and chapter 14. Certain rites were not
instituted by the apostles to be perpetual, but were free and could be changed
according to the circumstances of place and so forth, yet in such a way that,
in accordance with that general rule, all things are done decently, in order,
and for the church’s edification.

Third, he says that they can demonstrate by certain ways and arguments that
their traditions are apostolic. We assert, however, that this cannot be
demonstrated. Bellarmine says in book 4, chapter 4 that he will demonstrate
that without traditions the Scriptures were neither simply necessary nor
sufficient.

We, however, say that not everything Christ and the apostles said and did has
been written. Indeed, only seven of the twelve apostles wrote. Nevertheless,
everything necessary for faith and morals is clearly and abundantly explained
in the Scriptures. The things that concern the church’s decorum are either
essential or accidental to decorum. What is essential to decorum is that moral
element which belongs to decorum; it is recorded in writing and is un—

[1620 Tome I | col. 128 | BSB frame 00078]

—changeable. What is accidental to decorum consists in external circumstances
that can be changed.

The state of the controversy, therefore, is this: does Scripture contain in
itself all the doctrine necessary for salvation? The papists deny it and wish
unwritten traditions to be added to Scripture in order to perfect it. We
affirm the contrary, relying upon the following arguments.

Argument 1. That which contains doctrine to which nothing may be added and
from which nothing may be subtracted contains perfect and sufficient
doctrine. Such is Scripture (Exod. 2:14, 36; Deut. 4:2; 5:22; 12:32; Prov.
3:6). Thus the Jews were not permitted to add anything to the books of Moses,
because they contained perfect doctrine. Much less may anything be added to
the canon now that it has been enlarged by so many books, since the same thing
was forbidden at the end of the New Testament (Rev. 22:18–19). That Moses is
speaking about the word is evident from Exodus 24:4; Joshua 1:7; Deuteronomy
28:1 and 5:8; and Galatians 3:10. There he prohibits not only corruption but
also addition with respect to substance—an addition which the prophets,
Christ, and the apostles did not make, as is taught below, but which occurs in
the papacy. Therefore.

Argument 2. If under the law recourse had to be made to Scripture, and nothing
in a matter of faith could be received which was not contained in Scripture,
then this is much more the case now that the apostolic writings have been
added to the doctrine of the law. The former is true (Isa. 8:20; 1 Pet. 1:19).
Therefore.

Argument 3. That which renders a person perfect for salvation and perfectly
instructed is perfect and, without traditions, contains everything necessary
for faith and morals. Such is Scripture (Ps. 19:8; Col. 1:28; 2 Tim. 3:15–16).
And if this was true of the Old Testament, it is much more true of the
completed canon. The exception that *pasa* (πᾶσα) signifies “every,” not
“whole,” is of no force, because it is often used in this way, as in Matthew
3:5 and Luke 21:23;

[1620 Tome I | col. 129 | BSB frame 00079]

Acts 20:27; Colossians 2:9; 2 Thessalonians 1:11; and elsewhere.

Argument 4. If under the law people rightly thought that they had eternal life
in the Scriptures—that is, that the Scriptures contained those things by
whose knowledge and belief they might attain eternal life—then this is much
more to be believed under the New Testament, since the New Testament has been
added to the Old. The former is true in John 5. Therefore.

Argument 5. Those who preach something beyond, even if not contrary to, what
is written are anathema (Gal. 1:8–9), where Paul is speaking of his doctrine,
which he drew only from Scripture; see Acts 17:10 and 26:22–23. Augustine, in
book 3, chapter 6 of *Against the Letters of Petilian*, interprets this
statement of the Scriptures. But unwritten traditions, if they are not
contrary to Scripture, are at least beyond it. As for their claim that Paul
preached to the Galatians things beyond what he wrote, it is false if you
consider the substance of the doctrine, for he preached not different things,
but to different people. Therefore, those who preach traditions and receive
them are anathema, and consequently the traditions must not be received.

Argument 6. If the prophets, evangelists, and apostles wrote those things
necessary for faith and morals in such a way that those who believe them have
eternal life, then Scripture is perfect and sufficient, so that there is no
need for traditions. The former is true in John 20:31, where he is speaking
not only of miracles, whose proper end is not to produce faith but to confirm
it, but principally of doctrine, whose goal is faith. Therefore.

Argument 7. Scripture, or the Old Testament, is called perfect in Psalm
19:8–9, where a term is read which signifies the whole Old Testament then
written, and sometimes all Scripture, as at various places in Psalm 119, Job
15:29, and Romans 3:29.

Argument 8. The prophets, Christ, and the apostles condemn traditions (Isa.
29:13; Matt. 15:6, 9; Gal. 1:20; Col. 2:8

[1620 Tome I | col. 130 | BSB frame 00079]

and 22). He is not speaking, however, of traditions received from Moses and
the prophets, because they handed down no unwritten traditions. Christ refers
the Jews only to the Scriptures (John 5:39; Luke 24:27), and Christ condemns
traditions which have even the appearance of godliness, those discussed in
Matthew 15 and Mark 7 and the ancient ones Isaiah cites there. Therefore.

Argument 9. If Christ and the apostles always confirmed their doctrine from
Scripture and never from traditions, and taught nothing except what was in the
Scriptures of the Old Testament, then much less is it now permissible under
the New Testament to teach anything beyond what is contained in the
Scriptures, and Scripture contains perfect doctrine. The former is true in
Luke 24:25, 27, 44–45; Acts 15:11, 17; 6:17; 10:18; 28:24; 14:26, 23; Romans
1:2; and elsewhere.

Argument 10. Christ and the apostles preached the perfect doctrine of
salvation (Rom. 15:4); otherwise they would not have been faithful in their
office, contrary to the apostle in Hebrews 3:2. But what they preached was
also written insofar as it is expedient for salvation (Luke 1:1; Acts 1:1–2;
1 John 1:1, 3–4, where he speaks of himself and other sacred writers).
Therefore.

Argument 11. Those who establish dogmas of faith from unwritten traditions
think above and beyond what is written. But no believer ought to think above
what is written (Rom. 12:3; 1 Cor. 4:6). Therefore, no believer ought to
establish dogmas of faith from traditions.

Argument 12. If faith is conceived and perfected by hearing God’s word, then
everything necessary for faith is contained in the word. The former is true
in Romans 10:17, where “word” signifies Scripture, as in 1 Peter 1, the last
verse, and Matthew 5:18 and 24:35. But all Christ’s sayings necessary for us
have been written. Basil therefore rightly says, “Whatever is outside the
divinely in—

[1620 Tome I | col. 131 | BSB frame 00080]

—spired Scriptures is sin, because it is not from faith.” Therefore.

Argument 13. The church is built upon the doctrine of the prophets and
apostles (Eph. 2:19–20), which contains everything necessary for salvation. He
names the prophets and apostles, according to Thomas in lecture 6 on
Ephesians 2, because the apostles preached as accomplished facts what the
prophets foretold as future. But they foretold the things necessary for
salvation. Furthermore, according to Chrysostom, they wrote what they
preached. Therefore, if the church rests only upon written prophetic doctrine,
since they can produce no unwritten doctrine of the prophets, it must also
rest only upon the written doctrine of the apostles, and consequently there
is no need for traditions.

Argument 14. If in this dark life no other light is shown to us by which we
are led through the darkness of this life to eternal light except that of the
Scriptures, then we ought to rest in the Scriptures alone, leaving traditions
behind. The former is true in 2 Peter 1:19, where *ho logos* (ὁ λόγος) is taken
for Scripture, as it often is; see Luke 3:4 and Acts 1:13, 21, where the voices
of the prophets are said to be read.

Argument 15. A testament perfectly contains the testator’s will, even among
human beings, so that nothing may be added or subtracted (Gal. 3:15; Heb.
9:16–17). But Scripture is called God’s testament and covenant, as its very
title proves and as the apostle teaches in 2 Corinthians 3:14, and Christ
confirmed it by his blood.

Argument 16. Scripture is called canonical by everyone. According to
Augustine in book 19, chapter 18 of *The City of God*, it is called this for
no other reason than that it prescribes the rule of faith and life. But a rule
must be commensurate with what it regulates. Therefore.

Argument 17. That which has all its essential and integral parts is perfect.
Such is Scripture, because it has all its essential parts, namely

[1620 Tome I | col. 132 | BSB frame 00080]

matter and form, and its integral parts, namely law and gospel. Furthermore,
“whole” and “perfect” are convertible, while a whole is either essential or
integral; Scripture has both kinds of whole.

Argument 18. If Scripture is imperfect, this is either because God was
unwilling to reveal everything necessary for salvation or because he was
unable to do so. It is not the latter, because that is impious to say; nor is
it the former, because one who wills the end wills the means. But God wills
the end, that is, human salvation. Therefore, he wills the means, among which
the perfect revelation of the divine will is chief. That the Holy Spirit
willed to prescribe everything necessary is evident from John 20:31; Romans
15:4; 2 Timothy 3:6; and elsewhere.

Argument 19. If the apostles, led into all truth by the Holy Spirit as the
church’s public notaries and recorders, did not write certain things necessary
for salvation, this occurred either because they ought not to have done so or
because they could not. All these things, however, are utterly absurd.
Therefore, if they wrote everything faithfully, we must rest faithfully in
their writings.

Argument 20. If the books of the Old Testament perfectly contain everything
that pertained to the Old Testament, then the New Testament also contains
everything that pertains to the New Testament—unless they should say that the
Old Testament is more perfect than the New, which is absurd and contrary to
the apostle in Hebrews 1:1. The former is true (Deut. 29:21; Rom. 15:4; 2 Cor.
3:14). Therefore.

Argument 21. Unwritten traditions cannot be preserved uncorrupted for a long
time, even though God preserves the church forever according to his promises,
because he did not promise this concerning traditions. Even under the Old
Testament before Moses, because of various corruptions and corrupted divine
traditions, he often appeared to the Fathers and revealed and renewed his
will in vari—

[1620 Tome I | col. 133 | BSB frame 00081]

—ous ways, although the church then existed in only a few families. Finally,
because he saw that the church and religion could not long be preserved
without writing, he recorded his will through Moses in literary monuments.
Therefore.

Argument 22. There is no necessary observance of something nonexistent. But
there are no apostolic traditions outside Scripture. For how is it established
that they exist? Who received them? Who was their keeper? If a keeper of them
is granted, then either he was one person or there were several. If several,
perhaps they were the Fathers. But the Fathers disagree with one another,
because some affirm that a thing is an apostolic tradition while others deny
it.

The rule of faith, however, must be certain. If the keeper was one person,
perhaps it was the pope. But how did he preserve these traditions? Surely
either in a book or in his mind. Not in a book, because no one has ever had
such a book. If in his mind, then at the pope’s death the traditions would
perish, unless they establish a transmission of souls between popes, because
the pope has no different mind after his election than he had before.

Furthermore, things not written down easily perish; see Isaiah 30:8; Job
19:23; Psalm 102:19; Exodus 17:14; Philippians 3:1. Finally, if God willed the
traditions to be written, he certainly willed them to be written by the
apostles and evangelists themselves, than whom no one was more fit to write,
and not by Dionysius, Irenaeus, or others—unless they wish to ascribe to these
men no less credibility than to the apostles.

Argument 22.[^1] The ancient heretics, according to Irenaeus in chapters 23
and 24 concerning the Basilidians, Tertullian in his book *On Prescriptions*
concerning the Carpocratians, and other Fathers, charged Scripture with
imperfection. They asserted that the apostles handed down certain things to
certain people apart from Scripture. Therefore, this opinion of the papists
revives heresies long since put to rest.

[1620 Tome I | col. 134 | BSB frame 00081]

Argument 24. Papist traditions are superstitious, uncertain, ridiculous, and
conflict with written apostolic rules, as can be proved by induction.
Therefore, they are not necessary or apostolic, but must be rejected.

TESTIMONIES OF THE FATHERS

The Fathers agree with us. Irenaeus, book 3, chapter 1: “We learned the
arrangement of our salvation from no others than those through whom the gospel
came to us. What they then preached, they afterward, by God’s will, handed
down to us in the Scriptures to be the foundation and pillar of our faith.”

The same writer, chapter 47: “We ought to believe, knowing most rightly that
the Scriptures are indeed perfect, since they were spoken by the Word of God
and his Spirit.”

Tertullian, in his book *Against Hermogenes*: “I adore the fullness of
Scripture, which reveals to me the Maker and the things made,” and so forth.
The same writer, in *On the Flesh of Christ*: “Nothing should be received
which does not have the testimony of Scripture.”

Theodoret, in the second dialogue against the heretics: “I do not dare to say
anything about which divine Scripture was silent.”

Damascene, book 1, chapter 1 of *The Orthodox Faith*: “We receive everything
handed down through the prophets and apostles, seeking nothing else beyond
these things.”

Chrysostom, Homily 58 on John 10: “He who does not use sacred Scripture, but
climbs up from elsewhere—that is, by an unauthorized way—is a thief.”

The same writer, Homily 9 on the Epistle to the Colossians: “Do not wait for
another teacher. You have the words of God. No one can teach you in this way.”

Cyril, in his book *On Faith*: “It is necessary for us to follow the divine
writings and depart in no respect from their prescriptions.”

[1620 Tome I | col. 135 | BSB frame 00082]

Basil the Great, in his book *On the Confession of Faith*: “It is manifestly
a crime of unbelief and arrogance either to reject something which is written
or to add something which is not written.”

Athanasius, in his book *Against the Nations*: “Let the Scriptures suffice
for every instruction or training in the truth.”

Theophilus of Alexandria, in his second Paschal letter: “It belongs to a
diabolical spirit to think anything divine beyond the authority of the sacred
Scriptures.”

Augustine, book 2, chapter 9 of *On Christian Doctrine*: “Among the things
plainly set down in the Scriptures are found all those things which contain
faith and the morals by which one should live.”

The same writer, book 3, chapter 14 of *Against Maximinus the Arian*: “Neither
ought I to produce the Council of Nicaea, nor you that of Ariminum, as though
to prejudge the matter. Neither am I bound by the authority of the former, nor
you by that of the latter. Let thing contend with thing, cause with cause, and
reason with reason by the authorities of the Scriptures—witnesses common to
us both, and not peculiar to either party.”

The same writer, book *On Nature and Grace*, chapter 61: “I owe unreserved
assent to the canonical Scriptures alone.”

The scholastics and the papists themselves teach the same thing with us.
Thomas, commenting on 2 Timothy 3, says that the Scriptures make the man of
God perfect. Scotus, in question 3 of the prologue to the *Sentences*, says
that our theology has its limit from the will of God who reveals and
sufficiently contains the doctrine necessary for the traveler. Anthony, part
3, title 18, chapter 3, says, “The appropriate matter of preaching is sound
Scripture.” It speaks so copiously there, as Gregory expounds in book 22 of
the *Moralia* from Job 22, that God need not speak again anything necessary
for us, since everything is contained there. Hence Lyrinensis, chapter 2 of
his little book: “The canon of the Scriptures alone is more than sufficient
for everything”; and chapter 41: “The canon of Scripture alone is sufficient
for itself in all things.” The ordinary interlinear gloss on John 16:12, a
passage the papists cite for traditions,

[1620 Tome I | col. 136 | BSB frame 00082]

says that no one has authority to determine those many things which Christ
then withheld.
