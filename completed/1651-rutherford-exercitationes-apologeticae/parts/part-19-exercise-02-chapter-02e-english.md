It is proved that God cannot fail of his intended end. 1. Our God is in the
heavens and does whatever he wills. Therefore he does not fall short of the
end he wills. Schaafmann replies that God does whatever he wills by the
absolute will by which he created the world, but not by his conditional will,
for the latter is not armed with omnipotence.

[Marginalia: *Schaaf. de præd.* 1. p. 1. l. cap. 9.]

Reply. 1. By the will through which he causes the church freely to wage war
and rout its enemies by a free act, God does what he wills. For Psalm 115 is
the church's prayer of triumph in God concerning the idols of the nations;
yet in Schaafmann's view such a will is conditional. His distinction is
therefore worthless. Who does not know that in that psalm the church gives
thanks to God for victories obtained through the ministry of causes acting
contingently, as is evident from verse 2? 2. This answer does not distinguish
God from the nations' idols, which those nations believed did whatever they
willed conditionally, provided perhaps that God did not oppose them. 3. Psalm
135. This is given as a mark of divinity for which God is to be extolled with
praises: namely, that he does whatever he wills in heaven, sea, and earth. But
insofar as he does not do what he wills on earth, to that extent he is not
omnipotent, not God, and not to be extolled with praises. What could be more
absurd? Daniel 4:35: “He does whatever he wills among the host of heaven and
the”

[1651 logical printed p. 272 | IA leaf 290 | PDF p. 290]

“inhabitants of the earth, and no one can resist his hand.” The Arminians loudly claim
that free agents who inhabit the earth resist God's will, but that he effects
nothing through free agents by an absolute will. That passage proclaims the
contrary. For 1. in verse 25 God gives the kingdoms of men to whomever he
wills; but there he gives them through soldiers, commanders, and princes who
fight most freely and seize kingdoms. In this God's invincible dominion over
men shines forth, which that Babylonian tyrant refused to acknowledge. 2. In
verses 24 and 36 he attributes it to God's absolute will—not to the
conditional will, which can be resisted—that his nobles sought him out and
honored him in the customary ways. Yet the nobles honored the tyrant again
not necessarily but most freely. 3. He had been raised to the throne again
through wars most freely fought, as the histories establish. But in verse 37,
for this reason he extols the God of heaven with praises for the invincible
omnipotence by which he casts down the proud. Therefore God accomplished
whatever he willed through free agents, and there is no free agent capable of
struggling against his power. The same thing is proved most clearly by Isaiah
14:24: “As I have decreed, so shall it be.” There he proves from an invincible
decree that the Medes will destroy the Chaldeans, so that the distinction
between absolute and conditional will is dispersed by the text. For the Medes
laid waste the Chaldeans most freely, and yet it was done by the will of God,
which cannot be resisted. Otherwise God would swear there to what is false,
which is blasphemous.

[1651 logical printed p. 273 | IA leaf 291 | PDF p. 291]

15. 2. Whenever the Word is preached, it accomplishes what God intends, for it
cannot return empty to God, Isaiah 55:11. Therefore if God intended the
reprobate's obedience through the Word, they would undoubtedly obey. Thus God
cannot fail of the end intended through the Word. And in Ezekiel 2:7 God
plainly says that the Israelites will not obey, yet he wills the prophet to
speak. It is as if God were to say there: “I know they will not obey; yet I
will and intend that they obey.” But let the reader hear the Remonstrants'
astonishing commentary. They maintain that after God has failed of his end,
the hypothetical intention nevertheless remains in God. What a prodigy! Even
today God has a conditional intention to save Judas, and he wills Pharaoh's
obedience—by an optative intention, of course—even after Pharaoh has been
condemned to eternal punishments.

[Marginalia: *Apolog.* c. 17. fol. 161.]

16. 3. That which Scripture presents as a grave judgment and curse cannot be
attributed to God. But failure in counsel and in an intended end is the curse
of the reprobate, Psalm 2:4; Psalm 33:10; Isaiah 8:9; Isaiah 29:7; Jeremiah
19:7; Job 5:12–13. Otherwise they could return on earth, in kind, God's mockery
as he laughs in heaven at men's vain and ineffectual counsels.

4. The contrary of that which is a mark of divine perfection cannot be
ascribed to God. But attaining an intended end is such a mark. Therefore,

[1651 logical printed p. 274 | IA leaf 292 | PDF p. 292]

etc. The minor premise is Job 23:13: “God does one thing; who can turn him
back? What his soul desires, that he accomplishes.” Will God's soul, then,
earnestly desire the reprobate's obedience and not accomplish it? The
distinction assigned by Scripture in Psalm 33:11 would thus perish: human
counsels can be broken, but God's counsels stand through every age.

5. What is granted to men as part of blessedness and includes no imperfection
cannot be taken away from God. Attaining an intended end is such a thing.
Therefore. The minor premise is Job 22:28: “When first you decree a matter, it
will be established for you.” But, says Corvinus, “God is frustrated in his
desires not from a defect of power, but from the manner and limit which wisdom
has placed upon the exercise of that power, because he does not desire as one
in need of something. And so, if the desire were fulfilled, it would
contribute nothing to God's blessedness. In the same place, he could, if he
willed, fulfill those desires.”

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 5, § 8.]

[Marginalia: See Vázquez, vol. 1, disputation 87, ch. 4.]

Reply. 1. It does not arise from God's wisdom that God fails of his end. For
he does not intend the end from knowledge; therefore neither does he intend
it from wisdom. But it is vain to invent wisdom without knowledge. 2. I ask
the Arminians whether anything would be lost from divine blessedness if God
failed of an absolutely intended end. I believe they would say no. When he
intends the creation of the world, he does not do so as one in need of
something; nevertheless, if God failed in his absolute intention, he would
not be God. 3. It belongs to God's infinite perfection that his will can no
more be directed

[1651 logical printed p. 275 | IA leaf 293 | PDF p. 293]

to an object which it could not effect than his intellect can be directed to
what is false. 4. Creatures are often not always happier from the ends they attain. Indeed,
the wicked are the more miserable because they put wicked counsels into
execution. Nevertheless, because they fall short of their end, they show that
they are not self-sufficient, something that cannot be said of God. His
volitions are lawful.

4. If God could fulfill his desires, why does he not fulfill them? Wisdom and
justice, of course, protest and stand in the way. Away with a power in God to
do anything contrary to wisdom and justice, as though he could do something
unjust. Such a power is true impotence and transforms God into a non-God. It
is therefore impossible for God to save the reprobate.

2. Corvinus's reply. “If God's desires are considered insofar as they are
moderated by justice, and each desire and each will is evaluated from its
adequate object, then certainly, insofar as God desires the salvation of men,
to that extent the desire is fulfilled—namely, because he does not desire it
except insofar as justice permits it. But when we say that it is not
fulfilled, then we consider it absolutely, namely, insofar as it belongs
simply to divine goodness, insofar as those whom God would have saved are
often damned.”

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 5, § 2.]

Reply. 1. If God's desires are regarded as modified by justice, they are
always fulfilled. I take that premise. But according to Arminius, Public
Disputation 4, theses 73–74, and Corvinus, God's justice always moderates in
use and exercise God's natural desires for the reprobate's obedience and
salvation.

[1651 logical printed p. 276 | IA leaf 294 | PDF p. 294]

Therefore, I say, he wills the reprobate's obedience and salvation only by an
inclination or power tending toward the love of justice. But by the natural
inclination of justice he wills, by the same reasoning, the reprobate's
disobedience and destruction. 2. The Arminians say that the Pharisees made
God's counsel ineffectual, Luke 7. The question is whether they rejected God's
counsel in act and exercise or not. If the former, God's counsel that the
Pharisees should obey and be saved was not modified by justice. For thus he
wills only the obedience and salvation of obedient Pharisees. If they
rejected God's counsel insofar as it is devoid of all justice, then, since
they rejected the gospel—which is God's decree concerning the salvation of
believers and damnation of unbelievers—it follows that the gospel is devoid
of all God's wisdom and justice. What could be more absurd? For the gospel
contains the unsearchable riches of Christ and God's manifold wisdom,
Ephesians 3:10, and declares God's justice to us, Romans 3:21–23. Romans 7:12:
the law is holy, and the commandment holy, just, and good. 3. The reprobate
contravene the act of God's will in use and exercise. But thus God's will, as
determined by God's wisdom and justice, wills only the obedience and salvation
of the elect. There is therefore no will in God which the reprobate contravene.
4. I ask when God's desire is not fulfilled. It is, of course, when it is
devoid of wisdom. But then the reprobate sin not against God's wisdom and
justice, but only

[1651 logical printed p. 277 | IA leaf 295 | PDF p. 295]

against God's natural desire and inclination—one devoid, that is, of cognition. But the reprobate sin
gravely against all God's attributes, especially against his wisdom, Psalm
50:21, and his justice and forbearance, Romans 2:4–5. God in exercise wills
men to obey and be saved only insofar as justice permits. I take that premise.
But according to Arminius, justice permits only the obedient to be saved.
Therefore God does not intend and will the reprobate's obedience. 6. Men
contravene God's antecedent will, not simply, but insofar as it exists in the
Arminian brain; and thus all is well.

17. 7. God does not intend the obedience of at least some of the reprobate.
Therefore, by parity of reasoning, he does not intend the obedience of them
all. The antecedent is proved. After God had foretold that Absalom would defile
his father's bed and that Hazael would rip open Israel's pregnant women, God
could not, through the law of nature or the Word spoken to them, intend that
they obey. For in that case God would both intend David's chastisement through
Absalom's incest and Israel's through Hazael's barbarous and inhuman cruelty,
and he would not intend it but would intend that Absalom and Hazael obey by
abstaining from those crimes. Thus there would be two contradictory intentions
in God, which is absurd. Further, he does not always intend even the elect's
obedience. For after it had been foretold through Zechariah, chapter 13, that
the disciples would deny and forsake the Savior (for this was part of the
Savior's passion—that he should be forsaken by his own—

[Marginalia: 2 Kings 2. 12. 14.]

[1651 logical printed p. 278 | IA leaf 296 | PDF p. 296]

and it was foretold and decreed by God), who would believe that God intended
the disciples to confess him steadfastly before men? For then God would have
intended both that they should not adhere to the Savior—which is
contradictory—and that they should adhere to him.

18. 8. What God foreknows will never occur, he does not intend, for no one
would regard such an intention as wise. But God foreknows that the reprobate
will never obey. Therefore. Arminius answers that “God does not always intend
an end for himself from foreknowledge, but only,” says Corvinus, “from natural
affection and goodness.”

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 667.]

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Molina*, ch. 5, § 7.]

Reply. 1. If God has nothing certain concerning the futurition of those who
are called, without any act of providence, will, or knowledge, all reprobate
men and the spirits of darkness tumble into sin and eternal destruction. Thus
providence, which attends to hairs falling from the head and the flutterings
of the most wanton sparrows, neither foreknows nor attends to the falls of so
many most noble creatures. Indeed, Arminius the Anti-Perkinsian himself says
that “sufficient grace is employed by a decree of providence, with certain
foreknowledge that it will be rejected by man's free choice,” and he says that
this opinion is Augustine's and perhaps also Scripture's.

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, p. 665.]

But sufficient grace in the visible church is not to be separated from the
Word; indeed, if the Arminians are candid, it is only the Word itself. And what
sort of God is the Arminian God—an anthropomorphic one, or an Epicurean one,
who scatters the seed of the Word with no providence? Until now all theologians

[1651 logical printed p. 279 | IA leaf 297 | PDF p. 297]

have always believed that God does nothing except by certain providence. But this seems to me to be
a mystery: God foreknows that it is possible for the reprobate who are called
to obey or not to obey, because foreknowledge and antecedent will alike
destroy human liberty. Therefore they deem it the safest course here to bury
God's foreknowledge. But this is certainly Cicero's gospel, not Christ's. 2.
Intention is a volition of an end. Yet Arminius admits that God wills nothing
except what he knows. Therefore he cannot will the reprobate's obedience
unless he knows it. But then this divine volition will be a brute appetite and
worse than a brute's, like the appetite by which a stone inclines toward the
center without any cognition. Therefore that creator who fashioned soul and
reason in man will himself be devoid of reason—I shudder to report it. But,
fool, will he who formed the eye not himself see? Psalm 94:3.

[Marginalia: *Public Disputation* 4, thesis 49.]

3. Arminius himself subjects the whole army of affections in God to wisdom,
whose office, he says, is to prescribe for each its object, manner, end, and
circumstances.

[Marginalia: Arminius, disputation 4, thesis 73.]

Therefore wisdom prescribes the manner, object, and end for the intention by
which God wills the reprobate's obedience with a natural affection. “Indeed,”
the Davus-like slaves say, “wisdom prescribes the end, not knowledge.” O
unwise wisdom, devoid of knowledge! 4. In his cautious fashion he says that
God does “not always” intend the end according to foreknowledge. But tell me,
Jacob, upon your conscience: why does God intend an end in natural things from

[1651 logical printed p. 280 | IA leaf 298 | PDF p. 298]

foreknowledge? It is evidently because you believe that God does not foreknow
future contingents. 5. Perhaps he says that God does not always intend the end
from foreknowledge because he calls the elect according to the decree of
predestination, by which he foreknows that they will obey, but does not call
the reprobate in this way. Yet what explains such disparity between their
callings? It can happen that, with respect to their present condition, the
elect and reprobate are equally good and compliant. There is no cause here
unless he resorts to the absolute decree of predestination to find the
difference. But this displeases Jacob Arminius like an Epidaurian snake or
serpent. 6. Scripture says that the gospel was set before the Corinthians,
with God foreknowing that an unconquerable people would be gathered to God;
and before the Gentiles in Acts 13, with God foretelling, according to Isaiah's
prophecies, that their eyes would be opened; and before the Israelites, with
God foreknowing that they would not hear, Ezekiel 2. God's foreknowledge must
therefore accompany his intention here. I know they say that God foreknows the
reprobate's disobedience but does not intend it according to foreknowledge.
But give me the reason why he would not intend according to foreknowledge.
Here they will stick fast. And when the Savior says that someone intending a
building or a war who is ignorant of his own resources is considered rash and
imprudent by everyone, he will hardly be excused if he says that he intended
it not according to foreknowledge of the outcome but from benevolent
affection. How, then, will they excuse God here, who intends the

[1651 logical printed p. 281 | IA leaf 299 | PDF p. 299]

building of the reprobate's souls in Christ without foreknowledge of the outcome?
But fearful are God's judgments upon men struck with a spirit of stupor who
refuse to yield to God's truth.

19. Arminius gives another answer here: “God does not fail of every end in
this matter. For he satisfies his goodness and love toward those who are
called.”

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Antiperkins*, same place.]

And Corvinus says that “God does not precisely intend the reprobate's obedience
and salvation, but either the manifestation of the glory of justice or
salvation.”

[Marginalia: Corvinus, *Against Tilenus*, ch. 12, p. 488.]

Reply. Those who would have God be not altogether imperfect and incapable of
obtaining his wishes, but only imperfect in a certain respect, defend God's
perfection splendidly indeed! 2. Granted and conceded that the satisfaction
of divine love is God's end, it will only be a general and common end that
regards the reprobate and elect alike. But the question is for what special
end God calls reprobates who will not obey. Thus the answer is beside the
point. 3. How God satisfies his goodness here is hardly easy for the reader to
divine. For Arminius thinks that a good is to be evaluated from the mind of
the giver and from the end for which it is given. But in exhortation God
intends no good except salvation, and he fails of this end. For it is contrary
to reason and opposed to God's goodness that God should intend the reprobate's
salvation through a calling which he foreknows will always be ineffectual, and
should not intend it through a calling which, with all God's attributes
intact,

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Declaration of His Opinion*, p. 107.]

[1651 logical printed p. 282 | IA leaf 300 | PDF p. 300]

would infallibly bring about their salvation. Indeed, if God intends and wills
their salvation in this way, he would satisfy
not his goodness but his palpable hypocrisy. For one who intends an end by
means which he knows to be unequal and unsuited to the end, and does not
intend it by means which he knows to be so fitted to the end that they would
infallibly produce it, has a hypocritical intention. 4. He would have God
satisfy his love and goodness here because, it seems, he cannot be good unless
he calls everyone to obedience and salvation. Therefore, insofar as he does
not call the spirits of darkness, God is evil and hardly loves his creatures.
How absurd! 5. They might say that the exhortation itself is a benefit. We
grant this most freely. Come, then: when God bestows upon the stubborn and
defiant the benefit of a calling which they have frequently spurned and
treated with utter contempt, to the end that they may be punished more
severely—as the Arminians teach—the calling from God's intention will be the
greatest evil, not a good. For Arminius would have a good evaluated from the
giver's mind and the end for which it is given. But, as they teach, God's most
just mind is ill-disposed toward the defiant, and he calls them so that they
may be punished more severely, which is the greatest evil to them. Now tell
me, Jacob, how God satisfies his goodness and love. 6. An exhortation makes
the good offered merely possible for the reprobate. Thus by exhorting the
reprobate, God offers a potential good which, according to God's foreknowledge,

[Marginalia: Arminius, *Declaration of His Opinion*, p. 107, Bern edition.]

[1651 logical printed p. 283 | IA leaf 301 | PDF p. 301]

will issue in more severe punishments. And so he brings about an actual evil.
This, it seems, is how God satisfies
his goodness and love. 7. But I ask which goodness God satisfies: goodness
bestowed upon creatures, or God's natural goodness within himself? If the
former, he still has not satisfied such goodness, for no good is conferred
upon the reprobate; indeed, they are entangled in the gravest miseries by this
good. If the latter, God's natural goodness within himself cannot be satisfied
at all, since nothing is added or taken away from him, and no one sows or
reaps for him, even if he should call no mortal to blessedness. 8. In the word
“precisely,” Corvinus acts the consummate impostor. For by “precisely” he does
not mean intensely and vehemently. For why should God's desires for the
reprobate's obedience and salvation be less intense than his desires for the
elect's obedience and salvation? They will not have the cause sought in God's
predestination, yet it can be nowhere else. If by “precisely” he means
absolutely, then God's intention concerning the reprobate's obedience and
salvation is conditional. But on what condition does he intend them to obey?
On the hypothesis that they will, that is, on the hypothesis that they obey.
Behold the trifles he dictates! But if the Pharisees rejected God's
conditional counsel, it equally supposes that the Pharisees will not obey and
that they will obey, and so it is not despised. Indeed, if God intends to damn
the Pharisees if they do not obey, then by not obeying just as much as by
obeying they despise God's counsel. What could be

[1651 logical printed p. 284 | IA leaf 302 | PDF p. 302]

more absurd? In this way all the elect despise God's counsel by obeying. 9. But by
the word “precisely” he means that God, in a suspended manner, intends either
obedience if they obey, or disobedience if they do not obey, and so intends
either the glory of his justice or of his mercy. But I ask why God proposes
both ends indeterminately. It is because he sees that he will not definitely
attain one or the other, so to speak. But if he sees this, he conjectures and
divines that perhaps they will not obey. He therefore knows, at least by
conjectural knowledge, that he will not attain his end; hence he does not
intend the end from non-foreknowledge. 10. If he is ignorant of the intended
end, which is blasphemous, why does he intend contrary ends under a
disjunction? Here they must be caught. For God would thus precisely intend one
end only if he doubted its attainment; but if he does not doubt, he cannot
propose either one to himself under a disjunction. 11. It is absurd for God to
waver between two contrary ends, for his will is infinite and determined by
no creature. Aquinas says: “If preparation for grace is considered insofar as
it is from God moving, then it has a necessity for that to which it is moved,
not a necessity of coercion but of infallibility, by which God's intention
cannot fail.”

[Marginalia: Aquinas, 22, question 112, article 3.]

Suárez says: “If the preparation of means is confused and under a
disjunction—so that if one means has not had its effect, another is
applied—it does not agree with divine perfection.” So also

[Marginalia: Suárez, book 1, *On Predestination*, ch. 13, § 5.]

[1651 logical printed p. 285 | IA leaf 303 | PDF p. 303]

Gregory of Valencia: “What God once wills, he always wills, from eternity to
eternity.” And Peter Fonseca: “God's will, taken in particular together with
all the necessary circumstances concerning any particular thing, is not
suspended and indeterminate.”

[Marginalia: Valencia, vol. 1, disputation 1, question 19, point 4.]

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 7, ch. 5, question 3, § 7.]

20. Arminius maintains, however, that God's end in calling the reprobate,
namely their inexcusability, is not an end in itself. Why? “Because,” he says,
“exhortation does not render them inexcusable unless it is despised and
rejected. But this comes upon the exhortation from the wickedness of those
called, and God does not intend that end except insofar as he also foreknows
that the admonition will be ineffectual through the wickedness of those
called.” Reply. 1. I take that premise. But obedience also comes upon the
exhortation, for it is extrinsic to the exhortation's nature. The nature of an
exhortation is preserved whether men obey or not. Therefore will it not follow
very well from Arminius that even the obedience of those called is not God's
end in itself but an accidental end, so that disobedience will be an end in
itself? This is absurd. 2. Arminius would have the intention of obedience
included in the essential concept of an exhortation, than which nothing is
more false. For then, by commanding Isaac's sacrifice, God would have intended
the sacrifice. Who does not see how false this is? 3. Exhortation itself, in
and by its own nature, removes an excuse and the ground of the excuse—namely,
“I could not obey a gospel which was never revealed”—whether the man obeys or
not, just as non-revelation in itself excuses the unbeliever. But the obedience
or disobedience of the person called adds nothing to and takes nothing from

[1651 logical printed p. 286 | IA leaf 304 | PDF p. 304]

the essence of inexcusability. 4. God, he says, foreknowing that the admonition
will be ineffectual, intends inexcusability. Therefore, I say, God's
exhortation presents God as willing the reprobate's salvation, and yet he does
not will their salvation but intends their inexcusability. 5. Why does God
call some according to foreknowledge and others not according to
foreknowledge? Is the cause in God's absolute will? Or, by parity of reasoning,
does he call those who will obey while ignorant even of their future
obedience? Thus there will be a calling in God according to no foreknowledge.
6. Although inexcusability and damnation are not twin ends with respect to the
Word, which of itself urges obedience and offers salvation, nevertheless to
God, to whom nothing happens outside providence, no outcome is accidental.
Those who teach this sin not lightly against God's providence. 7. Arminius
would have the ground of inexcusability be the presentation of the Word and a
sufficient power to obey. Therefore God always intends the reprobate's
inexcusability—not at the fourth or tenth admonition, but at the first—which
Arminius will not tolerate. 8. That the reprobate should be inexcusable is a
good. It is therefore intended by God, who is the first efficient and ultimate
end of every good. But they say it is not a good for the creature. What does
that matter? God intends everything that conduces to the manifestation of his
glory. He does not measure the good which he effects or intends from its
agreement or disagreement with the nature of

[1651 logical printed p. 287 | IA leaf 305 | PDF p. 305]

creatures, but from whether or not it is directed to the ultimate end. The
Arminians, however, contend with the earlier Anabaptists that God wills the
creature's good by an impulse of nature, just as a raven loves its chicks and
a mother her offspring. This is utterly false. For God wills the creature's
good freely. If God willed the creature's good naturally, he could not permit
sin, the surest inescapable road to the creature's destruction, just as no
mother who knew and willed that an infant was rushing to destruction would
permit it if she could prevent it consistently with the right of her office.
But people err shamefully whenever puny mortals presume boldly to measure God
by a human foot.
