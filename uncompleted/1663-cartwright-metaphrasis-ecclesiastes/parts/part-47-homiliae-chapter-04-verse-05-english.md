VERSE 5. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward from their
labor.

Taking his reason and occasion from the solitude of the avaricious or
tenacious man and his lack of blood relations or relatives by marriage,
Solomon begins a discourse on men's association and union with one another.
He declares not merely that this society has a reward, but that it is singular
and excellent, so that he may incite men to pursue and embrace it. The love
both of entering and of preserving this society is implanted and innate by a
certain impulse and instinct of nature. Thus, to flee from this society or not
to cultivate it is something beastlike and abhorrent to human nature.

Indeed, most brute animals place the sweetness and delight of their life in
grazing together in the same field and lying and spending the night in the
same lair. Hence the Greeks also call them “gregarious,” so that the condition
of those who pursue solitude is worse than that of wild beasts. The Holy
Spirit confirms this instinct of nature through the prophet, so that those
who flee human society may rightly be seen, in the manner of the giants, to
fight both against nature and against God, who is the author of uncorrupted
nature.

Solomon specifically charges the miser, who seeks solitude like a wild ass,
with this crime of hatred for mankind. Yet the charge can no less truly or
fittingly be transferred to Roman monks, and especially to anchorites. Indeed,
they are guilty of this crime to a far greater degree because they openly
profess this solitude in hatred of human society and, what is still worse,
place great merit in professing it. Although these shunners of light represent
themselves as the light and salt of the world, they nevertheless hide
themselves in their cloisters as though in darkness,

evidently so that they may contract no stain or corruption of morals from the
common people and the mass of mankind. Yet it would be their duty to come out
in public, illuminate others with the light they advertise, and season with
their salt the tasteless morals of the people. Who lights a lamp in order to
enclose it beneath a bushel or table, instead of setting it upon a table or in
some elevated place from which it may shine upon everyone in the house? And
what use is salt unless, placed and applied to meat, it preserves the meat
from putrefaction? Away, therefore, with this monasticism from the Church of
Christ, and with anchoritism as well.

Nor should it be passed over that Solomon says two are better than one even
if they are poor, weak, and subject to other infirmities. Their condition is
nevertheless preferable to that of one robust man.

He confirms this from the reward attached to such society. There is indeed
benefit and reward in every act of obedience. Psalm 19. Even if there were
none, however, God's authority and command alone ought to suffice for our
prompt and cheerful obedience, much more when reward and recompense follow.
He next notes the object in which this reward or benefit consists: in the
mutual labor of those who associate and in things acquired by labor. It is
well known that those joined in fellowship in one labor undertake it more
cheerfully, evade its weariness to some degree, and strive in a certain
blameless rivalry for each to surpass the other by laboring vigorously.

He understands especially that things acquired by labor are preserved by the
benefit of society and that their use is pleasantly enjoyed and their fruit
conveniently received. Whatever this entails, however, will be understood
more fully from the explanation of its parts.

Society and union among men are of great use and benefit in civil affairs,
but are all the more necessary in spiritual and ecclesiastical life, inasmuch
as the loss of the future life and the inheritance of the kingdom of heaven
is far more grievous than the loss of this life and the resources directed to
preserving it. On this matter, see Hebrews 10:24–25.

*Translator's notes:*

1. The source marker discloses the p. 186 catchword `scilicet`; the paired
   texts complete it after the marker without repeating the catchword.
2. `Ἀγελαῖα` means herd-forming or gregarious creatures; `μισανθρωπίας` is
   hatred of mankind; and `ἀναχωρητὰς` denotes anchorites or religious
   recluses. All three Greek expressions are retained in the Latin.
3. Cartwright distinguishes `Monachismus` from `Anachorismus`, here rendered
   “monasticism” and “anchoritism.” `Lucifugæ` literally calls the recluses
   shunners of light.
4. The Latin retains the compressed `non tantum Salomon & mercedem esse`,
   `labore partis`, and `quoad ... jactura` constructions. The English conveys
   their contextual sense without silently altering the transcription.

   on p. 188 are outside this unit.
