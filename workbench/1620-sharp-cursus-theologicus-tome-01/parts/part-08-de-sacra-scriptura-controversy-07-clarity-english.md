Seventh Controversy: The Clarity of Holy Scripture

The papists either do not understand our position in this controversy or are
unwilling to under—

[1620 Tome I | col. 70 | BSB frame 00049]

—stand it. For they say that we teach that everything in Scripture is so easy
and clear that nothing in it is difficult. Thus Eck, in topic 4 on Scripture
in his *Enchiridion*, attributes this position to us, and for that reason says
that laypeople and foolish old women handle it arrogantly.

From these things the position of certain papists is evident: laypeople are
to be kept away from reading the Scriptures because they are obscure and
cannot be understood by them.

We, however, say that they are not so difficult that they cannot be read
profitably by laypeople, even though they cannot be understood in every
respect.

The Rheims Jesuits say in their annotation on 2 Peter 3:16 that all Scripture
is difficult, but especially Paul’s epistles, although Peter says neither of
these things in that passage. He says only that certain things in those
epistles are difficult to understand. The same Jesuits, in their marginal
annotation on Luke 6:1, ascribe to us the position that everything in
Scripture is very easy.

Sixtus of Siena says in book 6, annotation 151 of his *Library* that we teach
that the Scriptures are by their nature so clear and luminous that any
unlearned little fellow can understand them unless some external impediment
stands in the way. In the topic on Scripture in his *Enchiridion of
Controversies*, Coster indeed admits that many things in the Scriptures are
clear, but adds that not everything is such that anyone can understand it
without labor. Yet we say this too.

Bellarmine, however, contends in book 3, chapter 1 of *The Word of God* that
many things in the Scriptures are obscure, and he frames the state of the
controversy as follows: whether the Scriptures are of themselves very easy
and very plain, or whether they need interpretation to settle controversies
of faith. Here he fights against his own shadow, for we defend the affirm—

[1620 Tome I | col. 71 | BSB frame 00050]

—ative side of this question, just as they do.

From this it is evident, first, that the papists themselves do not agree about
this matter. Some say that all Scripture is difficult, others only a part.
Some maintain that it is partly easy and partly difficult. It is evident,
second, that they do not frame the state of the controversy correctly.

Our position, however, is this. 1. Two things must be considered in Scripture:
first, its words and sentences, and then the doctrines themselves. There is
often difficulty in the words, but all the doctrines and articles of faith
are manifest in Scripture. 2. Scripture is considered in two ways: either in
itself or with respect to us. In itself Scripture is clear, but to us it is
partly difficult because of our blindness and ignorance (2 Cor. 3:14–16;
4:3), and partly easy, as the papists themselves also admit. 3. The doctrines
themselves are considered in two ways: either in themselves or as they are
set forth in Scripture. Considered in themselves, some are less clear, such
as the doctrine of the Trinity and the distinction of the persons, and so
forth. Others are clearer, such as the doctrines of one God, and of the life,
death, and resurrection of Christ, and so forth. Yet all the doctrines, as
they are set forth in Scripture and insofar as it is expedient for us to know
them, are manifest. The reason some things appear more obscure to us is that
we are not content with what is set forth in Scripture, but investigate many
things more curiously than is proper. 4. The clarity of Scripture with
respect to us is either internal or external. Internal clarity is the clarity
of the heart itself, which cannot exist without the illumination of the Holy
Spirit, for otherwise everything is obscure to the understanding of the
natural person (Ps. 14:1; 1 Cor. 2:14). This clarity belongs to faith, and
this knowledge is solid and saving, since sacred Scripture inwardly
illuminates our minds through the power of the Spirit. External clarity lies
in the words themselves. In this way all the doctrines of faith are plainly
ex—

[1620 Tome I | col. 72 | BSB frame 00050]

—pressed in Scripture, so that even the ungodly, and indeed demons, understand
the doctrines of faith from Scripture, though only in a historical manner.

Here one must also note the difference between theology and philosophy. Only
the external light of nature is required to learn philosophy, but the inward
illumination of the Holy Spirit is required for theology, and this makes the
Scriptures inwardly clear to us. We concede, however, that many things in
Scripture are obscure with respect to us. God willed this for the following
reasons. First, to stir us to diligent reading, meditation, searching, and
comparison of Scripture, and to keep us from being idle in searching the
mysteries of our salvation. 2. So that we might be constant in prayers,
seeking God’s help to understand them and the light of the Holy Spirit. 3. To
remove weariness from us, because we more often grow weary of easy things. 4.
So that truth might be held in greater value among us, since “fine things are
difficult.” 5. To subdue our arrogance and expose our ignorance, since we
sometimes boast and triumph greatly in our knowledge and in ourselves. 6. So
that the holy mysteries might be so many gems for the saints and not be cast
indiscriminately before swine. 7. To accustom us to a certain inward holiness
of thought, for those who bring impure minds to the reading of sacred
Scripture waste their effort and labor. 8. Because God willed that there be
teachers and disciples in the church, and therefore he willed that many things
be obscure so that their proper honor might be preserved for the Scriptures
and the sacred ministry. We therefore say that the Scriptures are so easy
that even the people can read them profitably, and that everything necessary
for salvation is delivered clearly in Scripture. Nevertheless, there are
many obscure passages, and so the Scriptures need explanation, and for this
reason pastors and teachers must be heard and consulted.

The state of the controversy, then, is this: Whether

[1620 Tome I | col. 73 | BSB frame 00051]

Scripture is so easy, plain, and clear that the people can read it profitably
and controversies concerning religion can be settled from it. The papists
deny this; we affirm it, relying on the following arguments.

1. Argument. What is not hidden or remote from us, but near, in our mouth and
heart, is not wholly obscure either in itself or to us, but is so clear that
the people can read it profitably and all controversies can be decided from
it. Scripture is such (Deut. 30:11; Rom. 10:6–8). Therefore. Bellarmine says
that Paul’s statement must be understood of ease in doing, not ease in
knowing, but this is futile, because what we can easily do we can know much
more easily. Nor is it less futile when he says that it must be understood
only of the law, since the apostle says this very thing about the gospel in
Romans 10:7. And if the law is easy, the histories of the Old Testament and
the Prophets are much more so, since they are commentaries on the law. The
gospel itself is also much easier, because it is the fulfillment of the law.
Therefore.

2. Argument. What is luminous, a lamp to our feet, a light for our path, and
makes a person wise for salvation is so clear both in itself and to us that
the people can read it profitably and every controversy can be settled from
it. Scripture is such (Ps. 19:9; 119:115; Prov. 6:20; 2 Tim. 3:15–17).
Therefore.

3. Argument. What is a lamp shining in a dark place in order to drive away
the darkness and show the way is so clear that the people can read it
profitably and controversies can be decided from it. Scripture is such (2
Pet. 1:19). Therefore.

4. Argument. What is not covered but open to the faithful is not wholly
obscure, but is so clear that it can be read profit—

[1620 Tome I | col. 74 | BSB frame 00051]

—ably by the common people and controversies can be decided from it.
Scripture is such (2 Cor. 4:2–4; Isa. 29:11; Rev. 5:5). Therefore.

5. Argument. What God commands us to hear in controversies and continually
wills everyone to search is not wholly difficult, but can be read profitably
and controversies can be decided from it. But he commands us to hear and
search Scripture (Isa. 8:20; John 5:30). Therefore.

6. Argument. Every controversy concerning matters that have the plainest and
clearest testimonies in Scripture can be clearly decided from Scripture. But
all the doctrines of faith, whose sum is contained in the Decalogue, the
Apostles’ Creed, the Lord’s Prayer, and the sacraments, have the plainest
testimonies of Scripture. Therefore.

It does not follow that clear testimonies concerning them do not exist
because some call them into controversy. For some people, whose minds the
devil has blinded, have raised and still raise controversies about the
clearest doctrines of faith.

7. Argument. The fathers proved their positions from the Scriptures.
Therefore, the Scriptures are clearer than even the writings of the fathers,
and consequently are so clear that controversies of religion can be decided
from them. For no one proves the unknown by what is more unknown.

8. Argument. After the apostles, the primitive church understood the
Scriptures without commentaries and judged controversies from them.
Therefore, they are both so clear in themselves and so plain to us that they
can be read profitably and controversies can be decided from them.

9. Argument. If the Scriptures are so difficult that they can neither be read
profitably by the people nor used to decide controversies, then this happened
either because the Holy Spirit could not write more plainly or because he was
unwilling. No one will say that he could not; to say that he was unwilling
con—

[1620 Tome I | col. 75 | BSB frame 00052]

—flicts with the purpose of writing (Rom. 15:4). For if he had not willed to
be understood, he would have written in vain. Therefore.

10. Argument. There are two kinds of people, the faithful and the
unbelieving. The latter understand nothing in the Scriptures (1 Cor. 2:14),
but the faithful understand everything necessary for salvation (John 10:17;
Jer. 31:34; Luke 8:10; the last verse of 1 Cor. 2; 2 Cor. 4:6). Therefore,
Scripture is not wholly difficult, but is so clear that the people can read it
profitably and controversies can be settled from it.

Testimonies

Augustine says on Psalm 8: “God bent down the Scriptures to the capacity of
infants and nursing children.”

The same author says in book 2, chapter 6 of *On Christian Doctrine*: “The
Holy Spirit arranged the sacred Scriptures magnificently and beneficially,
so that he might meet hunger with plainer passages and remove weariness with
more obscure passages. For hardly anything is drawn out of those obscurities
which is not found stated most plainly elsewhere.” The same author says in
chapter 9 of that book: “In those things which are set down plainly in the
Scriptures are found all the things that contain faith and the manner of
living.”

The same author says in chapter 6 of *On the Usefulness of Believing*:
“Whatever is in those Scriptures is lofty and divine. Truth is wholly present
there, together with teaching most suited to restoring and renewing souls,
and it is arranged in such a way that no one can fail to draw from it what is
enough for him, provided that he approaches to draw from it devoutly and
piously, as true religion requires.”

Chrysostom says in homily 3 on chapter 3 of 2 Thessalonians: “Everything in
the divine Scriptures is clear and plain; everything necessary is open and
manifest.”

See also his prologue to the Epistle to the Romans, homily 3 on Lazarus, his
homily on Matthew, and homily 1 on John.

Clement of Alexandria says in his *Exhortation to the Greeks*: “Listen, you
who are far a—

[1620 Tome I | col. 76 | BSB frame 00052]

—way; listen, you who are near. The word is hidden from no one. The light is
common and shines upon all people. No one is a Cimmerian in relation to the
word. Let us hasten to salvation, to regeneration.”

Epiphanius says in Heresy 76: “Everything in divine Scripture is clear to
those who wish to hear the divine discourse with reasoning.”

Jerome says on Isaiah 19: “It is the custom of the Scriptures to attach
manifest things to obscure ones and to declare openly what they had said
before in riddles.”

Objections

Objection 1. David was faithful and knew the whole of Scripture and the
idioms of the Hebrew language. Nevertheless, he asks for an understanding of
Scripture in Psalm 119 and elsewhere. Therefore, it is not so easy that anyone
can understand it by reading, nor so clear that it does not need
interpretation.

Response 1.

This does not establish what is at issue. For we do not say that laypeople,
by reading the whole of Scripture, immediately understand it and have no need
of interpretation.

2. The proposition contradicts the assumption. For if David knew the whole of
Scripture, it was no longer difficult, nor was it necessary for him to ask for
an understanding of it.

3. In those passages David is not speaking of an external understanding of
Scripture, but of the inward full assurance of the heart, which is discussed
in Luke 1:1 and Romans 4:21. Constant prayers are necessary for us to attain
it. For David was partly ignorant of the senses and mysteries of the word,
and we concede that we, like David, are ignorant of many things. Therefore,
we must pray that God will illuminate the eyes of our mind by his Holy Spirit
and instruct us concerning the true sense.

Objection 2.

Christ interpreted the Scriptures to the disci—

[1620 Tome I | col. 77 | BSB frame 00053]

—ples, who knew Hebrew idioms because they were Hebrews and who were neither
proud nor unbelieving (Luke 24:27). Therefore, the Scriptures need
interpretation.

Response 1.

This does not establish what is at issue. At that time the disciples were
struck by a certain stupor, as is evident from the passage cited. Thus it is
not surprising that they did not then understand the Scriptures without
interpretation, since they did not even recognize Christ himself before their
eyes were opened, as verse 31 of that passage testifies. Finally, those who
understand the grammatical sense of Scripture ought to hear its explanation
and application so that they may understand it better and be stirred to put
Scripture into practice.

Objection 3.

That eunuch read the Scriptures diligently and was godly and humble. Yet he
admits that he could not understand the passage he was reading without an
interpreter (Acts 8:31). Therefore, they need interpretation.

Response 1.

This does not establish what is at issue, for we concede the same thing. 2.
This eunuch was still ignorant of many things, yet he also understood many
things. Likewise, we say that the faithful can understand many things by
reading the Scriptures and can also be ignorant of many things. Therefore,
interpretation is necessary. Through reading and interpretation, things that
at first appeared obscure later become manifest.

Objection 4.

The apostle Peter says expressly and absolutely that certain things in the
Pauline epistles are difficult (2 Pet. 3:16). Therefore, Scripture is not
wholly easy but needs interpretation.

Response 1.

This does not establish what is at issue. He does not say that everything is
difficult, but that certain things are. Therefore, certain things will also
be easy. 2. *En hois* does not refer to the Pauline epis—

[1620 Tome I | col. 78 | BSB frame 00053]

—tles, for otherwise *en hais* would have been used. Rather, it refers to the
immediately preceding *toutōn*, that is, the matters about which Paul wrote in
his epistles. In these matters—namely, the mystery of the Lord’s coming, the
burning and purification by fire of all things, and the new heaven and
earth—about which Peter dealt in the preceding verses and Paul also dealt in
his epistles, there is no small difficulty because of our ignorance and
incapacity. Yet someone can speak plainly about the most obscure matters. For
with respect to us, what is more difficult to believe than that the world was
created from nothing and that God and man existed in one person? Yet
Scripture discusses this clearly. Thus this argument contains a fallacy of
figure of speech, or rather a corruption of the text.

Finally, the antecedent is denied, because Peter does not say that these
things are obscure to everyone or absolutely, but to the unlearned, who are
not instructed, and to the unstable, that is, those who are not firm in the
faith.

Objection 5.

Two things can be considered in the Scriptures: the matters that are spoken
and the manner in which they are spoken. But Scripture is obscure and
difficult in both respects. Therefore. The assumption is proved: if one
considers the matters, they are most obscure, because they deliver the
highest mysteries concerning the Trinity, the incarnation, the sacraments,
and so forth. If one considers the manner of speaking, there are innumerable
other difficulties. Therefore.

Response 1.

Only this follows: certain things in Scripture are difficult, which we
concede. 2. The matters delivered in Scripture are in themselves supremely
clear. But they are obscure to us because we are blind. Indeed, as these
mysteries are set forth to us in Scripture, they are plain and clear.
Bellarmine attempts to prove this part of the disjunction with six instances.

Instance 1.

There are certain things in Scripture which at first

[1620 Tome I | col. 79 | BSB frame 00054]

glance appear contradictory, such as Exodus 20:5 and Ezekiel 18:20.
Therefore, if one considers the manner of delivery, Scripture is difficult.

Response 1.

Only this follows: certain things are difficult if one considers the manner
of delivery. 2. To someone who does not consider them attentively enough,
certain things in Scripture could appear mutually contradictory. God willed
these appearances of contradiction to exist in the Scriptures so that we
might thereby be stirred all the more to read, meditate upon, and compare
them with one another. If we employ this diligence, we will easily reconcile
the Scriptures with one another.

3. The cited passages are easily reconciled as follows. In Ezekiel 18 God is
said to punish people for their own sins. But in Exodus 20 he is said to
punish the sins of parents in their children—conditionally, namely, if they
continue in the sins of their parents.

Instance 2. The expressions and words of Scripture are often ambiguous, as in
John 8:25: “The beginning, who also speak to you.” Therefore.

Response 1.

Only this follows: certain things are obscure.

2. In the Vulgate version many things are ambiguous, and indeed false, as in
this passage. The words of the Greek text are *tēn archēn ho ti kai lalō
hymin*, that is, “I am what I tell you from the beginning.” And the sense is:
“I am no other than he whom I said from the beginning that I was.”

Instance 3. There are incomplete expressions in the Scriptures, as in Romans
5:12, where *hōsper* is placed without a corresponding clause and there is no
main verb in the whole period. Therefore.

Response 1.

Only this follows: certain things are obscure.

2. It is false that no corresponding clause is attached, for it occurs near
the end of verse 14. 3. It is likewise false that the main verb is lacking.

Instance 4. There are anticipatory expressions in Scripture, as in Genesis
10:31, where it is

[1620 Tome I | col. 80 | BSB frame 00054]

said, “These are the sons of Shem according to their clans, languages, and
regions,” and so forth. Yet in chapter 11:1 it is said, “Now the earth was of
one language.” Therefore.

Response 1.

This *hysteron proteron* is customary in those sacred histories, as in Genesis
2 and elsewhere. 2. The word of the text in chapter 11 is wrongly translated
“was”; it ought to be translated, “the people had been of one language.”

Instance 5. There are many expressions peculiar to the Hebrews, as in Psalm
89:29, “His throne is like the days of heaven,” and Psalm 119:118, “My soul is
always in my hands.” Therefore, and so forth.

Response 1.

The inference is denied, for every language has peculiar expressions which
produce not difficulty but beauty and emphasis for those who understand the
language well. Nor is there any difficulty in the expressions cited. Finally,
only this will follow: certain things are obscure.

Instance 6. In Scripture there are certain figurative expressions, tropes,
metaphors, allegories, and ironies. Therefore.

Response.

Scripture is not obscured but illustrated by them. For this reason they are
called the lights of discourse by rhetoricians. These matters, however, will
be treated in the following controversy.

Objection 6.

If Scripture were clear, why would the adversaries write so many
commentaries? Why would so many versions be published? Why would they explain
Scripture in such varying ways that twenty mutually conflicting positions
concerning justification exist among the Confessionists? Therefore.

Response 1.

Scripture can be understood without the greatest part of the commentaries. 2.
Commentaries were published so that the Scriptures might be understood more
easily. The use of commentaries does not prove the obscurity of Scripture,
but rather our weakness, since we grasp only by degrees the things that are
spo—

[1620 Tome I | col. 81 | BSB frame 00055]

—ken in the Scriptures. 3. There is great agreement among the Reformed about
this article, as there is about all the others.

Objection 7. When some difficult question arises, God commands people to
approach not the Scriptures but the priests. But if Scripture were clear, he
would command this in vain. Therefore.

Response 1. This is a fallacy of non-cause as cause, because God did not
command them to approach the priests because Scripture was difficult, but for
other reasons. 1. So that those who neglect Scripture and are therefore
ignorant of it might be taught by a priest, and so that those who know it in
part might understand it better. 2. So that the form of judgment established
by God in the church might be preserved and sinners might be rendered without
excuse. 3. The priests themselves judged only according to the prescription
of the law. Therefore, if controversies must be judged according to the word
of God, that word is consequently clear.

Objection 8. The judgments of God are unsearchable, and no one knows the mind
of the Lord. Therefore.

Response. The passage does not speak about Scripture but about the mind and
judgments of God, which are unsearchable unless they are revealed to us, such
as the number of the elect, the hour of judgment, and so forth. But the
question concerns Scripture, where the revealed will of God is found.
Therefore, this is a fallacy of equivocation. For the will of God is twofold:
either unrevealed, about which the cited passage speaks, or revealed in
Scripture, which is the matter in question. But the cited passages do not
speak about the latter.

Objection 9. Teachers disagree among themselves about the interpretation of
Scripture. Therefore, it is obscure.

Response. True teachers do not disagree among themselves in matters
absolutely necessary for salvation, and so this is a fallacy of what is said
in a qualified sense. 2. The inference is denied, because although some do
not agree among themselves, it nevertheless does not follow that Scripture
is obscure in itself. God permits teachers not to agree among themselves in
every respect for these reasons:

[1620 Tome I | col. 82 | BSB frame 00055]

First, so that they may recognize their ignorance in divine matters and thus
be humbled. 2. So that they may be stirred to investigate the true sense
through reading, meditation, invocation, and so forth.

Objection 10. There are many passages of Scripture which the most learned do
not understand, such as the visions and oracles of Ezekiel; Daniel 7, 9, 10,
and 11; Zechariah 11; Revelation; and so forth. Therefore.

Response 1. This does not establish what is at issue, because we concede that
many passages are obscure with respect to us, but not in themselves, as was
proved above. Nor is it likely that God willed to deliver things which the
prophets and people could not understand in themselves. With respect to us,
however, visions and oracles that were obscure before their fulfillment
become known to us after their fulfillment, provided that we have the Holy
Spirit illuminating the eyes of our mind.
