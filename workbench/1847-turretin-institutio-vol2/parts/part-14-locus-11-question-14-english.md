QUESTION XIV.

THE LORD’S DAY.

Is the institution of the Lord’s Day divine or human; and is its observance
necessary and perpetual, or free and changeable? We affirm the former and deny
the latter in both respects.

[Marginal heading: What the Lord’s Day is.]

I. By Christian usage Κυριακὴ ἡμέρα, or the *Lord’s Day*, is the name given to
the first day of the week, appointed for the public worship of God in memory
of Christ’s resurrection. It is so called not so much with respect to its
*efficient cause*, as though it had been formally instituted by Christ
himself—in the way that the *Lord’s Prayer* and the *Lord’s Supper* are named
by the apostle (1 Cor. 10:21), for, as will be seen later, no argument can be
given for such an institution—as with respect to its *end*. For it was
instituted in memory of the resurrection of Christ, who rose on this day
(Matt. 28:1), and for his honor and worship, just as an *altar of the Lord* or
a *feast of the Lord* is so called because it was instituted for his worship.
The ancients likewise call temples dedicated to divine worship Κυριακὰς, or
*the Lord’s houses*.

[Marginal heading: The origin of the Lord’s Day. State of the question.]

II. Two things are chiefly asked concerning this day: 1. What is its *origin*?
2. What is the *necessity of its observance*? As to the former, the question is
not whether the seventh day was changed into the first by the abrogation of
the Jewish Sabbath. Christians agree on this, acknowledging not only that
this change could have been made by him who is Lord of the Sabbath, but also
that it had to be made and was made in the most fitting manner. For the former
day had to be abrogated on account of what was ceremonial in it and therefore
belonged to the legal economy, so that another might be substituted in its
place. Under the new covenant no other day could have been more fittingly
substituted than the one now called the Lord’s Day, because our Lord Jesus
Christ rose on it. It was most equitable that the memory of this event should
always be celebrated in the Christian church, since by it the work of our
eternal redemption and of the new creation was most fully completed. This day
was also to be a public memorial of the abrogated ceremonial law and of the
distinction that had to exist between Jews and Christians. The question,
rather, concerns the principle and origin of this change: whether it was an
ordinance merely human and political or ecclesiastical, or instead divine.

III. Here the opinions of theologians vary. Some refer it either to canon law,
as the Roman Catholics do, who from it also infer the necessity of unwritten
traditions—although among them various writers are not lacking who,

[Printed p. 82 | Internet Archive PDF 110 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

as Azorius observes (*Moral Institutes*, part 2, chapter 2), such as
Anchoranus, Panormitanus, and Angelus Sylvester, contend for its divine
authority—or to a political ordinance. The Remonstrants take this latter
position, professing in their *Confession* that every distinction of days has
been entirely abolished under the New Testament, as do the Socinians, who
assert that its observance is arbitrary (*Racovian Catechism*, chapter 1, on
Christ’s prophetic office). Others ascend to a divine ordinance, saying
either that Christ himself instituted that day immediately and expressly, as
*Junius* maintains in his lectures on Genesis 2, with certain others; or only
mediately, insofar as the apostles, being θεόπνευστοι, *divinely inspired*,
sanctioned it in the Christian church by their command as well as their
example and practice. This is the more common opinion of the orthodox, and we
also embrace it.

IV. Those who refer the origin of the Lord’s Day to Christ rely especially,
first, on *Christ’s resurrection*: by rising from the dead on this day, he
seems to have consecrated it to his worship in memory of that event. They also
rely on the various *appearances* made on this same day after the resurrection,
when he presented himself to the assembled disciples (John 20:19, 26; Rev.
1:10), and on the *outpouring of the Holy Spirit* upon the apostles, which is
held to have occurred on this day. Although these things are stated with
probability and seem to have furnished the occasion for this institution,
nevertheless they cannot produce a firm and solid argument to prove it, for
that would require some express command or example of Christ.

V. It is far more fitting, therefore, to say that this was an apostolic
institution. The apostles replaced the Sabbath with the Lord’s Day and
commended it to the churches, not without the special guidance of the Holy
Spirit, by whom they were infallibly governed in prescribing not only the
things that pertained to faith and morals but also those that pertained to the
church’s εὐταξίαν, *good order*, and the exercise of divine worship. There are
three principal passages from which this institution is gathered. 1. Acts
20:7: “Now on the first of the Sabbaths,” that is, of the week, “when the
disciples had assembled to break bread, Paul discoursed among them, intending
to depart on the next day.” Why are the apostles said to have assembled for
the preaching of the Word and the administration of the Eucharist on this day
rather than another, or on the celebrated Sabbath of the Jews, unless the
custom of holding solemn assemblies had already become established at that
time, while the ceremony of the Jewish Sabbath was gradually disappearing?

Nor should it be said that μίαν σαββάτων here designates not the first day of
the week but merely one day, that is, some day, of the week. For it is not used
in another sense in Luke 24:1 and Mark 16:2. What is brought from Luke 5:17
and 8:22 does not bear on this, because it is one thing to say ἐν μιᾷ τῶν
ἡμερῶν, “on one of the days,” which denotes an indefinite time, and another
to say ἐν τῇ μιᾷ, “on the first,” with the article, which determines the day.

VI. Second, 1 Corinthians 16:1–2, where not only apostolic practice but also a
command is implied: “Now concerning the collection for the saints, as I
ordained for the churches of Galatia, so you also are to do: on the first day
of every week let each of you set something aside with himself, storing up
what the kindness of God has enabled him to give, lest collections be made
when I come.” Here the apostle wills collections to be made by the faithful on
the first day of every week, when public assemblies also had to be held. He
derives this from the custom of the Jews who, as Philo and Josephus observe,
were accustomed on every Sabbath when they assembled to make collections in
the synagogues from tithes and other voluntary contributions, which were
afterward sent to Jerusalem for the use of the temple and the Levites.

Therefore, because the Jerusalem church was being greatly oppressed by
poverty on account of persecution by the Jews, the arrival of many
foreigners, and continual zeal in propagating the gospel, the apostle wills
the faithful to make collections for its use. Thus, just as he ordains
collections on the first day of every week, he is understood in the same way
to have ordained the public assemblies in which they were customarily made,
or to have approved with his vote those already ordained.

[Printed p. 83 | Internet Archive PDF 111 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

VII. Third, Revelation 1:10, where John says that *he was carried away in the
Spirit on the Lord’s Day*. This was certainly not the Sabbath of the Jews,
which he would undoubtedly have named, nor merely some day of the week,
because then the title would have been ambiguous, suited more to entangle
than explain. It was instead that day on which Christ had risen, on which the
apostles were accustomed to assemble for sacred rites, and on which Paul had
ordained that collections be held, as was customary in the primitive church.
Since, therefore, John speaks of that day as known and customary in the
church, there is no doubt that it was designated by this name from the
received usage of the church. Otherwise, which Christian would have
understood what John meant by this designation if he had wished to designate
some other day?

VIII. Fourth, only he who is Lord of the Sabbath could change the Sabbath,
either immediately and by himself or mediately through the apostles (Matt.
12:8). It was most fitting that the day of worship under the New Testament
should be instituted by him by whom worship itself was instituted and from
whom every blessing in every act of worship must be expected.

IX. Fifth, if the Lord’s Day was appointed neither by Christ nor by the
apostles, the condition of the Christian church under the New Testament will
be worse than that of the Jews under the Old. For under the Old Testament a
day was appointed for rest from worldly labor, on which the household and
animals were granted relief from servile work (Deut. 5:14). Under the New
Testament there will be no such day, which everyone sees is absurd, since our
condition is far better than the state of the ancients, who were burdened by
the ἀβαστάκτῳ, *unbearable*, yoke of the law.

X. Sixth, if the institution of the Lord’s Day is only a human ordinance,
whether political or ecclesiastical, so that a human constitution has defined
the necessity of public worship, it can be annulled with the same ease by
which it arose. Nor can the necessity of its observance be so strongly urged.
For then a profane person could grant himself a dispensation not to attend
prayers and sermons, and anyone could indulge himself with permission to do
or neglect anything whatever, if nothing were derived from the Scriptures
that bound the conscience beyond a human institution. Wisely and piously,
therefore, in addition to the church’s solemn and uninterrupted tradition,
the apostolic sanction and practice are urged, so that it may be evident that
in a matter of such importance the church did nothing that it had not
received from men who were θεοπνεύστοις, *divinely inspired*, and that its
observance is therefore necessary.

XI. Seventh, there is also the authority of the ancients who were nearest to
the age and times of the apostles. Among them are *Ignatius*, in the epistles
to the Magnesians and Trallians; *Justin*, *Apology* 2; *Dionysius of Corinth*,
in Eusebius, book 4, chapter 22; *Melito*, in the same author, book 4, chapter
26; *Irenaeus*, in the same author, book 5, chapter 24; *Tertullian*, *The
Crown of the Soldier*; *Origen*, on Exodus 15, book 3; and not a few others.
To this belongs the law enacted concerning it by the emperor Constantine the
Great in Eusebius, *Life of Constantine*, book 4, chapter 18. It was repeated
and confirmed by succeeding emperors—Theodosius, Valentinian, Arcadius, Leo,
and Antonius—who under the severest penalties forbade the presentation of
shows or devotion to pleasures on this day, as can be seen in the Code on
holidays.

XII. Very many of our theologians assert the same thing. *Calvin*, on Acts
20, says: “It is more likely that the apostles at first retained the day
already in customary use and later, when compelled by Jewish superstition,
abolished it and substituted another.” *Bucer*, *The Kingdom of Christ*,
chapter 15: “The Lord’s Day was consecrated to sacred actions by the apostles
themselves.” Thus *Beza*, on Revelation 1:10, holds this tradition to be truly
divine and made by the apostles at the Spirit’s direction: “The worship of
the Lord’s Day, therefore, which Justin also expressly commemorates in
*Apology* 2, is of apostolic and truly divine tradition,” and so forth. Thus
*Gallasius*, the colleague of Calvin and Beza, on Exodus 31: “We received this
institution—that the Lord’s Day

[Printed p. 84 | Internet Archive PDF 112 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

should replace the Sabbath—not from men but from the apostles from the very
beginning, that is, from the Spirit of God by whom they were governed.”
*Fayus* speaks no differently in disputation 47 on the Fourth Commandment:
“Rightly, therefore, we may say that, under the Holy Spirit’s guidance, the
apostles substituted for that seventh legal day the day that was first in the
creation of the former world,” and so forth. *Bullinger* on Revelation 1;
*Gualter*, homily 162 on Matthew; *Junius* on Genesis 2; *Piscator*,
*Explanatory Aphorisms*, aphorism 18; *Perkins, Ames,* and *Hyperius* on 1
Corinthians 16:2; *Valaeus*, *The Sabbath*, chapter 8; *Voetius*, *Selected
Disputations*, part 4, p. 760; and not a few others hold the same opinion.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XIII. Although the Lord’s Day is said to be of apostolic institution, the
authority on which it rests is nevertheless divine, because the apostles were
actuated by the divine Spirit no less in sacred institutions than in
presenting the very doctrine of the gospel, whether orally or in writings.
Therefore, a divine ordinance is rightly alleged here, not formally and
immediately from an institution of Christ, but mediately from the sanction
and practice of the θεοπνεύστων, *divinely inspired*, apostles.

XIV. Although certain ordinances of the apostles that concerned the rites and
circumstances of divine worship were variable and instituted only for a time,
such as the sanction against eating blood and what had been strangled (Acts
15), and concerning covering the heads of women and, conversely, uncovering
those of men when they prophesy (1 Cor. 11)—because there was a particular
cause and occasion for which they were made, and when it ceased the
constitution itself also had to cease—nevertheless, there are other
ordinances of invariable and perpetual observance in the church. These do not
rest on any particular occasion that will endure only for a time, by which
they could be rendered temporary. Such are the imposition of hands in the
election of ministers and the distinction of the office of the diaconate from
the pastoral office. Since the institution of the Lord’s Day was of this
kind, it is inferred from this that the intention of its institutors was that
the observance of this day should be of perpetual and immutable law.

XV. The constitutions of emperors and canons of councils concerning the
observance of the Lord’s Day do not prove that it was only a human ordinance,
because they do not sanction it for the first time. Rather, they confirm by
their own authority and establish what the apostles had already instituted,
lest anyone presume to violate it with impunity. This was done by them most
piously, both on account of the pagans and on account of impious Christians,
by whom they did not wish that day to be profaned and who, without
constitutions of this kind, would have thought themselves free and unrestrained
to violate it.

XVI. What is customarily brought from Scripture against the divine
institution of the Lord’s Day—from Romans 14:5, Galatians 4:10, and Colossians
2:16—does not overthrow our assertion. 1. Because in all those passages the
observance of a particular day for religious use, according to the ordinance
of Christ, is no more condemned or denied than is the choice of a particular
food for religious use according to Christ’s institution. Yet no one would
say that the choice of bread and wine in the Supper for religious use is
either unlawful or not instituted by Christ. 2. In Romans 14 the apostle
speaks expressly of that valuation of days which was then giving rise to
offense among Christians. But the observance of the Lord’s Day, which the
apostle himself teaches was then established in all the churches (1 Cor.
16:1–2), could not furnish any occasion for offense. 3. The subject is the
Jewish distinction of days, which belonged to bondage under the beggarly and
weak elements (Gal. 4:9), insofar as it had something typical and ceremonial
and reproduced the rigor of the law. That now has no place with respect to
the Lord’s Day.

XVII. It cannot be said that Christian liberty is diminished by this
opinion. For if anyone thinks himself freed from the observance of any
commandment of the Decalogue and from the divine and apostolic sanction, this
is not liberty but unchristian license. And experience teaches only too well
that license and neglect of sacred things

[Printed p. 85 | Internet Archive PDF 113 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

increase more and more where due regard is not given to the Lord’s Day.

XVIII. Above all, however, it must be observed here that we should be
concerned not so much to investigate the first origin of this day as to
sanctify it seriously and diligently. Whatever opinion anyone wishes to
follow—for we freely allow each person the enjoyment of his own judgment—this
must be strictly and inviolably attended to by everyone: according to
Christ’s prescription, the faithful must be drawn away from profanations,
apply themselves earnestly to sacred exercises of piety, and observe this
most sacred day in a holy manner. The necessity and manner of its observance
will be treated in what follows.

[Marginal heading: The second question, concerning the observance of the
Lord’s Day. Its necessity is proved.]

XIX. There is also no small dispute concerning the observance of the Lord’s
Day. Some incline in excess toward too much rigor and severity and thus
approach Judaism; others, on the contrary, err by defect and employ excessive
laxity, which opens the door to profanity and license. The safest way appears
to us to be the middle way, which we explain by two propositions. The first
teaches the *necessity* and the second the *manner* of this observance.

XX. First proposition: *The observance of the Lord’s Day is not necessary in
itself as a part of divine worship or for the sake of mystical signification,
but it is nevertheless necessary by reason of εὐταξίας, good order, and the
observance of apostolic and ecclesiastical polity.* It cannot be called a part
of worship in itself but only its adjunct and circumstance, because the
evangelical and λογικὸς, *rational*, worship of the New Testament is no longer
bound to definite places or times, as under the Old Testament, but can be
offered everywhere and always in spirit and truth. Nevertheless, it is
necessary by God’s order on account of the polity to be perpetually preserved
in the church, because without a definite day neither order nor propriety will
be established in the church; there will instead be sheer confusion in
ecclesiastical assemblies.

2. Because it was not instituted from some particular cause for a definite
church of one time, but generally for the church of all times. For just as the
apostles, who sanctioned this by their example and command (1 Cor. 16:2; Acts
20:7), were universal legates, so in this sanction they had regard for the
good of the entire church. And just as it had already been received in the
age of the apostles, so thereafter it was constantly retained by all the
churches, as is clear from ecclesiastical history. 3. Because there can be no
cause for change, since the memory of Christ’s resurrection, like that of his
death, must be perpetual in the church (1 Cor. 11:26; 2 Tim. 2:8). 4. Because
it was afterward confirmed both by various canons of ecumenical councils and
by very many laws and edicts of emperors.

XXI. Although we readily grant that God, who is Lord of the Sabbath, could,
if he wished, change this first day into any other day of the week, we
nevertheless do not think this lawful for any mortal after such constant and
universal observance of this day. Nor, if cases can occur in which public
exercises of piety cannot be performed on this day, does it follow that this
observance is merely temporary and changeable. For this is done not
voluntarily but from necessity, which has no law.

XXII. If the ancient Christians also observed the Sabbath for some time
together with the Lord’s Day, holding sacred assemblies on it and considering
it a sin to fast on that day, as is gathered from the *Constitutions of
Clement*, book 2, chapter 59, and Socrates, book 3, chapter 21, this belonged
to the honorable burial of the synagogue. It is also evident that the
festivity of the Sabbath, even when it was observed, was considered far
inferior to the Lord’s Day. This is clear both because the Ebionites are
charged, among the errors for which they were condemned by the church, with
celebrating the Lord’s Day and the Sabbath together, in Eusebius, book 3,
chapter 21, and from canon 29 of the Synod of Laodicea, whose

[Printed p. 86 | Internet Archive PDF 114 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

words are these: “Christians ought not to Judaize and be idle on the Sabbath,
but they should work on that day; yet, preferring the Lord’s Day, they should
rest if they can”—which seems to have been added on account of servants who
served gentile masters—“as Christians. But if they are found to be Judaizers,
let them be anathema before Christ.”

[Marginal heading: And its manner.]

XXIII. Second proposition: *The manner of rightly observing* the Lord’s Day is
comprised in two parts. The first can be called *privative* and consists in
rest or cessation from every servile work. The other is *positive* and is
concerned with sanctifying that rest through the religious worship of God.

XXIV. The rest required is not spent in idleness and inactivity, much less in
revelry and gluttony, shows, dances, and other profane exercises condemned by
Paul in Romans 13:13. For this is the Sabbath of Jehovah, not a feast of
Ceres, Bacchus, or Venus. Rather, it consists in cessation from every work of
one’s ordinary and worldly calling that could draw us away from divine
worship. Thus one must abstain on this day: 1. From all those works properly
and strictly called servile, which are customarily performed by servants or
servile persons, insofar as this can be done apart from urgent necessity. 2.
From *our works*, namely, those that pertain to the uses of this life in
natural and civil affairs and properly concern our profit and advantage.

This is gathered from the opposite concession. For just as God grants us six
days in which to labor and do all our work, so on this day he commands
cessation from such work, lest any impediment be placed in the way of divine
worship. To this belongs the memorable law of Leo and Anthemius found in the
Code on holidays, whose words it will be no burden to transcribe: “We decree
that the Lord’s Day is always to be so honored and venerated that it is exempt
from every execution. Let no summons press anyone; let no exaction of surety
be demanded; let official appearance be silent and advocacy lie hidden. Let
that day be foreign to judicial examinations; let the herald’s dreadful voice
be silent; let litigants breathe free from disputes,” and so forth. And later:
“Yet in granting rest on this religious day, we do not allow anyone to be
detained by obscene pleasures. Let the theatrical stage, a circus contest, or
the tearful spectacles of beasts claim nothing for itself on the same day;
and if a solemnity to be celebrated falls on our [day] or birthday, let it be
postponed,” and so forth.

XXV. Nevertheless, the following are excepted here: 1. *Those works that
directly concern the worship and glory of God* (Matt. 12:5; John 5:8–9), for
in this case those works that are servile by their own nature pass into the
nature of sacred works and are not so much our works as God’s. 2. *Works of
charity and mercy*, which are numbered among the duties of piety (Matt.
12:10, 12; John 5:9; 9:14; Luke 13:15). 3. *Works of common decency*, because
just as always, so on this day above others we must conduct and act ourselves
honestly and becomingly. 4. *Works of necessity*, where the necessity is
neither fabricated nor summoned by design but imposed on us through
providence (Luke 14:5). This includes not only absolute and simple necessity,
so that only something we cannot possibly do without is called necessary,
but also qualified and relative necessity. Thus things are considered
necessary not only when they are required absolutely for existence or the
sustenance of life, but also when they contribute to well-being, so that some
great advantage and benefit returns to us or our neighbor if they are done,
or some great disadvantage and loss if they are omitted. For, as Christ
testifies in Mark 2:27, *the Sabbath was made for man, not man for the
Sabbath*.

XXVI. Therefore, we do not think that in this cessation the faithful are
bound to Jewish exactitudes, which some who are more scrupulous than is right
would all but wish to see restored: that it should not be lawful to kindle a
fire, cook food, take up arms against an enemy, continue a journey already
undertaken by land or sea, refresh oneself with wholesome relaxations of mind

[Printed p. 87 | Internet Archive PDF 115 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

and body, provided they are taken outside the hours appointed for divine
worship, or have any diversion, however slight, toward things that concern
the advantages or benefits of this human life. Although at first sight this
opinion has a fair appearance of piety and is undoubtedly proposed by pious
men with a good intention to secure the greater sanctification of this day,
which is customarily so shamefully profaned, it nevertheless suffers from
serious disadvantages. It cannot be retained without the result that in this
way a yoke ἀβάστακτον, *unbearable*, contrary to Christian liberty, opposed to
Christ’s gentleness and to the sweetness of the covenant of grace, is brought
back into the church and again placed upon the shoulders of Christians. It
agitates and torments human consciences with infinite scruples and
inextricable knots and all but drives them to despair.

XXVII. The other part of the observance of the Lord’s Day pertains to the
sanctification of rest, which is practiced in sacred assemblies and the solemn
and public worship of God. Although sacred gatherings for public exercises of
piety can and should be attended by each person on other days as well, insofar
as his affairs permit, and although every devout person is bound by the duty
of conscience to hold private exercises of his devotion every day,
nevertheless on this day above others a holy convocation must be held, as was
customarily done on the Sabbath (Lev. 23:3). In it people should devote
themselves reverently and attentively to the reading and hearing of the divine
Word (Heb. 10:25), the celebration of the sacraments (Acts 20:7), psalmody and
prayer (Col. 3:16; Acts 1:14), alms and aid to the poor (1 Cor. 16:2), and in
general all that sacred service which pertains to external and solemn
worship.

XXVIII. Everyone agrees that this is what must chiefly be pursued, setting
aside the many other controversies customarily raised here, whether curious
or less necessary and useful. The Synod of Dort has this in view in the
*Post-Acta*, section 164, when it determines “that this day must be so
appropriated to divine worship that on it there must be rest from all servile
works—excepting those that charity and present necessity require—and from all
pleasures of such a kind as could impede divine worship.” It also wills “the
magistrates to be petitioned, lest the people, distracted by other labors or
profane exercises on Lord’s Day afternoons, be drawn away from the afternoon
sermons, and that by stricter edicts they prohibit all servile or daily works,
especially games, drinking parties, and other profanations of the Sabbath by
which the afternoon is customarily spent, especially in villages, so that by
this means also the people can be brought more effectively to those afternoon
sermons and thus learn to sanctify the entire Sabbath day.”

For no other reason does God in the law and the prophets so strongly urge and
commend the sanctification of the Sabbath and threaten to avenge so severely
its violation and profanation. Although these things primarily concerned the
Jews, there can nevertheless be no doubt that in their own manner they also
concerned Christians, insofar as they included a moral duty of perpetual
observance.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the solutions.]

XXIX. Although the binding of conscience and the distinction of days and
other ceremonial, typical times of the Old Testament have been removed by the
New Testament, and although the superstitious distinction of days and times
customary among the gentiles has also been entirely forbidden, it does not
follow that the Sabbath of the Lord, transferred from the seventh day to the
first and freed from the typical use and economic rigor of the Old Testament,
has thereby been abrogated.

XXX. He who performs necessary works of charity and mercy on the Sabbath does
not profane the Sabbath. Indeed, the person who on the pretext of the Sabbath
abandoned a neighbor in danger would sin by the most shameful superstition
and hypocrisy; in assisting that neighbor, he could and should serve God
according to God’s own ordinance. The *Sabbath*

[Printed p. 88 | Internet Archive PDF 116 | IA institutiotheolo02turr]

indeed is said to have been *made for man*, so that he may take particular
thought for his salvation by
performing duties of piety toward God and charity toward his neighbor; *man
was not made for the Sabbath*, as though necessary charity or mercy toward
himself or his neighbor had to be neglected through a superstitious
observance of the Sabbath.

XXXI. Cessation from every servile work and carnal pleasure must not be urged
in such a way that the spiritual practice of true holiness is neglected.
Indeed, it must not be urged for its own sake, as though it were a part of
worship or as though this day were holier than others, but as a condition and
aid for private and public exercises to be performed in this manner. Thus this
doctrine is as far as possible from inducing people into the opinion that they
have discharged their duty admirably if, abandoning the pursuit of true piety
and holiness, they devote themselves to a scrupulous and complete cessation
from every work. We require the means for the sake of the end and the
condition for the sake of the principal work: namely, that rest for the sake
of the spiritual exercises of true piety and holiness.

Therefore, the practice of the Sabbath should no more be burdened with these
consequences drawn from the accidental abuse of men than should the practice
of sacred reading, hearing the Word, prayers, and sacraments, which can be
pressed with the same disadvantages, although no one denies that these moral
duties are of perpetual observance.

XXXII. The accommodation of the Fourth Commandment to the particular Jewish
state, which consisted in the observance of the seventh day from the
beginning of creation, no more renders the commandment itself ceremonial than
the promise of the land of Canaan given to the Israelite people renders the
Fifth Commandment ceremonial, or than the preface, where the leading of the
people out of Egypt is treated, renders all the commandments ceremonial. We
indeed grant that in those times a somewhat stricter observance of the Sabbath
was commanded, accommodated to the pedagogy and bondage of that time, which
does not obtain in every age. Nevertheless, this does not prevent the
observance itself from being moral and common to all ages.
