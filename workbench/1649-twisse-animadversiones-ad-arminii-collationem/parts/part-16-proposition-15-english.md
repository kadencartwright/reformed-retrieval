PROPOSITION FIFTEEN.

ARMINIUS. Second, because the grace of predestination, or the grace prepared for a human being by
predestination, is evangelical, not legal. But evangelical grace is not prepared for a human being
except as considered a sinner. That it is evangelical is clear because the decree of predestination
is peremptory. Therefore it concerns not legal grace, which a human being can fail to use—as one
transgressing the law—and yet be saved, but evangelical grace, by which he must be saved or be
excluded from salvation.

Further, the grace prepared by predestination is the grace of remission of sins and regeneration,
that is, of aversion from sin and conversion to God through the mortification of the old human
being and the vivification of the new.

JUNIUS. I accept the major when it is rightly understood, but it will be expounded in the question
concerning the minor. I distinguish the assumption you bring forward. For as regards evangelical
grace commonly understood, it is false according to nature but most true according to us. Yet, as
you know, an inference from what is said in a qualified respect to what is said simply is not valid.
I shall explain the matter briefly. There are two parts in supernatural evangelical grace. One is
the preservation of those who are in grace; the other is the acquisition of those who are not in
grace. But the order of this grace is one by nature and another according to us. The natural order
is that those who are in grace should be preserved—this was done for the angels by predestination
and election—and then that those who are not in grace should be acquired, as happens with human
beings. But according to us, who have now fallen from grace, the order is different: first those who
are lost must be acquired, as Christ does by the Gospel, and then preserved, as he will do forever
by his power in heaven when we shall be like the angels. 2. Therefore the minor is simply false if
you say, “Evangelical grace simply is not prepared for a human being except as considered a sinner.”
For it was prepared for the human being simply and in common, as God also testified to the human
being by setting forth the symbol of the tree of life in Eden.

3. But if you speak of this evangelical grace—that is, as constituted in this manner and order—I
accept the minor; yet the consequence is not valid, as we were just saying. For God’s grace is one
evangelical grace according to substance, but twofold in order and manner, and that manner and
order do not change the substance of the thing. Therefore it was irrelevant to prove the major,
which we also religiously affirm if it is rightly understood.

4. What you say—that a human being can fail to use legal grace and yet be saved—is harsh unless it
is suitably explained.

Finally, as for your circumscribing evangelical grace by the remission of sins, regeneration, etc.,
you yourself, my brother, see from what we have just said that it is incomplete, because you omit
preservation, its other essential part. Otherwise we concede and believe the matter itself.

ARMINIUS. The syllogism is constituted in its proper figure and mood and with three terms.
Therefore it concludes nothing other or more than what is in the premises. For even if evangelical
grace considered commonly had two parts, I nevertheless restricted evangelical grace to one part,
since I speak of grace prepared for a human being. Not that grace commonly understood, but only its
other part, has been prepared for the human being, namely, the acquisition of those who are not in
grace. For no human being has been preserved in the grace which he obtained in creation. All have
fallen from it. Therefore there is no fallacy here from what is said in a qualified respect to what
is said simply. In both places I understand evangelical grace according to us in precisely the same
way, namely, insofar as it is prepared for human beings, not angels.

2. But you say that it is simply false that evangelical grace was not prepared for the human being
except as considered a sinner, because it was prepared for the human being simply and in common,
as God testified to the human being by the symbol of the tree of life. I reply that there is an
equivocation in the word “preparing.” The preparation of grace belongs either to predestination or
to providence, insofar as the latter is distinguished from the former. By providence sufficient
grace is prepared, and, if efficacious grace is prepared, as some hold, it is not finally
efficacious; by predestination efficacious and finally efficacious grace is prepared. For
predestination adds to providence the certainty of the event. By providence common grace, which
belongs to human beings indiscriminately, is prepared; by predestination singular grace, which is
proper to the elect. By providence both legal and evangelical grace are prepared; by predestination
evangelical grace alone. By providence grace communicable both in paradise and outside paradise is
prepared; by predestination grace which could not be communicated in paradise is prepared. Now by
the symbol of the tree of life God testified to common, not singular, grace; legal, not evangelical,
grace; grace communicable in paradise; and finally sufficient, not efficacious, grace. But
evangelical grace of this kind—which is finally efficacious, singular rather than common,
communicable only outside paradise, and prepared for the human being by predestination—is nothing
other than grace suitable for the human being only as considered a sinner. Accordingly, as regards
evangelical grace constituted in this manner and order, I accept the major and the minor. Therefore
the inference is valid.

3. And although grace is the same in substance and varies only in manner and rationale, nevertheless
the variation of that manner brings it about that grace constituted in this manner and order can be
prepared only for one who is certainly a sinner. The whole matter will become clearer if I draw the
conclusion with proofs of the assumption of that preceding syllogism added. The evangelical grace
by which a human being is actually saved, which consists in remission of sins and regeneration,
does not suit the human being except as considered a sinner; but grace prepared by predestination
is evangelical grace. Therefore grace prepared for a human being by predestination does not suit
him except as considered a sinner, and consequently the human being was not considered in pure
natural endowments by God as he predestined. But if someone should conclude in this way:
“Evangelical grace was prepared for a human being simply and in common; but grace prepared for a
human being by predestination is evangelical; therefore grace prepared for a human being by
predestination was prepared for the human being considered simply and in common,” he will commit
more than one fallacy in his argument. For first, the major taken simply is false. The grace of
preservation in the primeval state—which you call evangelical—with respect to the angels was not
prepared for the human being. Next, there are four terms in the syllogism. For in the major
evangelical grace is spoken of simply; in the minor it is spoken of in a qualified respect. If it
is said to be understood in the minor in the same way as in the major, then the minor also is
simply false. For the evangelical grace prepared for the human being by predestination is
evangelical grace in a qualified respect, understood with respect to us.

But what if I deny that the grace given to the angels by election and predestination can be called
evangelical? For the sum of the Gospel is this: “Repent and believe the Gospel.” These things in no
way apply to the elect angels. I can conceive of no other rationale for that opinion of yours than
that Christ is also called the Mediator of angels and that the angels are said to be elect in him.
But your eminence knows that this is disputed among the learned, as something was already said to
us about it before. For my part, even if it is conceded that Christ can be called the Mediator of
angels, I cannot persuade myself that the grace given to the angels was prepared and obtained for
them by any merit of Christ or work which he accomplished with the Father on the angels’ behalf.
But in my judgment grace which Christ did not obtain cannot be called evangelical.

Further, I think that in general there are two ways of obtaining supernatural happiness: one of
strict justice and legal, the other of mercy and evangelical, just as God’s covenant is twofold,
one of works and the other of faith; one of justice and the other of grace; one legal and the other
evangelical, etc. Concerning the angels I think only the former manner was employed.

4. By the phrase in which I say that a human being can fail to use legal grace and yet be saved, I
meant the same as what you said: God, if he wills, can remove iniquity like a cloud.

5. But in circumscribing evangelical grace by the remission of sins and regeneration, I made no
error. For I explained it not insofar as it is considered commonly but insofar as it is considered
according to us. Yet when it is explained in this way, it excludes the part called preservation.
For we are not saved by preservation in the primeval state; the grace of preservation in the
primeval state was not prepared for us by predestination. For we have all fallen and sinned. Here
again it must be noted that we are treating nothing concerning the angels. Therefore those things
which are common to angels and human beings must here be narrowed so that they apply to human
beings alone.

RESPONSE. 1. The response to Arminius’s arguments is easy: we deny that evangelical grace was
prepared—that is, decreed—for a human being considered as a sinner. We also present a rationale for
why it is impossible for the matter to stand in this way. For if what Arminius says were true, the
decree to permit sin would rightly be prior to the decree to confer evangelical grace. But that
this cannot happen is proved as follows. The permission of sin would thus be prior in God’s
intention to the conferral of evangelical grace and therefore posterior in execution; that is,
evangelical grace would be conferred on human beings before they were permitted to sin. Again,
since nothing has the character of what is prior in intention except an end with respect to the
means to that end, it would follow that the conferral of evangelical grace is a means to the
permission of sin as an end. But this is impossible, for evangelical grace is not conferred upon
anyone to the end that he be permitted to sin. The reason why the decree to confer evangelical
grace seems to allow the consideration of sin is nothing other than that the conferral of
evangelical grace itself presupposes sin when the execution of the decree is reached. But they
wrongly infer that the decree to confer grace also allows the consideration of sin. For
glorification likewise presupposes good works, at least in adults; yet from this it is by no means
proved that the decree to glorify places before itself the consideration of good works.

How invalid the second argument is can be shown by the same method. For the decree to grant
remission of sins or regeneration does not place before itself the consideration of sin, because
in that case it would place before itself the decree to permit sin. Consequently, in God’s
intention the permission of sin would be prior to its remission and regeneration. Therefore the
permission of sin would assume the character of an end, while its remission would have the
character of a means to that end. But this is manifestly false. For it cannot happen that sin is
remitted to the end that it be permitted to occur. Again, if the permission of sin held the prior
place in intention, it would certainly obtain the posterior place in execution; that is, sins
would be remitted to human beings before they were permitted to sin.

Evangelical grace is either grace for obeying the Gospel or some grace granted from the mitigation
made in the Gospel, such as the remission of sins. Therefore legal grace, correspondingly
expounded, must be either grace for obeying the law or some grace consequent upon that obedience.
But however it is described, it is absurd to say that a human being can rightly fail to use it. I
am not unaware, however, that evangelical grace can also signify the ministry of the Gospel itself
and, correspondingly, that the ministry of the law can be called legal grace; of either of these a
human being can suitably be said either to make use or not to make use. But in this sense
evangelical grace is not prepared by predestination. For evangelical grace of this kind is common
to the reprobate and the elect, as Arminius well knows.

2. I cannot approve Junius’s response, since the question concerns only the predestination
[printed: *praedestinanione*] of human beings; consequently I cannot deny that this rejoinder is
exceedingly untimely. I also side with
Arminius rather than Junius in denying that the grace of the angels’ preservation in the state of
integrity was evangelical grace. Nor does the symbol of the tree of life set forth to Adam in Eden
do anything to establish Junius’s opinion. That symbol was a symbol of glory, not grace, and of
glory to be conferred not absolutely but under the condition of obedience, which Adam did not
perform; nor did God decree to give Adam efficacious grace for performing that obedience. But
evangelical grace is grace of the kind which God decreed to confer upon the elect so that they
would thereby be brought infallibly to eternal life.

3. We proceed to Arminius. We have already said what must be answered to that argument of Arminius,
setting aside Junius’s response as wholly unsuitable and utterly aberrant. Namely, we deny the
major proposition. We have already exposed the rationale for our denial and at the same time the
merely specious but insubstantial plausibility of the adversary’s assertion.

2. But when Arminius distinguishes a twofold preparation [printed: *paeparatione*] of grace, one of
predestination and the other of providence insofar as providence is distinguished from
predestination, he thrusts his old grandmothers upon his readers. We deny that providence is
distinguished over against predestination, for no sober person is accustomed to distinguish either
a genus over against a species or a whole over against a part; rather, part is suitably
contradistinguished from part and species from species. Nor, so far as I know, does any learned
person distinguish providence from predestination otherwise than as either a genus is distinguished
from a species or a whole from a part. By providence grace is prepared not only sufficient but also
efficacious, and indeed finally so. For whatever happens by predestination happens by providence,
since providence comprehends predestination. Again, by predestination both sufficient and
efficacious grace are prepared. For what? Is efficacious grace not sufficient? It is false that
predestination adds to providence the certainty of the event, and there is reason for me to fear
that Arminius has falsely invented testimonies of the scholastics for this point. The things
prepared by providence are just as certain as those prepared by predestination [printed: *adeo
quam*]. Nor do I believe that any scholastic of better reputation taught otherwise; if there was
one, it will be as permissible to profess, “We do not stand with that scholastic,” as it was
permissible for Arminius to profess, “We do not stand with Augustine.” Whatever is prepared by
predestination is prepared by providence, because providence, as a whole, comprehends predestination
as a part. But it is false that only grace proper to the elect is prepared by predestination, for
sufficient grace also is prepared by predestination. The true difference between providence and
predestination is twofold, one with respect to the object and the other with respect to the end.
All things are the object with which

providence is concerned; only rational creatures are the object with which predestination is
concerned. Again, just as providence directs all things to their ends, so it directs them to both
natural and supernatural ends; but predestination directs only rational creatures to their
supernatural ends. Therefore that species of providence which is distinguished from predestination
is concerned only with natural things and in respect of natural ends; consequently it is not at
all concerned with grace. But all grace, both legal and evangelical, serves predestination—and by
predestination I mean not predestination insofar as it is distinguished from reprobation, but
insofar as its scope comprehends both reprobation and election. Thus grace communicable both in
paradise and outside paradise would also have belonged to predestination if it had been
communicated. And the grace actually communicated in paradise, namely sufficient grace, regarded
predestination and served reprobation and election equally on either side. For these decrees belong
to predestination to life: namely, the decrees to create in the image [printed: *imagimen*] of God,
to permit sin, and to call to faith and repentance. The decree to create in the image of God
comprehends the decree to clothe the human being over with supernatural gifts. The decree to call
through efficacious evangelical grace comprehends the decree to prepare the human being for faith
through the ministry of the law; and thus both legal and evangelical grace serve predestination to
life. Again, predestination to death comprehends the same number of decrees. The former decrees are
common, namely the decree to create and the decree to permit sin; the third is proper to
reprobation, namely the decree to harden.

3. I acknowledge that Junius’s exception is of no value; nevertheless your argument is met without
difficulty. For it is false that grace which consists in remission of sins and regeneration is
destined for a human being only as considered a sinner. But observe in passing Arminius’s
sophistical cunning. His proposition is this: “Evangelical grace which consists in remission of
sins does not suit a human being except as considered a sinner”—as if the question were whether it
suits the human being as considered a sinner. We profess that it does not suit a human being not
only unless he is considered a sinner, but also unless he is actually a sinner. According to
Arminius, these two things differ very greatly. But the question is whether grace of this kind is
prepared—that is, decreed—for a human being only as considered a sinner. Arminius holds that it is
not even prepared or decreed for a human being except as considered a sinner. We judge this to be
impossible, namely, that the decree to permit sin should be prior to the decree to confer
evangelical grace or the remission of sins. We explained our reasons above and at the same time
showed on how unstable a foundation Arminius’s contrary opinion rests.

In my judgment it is absurd to say that fulfilling the law is to use legal grace, as if obeying the
law were using the law. But let us set terminology aside and examine the matter itself. I ask, who
fulfills the Gospel? Does not even the faith of the best sometimes waver? Is not the saints’ weak
and remiss faith and repentance reproved? Yet in some measure we believe and repent; and in just
this way we also obey God’s legal commandments.

With Arminius, I admit that the grace of preservation in the primeval state belonged to the
predestination of the angels, but by no means that it regarded the predestination of human beings.
I add that the denial of the grace of preservation was subordinate to the predestination of human
beings rather than the granting of that grace. The reason is that God predestined human beings to
salvation by the way of mercy, which necessarily requires the permission of sin and misery.
