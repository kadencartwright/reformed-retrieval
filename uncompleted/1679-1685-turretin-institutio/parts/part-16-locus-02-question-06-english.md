QUESTION VI

From What Source Does the Authority of Divine Scripture Become Known to Us?
Does It, Either in Itself or with Respect to Us, Depend upon the Testimony of
the Church?—Negative, against the Papists.

[Marginal heading: Occasion of the question.]

I. The aim of the papists in this and the other controversies they raise
concerning Scripture is not obscure. They wish to avoid the tribunal of
Scripture, in which they do not find sufficient protection for defending
their errors, and appeal to the church—that is, their pontiff—and so become
judges in their own cause. Hence, whereas doctrine was formerly disputed from
Scripture—whether it agreed with Scripture or not—people began instead to
dispute about Scripture itself: whether it was fit for controversies of
religion to be decided by its authority and testimony. To this end a serious
dispute was raised about its origin and necessity, its perfection and
perspicuity, the integrity and purity of the canon, and especially its
authority, so as, if not to overthrow it altogether, at least

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 80 | IA leaf 134 | PDF p. 134]

to undermine it in a remarkable manner. Thus what Irenaeus says about the
heretics of his time in book 3, chapter 2, applies especially well to them:
“When they are argued against from the Scriptures, they turn to an accusation
of the Scriptures, as though they were not sound and possessed no authority.”

[Marginal heading: Some papists speak more harshly, others more mildly.]

II. It must be observed, however, that some speak more harshly and others more
moderately in this question. For some “with uncovered head” (γυμνῇ τῇ
κεφαλῇ) simply denied the “authority” (αὐθεντίαν) of Scripture in itself
without the testimony of the church, and—though I shudder to relate it—judged
it no more worthy of belief than the Koran, Livy, or Aesop’s fables. Those who
in the preceding century began to dispute against our theologians concerning
the authority of Scripture belched out these blasphemies. To this belong the
impious words of Hosius, book 3 against Brentius, who asserts that it can be
said in a pious sense that “the Scriptures are worth only as much as Aesop’s
fables if they are deprived of the authority of the church”; of Eck, *On the
Authority of the Church*: “Scripture is not authentic except by the authority
of the church”; and of Bailius, treatise 1, catechism, question 12, who
professes that “without the authority of the church he would no more believe
St. Matthew than Livy.” So also Andradius, book 3 of the *Defense of the
Council of Trent*: “There is nothing divine in those books in which the sacred
mysteries were written, nor is there found in them anything which compels and
constrains us by religion to believe what is contained in them; but the power
and scope of the church are so great that no one can resist it without the
greatest mark of impiety.” Stapleton, against Whitaker, book 1, chapter 7,
says: “The church attests in such a way that we ought to believe the testimony
of the church no differently from the way the apostles believed the testimony
of Christ, and God is not believed to exist except because of the church.”

But because others saw that our theologians attacked this as impious and
blasphemous, and not without reason, they spoke more cautiously, explaining
their mind in such a way that they indeed admit Scripture to be absolutely and
in itself authentic and divine, as having proceeded from God, the source of
all truth. Nevertheless, they maintain that relatively and with respect to us
it does not obtain that authority except because of the testimony of the
church, by whose benefit it becomes known to us and is received by us as
divine. Hence arose the distinction between authority in itself and authority
with respect to us, which Bellarmine, book 2, *On Councils*, chapter 12;
Stapleton, *Doctrinal Principles*, chapter 1, and *On the Authority of the
Church*, book 2, chapter 11; and others repeatedly bring forward.

III. But in whatever way they explain their mind, if we consider the matter
correctly, it will be evident that this distinction was devised more to create
a false appearance and remove the odium of so impious a doctrine than to
explain the truth of the matter. For since authority belongs to the category
of relatives and is “one of the things relative to another” (ἐκ τῶν πρός τι),
it ought not to be considered absolutely but relatively. Therefore Scripture
cannot be authentic in itself without also being authentic with respect to
us, for the same arguments which secure authority for it in itself ought to
move us to assent, so that it is authentic with respect to us. But if the
authority of Scripture with respect to us is suspended from the testimony of
the church as the formal reason because of which I believe Scripture to be
divine, it must also be suspended from it in itself, which others admitted
more straightforwardly. It is also readily gathered from the other
controversies they raise here that their meaning is no different. For how
could they deny Scripture’s perfection, perspicuity, or purity if they truly
believed it authentic in itself?

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

IV. In order that the state of the question may be evident, 1. the question
is not whether Sacred Scripture is authentic and divine. The adversaries do
not deny this, or at least wish to appear to believe it. Rather, the question
is from what source it becomes known to us as such, or by what argument this
divinity can be established for us. The papists suspend this authority from
the testimony of the church and maintain that the principal motive by which
we are moved to believe the “authority” (αὐθεντίαν) of Scripture is the voice
of the church. But although we do not deny that the testimony of the church
has its own weight, as will be seen afterward, we maintain

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 81 | IA leaf 135 | PDF p. 135]

that Scripture is believed by us to be divine primarily and principally
because of itself, or the marks implanted in it, not because of the church.

V. Second, the question is not about the principle or efficient cause of the
faith by which we believe the divinity of Scripture—whether the Holy Spirit
works it in us or not. For this pertains to another question, concerning free
choice, and the adversaries acknowledge it with us: Stapleton, *Triplication
against Whitaker*, chapter 9; Cano, *Theological Topics*, book 2, chapter 8.
Rather, the question concerns the principal argument or motive which that
Spirit uses in persuading us of this truth: whether it is the inartificial
argument of the testimony of the church, as the papists maintain, or an
artificial argument drawn from the marks of Scripture itself, as we maintain.

VI. Just as a threefold cause can be given for the manifestation of anything—
objective, efficient, and instrumental or organic—so a threefold question can
be formed about the knowledge of the divinity of Scripture. The first
concerns the argument because of which I believe; the second, the principle or
efficient cause by which I am brought to believe; the third, the means and
instrument through which I believe. The threefold question receives a
threefold answer. In its marks Scripture stands as the argument because of
which I believe; the Holy Spirit stands as the efficient cause and principle
from which it comes about that I believe; but the church is the instrument and
means through which I believe. Hence if it is asked, “Why, or because of what,
do I believe Scripture to be divine?” I will answer that this occurs because
of Scripture itself, which demonstrates itself to be such through its marks.
If it is asked, “From what source or agent does it come about that I believe?”
I will answer, “From the Holy Spirit, who produced this faith in me.” Finally,
if it is asked, “Through what means or instrument do I believe this?” I will
answer, “Through the church, which God uses in handing Scripture down to me.”

VII. 3. The question is not about an “introductory” and “ministerial” motive
or means (εἰσαγωγικῷ and λειτουργικῷ), whose ministry the Holy Spirit uses in
persuading us of the authority of Scripture; for we readily concede this to
the church. Rather, it concerns the principal argument and motive by which we
are brought to faith—not human but divine—which they place in the church, but
which we believe must not be sought outside Scripture itself.

VIII. 4. The question is not whether divine revelation is the formal reason
of our faith simply and absolutely, for the adversaries acknowledge this with
us. Rather, it is which revelation is first and best known with respect to us,
which must be received through and because of itself, and not because of any
other revelation better known to us, and which is therefore the most general
and first principle of faith through which all things must be proved while it
is itself proved by nothing prior. Is that revelation to be sought in
Scripture, or in the church? We judge that this revelation is contained in
Scripture alone, which is the first and infallible truth and the rule of
faith. The papists, however, maintain that it is to be sought in the voice and
testimony of the church. Stapleton, book 1, *On the Authority of the Church
against Whitaker*, chapter 8: “The supreme external testimony on earth,
therefore, is the voice of the church”; and chapter 9: “God speaking through
the church speaks no differently than if God were speaking immediately
through visions and dreams, or through any other supernatural mode of
revelation”; and chapter 14, line 12: “The entire formal reason of our faith
is God revealing through the church.” Gretser, *On the Canon of Scripture*,
chapter 7: “Only the testimony of the church is fittingly returned to the
question, ‘How do you know that Scripture has been divinely revealed?’”
Bellarmine, book 3, *On the Word of God*, chapter 10, response to argument 13:
“It is certain that we do not know with certainty what God has revealed
except from the testimony of the church”; and book 6, *On Free Choice*,
chapter 3: “Catholics believe what they believe because God revealed it; but
they believe that God revealed it because they hear the church saying and
declaring it so.”

IX. The question, therefore, is reduced to these terms: Why, or because of
what,

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 82 | IA leaf 136 | PDF p. 136]

do we believe Scripture to be the Word of God? Or what argument does the Holy
Spirit principally use to persuade us of the divinity of Scripture? Is it the
testimony or voice of the church, or the marks and criteria implanted in
Scripture itself? The adversaries assert the former; we, the latter.

[Marginal heading: Scripture’s authority with respect to us does not depend on the testimony of the church.]

X. It is proved that the authority of Scripture, either in itself or with
respect to us, does not depend on the testimony of the church. 1. The church
is founded upon Scripture, Ephesians 2:20, and borrows all its authority from
Scripture, which the adversaries cannot deny, since when the church is in
question they flee to nothing other than Scripture to prove it. Therefore the
church cannot secure authority for Scripture either in itself or with respect
to us, unless we wish the cause to depend upon the effect, the principle upon
what is derived from the principle, and the foundation upon the building. Nor
ought it to be objected here that both can be true: that the church borrows
its authority from Scripture and Scripture in turn from the church, just as
John bore testimony to Christ, who himself also gave testimony to John. For
it is one thing to bear testimony to someone as a minister, as John testified
about Christ so that the Jews might believe “through him” (δι’ αὐτοῦ, *per
ipsum*), not “because of him” (δι’ αὐτόν, *propter ipsum*), John 1:7. But it
is another thing to secure authority for him as Lord, which Christ did for
John. 2. The authority of the church would be prior to the authority of
Scripture and so would be the first credible thing on which our faith first
depended and into which it was finally resolved. The adversaries will not
admit this, since they maintain that the church’s authority depends upon
Scripture. 3. An evident circle is committed when the church’s authority is
proved from Scripture and, in turn, Scripture’s authority from the church.
4. It is not yet settled among the adversaries what is to be understood by
“the church”: the present or ancient church, the collective or representative
church, a particular or the universal church? Nor is it settled what act
bears testimony concerning Scripture’s authority: one signified by some
judicial sentence, or one exercised through continuous and successive
tradition? 5. A fallible and human testimony, such as the testimony of the
church, cannot ground divine faith. Nor, if God speaks through the church
today, does it immediately follow that the church is infallible, because
special and extraordinary inspiration—which rendered the apostles and
prophets “free from error” (ἀναμαρτήτους), and of which Christ properly speaks
when he says that the Holy Spirit will lead the apostles into all truth, John
16:14—is one thing; common and ordinary inspiration, which does not render
pastors “God-breathed” (θεοπνεύστους), is another.

[Marginal heading: But it is proved through itself.]

XI. It is proved that Scripture becomes known to us through itself. 1. From
the nature of Scripture itself. A law does not borrow its authority from the
subordinate judges who interpret it or from the heralds who promulgate it,
but from the prince alone who sanctions it. A testament obtains its weight
not from the notary to whom it is committed but from the will of the
testator. A rule has its power of regulating from its innate perfection, not
from the craftsman who uses it. So Scripture, which is the law of the supreme
Prince, the testament of the heavenly Father, and the “unbending” rule of
faith (ἀκλινής), cannot have authority even with respect to us from the
church, but only from itself. 2. From the nature of highest genera and first
principles. For these are known through themselves and are “indemonstrable”
(ἀναπόδεικτα), unable to be demonstrated from elsewhere; otherwise the matter
would proceed to infinity. Hence Basil on Psalm 115 says, “It is necessary
that the principles of each discipline be unexamined” (ἀνάγκη ἑκάστης
μαθήσεως ἀνεξετάστους εἶναι τὰς ἀρχὰς). So Scripture, which is the first
principle in the supernatural order, is known through itself and has no need
of arguments sought outside itself by which it may be proved and made known
to us. If God implanted in all principles marks by which they may be known by
everyone, there is no doubt that he placed such marks in this holy principle,
which for salvation is

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 83 | IA leaf 137 | PDF p. 137]

supremely necessary. 3. From comparisons. When objects of the senses are
applied to faculties properly disposed, they are immediately judged and known
without any other external argument, because of the hidden proportion and
inclination of the faculty toward the object. Light by its own splendor, food
by its own sweetness, and fragrance by its own scent are immediately known by
us with the greatest certainty, even if no one bears testimony to them. So Scripture,
which with respect to the new man and the spiritual senses is described now
under the symbol of marvelous light, Psalm 119:105; now of the sweetest food,
Psalm 19:10; Isaiah 55:1–2; Hebrews 5:14; and now of the most fragrant scent,
Song of Songs 1:3, is easily recognized by the senses of the new man as soon
as it is presented to them. It offers itself to be known by its own light,
sweetness, and “fragrance” (εὐωδίᾳ), so that there is no need to seek
arguments elsewhere which teach that this is light, food, or a good scent.
4. From the testimony of the adversaries, who prove the divinity of Scripture
from its own marks. Bellarmine, book 1, *On the Word of God*, chapter 2: “Of
the Sacred Scriptures contained in the prophetic and apostolic writings,
nothing is better known, nothing more certain, so that anyone who denies that
faith should be placed in them must be most foolish.” See Cano,
*Theological Topics*, book 2, chapter 8; Gregory of Valencia, book 1, *On the
Analysis of Faith*, chapter 15; Soto, *Defense of the Catholic Faith*, chapter
47.

[Marginal heading: Sources of solutions.]

XII. We do not deny that the church has a manifold office concerning
Scripture. 1. It is the guardian of the oracles of God entrusted to it, and,
like a notary, keeps the authentic tablets of the covenant of grace with the
greatest faithfulness, Romans 3:2. 2. It is a guide which points out Scripture
and leads us by the hand to it, Isaiah 30:21. 3. It is a vindicator which
guards and vindicates it by separating genuine books from spurious ones, in
which sense it is called Scripture’s “support” (ἑδραίωμα), 1 Timothy 3:16.
4. It is a herald which presents and promulgates it, 2 Corinthians 5:19;
Romans 10:16. 5. It is an interpreter which searches out and discloses its
true sense. But all these things imply only ministry, not mastery, so that we
indeed believe through it but not because of it. Thus through John the Baptist
the faithful believed in Christ, but not because of John, John 1:7; and
through the Samaritan woman Christ became known to the Samaritans, but not
because of her, John 4:39.

XIII. The resolution of faith considered objectively with respect to the
things to be believed is one thing; considered subjectively or formally with
respect to the act of believing it is another. The former is resolved into
Scripture and the external testimony of the Holy Spirit expressed in
Scripture; the latter, into his internal testimony impressed upon the
conscience and speaking in the heart. For since two things are necessary to
produce faith—the presentation of the truth in the Word and the application
of the same truth in the heart—the Holy Spirit works in both, in the Word and
in the heart. Therefore he is properly said to testify in the Word
objectively, as the argument because of which we believe. In the heart he is
also said, though less properly, to testify efficiently, as the principle by
whose power we believe. In this sense, in 1 John 5:6, the Spirit, who is placed
among the witnesses of Christ’s divinity and the truth of the gospel, is said
to testify “because the Spirit is truth”; that is, the Spirit acting in the
hearts of the faithful testifies that the doctrine of the gospel handed down
by the Spirit is true and divine.

XIV. When article 5 of the French Confession says, “We know the books of
Scripture to be canonical not so much by the common consent of the church as
by the testimony and inward persuasion of the Holy Spirit,” “the Holy Spirit”
must be understood as the Spirit speaking both in the Word and in the heart.
For the same Spirit who acts objectively in the Word by presenting the truth
also works efficiently in the heart by impressing that truth upon our minds,
and so is altogether different from the enthusiastic spirit.

XV. A private judgment of the Spirit which is such subjectively, by reason of
the subject in which it inheres, is one thing; a judgment which is such
originally, because it depends upon a person’s own choice, is another. We
concede that the former is given here, but not the latter.

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 84 | IA leaf 138 | PDF p. 138]

For the Spirit who testifies in us concerning the divinity of Scripture is
not proper to individuals by reason of his principle and origin, because he
is common to the whole church and to all the faithful in whom he produces the
same faith. Nevertheless, he is such subjectively by reason of each
individual, because he is given privately to each of the faithful.

XVI. Although the church, considered formally and with respect to the mode of
writing, is older than Scripture, it cannot be called older with respect to
Scripture considered materially and with respect to the substance of the
doctrine, because the Word of God was older than the church itself, since it
is the church’s foundation and seed. 2. The question does not concern the
testimony of the ancient church of the patriarchs which existed before
Scripture, but of the present church, which is itself far more recent.

XVII. Although the faithful are persuaded of the divinity of Scripture by the
testimony of the Holy Spirit, it does not follow that all who have that Spirit
ought to agree in receiving every book alike. For just as the Spirit is not
given to all in equal measure, so he does not suffuse all with an equal light
of knowledge either concerning the principle of religion or its doctrines,
nor does he move all to assent with equal efficacy. Hence certain Protestants
could doubt one or another canonical book because they had not yet been
sufficiently illuminated by the light of the Holy Spirit.

XVIII. It is not always necessary that whatever is proved through something
else must itself be proved. For according to the philosophers certain things
are known through themselves, such as the highest genera of things, ultimate
differences, and first principles. They cannot be demonstrated from elsewhere
but are evident by their own light and are assumed as certain and indubitable.
And if perhaps someone denies them, he must not be met with arguments but sent
back to his relatives or restrained by punishments, as one who, according to
the Philosopher, needs either sense or punishment. Thus in *Posterior
Analytics* 1.10 he says that certain axioms have no external cause of their
truth: “What necessarily exists through itself must also necessarily appear
such” (ὅ ἀνάγκη εἶναι δι’ αὐτὸ καὶ δοκεῖν ἀνάγκη); that is, it is not only
“self-evident” (αὐτόπιστον), but cannot be seriously denied by anyone who is
of sound mind. Since, therefore, Scripture is the first principle and the
first and infallible truth, what wonder is it if it is said to be proved
through itself? 2. Scripture can prove itself, either one part from another,
when all parts are not equally called into doubt, as when we convict the Jews
from the Old Testament, or the whole from the whole—not by the inartificial
argument of testimony, because it calls itself divine, but by an artificial
and ratiocinative argument, because divine marks which do not belong to human
writings are found in it. Hence this is not begging the question, because
these criteria are something distinct from Scripture, if not materially, then
formally, as adjuncts and properties demonstrated concerning a subject. Nor
is the same thing proved through something equally unknown, because these
marks are better known to us, just as we properly demonstrate a cause from
its effects and a subject from its properties. 3. The papists’ argument that
Scripture cannot be proved through itself because it would be both better and
less known than itself can be turned back much more forcefully against the
church.

XIX. If some do not acknowledge the divinity of Scripture, this is not
because the object is not known or knowable through itself, but because they
lack a properly disposed faculty. The gospel is veiled to them because Satan
has blinded their eyes, 2 Corinthians 4:4. So those who deny God, who is
supremely intelligible, do so because their minds are deranged, and those who
do not see the sun do so because they are blind. Thus in Seneca the woman who
had lost her sight complained that the sun did not rise. Nevertheless, the
sun no less continually sends forth rays which those with sound eyes perceive
through the rays themselves.

XX. It is one thing to discern and declare the canon of Scripture; it is
another to constitute the canon itself and make it authentic. As for the
latter, the church cannot

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 85 | IA leaf 139 | PDF p. 139]

do it, for this belongs to God alone, the author of Scripture; but it only
does the former, which belongs to ministry, not mastery. A goldsmith who separates
dross from gold, or one who tests gold with a touchstone, indeed discerns the
true from the counterfeit, but does not make it true, either in itself or with
respect to us. So the church by its examination indeed discerns canonical
books from noncanonical and apocryphal ones, but does not make them such. Nor
can the judgment of the church secure for books an authority which they did
not have through themselves; rather, by arguments drawn from the books
themselves it declares the authority already existing.

XXI. A confused knowledge of a thing is one thing; a distinct knowledge is
another. By a confused knowledge the church can be known before Scripture,
but a distinct knowledge of Scripture must precede, because the truth of the
church can be discovered only in Scripture. Before Scripture, the church can
be apprehended by us with human faith as an assembly of people using the same
sacred rites. But it cannot be known and believed with divine faith as the
assembly of the faithful and communion of saints except after Scripture is
known, since Scripture supplies us with the marks of the church.

XXII. When the apostle says in Romans 10:17 that faith is from hearing, he
indeed intimates that the ministry of the church ought to intervene as the
ordinary means for producing faith in adults, but he does not therefore teach
that the church is clearer and better known than Scripture.

XXIII. It is one thing to inquire about the number, authors, parts, and
individual words of the sacred books; another, about the fundamental doctrine
contained in those books. The latter knowledge pertains to every believer,
but not the former. The salvation of one who did not know by whom the Gospel
of Matthew was written would not be endangered, provided he believed the book
to be authentic and divine. Knowledge of the primary author of a book is one
thing; knowledge only of his amanuensis is another. The latter belongs only
to historical faith; the former, to divine faith.

XXIV. Since according to the philosophers a circle is a sophistical argument
in which the same thing is proved through itself, revolving without end
within the same kind of cause, we cannot be charged with a circle when we
prove Scripture through the Spirit and, in turn, the Spirit from Scripture.
For here the question is different and the means or kind of cause is another.
We prove Scripture through the Spirit as through the efficient cause by which
we believe, but we prove the Spirit from Scripture as from the object and
argument because of which we believe. In the first, the question “From what
source, or by what power, do you believe Scripture to be divine?” is answered:
namely, through the Spirit. In the second, however, the question “Why, or
because of what, do you believe the Spirit who is in you to be the Holy
Spirit?” is answered: namely, because of the marks of the Holy Spirit which
are in Scripture. But the papists who object to our circle evidently fall
into it themselves in this question when they prove Scripture from the church
and the church from Scripture, for this is done through the same means and
the same kind of cause. If we ask, “Why, or because of what, do they believe
Scripture to be divine?” they answer, “Because the church says so.” If we ask
again, “Why do they believe the church?” they reply, “Because Scripture
attributes infallibility to it when it calls it the pillar and support of the
truth.” If we press, “How do they know that this testimony of Scripture is
‘self-evident’ (αὐτόπιστον)?” they add, “Because the church makes us certain
of it.” Thus they circle back to the point from which they began the dispute
and proceed to infinity, unable to stop at any first credible thing. Nor is
the question different here, for in both cases the inquiry concerns the
reason and argument because of which I believe, not the faculty or principle
through which I believe.

XXV. The church is called “the pillar and support of the truth” (στύλος καὶ
ἑδραίωμα τῆς ἀληθείας), 1 Timothy 3:15, not because it supports the truth and
secures authority for it, since the truth is rather the foundation of the
church upon which it is built, Ephesians 2:20, but because in

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 86 | IA leaf 140 | PDF p. 140]

the church, as upon a pillar, the truth stands forth and presents itself to be
seen by all. The church is therefore called a pillar not in an architectural
sense, inasmuch as pillars are used to support a building, but in a public and
political sense. Edicts of the prince and decrees and laws of the magistrate
were customarily affixed to pillars before courts and praetoria and in front
of the doors of basilicas, so that they might become known to everyone, as
Pliny notes in *Natural History* 6.28 and Josephus in *Antiquities* 1.4. Thus
the church is the pillar of the truth, both by reason of promulgation and
proclamation, because it is bound to promulgate the laws of God and heavenly
truth is hung upon it so that it may become known to everyone, and by reason
of custody, because it ought not only to present the truth but also to
vindicate and defend it. Therefore it is called not only a “pillar” (στύλος)
but also a “support” (ἑδραίωμα), the bulwark by which known truth is vindicated
and preserved safe and sound against every corruption. But it is not the
“foundation” (θεμέλιον) which gives the truth itself its “substance”
(ὑπόστασιν) and firmness. 2. Whatever is called the pillar and support of the
truth is not therefore immediately infallible. For the ancients called those
who excelled others in the splendor of doctrine, holiness of life, or firmness
of constancy, and who confirmed the doctrine of the gospel and the Christian
faith by doctrine or example, by this name. Thus the believers of Lyons in
Eusebius, *History* 5.1, call Attalus the martyr by this name; Basil, epistle
120, designates by this name the orthodox bishops who opposed the Arian
heresy: “the pillars and the support of the truth” (οἱ στύλοι καὶ τὸ ἑδραίωμα
τῆς ἀληθείας). Gregory Nazianzen similarly calls Athanasius this. In the same
sense, upright and incorrupt judges in a republic are called the pillars and
supports of the laws. 3. The passage teaches the office of the church, but not
its infallible privilege: what it is bound to do in promulgating and defending
the truth against the corruptions of the adversaries, not what it always
does. Thus names are often drawn from an office rather than the reality of the
matter. In Malachi 2:7 the lips of the priest are said to preserve knowledge
because he is bound to do this, although he does not always do it, as verse 8
teaches. 4. Whatever is attributed here to the church pertains to the
particular Ephesian church, to which the papists nevertheless would not wish
to give the privilege of infallibility. Furthermore, the passage concerns the
collective church of the faithful, in which Timothy was to live and exercise
his ministry, not a representative church of pastors, and much less the
pontiff, in whom alone they suppose “freedom from error” (ἀναμαρτησίαν) to
reside.
5. Paul alludes here, first, to the use of pillars in the temples of the
pagans, on which images of the gods, laws and moral precepts, and even oracles
were hung, as Pausanias and Athenaeus testify. Thus he contrasts those pillars
of falsehood and error, where nothing but fables and images of false gods were
presented, with this mystical pillar of truth, in which the true image of the
invisible God is presented, Colossians 1:15, and the heavenly oracles of God
are set forth. Second, he alludes to that memorable pillar which Solomon had
caused to be erected in the temple, concerning which see 2 Chronicles 6:13;
2 Kings 11:14; 23:3. The kings ascended it as a platform whenever they

[Marginal heading: The passage of Augustine, *Against the Fundamental Epistle*, chapter 5.]

XXVI. The passage of Augustine, *Against the Fundamental Epistle*, chapter 5,
“I would not believe the gospel unless the authority of the church moved me,”
does not favor the papists. 1. Augustine is speaking about himself as still a
Manichaean and not yet a Christian, and what he puts in the imperfect has the
force of the pluperfect: “I would believe” and “would move” for “I would have
believed” and “would have moved,” in a form of speech very common among
Africans, as the learned observe. So in *Confessions* 2.8, “If I then loved
those apples,” for “If I had loved.” 2. The authority of which he speaks is
not the authority of right and command which the adversaries allege here, as
though he believed because the church said so, but the authority of dignity,
which

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 87 | IA leaf 141 | PDF p. 141]

was derived from the great and illustrious arguments of God’s providence
which were seen in the church, such as miracles, antiquity, the agreement of
peoples, succession, and so forth, chapter 4. This authority can lead one by
the hand to faith, although it cannot principally produce it. 3. Thus an
external motive to faith is noted here, not an infallible principle of
believing. Augustine teaches in chapter 4 that this must be sought in truth
alone, when he acknowledges that truth must be preferred to all things if it
is demonstrated so perfectly that it cannot be called into doubt; and in
chapter 14: “Let us follow those who invite us first to believe, when we are
not yet able to perceive, so that, having been made stronger by faith itself,
we may deserve to understand what we believe—not now by human beings, but by
God himself inwardly forming and illuminating our mind.” Thus Peter of Ailly
understands it in question 1 on book 1 of the *Sentences*, article 3; and Cano,
*Theological Topics* 2.8, Gerson, Driedo, and Durandus interpret it of the
primitive and apostolic church, not of the present church for whose authority
the dispute is waged here. See our *Disputation on the Authority of
Scripture*.
