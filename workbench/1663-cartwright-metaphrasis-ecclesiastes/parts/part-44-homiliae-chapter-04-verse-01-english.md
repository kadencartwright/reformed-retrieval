CHAPTER IV.

VERSE 1. A fool folds his hands and consumes his own flesh.

VERSE 2. Better, he says, is a palmful of rest than both fists full of trouble
and affliction of spirit.

VERSE 3. But again I saw this to be vanity beneath the sun.

VERSE 4. There is one man who has no second; he has neither son nor brother,
yet there is no end to all his labor. Even his eye is not satisfied with
riches, so that he should say, “And for whom do I labor, depriving my soul of
good?” This too is vanity and an evil occupation.

VERSE 5. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward from their
labor.

VERSE 6. For if they fall, one of them raises his companion; but woe to him,
to that solitary man who falls, when there is no second to raise him.

VERSE 7. Again, if two lie down, they grow warm; but how would one alone grow
warm?

VERSE 8. If someone is stronger than one of them, the two together will resist
him; in short, a threefold cord is not quickly broken.

Having in the preceding discourse demonstrated life's vanity from the
injuries which men inflict upon one another, as beasts do, and which they
suffer not only from those who possess power but also from other men who are
their equals and even their inferiors, and who envy their advantages and deeds
well done, he now proceeds with the same argument, explaining the vanity to
which this life is subject. For he teaches that destruction is not only
prepared for men by others, but that men themselves moreover bring harm upon
themselves, in such a way that even if no trouble were inflicted by others,
harm would nevertheless be prepared by the men themselves.

The summary is that both alternatives are full of vanity: both languishing in
idleness and torturing and mangling oneself with immoderate labor.

The parts concern the vanity of hatred and sloth, and immoderate care and
labor. He begins with the idle man, who is set in opposition to the man who
contracted envy through excellent labors.

VERSE 1. A fool folds his hands and consumes his own flesh.

The idle man is described by the circumstance of the title of folly which is
attributed to him—and that by Solomon, a man of surpassing wisdom, who brands
him with this blemish and mark of disgrace by the Holy Spirit's authority.
The Prophet appropriately bestows this title upon him, not only because he
wished by this means to deter men from idleness, but also in order to beat
down and restrain the sluggard's

high opinions of himself. For, to sum up, it is clear from Solomon that
although the idle man is ignorant, he nevertheless swells and puffs up with an
utterly empty opinion of his own wisdom. Proverbs 26:16. He therefore gives
him this title of folly in
order to sting him more sharply, when the idle man is declared the opposite
of what he imagines himself to be. 1 Timothy 6:4.

He next describes him by the effect of folding his hands. See Proverbs 6:10;
elsewhere, in Proverbs 26:15, the same sense is conveyed by inserting his hand
under his armpit, or into his sleeve, as some prefer. By this manner of speech
he most aptly describes the idleness and sluggishness of the slothful man. For
the hand is the instrument with which we undertake and complete every kind of
occupation; hence every kind of action is also often called the work of the
hands. Therefore, when the hands are folded and confined by idleness as if by
bonds, the man is permitted to undertake or handle nothing. Conversely,
persistence in work is indicated in Proverbs 31:17 and 19 by stretching out
and applying the hand to the task. It is not enough to talk with the tongue
about business and labor, nor is it enough to run here and there on one's feet
unless the hands are applied to the task. This is the point of Proverbs 14:13:
“In every labor there is abundance, but in the words of the lips there is
poverty only.”

This sloth and folding of the hands in the exercise of good works is no less
hateful to God than abstention from labor is harmful to men. While displaying
zeal for God with their tongue and outward profession, they remain empty and
unfruitful in deed; hence they must attain utter poverty in unfeigned piety
and religion.

The effect of this idleness and folding of the hands is indicated by the
statement that the fool eats or consumes his flesh, that is, himself. As often
in Scripture, “flesh” is taken by synecdoche for the whole man. He consumes
himself in many ways. First, he contracts poverty and lack of everything,
which will attack him like an armed soldier and an unexpected guest. If you
add to this the Apostle's command to the Thessalonians that food is not to be
supplied to the idle, everyone sees how truly it is said that the man who
languishes in idleness consumes himself. The saying of Solomon in Proverbs
26:15 seems to refer to this: the slothful man is weary and exhausted by
bringing his hand back to his mouth.

Next, when the body's health has been weakened by idleness, diseases are
summoned by an excess of humors, which, when exhaled by labor and honorable
occupations, preserve health. Moreover, the mind, cultivated and trained by
neither arts nor knowledge, loses its force and vigor. It is just like iron
which, because it is not polished or worn by use, contracts the rust by which
it is consumed; or like standing, stagnant waters which become corrupt. Thus
the idle man is found guilty not only of theft but of murder and even of
suicide. From this idleness he rushes into frauds and thefts, thereby
summoning the fetter, prison, whip, and gallows. He consumes not only his own
flesh but also that of his household, wife, and children, because they are his
flesh.

The whole world swarms with fools of this kind. There is no province, no town,
no village or hamlet, and scarcely any household which is free of them. Here
you should appropriately censure the reasons why the children of nobles and
the rich consider themselves demeaned if they learn an art by which they may
sustain this life.

2. The source divides `spiritus` as `spi- / ritus` across the PA177–PA179

   the exposition immediately proceeds to the idle man's aversion to work.

   extends Cartwright's claim that destructive idleness makes the sluggard
   responsible for consuming his own life.
