# CHAPTER IV

## That middle knowledge overturns God's dominion

**Argument 9.**

[Marginal reference: See the Scotsman Camerarius on the recent origin of this
knowledge, in *Selected Philosophical Disputations*, part 2, question 9, doubt
4, no. 1.]

I have placed the argument from God's dominion over all beings last because
this argument militates against: 1. middle knowledge; 2. the resistibility of
Arminian grace and the Jesuits' congruous grace; 3. it also proves that God
inflexibly predetermines created wills; 4. it strongly proves that the efficacy
of grace must be referred to grace itself and not to free choice. I have
therefore resolved to press this argument once here against them all: Jesuits,
Arminians, Socinians, and Pelagians.

[Marginal summary: Middle knowledge overturns God's dominion over every
contingent act of creatures.]

This middle knowledge overturns God's dominion over all the beings which God,
by his own independent choice, can cause either to exist in the nature of
things or not to exist. This knowledge therefore does not preserve providence
but overturns it. The antecedent is proved. One who has no dominion over free
acts, and cannot cause free acts to exist or not exist as it pleases him, has
no dominion over all beings. Yet given middle knowledge, the Arminian doctrine
of the resistible operation of grace, the dependence of grace's efficacy upon
free choice, and a liberty released from every predetermination by God, God
cannot cause free acts to exist in the nature of things rather than not to
exist, or the reverse. Therefore, given middle knowledge and Arminian doctrine,
God has no dominion over free acts. But it is evident from Scripture and sound
reason that God has dominion over every free act. 1.

[Chapter IV; printed page 29; PDF page 81]

Ephesians 3:20: “Since God can do all things superabundantly, beyond what we
ask”—and therefore can furnish faith and the free acts of perseverance for
which the saints daily pray to God—“or think, according to that power at work
in us.” Jude 24: “God is able to keep us from falling.” Romans 11:23: “God is
able to graft them in again,” and thus to procure the free conversion of the
Jews. Romans 14:4: “For God is able to establish him.” 1 John 4:4: “Little
children, you are from God and have overcome them, because he who is in you is
more powerful than he who is in the world.” 1 Peter 1:5: “You who are guarded
by the protection of God's power through faith for salvation.” John 10:29: “My
Father, who gave them—the sheep—to me, is greater than all, and no one will
snatch them from my hand.”

No firm ground of hope, confidence, consolation, patience, or gratitude toward
God can be drawn from these passages unless God possesses efficacious dominion
over a free act, so that, notwithstanding every force or innate power of the
created will, he can procure either the existence or the nonexistence of every
free act. 2 Corinthians 9:8: “God is able to make all grace abound toward you.”
He can make it necessary that you abound and overflow in riches, which often
come through contingently operating causes, or in grace—that is, Christian
generosity—which is performed through the exercise of liberty.

Matthew 19:26: “With human beings this is impossible, but with God all things
are possible.” That is, God can procure that the rich rely on God rather than
riches and thus enter the kingdom of heaven, something as impossible for
people endowed with free choice as “for a camel to pass through the eye of a
needle.” Nehemiah 1:10: “They are your people whom you redeemed by your great
power and strong hand.” This redemption, however, was accomplished through
many free acts: Moses and Aaron freely went into Egypt to Pharaoh and freely
spoke; the people freely left Egypt; and the Egyptians freely pursued them
into the channel of the Red Sea. Romans 15:13: “That you may abound in hope
through the power of the Holy Spirit.” Romans 16:25: “Now to him who is able to
establish you according to my gospel, be glory,” and so forth.

But given middle knowledge, prior to his free decree and therefore to the free
exercise of his power, God knows that many free acts will occur and knows their
impending occurrence or futurition. These acts therefore precede God's middle
knowledge. Consequently, they possess true futurition by nature before God
knows it by the intuition

[Chapter IV; printed page 30; PDF page 82]

of middle knowledge. Much more, then, do they possess futurition before God
decrees that such acts will occur freely. There is consequently no providence
of God here. The Tyrians' repentance emerges if they see signs, Job's patience
if the Chaldeans plunder him, and Peter's conversion if he is called in this or
that way, because neither God's knowledge nor his free will contributes any
causality toward procuring the existence or nonexistence of these free acts.

The more subtle defenders reply that, from infinite possible worlds and
infinite arrangements of things and circumstances, God continually rejects
this or that world and arrangement of things until he finally comes upon a
world or arrangement in which he foreknows that the Tyrians will be converted
if they see signs and that Job will be patient if the Chaldeans plunder him.
Then, when he has come to such an arrangement, God definitely chooses it and,
from the absolute liberty of his dominion, rejects the others. I answer as
follows.

1. Let God turn over infinite worlds and arrangements of things: it is possible
that he should never come upon an arrangement in which he foreknows that the
Tyrians will be converted if they see signs. For the Tyrians always remain free
not to repent even if they see a thousand times a thousand signs, since the
Jesuits teach that God cannot employ any omnipotent force upon the free will
but must act resistibly. In this case, therefore, God has no more dominion over
*the act of repenting* if the Tyrians see signs than over *the act of not
repenting* if they see miracles. God cannot foreknow as future what will never
be future. Yet throughout the infinite possible worlds, the essence of the
free will remains inviolate, which, according to our opponents, always and in
every case can act and can refrain from acting.

[Marginal summary: Foreknowledge of such an arrangement of things as the
Jesuits imagine does not preserve God's dominion over the free acts which
middle knowledge sees as future.]

2. Even in this case, God would evidently have dominion in choosing this
arrangement of things and rejecting the others after this arrangement already
possessed futurition. But it is not evident that God has any dominion over *the
act of repenting* rather than *the act of not repenting*, because both the act
of repenting and that of not repenting precede God's knowledge, and therefore
much more precede God's decrees and power, which does not operate except as it
is determined by the will.

3. That arrangement of things is not necessary, as is evident, because in that
case it could not fail to exist; it is chosen as contingent. Therefore the same
argument comes alive again concerning that arrangement: does God have dominion
over it? I demonstrate that he does not. God cannot fail to foreknow the
futurition of that arrangement which will in fact be future. For

[Chapter IV; printed page 31; PDF page 83]

he cannot fail to foreknow every future. No knowable thing—and according to our
opponents this arrangement is knowable—can escape God's infinite cognition.
Nor can he foreknow other possible worlds as future, because in reality they
will never be future. God's knowledge is therefore bound here; but in this way
God's will is bound much more, and all God's free dominion will perish.

I suppose that God wishes to choose that there be a certain determinate number
of elect people, say 14,467. Given the indifference of the created will, before
God's decree at least two of them could have failed to believe, and so the
number of the elect could have been only 14,465. For according to the defenders
of middle knowledge, all who believe when they are called in this or that way
believe antecedently to all God's foreknowledge, and thus antecedently to all
God's will. God consequently possesses no free act and therefore no dominion
over the determinate number of the elect. They may say that “the Tyrians'
repentance if they see signs” is indeed future antecedently to God's absolute
will, but not antecedently to God's hypothetical will. Yet this is really to
fall into a more serious absurdity. God's conditioned will is not prior to the
determinate truth, “If the Tyrians see signs, they will repent.” Otherwise
God's will would rest upon another supposition prior to the truth of “If the
Tyrians see signs, they will repent,” and there would be an infinite regress.
As I taught above, this proposition is not formally hypothetical, for the truth
of the proposition rests upon no hypothesis. The Tyrians' repentance is
formally hypothetical, but the proposition itself is not, nor is God's will
concerning the Tyrians' repentance.

13. The Jesuit Fasolo replies as follows: “God foreknows that if Peter were
placed in such a set of circumstances, he would deny Christ. But,” he says,
“God can simply make him not deny Christ by removing some foreseen circumstance,
for example fear of death, or by adding another, for example greater light in
the intellect or a greater inclination in the will toward the good of
confession. If God did not wish to remove the circumstances, he could not fail
to foreknow that the sin will occur; nevertheless, its futurition depends upon
his conditioned will, not his absolute will.”

[Marginal reference: Fasolo on Aquinas, part 1, tome 2, question 14, article 13,
doubt 13, point 2, figure 261.]

Response 1. To establish God's dominion it does not suffice that God could make
Peter not deny Christ. God could have called Peter out of this life before
Christ was captured or before Peter conversed with the maidservant who tempted
him, but this would be for God to possess dominion

[Chapter IV; printed page 32; PDF page 84]

over Peter's life, not over Peter's free choice. For God to possess dominion
over Peter's free choice and free acts, it is necessary that, once all the
conditions required for acting have been posited, God can procure that *the act
of not denying Christ* belongs to Peter's free choice, and that most freely,
namely through effective permission—or that Peter not deny Christ but piously
and freely abstain from denying him. For in this way, according to our
opponents, Peter himself alone, not God, possesses dominion over his free acts.
Yet it is certain, according to Augustine, that “God has greater dominion and
power over our wills than we ourselves possess over them.”

[Marginal summary: The Jesuits establish God's dominion over free acts very
badly.]

While I was living at Aberdeen, punished with imprisonment by the council's
High Commission, a certain most learned man said that God's dominion over free
acts was sufficiently established because God could prevent any free acts and
cause such acts never to exist in the nature of things. But I answer now as I
did then: to cause such free acts never to exist in the nature of things by
annihilating the will, as Grevinchovius said, by calling the person out of this
life, or by entirely removing the object is not to rule over free choice or
free acts as such. Perhaps in this way it is evident that God possesses
dominion over free nonacts, but not over free acts. Thus, 1. Judas possessed
dominion over many acts which he could have elicited in this life had he not
ended his life with a noose; but God's dominion over free acts is different
and more independent.

[Marginal reference: Dr. Robert Baron, professor of theology at the University
of Aberdeen.]

2. God rules over our free acts in a far different way than over the free acts
of the infinite number of people whom he decreed never to create. We do not say
with Scripture, “God can effectually call all those people whom he never creates
in the nature of things, graft them into Christ, and keep them from falling.”
But God does possess such dominion over the free choice of all who live in the
visible church.

3. For God to rule over free choice as such is for God to be able to cause free
acts either to exist here and now or not to exist here and now, even after all
the conditions required for acting have been posited. Jurists define dominion
as “each person's proper power to use a thing for every use permitted by law.”
Such dominion over free acts therefore belongs to God.

4. I return to Fasolo. Nor, according to the defenders of middle knowledge,
could God cause Peter not to deny Christ if he were placed in such a situation
by removing the circumstances, because those circumstances—namely Peter's
entrance

[Chapter IV; printed page 33; PDF page 85]

into the courtyard, the maidservant's questioning, and Peter's fear—are purely
contingent things dependent upon the entirely free choice of Peter and the
maidservant. They therefore possessed futurition from eternity antecedently to
middle knowledge, and thus antecedently to God's free decree. Yet it is
impossible for God to rule over and cause to exist or not exist in nature, as
he freely wills, what was future antecedently to his free will. It is impossible
for me today to have dominion over my free acts of yesterday.

5. That fear is either removed while Peter's liberty is preserved, or it is
not. If the former, it is just as possible for the fear not to be removed as to
be removed, and so God's dominion in this matter falls. If the latter is said,
once the fear was removed a hope of gain, honor, or leisure could succeed it,
tempting Peter to deny the Lord just as much as fear of death. Where then, I
ask, is God's dominion? “Even when fear of death was removed and greater light
was added to the intellect and a greater inclination toward the good of
confession,” Peter could still have denied the Lord. For the removal of the
fear of death, that greater light, and that inclination would not have
determined Peter's will not to deny the Lord. Where, then, is God's dominion?

6. According to the Jesuits' tenets, it is false “that the futurition of sin
depends upon God's conditioned will, not his absolute will.” For, 1. on this
account God wills sin to occur by his conditioned will, which the Jesuits would
not admit. 2. God does not say, “By my conditioned will I will Peter to sin
rather than not to sin, so that I possess dominion over *the act of sinning* or
*the act of not sinning*.” No condition upon which God's will depends can be
imagined here. On what condition, I ask, does God will Peter to sin if he is
tempted? Even that ought not to be said.

1. The question concerns the connection between Peter's sin and his temptation:
is this connection made by God's will or by a conditioned will? In this way a
new condition of the condition would be given which cannot be imagined.
3. The maidservant's temptation is itself a sin, and another question will
return concerning it.

5. Consider conditionally future things which are not sins, such as this: “If
the Chaldeans plunder Job, Job will be patient.” God will evidently have no
dominion over Job's patience or non-patience, because the connection between
Job's patience and the Chaldeans' active plundering does not arise from God's
will ruling over either part of the contradiction. Otherwise God's will would
be the ruling cause

[Chapter IV; printed page 34; PDF page 86]

of Job's patience rather than his non-patience if he is plundered. But a cause
which possesses dominion over a free act so that it exists rather than does not
exist is a cause which equally and just as strongly necessitates that free act
by antecedent necessity. It is a physically predetermining cause, which is an
intolerable dogma to the Jesuits and Arminians.

Suppose Peter foreknows by God's revelation that he will persuade John to
dispute tomorrow, provided John wishes to dispute. No one would say that Peter
possesses dominion over John's will so that tomorrow's disputation occurs
rather than does not occur, because unless John wishes, he will certainly not
dispute. One who foreknows something future does not, by foreknowing it, possess
dominion over that thing's futurition, for foreknowledge is not the cause of a
thing. It is therefore not a cause possessing dominion over the thing. Yet God
only foreknows that “Job will be patient if he is plundered”; he does not will
Job to be patient if plundered. According to Fasolo, no will of God is required
to cause the connection, and this connection is prior to every act of divine
knowledge. Much more, therefore, is it prior to every act of the divine will.
God's will is consequently not the cause of such futures, and much less is it
their ruling cause or a cause possessing dominion over such futures.

14. The Arminians say that “after God deigned to enter the covenant of grace in
Christ with everyone and established certain and most wise laws for creatures,
he cannot will to deal with human beings according to his absolute dominion.”

[Marginal references: The Remonstrants' written submission to the Synod,
article 1, p. 258; Conrad Vorstius, *On God*, thesis 7, no. 3.]

They say that to establish God's dominion it is sufficient that he could
absolutely annihilate the will, even though ordinarily and on the hypothesis
of his decree he cannot restrain the will from freely acting, willing, refusing,
choosing, or suspending an act. The Jesuit Raynaud therefore says, “God cannot
prevent sin while preserving the order of the universe and the office of the
most universal cause.”

[Marginal references: Théophile Raynaud, *Natural Theology*, distinction 1,
question 3, article 1, figure 52. According to the Jesuits, God cannot prevent
sin. Romans 11:23; 14:4.]

Response 1. It is certain that God established laws for creatures according to
his immutable decrees. But it begs the question to say that God decreed this
natural law of free choice to be fixed: that it can turn itself wherever it
wishes independently of all God's dominion. This is granted not even to
sparrows or to the hairs of our heads. For ordinarily and absolutely, “he who
can do all things and turns the hearts of kings wherever he wishes can convert
the Jews, establish and confirm the elect who are falling, and preserve them
from falling,” not by absolute and extraord-

[Chapter IV; printed page 35; PDF page 87]

inary power, but according to the counsel of his will and the settled and fixed
law by which he brings the elect to glory. Yet he can convert others by the same
power unless something different had been settled and decreed by him from
eternity.

[Marginal references: Romans 9:24; Philippians 2:13; Proverbs 21:1; Ephesians
1:11.]

2. According to our opponents, not even by absolute power can God procure
Peter's free act if Peter resists, for to them it is a contradiction that God
possesses dominion which determines free consent.

3. If God rules over a being as being, he also possesses dominion over this
being—over this or that free act. For, supposing the existence of the Medes'
free will, God swears, by a reason drawn from his invincible dominion and decree
which cannot be resisted, that he will cause the Medes to devastate the
Babylonians. Supposing the existence of the Jews' free will, Paul says that
“God is able to graft in the Jews,” and experience establishes that God is able
not to graft the Jews into Christ, for they remain hardened to this day.

[Marginal references: Isaiah 14:17; Romans 11:23.]

4. The sacred oracles will us to place confidence and hope in that power of God
by which he can support us, establish our wills to will rightly and
supernaturally, and preserve us from falling until the day of the glorious
coming of our Lord Jesus Christ. But consolation would have no support if God
could not do this except by annihilating our will. The whole comfort is that
God possesses the power to procure holy willing until Christ's coming and that
the created will does not possess the power to procure an unwillingness to
persevere. Yet what consolation, confidence, hope, or joy in this power of God
would remain for us if what the Jesuit Raynaud says is true: “God cannot
prevent sin, and thus the final apostasy of all the saints, while preserving
the order of the universe and the office of the most universal cause”? Can our
faith stand in this way?

Whenever I pray, “Lord, do not lead me into temptation; do not permit me to
wander from your commands,” I am saying, “I pray, Lord, that you overturn the
order of the universe and abandon the task and office which you discharge as
the first cause of all. I pray, my God, that you annihilate my will. I pray that
you prevent me from sinning but grant me freely to abstain through more
abundant grace—something, however, which is impossible for you, O God.”

15. Diego Ruiz raises this objection to himself and answers it: “Dominion is
defined as each person's proper power to use a thing for every use permitted
by law. Since, therefore, God is the true Lord of all secondary causes, he uses
them when and how he wills. But he cannot do this unless he physically
predetermines the will to its acts.” “I answer,” says Ruiz,

[Marginal references: Ruiz on Aquinas, *On God's Knowledge*, disputation 49,
section 2; Thomas, *On Power*, question 7, article 10, reply 4.]

[Chapter IV; printed page 36; PDF page 88]

“God therefore possesses absolute and independent dominion because he can
transfer another, secondary dominion over the created will, which would be
bound by physical predetermination. The fullness of God's dominion would
therefore be bound if he could not transfer this dominion. For one who cannot
grant the dominion and use of an estate to another person is not its perfect
lord.”

[Marginal summary: Diego Ruiz grants God a certain dominion over the created
will with respect to *being*, but with respect to *operation* he takes still
more away.]

Response 1. One who grants another greater dominion than he himself possesses
or can possess is neither an absolute nor a just lord, except by a palpable
contradiction. God therefore possesses no absolute dominion over free acts; but
according to the Jesuit-Arminians, the created will possesses such dominion.
The antecedent is proved: in no case, without an open contradiction, does God
possess such dominion over free acts that *the act of willing*—that is, the
human will's election and consent—rests with God rather than *the act of being
unwilling*—that is, the same will's rejection and dissent. According to our
opponents, the will would then be both free and not free. For God would cause
the act of willing rather than the act of being unwilling, or the reverse,
just as strictly and precisely as if he physically predetermined the free will
to a free act, which is plainly contradictory to our opponents.

God does indeed possess the power that no free acts should exist in the nature
of things, as would have been the case had he created neither human beings nor
angels. In that case neither God nor rational creatures would have possessed
actual dominion over being unwilling or willing. Yet in this way God would have
possessed dominion over wills with respect to *being*, not over wills as they
are principles of free actions and with respect to *operation*. A person is
said to rule primarily and independently over a free act, with the independence
of free dominion over acts, when it rests with him that willing or choosing
exists rather than being unwilling and rejecting. Yet according to our
opponents such dominion belongs to the created will, not to God, except by an
open contradiction. God therefore cannot grant a rational creature dominion
which he himself does not possess—indeed, which it involves a contradiction
for him to possess.

This would imply injustice in God—let such blasphemies be far from us—just as
if someone who was not himself the full lord of an estate, since he could not
lawfully sell or alienate it, were to grant the estate to another person with
power to sell and alienate it. One who gives another that of which he himself
is an unjust possessor is unjust. Yet it is obvious to all that this dominion
of God is secondary and dependent, while that of the created will is primary,
absolute, and

[Chapter IV; printed page 37; PDF page 89]

independent, according to our opponents. For them this is a valid consequence:
“The Sabeans will this; therefore the free act of pursuing Job with hatred
exists, whether God wills it or not.” Yet this consequence is invalid: “God
wills the Sabeans to love; therefore the Sabeans love Job.”

2. It is incompatible with God's absolute dominion to confer upon another a
dominion by which something can occur simply against God's will. Yet according
to our opponents God gives such dominion to another, namely to free agents. Not
even through his omnipotence can God cause the Sabeans freely to love Job and
their free action not to be infected with hatred, supposing that he has created
free Sabeans who live near the wealthy Job.

3. A more absolute lord of an estate is one who can grant its dominion and use
to another in such a way that the actual use of the estate in this or that
manner always remains with the grantor. He is more absolute than one who gives
another the estate with absolute liberty to use it in a way which the grantor
himself detests and does not will to occur, but which he cannot in the least
prevent. The Jesuit Juan de Lugo speaks more correctly: “God would not be
essentially and perfectly Lord if by promise or covenant he abdicated dominion
over any of our operations, for his dominion is incommunicable to a creature.”

[Marginal reference: Juan de Lugo, *On the Mystery of the Incarnation*,
disputation 3, section 2.]

The analogy also limps in this respect: God's dominion over every being—and
therefore over *the act of willing* rather than *the act of not willing*—is
greater and more absolute than any created lord's dominion over an estate,
since every created being is a being through dependence upon, and the free
grant and generosity of, the first being. According to this opinion, however,
God cannot procure the act of willing rather than the act of being unwilling
when he wishes. What sort of dominion is this?

16. Ruiz replies in the same place: “If dominion is taken strictly, it follows
only that God can apply secondary causes to their operations and use them when
and how he wills. But one who can use can also refrain from using, as Aristotle
says in *Ethics* 3.5. Therefore, because of that faculty of using them, God can
refrain from using and applying them to their operations and merely permit
them.” This is confirmed. 1. The faculty of using the will through physical
predetermination would not be dominion but servitude, and God would necessarily
be bound to use it. 2. His dominion would be diminished if he could not permit
the creature to will and do things which it is fitting for God neither to will
nor do. 3. Just as it is more perfect wisdom to permit evil actions, so this is
more perfect dominion.

I answer. 1. If dominion is defined in this way, then it does not be-

[Chapter IV; printed page 38; PDF page 90]

long to God as strict dominion over free acts, but, according to the Jesuits,
rightly belongs to the created will. God cannot procure in the Sabeans' will
*the unwillingness to hate Job* or *the willingness to love Job*. Once
everything required for acting has been posited on God's part, it can happen
that the Sabeans love Job even if God has decreed to inflict their hatred upon
Job as something penal and corrective. And it can happen that the Sabeans
pursue Job with hatred, although in accordance with the promise of Proverbs
3:4 God most greatly wished to procure their favor and love for him. Dominion
over the acts will therefore evidently belong here to the Sabeans, not God.

2. According to the Jesuit-Arminians it is false, despite the argument from
Aristotle, “that God can refrain from using the free will because he can use
it.” God cannot refrain from using the Sabeans' evil will in punishing Job,
because according to our opponents, notwithstanding every volition and action
of God, and with God simply unwilling, the Sabeans pursue Job with hatred.

3. Given Jesuit permission, by which God is released from applying the will to
willing rather than being unwilling, God cannot possess dominion over the free
act. Thus “he can refrain from using” is the same as saying that he lacks the
power of using and consequently lacks dominion.

4. On our position God's faculty of using the created will is in no way
restricted or bound by physical predetermination. Rather, God most freely
predetermines a will which acts freely. There is therefore no “servitude or
necessitation of God” here.

5. Nor would God's dominion be diminished if he could procure in good acts
*being unwilling* rather than willing, or *willing* rather than being unwilling.
The question concerns God's dominion over good acts—which is entirely removed
by the Jesuit-Arminian opinion—as much as God's dominion over evil acts. Nor do
we deny that God permits evil acts. Yet he permits them so that God's dominion
over *the act of sinning* rather than *the act of not sinning* remains evident,
since sin is often penal in the obstinate. The supreme Judge possesses absolute
dominion over every penal act and over the punishments to be inflicted upon a
creature which strays.

6. To permit evil actions by a permission consonant with the dominion of
providence is most worthy of God's wisdom—not by the Jesuit-Arminian permission
which releases the will from God's dominion in inflicting punishment upon the
sinner.

17. Ruiz raises and answers an objection to himself: “But all God's dominion
excludes there being with God more or fewer volitions of de-

[Marginal summary: Diego Ruiz withdraws the number of contingent volitions
from God's providential dominion.]

[Chapter IV; printed page 39; PDF page 91]

mons and human beings than demons and human beings will, rather than as God
wills and decrees?” Ruiz answers: “Through the material action of sin, the will
of demons and human beings in some way withdraws itself from God's dominion and
use, because it is not subjected to God as perfectly as upright actions are.
But permission, indifferent concurrence, and punishment of the evil action
suffice for dominion.”

I answer: 1. This argument concerning God's dominion over every good and evil
free act militates equally against our opponents, but the Jesuit answers only
about God's dominion over evil acts. If God does not rule over every free act
in such a way that choosing rather than rejecting rests with God, then God's
providence determines no certain number of human and angelic volitions and, by
the same reasoning, no certain number of elect and reprobate people or angels.
This is blasphemous against God's providence. The number of drops in the ocean
and rains, the tiny grains of sand on all the earth's shores, leaves on trees,
stars in the heavens, hairs of human beings and beasts, plants, flies, and all
beings according to their individual instances is determined and predefined
by God's providential decree—unless we wish to deny God's particular providence.

2. Every positive being as such is equally subjected to God's dominion.
Therefore the material act of hatred of God in demons and human beings is also
subjected to that dominion.

3. How rigid this providence is! Notwithstanding God's providence and the
dominion which he possesses over the spirits of darkness no less than over
rabid lions, can demons nevertheless elicit as many evil acts of wicked
volitions as they, rather than God, please?

[Marginal summary: All acts of the will, both elicited and commanded, are
equally subjected to God's dominion.]

4. Nor is there any reason why the commanded acts of demons should escape God's
dominion any more than their elicited acts. Both their commanded and elicited
acts are wicked, diabolical, and opposed to the dominion of God's holy and
eternal law. Devils could therefore rage against people, God's church, and its
pastors as much as they please. For the question arises why devils and
reprobates should not be equally free, notwithstanding the dominion of divine
providence, to perpetrate evil against God's church, since it remains within
their power to intend, contrive, and plot evil in their minds. Every act of
creatures is equally subjected to the dominion of providence.

material act of sin, and to deny this is merely to beg what is in question.

6. Dominion does not consist in the permission of sin, for their permission is
nonaction, nonuse, and nondominion over the will. But it is ridiculous for
dominion to consist in nondominion.

7. Nor does God's dominion consist in Jesuit general concurrence. By nature
the will of demons and human beings first exercises influence, and thus rules
over the free act or free nonact, before God exercises influence through an
indifferent concurrence which is determinable by the free cause toward willing
rather than being unwilling. Since it therefore rests with the created will
that God concurs rather than does not concur, and that God concurs toward good
willing rather than good unwillingness, or the reverse, the created will in
this way rules over God's concurrence rather than nonconcurrence, and over the
free act rather than the free nonact. Consequently no dominion belongs to God's
concurrence. Nor would any sane person say that, in the dominion which he
possesses by concurring with the evil will of demons and the damned, God intends
demons and the damned to obey him rather than disobey, as the nature of dominion
requires.

8. Punishment of an evil action is dominion over the punishment of an evil
action which already actually exists. It is not dominion over the action itself
to cause the action's existence rather than its nonexistence. The question is
therefore evaded, and no dominion over the determinate number of the actions of
demons, human beings, or good angels is left to God.

and under some precise aspect or formality it is not subject to God's free
power, on the supposition that God freely wished to deal with the will in such
a way as to leave it to its own liberty. Just so, it is not absurd that God
could not cause Peter not to be absolutely about to will this, on the

[Marginal reference and summary: Diego Ruiz on Aquinas, disputation 67, section

I answer: 1. The reasoning is in no way parallel

future which were future before every decree of his. For a future not to be
future involves a contradiction.

2. The Jesuit puts the weapons into our hands. Supposing the existence of human

contradiction for God to cause this free act not to be future on the hypothesis
that it is future. Yet it is future antecedently to every decree of God, and,
according to our opponents' tenets, not even by all his omnipotence can God
prevent such free hypotheses which possess futurition antecedently to God's

19. “If the will is physically predetermined by God, then the free will has no

will possesses such dominion that it can dispose of an act or nonact just as
one who is not subject to a guardian can dispose of his goods without the

dominion over acts belongs to God than to the free will itself. Yet according

subordinate dominions, such as those of a king and his legate, can belong to

I respond: Boethius defines only penal dominion, not free dominion. 2. Grant,

the Lord. If, however, Judas does not abstain from betrayal with a holy end and

dominion lies in procuring and using a thing no less than in preventing it.

3. Raynaud candidly confessed, consistently with the Jesuits' tenets, that
“God cannot prevent sin while preserving the order of the universe and the
office of the most universal cause.” For, given every action of God, if Judas
is tempted, God cannot cause him not to consent to betraying the Savior.

3. If God cannot prevent sin simply—as on our opponents' doctrine he cannot—he
cannot prevent sin six or eight times either, for the reasoning is the same in
both cases.

4. A force in the will which paralyzed its power to act would remove liberty.
God would therefore not procure holy and praiseworthy abstinence from sin and
would not rule over the free act as such. Yet in the prayer “Lead us not into
temptation,” we ask God to procure free, holy, and praiseworthy abstinence from
sin through his gracious dominion over the will.

“But,” à Dola says, “he could prevent the action of the cause by not conferring

and individuation. For if it could not determine itself, it would in fact
produce no action.” I answer: 1. If God removed from the will the power by which
it could determine itself, he would remove liberty. Its abstinence would then
not be praiseworthy but a compelled nontransgression of the law. 2. God would
thus annihilate the will, and the question is evaded.

“He can,” à Dola says, “prevent the action by introducing or increasing
resistance in the passive subject.” I answer that this resistance would not
remove liberty and so would not effectually prevent sin. But if it removed
liberty, where, I ask, is God's dominion over the will as free? 2. God cannot
increase the propensity to sin unless he is the author of sin.

“He can,” à Dola says, “prevent the creature's action if we say that creatures

the causes from acting,”

upon God's permission.]

inclined to act to be prevented by bare nonaction as such.

2. Suppose permission is a required condition of acting. Once permission is
given, the will can act or not act; otherwise, on our opponents' account, God
will be the author of sin. Or, if the will can refrain from acting once
permission is given, God could punish the sinner by permission while the sinner
went unpunished, which is absurd. For it would thus be possible for the sinner

would be compelled, neither holy nor praiseworthy, and not the sort of benefit

“God,” Ludovicus à Dola says in the same place, “determines the will through

1 John 3:8. For once God has permitted them to tempt, they cannot refrain from
tempting toward murders, poisonings, and such things. For in the same place you

23. Fourth, Aegidius Coninck attempts to establish this dominion—or, rather, to

created will will then be the first principle of its own determination. Coninck
answers: “For something to be called the first principle simply and without

I answer: 1. By this argument, however, we do not contend that the created will
is the first being simply, but that this follows as a consequence. That is, by
this argument we show that the created will possesses the incommunicable
property of the first being. It is not simply first, but is the first agent—a
property that belongs to the first being alone—because it acts prior by nature
to God or to God's providence. Nor does Raynaud's claim help: “Free actions are
subject to God because God can deny them his cooperation while preserving their
being and active power.” For on this account, 1. Peter would not sin by failing
to obey God's call, because an impossible act—one lacking the divine influx
indispensably required for a creature's act—is commanded to him.

[Marginal summary: Aegidius Coninck's escape concerning God's dominion over the
free will is rejected.]

2. The Jesuits falsely teach that it belongs to a creature's liberty to have
God's concurrence in its own power. But if God can, by his dominion, deny that
concurrence, the creature does not have God's concurrence in its own power.

24. Fifth, Hieronymus Fasolus argues against God's dominion over free acts as

2. If “the free nature of the will itself is the reason why God concurs with the

nor God's grace is that reason,” then the free nature of the created will—and
not God or God's grace—is the proximate reason why Peter responds to God's call

converted man glories in the nature of liberty alone, not in the Lord. He owes
the praise and glory of his conversion rather than nonconversion not to God and
his grace but to the indeterminate nature of his own liberty. The nature of
liberty itself, rather than God's grace, will be the separating and
discriminating cause between the converted and unconverted man. Pelagius once
taught nothing, and Socinus today teaches nothing, cruder than this.

3. A man is therefore converted rather than unconverted not because God wills,
but because the will itself wills. God consequently has no more dominion over
Peter's conversion than over his nonconversion.

4. It is absurd that determination “as a positive and real action depends more
intrinsically upon God than upon the creature, while as free it depends upon the
free nature of the will alone.” For if an action depends more upon God than upon
the creature, then this or that action, insofar as it is this action—for example,
insofar as it is free—will depend much more upon God than upon the nature of the
will. For what belongs to a genus in itself nec-*

[Chapter IV; printed page 48; PDF page 100]

“essarily belongs to all its species.” Since the action is more intrinsically
from God than from the will, the action identified with this free action—for a
genus does not exist separately from its species—will also be more intrinsically
from God than from the will. For it is incoherent that a determination of the
will should exist because God wills it, namely in the genus of action, but not
because the created will wills it; and that the same determination, considered
as free, should exist not because God wills it but solely because the
indeterminate nature of liberty requires it. For in good acts God wills that the
good act exist, while the created will often causes that good act not to exist.
The determination of the will, then, by which the action will exist in reality
—for in this respect the action depends much more intrinsically upon God than
upon the created will, as Fasolus holds—and the same determination as a free act
does not exist in reality—for so the created will, turning aside from God's law,
wills. Thus the same determination would both exist in reality and not exist,
which is a contradiction.

5. With respect to the specification of an act, the will's nature is indeed the

opponents does not concern God's dominion over acts of the will with respect to
the specification of the act. For it is ridiculous to attribute to God dominion
over creatures' acts with respect to their specification, except insofar as God,
the Creator of all, gives creatures such a nature: he gives fire the natural
principle of heating, the sun of illuminating, ice of cooling, and the will of
willing and not willing. The question instead concerns God's dominion over acts
of the will with respect to the exercise of those acts. I say that the proximate
cause why God concurs with fire toward this act of heating rather than that act
of heating is not the nature of fire but the free will of the first cause. The
Jesuit Coimbrans and eminent philosophers and theologians hold this. Therefore
the proximate cause why God concurs with the will toward this singular act of
believing in and embracing Christ rather than the act of resisting God when he
calls is God's gracious will alone, not the free nature of the will.
