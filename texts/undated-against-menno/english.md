# Against Menno

*A fresh translation of the complete consilium in CO X/1 167–176.*

## Translation

### [CO X/1, p. 167]

*Calvin to Micron.*

You certainly do not wear me out with your letters, most upright brother; it
is welcome and pleasant to be interrupted by them. I only wish that a happier
subject were given you, so that your joy might be shared with me. But since it
pleases the Lord to exercise us in this way, it ought not to be burdensome for
us to share between ourselves the cares that distress you on account of the
public cause of the church. If only a capacity equal to my desire were
available for helping you. Yet whatever effort and assistance is in me I shall
gladly impart to you.

Menno's doctrine, which he drew from the ravings of the Manichaeans, was not
unknown to me. I did not know his fallacies quite so thoroughly. To meet them,
it must be confessed and clearly explained—as I do not doubt that you have
done—that Christ, God manifested in the flesh, is the true Son of God. And
although the name *Son* properly belongs to the divine nature and is referred
to it, nevertheless that man who was born from the virgin is properly called
the Son. For insofar as he is mediator and head of the church, God and man, he
must be the Son of God by nature, so that by the grace of adoption he may join
us to himself.

But from this it is perversely inferred that the man was begotten from the
Father's seed. For on account of the secret and sacred union of divinity with
flesh, the whole Christ is truly called the Son. This is especially clear from
Paul's words in the first chapter of Romans (vv. 3–4), and also the ninth
(v. 5). Whatever quibbles Menno may play with, the meaning is plain: the Son
of God is from the seed of David according to the flesh. Likewise, Christ,
who is God blessed forever, derives his origin from the Jews according to the
flesh. Therefore he grasps in vain at passages where Christ the man is called
the Son of God, for this name is rightly conferred upon the whole person of
Christ.

The statements that Christ came from heaven and proceeded from the Father are
wrongly applied to the substance of his flesh. Although one may infer from
them that Christ is true God, he speaks here not so much about his substance
as about his office, for he wishes to testify that he teaches nothing that is
not divine.

### [CO X/1, p. 168]

Next, in wishing to dissolve the arguments by which orthodox doctrine is
proved, he escapes by nothing but frivolous quibbles. If the Spirit could be
changed into flesh because all things are possible for God, then the Father
also could have been turned into flesh. If we grant that, what will become of
the saying, “I fill heaven and earth”? The whole divinity will be one body,
which must necessarily be contained in a place. Again, if divinity had been
corporeal in Christ, he would have said falsely that God is spirit. He would
likewise have said falsely after his resurrection that a spirit does not have
flesh and bones.

If Menno replies that spirit was changed into flesh so as to cease being what
it was, nothing more absurd can be imagined. For the eternity of spiritual
essence will perish. Add that if Christ as man is entirely from God's seed,
God's essence will no longer be one or simple but will be cut into parts. Thus
there will be a division in God which Menno does not admit between Christ's
two natures.

His objection that John's statement, “the Word was made flesh,” would not
otherwise be true, because “to be made” does not mean “to assume,” is
nonsensical. For the apostle in Hebrews chapter 2 (v. 14) certainly does not
disagree with John, yet he uses the verb “to assume.” The rationale of this
expression is sufficiently clear: a person who was previously a private man
is also said to be made king, because he begins to be what he was not before.
Thus when the Son of God put on flesh, he is truly and appropriately said to
have been made flesh.

It is a stale escape to say that he is called the Son of Man because he was
promised to human beings. By offering a second invention, Menno sufficiently
shows that he is looking for evasions. It is plain, however, that in the
Hebrew manner a true human being is called a son of man. Christ undoubtedly
retained the idiom of his own language. What should be understood by “sons of
Adam” ought to be beyond controversy. And so that we need go no farther, the
passage of the eighth Psalm (v. 4), which the apostles apply to Christ,
settles the whole controversy: “What is man, that you remember him, or the son
of man, that you visit him?” His trifling about the masculine gender is
entirely without plausibility, for a person whose origin truly flowed from
Adam is called a son of man.

When we say that the sins of the world had to be expiated in our flesh, Menno
tries in vain to overturn this principle, which Paul clearly asserts in Romans
chapter 5 (vv. 10, 19). Against it he objects that no one born from sinful
flesh could fulfill the law. The answer must be sought in Romans chapter 8
(v. 3), where Paul teaches that expiation was made in the likeness of sinful
flesh. It is well known that human nature was not corrupt from the beginning,
but by an accidental fault. What, then, prevents God from having been able to
sanctify the flesh of his Son?

This argument also remains firm: unless there was a begetting of Christ as
man, there would have been a new creation. His objection that there have not
been new

### [CO X/1, p. 169]

creations whenever something has been changed by a miracle into a new nature
is irrelevantly twisted in this direction. God's essence could not be changed
as created things can. And it is a detestable invention to imagine God's
essence to be spirit in the Father and body in the Son.

He brazenly skips the argument drawn from participation in gifts. Whatever
the Father bestowed upon Christ certainly pertains to us, because he is the
head from whom the whole body, joined through its connecting bonds, grows
together. Indeed, the statement that the Spirit was given to him without
measure, so that we might all draw from his fullness (John 1:16; 3:34), will
fit no other way—unless perhaps Menno does not consider it absurd for God in
his own essence to be enriched by an adventitious gift. For this reason
Christ himself also says, “I sanctify myself for their sake” (John 17:19).
Therefore Menno lies wickedly when he says that these things are read nowhere
in Scripture.

As for Moses' testimony, if it is properly weighed, it presses Menno harder
than he can escape. The discussion there is not merely about Christ alone but
about the entire human race. It is indeed true that victory was won by Christ,
but God declares generally that the woman's descendants will be superior to
the Devil (Gen. 3:15). It follows that Christ was begotten from the human
race. Menno's cabalistic allegories, according to which the woman is called
the church, overturn the entire sense, for God's purpose is to raise Eve, whom
he is addressing, to good hope. Here too the variety of his answers reveals a
twisting and slippery snake. Meanwhile, by saying that the serpent's
conqueror is promised from a woman who is *asporos*, “seedless,” he by no
means extricates himself. For if there were no seed in the woman, the promise
would be false.

He most wickedly corrupts the testimonies in which Christ is called Abraham's
seed and the fruit of David's womb. If the word *seed* had been used
allegorically, Paul certainly would not have left this unstated in Galatians
chapter 4 (v. 4). Rather, he clearly and without a figure affirms that there
are not many sons of Abraham as redeemers, but one Christ. I admit that one
cannot infer from the genealogy that Christ is the son of David except because
he was born from the virgin. But in order to establish his impious error,
Menno most arrogantly overturns the first principles of nature by contending
that women are *asporoi*, “seedless.”

He evades the eighth argument, concerning Christ's genealogy, by sophistry.
Although Matthew does not recount Mary's parents but Joseph's, since he speaks
about a matter then commonly known, it is enough for him to show that Joseph
arose from David's seed, when it was sufficiently established that Mary was
of the same family. Luke presses the point still further by teaching that the
salvation brought by Christ is common to the whole human race, because Christ,
the author of salvation, was descended from Adam, the common father of all.

It is properly inferred from Matthew's words that, because Christ was born
from

### [CO X/1, p. 170]

Mary, he was begotten from her seed. Likewise, when Boaz is said to have been
born from Rahab, a similar begetting is denoted. Matthew does not describe the
virgin here as though she were a channel through which Christ flowed. Rather,
he distinguishes this wonderful mode of begetting from the ordinary mode:
through her Christ was born from the seed of David. In the same manner in
which Isaac is said to have been born from Abraham, Solomon from David, and
Joseph from Jacob, Christ likewise is said to have been born from his mother.
For the evangelist is constructing a continuous sequence of discourse.

In the tenth argument Menno reveals his shamelessness in a particularly ugly
way. How will these two claims fit together: that Christ according to the
flesh is from David's seed, and yet that he is called the son only because he
was promised and given? After naming the Son of God, Paul immediately adds
(Rom. 1:3) that he is from the seed of David according to the flesh, thereby
certainly denoting something distinct from the divine essence. So too in the
ninth chapter (v. 5), while proclaiming him God blessed forever, Paul states
separately that according to the flesh he descends from the Jews.

Now if he were not truly born from David's seed, what force would the
expression have that he should be the fruit of his womb and have descended
from his loins? Menno foolishly objects that he cannot be called David's
natural son because the Word was made flesh. But it is established that this
is said in no other sense than that he put on flesh and was manifested in the
flesh.

Menno says that a woman has no generative seed, but who, contrary to the
common judgment of everyone, will believe the invention of an ignorant man?
Nevertheless, it is remarkable that in this way he denies the true miracle
itself while inventing an utterly false prodigy of his own. God's power would
be more wonderful in that prodigy—if what he urges were true—namely, that the
eternal essence of God was changed into a mortal body.

His repeated claim that it cannot be taught with certainty that Mary was born
from David's seed is likewise nonsensical. Matthew wishes to prove that Christ
was born from David's seed, and in being content with this one point—that he
was born from Mary—he surely takes it as an admitted fact that Mary was
Joseph's blood relative. His suggestion that *seed* is taken to mean
posterity is too feeble, for Christ is not simply called “seed” but is said to
be “from seed.” It is also intolerably shameless to overturn the clear meaning
of Paul's words in order to deny that Christ was made or formed from David's
seed.

As for the eleventh argument, I admit that the church is spiritually flesh
from Christ's flesh, and so forth. But in turn I say that, for this to happen,
Christ took flesh from human beings, in which he might sanctify himself on
their behalf.

The claim that Christ is called heavenly and from heaven because he brought
his flesh from there lacks all reason, and the contrast clearly proves it.
For “earthly man” is equivalent to “natural man,” although it is sufficiently
clear that the soul did not proceed from the earth.

### [CO X/1, p. 171]

Thus nothing is more certain than that Christ is called heavenly with respect
to his life-giving spirit.

In the second chapter of Philippians (v. 7), I do not deny that “the form of a
man” is a low and abject condition. But from the whole context one can easily
infer that Christ was emptied in a true human nature. What else does it mean
that he was found in appearance as a man, except that for a time the divine
glory did not shine forth, but only a human appearance was visible? His
exaltation also bears on this point, for Christ is expressly said to have
obtained a new glory because he emptied himself.

However Menno may twist the passage in Hebrews chapter 2 (v. 11), he cannot
extricate himself. He says that the author of holiness and those who are
sanctified are from one, namely from God. But the context proves that a
sharing of nature should instead be understood. For the apostle immediately
adds, “Therefore he is not ashamed to call them brothers.” But if he had
previously said that believers were from God, amid such dignity what reason
would there be for shame? Christ is said not to be ashamed because in his
immeasurable grace he associates himself with the filthy and ignoble.

Menno objects in vain that otherwise the ungodly would be Christ's brothers,
for we know that the children of God are born not from flesh and blood but
from the Spirit through faith. Mere fellowship in flesh therefore does not
create brotherly communion. He foolishly trifles about the “children,” when
the prophet (Isa. 8:17), whose testimony the apostle uses, simply means
servants or disciples. He also babbles no less rashly that Mary would be the
mother of her own brother if Christ according to the flesh is our brother,
since it is more than sufficiently commonplace in Hebrew usage for any blood
relatives to be called brothers.

Although the apostle assigns to believers alone the honor of being from one
with Christ, it does not follow that unbelievers are not born from the same
source. Similarly, when we say that Christ was made man in order to make us
God's children, this expression is not extended to everyone, because faith
intervenes as the means which spiritually engrafts us into Christ's body.
Menno objects most impiously and wickedly that Christ ought to have been born
from Adam immediately at the beginning, since he is the firstborn among the
brothers. But firstborn status refers not to age, but to rank of honor and
preeminence in virtue.

He evades the fifteenth argument most ignorantly. The apostle's words are
clear: since the children share flesh and blood, Christ also had to become a
sharer in them (Heb. 2:14). What was said about human nature Menno twists,
without reason and without shame, into a statement about sin. It is still
more wicked that he restricts the plural relative only to flesh—and to
fictitious flesh—as though the apostle said nothing when he established
Christ as a partner and sharer in the same nature with us. Nor

### [CO X/1, p. 172]

is there any weight in his quibble that Christ would be a sinner born from
sinners if he were a true man. We do not concede that nature's corruption
was in Christ, but teach that the man was created without fault.

The passage in Hebrews chapter 2 (v. 16), where the apostle says that Christ
assumed Abraham's seed, repudiates the impious invention of Menno and
Servetus that Christ received human beings into favor. For there, in order to
magnify the grace with which Christ deigned to honor the human race, the
apostle compares angels with human beings: Christ preferred human beings to
angels by putting on human nature. If by *seed* he means descendants, Menno
will be compelled to admit the same about angels, namely that offspring is
begotten by them—as though Christ had lied in saying that after the
resurrection we will be like the angels. His subtlety about the verb's present
tense is worthless. The apostle spoke in the customary demonstrative manner,
so as to set so remarkable a grace before our eyes more clearly.

Menno not only babbles ignorantly about each nature in Christ, but mixes
everything together confusedly and contradicts himself. Peter teaches that
we will become sharers in the divine nature (2 Pet. 1:4), but this can refer
only to quality, insofar as we will be renewed into heavenly glory. Otherwise
this corruptible body of ours would then become God. How can Menno fail to
shudder at and abominate the change of sinful flesh into God's essence, when
he so greatly recoils from something which is nevertheless free of absurdity:
that the Son of God was clothed with our flesh? Add that, according to Peter's
meaning, sharing in the divine nature will abolish the present condition of
human nature insofar as it is weak. The case is very different in Christ, who
was God and man at once and simultaneously.

Now if Menno attributes to Christ a complete human substance pure from every
stain, such as it was in Adam before the fall, it follows that Christ's flesh
was begotten not from God's seed but from a human being—unless perhaps he
would rather say that it was created either from nothing or from the earth.
If he disapproves of Athanasius' statement that Christ consists of two natures
as a human person is constituted of soul and body, he must show why so apt a
comparison displeases him.

I am not surprised if he entangles by evasion Peter's passage (1 Pet. 3:18),
where he says that Christ was put to death in the flesh and made alive in the
spirit. How will he escape, when Peter ascribes to the spirit a power which
Christ's flesh lacked? Paul's statement presses him still harder when he
affirms that Christ suffered on account of the weakness of the flesh (2 Cor.
13:4). For if Christ's flesh was divine, divinity was weak. This also bears
upon the fact that Christ endured our passions and affections, being made like
us in every respect except sin. For if pain and fear, and finally all our
weaknesses, subjected God's

### [CO X/1, p. 173]

nature, then neither will the sun be bright, but the whole order of heaven and
earth will be a formless and dreadful chaos.

Whatever he babbles in order to prove that women have no generative seed is
unworthy of refutation. I leave this rather for physicians to treat. Yet the
arguments he offers have such futility that they can be refuted without any
difficulty.

Onan, the son of Judah, spilled seed upon the ground. The same is read nowhere
about a woman—as though this scoundrel delighted in exposing women's private
parts.

A man from whom an emission of seed goes out is called unclean. Menno most
foolishly infers that women do not experience an unclean discharge. On that
reasoning there would be no native uncleanness in women, as a remedy for which
circumcision was given to men alone.

In Wisdom chapter 7 (v. 2), a human being is said to be coagulated in blood
from the seed of a man. From this Menno contends that what is proper to a woman,
namely blood, is ascribed to her—as though sperm were anything other than
blood congealed for generation. It follows that a fetus is formed from the
substance of the woman as well as that of the man.

This scoundrel wickedly corrupts Paul's passage where he teaches that man is
not from woman, but woman from man (1 Cor. 11:8). There can be no doubt that
the origin of the human race is treated there, not its continual propagation,
which the apostle mentions immediately in the second part when he says that
man is through woman.

Menno objects that Aaron and Jehoiada married wives from the tribe of Judah,
and from this infers that the distinction of tribes would then have been
confused if a woman possessed generative seed. This is exceedingly feeble.
It is well known that, as far as civil order is concerned, offspring is
reckoned from the male seed. Yet the dignity of the superior sex does not
prevent the woman's seed from combining in generation. This answer also
extends to all genealogies. Scripture often names only the men when it
recounts a catalogue of human beings. Must it therefore be said that women
are nothing? On the contrary, even children know that women are included
under men. For this same reason women are said to bear children for their
husbands, because the family name always remains with the males.

He prophesies childishly about the mystery of the church. If the church is
called our mother, it does not immediately follow that all the individual
parts correspond to each other in physical and spiritual generation.

Creation by no means proves Menno's claim that women lack seed. Just as the
first woman was created from a man, descendants were thereafter born from
both.

He reasons foolishly from the word *seed*. If the earth receives seed cast by
a human hand, it does not follow that earth and woman play the same role in
the intercourse of man and woman. Indeed, one might argue instead that,
because mothers were once called *genitrices*, “begetters,” their seed
contributes to generation. Otherwise a marriage between a maternal uncle and
his niece

### [CO X/1, p. 174]

would have been wrongly forbidden by God's law, because there would be no
blood relationship. A man would also be permitted to take his maternal
half-sister in marriage, provided that she had been begotten by another
father.

His reasoning is no less inept when he says that children are accounted noble
or ignoble according to the condition of their fathers. Just as this is
granted to the male sex by reason of its preeminence, so too in slavery the
offspring follows the womb, according to the jurists. From this one could
readily infer that a fetus is begotten from maternal seed.

Menno's inference that women, if they contribute seed, will be the fathers of
their own fetus is no less barbarous. A mother is called a parent no less than
a father. Since *parent* is therefore a name common to both, the terms
*father* and *mother* denote only the distinction of sex.

His last argument vanishes by itself. Just as I admit that passive power is
ascribed to women, so I say that the same things are predicated indiscriminately
of women as of men. For Christ himself is not said to have been made *through*
a woman, but *from* a woman (Gal. 4:4). This shows how this dog barks without
any sense of shame. He mockingly asks whether we mean to say that Christ was
begotten from the virgin's menstrual seed. I should like to know from him in
turn whether Christ was not formed in his mother's blood, since Menno himself
does not elsewhere deny that she fulfilled the role of a mother.

The answers he assembles do not overturn the testimonies of Scripture in
which seed is attributed to women. The condition of a woman is not the same
as or equal to the condition of earth. And when someone is said to be from a
woman's seed, *posterity* cannot be understood under that word. In the third
chapter of Genesis the whole human race is called the woman's seed in no
other sense than that the woman truly cooperates in generation.

Nor does the passage in Hebrews chapter 11 (v. 11) allow itself to be so
easily explained away: Sarah received power for the emission of seed, because
that word plainly denotes seed being emitted by women, and there is a clear
allusion between *parabolēn* and the verb *lambanesthai*.[^greek] As for
Christ, a bare and naked promise will not bring it about that he is said to
have proceeded from the loins of Abraham and David.

The absurdities with which Menno tries to burden us are full of wicked
calumnies. Unless he grants that the Word of God was also his Son from the
beginning, God was not at that time the Father of angels; yet before he was
born he lacked a mother. We grant that Christ the man, who was born from Mary,
is the Son of God no less than the son of a human being, although in different
respects. We do not imagine two Christs after the fashion of Nestorius, but
establish one person from two natures, so that he is truly God manifested in
the flesh (1 Tim. 3:16). If Menno rejects our doctrine in this respect, let
him mock Paul, from whom we borrow the words.

### [CO X/1, p. 175]

Menno contends that the crucified Christ is the only-begotten Son of God. We
teach the same. Meanwhile, however, the distinction which Christ expressly
asserted with his own mouth is not abolished, for he called his body a temple
(John 2:19). How could God himself, or his essence, be a temple? We therefore
infer that the body is the dwelling place of deity. Paul's statement that the
fullness of deity dwells in him bodily (Col. 2:9) also bears upon this.

His slander that Christ is divided into God and man if we say that he is God
and man is dismissed without difficulty. A distinction is not a tearing
apart. It is equally shameless for him to say that Christ is compounded in
this way from a pure and impure nature, a blessed and accursed nature. When
Paul declares that sin was condemned in the flesh (Rom. 8:3), he judiciously
directs us back to the likeness of sinful flesh.

Menno reasons childishly that, if Christ was born from his mother's seed by
the Spirit's secret operation, it was consequently not the woman's seed that
was impure, but only the man's. We do not make Christ free from every stain
because he was born only from his mother, but because he was sanctified by
the Spirit, so that the generation was pure and uncorrupted, such as it would
have been before the fall.

He next adds that all Adam's offspring without exception is enclosed under
sin, and therefore that Christ, if he derived his origin from human beings,
could not be exempted from the common law. The contrast Paul uses in Romans
chapter 5 (v. 12) easily answers him: “Just as through one man came sin, and
through sin death, so in the grace of the one man Christ did grace abound.”
Likewise 1 Corinthians 15:47. Adam's accidental corruption did not prevent
Christ from being born pure and whole from him.

This point also remains firm and is not evaded by Menno's quibble: whenever
Scripture reminds us of Christ's purity, a true human nature is denoted, for
it would be superfluous to say that God is pure. The sanctification of which
John speaks in 17:19 would likewise have no place in the divine nature. Nor
are two seeds of Adam imagined, although no contagion reached Christ, because
human generation is not unclean or defective in itself, but accidentally so
because of the fall. It is therefore no wonder that Christ, through whom
integrity was to be restored, was exempted from the common corruption.

Menno babbles in vain that, if Christ was begotten from Mary's seed, she would
be both father and mother. The generation in which God exhibited a memorable
proof of his secret and incomprehensible power ought not to be measured by
the ordinary rule.

Moreover, that axiom which he so boldly thrusts upon us again and again—that
Mary was *asporos*, “seedless”—needs proof, because it conflicts with both
philosophy and experience.

### [CO X/1, p. 176]

He contends that it is idolatry to adore God manifested in the flesh. We deny
it. What arrogance it is to bind us to his own decrees!

His objection that, if Christ was begotten from Mary, he would be more a
Gentile than a Jew by descent, because he traces his lineage through women
from the Gentiles, is of the same futility—as though Ruth as a great-grandmother
prevented David from being accounted Abraham's son.

He also presses this upon us as an absurdity: if the Word of God put on flesh,
then he was shut within the narrow prison of an earthly body. This is sheer
insolence. Although the immeasurable essence of the Word coalesced with human
nature in one person, we imagine no confinement. The Son of God wonderfully
descended from heaven without leaving heaven. He wonderfully willed to be
carried in the virgin's womb, to live on earth, and to hang upon the cross,
while always filling the world as he had from the beginning. It is also
profane ignorance to imagine that in death itself the Word was separated from
the flesh.

But I should like to learn from Menno in turn how well it fits for the essence
of God, which heaven and earth do not contain, to have been made a little body
that lay hidden in the virgin's womb and in the tomb. Let him untie this knot,
if he can: how at the same moment God was spirit everywhere and was a tangible
man on earth.

This much I see for certain: nothing more arrogant than this ass, and nothing
more insolent than this dog, can be imagined.
