# Response to Baudouin's Invectives

1562

This is a fresh English translation of John Calvin's complete Latin prose in
*Ioannis Calvini opera quae supersunt omnia*, vol. IX (Corpus Reformatorum
XXXVII), pp. 565–580. The CO editorial title opening occupies pp. 561–562 and
the next opening is blank. The translation was made from the normalized Latin
and checked against the page images. No modern English translation supplied
its wording.

The title means *John Calvin's Response to Baudouin's Invectives*. CO running
heads, nineteenth-century notes, and bracketed page locators for the 1562
printing are not translated as Calvin's prose.

## Translation

### [CO IX, p. 565]

I do not know whether François Baudouin published four years ago the chilly
patchwork pieces which have now appeared in the light again in connection with
the laws *On Defamatory Libels*. He obtained a privilege on 25 July 1557. Now
that he has reworked the production in order to vomit out his venom against me
at full throat, I should like the jurist to answer whether he lawfully adapted
to a new invective the privilege then granted to the book he had composed.
Certainly it was absurd to adorn a work written four months ago with the favor
of a king who died two years ago. Even if I omit the fine points of law, as
they call them, common sense declares that a forger is caught here.

Although his audacity and wickedness have long been known to everyone, I never
thought he would break out into such madness as to curse me so insolently. But
just as despair drives men of this sort into a rage, his bad conscience drove
him headlong, so that in blind assault he rushed upon himself rather than upon
me. I will not deny that his intemperance disturbed me, since such an outrage
cannot fail to turn the stomachs of sensible and honorable men; but I easily
despised his insults as though they did not touch me. Socrates' saying is
famous: “If a donkey had kicked me, would I summon it to appear before the
praetor?” Although I am far removed from the greatness of spirit in which
Socrates excelled, long practice has by now hardened me to the barking of
those dogs; and from a better school I have learned that God subjects his
servants to reproaches and insults in order to test their endurance. I will
not boast about the greatness of revelations as though I came near to Paul.
Yet I acknowledge that I share this much with Paul: an angel of Satan has been
sent to me by God, lest I be exalted, to train me in humility by striking me
with his fists. Nevertheless, since we must ask God every day to restrain the
devil and his ministers, it is also our duty to resist their unbridled license
so that the Truth is not overwhelmed by their lies.

Five months ago a writing was published at Basel without its author's name.
It was given an attractive title, as though it offered the best remedy for
calming the church's divisions.

### [CO IX, p. 566]

A brief refutation was set against it, in which Baudouin was censured. On that
occasion he now attacks me and summons—or rather drags—me to a capital trial,
as though he had been unjustly denounced by me. But what if I deny that I wrote
the pamphlet to which he is so fiercely hostile? Certainly, if I wished to
quibble, denial would be easy. Yet because it was taken down in large part
from my conversation, I acknowledge it as mine.

Here Baudouin sails against me with every sail spread, complaining that he is
burdened by a false accusation; and although he does not name the author, he
says that someone else's work is undeservedly attributed to him. I gladly help
him and want it attested to the readers that those dirges were stitched
together by more than one man. There is a certain Cassander, withdrawn, as
they say, from human company. His name would never have become known had he
not now, at Baudouin's prompting, been brought by me upon the stage. From
solitude he contracted *authadeia*—self-willed disagreeableness, as often
happens—so completely that peevishness deadened nearly all his senses.

Baudouin had sniffed out that Cassander had spent twenty years of leisure
hammering out an intermediate and winding religion. He could therefore easily
draw from Cassander's bosom those ill-fated inventions which, by mingling light
with darkness under the pretext of a pestilent harmony, would cherish every
corruption in the church. It is scarcely credible how keenly he tracks down
whatever is harmful to piety. Whatever that hodgepodge was, Baudouin polished
it with his own craft.

If anyone asks why Cassander was not mentioned at all in the *Refutation*, I
answer that there was no reason why that witch, or mask, ought to be drawn out
of the den in which it had so far lain well hidden. But Baudouin had impressed
upon the pamphlet everywhere unmistakable marks of his style, so that it was
plain to everyone that it had come only from his hand (nor would the printer
deny it), and all who possessed moderate judgment thought and said the same.
No religion or fairness, I think, forbade censuring indirectly the man who, by
publishing the pamphlet, had poured poison from a closed box. Here he stands
convicted of a gross lie when he pretends to have known nothing about the
entire affair,

### [CO IX, p. 567]

since long before that time he had hunted down this prey at Cassander's house.
Add that the man who has lain at Cologne all these years in his own dung-heap,
right to the present day, laments the turbulent condition of the kingdom as
though he lived in the heart of France. What is the purpose of this stinking
tale? And who is so dull that he does not recognize Baudouin's brush? No ink
will ever erase this, whatever it is.

This, then, is the defense of an upright and honorable man, as with an actor's
plaintive tears he tries to move fools: “While I was in the heart of France,”
he says, “someone at the farthest bounds of Germany, who neither knew nor
thought anything about France, composed and published a pamphlet.” Why, then,
does that person, whoever the pamphlet's author may be, lament France's
troubles in his complaint? And why place Cologne Agrippina in some remote
corner of Germany? I say nothing of the “heart of France,” since after
releasing his sting he betook himself to Reims to advertise his services to
the Cardinal.

Baudouin begins by saying that two questions must be examined: one of fact,
whether he composed the book himself, and one of law, whether the book deserves
so bitter an attack. Let the readers judge how successfully he dispatches the
first part. His argument is, of course, that blame for a pamphlet of which he
is not the author is undeservedly transferred to him. He clears himself by
denial. If he likes, let him add perjury too, in order to make me guilty of
rashness for having dared to pronounce freely on an uncertain matter.

But I will explain frankly and plainly the entire affair about which he
stammers in alarm; nor do I want to load him with ill will beyond what the
facts allow. A writing was published in order to stain me with disgrace before
everyone. Its author's and printer's names were deliberately suppressed, so
that it might be free to tear me apart with impunity. Baudouin pleads that the
work is not his. Let me grant him this: since he was the broker, or the pimp
and producer—since, in short, he handed the pamphlet over with his own hand and
arranged for its publication—he is held guilty of the same offense. If I
summoned him to court, the case would be finished in three words.

I do not rehearse the savagery of the injuries with which that writer tried to
wound me, because he reproaches me with nothing private. But since he could
win no credibility for himself except by taking it away from me, he maliciously
slanders all the labors I have so far sustained in defense of heavenly

### [CO IX, p. 568]

doctrine and all the zeal and diligence I have spent in vindicating the
worship of God. He makes me the leader of a violent sect that would rather
ruin the entire church than yield the least point of its stubbornness. He
compares the divisions by which the church is now miserably torn to civil war.
He pretends that we burn with such hatred for the opposing party that, with no
care for healing, we are borne along solely to destroy it.

This curse is easy to refute among those who care that simple truth should not
be undeservedly burdened, because they do not limp toward either side. But,
leaving that worthless insolence aside, what was of the first necessity was
done: the mediator's treachery was exposed, a treachery which, under the
deceitful pretext of purchasing peace, was betraying religion together with
the eternal salvation of souls.

Here Baudouin is astonished that I raise an alarm as though Hannibal were at
the gates. He once read that phrase in Cicero. It pleased him. He fills all
his pamphlets with it as though it were a proverbial witticism. I, however,
did not think it wasted effort to warn the faithful—not to meet Hannibal
outside the gates, but, since Sinon was already inside, to take timely
precautions against his snares. A book was being printed at Basel; at Reims
the traitor was bargaining with the Cardinal. Was I to be silent?

Why, then, was there any need to summon judges to inquire more carefully why I
am trembling here, when at other times I so calmly despise the darts directed
against me? The answer is that such rubbish poses no danger to me, but, because
I fear for the simplicity of many, I think they must be warned in time to
beware of traitors. As for his denial that the man I call a mediator should be
treated as a mortal enemy: first let him consult the rudiments of grammar,
where he will discover what it means to speak by concession. Then let him run
through the book he attacks with a sound and composed mind. There it is openly
shown at what deadly price the man who insinuates himself under a deceptive
character wishes peace to be bought.

It would certainly have been better to omit a bad cause's defense than to
evade an evident charge with witless taunts. He will never wash the hatred and
abhorrence of it from godly hearts. The question is what the book is like. If
Baudouin did not beget it, he nevertheless discharged a midwife's office at
its birth. The *Response* at which Baudouin is angry points out as though with
a finger that it is filled with nothing but impostures and composed for the
church's ruin. To cover a disgrace exposed to every eye, the good man throws
the whole case into confusion with his abuse.

To persuade readers that I pour out a dreadful bile, as he puts it, in blind
fury, he fabricates conjectures about why, forgetful of our old friendship, I
have flared up against him as an enemy. First he alleges that, at the request
of godly nobles, he put in writing an opinion contrary to my novel and deadly
decrees, because I hold that heretics ought to be severely punished. I do not
ask who these nobles are whose names he trumpets. Let them indeed be Castalio
and Boinus. I wished to touch that claim in a single word, so that this trifler
might peddle his trappings elsewhere. But how can he charge with novelty what
I took solely from God's law? Indeed, he does not shrink from staining God
himself, whose laws I quote, with the disgrace of cruelty. In fact, it would
never have entered my mind to touch this argument except at the urging of our
venerable brother Dr. Heinrich Bullinger. I do not substitute him as the
defendant, however, because in accommodating him I merely testified that I
approved his advice.
### [CO IX, p. 569]

I had indeed heard that Baudouin was babbling something against me, but I
laughed, as I was entitled to do. So far was I from being disturbed that I
felt sorry for his tastelessness. He therefore guesses badly when he says this
was the cause of my anger.

He also assigns another cause inappropriately: his view that account should be
taken of antiquity in the church's ceremonies. But when, at last, did he begin
to be an antiquarian? He lived with me on familiar terms. He professed his
faith in this church and, at Strasbourg, among people who speak our language.
Did he ever indicate with so much as one little word that he wanted more
ceremonies than those we use? He adds that he hates the youthful lack of
restraint in establishing order which seems to mingle heaven with earth. You
would say that some Nestor was delivering a speech. But what is the purpose of
such stinking ostentation, as though he had ever produced any specimen of
prudence or moderation? Yet, to strut with some appearance of gravity, he puts
on the tragic buskin and proclaims that he is all the more religious. Let him
assert it, certainly, provided no one believes him. His desire that I
acknowledge it is as ridiculous as the rest.

Finally he insists that, if this troubles me, I should speak frankly. As if
Baudouin were the man whose judgment scorches and tortures me! I care nothing
for the worthless chatter of a man about church affairs who is as devoid of
piety as he is ignorant of everything sacred, provided that he does not try by
secret arts to overthrow the pure doctrine of religion which Christ's
faithful servants have long labored to erect and establish. Nothing is
therefore more foolish than to charge me with deceit, as though I fabricated a
different state of the case.

Although I had learned from many trustworthy witnesses that throughout these
two years Baudouin had acted treacherously, had contrived many wicked and
villainous things with a few men like himself, and had been an enemy within
the church, I nevertheless restrained myself. I grieved over his defection and
mourned the wretch's destruction just as though he had never conspired with
wicked scoundrels against my own life. His old friends could scarcely endure
my patience. But when he brought out this final act, he did not permit me to
be silent.

Yet, rubbing his filthy mouth, he repeatedly asks what intemperance drove me
against him. As if I had not shown more than enough restraint when I spared an
apostate and did not prick him with even one hostile word before his desperate
wickedness was disclosed to me, when he did not hesitate to hire or sell
himself to the Cardinal. His more hidden wickedness could have lain undisturbed
as far as I was concerned. But when it broke out of its own accord and
insolently displayed itself, what is the point of asking about a manifest
fact? I did not seek him out. Let him blame his own madness if, while attacking
the church, he ran into one of its faithful guards who did not permit him to
do as much harm as he wished.

Yet he thinks himself cleared of such an evident charge if in one little word
he objects that I pick up street-corner tales or scatter clouds which time had
long ago dispersed while he kept silent. As if the memory of such a

### [CO IX, p. 570]

criminal conspiracy and abominable conference could be erased from godly
hearts by a sardonic laugh. Let him lift up clouds as much as he pleases.
Sensible men will never forget that, while he pursued profit-making employment
with his captious arts, it was not thanks to him that a severe wound was not
inflicted upon the French church. Let him therefore stop putting forward the
name of advocate, from whose clientele he will fall away as easily, once he
has become thoroughly known, as he slipped into it deceitfully.

From this it is also apparent how empty his ambition is when he so insolently
boasts of some unnamed thing, as though it were doubtful that the worst slaves
sometimes obtain favor in princes' courts. He even appeals to my conscience
before God: where I know there is no dispute, he says, I spitefully wish to
persuade others that the dispute is very great, while what really exists I
cunningly conceal. As if it were necessary to appeal to God's secret judgment
about a matter known to everyone. Yet it is worth noting how well cunning and
the blind fury into which he pretends I was suddenly driven by another's
impulse agree with one another. Who does not see a man devoted to insolence,
vomiting up what weighs on his stomach rather than speaking?

Thus, of course, the knot of a great and difficult question is untied: how I
dared provoke Baudouin when I have so many enemies. My defense is ready: all
who are my enemies are avowed enemies of Christ; I have never undertaken any
enmities for a private cause or eagerly sought them; and, finally, none arose
through my fault. It is more than enough for me that no one is my enemy for
any other reason than that he dared to rise with sacrilegious audacity against
the doctrine of piety and the church's welfare. In our time, whenever men have
tried to attack Christ and his church as enemies, I have stoutly opposed their
fury. As I received them sharply, so I pursued them with tireless constancy. I
will never tire of this continual warfare. For this reason I am an enemy to
Baudouin, whom I once loved.

Now I should like him to answer me: how did he acquire so many enemies for
himself? Why did he embroil himself and them in savage contests? Why did he
leave no true friend anywhere? I do not know who approves him. It is certain
that every learned and upright man with whom he has associated detests him. If
we seek the source, none will be found but his ambition, pride, envy,
treachery, and any similar virtue there may be. Throughout the pamphlet his
shameful stupidity betrays itself. With that word I wound him more severely
than if I heaped upon him every class of crime. He long ago said farewell to
what is right and honorable, provided he be thought clever and crafty. Yet he
must be taught seriously how foul he makes himself by his tasteless jokes.

He says that he does not accept the judgment I delivered, and that he leaves
me to myself,

### [CO IX, p. 571]

since I tolerate no colleague. First, let the readers see how haughtily the
Baudouin-Cassander of the pamphlet lifted himself above every judge. Then let
them investigate how mild this man of ours was, how courteous and humane
toward his own colleagues. They will find that he was intolerable everywhere.
But let him enjoy Ishmael's glory: his hand is against everyone, just as
everyone's hand is against him.

My moderation in bearing not only colleagues but also superiors—and not only
in bearing them but also in wishing for them—is too well known for there to be
any need to refute a worthless quibble. No one in my generation has excelled
in piety and learning whom I have not honored with due respect and whose
virtues I have not praised generously. Nor was there any one of them who did
not embrace me with the greatest love and pay me more honor than I wished.

I appeal to Philipp Melanchthon himself as witness of how he was accustomed to
speak about me; his single voice can drown all Baudouin's barking. If Baudouin
denies that this is known to him, a great many people will convict him.
Melanchthon's honorable praises of me were so far from puffing up my spirit
that, when he preferred me to himself—and did so in writing—I said that the
lie was intolerable. He took no offense at all, but answered that he meant
what he had written and that it was true. I am not so foolish as to believe it
true or to want others to believe it. I recount it so that all good people may
smell the insolence of this scoundrel.

Baudouin even reports Bucer's judgment of me, which, I call God and his angels
to witness, he fabricated most wickedly. “Bucer,” he says, “once told you that
you kept no measure in hatred or love, but possessed such vehemence that you
would either lift a man above the heavens or cast him down as far as hell.”
As surely as I pray God to be gracious to me, I never heard anything of the
sort. Rather,
that man, whom I revered as a father, cultivated a brotherly friendship with
me in return with such kindness that he could scarcely bear my removal from
Strasbourg. Indeed, he contended to the very end that I must be retained by
whatever means possible. His letters to our Senate also survive. In them he
complains that I was being summoned back here with the greatest loss to the
whole church; and he finally goes so far as to say that among ministers of
sound doctrine I was second to none and had few equals. If his immoderate love
for me blinded him and he exceeded the limit in praising me, at least let
Baudouin be ashamed of his unbridled insolence—or let the readers recognize
the madness with which he is carried into abuse.

I tolerate no colleague, apparently. Yet there is no faithful servant of
Christ anywhere in the world to whom I have not extended the hand of brotherly
fellowship. Let my colleagues, with whom I share the administration of this
church, answer concerning our harmony at home. Whenever men of great promise
arise, I help them in every way I can so that they may flourish in favor and
authority. I may testify with Moses, “Would that they all

### [CO IX, p. 572]

prophesied in God's camp!” In short, if he can, let this detractor name one man
whom I held back through envy or rivalry from standing even above his colleague
in rank, if that were possible. Thus the childish quibble about the supreme
pontificate vanishes of itself, as though I had not always striven both
sincerely and openly to flee whatever might drag me beyond the bounds of
modesty.

But what does he mean when he tells me to feel secure if I have no rival or
competitor for the supreme priesthood except him? If he wished to trifle so
tastelessly, he should at least have abstained from an introduction so ill
suited to it. “Away with nonsense,” he says. You expect something grave and
serious. He immediately surpasses every piece of nonsense: he has declared,
apparently, that I need not fear his throwing me down from my rank. Who would
have suspected that a man of no account claimed so much for himself unless he
had betrayed himself? Let him go now and puff out his cheeks with empty
boasting. “If I were ambitious,” he says, “only now would I think myself to be
something.” He has, of course, raised his fraudulently procured schoolmastership
above the clouds so that he may look down contemptuously upon everyone else.
Meanwhile his foolish ambition is certainly capable of making everyone laugh.

He severely remonstrates with me because, in provoking him, I forgot not only
piety but humanity too. I do not grasp what he means by his services toward
me. I admit that he was close to me. While he gave signs of a better
disposition, he was a guest in my house. I was, of course, nurturing a serpent
and a pest in my home. But by what law he derives from this pretext the claim
that I am bound to him, I do not know.

He goes farther: I deserve more savage punishments than Orestes, who killed
his mother when provoked by some cause, because, a cruel teacher, I tore apart
an innocent former pupil, condemned without a hearing and nearly a son—not
with horses, as the unhappy father tore apart Hippolytus, but with my pen. I omit the oblique
turnings in which he thought he would be witty: that I signify I had been his
teacher and boast in him as my pupil or son. For my part, if I could ever have
claimed any right over him, I freely surrender it, because I have nothing in
common with Satan, to whom he has wholly pledged himself.

But readers will recognize from his letters that, if he now rages savagely
against me, the guilt of ingratitude rests with him. Come, then, let him prove
himself innocent, as he boldly threatens to do. I, however, offer this plain
defense: because he wickedly deceived my hope not only by dealing treacherously
with me, but by rampaging toward the common ruin of the church, now through
plots and now through open effort, I had all the juster reasons for anger against him
than against any outsider or stranger. Had he complained about me in a human
manner, perhaps, deceived once more by the same hope which had already
disappointed me, I would have wished to relax my vehemence somewhat. But now,
since his bestial rage is a very clear sign of a ruined and desperate mind, I
think I may say

### [CO IX, p. 573]

with the prophet, “Let what is perishing perish.”

There are many trustworthy witnesses who can attest how little rashness there
was in my boiling over against him, for his illustrious actions were known to
them. Yet from a short narrative let sound and upright readers judge how
humanely I endured him.

After he had professed his faith here, he left for France. As his tongue always
displayed one thing while he kept another in the recess of his heart, he said
that he needed only a little time to dispatch certain business. Because his
return concerned him more than it concerned me, he persuaded me he would come
back quickly. Those who knew his character more closely mocked my credulity.
Experience certainly showed that, because our poverty displeased him, he was
hunting for rich and splendid positions elsewhere. As he evaded the matter
and linked delay to delay, his cunning did indeed displease me. Yet I gave no
sign of offense and was not even more gravely offended until he again polluted
himself at Bourges with the filth of idolatry.

Even after such a disgraceful defection, however, my readiness to forgive him
was so great that he was reconciled to me. Thus, when that position at Bourges
weighed him down—his show, the only thing in which he excels, had vanished and
so did not satisfy his hope and desires—he did not hesitate to return here.
Although former friends harried him with free-spoken abuse on every side, he
was humanely admitted after obtaining pardon from me. I did what was necessary:
corrected by severe rebuke, he had to acknowledge the foulness of his fall. He
gave slavish assent and flatteringly permitted himself to be governed by my
counsels. When he went to Strasbourg, he enrolled with the pastor and elders
of the French church.

Because sinister rumors about his departure, which resembled flight, soon
spread among the people at Bourges, he attacked the distinguished jurist
François Duaren, whom he himself had exalted to heaven, in order to wash away
this stain. How fierce the contest was is more than sufficiently clear from
the books published by both sides. When Baudouin saw himself deprived of real
weapons in that battle, he stooped to ignoble treachery. By creating hostility
against Duaren because of his approval of the pure and orthodox faith,
Baudouin exposed him to the executioners' fury. As is known, it was a capital
offense in France not only to subscribe to our doctrine but even to read our
books secretly. This fine follower of piety, in trying to show that Duaren had
learned from me and borrowed from my books whatever he had taught correctly
and sincerely in his book *On Benefices*, had no other aim than to put into
the hands of the church's furious enemies a sword with which to slaughter the
man.

It is no wonder if this barbarous savagery displeased me. Indeed, by this one
stratagem he made himself detestable to all the godly. Yet when Duaren
complained about him both by letter and in person, I calmed a man so equipped
with talent, eloquence, and learning that in the

### [CO IX, p. 574]

contest he would have been by far the superior. A great many people know how
faithfully I labored to calm the spirits of some who, unjustly provoked by
him, would have caused great disturbances had I not intervened. Baudouin will
certainly not deny that I acted as mediator in such a way that I protected his
reputation as far as I could. The man whom I spared so mercifully and bore
with such patience still complains of my inhumanity.

I have briefly described part of the unworthy reward he returned; I pass over
part lest I seem to prosecute private injuries. I was never led to draw my pen
against him until he offered himself voluntarily. Even though he did not cease
from wicked activity, I nevertheless abstained from him. So far was it from my
intention to stab him while he was safe and sleeping, as he lies. All the more
ridiculous, then, is his foolish insolence when he calls it almost parricide
that I dared scourge him with the rebuke he deserved. To prevent me from
claiming zeal as my defense, he anticipates me and objects that rage which
tears an innocent man with its teeth is far removed from zeal. I hear the
curse; the book in question will refute it without my speaking. Meanwhile he
betrays himself as an executioner who speaks so readily of butcheries.

His spiteful comparison of us to the Zealots, who miserably destroyed Judea by
rampaging through murders and robberies, is no less an example of tasteless
mockery than of abandoned audacity. This witty buffoon rushes into abuse just
as gladiators rush to slaughter; branding alone would not suffice, but
fastening to the stake would be needed if the vilest slanderer were punished
according to his deserts.

He thinks it an attractive saying to boast that he has nothing to do with my
factions and that he strongly recoils from the intemperance of those who
increase religious divisions by raising disturbances and hate every plan for
a godly and peaceable settlement. So some scoundrel emerging from the mud will
cover the sun with darkness by his filthy impudence! Jeremiah was apparently
a factionalist when he stripped the mask from perverse impostors. All the
prophets were apparently raising disturbances when they cried out that nothing
should be changed in the genuine worship of God. Christ and the apostles were
called leaders of a new sect by wicked despisers of God. In the same way,
Epicureans like Baudouin once cast blame for every disturbance upon the
Christians.

Since each man has his own faction, what purpose does it serve to heap many of
them upon me? Let him show what all these factions are. Let him prove that I am
an enemy of peace and concord; then I will admit that he has said something.
Yet he is greatly mistaken if he trusts that he will achieve anything except
to make his intolerable pride stink in everyone's nostrils.

His pretense that he mourns the church's dispersion is the height of foolish
boasting. Sometimes, however, he softens while playing the buffoon. At one
moment he echoes Vergil's verses, so that you would say he was uttering oracles
from the tripod; at another he stitches together chilly

### [CO IX, p. 575]

jokes from here and there, by which he displays the ease with which he spins
his tales. Thus, if he despises me, “he will at once strike the stars with his
exalted head.” He loudly proclaims the reason for his trifling, hurling at me
some frivolous witticisms or other. “What else was I to do?” he asks. Of
course he will be judged a great man if in sport and jest he harasses me with
no effort. What, then, is the meaning of all the tragic exclamations in which
he invokes heaven and earth? Trifler—a name you truly assumed for yourself—if
poetic melody delights you, how did Horace's words fail to enter your mind:
“If you wish me to weep, you must grieve”? It is scarcely consistent for a
man who laments that he was attacked by what was almost an abominable parricide
to make a pleasure for himself and jest sweetly in his own ruin.

For this reason he also mocks me because not everyone knows at what point the
elegant shoe pinches me. As though simplicity known to everyone could be
overcome by casually hurling a common proverb at a man far removed from all
ostentation. After satisfying himself with such fine witticisms, he assumes
the parts of severe adviser and teacher—modestly, of course, since he is a bad
comic actor who brings false characters on stage in the wrong order.

I have no need to summon Bucer from heaven to refute the buffoon whose name
Baudouin abuses no less childishly than wickedly. The dead man's letters and
even his publicly issued books survive. From them it is easy to recognize how
foolishly Baudouin put on Bucer's character so that he might indulge his
insolence without restraint. He had already launched a savage reproach against
me when he asked what had become of common liberty if no one may speak except
according to the decision and formula of some one new doctor. I omit that he
calls a man a new doctor whose answers he once wrote were oracles to him. But
those who freely disagree with me and nevertheless do not cease to be friends
are witnesses of how falsely he invents this slavery which I supposedly
impose upon them, making them depend upon my nod.

What follows is more odious: “Unless my counsels are resisted, not only the
cause but life too must be lost; everyone must perish if something escapes
which does not please me.” Perhaps Castalio obtained this from him as a firm
pledge of friendship: that Baudouin should undertake the defense of Servetus'
cause. Servetus certainly paid the just penalty—but was it at my decision?
Surely arrogance destroyed the man no less than impiety. But what crime was
mine if our Senate, at my urging indeed but in accordance with the judgment of
several churches, avenged his execrable blasphemies?

Let François Baudouin condemn me as much as he pleases in this respect,
provided that, in Philipp Melanchthon's judgment, posterity owes me gratitude
for purging the church of so deadly a monster. Let this ungrateful guest also
censure our Senate, under whose rule he once lived, provided that the same
Philipp testifies in a publicly issued writing that it is an example worthy to
be

### [CO IX, p. 576]

imitated by every Christian prince. I acknowledge the great disgrace of
vanity, worthlessness, slander, and abuse. Why, then, does he acquire the
disgrace and hatred of everyone through all these vices? Let there indeed be
great hatred for cruelty inflamed against the innocent. Let there also be
great hostility toward a dictatorship that proscribes good citizens. Let
tyranny in the church be an intolerable thing. Since none of these applies to
me, I freely admit it.

If the filthy abuser's purpose is to brand me, let him recount the deeds of my
cruelty, name innocent people whom I afflicted, and produce the proscription
lists. In short, let him bring out the tyrannical acts I committed under the
appearance of religion. Yet after indulging his lust so intemperately, he
threatens far worse things in the future, and with his customary elegance
jokes that for now he is sparing me. Nothing is better than to look safely
down upon this mad fury, which does not reach me, because when he has exhausted
all his barking he will remain what he always was: an obscene dog.

Let him boast that he does not yield to me in zeal for saving the commonwealth
and the church, provided that even boys mock him as he bellows with empty
pride. Nor is it necessary for France to strain its eyes in order to judge my
constant faithfulness, diligence, integrity, moderation, patience, and
continual labors for the church, for these have been proved by so many notable
demonstrations ever since my youth. With this confidence it will be enough for
me to persist in my station to the very end. His shout that I am mistaken if I
think myself now placed upon such a throne that I may do whatever I please,
insult everyone, impose silence on everyone, and alone rule and thunder is of
the same worthlessness already disclosed in his other filthy insults.

Yet he is determined to leave nothing undone in his insolence. Once, to keep
my name from creating danger for my friends when I wrote to them privately, I
called myself “Lucanius” by reversing letters. This fine defender of the
commonwealth drags that into an accusation. At the same time, mixing in the
name Lucian, he tries to sprinkle me with disgrace by turning Calvin into
Lucian. By this jest, naturally, he wished to prove himself religious—or at
least to anticipate being sent off to Lucian, his own master. These, to be
sure, are the salty refinements of the fine rhetorician. Beside them a quibble
would be tolerable if someone called Baudouin “Badinus,” although even that
would be an affected piece of rottenness.

It is no wonder that a man so alienated in mind talks foolishly. In trying to
wash away the charge of *astorgia*, lack of natural affection, lodged against
him, he reproaches me with childlessness. God gave me a little son; he took him
away. Baudouin counts among my reproaches even the fact that I am without
children. Yet I have myriads of sons throughout the whole Christian world. If
your little daughter, whom you celebrate and in whom you take pride, grows up,
she will rightly be ashamed to have been begotten by such a father. I confess
that I cannot follow the worthy philosopher's inference that, because I am not
yet a father, I do not know what *storgē*, natural family affection, is. I had
thought that relationships of natural affection

### [CO IX, p. 577]

extended also to the other degrees of human devotion. The people of Bourges
loudly proclaim with what justice he is himself very far from being
*astorgos*, without natural affection, when he robbed his own stepchildren
together with their grandmother. Further, if it is shameful for an elderly
theologian to treat somewhat harshly a man still in the flower of life, what
gives younger men such insolent license to insult their elders? By God's
kindness I am indeed old, but not yet sixty, so that this grandson should throw
me from the Sublician Bridge. Anyone who weighs these things—I do not call them
obscenities, but absurdities—will hardly believe that Baudouin, as he pretends,
keeps silent about anything out of respect for me.

I am not surprised that he bears proud witness to the illustrious monuments
of his studies and of his excellent goodwill toward the commonwealth which he
left in Germany (these are his own words), since he says that I am swept into
attacking him while gnashing with envy. What do I envy in you, wretch? What do
you think you gain by such a foolish comparison, except to make everyone spit
upon your filthy mouth? If I were to review everything that it pleased a man
with a brazen—or no—forehead to invent and babble, there would be no end.

At last, as though he had returned to himself and were reasoning
philosophically, he warns that in me God has set forth a singular example of
vanity whose sad memory may perhaps remain to all posterity, so that everyone
may lower his crest and take pity upon our common misery and blindness. Let
this, rather, be the memorable trophy of your vanity. You have begun not only
to raise your crest more insolently than before, but so to sharpen your tongue,
nails, and teeth that nothing prouder or more savage has ever existed. Let him
go now, wallow in his own dung, and delight in the smell of his stench until,
overwhelmed by the abhorrence of every order of society, he begins to learn
what shame and mercy are.

I neither fear nor evade the jurisdiction of the judges to whom he appeals.
How could I fail to submit to the authority of men whom he marks out with rare
and exquisite virtues? But his declaration that they will be brave avengers
of law against injuries, defenders of just liberty against the savagery of a
new tyranny, and patrons of innocence against violence and plots has nothing
to do with me. It has been customary in every age for ranting advocates, whom
a starving appetite for glory drove to madness, to thrust out the tongue more
insolently against a person the more eminent he was in rank. It is the old
rhetoric of buffoons—or rather poisonous mockery—to defile every splendor of
virtue with the name of tyranny.

What can I do for you, Baudouin? When my intention was to remain hidden all my
life, God raised me up where I did not wish to go and willed me to have some
standing in his church. You, scorched and tortured by a mad hunger for glory,
vainly gape after honors and accomplish nothing by so many wandering hunts.
Must I pay the penalty for your folly? By your worthless lie you will no more
persuade anyone that I oppress innocence with violence and threats

### [CO IX, p. 578]

than that, at the jingle of a Vergilian verse, I sent assassins to Cologne to
kill Cassander—a man whom on the very next page you declare to be still alive.
By this honesty, of course, you have acquired the freedom to inveigh against
lies no less sternly than the best champion of truth. But what does he mean by
saying that the people whom he denies to be blind, foolish, or cast down into
degrading servitude are led by me by the nostrils like cattle? Who would
believe it the work of a sane or sober man to entangle such contradictory
words?

What follows bubbles out of the same insanity. In a lamentable voice he
deplores that he is a miserable ward, and at the same time he boasts that,
although I, his terrifying teacher, vomit flames from my mouth, he trusts in
his great guardian who is in heaven, rises above the terrors of every specter
and Fury, and remembers what a better teacher taught: blessed are those whom
men revile with lies. If he wished to be regarded as God's ward, why did he
display no appearance of modesty? What is the point of so abusing God's name
when he does not hesitate both proudly to despise and wickedly to violate
divine and human laws? A man who was such a bad stepfather flees wrongly to
God's guardianship. He wished, of course, to obtain favor among simple people
through a reputation for religion, as though he believed that there were any
divine power in heaven. This is not to rise above the masks, but to heap up the
disguises of every mask in order to deceive.

As for his summoning me to judgment, readers will discover that he did it for
no other purpose than to insert what he had read about Publius Galba in Cicero:
that Galba was the first of the pontiffs to be dragged before the people's
judgment. Because he thought this would be attractive as a barb against me,
he spends three or four pages inveighing against my power rather than lose so
fine a witticism. I supposedly rule as a tyrant, like a Roman idol released
from every bond of law. Evidently his cruel hatred of me is not satisfied with
all the slanders with which every enemy of Christ attacks me, unless I am
thrown into the arena to be torn apart by beasts. This is my immunity: every
assault from every quarter rushes upon me, and their weapons are aimed at me.

From this it is apparent how foul the man's ostentation is when he orders me
to come out of my Aeolia so that he may fight me in open battle. As if his
curses and those of men like him have not penetrated far enough here already,
or as if Geneva blows out the winds with which he himself blusters. Yet I did
not wait for him to draw me from my safe nest, as he pretends. At the time
when villains had inflamed against us the hatred of the whole royal council,
without requesting a safe-conduct, as they call it, I freely asked to be
allowed to plead our cause amid the flames. Baudouin relates something similar
about himself. But because there would be no end of quarreling with a Cynic
buffoon, it is better to oppose the solid truth of the facts to the swollen
bombast of words.

### [CO IX, p. 579]

In one thing he takes the greatest pride: that, after leaving his homeland and
despising wealth and honors, he endured exile and want for professing the
gospel. I certainly count it to his credit that, when brought into danger of
his life on account of the gospel, he took care of himself by flight. His
countrymen and friends deny that he was stripped of his property. Outside the
Emperor's jurisdiction his mother and coheirs permitted him to take as much as
could have come to him from the inheritance had his legal condition remained
unimpaired, so that he did not suffer the loss of even a penny. At one time I
congratulated the man in person for having recovered so easily what he thought
he had lost.

But lest he grow proud against Christ, the king of glory, with false praises,
the same friends affirm that as soon as he had escaped danger, he attempted
through Claude Chansonnette, then chancellor of Lorraine, to arrange a return,
which he was prepared to purchase by foul denial of the gospel. Although,
after being rebuffed, he turned elsewhere, he was already at that time an
apostate by choice. I do not recount the pleasures, advantages, and riches to
which I preferred Christ. I say only that, had I possessed Baudouin's spirit,
it would not have been so difficult for me to obtain what he always sought in
vain and still gapes after far too greedily today. But let that pass.

Content with my slender means, I have practiced frugality in such a way as to
burden no one. I remain quietly at my post. From the salary established for
me, which was modest, I gave a portion back; so far was I from speaking of
increasing it. All my cares, labors, and studies I devote not only to the
benefit of this

### [CO IX, p. 580]

church, to which I am particularly bound, but to helping every church with
whatever aid I can. I discharge the office of teaching so that amid the
greatest faithfulness and diligence no ambition appears. I swallow many
troubles and endure the roughness of many people. No one's power obstructs my
freedom. I therefore do not indulge the great through flattery and fear no
offenses. No favorable successes have yet puffed me up; and when battered by
many severe storms I sustained them fearlessly until, by God's singular
kindness, I emerged. I live courteously with my equals and strive faithfully
to preserve my friendships.

What does Baudouin do? Like a hunting dog, he continually wanders here and
there. For the sake of degrading gain he conducts a sordid market in law. A
position that pleased him just now shortly afterward becomes disgusting to
him. Given over to empty display, he does not know what it is to benefit the
public. Whether religion flourishes or goes to ruin is all one to him. His
private cares do not leave him enough leisure to think about the common
welfare and the church's condition, except that by an unhappy disposition—or
some fatal disease—he always exerts himself to harm it. Devoted only to
himself, he haughtily despises everyone from whom he expects nothing. He
chases the favor of the powerful without restraint through fawning flattery.
He attacks good men whom he envies with more than savagery. Wherever he comes,
he is a truly frenzied firebrand for stirring up conflicts. In short, just as
he has never loved anyone sincerely, so among upright and honest worshipers of
God he has no friend. If I appear too harsh, on Paul's authority I will cast
the blame upon the very man who compelled me.

## Translator's notes

1. *Ad leges De famosis libellis* refers to Baudouin's juridical commentary
   *Ad leges de famosis libellis et de calumniatoribus commentarius*. Calvin
   mocks its reuse of a French royal privilege dated 25 July 1557 for the new
   polemical material. The “king who died two years ago” is Henry II of France,
   who died in 1559.
2. The “writing” printed anonymously at Basel was the conciliatory tract
   conventionally associated with Georg Cassander. Calvin's reply was the
   1561 *Responsio ad versipellem quendam mediatorem* at CO IX 525–560.
   Calvin here acknowledges that its text was largely taken down from his oral
   remarks. The present response is a distinct, later work.
3. “Angel of Satan” (*angelum Satan*) invokes Paul's “thorn in the flesh” at
   2 Corinthians 12:7. Calvin identifies Baudouin with that humiliating
   assailant; at the close he alludes to Paul's “you compelled me” at
   2 Corinthians 12:11.
4. Greek *authadeia* (αὐθάδεια) at p. 566 can denote self-will, arrogance, or
   surly self-satisfaction. “Self-willed disagreeableness” retains both the
   moral and social force of Calvin's explanation.
5. “Hannibal at the gates” is a Roman alarm formula. Sinon is the Greek who,
   in the Trojan-horse story, deceives the Trojans after he is admitted within
   their walls. Calvin contrasts an obvious enemy outside with a deceptive
   mediator already inside.
6. CO prints *Boinus* at p. 568 and records *Boionus* in Beza's and the
   Amsterdam editions. The person's identity is not securely established in
   this draft. *Castalio* is Sebastian Castellio. Calvin reports rather than
   independently proves the positions and associations he assigns to either
   man.
7. “Bargaining with the Cardinal” and “profit-making employment” render
   allusions whose exact circumstances are not restated here. The latter,
   *meritorium quaestum*, may carry the sharper sense of lucrative service or
   trafficking in ecclesiastical preferment.
8. *Balduino-Cassander* at p. 571 is Calvin's hyphenated polemical fusion of
   Baudouin and Cassander. “Ishmael's glory” recalls Genesis 16:12: his hand
   against everyone and everyone's hand against him.
9. The alleged Bucer quotation says that Calvin observed no limit in hatred
   or love, either raising someone above heaven or casting him down to hell.
   Calvin denies ever hearing it and counters with Bucer's surviving letters.
10. *Paedagogia* at p. 572 is unusual and the immediate referent is not named.
    It can mean a schoolmastership, educational office, or tutelage. “His
    fraudulently procured schoolmastership” is deliberately cautious; the
    historical office or advantage at which Calvin aims remains uncertain.
11. Orestes killed his mother Clytemnestra. Hippolytus was destroyed after his
    father Theseus invoked a curse and his horses bolted. Calvin reports
    Baudouin's elaborate comparison, including the claim that Baudouin was
    condemned *inauditum*, without being heard.
12. “Let what is perishing perish” echoes Zechariah 11:9. *Bituriges* is
    Bourges. François Duaren (Duarenus) was the jurist with whom Baudouin
    quarreled; Calvin says that Baudouin endangered him by publicizing the
    orthodoxy of teaching in Duaren's *De sacerdotiis*.
13. *Suffixio* at p. 574 can mean fastening to a stake or cross. The sentence
    deliberately escalates from a criminal's brand (*stigma*) to a more severe
    public punishment; “fastening to the stake” avoids deciding the precise
    instrument.
14. The verses and tags are part of Calvin's attack on Baudouin's literary
    display. “He will strike the stars with his exalted head” adapts Horace,
    *Odes* 1.1.36; “If you wish me to weep” abbreviates *Ars poetica* 102–103.
    The “shoe pinches” saying is retained as the familiar proverb Calvin cites.
15. The passage about Servetus is Calvin's polemical self-defense, not this
    project's endorsement or an independent account of the prosecution. His
    appeal to Melanchthon likewise records Calvin's claim about Melanchthon's
    published judgment.
16. “Lucanius,” “Lucian,” and “Badinus” form a sequence of name-play whose
    full force is not reproducible in English. Calvin says he had used
    *Lucanius* by transposing letters for correspondents' safety; Baudouin
    connected it with Lucian, whom Christian polemic treated as irreligious.
17. The Greek family of *storgē* at pp. 576–577 denotes natural affection,
    especially within a family: *astorgia* is its absence and *astorgos* a
    person lacking it. The allegation that Baudouin robbed stepchildren and
    their grandmother is Calvin's accusation and has not been independently
    verified here.
18. The Sublician Bridge was associated in Roman antiquarian tradition with
    the proverbial disposal of sexagenarians. Calvin, not yet sixty, mocks the
    younger Baudouin as a “grandson” prematurely throwing an old man from it.
19. CO's base text at pp. 577–578 reads *Me vero innocentiam vi et minis
    opprimere ... persuadebis*—literally, “you will persuade that I oppress
    innocence by force and threats.” CO notes that the early French agrees
    with this sense, whereas Beza's and the Amsterdam Latin editions read
    *Meam vero innocentiam*, “my innocence.” The translation follows CO's base
    text and supplies the indefinite object of *persuadebis* required in
    English.
20. “Aeolia” is Aeolus's realm of winds and extends the surrounding charge of
    verbal bluster. “Safe-conduct” translates the contemporary diplomatic
    expression *salvi conductus diploma*.
21. Calvin's Publius Galba allusion depends on Baudouin's citation of Cicero.
    The precise Ciceronian individual and passage have not been securely
    identified in this draft; the translation therefore does not expand
    Calvin's compressed antiquarian reference.
22. CO identifies *Claudius Cantiuncula* as Claude Chansonnette. Calvin's
    claims about Baudouin's inheritance, attempted return, and intended denial
    of the gospel are adversarial allegations, not findings independently
    established by this edition.
23. At p. 580 CO prints *obstrictus* (“bound”) and records *adstrictus* in
    Beza's and the Amsterdam editions; the difference does not materially
    alter the translation. The closing appeal to Paul refers to the apostolic
    convention of blaming compelled self-commendation upon the opponents who
    occasioned it.
