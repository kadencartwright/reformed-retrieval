## Part XIV: Predestination Thesis IV—The Fixed Numbers of the Saved and Condemned

Thesis IV of the Württemberg Doctors

That the number of those who will be saved is fixed before God.

Antithesis IV, Theodore Beza

We agree, and by the same reasoning we affirm that the number before God of those destined to just condemnation is fixed.

1. Yet this is not by an absolute decree of God, namely that God does not will them to be saved, but because of their impiety, by which they spurn God’s grace offered in Christ, whom God the Father wills everyone to hear. Matthew 17.

Theodore Beza’s response to Dr. Andreae’s observation

The cause of condemnation is one thing, and the cause of the decree concerning those to be reprobated and condemned in their own time another; nor are all who are condemned condemned for contempt of the gospel.

Is this truly an answer, or rather a wandering beyond the olive grove? I said, and I say, that if the number of those who will be saved is fixed before God—which Dr. Andreae concedes—it necessarily follows that the number of those who will be condemned is also fixed. If he concedes this, why did he not express it in his thesis? But if he denies it, which plainly cannot be denied with any appearance of reason, why does he not openly deny it? Furthermore, since the certainty of both numbers depends upon the decree we are discussing, and the decree itself does not depend upon either group’s salvation or condemnation, why does he discourse on the cause of condemnation?

The cause of condemnation is manifest and placed outside controversy: sin, whose wages is death. But the cause of the decree we are discussing—not to dignify some, and these rather than those, with mercy—is situated in God’s will alone. Augustine treats this in *On Genesis against the Manichees* 1.3: “Where it is asked why God made it so, the answer must be: because he willed. If by asking you continue, ‘Why did he will it so?’ you seek something greater and higher than God’s will, which cannot be found. Let human rashness therefore restrain itself: let it not seek what does not exist, lest it fail to find what does exist.” And in *To Simplicianus* 1, question 2, he says: “If we granted that hatred”—considered, that is, in the decree’s execution—“depends upon foreseen evil works, would we not conversely concede that his love arises from foreseen good works?”

When I said *just condemnation*, did I not sufficiently signify that between that inscrutable divine and eternal decree concerning the condemnation of certain people—which precedes in order every intermediate cause of its execution—and their condemnation, there intervene just causes of the condemnation itself, subordinated to that decree of God? But here it is worthwhile to see again how greatly this man errs. He wills the cause of condemnation to be “the impiety by which they spurn God’s grace offered to them in Christ, whom God the Father wills everyone to hear.” I indeed acknowledge that contempt of the gospel is the gravest of all sins, so much so that Christ therefore cries out that the condition of Sodom and Gomorrah will be more tolerable than that of Chorazin and Bethsaida. But why does Andreae stop at this alone? Surely so that he may heap error upon error. For in his judgment it is great heresy to deny that even one person exists whom the Lord does not will to be saved and to come to knowledge of the truth, that is, of Christ. “For,” he says, “he wills everyone to hear him,” citing Matthew 17:5, where nevertheless no such thing is related. But on the contrary, if matters stood this way, one of these three positions would have to—

—be maintained: either that the capacity to believe is implanted by nature in everyone, which is purely Pelagian; or that the grace of believing is bestowed upon every person, which, if true, would make false the sayings, “He did not do so with every nation,” and, “To you it has been given to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven”; or finally that the efficacy of the divine decree itself depends upon human choice.

Paul takes a far different course. He derives the common condemnation of humanity from the fact that all people—unless regenerated by grace—knowingly and deliberately extinguish within themselves the sparks of the natural knowledge of God and do not listen to conscience’s admonition, which serves them in place of law. If anyone compares people sinning in this way and deservedly rushing to destruction because of it with those who despise the gospel, who could recount how much more numerous the former are than the latter? And what will become of so many myriads of infants, some dying in unbelieving mothers’ wombs, others outside the church and Christ before mature age? Will our exceedingly confident adversary assert in his customary manner that God also willed Christ to be heard and known by them?

Finally, let Andreae himself see where he borrowed that phrase *God’s absolute decree*, as though God prevents someone willing to believe, rather than that unbelievers do not believe just as it pleases God to abandon them in their unbelief. He would do no one an injury even if he gave no one the ability to believe, to will good, and to do it.

This, therefore, is not our language. With the Apostle we profess and adore that will of God which is a law to itself, not only hidden but also unsearchable; yet we affirm that it is never detached from justice, indeed from the most perfect justice. By that will he has mercy upon whom he wills as a most gracious Father, and hardens whom he wills—and with a will which cannot be resisted. On this point we agree with Augustine, who thinks and speaks in this way from God’s Word. In *Sermons on the Words of the Apostle* 11 he says: “Do you wish to dispute with me? Rather, marvel with me and cry, ‘O depth!’ Let us both agree in trembling, lest we perish in error.” And in chapter 20 of the same work: “Peter denies; the thief believes. O depth! Do you ask for the reason? I will tremble at the depth. You reason; I will marvel. You dispute; I will believe. I see the depth; I do not reach the bottom.”

And in *On the Predestination of the Saints*, chapter 8: “When it had been said, ‘Why does he still complain?’ was Paul’s answer, ‘O man, what you have said is false’? By no means. Rather the answer was, ‘O man, who are you?’” Thomas, agreeing with Augustine, likewise says in *Summa Theologiae* I, question 23, article 5, reply 3: “With regard to some people whom he predestined, God willed to represent his goodness by sparing them according to mercy; and with regard to some whom he reprobates, by punishing them according to justice. This is the reason”—the final cause, namely the manifestation of mercy and justice—“why God chose some and reprobated others, which the Apostle assigns in Romans 9 when he says, ‘God, willing,’ and so forth. But why he chose these for glory and reprobated others”—that is, the reason for God’s eternal decree itself by which it pleased him to save some and condemn others—“the Apostle has—”

“—no reason except the divine will, just as it depends upon an artisan’s simple will that this stone is in this part of the wall and that one in another, although the rationale of the art requires that some stones be in this part and others in that.”
