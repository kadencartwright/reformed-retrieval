## Book II. On Baptism

### Chapter I. Question 1. On the necessity of Baptism

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. There is no question that Baptism is necessary for salvation by
Christ's divine command, for Calvin admits this. But the controversy is
whether it is necessary as a means to salvation, such that if someone is not
baptized, he perishes, even if perhaps through ignorance he is excused from
transgressing the command.

Protestant reply. We concede that the sacrament of Baptism is necessary, not
only as a thing commanded but also as the ordinary means of salvation. We
nevertheless deny that this necessity is so absolute that in all the
unbaptized, even infants, the mere absence of baptism is a certain proof of
perdition.

Bellarmine's arguments.

2. Bellarmine. John 3:5: “Unless someone is born again of water and the Holy
Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God.”

Protestant reply. 1. In chapter 5 Bellarmine admits that Baptism was not
absolutely necessary at the time when the Lord spoke these words with
Nicodemus, nor before the day of Pentecost. But the Lord speaks there of that
necessity which Nicodemus and all the teachers of Israel ought to have
acknowledged at that time, verse 10. He therefore does not speak of the
necessity of outward Baptism. 2. In chapter 6 Bellarmine also concedes that
the baptism of the stream or of water is not so necessary that it cannot
sometimes be supplied in true conversion by the baptism of breath or of the
Spirit. Since this is conceded concerning adults, it cannot be denied
concerning infants except very inconsiderately and absurdly. 3. In those
words of Christ the necessity belongs to regeneration as the effect, verse
3, and to the Spirit as its proper cause, verse 6. Water is mentioned as
perhaps the ordinary means belonging to regeneration by the Spirit, but not
as having the same necessity as regeneration and the Spirit. Thus Alexander
of Hales, part 4, question 8, member 9, article 4, section 5, from the Gloss
on Hebrews 11: “Unless someone is born again—that is, unless he has been
regenerated by that regeneration by which people are reborn of water and the
Holy Spirit—he will not be saved. But people are reborn by it in different
ways.” That is, the regeneration which is customarily represented and
seal—

[1632 witness | printed p. 47]

—ed in outward Baptism is plainly necessary. Nevertheless, that regeneration
is effected in another way by the Spirit in some infants and others without
outward Baptism. The same Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 6, member 7,
confirms the same explanation from Hugh and draws a conclusion of this kind:
“Hence that word of the Lord, ‘unless someone is born again,’ and so forth,
is understood as ‘unless he is saved by faith in Christ,’ which is contained
in that word of the Lord: ‘He who believes in me will not die forever.’”

3. Bellarmine. The church has always believed that infants perish if they
depart this life without Baptism.

Protestant reply. 1. It cannot be proved that the church has always believed
this. 2. Around the time of Augustine, whose authority is especially pressed,
the church also believed that infants who departed this life without the
Lord's Supper perished. Thus Maldonado on John 6: “It was the position of
Augustine and Innocent I, which prevailed in the church for almost six hundred
years, that the Eucharist was necessary even for infants. They considered
that position to be as well founded on John 6:53 as the other on John 3:5.”
Toledo also observes that these passages are plainly similar.

4. Bellarmine. Christians are not born but made.

Protestant reply. 1. This consequence is null: therefore they cannot be saved
except through outward Baptism. 2. The sense of this maxim is that true
Christians are made by the power not of bodily but of spiritual birth. In the
ordinary dispensation of grace, this spiritual birth also appears to follow
bodily birth, as Alexander of Hales observes, although there are some who are
sanctified in the womb.

Ames's arguments.

5. Protestant argument. Genesis 17: “I will be your God and the God of your
offspring.” The children of those who share in Abraham's blessing are children
of God even when they are first born.

Bellarmine. Spiritual promises descend to us not through the carnal generation
of parents but through spiritual regeneration by Christ. We begin to be
children of Abraham when we begin to be children or brothers of Christ, and
it is certain that this does not occur except through Baptism.

Protestant reply. 1. We indeed admit that spiritual regeneration is necessary
for solid participation in the promises. But the very formula of the covenant
plainly declares that this regeneration is part of the promises and belongs
in a special way to the children of believers. 2. We begin to be children of
Christ through faith before Baptism. 3. People are properly baptized because
they are regarded as children of God, not so that they may begin to be
children. Otherwise there would be no reason why the children of unbelievers
should not be baptized just as much as the children of believers.

6. Protestant argument. 1 Corinthians 7:14: “Your children are holy.”
Bellarmine 1. The unbelieving spouse is also said in the same passage to be
sancti—

[1632 witness | printed p. 48]

—fied through the believing spouse, but by this alone the unbelieving spouse
cannot attain the forgiveness of sins.

Protestant reply. 1. The unbelieving spouse is said to be sanctified not
simply but with respect to the use of marriage, just as all creatures are
sanctified for their use, 1 Timothy 4:5. But believers' children are called
holy simply, not with respect to any human use but in relation to God. 2. The
unbelieving spouse is not eligible for Baptism because of the believing
spouse, but the children of a believing parent are eligible for Baptism by
that very designation. The nature of the holiness is therefore different.
3. Believers' children are called holy because they are included in the
covenant and ought to be enrolled in the church, as appears from the contrast
the text makes between sanctified and unsanctified parents and between
unclean and holy children.

Bellarmine 2. Such children are called not unclean—that is, infamous and
illegitimate—but holy, that is, legitimate and free from civil disgrace.

Protestant reply. On this reasoning the apostle would attribute nothing more
to believers' children than to unbelievers' children, nor to the believing
spouse than to the unbelieving one. This plainly conflicts with both his words
and his purpose.

Bellarmine 3. They are called holy because they are consecrated to God through
Baptism and are educated in the fear of God.

Protestant reply. 1. Their being consecrated to God through Baptism would have
no more place among believers' children than among unbelievers' children
unless they had some prior relation to God. 2. This would not confirm the
sanctification of the parents' marriage, because it can also have a place in
children conceived in fornication.

Bellarmine. Although an evil thing cannot become good through union with a
believer, a good thing can nevertheless become better.

Protestant reply. But the question was whether the marriage of a believer
with an unbeliever was a good thing or not. That could in no way be proved
from a fruit common to a good and an evil thing.

Bellarmine 4. The unbelieving spouse is said to be sanctified through the
believing spouse because the unbeliever learns to observe the laws of
marriage.

Protestant reply. 1. Observance of the laws of marriage can be found between
two unbelievers and can also be lacking between two believers. 2. Children
born from a legitimate marriage do not become more unclean with that
uncleanness which is opposed to holiness merely because all the laws are not
observed.

7. Protestant argument. Baptism is profaned when it is given to someone who
does not belong to Christ.

Bellarmine. Baptism is not profaned when it is given to the unclean but
cleanses them.

Protestant reply. Why, then, should it not be given to the children of
unbelievers and to unbelievers themselves, even against their will?

[1632 witness | printed p. 49]

8. Protestant argument. The infants of the Jews who died before the eighth
day did not perish. Therefore neither do Christian infants who die before
they are baptized.

Bellarmine 1. This whole argument consists of uncertain matters.

Protestant reply. This argument is held to be certain and firm by Cajetan, on
part 3, question 68, articles 1 and 2; Tilmann of Segeberg, *On the Sacraments*,
Book 7, chapter 1; Thomas Elisius, *On Baptism*, article 3; and Cassander, *On
the Baptism of Infants*, where the preceding testimonies are set out at length
and approved.

Bellarmine 2. Many of the ancients deny that Circumcision was given as a
remedy against sin.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself testifies in Book 2, chapter 13, that
Alexander, Bonaventure, Scotus, and Gabriel attributed as much to Circumcision
in justification as they attribute to Baptism, and that the others regarded
Circumcision as a condition without which justification did not occur.
Therefore there is no controversy among the scholastics regarding necessity.

Bellarmine 3. We do not know whether the infants of the Jews had some remedy
before the eighth day, or what it was.

Protestant reply. We know that no outward remedy is revealed in Scripture,
and we also know that Scripture is not silent about remedies necessary for
salvation.

Bellarmine 4. A certain, most widely available, and very easy remedy has now
been determined by Christ, beyond which it is not lawful to invent another.

Protestant reply. 1. At that time too, a certain remedy was determined beyond
which it was not lawful to invent another. But the universal availability and
ease of Baptism afford no relief to those infants who die without Baptism.
2. The determination found in the New Testament does not restrict God's grace,
dissolve the power of faith, make the condition of believers worse, or make
their consolation less.

9. Protestant argument. It is plainly foreign to God's mercy that all the
children of believers who die without Baptism should perish eternally. This
is the argument of Gabriel, Gerson, Cajetan, and the authors previously cited
from Cassander, along with many others.

Bellarmine 1. God's judgments are hidden.

Protestant reply. 1. But God's hidden judgments never become manifest through
the administration of the sacraments where everything else is equal. 2. God's
mercy also is hidden. There is therefore no difference in this respect that
prevents the unbaptized from secretly being vessels of mercy just as much as
vessels of wrath.

Bellarmine 2. Infants have original sin.

[1632 witness | printed p. 50]

Protestant reply. What all possess equally cannot be the reason for unlike
treatment.

Bellarmine 3. Those who die in infancy, if they are predestined, are without
doubt baptized.

Protestant reply. According to the position of the Roman Church, a pious
stepmother, therefore, all who are not baptized are reprobate. But “who has
known the mind of the Lord, or who has been his counselor?” We know certainly
from God's revealed mind that believers ought not to grieve over their dead
like the Gentiles who have no hope, 1 Thessalonians 4:13.

### Chapter II. Question 2. On the minister of Baptism

1. Bellarmine. 1. By ordinary office, the right to baptize belongs only to
priests. 2. By office it also belongs to deacons to baptize, but in the absence
of priests or at their command. 3. In a case of necessity baptized laymen, and
even unbaptized laymen, are permitted to baptize if they know the rite, though
not solemnly. 4. Women also can baptize in extreme necessity.

Protestant reply. Because we hold that the right to baptize is a proper effect
of the calling by which that right is communicated, and Bellarmine himself
concedes that by office or ordinary calling the right belongs only to
ecclesiastical ministers ordained for this very thing, while no extraordinary
office ordinarily granted to anyone in this matter can be shown, we say that
only those ministers to whom the power of baptizing has been committed are
permitted to baptize. The foundation of the contrary position, which is
placed in the absolute necessity of Baptism, was overthrown in the preceding
disputation.

2. Bellarmine. Zipporah, the wife of Moses, circumcised her son and in this
way delivered her husband from the danger of death, Exodus.

4. Protestant reply. 1. The right to circumcise is not read to have been
committed specifically to any fixed order of people so that it belonged to
them by office. The reasoning for Circumcision and Baptism is therefore
unequal. 2. What Zipporah did occurred before polity was established in the
Israelite church and in a time of great confusion. Nothing can therefore be
concluded from it about a well-ordered church. 3. If what Bellarmine affirms
is true—that Moses indicated to his wife that she should do this when he
could not—then Zipporah appears to have had an extraordinary command. 4.
Zipporah did this neither to provide for the boy's salvation nor from
obedience toward God, but from agitation of mind, as appears from those words:
“You are a bridegroom of blood to me.” There is therefore nothing in that deed
which can be made an example.

Bellarmine. God was appeased because of that Circumcision.

[1632 witness | printed p. 51]

Protestant reply. He was also appeased in some way toward those Gentiles who
worshiped him perversely, 1 Kings 17.

Bellarmine. This was because they worshiped the true God with a true rite,
not because they employed some rite devised by themselves.

Protestant reply. So too here God was appeased because Circumcision, instituted
by him, was observed in some way, not because Zipporah thrust herself into
this business.

3. Bellarmine. Everyone is bound in a moment of necessity to teach and
instruct the ignorant, especially when eternal salvation is in danger;
therefore everyone is also bound to baptize.

1. Protestant reply. By the same line of consequence, everyone would also be
permitted in a moment of necessity to administer the remaining sacraments:
the Eucharist, Penance, Orders, Confirmation, and Extreme Unction. 2. Private
and fraternal instruction is the duty of all believers according to the
ability and opportunity they possess, even apart from a moment of extreme
necessity. But no one is permitted to engage in public instruction by
occupying a minister's place unless legitimately called. 3. Instruction
considered simply is not an act of authority but of love. Yet baptizing always
and everywhere is an act of authority.

3. Bellarmine. Philip in Acts 8, Ananias in Acts 9, and those who were with
Peter at Cornelius's house in Acts 10 baptized. So too did others besides the
apostles, presbyters, or deacons in Acts 2.

Protestant reply. 1. While the church was first being constituted, some things
were done outside the ordinary order which are not to be observed in the
constituted church. 2. Philip and Ananias were extraordinary ministers and
had a special revelation. Those who were with Peter baptized at Peter's
command, and it cannot be proved that they were laymen. The same reasoning
applies to Acts 2.

4. Protestant argument. Matthew 28:19: “Teach; baptize.” These things are
spoken only to the apostles and ministers of the word.

Bellarmine 1. It is not certain whether these things were spoken only to the
apostles.

Protestant reply. 1. Nothing can be more certain than that the offices of
teaching and baptizing are joined in these words. 2. Either these things were
spoken directly and immediately only to the apostles, or other people had
commands just as general as the apostles themselves. 3. It is certain that
these things were not spoken to private persons, pagans, and women.

Bellarmine 2. Apostolic deeds explained this command: namely, that when
necessity presses, everyone is permitted to baptize, although otherwise it
belongs to the special office of certain people.

Protestant reply. 1. In the Acts of the Apostles, absolutely nothing is read
of private Christians baptizing without special revelation, much less of
pagans and women. 2. The deeds of the apost—

[1632 witness | printed p. 52]

—les do not conflict with their teaching. But the baptism performed by laymen
and laywomen conflicts with the teaching not only of the apostles but even of
the papists themselves. Thus Bellarmine says in *On the Sacraments*, Book 1,
chapter 27: “There are two positions of the theologians. Some, such as Cajetan,
Soto, and Ledesma, maintain that in the minister himself there is also an
operative power acting efficiently and instrumentally, as there is in the
sacrament. According to this position an operative power exists in the
sacramental words dependently upon the minister, for those words have power
only when they are joined to the power that is in the minister. Others hold
that the minister concurs only by applying the sacraments. According to this
position the sacramental words do not have power and consequently are not
sacramental unless they are moved by God as instruments; but they are not
moved by God as instruments unless they are pronounced by the person to whom
he himself committed them.” Administration of the sacrament by a pagan, a
private person, or anyone who does not possess a power committed by God
himself to administer the sacraments as God's instruments and ministers can
be reconciled with neither of these positions.

### Chapter III

Question 3 concerns the faith required in the Baptism of infants. But
Bellarmine frames no controversy here that touches us. Nevertheless, a few of
Bellarmine's propositions should be considered briefly.

#### Thesis I

Bellarmine. Infants are not justified without any faith.

Protestant reply. This is indeed true if it is understood of that grace which
is the principle of faith and, together with actual faith, establishes us in
the same relation to God in Christ. Yet Bellarmine attributes no efficiency
to faith in the justification of infants. For these are his words in *On the
Effect of the Sacraments*, Book 2, chapter 1: “I say that the performed work
excludes faith from the efficiency of sacramental grace.”

2. Bellarmine. The habits of faith, hope, and love are infused into infants
in Baptism.

Protestant reply. 1. This has no bearing on the faith required for Baptism,
for that faith must precede Baptism itself, not be infused through it. 2.
Experience itself proves that these habits are not infused into all the
baptized, since many children of Christians who are seized and raised by the
Turks show no other inclination or disposition toward acts of faith than the
children of the Turks. 3. Nevertheless, we do not deny that when some are
baptized God infuses a habit or principle

[1632 witness | printed p. 53]

of grace into them. 4. Outward Baptism cannot be a physical instrument of the
infusion of grace, because it does not possess that grace in itself in any
way. God can therefore communicate the same grace either before or after
Baptism.

3. Bellarmine. Two kinds of faith are required in Baptism. One is actual and
precedes Baptism as a certain disposition; this for the most part does not
arise from the habit of faith. The other follows Baptism, is an essential part
of justification, and is not an act but a habit.

Protestant reply. This distinction conflicts with: 1. Scripture, which teaches
that justifying faith precedes Baptism, Acts 15:9. 2. Reason and experience,
which dictate that Baptism is received by precisely the same faith by which
the fruit of Baptism is received. 3. Bellarmine's own confession found in
*On the Effect of the Sacraments*, Book 2, chapter 13: “Adults are justified
through faith and true contrition before they actually approach the
sacrament.”

### Chapter IV. Question 4. On the effects of Baptism

1. Bellarmine. Baptism actually removes all sins, so that not only are they
not imputed, but there is nothing which could be imputed as fault.

Protestant reply. 1. This is indeed said truly when the efficacy of Baptism
is fully received, as Alexander of Hales and Bonaventure observe. But that
this occurs immediately and ordinarily conflicts with: 1. the express words
of Scripture which condemn this position as a lie, 1 John 1:8; 2. the Lord's
Prayer prescribed to all the baptized: “Forgive us our debts”; 3. the common
experience of the baptized, who feel in themselves the flesh lusting against
the spirit and are subject to evils of punishment which are never separated
from the evil of fault; and 4. the position of the scholastics, who call
baptismal grace the grace of beginners, not of the perfect.

2. Bellarmine. This is proved from the expressions cleansing, washing,
removing, blotting out, and taking away which are used in Scripture.

Protestant reply. 1. These expressions are applied not only to Baptism but
also to the forgiveness of sins committed before Baptism was instituted and
after Baptism was received. They therefore do not demonstrate the special
efficacy of Baptism any more than that of faith and repentance. 2. All these
expressions are rightly explained of the perfect abolition of guilt and the
begun mortification of vice or stain.

3. Bellarmine. Stains, defilements, and iniquities are said to be removed.

Protestant reply. These are removed both in effectual Baptism and apart from
Baptism, but in such a way that they can be removed still further or to a
greater degree day by day, 2 Corinthians 4:16.

[1632 witness | printed p. 54]

4. Bellarmine. In the figures of Baptism something was truly and properly
effected.

Protestant reply. Although we do not recognize figures of outward Baptism,
we nevertheless concede that in effectual Baptism sins are truly and properly
removed.

5. Bellarmine. The cleansing of justification is compared to things that are
truly and properly clean.

Protestant reply. 1. So too believers both before they are baptized and when
they afterward rise again from some sin are truly and properly clean. 2. Not
even the papists attribute the perfection of righteousness to the baptized.

6. Bellarmine. In Adam all are truly dead, and Christ truly died; therefore
those who are in Christ are truly justified.

Protestant reply. Who doubts it?

7. Bellarmine. How are we truly regenerated and made alive if death still
remains in us?

Protestant reply. In the same way that the principle of life can coexist with
a mortal wound which has begun to be healed but is not yet perfectly cured.

8. Bellarmine. Light and darkness, sin and righteousness, cannot dwell
together.

Protestant reply. That is so, of course, if each is to exercise dominion. But
the papists themselves concede that the sins which they call venial can dwell
together with righteousness, and they also admit the same concerning the old
man, the flesh, and concupiscence.

Bellarmine. Concupiscence is not sin unless we consent to its desires by free
will.

Protestant reply. The apostle expressly calls it sin, Romans 7, and everywhere
admonishes that it be mortified.

9. Bellarmine. God hates sin and therefore punishes it where it remains.

Protestant reply. 1. This has no more bearing on Baptism than on repentance.
2. That which is worthy of hatred and punishment can remain without vengeance
following; otherwise sin would never be removed by God, for God as avenger
does not remove sin. 3. Hatred of sin can coexist with love for the sinner.
The person who hates vices does not immediately hate people. 4. Bellarmine
could not deny that every punishment of sin is removed in the regenerate,
for his own defense is found in *On Purgatory*, Book 1, chapter 11: “Death is
a true punishment of original sin, and righteous people suffer it.” Although
he therefore sets out the papists' position in these words, “Baptism truly
removes and blots out every fault and punishment,” he nevertheless makes no
mention of punishment in the proof, because common experience teaches that
the baptized die even before committing a new sin. This sophistical method of
disputation is excessively shameful and disgraceful. Bellarmine must have
promised himself readers who were either blind or dull.

[1632 witness | printed p. 55]

10. Bellarmine. Baptism does not make people incapable of sinning.

Protestant reply. Was Bellarmine sufficiently sane when he simultaneously
objected to us that we deny sins are removed through Baptism and yet affirm
that people are made incapable of sinning through Baptism?

Bellarmine. Baptism does not bring it about that the baptized cannot lose
that grace except when they are unwilling to believe.

Protestant reply. Effectual Baptism unto grace through faith undoubtedly does
bring it about that this grace cannot be lost while the same faith remains.
We teach nothing else on this matter.

11. Bellarmine. Through Baptism people are not freed from keeping God's law.

Protestant reply. This is so far from our confession that we believe precisely
the contrary: through the covenant of Baptism people are bound to keep God's
law, although we know that salvation from grace, not from law, is promised in
Baptism.

12. Bellarmine. Baptism does not free people from obligation to ecclesiastical
law.

Protestant reply. The obligation of no legitimate law is removed through
legitimate Baptism.

13. Bellarmine. Through Baptism all vows are not rendered void.

Protestant reply. Certainly not those contained in Baptism itself, either
actually or virtually.

14. Bellarmine. By the remembrance of Baptism alone, sins committed after
Baptism cannot be forgiven.

Protestant reply. Nor do we affirm this. But we teach that sins committed
after Baptism are forgiven through faith and repentance renewed from the
remembrance of Baptism.

Bellarmine. The efficacy of Baptism does not extend to the future but only to
the past.

Protestant reply. 1. The scholastics themselves teach the contrary concerning
a person who approaches Baptism falsely and hypocritically: although he
approaches falsely and afterward even commits mortal sins, yet when the
pretense departs, Baptism has its efficacy together with his faith and
contrition. Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 8, member 7, article 2,
section 2. 2. Bellarmine himself contends in *On the Effect of the Sacraments*,
Book 2, chapter 19, that the baptismal character is indelible and remains
forever, and that the same character is both the sacrament and the thing of
the sacrament. Therefore the sacrament of Baptism remains forever.

### Chapter V. Question 5. On the Baptism of John

1. Bellarmine. The Baptism of John was not the same sacrament as the Baptism
of Christ.

Protestant reply. It was indeed not the same in every single circumstance,
but in essence it was the same.

[1632 witness | printed p. 56]

Bellarmine 1. The Baptism of John was instituted by John himself.

Protestant reply. 1. Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 6, member 8, answers:
“By John's ministry indeed, but by God's authority and command.” 2. Many
sacrifices were instituted in a similar way by the first patriarchs and yet
were the same sacrifices when they were instituted through Moses.

Bellarmine 2. It is everywhere called the Baptism of John.

Protestant reply. Bellarmine himself answers in *On the Sacraments*, Book 1,
chapter 23: “In the same way the old law is called the law of Moses, although
Moses did not properly institute it but only promulgated it.”

Bellarmine. The Baptism of John did not have an invocation of the Trinity.

Protestant reply. It had one at least implicitly. For it is clear from Christ's
Baptism that John preached the doctrine of the three divine persons, and he
confirmed his doctrine by baptism.

Bellarmine 4. John's Baptism was not instituted under the New Testament.

Protestant reply. 1. It was instituted in the same way as the sacrifices of
the patriarchs were under the Old Testament. 2. Christ himself testifies to
the contrary, Luke 16:16: “The Law and the Prophets were until John; from that
time the kingdom of God is evangelized.”

2. Bellarmine. The Baptism of John did not have the same power and efficacy
as the Baptism of Christ has.

Protestant reply. 1. Bellarmine brings forward no proof of this assertion
from Scripture except those words in Mark 1: “I baptize with water, but he
with the Spirit.” These were sufficiently discussed above. 2. Bellarmine
himself concedes in this place that “the Baptism of John could forgive sins
from the devotion and faith of those approaching, or even from the contrition
which people conceived from John's sermons and baptism.” But we proved above
that greater power and efficacy ought not to be attributed to any sacrament
of the New Testament.

3. Bellarmine. After the Baptism of John, Christ's Baptism necessarily had to
be received.

Protestant reply. In this proposition Bellarmine contradicts the Master of
the Sentences, Book 4, distinction 2, who is defended at length against people
like Bellarmine by Alexander of Hales, part 4, question 6, member 7.1. “Thus
plainly to contradict the Master of the Sentences,” he says, “and thus to
evade Jerome's words and the things sufficiently clearly intimated in
Scripture, Acts 8, appears unfitting,” and so forth. The Scriptures twisted
in this direction by Bellarmine were treated above, except for the history
which now remains to be considered.

4. Bellarmine. It is not credible that among so many thousands whom the
apostles baptized in Jerusalem in Acts 2, 4, and 8, none had previously been
baptized by John, if what Mark said in chapter 1 is true: that all the people
of Jerusalem were baptized by John.

[1632 witness | printed p. 57]

Protestant reply. 1. Cajetan answers on Mark 1: “The sense is not that everyone
who went out to hear him was baptized, for many did not receive his baptism,
but that all who went out to receive baptism were baptized not by the
disciples but by John himself.” 2. It is just as credible that among those
thousands in Acts 2, 4, and 8 none had been baptized by John and none had been
baptized by Christ's disciples, for the latter had baptized more people than
John, John 4:1, 3. Among those thousands of people who were baptized on the
day of Pentecost, many had assembled at that time from every nation under
heaven, Acts 2:5. It is more than credible that they had not been baptized by
John.

Ames's arguments.

5. Protestant argument. There was the same doctrine, the same rite, and the
same offer of grace in the Baptism of John and of Christ. It was therefore the
same Baptism with respect to its substance and efficacy.

Bellarmine 1. It is not true that the doctrine of John and Christ was the same,
except in the sense that John taught nothing contrary to Christ.

Protestant reply. 1. The sum of John's doctrine in Matthew 3:2 was the same as
that of Christ's disciples in Matthew 10:7 and Luke 10:9: “Repent, for the
kingdom of heaven has drawn near.” 2. John 1 taught Christ in his entirety:
namely, that he is that Son of God and that Lamb of God.

Bellarmine. His calling Christ the Lamb of God could have referred to
innocence and gentleness even if he had not been a victim.

Protestant reply. But he called him “that Lamb of God who takes away the sins
of the world.” Can this be referred to anything other than Christ's
redemption? John's doctrine concerning Christ was more illustrious than the
doctrine of Christ's disciples before his resurrection.

Bellarmine 2. Doctrine contributes nothing to the essence and truth of a
sacrament.

Protestant reply. The word, promise, and covenant of God are therefore of no
account in the papists' sacraments.

Bellarmine 3. Heretics who have false doctrine have true sacraments.

Protestant reply. There are various degrees of falsity. Those who do not in
some way profess that true doctrine on which a particular sacrament properly
rests do not truly have that sacrament.

Bellarmine 4. The offer of grace was not the same in each Baptism, for
repentance followed the Baptism of John as its effect, but precedes the
Baptism of Christ as its disposition. Moreover, forgiveness of sins is
attributed to Christ's Baptism as its immediate effect, but repentance is
attributed to John's Baptism, not to Baptism, Mark 1:4.

[1632 witness | printed p. 58]

Protestant reply. These evasions are empty and purely fictitious. For: 1.
Humble confession of sins, and consequently repentance, preceded John's
Baptism, Mark 1:5. 2. The mortification of sin, and thus repentance, is also
an effect of Christ's Baptism, Romans 6. 3. If forgiveness of sins was an
effect of repentance, and repentance an effect of John's Baptism, then
forgiveness itself was also an effect of the same Baptism. The mediate and
immediate operation of the sacraments was discussed above in its place.

6. Protestant argument. It is the sweetest consolation of Christians that
they are baptized with the same baptism with which Christ was baptized. But
Christ was baptized with John's baptism. The Baptism of Christians is
therefore the same as John's Baptism.

Bellarmine 1. We can scarcely attribute Christ's Baptism to Christ himself
without injury to him, because he would appear to have needed regeneration
and adoption.

Protestant reply. 1. By similar reasoning Bellarmine could conclude that
Circumcision, participation in the Passover and the Eucharist, or even John's
Baptism itself cannot be attributed to Christ, because he would appear to
have needed the grace of these sacraments. 2. We do not say that Christ
received the inward and spiritual baptism but that he gave it. Yet to show
that he had sanctified outward Baptism for this use, he received that outward
Baptism which he sanctified. This is what the scholastics hand down from the
Fathers: “Baptism has its force and power from contact with the flesh of
Christ.” Alexander of Hales, part 3, question 8, member 4; Thomas, part 3,
question 39; Cajetan on Matthew 3.

Bellarmine 2. Whatever the case may be, it was certainly more glorious in
Christ not to receive his own baptism than to receive it.

Protestant reply. We must determine what was glorious for Christ not from
human petty reasonings but from the word and Christ's own practice. In the
same way that it was more glorious for Christ to receive his own supper than
not to receive it, it was also more glorious for him to receive his own
Baptism than not to receive it.

7. Protestant argument. When Christ was baptized by John, the Holy Spirit was
visibly present and the voice of grace sounded. These are the same chief
things which occur invisibly in our Baptism. The Baptism of John and ours is
therefore the same.

Bellarmine 1. Before the Lord was baptized, a dove never appeared and a voice
from heaven was not heard, yet John's baptism was the same then as it was
afterward.

Protestant reply. Nor afterward did these things appear visibly in Christ's
Baptism. It was sufficient for the operation of invisible grace to appear
visibly once; yet it had to appear in the same sacra—

[1632 witness | printed p. 59]

—ment to which it belonged. Otherwise it would commend no kind of sacrament
at all any more than a pipe, lyre, or trumpet giving an uncertain sound
without any distinction. Bellarmine brings forward nothing about the
ceremonies of baptism drawn either from Scripture or from reason. It will
therefore suffice to note briefly here: 1. Since by Bellarmine's own admission
the papists add twelve ceremonies before baptism, five in baptism, and five
after baptism, besides other particular customs, they manifestly sin against
that rule which Bellarmine acknowledges should be approved in *On the Effect
of the Sacraments*, Book 2, chapter 30: “Ceremonies are not to be multiplied
so greatly that by their multitude they in some way overwhelm the religion
they ought to serve.” Twenty-two ceremonies cannot fail to overwhelm the
simplicity of baptism's washing. Certainly so many were not prescribed for
any sacrament of the ceremonial law itself. 2. Bellarmine very absurdly places
among ceremonies instituted by human beings certain actions founded in
Scripture and suited to the celebration of baptism not by human choice but
from the nature of the thing itself. 3. Testimonies from antiquity for human
ceremonies prove nothing other than the antiquity of error and human
presumption.
