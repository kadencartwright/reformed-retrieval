## Part 39: Seleucus Philopator and Antiochus Epiphanes

Antiochus the Great left two sons after him. Seleucus Philopator, the elder by
birth, succeeded him in the kingdom, while the younger, Antiochus Epiphanes,
lived at Rome as a hostage. Seleucus was a man of such meager virtue that in
the twelve years of his reign he accomplished nothing distinguished. He was,
to be sure, kind to the Jews until Simon the Benjaminite, because of a private
quarrel with the high priest Onias, persuaded him that enormous treasures were
stored in the Jerusalem temple. To seize them Seleucus sent Heliodorus to
Jerusalem. The high priest Onias III, son of Simon II, a prudent and holy man,
strove with all his strength to turn Heliodorus away from sacrilege. But when
he had labored in vain and Heliodorus entered the temple to plunder everything,
an angel on horseback met him, accompanied by two foot soldiers who beat the
sacrilegious man with whips almost to death. Heliodorus was carried out of the
temple by the hands of his men and, after several days, was restored to health
by the prayers of Onias. Josephus calls him Apollonius, the envoy of Seleucus.
Third Maccabees—which the papists themselves pronounce apocryphal—claims that
Ptolemy Philopator was treated in precisely the same fashion by the angel who
guarded the Jerusalem temple, when, while sacrificing at Jerusalem after the
defeat of Antiochus, he wished to inspect the sanctuary.

Josephus claims that the Lacedaemonians sent envoys to this Onias who asserted
that Abraham was the common stock of both the Spartan and Jewish nation. We
shall perhaps see the truth of this in its proper place.

> Marginalia, printed p. 224: *Seleucus sends Heliodorus to plunder the
> Jerusalem temple*.

In order to release his brother Antiochus, Seleucus sent his son Demetrius,
barely eight years old, to Rome as a hostage. While Antiochus was traveling to
his brother, he heard on the road that Seleucus had been killed and the kingdom
seized by Heliodorus—the very man, according to Torniellus, who had been cast
out of the Jerusalem temple by an angel. Yet it was by no means inconsistent
with Antiochus’s nature and arts that, as some also report of him, Seleucus was
killed by his counsel and cunning and the kingdom snatched away before
Demetrius could take it. In any event, with the aid of Eumenes of Pergamum,
Antiochus easily overthrew and killed Heliodorus. Then, after sending a splendid
embassy to Rome with great gifts, he established himself in his brother’s
kingdom.

Antiochus the Great, the father of Epiphanes, had given his daughter Cleopatra
in marriage to Ptolemy Epiphanes so that through his daughter’s agency he might
add the kingdom of Egypt to his own. But Cleopatra loved her husband more than
her father and, by her prudence, greatly aided her husband against both her
father’s violence and his deceptions. When Ptolemy Epiphanes died after a reign
of twenty-four years, he left three children: Ptolemy Philometor, Ptolemy
Physcon, and the younger Cleopatra. Greatly fearing Antiochus for them, their
father named the Roman Senate as their guardians in his will.

> Marginalia, printed p. 224: *He covets Egypt*.

Antiochus, the Ptolemies’ maternal uncle, labored above all to be admitted as
the boys’ guardian, with the purpose of occupying the kingdom of Egypt once,
during his nephews’ tender age while the Romans were occupied by the Macedonian
War, and then retaining it forever. His first concern was to recover Coele
Syria, which his father had given to the Egyptian as a dowry with Cleopatra.
The work was easy for him while he employed his own deceptions and the forces
of Eumenes of Pergamum.

When Antiochus came to Jerusalem, he was received amicably by the high priest
Onias. From there he hastened into Egypt with an enormous army and, on the
pretense of being his nephews’ guardian, occupied most of the country. The
elder of the Ptolemies was called Philometor, not, as Christianus Matthius
claims *by way of hyperbole*, without any witness, because he married his
mother Cleopatra (it is certain that each brother married their sister
Cleopatra), nor, as Rhalegus claims *by way of antiphrasis*, likewise without
any witness, because he killed his mother, but because he was dearer to his
mother than his brother Physcon was. Philometor and his brother did not get
along well; Antiochus fostered their discord. But to avoid incurring the
Romans’ displeasure, he quickly led his army back from Egypt into Syria with a
great sum of money which he had forcibly extorted from the Egyptians, leaving
a garrison at Pelusium.

> Marginalia, printed p. 224: *Why Philometor was so called*.

While Antiochus was at Tyre, Jesus, the brother of the high priest Onias, came
to him with gifts. This utterly wicked man conferred privately with Antiochus
and obtained the right to depose his brother and himself ascend to the
pontifical office at the price of 360 talents, or 495,000 gold pieces.
According to Epiphanius, a Hebrew talent contains one hundred

and twenty-five pounds of silver. Besides the 360 talents, Jesus promised
Antiochus another 230 under other headings; added to the former sum, according
to Salianus’s calculation, these make 811,250 crowns. Josephus’s statement
about three thousand talents to be paid annually must be an error either in a
copyist or in the author’s calculation. In any event, through this sacrilegious
bargain, by Antiochus’s command and force, Jesus cast out his brother and
seized both the high priesthood and the principality, which the high priests
appear also to have possessed before.

Not content with these crimes, Jesus became an open apostate from the true
worship of God to the Greeks’ most loathsome idolatry. He changed his name to
Jason, seduced most of the priests and the people from the God of the
Israelites to the idols of the nations, and built a gymnasium at Jerusalem
below the citadel and the temple. There Jewish youth, naked and with their
foreskins restored in the Greek manner, exercised themselves not only with the
discus and in boxing, but also under the broad-brimmed hat in every wickedness.
Is it any wonder that this public apostasy of Jerusalem’s high priest, priests,
and people not only from true worship but also from all moral decency soon drew
Antiochus’s horrible persecutions upon them under the direction of divine
justice?

> Marginalia, printed p. 225: *The horrible apostasy of the priests and
> people*.

Jason enjoyed the office he had acquired most criminally and administered
still more criminally for scarcely three years. Onias, his equal in every
crime, who had taken the name Menelaus and had been sent by him with the
tribute, purchased the high priestly office from Antiochus by promising a
higher price. Josephus claims this Menelaus was a full brother of Onias and
Jason, but the author of Second Maccabees makes him a brother of Simon the
Benjaminite, who enticed Heliodorus from Seleucus to plunder the temple.
Because of this and many other discrepancies with the books of Maccabees,
Salianus often assails Josephus as a falsifier and an adversary of Holy
Scripture. But among sensible men Josephus’s authority is greater than that of
this apocryphal book, which, if not much more recent, is certainly much less
learned.

Driven out of Jerusalem by Menelaus, Jason fled to the Arabs. His pious brother
Onias lived at Antioch in the sanctuary of Daphne, a most impure place.
Andronicus was satrap of Syria under Antiochus. In order to obtain money for
Antiochus, the false high priest Menelaus sold many of the temple’s golden
vessels at Tyre. When Onias and other Jews accused him of this sacrilege, he
arranged for Onias to be led out of the sanctuary of Daphne and killed by
Andronicus. Angered by the shameful killing of Onias, Antiochus ordered
Andronicus to be executed at Antioch on the very spot where he had killed
Onias. Nevertheless, corrupted by Menelaus’s gifts, he condemned the other
Jewish accusers to the ultimate punishment on false charges. Still, when
Menelaus did not pay the great sums of money he had promised, before the end of
the year he too was deposed from the high priesthood by Antiochus and its
administration entrusted to his brother Lysimachus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 225: *Antiochus avenges the killing of Onias*.

Meanwhile Jason entered Jerusalem from Arabia with an army and perpetrated an
indiscriminate slaughter of people of every age and sex, until the people rose
up and forcibly cast him out, driving him back to his former hiding places.
These were only the beginnings of the calamities of the Jews. In any event,
Jason was driven from Arabia into Egypt, expelled from there to Lacedaemon,
and died there of contempt and starvation.

> Marginalia, printed p. 225: *Jason’s cruelty toward his own people*.

Antiochus coveted Egypt above all else. The Ptolemy brothers, fearing him,
were reconciled to one another. Antiochus took this concord between the
brothers very badly and therefore immediately flew to Pelusium with his army,
devouring the kingdom of Egypt in certain hope. The brothers fled to the only
refuge left, that of the Romans. The Senate sent the former consul Popilius
Laenas as envoy to Antiochus. He met Antiochus and ordered him to abstain from
Egypt. When Antiochus answered that he would deliberate with his friends,
Popilius used the staff he carried in his hand to draw a circle around
Antiochus, ordering him to summon his friends into it and not to leave the
circle before he had answered plainly whether he wished peace or war with the
Romans. Terrified by Popilius’s severity, Antiochus promised to obey the
Senate’s command. He therefore led his army back from Egypt, filled with an
enormous weight of indignation that fear of the Romans had snatched the war
from his jaws, and came to Jerusalem. Before Antiochus’s descent into Egypt,
for forty days continuously in broad daylight the Jews saw armies in the sky
above Jerusalem charging one another. Foolishly they interpreted this not as a
portent of their own calamities, but of Antiochus’s death. Antiochus paid both
great tribute and gifts to the Romans every year, and the highest extravagance
in banquets and other entertainments caused the highest rapacity and the need
to scrape money together from everywhere. A great inclination toward the
superstition of his ancestral Greeks also made him abominate Jewish rites, and
he observed that most of the Jews were more favorably disposed toward the
Ptolemies, their old masters, than toward him. Finally, divine anger, boiling
up against the Jews’ sins, now ripe, was bringing forth vengeance. All these
and other causes brought on the Jews’ disaster.

When Antiochus approached Jerusalem with an enormous army, according to
Josephus he was admitted amicably; according to the author of Maccabees, he
entered the city after taking it by force and carried out a great slaughter.
As Josephus claims, he killed ten

thousand and sold as many under the spear. But according to the Greek
interpreter or author of Maccabees, Antiochus then killed forty thousand
inhabitants of Jerusalem and condemned as many to slavery. Indeed, according
to the Latin translator, as Salianus maintains, he destroyed two hundred
thousand Jews at that time, whether by the sword or by slavery. Led by
Menelaus, he entered the temple and even the Holy of Holies, and plundered
treasures and gold and silver vessels to the value of eighteen hundred
talents. Departing for Antioch with this enormous booty, he placed Apollonius
over Judaea. Two years later, by Antiochus’s command, Apollonius entered
Jerusalem with an army in peaceful array. On the Sabbath he indiscriminately
slaughtered many thousands of those he encountered, sold most of the rest,
burned the city, razed its walls to their foundations, and constructed a
citadel on Zion, where he placed a Greek garrison who attacked people
approaching the temple. To make the Jews shrink still more from the temple, he
placed a statue of Olympian Jupiter above the altar and sacrificed pigs and
unclean animals to it. Every copy of the Mosaic law that was found was
committed to the flames. All the Jews were commanded, under penalty of death,
to make a complete defection from Moses and pass over to the idolatry of the
Greeks. Very many obeyed the royal commands; the rest fled from the cities to
the mountains and hid among wild animals in caves. Meanwhile Epiphanes
indulged himself at Antioch. He sent enormous gifts to many Greek cities both
on this and the other side of the sea to be placed in the shrines of the gods,
established the most sumptuous banquets, and staged public games at insane
expense. He displayed himself publicly to everyone as such a contemptible
buffoon—as Athenaeus recounts at length—that to many he seemed to be raving
and *epimanēs*. Thus, with a slight change to his epithet, Polybius calls him
Epimanes, that is, “the madman.”

> Marginalia, printed p. 226: *The Jews’ lamentable affliction*.

Among those who fled from Jerusalem to the mountains was the priest Mattathias,
a descendant of Hasmonaeus from the course of Joarib—which was the first of
the twenty-four courses—with his five sons. For some time Mattathias and his
family remained in nothing but mourning and darkness. But when Antiochus’s
boundless fury increased, the worst evils were inflicted upon the Jews in
every city throughout Asia, and at Antioch in the king’s very presence the
scribe Eleazar and a widow with her seven sons were tortured to death in cruel
martyrdom solely for their steadfastness in true piety. Then, in the city of
Modin, where Mattathias lived, altars were erected to idols and willing Jews
were led, and the unwilling driven, to sacrifice. Only then did divine zeal
seize Mattathias. In the marketplace at the altar he killed a Jew who was
sacrificing to an idol and at the same time the royal governor who was forcing
the people by torture to perform the abominable sacrifices. After making this
beginning, he publicly took up arms with his sons and many followers and, with
the blessing of God favoring him, held the persecutors in check for almost a
full year while killing many of them. As the old man was dying, he urged his
sons vehemently to persevere bravely in the protection of the people which he
himself had undertaken.

> Marginalia, printed p. 226: *Mattathias takes up arms*.

After Mattathias the army appointed his son Judas as its leader. He was given
the surname Maccabaeus because on his military standards and in his speeches
he frequently used the words of Exodus 15:11, “Who is like you among the gods,
Jehovah?” מִי כָמֹכָה בָּאֵלִם יְהוָה? From the initial letters of these four words,
*mem*, *caph*, *beth*, and *iod*, comes the name Macabi. This Judas Maccabaeus
was an exceedingly brave and successful hero, adorned by the Spirit of God
with all the gifts which the deliverance of a most wretched people from the
greatest evils required. Often with a small band he routed Antiochus’s great
armies. He killed in battle various governors of Judaea and indeed of all
Syria, recovered almost all the cities of Judaea, cleansed the Jerusalem
temple approximately three years after its profanation, and, when he could not
wrest the citadel of Zion from the Greeks, surrounded the courts of the temple
with a wall and strong fortifications. He inflicted devastating losses upon
the Idumaeans, Ammonites, Philistines, Arabs, and every neighboring enemy. He
repelled Lysias, the governor of all Asia on this side of the Euphrates, when
he invaded Judaea with sixty thousand infantry and five thousand cavalry.

> Marginalia, printed p. 226: *The victories of Judas Maccabaeus*.

Antiochus set out for Persia to replenish his treasures, which he had exhausted
on the most frivolous spectacles and the Jewish war. Undeterred by his
father’s death, he went to plunder the temple of Diana at Elymais, or
Persepolis, where Alexander was said to have deposited a great quantity of
gold. When he entered the temple, the priests killed all his companions and
almost buried him beneath stones. Returning empty-handed from there to
Ecbatana, he heard of Maccabaeus’s victories and, in a rage, set out for
Babylon, breathing the cruelest threats against the Jews. Meanwhile, the most
intense pain seized his bowels. Nevertheless, he continued on the journey, and
while he hastened too much he fell from his chariot. All his limbs were so
badly battered by this accident that he recognized the hand of God upon him,
avenging the injuries done to the Jews. At this point he promised many gifts
and vowed that he would become a Jew if only he recovered his health. But the
Lord refused to hear the utterly impious man. Therefore, in the greatest pain,
offending everyone who came near and even himself with the most hideous stench
of his ulcer, he died of the disease of lice on the borders of Persia and
Media.
