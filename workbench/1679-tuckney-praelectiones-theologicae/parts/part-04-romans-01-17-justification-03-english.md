First, as concerns redemption: since redemption in general is the liberation
of a person or thing that has been alienated, occupied, or captured—whether
by a price, by some compensation, by force, or by any other means—Christ’s
redemption, or the redemption accomplished in Christ or through Christ (as
it is called ἀπολύτρωσις ἡ ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ, “the redemption which is
in Christ Jesus,” in Romans 3:24), comprehends generally everything by which the
elect, formerly captives in themselves, are freed from the power of Satan,
sin, death, and, in short, all their enemies, Luke 1:74. They are therefore
frequently called “the redeemed of the Lord” in Holy Scripture. Theologians
ordinarily distinguish this redemption into a first and a second. They call
the first the redemption of merit and the second the redemption of efficacy.
Because this second redemption exercises its efficacy most fully on the last
day, which is followed by eternal glory in heaven—where the elect, death the
last enemy having finally been abolished, 1 Corinthians 15:56, will be freed
forever from absolutely all the tyranny of all their enemies—this final and
most perfect liberation is therefore emphatically and preeminently (κατ᾽
ἐξοχήν) called *redemption*. So Ephesians 4:30 says, “by whom you were
sealed unto the day of redemption”; and Luke 21:28, “Look up and lift up your
heads, because your redemption draws near.” Thus Romans 8:23 speaks of “the
redemption of our bodies,” and in 1 Corinthians 1:30 Christ is said to have
been made by God for us “wisdom, righteousness, sanctification,” and—what
closes the column and fully consummates everything—“redemption.” Although
this is displayed most fully in that final and most consummate liberation,
nevertheless, together with that eternal salvation, it contains and
comprehends in itself all the benefits that precede it—reconciliation,
justification, adoption, and sanctification—either as its parts or as its
effects.

Thus, as concerns reconciliation and justification, which are liberation
and redemption from the guilt and punishment of sin, Romans 3:24–25 says
that we are “justified freely through the redemption which is in Christ
Jesus, whom God set forth as an ἱλαστήριον”—a propitiator, reconciler,
or reconciliation—“through faith in his blood.”

As concerns adoption, which is redemption from our enmity and alienation
from God, the apostle says in Galatians 4:5 that God sent his Son so that he
might redeem those

[Marginalia: Redemption; Isaiah 35:9; 51:11; 62:12; 63:4.]

[1679 printed p. 52 | IA leaf 60]

who were under the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons.

As concerns sanctification, which is redemption from the pollution and
dominion of sin, Psalm 130:8 says, “He himself will redeem Israel from all his
iniquities.” Titus 2:14 sings in harmony with it: “He gave himself for us that
he might redeem us from all iniquity and purify for himself a peculiar
people, zealous for good works.”

Finally, as concerns eternal salvation and glorification, which is the full
and final redemption from all the remnants of sin, the temptations of Satan
and the world, the miseries of this life, and finally death: in Hebrews 9:15
Christ is called the “Mediator of the New Covenant, so that, by means of death
for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the former
covenant, those who are called might receive the promised eternal
inheritance.”

From these passages it is clear that although *redemption* is used
preeminently of that final and most perfect liberation after the
resurrection, when taken more generically it contains within its compass
reconciliation, justification, adoption, sanctification, and glorification,
as though they were its parts or effects.

In glorification, redemption is from death and from all the remnants both of
afflictions and of sins.

In sanctification, it is from the dominion of sins.

In reconciliation, we are redeemed from the mutual alienation and enmity
between God and us.

In justification, finally, we are redeemed from the guilt of sins.

This, then, is first the agreement between justification and redemption.
There is also this difference.

But our theologians do not agree so well among themselves about how
justification and reconciliation agree with, or differ from, one another.
Indeed, our Wotton dared to say that none of our writers or of the Roman
Catholics had written distinctly about reconciliation or how it differs from
justification. Perhaps fewer writers did so before that time. By now, at
least, quite a number have left us their opinions in
writing about the difference between justification and reconciliation, and
these opinions are very diverse.

1. Some wish reconciliation to be the cause of justification, so that,
because God was first reconciled to the sinner, he consequently justifies
him. So Maccovius and Crellius.

2. Others prefer reconciliation to be the effect of justification, so that
only after sins have first been remitted in justification is God then
reconciled to the sinner and returns into favor with him. So it seemed to
the Leiden theologians in their *Synopsis of Purer Theology*, and also to our
Forbes and, with him, Essenius.

3. Third, Wotton himself makes reconciliation the whole, whose parts he
holds to be justification and adoption. By the first—namely,
justification—we are reconciled to God insofar as by it we are absolved from
the guilt and punishment of sins. By adoption, however, we are reconciled
insofar as by it we are restored to God’s favor and accepted so as to obtain
eternal life by inheritance. He enlists Calvin and Perkins as supporters of
this opinion.

4. Fourth and finally, according to our Ames, reconciliation and
justification are the same thing, which receives different names under
different considerations: insofar as the state of sin is a state of guilt,
our liberation from it is called *justification*; but insofar as it is a
state of enmity, the same liberation from it is called *reconciliation*.

All these opinions are indeed godly. Although they differ somewhat from one
another, they contain nothing contrary either to sound doctrine or to good
morals. Accordingly, since we are now treating reconciliation, it seems
fitting to think also about reconciling and welding them together. This
appears capable of being done quite suitably in the following way, without
doing any injury to the truth.

The first two opinions—one of which holds reconciliation to be the cause of
justification, and the other its effect—appear entirely contrary. Yet
contraries must be taken not only of the same thing but also in relation to
the same thing and under the same consideration. If we consider
reconciliation under one consideration and another, these opinions which
seem to fight each other can agree amicably. For reconciliation can be
considered either with respect to its effect.

1. The effects of reconciliation, both among men and between God and men,
are association, Amos 3:3, peace, joy, and things like these. But these
follow justification and are its effects: “Having been justified by faith,
we have peace,” Romans 5:1; τὴν προσαγωγήν, “access,” verse 2; and
“unspeakable and glorious joy,” 1 Peter 1:8. In this sense, justification is
the cause of reconciliation of this kind when it has been brought to its
height (ἀκμήν) and attested by effects and signs of this kind.

But if we regard reconciliation with respect to its affection, it must not
be denied that there is some such affection in God—we ordinarily stammer in
this way when we attribute affections to God—which precedes justification
and which we may call its cause. Yet even concerning this affection a
distinction must be made.

2. For this affection of reconciliation may be understood either of the
love of benevolence or of the love of friendship. With respect to the love
of benevolence, God is reconciled to us thus far before we are actually
justified. For this flows from eternal election. Hence, immediately after we
had fallen and become enemies of God, from that love of benevolence and
desire to return into favor with us, he set enmity between the seed of the
woman and the seed of the serpent and promised that blessed seed to restore
the former friendship between himself and us, Genesis 3:15. He also sent him
in the fullness of time to merit this very thing, Galatians 4:4–5. Hence we
read that peace was made through his blood, Colossians 1:20, and that we were
reconciled by his death, Romans 5:10. From that love of benevolence God also
proceeds further and draws nearer to us when עֵת רָצוֹן, καιρὸς δ᾽ ἀπόδεκτος,
“the acceptable time,” approaches—as Stephen speaks of the time of the
promise in Acts 7:19—when, I say, he is now about to send us the Spirit,
convert us, and actually justify us, and is, as it were, standing ready for
action, like the merciful father prepared to go out to meet the prodigal son
already contemplating his return. All these things openly indicate the love
of benevolence and the affection of reconciliation which precede
justification and are its cause. For because he is thus disposed and
affected toward us, he grants us the remission of sins, in which
justification consists. Nevertheless, in all these things, if we regard the
full result (ἀποτέλεσμα) of reconciliation, God can be called *reconcilable*
rather than fully and properly

[Marginalia: Ames, *Marrow*, book 2, chapter 27, sections 20 and 22; 2
Corinthians 6:2; *Gneth Ratson*.]

[1679 printed p. 54 | IA leaf 62]

*reconciled*. I say *reconcilable*, but not in the Arminian sense. They say
that through Christ’s death God is only reconcilable to the elect, no more
than to all others, under a condition to be freely fulfilled by them which,
notwithstanding either God’s election or Christ’s death, can be fulfilled by
no one. Rather, he is reconcilable with respect to what he himself will
certainly accomplish by his grace in our effectual calling and
justification—that is, reconciliation.

For full and perfect reconciliation includes in itself not only that love
of benevolence but also the love of friendship, when there is an actual and
complete return into favor and all the benefits and duties of mutual
friendship are possessed and performed. But these are effects of
justification, as we showed before. It is therefore clear that reconciliation
taken under one consideration and sense is the cause of justification,
according to the first opinion of some theologians, while under another it
is the effect of justification, according to the opinion of others.

But what must be decided about the third opinion, which wished justification
and adoption to be parts of reconciliation? The author of this opinion
understands integral parts, which for that reason he calls members, as though
reconciliation as a whole were constituted from justification and adoption.
He illustrates this with two examples. The first concerns a servant accused
of badly administering an affair, expelled for this reason from his master’s
household, and thrust into prison. He can be said to be fully reconciled to
his master only when not only is his fault forgiven, but he is also once
again in the same place and standing with his master as he had been before.
The other example concerns Absalom, who fell from the grace and favor of his
father David because he killed his brother Amnon. Two parts or degrees of
his reconciliation with David are set down in the permission granted him to
return: first, to his own house without fear of the punishment due him for
the murder, where the remission of fault corresponds to justification; and,
second, to the presence, and thus the favor, of his father, which he had
previously enjoyed as a son and to which adoption corresponds. Likewise,
when in 1 Corinthians 7:11 the apostle gives the command concerning a wife
who has left her husband, τῷ ἀνδρὶ καταλλαγήτω, “let her be reconciled to
her husband,” he wishes not only that she lay aside hostility but also that
she live in the closest union with him. I do not wish to accept each of
these points without examination (ἄνευ ἐξετάσεως), since some may perhaps
be subject to exception. As far as the matter we are now treating is
concerned, I say:

1. I grant that two things are required for perfect reconciliation: the
pardon of fault and restoration to the former state of love and favor, as
the examples adduced sufficiently prove.

2. I acknowledge also that something like this is found in justification as
well as in adoption. Although the pardon of fault is properly found only in
justification, Romans 4:7, nevertheless in each there is a renewal of love
and a restoration of sinners to the state from which they had fallen,
insofar as by the one guilty men are once again made righteous, and by the
other enemies are made friends and sons. In this sense, then, they may, so
far as I am concerned, be regarded as parts or members which constitute
reconciliation. Forbes nevertheless says that this is an entryway into
various errors.

3. I add just this warning: if anything beyond pardon and this restoration
is added, it also adds something to reconciliation. If a runaway or
unfaithful servant enjoys not only pardon of his fault and the renewal of
his master’s former favor—which fulfill the whole nature of
reconciliation—but in addition is newly granted some immunity or reward
which he did not have before, this is more than reconciliation

[Marginalia: Wotton, chapter 9, number [illegible]; 2 Samuel 14:24–25;
Forbes, *On Justification*, chapter 9, p. 26.]

[1679 printed p. 55 | IA leaf 63]

itself. Accordingly, when Wotton defines reconciliation as “the act of God
the Father by which he returns into favor with sinners who believe in Christ
in order to impart eternal life to them,” and Perkins, whom he cites,
establishes two parts of reconciliation—the remission of sins, which occurs
in justification, and acceptance unto eternal life, which is included in
adoption—I would not deny that in the economy of the covenant of grace this
eternal life in heaven is a consequence of reconciliation. Yet I would not
affirm that it is contained in the formal nature of reconciliation. For
reconciliation, when taken strictly, is only restoration to former favor and
grace. But even in the state of innocence, before fulfilling the condition
of the covenant made with him, Adam had no right to this eternal life. From
bare reconciliation, therefore, neither he nor any other believer obtains
this right, but from adoption or something else added in the covenant of
grace. Thus he does not obtain it even from adoption itself insofar as
adoption is merely a part of reconciliation and shares in part in its common
nature, but from its further difference. Whether this can also be said of
justification is a more difficult question. For some wish justification to
consist in the imputation of Christ’s passive obedience alone and in the
remission of sins alone. If that were true, justification would agree
perfectly with reconciliation in this respect: for if reconciliation denotes
only restoration to the former state, the remission of sins alone adds
nothing to that restoration. But some add a second part to justification:
namely, the right to eternal life conferred from the imputation of Christ’s
active obedience. In this respect justification would indeed correspond to
adoption, but it would exceed the limits of reconciliation taken by itself,
because reconciliation is merely restoration to the former state. There
will, however, be a more suitable place to speak of this matter when we come
to the question whether justification is by the imputation of Christ’s
passive obedience alone or also by the imputation of his active obedience.

There remains Ames’s fourth opinion, which says that justification and
reconciliation signify the same thing, though under different
considerations: insofar as this liberation is from the state of guilt, it is
called *justification*; insofar as it is from the state of enmity, it is
called *reconciliation*. Justification and reconciliation have the same
preceding cause (προηγουμένη), the good pleasure (εὐδοκία) of God the
Father; the same antecedent cause (προκαταρκτική), the merit of the Son;
the same instrumental cause, faith; and the same final cause, the glory of
God and the salvation of the elect. They have the same essence, at least
generically—liberation from the state of sin and wrath—and also the same
integral parts. According to Perkins these are the remission of sins and
acceptance unto eternal life, which very things, as very many maintain, are
the constitutive parts of justification. Since all these things are the same
in both, what therefore prevents the things themselves from also being the
same? The apostle also appears to support this in 2 Corinthians 5:19, where
he describes reconciliation by something proper to justification: ὁ Θεὸς ἦν
ἐν Χριστῷ κόσμον καταλλάσσων ἑαυτῷ, μὴ λογιζόμενος αὐτοῖς τὰ
παραπτώματα αὐτῶν, “God was in Christ reconciling the world to
himself, not imputing their offenses to them.” If you ask how this is done,
he answers: by not reckoning, or not imputing, their offenses to them—as
though the whole of reconciliation consisted in the non-imputation of sins,
which is most properly the benefit of justification. I indeed acknowledge
that the words μὴ λογιζόμενος, “not imputing sins,” as far as the
phraseology is concerned,

can denote the cause, so that because God did not impute sins to them,

[Marginalia: Wotton, *On Reconciliation*, part 1, book 1, chapter 1, number
2; Perkins, *On the Right Knowledge of Christ Crucified*.]

[1679 printed p. 56 | IA leaf 64]

his reconciliation with them follows as the effect.

Or they can designate the effect, as though because God was reconcilable to
them and returned into favor with them, he consequently and by way of effect
remitted their sins.

According to this fourth opinion, however, the words can also express the
formal act of reconciliation, as though *to be reconciled* and *not to
impute sins*, which is properly to justify, were entirely the same thing in
substance, although they could be considered in different ways insofar as
they concern things which are distinct in themselves but most closely joined
in their subject, namely the sinner—I mean guilt and enmity. Thus, although
justification and reconciliation would be the same in substance and
simultaneous in time, yet under this different mode of consideration,
inasmuch as guilt precedes enmity as its cause, so justification, which
removes guilt, would be prior in the order of nature; and reconciliation,
which properly removes the enmity that arises from guilt, would follow as
its effect. But we showed earlier how they can both precede and follow in
different senses, when we spoke of reconciling the first two opinions about
this matter. There is therefore no reason to linger on this any longer.

Rather, to add this as a supplement, there is reason for us reverently to
look up to the manifold wisdom (πολυποίκιλον σοφίαν) of God and his grace
which surpasses every understanding (χάριν πάντα νοῦν ὑπερέχουσαν),
since we certainly possess in union things which we cannot explain so
distinctly. Thus an infant heir owns coins which he cannot count, and estates
whose boundaries and limits he cannot describe. Such are we while we are
here: we are little children. So great and unsearchable, by contrast, is
God’s goodness and munificence toward us in Christ that we can possess and
experience his gifts, but cannot understand them fully and distinctly, much
less speak clearly about them, according to that statement of the apostle
and prophet in 1 Corinthians 2:9: “What eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor
have entered into the heart of man, God has prepared for those who love him.”

Let this at last suffice concerning justification compared with
reconciliation.

Third, let justification be compared with adoption. First, a great closeness
and kinship exists between these two.

1. Each is σχετική, or relative, and posits nothing absolute and inherent
in its subject, as regeneration and sanctification do. Hence, when Holy
Scripture treats these matters, it uses the phrase that we are “sons of God
in Christ,” Galatians 3:26, and also “the righteousness of God in Christ,” 2
Corinthians 5:21. Roman Catholics say the contrary, since for them formal
justification and adoption consist entirely in grace implanted in and
inherent in us. We are indeed sons of God by a twofold title—and for that
reason one which is more illustrious and secure—namely, by adoption, whence
we are said in Galatians 4:5 to receive the adoption of sons, and by
regeneration, whence we are called sons of God in John 1:12, who in verse 13
were born of God. But we are called regenerate insofar as we are made
inherent participants in the divine nature, 2 Peter 1:4, and, the heavenly
Father’s image and holiness having been impressed on us, are made like him,
1 Peter 1:14–15. Adoption, however, since it is merely relative and consists
in reputation alone, changes our status but not our nature intrinsically,
just as occurs with adopted children among men. Yet this must be noted here:
human adoption is only an external denomination and communication of
external things; but divine adoption, although it too is only formal in
itself, is nevertheless a real relation founded upon an intrinsic action,
namely regeneration and the communication of new internal life.

2. They agree in very many other respects as well. For if Wotton’s former
opinion holds—that justification and adoption are species of
reconciliation—it is necessary, as ordinarily occurs in all species, that
everything pertaining to their generic nature be common and identical in
both.

They have the same efficient cause: God the Father in Christ, Ephesians 1:5,
through the Spirit, who is therefore called the Spirit of adoption, Romans
8:15. They have the same preceding cause (προηγουμένη), the free love of
God, 1 John 3:1, and the same antecedent cause (προκαταρκτική), Christ’s
merit, Hebrews 2:14 and Galatians 4:4–5. They also have the same instrumental
cause, namely faith, through which we are both justified, Romans 5:1, and
receive the right of sons, John 1:12.

They also have the same matter and the same subject—namely, the same
persons. With respect to God they are the elect and those predestined to
life: these alone are justified, Romans 8:30, and adopted as sons, Ephesians
1:5. In themselves they are guilty and ungodly, Romans 4:5, and enemies,
Colossians 1:21.

Each also has the same variety of degrees.

First, justification can be considered in the divine decree, and so can
adoption: “He predestined us to adoption as sons,” Ephesians 1:5. Second,
justification is regarded in Christ with respect to its procurement. The
same is said of adoption: “He sent his Son so that we might receive
adoption,” Galatians 4:4–5. Third, justification is considered most formally
in us, where it has now been accomplished, and so is adoption: “Because you
are sons, God sent the Spirit of his Son into your hearts,” and so forth,
Galatians 4:6. Finally, the greatest manifestation of our justification
before all will occur on the last day, as will that of adoption, which is
therefore called our final (ἐσχάτη) adoption: “Awaiting our adoption, the
redemption of our body,” Romans 8:23. There are also those who assign the
same fruit to each, namely a right to eternal life. This is evident
concerning adoption: “If sons, then heirs,” Romans 8:17. The greatest
theologians also attribute this right to that other part of justification,
which consists in the imputation of Christ’s active obedience; others,
however, oppose them. More must be said about this later, when, God willing,
we treat this controversy. In these and perhaps other similar respects,
therefore, justification and adoption agree.

But in the following respects they are distinguished from one another.

1. Justification concerns righteousness; adoption, friendship. Guilt is
removed by justification, enmity by adoption. By the former, though
previously guilty, we are constituted right in court in the divine forum,
Romans 4:7. By the latter we are made friends of God, John 15:15; sons and
heirs of God; and brothers and coheirs of Christ, Romans 8:14–17 and Hebrews
2:10–11. It is as if one and the same man were both judge and father and had
a son who was guilty of an offense, on which account he was subject to him
as judge and to his sentence of condemnation; but he was also displeased
with him as father and had entirely disowned him. Once a surety has rendered
satisfaction to the law and paid the price, he not only as judge absolves
him from guilt and punishment—to which justification corresponds—but as
father returns with him to their former favor, not only acknowledges him
again as a son, but appoints him heir to an inheritance far ampler than that
to which he had formerly been born. Adoption provides this, and therefore

2. A second difference arises between them: justification says something
less, while adoption adds a more sublime degree of dignity and excellence.
It is indeed a great thing if a king grants pardon to a rebel and absolves
him from guilt and punishment; but it is far greater if he further adopts
him as a son and heir of the kingdom. The same occurs here for us: “that he
might redeem those who were subject to the law, that we might receive
adoption.” Thus the apostle speaks in Galatians 4:5. Not only the Jews were
under the law, as concerns that shadowy pedagogy, but absolutely all men were
also under its curse as those guilty of sin. Our redemption and liberation
from it were an immense benefit. Christ provided this for us by his death as
regards its merit, but by our justification as regards its efficacy. Yet he
provided this great benefit so that he might provide one greater still: by
dying for us and justifying us he redeemed and freed us from being under the
law—if it is permissible to speak in this way—so that we might receive
adoption and, through him, a sonship far more illustrious than we originally
had in Adam.

3. Hence, among most theologians a third difference between justification
and adoption also arises, concerning their priority and posteriority to one
another in the order of nature. Although by everyone’s agreement they are
simultaneous with respect to time, most hold that justification is prior to
adoption with respect to the order of nature. Wotton asserts this clearly in
*On Reconciliation*, part 1, book 1, chapter 9, number 6. Spanheim places
adoption among the effects of justification in *Theological Disputations*,
part 1, disputation 1 *On Justification*, number 21, and disputation 2,
number 14. According to Ames in *Marrow*, book 1, chapter 28,
“Justification has the character of a foundation, because through faith and
the righteousness of faith the right of adoption is acquired, John 1:12.
For although adoption follows from faith, it does not follow so immediately
that justification does not intervene. Adoption presupposes and requires
beforehand that reconciliation which is found in justification. God first
grants pardon and absolves, and then at last regards the man as a friend and
son. In this way human adoption differs from divine adoption: human adoption
is of a person who has no right, but divine adoption is of persons who,
although they have no right in themselves, nevertheless have this right
adjudicated to them by virtue of regeneration and justification.” Thus far
Ames. Arminius appears to approach this opinion. He first places
justification among the fruits of communion in Christ’s death, but enumerates
adoption among the benefits arising from communion in Christ’s life in
*Private Disputations*, theses 46 and 47, and says that it is joined to
justification in thesis 49, number 8. The Church of England, too, in Article
17, where it sets the effects of predestination in order, places
justification before adoption.

But Calvin, in *Institutes*, book 2, chapter 12, when he says that our
reconciliation with God has two heads, puts first that we are restored to
God’s favor so that from sons of men we become his sons—which speaks of
adoption. Then he adds the other, that those who had destroyed themselves by
their disobedience might set Christ’s obedience in its place as a remedy,
which occurs in justification. From these words—which, however, I do not
find word for word (κατὰ λέξιν) in Calvin at the passage cited—Wotton
inferred that Calvin held this opinion and maintained that adoption is prior
to justification by nature. Forbes, however, openly asserts and defends this
with many arguments in his most learned treatise *On Justification*.

For he maintains that adoption is the immediate fruit of faith, by which we
are first

united to Christ, justification being mediated by this union, chapter 14. He
also wishes adoption to be the primary thing to which we are properly
predestined. He proves this from Ephesians 1:5, προορίσας ἡμᾶς εἰς
υἱοθεσίαν διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, “he predestined us to adoption through
Jesus Christ.” Thus the decree of adoption precedes the decree of all other
benefits conferred upon us through Christ—indeed, Christ’s own foreordination
as our mediator, chapter 25. He holds adoption to be the foundation of all
the remaining benefits, chapter 8, and that in which Christ is first
manifested to us, chapters 2 and 3. He wishes adoption to be the same as
effectual calling, chapter 13; sometimes says that it is accomplished in
calling, in which we are first led to the Son, chapter 10; and sometimes says
that calling is either the same thing as adoption, or at least its first
part or beginning, chapter 15. If this is true, the matter will plainly be
settled. For almost everyone acknowledges that in calling, which Junius
calls regeneration, the merits of Christ are first applied to us, and many
assert that calling is prior to all the other benefits conferred upon us,
and hence also to justification, which is among them.

It therefore remains for us to weigh what has been said on both sides so
that it may become clearer whether priority by nature should be assigned to
adoption or to justification.

1. First, as concerns what Forbes lays as the foundation of his opinion:
namely, that adoption is the same thing as effectual calling, or is
accomplished in that calling, or that calling is adoption’s first part or
beginning.

1. The latter two assertions appear hardly consistent with one another. For
if adoption is accomplished through calling, then it would either be
entirely the same as calling or would be comprehended in it as a part in a
whole. This is altogether contrary to what he says last: namely, that calling
is only the first part and beginning of adoption, as if adoption contained
more within itself and extended further—which is in fact his opinion.

2. His first statement, that adoption is entirely the same thing as calling,
does not appear to be said so truly. For adoption, even by his own admission,
is entirely σχετική, or relative. But effectual calling—at least when
taken passively, insofar as through faith we answer and approach God who
calls—denotes something inherent in us, something certainly real in us,
done and produced by us.

But it may be objected that in the apostle’s golden chain in Romans 8:30,
“those whom he predestined he also called; those whom he called he justified;
and those whom he justified he glorified,” adoption, which must by no means
be excluded, is included in calling and is therefore the same as calling and
precedes justification.

The answer is easy and twofold.

1. In summaries and contracted enumerations of things of this kind, it does
not follow from the fact that one thing is included and understood under the
name of another that it is identical with the other. This is plainly clear
even from that very passage. Since sanctification must by no means be broken
off from that chain, it must be understood either under calling, as its
beginning, as some wish, or under glorification, as others wish, which is its
perfection and result (ἀποτέλεσμα). Yet it is sufficiently distinct from
each, since it is entirely identical with neither.

But you will say: although adoption is not altogether the same as calling,
if it is nevertheless understood under its

[Marginalia: Forbes, chapter 19; Objection; Answer.]

[1679 printed p. 60 | IA leaf 68]

name, it will at least be prior to justification. For those whom he called,
as from what is prior, and adopted, as what is included in calling, he
justified, as the consequence. Therefore it is prior.

2. Let this be the answer. If adoption must necessarily be understood under
the terminology of one of these links, nothing compels it to be placed under
the name of *calling*. There is at least something to suggest that it be
placed rather under the name of *justification*. Although adoption is a
younger sister, it is justification’s twin and is joined to it indivisibly,
since each is a relation (σχέσις): as by justification we are accounted
righteous, so by adoption we are accounted sons and heirs, through which we
obtain a right to the heavenly inheritance. We owe this to adoption, Romans
8:17, though the most learned theologians credit it to that part of
justification by which Christ’s active obedience is imputed to us. More on
that later.

Thus, without doing the least injury to either the truth or the apostle’s
intention, you may interpret the words in this way: “Those whom he called, he
justified”—that is, he regarded them as both righteous and sons, first
abolishing guilt and then enmity. The former belongs to justification taken
strictly and formally; the latter, to adoption immediately connected with
it.

2. Second, when the learned man says that adoption is the immediate fruit of
faith, by which we are first united to Christ, but that justification is a
mediate fruit of faith, namely through the mediation of this union, I answer:

1. Union with Christ is the immediate fruit of faith, since by faith alone
we first receive Christ, John 1:12, and thus are united with him, Ephesians
3:17.

2. This union with Christ through faith first occurs in effectual calling,
by which we draw near to Christ, 1 Peter 2:4, and gratefully receive him when
he is freely offered to us in the Gospel.

3. I grant that justification is the fruit of faith through the mediation
of this union which is made in calling, because those whom he first called,
he then justified, Romans 8:30.

4. But I think it must by no means be granted that adoption is the immediate
fruit of faith in such a way that by it we are first united to Christ and
are justified through the mediation of adoption. Rather, I judge that the
contrary should be maintained with Ames: adoption indeed follows from faith,
but not so immediately that justification does not intervene. This will
become clearer in the fuller exposition of this opinion.

3. Meanwhile, what he says in the third place—that adoption is the primary
thing to which we are predestined—is hardly proved invincibly from Ephesians
1:5, because there the apostle says only that God predestined us to adoption.
By the same reasoning someone could prove against him, with greater truth,
that we are primarily predestined to salvation, because believers are said
to have been appointed to eternal life in Acts 13:48. These passages say that
we are predestined to adoption and eternal life, but say nothing about the
order either of the decree or of its execution. It therefore does not follow
from this manner of speaking that adoption is the first objective effect of
the decree. That description belongs rather to salvation or eternal life,
which God decreed in the first logical moment of election. Indeed, the
contrary of his opinion follows from this: for if, as he wishes, adoption is
prior to the other things in the decree, it will be later in execution, just

[1679 printed p. 61 | IA leaf 69]

as eternal salvation, which was first in the intention of the decree, is
last in its execution. The learned man also wishes the decree of adoption to
precede the decree concerning Christ the mediator. But in the execution of
the decree Christ is given to us before adoption.

4. His fourth assertion in support of the priority of adoption—namely, that
the decree concerning adoption precedes not only the decree concerning all
the other benefits conferred upon us through Christ but also the very
foreordination of Christ as our mediator—appears even further removed from
the truth. We readily acknowledge that election to eternal life is prior to
the decree concerning Christ the mediator, since the former concerns the
end and the latter the means to the end. Although the apostle says in
Ephesians 1:4 that we are chosen in Christ, he does not mean that Christ is
the foundation or even the antecedent cause (προκαταρκτική) of election. He
refers it wholly to God’s good pleasure (εὐδοκίαν αὐτοῦ) in verse 5,
“which the Father purposed in himself” (ἣν προέθετο ἐν αὑτῷ), verse
9, although “he favored us in him” (ἐχαρίτωσεν ἡμᾶς ἐν αὐτῷ), in
Christ the Son, verse 5. But if we regard the decree concerning the means to
this end, which is predestination, Christ rightly holds the first place.
Here he is first appointed head of the new covenant, so that through him we
might return into favor, Isaiah 42:6. Here he is designated the expiatory
victim by whom we are absolved from guilt in justification, Ephesians 5:2.
Here he is acknowledged as the beloved Son, in whom—as regards adoption—we
also become sons and heirs of the glory to which we had previously been
chosen, Ephesians 1:5. “A Son is given to us,” Isaiah 9:6. Christ is made
everything for us—wisdom, righteousness, sanctification, and
redemption—that in him we may possess everything, 1 Corinthians 1:30 and
Colossians 3:11.

But leaving aside what he proposed to explain his opinion, let us weigh the
arguments he adduces to confirm it. There are several.

First, he tries to prove that adoption is prior in order to justification
from the order of the three persons in the Godhead and of their respective
operations. The argument is framed in this way:

Those operations of grace which are attributed principally and more
properly to the first person are prior in order to those which belong to the
second or third person. For the order in operation corresponds to the order
in existence.

But adoption is an operation of grace attributed principally and more
properly to the first person. For the Father is he who properly adopts as
sons, since father and son are correlatives. Justification, however—since it
occurs through redemption, Romans 3:24—belongs to the Redeemer, that is, the
second person; just as sanctification and glorification, which includes
sanctification in itself, belong to the Holy Spirit, namely the third
person. The apostle observes this order in Romans 8:30: “Those whom he
predestined he called,” namely the Father, and at the same time adopted;
“those whom he called he justified,” namely the Son; “those whom he justified
he glorified” and sanctified, namely the Holy Spirit.

Therefore, just as the Father is prior in order to the Son, so adoption,
which is the Father’s work, is prior to justification, which is the Son’s
work.

I answer:

1. As concerns the major premise, I freely acknowledge that it is commonly
received among theologians: namely, that the order of the operations,
especially the external ones, follows the order of the persons themselves,
so that the operation of the first person is prior in order to that of the
second. Thus election and creation, which are the first of all operations,
are

[Marginalia: Forbes, chapter 8; Argument 1, chapter 10; 1 Peter 1:3–4;
Galatians 4:4–5; Answer.]

[1679 printed p. 62 | IA leaf 70]

attributed to the first person; redemption and justification to the second;
and sanctification to the third. Yet since the external operations are
undivided and common to all, I judge that one should not insist so sharply
on that postulate that this or any other matter sufficiently disputed in
itself is to be decided from it.

This is especially so when we come to the execution of divine election and
the restoration of fallen man. In this the second person, Christ the
mediator, holds first place. In this class of operations, after his mission
by the Father to undertake this office, redemption itself is first of all.
Yet redemption is in its own manner proper to the Redeemer—that is, to the
second person—and precedes adoption, which Forbes contends is first and
proper to the first person. Likewise, effectual calling, which is nothing
other than begun sanctification and thus in its own manner proper to the
third person, is not on that account to be numbered among the last
operations. In fact, it is first, even by Forbes’s admission, and precedes
both adoption, which he wishes to appropriate to the first person, and
justification, which he attributes to the second. All the holy persons
mutually participate in all these connected works. Each occurs from the
Father, in the Son, through the Spirit—from the Father as origin, through the
Son as meritorious cause, and through the Spirit as operative cause: namely,
we are redeemed, justified, adopted, sanctified, and receive the remaining
benefits which follow. Thus from these operations we can discern the order
of the persons who operate more than the order of the operations; or rather,
we can discern the order of the persons who operate and also the order of
their operations, but not equally the order of the things produced by these
operations. It is just as ordinarily occurs in things made either by nature
or by art: several causes, although subordinate to one another, nevertheless
produce effects together and at the same time; the subordination of causes
does not also entail a subordination of effects of this kind.

2. But let us come to the minor premise of the argument. He says that
adoption properly belongs to the Father, since father and son are
correlatives. But because justification occurs through redemption, Romans
3:24, it belongs to the Redeemer, who is the Son; and hence justification is
later than adoption, just as the Son is later than the Father. I have several
replies to make.

1. The Father does not adopt us so separately and distinctly that the Son is
excluded. Although by nature the Son is the Father’s Son, by grace he is also
our Father: אב עולם, “the Father of eternity,” Isaiah 9:6. Nor should we
suppose that when God is everywhere in Scripture called our Father and we
are called sons of God, the word *Father* must always be taken
hypostatically and personally. Sometimes it must be taken essentially
(οὐσιωδῶς) and understood of all the persons, 1 John 3:1–2.

It is indeed true that *father* and *son* are correlatives, so that the son
is the father’s son. But now the question is who that father is in relation
to whom we are called sons: is it not *father* taken essentially, as we
believe, or is it only the first person taken hypostatically, which remains
for him to prove? I would not deny that through the Son we are sons of the
Father; yet I would not on that account wish it denied that the Father’s Son
is a Father to us. We are the Father’s sons through the Son as mediator, yet
the Son is a Father to us as in himself God.

All the holy persons operate in this filiation of ours, as the apostle
plainly teaches us in Galatians 4:4–6: “God sent his Son so that we might
receive adoption,” or filiation; “because you are sons, God sent the Spirit of
his Son into your hearts, crying, Abba, Father.” Ἐξαπέστειλεν, “he sent,” and
indeed

[Marginalia: *Ab Gnalam*.]

[1679 printed p. 63 | IA leaf 71]

προώρισεν, “he predestined,” did the Father, Romans 8:29; and Ephesians
1:5 says, προορίσας ἡμᾶς εἰς υἱοθεσίαν διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ, “he predestined
us to adoption through Jesus Christ.” The
Father, I say, predestined and sent; but the Son merited it, and the Holy
Spirit both seals it and makes us assured of it. This must not be understood
as though

the Holy Spirit contributes to our filiation only by sealing. For we are
regenerated from the Spirit as the physical and primarily operative cause,
John 3:5, and thus by his productive power we are made sons of God.

Nor should it be understood as though the Son were only its meritorious
cause and not also, so to speak, its decretive and constitutive cause. For
John 1:12 says concerning him: “But as many as received him,” ὅσοι δὲ ἔλαβον
αὐτόν, ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τέκνα Θεοῦ γενέσθαι, “he gave them power to
become sons of God.”

2. Our second reply is that, even if it is granted that the Father alone
adopts us as sons—which we freely grant that he does under some special
consideration—the priority of adoption over justification will not thereby
be proved, since he justifies just as much as he adopts. The Father adopts,
the Son merits this, and the Spirit seals it. In precisely the same way the
Father justifies, Christ merited it, and the Spirit seals it. Thus the
reasoning is altogether the same in both, and there is no priority or
posteriority in this respect. The Father adopts as the supreme father of the
household; the same Father justifies and pardons sins as supreme judge or
ruler.

For although the Son rendered obedience to justify us, offered an expiatory
sacrifice, and thus merited justification—according to the apostle’s
statement in Romans 4:25, “who was delivered for our justification”—yet, as
far as the economy (οἰκονομίαν) of grace is concerned, the Son is hardly
ever or very rarely said on the sacred page to justify us, as theologians
have noted. He is nevertheless expressly said, as we have already seen, to
give us power to be called sons of God, which is to adopt. For in this
economy (οἰκονομίᾳ), the Son performs the part of mediator, and the
Father the part of ruler and judge. Thus, if we attend to the express wording
(τῷ ῥητῷ) of Holy Scripture in this business of adoption, the Son’s part
is equal to, if not greater than, the Father’s.

3. As for the confirmation of his argument which he adduces from Romans
8:30: “Those whom he predestined, he also adopted”—that is, according at
least to this interpreter, the Father; “those whom he called, he also
justified”—namely, the Son; “those whom he justified, he also
glorified”—namely, the Holy Spirit. From this he carves out both the three
persons and their order both of subsistence and of operation. He therefore
assigns adoption, contained under calling, to the first person and seeks to
prove from this that it is prior to justification, because justification
follows there as an operation proper to the second person. All these things
seem to me ingenious and forced and to possess nothing certain or solid. For:

1. Although I would gladly reject no proof of the most holy Trinity from any
passage of Scripture properly expounded, lest I should deservedly be subject
to the charge for which Calvin is often undeservedly berated by Hunnius,
Lutherans, and Roman Catholics, nevertheless, since there are very many
plain testimonies on the sacred page to confirm it, I would not wish—I say,
I would not wish—doubtful and uncertain passages, which can be rightly
explained otherwise, to be dragged here against their will, lest we become
a scandal and laughingstock to Jews and antitrinitarians. This perhaps will
happen here too if we wish to carve the Trinity of persons out of this
statement of the apostle. For here, in the apostle’s thought and words, one
and the same God predestines, calls, justifies, and glorifies, without either
an expressed or intended distinction of persons.

[Marginalia: Ravanellus; the Leiden theologians in the *Synopsis*.]

[1679 printed p. 64 | IA leaf 72]

2. Much less can we extract from this the order of subsistence or operation
of these persons, as though the first person first called us, the second
person then justified us second, and finally the third person glorified us
third. For the third person, namely the Holy Spirit, properly and effectually
calls and regenerates us. The Father, who is the first person and judge and
ruler, most properly justifies us, just as together with the Son as well as
the Holy Spirit he glorifies us.

3. Least of all can the priority of adoption over justification be inferred
from this. Although adoption, of which no express mention is made here, is
understood, it is not contained under the name of *calling*, as that most
learned man contends. Rather, we have already sufficiently shown, in my
judgment, that it is contained under *justification*, to which adoption is
in many respects a twin and most closely joined.

This is more than sufficient in reply to his first argument.
