9. Since these things are so, our next task is to examine the nature of Adam's
death; by doing so we will at the same time discern how widely the Earth of
death extends. There are three opinions concerning the death of the first
parent. The first belongs to those who maintain that he was created mortal
from the beginning, but that God attributed the cause of death to sin so that
we might pursue sin with greater hatred and detestation. This was the Pelagian
opinion, although it also pleased other men of better reputation, among them
Theodore of Antioch, as may be seen in Photius. All of them understand “you
will surely die” as the death of the soul. They think this is also confirmed by
the argument that the sinner did not die on the very day of his transgression,
as had been foretold, but only after nine hundred years.

Others acknowledge what could not be denied without opposing Scripture: that
the body too fell into death through sin. Yet they mean death common to
everyone, as they say, not the death peculiar to the ungodly—that is, the
death by which soul and body are separated for a time, not that by which soul
and body together provide everlasting fuel for the avenging flames. This is
the opinion of many, and especially of Chrysostom. He insists that “in Adam all
die and in Christ all are made alive” can only be understood of temporal life
and death.

A third group follows Origen's authority. For him bodily mortality is the
shadow of death, while true death is that of which the Prophet also says, “The
soul that sins will itself die.” From the fact that Adam is also said to have
died in body, it is therefore concluded that he had already met the death of
the soul and, beyond that, had become enveloped in the actuality and power of
both. Thus Origen holds that man was mortal from his origin, yet in such a way
that he might perhaps never die. This has no basis in Scripture and seems to have
looked toward the lesser gods of the Platonists, who are indeed dissoluble but
will never be dissolved by any length of time.

Augustine treats this passage expressly. When the question is asked what death
God threatened against the first human beings, he says the answer must be:
every death. Since I come very close to his opinion, it will be relevant to
gather and bind into a single bundle what he discussed in several places.
Death, then, is man's accursed corruption, and it is twofold: either the death
of guilt, by which man voluntarily abandons God, or the death of punishment,
by which he is abandoned by God against his will. The death of punishment is
also twofold. The first occurs through dissolution before the resurrection,
and this is either common, containing only the penalty of loss,

or special, in which there is also the penalty of sensation. The second occurs
when the parts are joined together for everlasting torments, and it follows

10. If we have not set all this out incorrectly, every station and every
condition of human beings, whether in this age or in
the age to come, will certainly be found in the Earth pronounced by God. The
station will be that of bodies, the condition that of souls: the former
expressed by the name, the latter shadowed by a modification of the name. No
argument is needed to establish that Earth is the dwelling of the living, for
that is obvious. The places of the dead are an obscure question, one that
antiquity consecrated with certain mystical names. It scarcely seems likely
that so many and such varied names should apply to one Earth. Yet it is well
that many things can be entirely true without flattering the unlearned with
any appearance of likelihood.

After unfolding the whole of antiquity, we will demonstrate that the Hebrews'
*Sheol*, the Greeks' *Hades*, the Arabs' *Mahad*, the Egyptians' *Amenthen*,
the Magi's *Aremania*, and the Latins' *Orcus* not only designated Earth for
their earliest authorities, but were also taken from this very passage of
Scripture and agree with the accursed Earth—all of them in substance, and some
even in sound. The thing in this passage is Return; the name is *Adamah*. The
former has spread through every religion, while the latter has been carried
into the languages not of everyone, certainly, but of the most learned. I
therefore recover the thing from this: “You are Earth and will return to
Earth”; and the name from what came before: “Cursed is *Adamah* because of
you.”

First, that Return is an action so proper to earth that no name is equally
common to any other nature. Nothing returns except to the place from which it
was taken, and Adam was taken only from *Adamah*. It is therefore rightly said
of Adam's offspring, *iashub leadmatho*, at Psalm 146:4: “He returns to his
Earth.” In this one expression both properties are joined as if by a covenant.
Consequently, when either this property or that one occurs in any of these
names, all of which we will examine individually, let us not doubt what it
means. Rather, where either the nature or the name of Earth fits, compelling
reason will make us acknowledge that the thing itself is Earth.

11. Let us first demonstrate that the Hebrew *Sheol* is Earth. Two principal
matters must be considered in this word: its origin and its use. Everyone is
right to refer *Sheol* to *Shaal*, but the majority are wrong to assign this
verb only the sense of asking. It equally means to give and to ask, whether by
gift or by loan. By this word Hannah gave Samuel to the Lord, and the Egyptians
lent the Israelites vessels of silver and gold and a great quantity of
clothing. What, then, could agree more closely with this derivation than Earth,
which indeed supplies everything, but as things that must be returned?

[1611 Book II, printed p. 15 | Google Books RA1-PA15]

Its use follows, and by that use too *Sheol* is proved to be Earth. The
Prophets celebrate a twofold nature of Earth: one that brings things forth
into the light, and another that receives and conceals them; the former is the
beginning of things' existence, the latter the end of their destruction. In
the same way the language of the Prophets distinguished Earth by various
names, some of which declare both of its powers, while others declare only one
or the other. *Eretz*, *Adama*, and *Aphar* correspond to the Latins' “earth,”
“soil,” and “ground,” and are applied alike to the origin and destruction of
things. *Tebel* and *Sheol* also signify Earth, but *Tebel* only the Earth where
things stand constructed and flourish, and *Sheol* only that Earth where
things, once destroyed, perish. Thus neither *Tebel* nor *Sheol* is properly
Earth, for neither expresses its whole nature as the other words I have named
do. The former designates the surface of Earth from which corrupted substance
is removed; the latter, Earth's lower parts, to which what has been destroyed
descends. Hence *Tebel* is rightly translated “age” or “world,” the Region of
the living, and *Sheol* “hell” or “grave,” the homeland of the dead.

When the lower regions are mentioned, however, no one should immediately
imagine that I mean the center of Earth and some vast abyss, in company with
the superstitious multitude. One should know instead that the name “lower
regions” designates any hidden and concealed parts of Earth, whether they lie
closer, even very close, to Earth's surface, or reach to the middle of the
opaque body and even to an immeasurable depth. Once you have departed from the
face of Earth on which people live and, as the Poet says, *gaia amphikalypse*,
so that you are called buried and enclosed in *Sheol*, it makes no difference
how deeply you are dug down.

Nor should one who hears “grave” suppose that he hears it in the sense in
which jurists discuss violation of a grave. He should take it instead in the
Poet's sense: “Nature buries those left behind, with no care for a tomb.” It is
first the common grave of all human beings, not the private grave of anyone
(hence we read of the grave of Joseph and of Abner, but not of this or that
person's *Sheol*); and then the grave not only of human beings, but of plainly
everything generated from Earth. People otherwise far too credulous may doubt
that the thing called *Sheol* is such a common receptacle of things. We, who do
not wish to disbelieve the sacred Preacher, can have no doubt at all. For he
says, “All things go to one place,” and elsewhere, “There is no work or reason
or knowledge or thought in *Sheol*, where you are going.” From this it
necessarily follows that, if *Sheol* is where we go, *Sheol* is also where
other things pass away. It is already apparent,

from the thing itself and from that very witness, what this is: Earth. We need
only recall the Earth of which the Prophet speaks, “You led me into the earth
of death,” not the one of which he boasts, “My portion is in the Earth of the
living.” Although Earth and *Sheol* are often found with the same meaning
indiscriminately—the “mouth of *Adamah*” in Moses is the same as the “mouth of
*Sheol*” in the Psalms, and what is elsewhere *Sheol Tachtith* in sacred
writings is in another place *Eretz tachtith*—this nevertheless scarcely ever
occurs except when the subject is destruction or places especially suited to
destruction, that is, places farthest removed from light and life.

Thus *Sheol* is Earth everywhere, but Earth is not *Sheol* everywhere. Earth
is *Sheol* only where its nature is considered as stripped of generation,
form, life, and good, and viewed in corruption, deprivation, death, and evil.
Considered in this way, it gives nothing, brings forth nothing, and supplies
nothing, but asks for everything, gapes after everything, and swallows
everything with an insatiable appetite. It is therefore reasonably believed
to take its name from asking and is regarded as the universal grave of things.
It is no objection that Scripture applies *Sheol* to the human race more often,
indeed almost always. On the contrary—as true things always agree with true
things—this accords wonderfully with what we teach. For Earth was cursed not
because of itself, but because of you. Although guilt wounded all earthly
things through man, it was nevertheless said to no one but man, “You are Earth
and will return to Earth.” Both Scripture itself and the Rabbis from Scripture
locate *Sheol* in this sentence.

“All nations that forget God,” says the Prophet, “will return to *Sheol*.” Will
they indeed return? Do I not hear here God consigning the sinner to dust? You,
however, may hear R. David Kimchi: “For *Sheol* is the grave,” he says,
“according to Genesis 3, ‘You are dust and will return to dust.’” This Doctor
is plain. What does this statement leave undone, whether in showing the
thing's origin or the meaning of its name? He derives *Sheol* from Earth, and
specifically the accursed Earth. He says that it signifies the grave, but one
that was born, so to speak, rather than made: in short, a grave in which there
appears not only the corruption common to everyone but also lies hidden the
corruption peculiar to the ungodly.

12. From this it is apparent that the opinions of the Hebrews which are
thought to conflict do not oppose one another. Some hold that *Sheol* is the
grave, others the deep hollows of Earth, and others a place opposed to heaven.
Since *Sheol* is first and properly Earth, as either has been sufficiently
established or certainly will be, it is easily transferred to all the senses
we have stated. Those who do not interpret *Sheol* as the grave do so with a
great matter in view,

and with no small deliberation: they understand it figuratively, not simply.
Although this is perfectly true, I would not say it unless they themselves did.
On the passage in Genesis where Jacob says that he will descend into *Sheol*,
R. Solomon's gloss has: “Into *Sheol*, that is, into the grave according to the
surface of the letter, but according to the Midrash, into Gehenna.” He could
not have distinguished the two meanings of this word more clearly: one which
the letter itself offers the reader, and another which lies more deeply hidden
and is brought out only by searching.

The arguments customarily brought against this are taken either from sacred
writings, from their interpretations, or from the nature of languages. We
will go through them all in order without concealing anything, so that truth,
at last drawn out of Democritus's well, may commend itself not only by its own
strength but also by the weakness of error.
