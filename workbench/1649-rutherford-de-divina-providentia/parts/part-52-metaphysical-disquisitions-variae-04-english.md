[Marginalia: The simultaneous influx of God and the creature overturns the
creature’s dependence upon the Creator in acting.]

[Witness note: Both copies read *dependentiam creatura Creatore*, omitting both
the expected genitive ending and the preposition before “Creator.”]

Question 21. Does not the second cause depend upon the first, since the action
of the second cause cannot exist without the action of the first—and that a
simultaneous action—because, although one action may be from both causes,
nevertheless, insofar as it is from one, it cannot be from the other? I answer:
on this account the first cause would equally depend upon the second cause. Yet
the second depends upon the first, because God cannot produce in a human being
the vital acts of believing, hoping, and loving without that person’s vital
action.

Second, since the action of the second cause, even as it is in an actual
tendency toward the act and the efficacy of action, is being by participation
and not being by essence, and since an actual created action cannot derive from
the actual created action itself—for this is impossible—it must derive from the
first being through itself, namely from God.

Third, insofar as an action is from one cause, it can very well be from another
when those causes are subordinate, although not under the same reduplication.
But the matter is otherwise when the first and second causes are imagined as
coordinated and parallel causes. The Jesuits beg the question, and in this sense
one action cannot be from two collateral causes.

[Marginalia: The simultaneous influx of God and the creature introduces two
first principles.]

Question 22. Does it follow that the second cause is a collateral first cause,
simply operating together with God as the first operator, and that, with the
Manichaeans, two first principles are posited if GOD and creatures are two
simultaneous causes? I answer that it follows perfectly. For fire’s being a
principle actually applied in the exercise of an act to burning, and the will’s
being actually applied to loving, derives either from itself, from God, or from
a third common cause determining both collateral causes. It does not derive
from itself, because what is not first being from itself is not a first operator
from itself. The Jesuits teach that, for the character of a first mover, it is
enough that it have being from itself; but since every second cause has being
from God, it will not be a first mover. Yet this is what the Durandists taught:
God is the first cause of heating because he gave fire the power of heating.
This is what Lodovicus a Dola and others teach. But because no being has being
from itself, neither will actually operating and primarily determining itself
to operate derive from a second cause. The Jesuits answer that *the second
cause is not a first mover because it cannot operate without GOD’s simultaneous
concurrence.* But in that case God likewise will not be the first cause in vital
actions, because God cannot produce vital actions without

[Various Questions 21–26; printed page 595; IA PDF page 669; BSB canvas 645]

the simultaneous concurrence of the vital principle. But if actually operating
derives from God, we have what we seek. If the third alternative is asserted,
namely that there is a third common cause determining both the first and second
causes to act—as must necessarily be said in this case—then there will be:
first, a cause prior to the first cause; second, a certain God above the Creator
of heaven and earth; and third, this cause will actualize the habit or potential
power of the second cause for acting.

But since there are two simultaneous causes in acting, they will be coordinated
and thus not subordinate in acting. For according to the Jesuits the first
cause cannot apply the second to acting; and according to sound theology or
right reason the second cannot apply the first to acting. Nor can God know
whether the second cause will contribute its influence, for middle knowledge
wavers, if it does not vanish altogether. And since the second cause is
absolute and equally independent in respect of commanding, and indeed equally
first in its influx of first operation and as supreme in dominion as God
himself, how will God be assured of volition rather than nonvolition, or of the
metaphysically infallible and indefectible exercise of illumination in the sun?
For according to the Jesuits, actual causation belongs to the creature operating
equally first with God, not to the Creator, since God’s influence is terminated
in the effect of the second cause before it is terminated in the second cause,
with a priority of power or potency, not a priority of act or exercise—if those
guides are to be followed.

[Marginalia: Simultaneous influences overturn God’s certain knowledge.]

Question 24. Is it enough for God to be said to concur with the second cause in
a manner neither blind nor fortuitous that he knows, through the knowledge of
simple intelligence, that the second cause will contribute its influence if no
impediments are present? For second causes are determined of themselves to act
when they are natural causes, and free causes will operate unless God prevents
them; and God knows well enough whether he himself will prevent them. I answer,
first: for a metaphysically indefectible and inevitable exercise of the act, it
is not enough that God knows the second cause will act if no impediments are
present. This cognition is common to the angels with God; but a more eminent
knowledge must be attributed to God than to the angels. Second, natural causes
are determined to act with respect to specification,

[Various Questions 21–26; printed page 596; IA PDF page 670; BSB canvas 646]

not with respect to exercise, which is the matter in question, as the Jesuits
of Coimbra admit. For the sun might not rise tomorrow if other, more efficacious
natural causes intervene to impede it. Yet God cannot premove these other
causes, but, as the Jesuits please, entrusts the matter to the simultaneous
created providence and determination of the first creature. Therefore God does
not know whether they will determinately act.

Third, free causes are nevertheless not determined to act, but are absolutely
and independently indifferent with respect to the exercise of the act. And it
cannot be conceived how God removes this indifference while the will remains
free to apply itself to acting or not acting under these conditions posited by
the Jesuits: first, that the influx of the created will is simultaneous; second,
that it is prior by nature to the influx of the first cause; and third, that in
actual exercise it is independent of God with respect to actual causation,
though not with respect to power—an absoluteness that God, to be sure, could
overcome only by annihilating the will through omnipotence.

[Marginalia: GOD does not cause the second cause to act if simultaneous
influxes are maintained.]

Question 25. Would GOD be said to move the sun to illuminate if he were not the
author of that illumination insofar as it is from the sun? I answer: he would
not be the author of that illumination insofar as it is from the sun, although
he would be a concurrent cause of the illumination that is from the sun. Nor
would God have any dominion over the use or nonuse of the operations of
creatures, because in application to actual exercise or in nonapplication every
second cause would possess all and sole dominion over this, while God would
have no providence. Remove the dominion of providence and you remove providence
itself.

[Marginalia: God’s influx into a second cause is not a permanent quality
inhering in the second cause.]

Question 26. Therefore, whenever a second cause actually acts, is some real and
permanent quality impressed upon it, and in what category does this quality
belong? I answer: although many very weighty Thomists who strenuously oppose the
Jesuits say that it is a permanent quality, like the colors on a dove’s neck, I
nevertheless have considerable doubt. I think it is something real—indeed, the
efficacy of the command of divine concurrence—through which the second cause is
something beyond merely being operative, namely, that it actually operates.
But I would believe it to be an efficacious impulse which is not properly
outside God. Just as the motion of a saw by a craftsman, of a pen by a writer,
or of a sword by a soldier is not a permanent real quality subjectively
inhering in the saw,

[Various Questions 21–26; printed page 597; IA PDF page 671; BSB canvas 647]

pen, or sword, it is in the saw, pen, or sword—or rather its effect is received
in the saw, pen, or sword in the manner of a transient quality. Such an effect
is nothing other than actually and efficaciously operating because the first
cause impels the second.
