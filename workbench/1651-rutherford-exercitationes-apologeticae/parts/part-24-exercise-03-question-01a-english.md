EXERCISE III

On God's temporal acts upon human freedom.

QUESTION I

*Whether free choice is determined by God, both in acts of nature and in acts
of grace.*

1. The very weighty question concerning the efficacy of grace borrows no
little light from the question concerning the determination of the will. To
begin with the word: to determine is to set limits, by which indetermination
and indifference are removed. The Greek Ὁρίζειν denotes this. גבל, as also
תאה, means to set and delineate the boundaries of fields (Joshua 15:9, 21;
Deuteronomy 19:14).

2. Since three principal causes concur in free acts—namely, the will; 2.
natural reason, and, in sacred acts, God's Word; and 3. God as the first
mover—and, in spiritual acts,

[1651 logical printed p. 364 | IA leaf 382 | PDF p. 382]

the grace of the Holy Spirit, I therefore posit three determinations. The
first is objective, material, or moral: when a reason drawn either from nature
or from the light of the sacred oracles, proposed to the will through the
mediation of the intellect, moves it. Here reason, or God's Word, is the
will's object—not materially but formally, insofar as it moves and affects the
will. It is therefore called a moral, ethical, or objective determination. 2.
Because God is the mover of every second cause, whether by a physical impulse
to will, choose, and reject, as happens in acts of nature, or by a
hyperphysical impulse, as in acts of grace, this determination is real (not
that the objective determination is merely imaginary, for it too is real in
its own kind and by no means fictitious), effective, or also equivocal. 3.
The will itself, moved and predetermined by God, by reason, by God's Word, and
by the grace of the Holy Spirit, formally and vitally determines itself. This
is formal and univocal determination, and is nothing other than the volition
itself. There is no present controversy between us and the Jesuits and
Arminians about this last determination. Volition and nolition are formally
neither from God nor from reason, but from the will itself.

3. Concerning the first determination, namely that of the intellect, which
determines the will, I do not wish anxiously to raise a quarrel with anyone,
since I am quite satisfied that our orthodox position will remain safe and
sound whether we think one way or the other. Yet I shall say what is chiefly

[1651 logical printed p. 365 | IA leaf 383 | PDF p. 383]

at issue here and which view pleases me more. 1. The question is not whether
the judgment of the intellect specifies acts of freedom. It is from the will,
not from the intellect's judgment, that an act of the will is of this species:
for example, volition rather than nolition, election rather than rejection.
2. Nor is it whether the mind moves the will or inclines it by the weight of
light; both sides grant this. 3. One judgment is last in a qualified respect,
another last simply. The former is last conditionally, as conscience dictates
that adultery must be fled from conditionally—if we wish to please God—not
absolutely, since it can happen that indulging the flesh is more pleasing, and
therefore the will can recoil here. Another judgment is simply and absolutely
last: what the mind absolutely judges simply must be done here and now. When
this last judgment formally stands under God's motion, it appears necessarily
to determine the will. I say “when the last judgment stands under God's
motion,” who by infallible light, with all darkness removed, advises and
persuades. But when the last judgment is considered in itself and formally, it
appears that the will can call the intellect away from thinking about that
object thus ultimately proposed to other things which, although they move and
persuade the will less effectively than that final object, nevertheless can
engage it. For the will can do two things here: 1. call the mind away from
considering that final object to contemplate another; and 2. call the mind
away from actually comparing this latter earthly good with that heavenly good
which in itself was moving the will more effectively. Therefore it does not

[1651 logical printed p. 366 | IA leaf 384 | PDF p. 384]

necessarily determine freedom. 2. If the last judgment necessarily and of
itself determined the will, grace would be mere persuasion, and inward grace
would not be necessary to heal the will. It would be enough to remove the
darkness and instruct the mind about what lay hidden—which is Pelagian grace.
Thus, by an acquired habit of faith and the clearest evidence of the object, a
reprobate's mind could be instructed in what seems to me a possible case:
namely, that the mind should perceive in an act of obedience 1. ease, 2.
sweetness, and 3. usefulness; and that the will, as it could, should neither
call the mind away to investigate in that act some aspect of difficulty, or
of some other thing under which evil would appear, nor call it away from that
actual contemplation to think about earthly things. In this case it could
believe and perform a supernatural act without any healing of the will, and
there would be no need to remove the stony heart and infuse a heart of flesh.

[Marginalia: Objection.]

4. The passages of Scripture do not prove the contrary; but it may be said:
Psalm 9:10, *Those who know your name trust in you.* If that knowledge did not
determine the will, it could happen that those who knew God's name would not
trust in Jehovah. Likewise John 4, verse 1: *If you knew that gift of God, and
who it is who says to you, Give me a drink, you would ask him for living
water.* If that knowledge did not determine the will to ask Christ for living
water, then the woman could know that gift of God and not have asked him for
living

[1651 logical printed p. 367 | IA leaf 385 | PDF p. 385]

water, and Christ would have spoken falsely. I answer: in propositions of this
kind God's physical motion is always tacitly understood. Otherwise, if they
were pressed to the letter, it would follow that bare knowledge of the object
is sufficient for the act of believing without the infusion of a new power,
which no one would say. Likewise, from the statement that if the Tyrians had
seen the signs they would have repented, it does not follow that the sight of
miracles undeviatingly determines the will to repentance. For the consequent
is plainly false. Therefore repentance always requires a tacit condition—not
expressed but understood—from the preceding motion of effectual grace.
Otherwise the Tyrians could have seen signs, indeed more than Capernaum and
Bethsaida saw, and yet never repented. But I have not resolved to pull the rope
of contention here; neither opinion lacks its own probability.

5. The whole dispute, therefore, concerns the determination of the second
class, namely the equivocal one, both in acts of nature and in acts of grace;
for brevity's sake I shall discuss both together.

The adversaries give us various determinations here, though with ungenerous
munificence. 1. Fonseca says that God moves the will by a motive efficiency in
the manner of an affection—by persuading (which Pelagius did not deny)—not by a
physical efficiency which determines absolute consent. 2. In the distinction
there, he maintains that God's action upon the will is motive, in the manner
of nature, insofar as the author of nature gives it the power to determine
itself,

[Marginalia: Fonseca, *Metaphysics*, book 6, chapter 2, question 4, section 13.]

[1651 logical printed p. 368 | IA leaf 386 | PDF p. 386]

but is not undeviatingly determinative. So also Molina. 3. distinction.
“Only the will” (Fonseca says in the same place) “determines itself—not in the
solitude, so to speak, of one concurring, but in the solitude of one
determining.” So Arminius, and so Grevinchovius. God moves free choice, they
say, so that it may determine itself; and he jointly effects the determination
with free choice. 5. The distinction of Suarez, Vasquez, Lessius, and other
Jesuits is that God moves the will by an approving act in good things, a
permitting act in evil things, and a repudiating act in acts which never
occur. But what else is this than for God not to move the will at all? For the
Jesuits' permitting act is the negation of an act, by which the will is left
entirely by God to its own indifference. Therefore God does nothing upon the
will. An approving act presupposes an act of the will already performed. Thus
the free act is not even made by God's approval, much less determined; rather,
once performed it is approved. There is no predefining act here. 6.
distinction. Bellarmine, Fonseca, Suarez, and Vasquez forge a congruous and an
incongruous determination. The worth of that distinction will be declared
later. 7. distinction. The Arminians say that God moves the will according to
the manner of nature, or as it is a natural affection, because God inspires a
desire for the good in the affections, but not according to the manner of
freedom. Yet here God's action terminates in no way upon the free will, but
upon the intellectual affections.

[Marginalia: Arminius, response to article 7, p. 143. Grevinchovius, p. 221.]

[1651 logical printed p. 369 | IA leaf 387 | PDF p. 387]

The Arminians deny that even affections which incline most intensely toward
the love of God determine the will to the love of God. But are these men
sufficiently sane? They teach that by the affections we can earnestly love God
with the whole heart and all our strength, while the free will could not only
be averse to the love of God but blaze with hatred both of God and of the
neighbor. That is for men to hate God and not to hate God. For what, I ask,
are the intellectual affections—joy, love, hope, and so on? Perhaps they would
say that they are intellectual inclinations in the will. Granted; I do not
deny this. But then it cannot be denied that the acts of joy and intellectual
love are acts of the will. Thus the will can elicit an act of joy and love
toward God while at the same time neither willing nor choosing God, but
utterly rejecting him. This is for the will to will God and not will him.

6. To unravel the question, it must be noted that grace can determine the will
in two ways. 1. According to specification, so that if the will acts according
to the inherent principle of grace, the action will necessarily be gracious.
This determination is not in question, for with respect to exercise this
necessity is conditional, since the will can fail to act according to the
inherent principle of grace, as is clear in the falls of the saints. 2. The
question concerns the actual determination of the will with respect to
exercise. We say that habitual grace does not determine the will in exercise,
since

[1651 logical printed p. 370 | IA leaf 388 | PDF p. 388]

it is itself a power which needs to be actualized.

7. But we assert that in all acts, both of nature and of grace, God determines
the will by a truly real impulse in the manner of a physical cause. Now the
common refrain of the Arminians, when they answer the Scriptures we allege, is
that the manner in which God moves the will is not expressed, although the
principal question, they say, is not whether God moves the will but whether he
moves and determines it by a physical impulse—which, they contend, can nowhere
be proved from Scripture. But I would dare to say that, conquered by the
weight of the arguments, they pervert the state of the question. That is not
the case at issue, and we raise no quarrel over the phrase *physical
predetermination*. The question is whether God moves the will in such a way
that under that motion it cannot, once moved, fail to be effectually moved; or
whether God's action upon the will so bends it that it cannot fail freely to
bend itself in the direction in which God inclines it. From Scripture we prove
the necessitation of the will by God's motion, by which it is undeviatingly
effectually moved. Therefore the principal question concerns the
undeviatingness of a free act. If they would grant us that, we are prepared to
say farewell to *physical predetermination*, although in reality God
determines the will in the manner of a physical cause.

8. I proceed, therefore: 1. by Scriptures, 2. by arguments, and 3. by
testimonies.

1. Genesis 33. God changes Esau's heart and will

[1651 logical printed p. 371 | IA leaf 389 | PDF p. 389]

from hatred to favor, and that through Jacob's prayers. “What if,” Corvinus
says, “Esau's heart was changed by the sight of his brother and his brother's
offspring, and by his gentle address?”

[Marginalia: Corvinus against Molina, chapter 5, section 14.]

I answer: we shall not dispute about the manner. The text proves that Esau's
heart was so changed that he could not fail to receive his brother Jacob with
a serene and gracious countenance and honor him with gentle and mild speech.
For if it was by moral persuasion, then God so changed Esau's heart that it
could have remained unchanged. But Jacob in Genesis 32:11 prayed in faith that
God would make Esau favorable to him, and not by an extraordinary manner. For
with what faith would Jacob pray that God perform a miracle? Therefore Jacob
prayed in reliance on God's omnipotence and wisdom that God would so make Esau
favorable that he could not remain angry and hostile. 2. Granted: God changed
Esau's heart by persuasion. Therefore he did not do so by a real impulse. This
does not follow; indeed, the contrary follows. For there is no action of a
second cause without the action of the first cause. Thus Jacob says in Genesis
45:14, *May almighty God grant you to find favor in the eyes of the man*—that
is, Joseph, whom the patriarch believed to be an irreligious Egyptian. I ask:
did not the old man, resting by faith upon God's omnipotent providence, which
bends the wills of all men, pray to God? I am confident that no one doubts it.
For unless we firmly believe that God is able to help, we pray in vain.
Therefore he prayed that God would change Joseph's will from hatred to favor,
either undeviatingly or in a way that could be deflected. If he prayed that it
be changed undeviatingly, I have established my point: namely, that it is the
faith

[1651 logical printed p. 372 | IA leaf 390 | PDF p. 390]

of patriarchs and prophets that God undeviatingly determines human wills. If
he prayed that God change Joseph's will in a way that could be deflected, he
prayed to God in this manner: “May almighty God grant you that that Egyptian
ruler show himself either favorable toward you, or inexorable, difficult, and
utterly hostile to your prayers.” Would he then, I ask, pray for anything more
good than evil for his sons? Indeed, Esther and her maids and all the Jews
would have prayed not so much to God as to the indifference of Ahasuerus's
will, so that the king would grant the Jews life at Esther's prayers; and,
notwithstanding God's motion by which he bends kings' hearts, he could have
remained stony and inexorable. Thus the Arminians will here place upon the
throne either man's free choice or a providence which undeviatingly determines
the acts of kings.

1 Samuel 10:26: *Men whose heart Jehovah had touched followed Saul.* Ezra 7:7:
*Blessed be the Lord God of our fathers, who put such a thing into the king's
heart, that he should adorn Jehovah's house in Jerusalem.* 2 Chronicles 30:12:
*The hand of God was also upon the men of Judah, so as to give them a heart to
perform the king's command according to Jehovah's Word.* 2 Chronicles 29:18.
Thus Solomon prays: *O Jehovah, Lord God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, preserve
this forever in the imaginations of the thoughts of the heart of this people,
and prepare their hearts for yourself.* 1 Kings 8:57: *May Jehovah our God be
with us as he was with our fathers; may he not*

[1651 logical printed p. 373 | IA leaf 391 | PDF p. 391]

*leave us or forsake us. 58. By inclining our heart to himself, that we may walk
in all his ways,* and so on. The Remonstrants answer in their Apology as
follows: “To incline the heart to act can signify the end, not the effect, of
divine beneficence.”

[Marginalia: Remonstrants, *Apology*, chapter 14, fol. 145.]

Answer 1. Interpreters can make it signify what it does not signify, but the
question is what it does signify. 2. If it signifies an end which God
undeviatingly attains, we have what we seek. If in a way that can be deflected,
then Solomon prays that God so incline the heart and bend it to obedience that,
casting away reverence for the divine majesty, they can utterly fall away from
obedience. This is to say that believers ought to pray with no faith, but with
doubtful and conjectural knowledge.

2. Answer. “This manner of determining the will is not obscurely refuted by
Solomon in the preceding verse: *If they sin against you, and you are angry
with them,* and so on. For this signifies that it is possible for the people
to be turned away from God,” and so on. But I answer: who denies that the
people can sin through a lack of gracious determination? If those teachers
wished to oppose our thesis, however, they ought to have proved that a people
inclined by God in this way can sin. They relieve themselves of this burden by
silence alone. But Solomon does not say that those who sinned had been
inclined by God through a gracious bending to walk in Jehovah's ways and yet
sinned. Therefore he confirms this determination rather than refuting it.

[Marginalia: *Apology*, ibid.]

3. Answer. “But if verse 39 is added, it will appear,” they say, “that Solomon
indicates that it can happen that by the very same

[Marginalia: *Apology*, ibid.]

[1651 logical printed p. 374 | IA leaf 392 | PDF p. 392]

thing or occasion by which this man is moved to repent, or by which this man's
heart is inclined to fear God, another is not moved; and thus God is moved to
give to each one according to his ways.” I answer: who does not see a spirit
of slumber here? They tell us that “by the very same thing or occasion by which
this man's heart is moved to fear God, another is not moved.” Who denies this?
But does the thing or the occasion determine man's will to fear God, or rather
God's gracious bending? Reader, do you see these Pelagians attributing to
God's judgments—the plague, blasting, mildew, locusts which devour the land
and crops—that men are thereby moved to fear God? The Holy Spirit in express
words attributes this in verses 57–58 to God's gracious presence, to his not
divinely abandoning them, and to the effectual and gracious bending of the
will. Behold, locusts and famine are Arminian grace—a grace indeed worthy of
the enemies of grace! But let them answer from conscience whether the question
is whether the will is determined by occasions of place, time, secondary
things, or adversities.

2. They must prove the antithesis: that among several moved by the same bending
of God, some are moved to repentance and some are not. They cunningly pass
over this and flee as to an anchor to external occasions, which good men are
known to use rightly and wicked men to abuse.

4. They answer as follows: “The sense of the words is clear in itself: May God
benefit this people as he benefited our fathers, so that from those benefits
they may have cause to adhere indivisibly to their God, and may continually
keep his precepts and statutes

[1651 logical printed p. 375 | IA leaf 393 | PDF p. 393]

before their eyes, and willingly desire to observe them freely; for minds are
joined by benefits, and hearts are inclined to spontaneous obedience.” I
answer: the man who calls this the clear sense of the words is certainly no
lynx. For 1. it is certain that Solomon prays that God may benefit the people,
but by benefits? Which ones? Only external and temporal benefits—peace,
abundance of crops, defeat of enemies? If this is what they mean, they err
shamefully. *May Jehovah our God be with us as he was with our fathers*—whose
God he was, and the circumciser of their hearts. But if such a presence of God
consists in the possession of earthly things, God was present to the Moabites
and Ammonites in this way. Does Solomon therefore pray for nothing further?
Certainly this supposedly self-evident sense is more worthy of swine than of
God's covenant people. 2. If they also mean spiritual benefits, I ask whether
they understand the circumcision of the heart and the effecting of obedience
and repentance. They do not dare say this. For then, by an argument drawn from
the benefit of regeneration, God would effect regeneration, which is plainly
absurd. 3. If, therefore, they wish all other temporal and spiritual benefits
except these alone to be signified, I hear of benefits that move by
persuasion—that is, external grace which morally arouses. This Pelagius taught,
as Augustine reports, by which God works in us to will what is good and holy,
“when he kindles us, who are devoted to earthly desires and who, after the
manner of dumb animals, love only present things,

[Marginalia: Augustine, *On the Grace of Christ against Pelagius and
Cælestius*, chapter 10.]

[1651 logical printed p. 376 | IA leaf 394 | PDF p. 394]

by the greatness of future glory and the promise of rewards; when by the
revelation of wisdom he raises the astonished will to desire God; when he
persuades us of everything good (which nevertheless elsewhere he is not afraid
to deny).” You see, reader, that the Remonstrants speak much more generally and
meagerly than Pelagius himself concerning God's inward grace which inclines
the heart to obedience. For they contend only that God moves by benefits, but
do not teach what sort of benefits. Pelagius, however, expressly mentions the
benefit of eternal glory. 4. The supposedly self-evident sense here is this:
Solomon says, “May Jehovah be with us, so that he may bend and incline the heart
to obedience.” The Remonstrants say: “May Jehovah so be with us by benefits
that we can either perform or not perform God's commands.” Thus Solomon prays
as much for disobedience as for obedience. Nothing can be said to the contrary
except that by those benefits God intends obedience, not disobedience. But
God's intention is a merely immanent and inward act, doing nothing outwardly
to incline the heart actually to obedience. It signifies only God's desire,
which is not absent toward Cain, Saul, and Pharaoh, whom God did not cease to
bless with temporal and spiritual benefits and whose obedience he desired. 5.
The text means something far different from what the Remonstrants imply: “May
Jehovah our God be joined to us by the new covenant, by which he gives a new
heart, so that we may be taught by God and drawn by the Father to Christ,” as
in Jeremiah 31:33–34; Ezekiel 36:26; John 6:44–45. 2. It is added, “as he was
with

[1651 logical printed p. 377 | IA leaf 395 | PDF p. 395]

our fathers”—namely Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. But I ask: how was he present to
them? Merely by grace persuading externally? I appeal to their own conscience:
did not God's presence with the patriarchs and the covenant people consist in
his circumcising their hearts and granting them faith and repentance? Will
this count for the Remonstrants as no divine presence at all? 3. The manner in
which he was present to them, and in which Solomon prays that he be present to
the people now living, is added: *may he not leave us or forsake us.* This is
explained by “inclining the heart to act, so that we may walk in his precepts.”
I ask: what is this non-forsaking? For the Arminians it will surely be merely
the withdrawal of morally persuasive grace and benefits, not such a forsaking
as makes it necessary that they not walk in Jehovah's ways. Yet it is evident
that this is the greatest forsaking. 4. The Remonstrants explain *that we may
walk* as “that we may will to adhere indivisibly and keep his precepts, and
indeed that we may will it willingly.” Now I ask you, doctors, what is it to
will willingly? Is it to will freely? And what is it for you to will freely
other than so to will that you can not will? Therefore, “May Jehovah be present
to incline the heart so that we may walk” will mean the same as “May Jehovah be
present to incline the heart so that we may not walk.” Let the reader now
consider this supposedly self-evident sense, since this clear sense openly
contradicts the text.

9. I proceed to the remaining texts. Psalm 119:36: *Bend my heart toward your
statutes.* To bend in this way is to

[1651 logical printed p. 378 | IA leaf 396 | PDF p. 396]

determine. The Remonstrants answer in the same place: “If in that prayer David
asks for determining aid, then David asks that God not determine his will to
evil—for example, to covetousness. For David says: *Incline my heart to your
testimonies, and not to covetousness.* Therefore God will be the author of sin
in the same way that he is of good.” I answer: 1. I turn the reply back upon
them. For the Remonstrants the sense will be: “Morally arouse the heart and
persuade it to keep the testimonies, and do not persuade it to covetousness.”
Therefore God will be the author of sin in the same way he is of good, if they
wish the God who persuades to be an efficient cause. 2. They answer nothing
except by alleging an inconvenience, and one which does not follow; therefore
they do not solve the argument. 3. I strengthen the argument thus. David
either prays in faith or he does not. No Christian should say the latter. If
in faith, I ask what the object of faith is in the prayers of the saints. It
is what Christ indicates in John 14:13–14: the omnipotence and benevolence of
God the Father in Christ, by which he says that he will give what we ask in
Christ's name. Therefore David believed that God would bend his heart to God's
testimonies and not to covetousness. But if God morally bends the will in such
a way that it can remain unbent, David had to believe just as much that God
would not bend his heart to God's statutes as that he would, since under that
persuasion the will always remains indifferent. Indeed, David would then pray:
“O Jehovah, so bend my heart to keep your testimonies that I may be equally
able not to keep them as to keep them.” I know they will answer that God bends
the will

[1651 logical printed p. 379 | IA leaf 397 | PDF p. 397]

of David to God's statutes on the hypothesis that the will itself has bent
itself. That is: God will effect the willing if the will has effected the
willing. But then in this prayer David trusts his free choice as much as God's
grace—indeed, he trusts his own choice more, since the actual bending, whether
it occurs or not, depends not upon grace but upon choice. Even when God's most
effectual motion is posited, a non-actual bending can follow just as much as an
actual bending. 4. It in no way follows that God determines the will to sin
because he determines it to the act, any more than it follows, “God co-effects
the act of sin by a collateral influx; therefore, according to the Arminians,
he is a co-efficient cause of sin.” The latter inference is as strong as the
former. Nor does it follow that God is the author of sin because he determines
to the act, for the same reason. But because, according to the Arminians, God
concurs by a general influx in all free acts in such a way that it is not from
God that an act is good rather than evil, it follows very well from Arminian
principles that God is the author of sin in the same way that he is of good.
