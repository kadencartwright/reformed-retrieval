VERSE 4. Whoever observes the command does not experience an evil thing, for
the mind of the wise knows time and judgment.

VERSE 5. For every purpose has its time and judgment, but a person's evil
weighs heavily upon him.

VERSE 6. Since he does not know what will happen—for who can tell him how it
will happen?

VERSE 7. No person rules over his own spirit so as to restrain his spirit, nor
is there any dominion in the day of death, nor any weapon in that battle;
finally, struggling does not deliver the person who employs it.

Thus far Solomon has urged that submission be rendered to the King from the
attendant punishment of those who forsake their duty. Now he exhorts to
obedience from its attendant reward: the obedient person will experience no
evil. Do you wish not to fear the authority? Do what is good. For even though
he may not receive benefits corresponding to the merit of his obedience, he
nevertheless gains at least this, even under Tyrants themselves: he is not
harassed and troubled by evils. This benefit is certainly not to be despised
in this life; before God, moreover, he will obtain the reward and recompense
of obedience. For what God promises in Ephesians 6:7–8 to servants who have
received from their own masters less than they deserve has a wider scope and
must be extended to subjects who have deserved well of ungrateful kings.

Another attendant feature of true obedience, and of the reward that will be
given to the obedient person, is that he obey at the right and proper time.
For to anticipate the time, or to execute what is commanded later than the
purpose of the command requires, is attended by danger, even when a person
performs the very thing contained in his instructions. For example, if the
ambush that Joshua had stationed behind the city when taking it had rushed out
earlier or later than he commanded, it would have inflicted grave loss upon
the army of Israel.

This creates all the greater danger in divine obedience because God holds the
precise moments of time in which each thing must be accomplished. Hence Saul,
who anticipated the time for sacrificing that God had appointed through the
Prophet Samuel, is condemned for folly. Hence also the Israelites, because of
the reputation for strength and might in which they persuaded themselves the
Canaanites excelled, refused battle with them and looked back toward Egypt;
later, when they wished to engage them, they did so not without a great
slaughter. God also invites us at the present time to repentance. If we put
off repentance to another age or year, or indeed even to another day, we will
not make this attempt without grave danger. Another attendant feature is that,
with judgment and discrimination and not rashly and indiscriminately, this
obedience should be

rendered. There are circumstances in which you may safely obey the King's
command, so that by obeying him you do not violate an oath made to God. There
are also circumstances in which, by obeying the King, you may become a rebel
against God. There are circumstances in which you may rightly refuse to
execute one part of the royal command while legitimately carrying out another
part. Here,
if you either fulfill everything that is commanded or refuse to do all of it,
you will sin gravely in either case. So it was with Daniel's three companions:
when commanded to assemble at the place where the idol was worshiped, they
obeyed; but when commanded to worship the idol, they refused.

Solomon identifies wisdom of heart as the efficient cause of these cautions
in obedience to a king, and this wisdom must be sought nowhere but from God's
word. Obedience to a King requires not only a subdued mind ready to obey, but
also knowledge and understanding of the divine will. Without it people will
either obey the King where they should not, or refuse obedience where it is
due and just. Either of these is harmful not only to the person obeying but
also to the person commanding. For if Jeroboam, when he erected the calves in
Israel, had not been obeyed by his subjects, he would both have remained in
his kingdom for less time and have called down upon soul and body a less
fearsome wrath of God. The Holy Spirit very frequently lays this charge upon
him: not only that he himself was an idolater, but that he led the Israelites
into error.

If knowledge of the divine will is necessary in order to obey Kings, how much
more fully is it required in obedience to God? Away, then, with the Pontifical
“good intention” that is separated from knowledge of the divine will. In this
way it comes about that under the Papacy legitimate obedience is rendered
neither to God nor to human beings.

From the general principle Solomon teaches that there is a time and a choice,
or judgment, in obedience to the King. For in all things that are lawfully
done and that we undertake to do, account must be taken of the time and
judgment must be employed, lest any fault be committed in the circumstances
of places, persons, time, or things. The Prophet reproaches the people for not
recognizing the time, although birds know it by natural instinct, Jeremiah 8;
see Luke 19:44. Thus farmers observe the time for plowing, sowing, harvest,
planting, grafting, pruning, and so forth. Thus David prudently observed the
time for asking provisions from Nabal when he was holding a feast. Thus
Abigail chose the time to admonish her husband after he had slept off his
gluttony and drunkenness. “A word in its time—oh, how beautiful,” Proverbs
15:23. This is said no less truly of deeds and actions. Finally, in everything
we must observe the time, the place, and the persons with whom we have
dealings.

The opposite of this is precipitancy of mind, which the same Solomon convicts
of the greatest folly, Proverbs 29:20: “Have you seen a man hasty in his
words? There is more hope for a fool than for him.” The same should be declared
of deeds and actions. “A time for every purpose” is taken in a very different
sense in chapter 3 of this Book. Solomon gives the reason why time must be
carefully observed: a person is exposed to so many and such great evils that,
even when he orders his life cautiously and circumspectly, they would utterly
overwhelm and crush him unless he employs judgment and acts cautiously. Hence
somewhere in Job the way of the wicked is said to be strewn, as it were, with
nets and snares, so that unless you watch where you set and plant each foot,
you must necessarily be held entangled.

The first danger is within a person and in himself—that is, in his soul. It is
that ignorance and inexperience in dealing with human beings that may cast a
person into various dangers. The body, moreover, is the workshop of all
diseases. He is not free from danger indoors, nor at night when he gives
himself to rest, where the bed is often a tomb. Houses are tunneled into by
thieves. As he walks in the streets, the danger of death from a falling roof
tile hangs over him. In a garden a serpent and snake lie in wait for him. If he
rides outside, the wavering foot of a horse threatens death or

mutilation; if he makes the journey on foot, robbers attack him; in his food
there may be a pin or needle, and in the pot, death. Solomon amplifies this
evil and the calamitous condition of humankind from the attendant ignorance
of events, by which an evil strikes more grievously when a person neither
thinks of nor expects it. This ignorance of future evils is demonstrated from
its effect: there is no human being, nor any of the blessed Angels, much less
of the accursed ones, who knows how to declare when evils will occur. Isaiah
proves in a long and extended discourse that this is God's prerogative: by
this one thing, namely foretelling the future, the true God is distinguished
from Idols. For that reason judgment and a timely administration of all things
must be employed so that you may avoid these evils. This will happen if you
apply judgment from God's Word.

For just as in Matthew 6 Christ dissuades us from care that torments and
tortures, because each day's evil is sufficient, so in this passage Solomon
exhorts us to a cautious and circumspect manner of life, because human life is
sufficiently afflicted and wretched of itself. Solomon further heightens the
evils that will befall a person: although other evils are uncertain, death,
the greatest of all evils, is nevertheless most certain. For wicked people it
is the entry and vestibule leading to hell itself, and it cannot be avoided by
any power, industry, or skill. See Psalm 49. Here the saying applies that death
is the way of the whole world, Joshua 13:13. Likewise, Hebrews 9 says that it
is appointed for everyone to die.

This is also illustrated by a comparison of corresponding things: death is an
unconquerable war or battle against which no weapons prevail, no oratorical
ability or persuasion, no force, no fruit or herb in gardens, no medicine,
antidote, or amulet. Death knocks with impartial foot at the palaces of kings
and the cottages of the poor; when it knocks, the door must be opened. Finally,
this very point grows stronger by comparison: not even when a person struggles
with all his might and strains every sinew can he escape death's edge. But for
the person who prudently administers the affairs of this life, governing
everything at the right time and with judgment, death comes welcome and
longed-for, as it does to all the godly. In a certain sense it is not death,
since its sting is removed and its point and edge are blunted.

The repeated lemmata here print `experitur` (“experiences”) in verse 4 and
`isti` (“to that person”) in verse 6, where the initial Chapter VIII lemma
block in Part 93 prints `experietur` (“will experience”) and `ipsi` (“to him”).
The transcription retains both variations; the English preserves the tense
change in verse 4 and translates both verse-6 forms “him.”

The witness prints `Iehos. 13. 13` for the saying that death is “the way of the
whole world.” The transcription and translation retain that printed citation
without silently correcting it.

The Latin's `comparato major crescit` is grammatically irregular. The English
renders its contextual force, “this very point grows stronger by comparison,”
without silently altering the transcription.
