VERSE 17. For there will be no remembrance of either the wise man or the fool
forever, since all the things that now exist are consigned to oblivion in days
to come; but the wise man himself dies with the fool.

He now descends to the parts from the whole and to the species from the genus,
and first shows that this occurs in the fact that each is consigned to oblivion
after death, so that Wisdom is no more able to rescue a man from oblivion than
folly is. This is also confirmed from Solomon's Proverbs 10, when he says that
the righteous man will be in everlasting remembrance. By this he declares that
nothing can preserve a pleasing and desirable memory of a man except piety;
without it a man is either buried in perpetual silence or remains infamous and
inglorious forever, so that the condition of the man of whom there is no
remembrance at all among mortals is preferable. To this is added that saying of
the Savior: Rejoice that your names are written in heaven. Luke 10.10. Here,
therefore, that saying is found true: glory flees one who follows it and follows
one who flees it. For the godly flee glory, which they refer to God alone; on
the other hand, the wise of this world, who pursue it above all, lose it.

But here some object that Plato, Aristotle, and others are still memorable for
their learning or their justice. I reply, first, that there have been men who
surpassed them in these virtues, whose name has been extinguished; next, that
infamy is mixed with their praises—for example, that the former [advocated]
community, while the latter, laboring under envy against those whom they ought
to have revered, shamefully exchanged life for death; finally, that their praise
is useful to others but useless to themselves.

He adds another particular, namely, that each dies. And, someone says, this
falls alike to one who fears God and one devoid of divine fear. But it should be
noted that the righteous man does not die in the same manner as others who are
not righteous, even if they should equal Solomon in that worldly wisdom. For
the righteous man enters life through death as through a gate or bridge. John
5.24. Indeed, the man who believes in Christ does not die. John 11.26. For the
death of the godly should not be called death; this is by far the greatest proof
(`τεκμήριον`) of the vanity of wisdom, not to mention folly. And indeed these
outcomes are common to all, while there are others that happen to either of the
two (`εἰς ἑκάτερον`) in the activities of this life.

Sometimes things turn out no better for counsels prudently undertaken than for
those who proceed rashly and inconsiderately. Indeed, what is more, fools and
sluggards sometimes have a more advantageous condition in life than wise men;
and fools are often valued more highly by those in authority than men who have
served the commonwealth well by their wisdom. See chapter 9, verse 13. And just
as this often happens through the envy and perversity of others, so it not
rarely happens through the confidence with which wise men trust in wisdom. The
same must be understood of exile, poverty, bereavement, blindness, captivity,
slavery, and diseases.

From this it also becomes clear that it is foolish always to render judgment
about the goodness of things from their outcome; it is likewise foolish to wish
to remain in folly because at some time it turned out well for you. Finally,
from these things we are taught that we must rely neither upon our own wisdom,
however great it may be, nor upon even our most carefully considered counsels;
rather, all our plans and endeavors must be entrusted to God through prayers,
so that he may direct and lead them to a happy outcome.

*Translator's notes:*

1. The edition prints `Prov. 10.` beside wording that says the righteous will
   be “in everlasting remembrance.” Proverbs 10:7 concerns the blessed memory
   of the righteous, while the wording closely echoes Psalm 112:6 (Psalm 111:7
   in the Vulgate's numbering); the translation retains the printed reference.
2. The edition prints `Luc. 10. 10.` for the saying about names written in
   heaven; the saying occurs at Luke 10:20. The printed citation is retained.
3. The compressed construction `ut quod communitatem ille, hi ...` supplies no
   explicit verb for the former figure, Plato. The translation marks its
   contextual supplement, “[advocated],” rather than silently completing the
   Latin.
4. The Greek `τεκμήριον` means a conclusive proof or sure token, and `εἰς
   ἑκάτερον` means “to either of the two.” Both expressions are printed in Greek
   in the Latin witness.
