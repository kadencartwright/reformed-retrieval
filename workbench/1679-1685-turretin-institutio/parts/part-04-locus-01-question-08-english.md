QUESTION VIII

Is Human Reason the Principle and Norm by Which the Dogmas of the Christian
Religion and Theology That Are the Object of Faith Must Be Tested? We Deny It
against the Socinians.

[Marginal heading: State of the question.]

I. For the state of the question to be perceived more clearly, the terms must
first of all be explained and certain presuppositions laid down. 1. *Human
reason* is taken either subjectively, for the faculty of the rational soul by
which a human being understands and judges intelligible things presented to
him, whether natural or supernatural, divine or human; or objectively, for
the natural light both proposed externally and impressed inwardly upon the
mind, by which reason is disposed to form certain concepts and draw
conclusions about God and divine things. The former can in turn be viewed in
two ways: either as sound and

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 23 | IA leaf 77 | PDF p. 77]

whole before the fall, or as corrupt and blind after it. The *principle* at
issue here must be the first and self-authenticating principle from which all
the truths and articles of faith are first derived and into which they are
finally resolved. Thus all arts have their own principles from which they are
constructed and demonstrated and above which one may not ascend. The *object
of faith* is understood here as the formal object, not the presupposed object,
that is, the articles of saving faith properly and strictly so called, not
the presupposed articles that are common to natural theology and sound reason
itself, such as that God exists, that God is just, wise, and good, that the
soul is immortal, and so forth.

II. In this controversy there has been error both by excess and by defect: by
excess among those who ascribe more than is right to reason in matters of
faith, as the Socinians do; by defect among those who do not ascribe enough,
as the Anabaptists, Lutherans, and papists do. Here we dispute against the
first group; we will deal with the others in the questions that follow.

III. The question is not whether reason has any use in theological matters.
We acknowledge that its use is manifold: for *illustration*, by explaining
divine mysteries from human and earthly things; for *comparison*, by
comparing old things with new, translations with the original texts, and the
opinions of teachers and decrees of councils with the norm of the divine
Word; for *inference*, by connecting consequences; and for *argumentation*,
by drawing reasons from its own storehouse to defend orthodoxy and attack
heterodoxy. The only question is whether it functions as the *principle and
norm* by whose balance the greatest mysteries of religion must be weighed, so
that nothing is to be admitted which does not suit its palate, is not founded
upon reason, and is not elicited from reason. We deny this against the
Socinians. To reject more easily the mysteries of the Trinity, the
incarnation, Christ's satisfaction, and other things of this kind that are
most clearly revealed in Scripture, they contend that reason is the norm of
religion and of things to be believed, and that things which appear
impossible to the mind are not to be believed. Ostorodt says in
*Institutes*, chapter 6, “A human being is not bound to believe what reason
dictates to be false.” And Smalzius says against Frantzius, “If religion were
opposed to reason, by that very fact it would not be religion, for religion
is the highest and very reason itself.”

IV. The question is not whether reason is the *instrument by which*, or the
*medium through which*, we can be brought to faith. We acknowledge that
reason can be both: the former always and everywhere, but the latter when
presupposed articles are in question. Rather, the question is whether it is
the *principle from which* the dogmas of faith are proved, or the
*foundation upon which* they rest, so that whatever natural light or human
reason does not grasp must be considered false in matters of faith. We deny
this.

[Marginal heading: Reason is not the principle of faith.]

V. The reasons are as follows. 1. The reason of an unregenerate person is
blind concerning the law, Ephesians 4:17–18; Romans 1:27–28 and 8:7, and is
utterly blind and sheer darkness concerning the gospel, Ephesians 5:8; 1
Corinthians 2:14. It must therefore be taken captive and subjected to faith,
not exalted to rule over it, 2 Corinthians 10:3–4. 2. The mysteries of faith
are above the sphere of reason, and the natural person cannot rise to them.
Just as sense does not assume judgment over things set above sense, neither
does reason assume judgment over things that surpass reason and are
supernatural. 3. Faith is not finally resolved into reason, so that I ought
to believe because I understand and grasp it so, but into the Word, because
God speaks so in Scripture. 4. The Holy Spirit directs us to the Word alone,
Deuteronomy 4:1; Isaiah 8:20; John 5:39; 2 Timothy 3:15–16; 2 Peter 1:19. 5.
If reason were the principle of faith, first, it would follow that all
religion is natural and demonstrable by natural reason and light. Nature and
grace, and natural and supernatural revelation, would thus be confused.
Second, it would follow that reason must nowhere be taken captive and denied,
contrary to the

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 24 | IA leaf 78 | PDF p. 78]

express passages of Scripture, and that those endowed with more acute reason
and a more cultivated intellect can better perceive and judge the mysteries of faith,
contrary to universal experience, 1 Corinthians 1:19–20; Matthew 11:25. 6.
Reason cannot be the norm of religion: not as corrupt, because it is not only
beneath faith but also opposed to it, Romans 8:7; 1 Corinthians 2:14; Matthew
16:17; nor as sound, because such reason does not exist in a corrupt human
being, and even in an unfallen human being it could not have been the norm of
supernatural mysteries. Nor, when it is now corrected by the Spirit, must
judgment be made according to reason itself, but according to the first
principle that illuminated reason now admits, namely, Scripture.

[Marginal heading: Sources of the replies.]

VI. It is one thing to function ministerially and instrumentally, another to
function principally and despotically. Reason functions in the former manner
with respect to theology, not in the latter. It is the servant Hagar, who
must be subjected to Scripture, not the mistress Sarah, who is to be set over
it. Reason must test the things proposed for belief against Holy Scripture as
the inflexible norm of truth, just as we use hand and eye to test the things
we wish to measure against the public ell; but reason itself cannot and must
not be constituted the norm of things to be believed.

VII. The *instrument of faith* is one thing, the *foundation of faith*
another. Inferring something that must be believed is one thing; bringing out
what is to be understood and explained from what has been stated is another,
not by putting something into the text, but by expounding what appeared
enveloped in it. Reason is an instrument the believer uses, but it is not the
foundation and principle upon which faith rests. If the use of reason is
commended in various passages of Scripture, this does not immediately make
it the foundation of faith, as if I ought to act from reason as a norm. It
indicates only reason's ministry, so that believers operate according to and
through it as an instrument.

VIII. *Rational worship* is spoken of in two ways: either originally, when it
arises from reason as its source and principle; or subjectively and
instrumentally, when it is situated in reason as its subject and exercised
through reason as its instrument. When Paul prescribes rational worship for
believers in Romans 12:1, he does not understand it in the first way, for
then he would approve the will-worship that he condemns elsewhere. He
understands it in the second way, for worship that is situated in reason and
exercised through reason—that is, worship that is spiritual and internal,
not carnal and external—in contrast to the Levitical and ceremonial worship
of the Old Testament, which was carnal in the offering of brute animals. Now
God no longer requires brute animals, but rational and spiritual sacrifices,
as Peter calls them in 1 Peter 2:5.

IX. Christ can be said to have taught rationally either with respect to the
kind of doctrine or with respect to the manner of teaching. If it is
understood in the first way, which is at issue here, it is false; indeed, he
brought doctrine opposed and unknown to blind reason, Matthew 16:17. The
second manner does not pertain here, for we acknowledge that reason functions
as an instrument.

X. Drawing doctrine from nature is one thing; illustrating doctrine already
known in some way, or taking an occasion for teaching from nature, is
another. We acknowledge the latter in the Lord's parables, but not the
former. For through the parables he did not expressly prove his mysteries,
but only illustrated them so that they might be perceived more easily under
these emblems.

XI. The truth of propositions is one thing, the truth of connections
another, as Augustine observes in *On Christian Doctrine* 2.32. The former
corresponds to axiomatic judgment, the latter to dianoetic judgment. Divine
revelation in Scripture dictates to us the axioms or propositions of faith.
Therefore, where they surpass our grasp, they must simply be believed on the
authority of that infallible Master of propositions, whose “he himself said
it” must be held in all things. But right reason apprehends the truth of
connections and judges in itself what follows from another thing.

XII. Apprehending the rationale of a consequence is one thing, apprehending

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 25 | IA leaf 79 | PDF p. 79]

the consequent itself another. The rationale of a consequence is often
apprehended when neither the
antecedent is apprehended nor the consequent comprehended; one understands
only that this follows from that. Faith apprehends the consequent, but reason
the consequence. For it belongs to reason to apprehend the rationale of the
consequence, whether it is right and necessary or otherwise. It does not
follow from this that the faith by which the consequent is apprehended rests
upon reason, because reason here is not the argument but the instrument. In
the same way, when faith is said to come from hearing, hearing is not the
argument of faith but its instrument. For reason does not introduce into the
text a sense that was not there; through a legitimate consequence it draws
out something that lay hidden in the text and was therefore taught there
implicitly.

XIII. In matters of faith, reason functions not only in the manner of an
instrument *by which*, but also sometimes as a medium and an argument *from
which* the theologian argues, when he draws arguments for the faith from his
own storehouse. He may argue on behalf of the principles, showing their
credibility to those who do not acknowledge the faith, or argue from the
principles, producing arguments from nature to prove or confirm theological
conclusions. Hence the same conclusion is a matter of faith insofar as it is
proved from Scripture, but a matter of knowledge insofar as it is
demonstrated from reason. Yet it cannot be inferred from this that reason has
the character of the principle and norm against which the dogmas of faith
must be tested.

[Marginal heading: Mixed syllogisms are matters of faith.]

XIV. In mixed syllogisms, where one proposition is from faith and the other
from reason, reason in this respect is not the foundation and norm upon which
the conclusion rests, but only the medium and instrument by whose operation
the truth lying virtually hidden in the other premise is elicited.
Accordingly, in syllogisms of this kind the middle term is taken not from
reason but from Scripture. When an adversary denies the connection of the
middle with the major extreme, however, that connection is shown through the
principles of reason—not to corroborate the truth of the middle, but the
truth of the connection. For example, I deny that Christ's glorious body is
everywhere by taking from Scripture this middle term, that it is a true body.
The major premise, however—that no body is everywhere—is taken from reason.
Thus, in arguments of this kind, the conclusion that emerges is theological
from the inferential middle and logical from reason, which joins the
consequence. One of the premises, which is from faith, communicates its force
to the conclusion to such a degree that, as to its matter and consequent, the
conclusion rests upon revelation alone, although as to its form and the mode
of the consequence it depends upon reason.

XV. Nor should it be replied here that the conclusion always follows the
weaker part, that is, reason, which is weaker than divine revelation. For 1.
that axiom must not be extended beyond quantity and quality: if one premise
is universal and the other particular, or one affirmative and the other
negative, the conclusion must always be particular or negative. 2. According
to most, the conclusion follows the weaker part when it follows revelation,
which is not evident, at least speaking philosophically. Here the proposition
that rests upon authority is judged weaker and less evident than the one that
rests upon reason, because it is further removed from scientific
demonstration.

XVI. Hence we rightly conclude that in mixed syllogisms of this kind the
conclusion is theological and a matter of faith, because every proposition
takes its designation from its subject, not from its predicate. Therefore,
when the subject is theological, the proposition is theological even though
its predicate is philosophical or is derived from natural light.
Consequently, the assent given to conclusions of this kind is the assent of
faith rather than of knowledge, because assent follows the nature of the
proposition to which it is given. Thus, if the proposition is philosophical,
it has an evident assent toward which the mind is inclined by the light of
reason and knowledge. If it is theological, however, it has the assent of
faith toward which the mind is inclined by the light of revelation.
Therefore, although assent to the conclusion arises

[1847 vol. I, printed p. 26 | IA leaf 80 | PDF p. 80]

from both propositions, nevertheless, because the character of the
proposition derived from theology is more powerful than that of the
proposition obtained from philosophy, that assent is said to pertain simply
to theology and not to philosophy.

XVII. Reason is taken either materially, for the kind of doctrine sought from
the light of reason, or formally, for the manner of delivering it, which is
commonly called the “method of instruction.” But in neither way can it be
called the principle of theology: not in the former way, because theology is
neither constructed from reason nor resolved into it; not in the latter,
because although in this sense reason is an instrument, as has been said, it
still cannot be considered a principle.

XVIII. Being *contrary to reason* is one thing, being *above and beyond it*
another; being overthrown by reason is one thing, being unknown by it
another. The mysteries of faith are indeed contrary to corrupt reason and
are attacked by it, but they are only above and beyond right reason and are
not taught by it. Thus in neither way can reason be called their principle.

XIX. Although the things of faith agree with reason and no dogma conflicts
with right and illuminated reason, it does not follow that they agree with
corrupt and blind reason, or that even sound reason is their principle. For
agreement alone does not make something a principle; dependence does, as
exists between cause and effect.

XX. The proper norm of things to be believed and not believed is not a sense
of possibility or impossibility, but the Word of God. For the only things
possible to God are not those that appear so to the human mind. He can do
more than we can think, Ephesians 3:20; Matthew 19:26, and it would be impious
to circumscribe God's infinite power by the narrow limits of the finite mind.

XXI. Although light is not contrary to light, and natural and revealed truth
do not conflict with each other, natural truth itself is nevertheless often
not what human reason dictates, since reason often goes astray by abusing
natural and revealed light. Revealed truth can therefore be opposed to human
reasoning and concepts, although it agrees with natural truth, which reason
often neither sees nor grasps. Here the first principles of nature known
through themselves must be distinguished from the conclusions and concepts
of reason drawn from those principles. The former are true and certain, but
the latter are obscure, often erroneous, and fallible.

XXII. A small and a great light can differ in degree and in kind: in degree
with respect to a natural object, and in kind with respect to a supernatural
object. Reason can be a small light, but in civil and natural matters, not in
supernatural ones.

XXIII. Although reason is not the principle of faith, it does not follow that
atheists cannot be converted. The method of dealing with them can be either
theological, through arguments inherent in Scripture, or philosophical, so
that prejudices against the Christian religion drawn from corrupt reason may
be removed by principles of reason.

XXIV. The Lutherans wrongly charge us with making reason the principle and
norm of demonstration in controversies because we sometimes seek arguments
from reason's resources and attack the ubiquity of Christ's body from reason.
For we ascribe to reason only ministerial and instrumental functions, not
principal ones. If in mixed questions we use reason to prove something, it
has the character not of a principle, but of a medium from which the
theologian argues; and for us these are not primary arguments, but only
secondary and auxiliary resources. Add that when a theologian uses arguments
drawn from reason, he acts not so much as a theologian as a philosopher. As
for the ubiquity of Christ's body, we reject this dogma both philosophically
and theologically, because it is absurd and contradicts the principles of
theology and philosophy.
