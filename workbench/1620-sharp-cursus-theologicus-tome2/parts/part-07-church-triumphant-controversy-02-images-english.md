## CONTROVERSY II: On the Worship of Images

It is agreed between us and the Papists that there is a threefold legitimate
use of images. First, a civil use, because the arts of the painter and sculptor
are from God, Exodus 35:30. This civil use consists, first, in ornamenting
buildings; second, in distinguishing coinage, as in Matthew 22; third, in
retaining in memory friends and departed saints. This use was at first private
and merely civil, from which a superstitious abuse afterward crept in little by
little, namely, that images should be set up publicly and afterward also
adored. Second, a historical use for representing things done; thus histories
may be depicted publicly and privately, but outside sacred places. Third, a
religious use, by which it is lawful to construct an image to bear witness to
God’s presence and to signify the effects of God’s majesty. Thus Moses
presented figures of serpents in the wilderness; thus the cherubim were in
the temple and

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 26 | printed col. 52 | bsb10175568]

tabernacle. We say, however, that this religious use can take place only when
and insofar as God commands it. Thus it is said in the second commandment,
*You shall not make for yourself*, and so forth.

## QUESTION I

We differ from the Papists, first, over the difference they establish between
an image, an idol, and a likeness. For Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 5, *On the
Church Militant*, distinguishes them as follows: an image is a true likeness
of a thing, but an idol a false one, because it represents that which does not
actually exist. Thus he says that images of Christ and the saints are not
idols, nor are images of the Trinity or angels, because they are not made to
represent the very nature of the Trinity or angels, but to express the form in
which they appeared, or their attributes. He maintains that “likeness” is a
more general term, so called from similitude—or, more accurately, from
simulating—and therefore is used in Scripture for idols.

We, however, say that these words signify the same thing and that the
difference is only in use, because an idol is nothing other than an image to
which some religious worship is rendered. Thus the serpent in the wilderness
was at first a typological image of Christ, Numbers 31:9 and John 4; but when
the people rendered idolatrous worship to the serpent, it became an idol and
was therefore destroyed, 2 Kings 18:4. That there is no difference except in
use is proved as follows.

Argument 1. From the etymology of the words. According to Bellarmine, “image”
is derived from “imitating,” because it imitates the outward appearance of a
thing; likewise “likeness” is derived from “similitude,” and εἴδωλον from
εἶδος, that is, “form.” They differ in no other way

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 27 | printed col. 53 | bsb10175568]

than that εἴδωλον is a Greek word, while the other two words are Latin.

Argument 2. Where the seventy translators have εἴδωλον in the Greek text, the
Latins rendered it “likeness,” which is the same as “image,” Acts 7:41; 15:20;
1 Corinthians 12:2; 1 John 5:21; and elsewhere. In these passages the Vulgate
translator renders εἴδωλον as “likeness.” Therefore.

Argument 3. Just as to exhibit divine honor to a creature is to commit
idolatry, so that to which divine honor is rendered is properly an idol, 1
Chronicles 10:7. But the Papists exhibit divine honor to images and likenesses,
such as trust, invocation, vows, and adoration. Therefore they are properly
called idols, and consequently Bellarmine’s distinction is futile.

Argument 4. If those who adored Christ as only a true man were idolaters, and
thus made an idol out of Christ’s human nature, then much more are those who
adore images of Christ and the saints idolaters, and those images are idols,
because Christ’s human nature itself approaches the divinity more closely than
his images do. But the former is true, because the Arians, who adored Christ
as a mere man, were called idolaters by the Council of Nicaea, and the
Nestorians were called ἀνθρωπολάτραι, that is, worshipers of a man. See the
Second Council of Nicaea, action 7, and Athanasius, oration 5 against the
Arians. Therefore, consequently, idols and images are the same in reality.

Argument 5. The idols of the Gentiles represented that which really had
existed, such as human beings, beasts, and so forth, which were regarded as
gods; and images of God and angels represent that which really does not

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 27 | printed col. 54 | bsb10175568]

exist, because God is not in reality an old man, nor are angels winged infants,
as they imagine them. Therefore Bellarmine’s distinction between an idol and
an image is futile.

Argument 6. Profane writers use these words interchangeably. Thus Cicero, book
1, *On Ends*, calls atoms images and idols, and so do others. Therefore.

Argument 7. Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 11, *On the Church Triumphant*, calls
images of Christ idols: “It is certain,” he says, “that Alexander Severus
worshiped images of Christ along with his other idols,” and so forth.
Therefore.

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. Scripture nowhere assigns the name “idol” to an image, but only
to the likenesses of the Gentiles that represented false gods. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because those likenesses were true images,
for they expressed the appearance of the thing which the idolaters fashioned
in their mind. Furthermore, the images of the Gentiles were called idols only
because of their use. Finally, even likenesses made by the Jews were called
idols, Exodus 32:4; Acts 7:41; and elsewhere, and according to Bellarmine,
book 2, chapter 13. Indeed, the idols of the Gentiles were established to
represent the true God, Acts 17:23–24.

Objection 2. Scripture calls the Son of God the image of the Father, Hebrews
1:3; Colossians 1:15; and Solomon is said to have made images to ornament the
temple, 1 Kings 7. Yet these are never called idols. Therefore.

Response. In Hebrews 1, neither the word “image” nor “likeness” occurs in the
Vulgate version. Furthermore, in Colossians 1 Christ is called the image of the
Father essentially, but the question concerns an accidental image. Therefore
this

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 28 | printed col. 55 | bsb10175568]

is a fallacy of equivocation in the word “image.” Those who wished to adore
Christ as man with divine worship were called idolaters, as was shown above.
Furthermore, the images made by Solomon were idols if you consider the meaning
of the word, though not if you consider the abuse on account of which words
are often used in a bad sense.

Objection 3. In Scripture idols are designated by names that signify that they
represent what does not really exist, as they are called in Leviticus 19:1.

Response. Those names are assigned neither from their matter nor from their
form, but from their use—or rather, from their effect—and they are assigned
not only to idols but also to human beings, as in Isaiah 41:29; Psalm 62:10;
and Job 13:4.

Objection 4. From 1 Corinthians 8:4: an idol is nothing—namely, formally, not
materially—since it represents what is nothing, and therefore neither
represents nor exists. Therefore.

Response. There is multiple equivocation in this argument. The first is in the
word “idol,” for, first, it signifies a likeness or image by which some deity
is represented or worshiped, as in Revelation 9:20; second, the worship
fashioned for a likeness, as in 1 Chronicles 16:26. The second equivocation is
in the verb “is,” which signifies two things: first, the substance itself
composed of matter and form, as when we say “a man is”; second, an accident of
the thing, namely, its force and efficacy, as in 1 Corinthians 7:19. The third
is in the word “nothing,” which is taken in two ways: first, properly, as in
Matthew 11:13; second, figuratively, by emphasis and hyperbole in a comparison,
as in Galatians 6:3 and chapter 2:6, 9. But Bellarmine takes these three in the
former sense, whereas the Apostle takes them in the latter.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 28 | printed col. 56 | bsb10175568]

## QUESTION II

The second difference between us and the Papists is that Bellarmine asserts in
chapter 8 that images of God are not prohibited, and then that it is lawful to
fashion an image of God the Father in the form of an old man, and the Holy
Spirit in the form of a dove.

We, however, say that it is unlawful and impossible to paint or sculpt images
of God, which is proved by the following arguments.

Argument 1. There is a prohibition against anyone representing God by an
image, Deuteronomy 4:15, 23; Isaiah 40:18; 41:7; 45:9; 46:5; Romans 1:21;
Exodus 20:4, 22; 1 Kings 12:28; Acts 17:29; and elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument 2. What God showed the faithful by word and deed when promulgating the
law, he wished to be regarded as the rule of duty, Deuteronomy 4:12, 15. But he
showed no likeness there, and John 1:18. Therefore.

Argument 3. God is incorporeal, infinite, incomprehensible, and so forth, so
that not even creatures themselves can express or delineate him. Therefore
much less can images made by human beings do so.

Argument 4. Images made of God are and have been a cause of idolatry and
punishments, Exodus 32 and frequently elsewhere. Therefore.

Argument 5. God ought not to be represented by that which has nothing like
him. But in an image there is nothing like God, Acts 17:29, because there is no
correspondence in matter, form, or anything else. Therefore.

Argument 6. Images of God are teachers of vanity, lies, filth, and so forth,
Deuteronomy 7:26; Jeremiah 10:3; Habakkuk 2:18–19, but are not a means by which
human beings are led, through analogical likeness, to some

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 29 | printed col. 57 | bsb10175568]

knowledge of God.

Argument 7. The Council of Elvira, canon 36, forbids images of God to be kept
in churches. Damasus, book 4, chapter 17, *On the Orthodox Faith*, says that
Christians have no image of God because he is incorporeal, invisible, and so
forth. Bellarmine himself admits that many Papists assert that an image of God
is not rightly made and is perhaps tolerated by the Church, but not approved.
He then says that it is not as certain in the Church whether images of God or
the Trinity are to be made as it is concerning images of the saints and
Christ, because the latter is certain, while the former is only a matter of
opinion. Therefore.

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. Angels are incorporeal, and yet in the Old Testament they are
sculpted and painted, Exodus 25:17; 1 Kings 6. Therefore, although God is
incorporeal, he too can be depicted.

Response. The inference is denied, because painting and sculpting cherubim was
commanded by God, whereas fashioning any likeness of God was forbidden,
Deuteronomy 4 and elsewhere as above. Therefore this does not follow: “Images
of angels were depicted by God’s command; therefore human beings can depict
God contrary to God’s command.” A similar argument would be: “It was lawful
for the Israelites to carry away the goods of the Egyptians; therefore it is
lawful for us today.” This does not follow. Furthermore, those images were in
the sanctuary, beyond the danger of idolatry, but the Papists’ images are
exposed to everyone’s eyes.

Objection 2. God was seen in bodily form, Genesis 3:8; 28:13; Exodus 33:23;
24:10; Isaiah 6:1; 1 Kings 22:19; Amos 9:1; Daniel 7:9; Hosea 12:11; the
penultimate verse of Matthew 3; and angels often appeared in human form. As
they appeared, they can

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 29 | printed col. 58 | bsb10175568]

be represented. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because this is forbidden, as was proved
above. Furthermore, those appearances were merely signs of God’s presence for
a time. Therefore their likeness is not to be imitated in perpetuity. Third,
if God ought to be painted because he appeared in human form, why not also in
the form of cloud, fire, and so forth, by which he signified his presence?
Finally, in many of the passages cited by Bellarmine it is not said that God
appeared in human form. Thus they are wrongly cited by him. In the other
passages, however, where he is described in human form, there are dreams and
visions, not events.

Objection 3. Scripture attributes human members to God. Therefore a picture of
him such as he is depicted can be exhibited.

Response. Bellarmine concedes that in Scripture human members are attributed
to God metaphorically. “Therefore God ought to be depicted in that way” does
not follow. Rather, on the contrary, Scripture speaks thus
ἀνθρωποπαθῶς—anthropopathically—about God so that we may conceive something
spiritual about him, namely, his power, justice, and so forth.

Objection 4. Things are painted which seem less capable of being painted than
God himself, namely, virtues, which are not only spiritual but also accidents.
Therefore.

Response. The inference is denied, because it is lawful to paint those things,
but it is forbidden to represent God by an effigy. Furthermore, virtues are
finite and comprehensible things, but God is infinite and incomprehensible.
Furthermore, this does not follow: “Accidents can be painted; therefore God
can be painted.”

Objection 5. A human being is the true image of God, but an image of a human
being can be painted. Therefore an image of God can also be painted,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 30 | printed col. 59 | bsb10175568]

for that which is the image of an image is also an image of its exemplar,
because things similar to one and the same third thing are also similar to one
another.

Response. There are four terms, for the image of God and the image of human
beings are not the same. An image of human beings that is painted contains
only the outlines of the body, but these outlines are not the image of God.
Indeed, contrary to Bellarmine’s position, not even the whole human being is
the image of God; rather, the whole human being was created according to the
image of God, which is situated in righteousness, holiness, and dominion.

Objection 6. Images of God have now been received by the Church almost
everywhere.

Response. By this very statement he concedes that the whole Church does not
receive images of God. Furthermore, the true Church ought not receive them
because of God’s prohibitions, and when the Church of the Old or New Testament
fashioned images of God, it erred, as in Exodus 32:1 and elsewhere. Therefore.

Objection 7. Images of God are made so that minds may be stirred to think lofty
things about God, because we are often distracted by worldly cares and forget
our duty. Therefore images of God ought at least to be made as reminders.

Response. The inference is denied, because although we need reminders lest we
forget our duty toward God, we do not need those reminders which are forbidden
by God. Furthermore, he has given as many reminders as are necessary: all
creatures, Psalm 19; Romans 1; and elsewhere; likewise the Word and sacraments.
Therefore other unlawful reminders are not to be fashioned.

## QUESTION III: On the Worship of Images of Christ and the Saints

The third difference between us and the Papists concerns the worship of
images.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 30 | printed col. 60 | bsb10175568]

Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 12, relying on the Council of Trent, session 25,
asserts that images of Christ and the saints are to be honored, provided,
however, that no trust is placed in the images, nothing is requested from
them, and no divinity is believed to be present in them; rather, they are
honored only because of those whom they represent to us. In chapter 20 he
admits that the Papists do not agree among themselves about the kind of worship
and lists three opinions. The first is that of Durandus, book 3 of the
*Sentences*, distinction 9, Alphonso de Castro, and others: that an image is
not to be worshiped in any way, but only the exemplar is to be worshiped before
the image.

The second is that of Thomas, part 3, question 25: that the same honor is owed
to the image and to the exemplar, and therefore that an image of Christ is to
be adored with the worship of latria, since the honor of the image passes to
the exemplar. The third is that images ought to be honored in themselves and
properly, but with a lesser honor than the exemplar itself. Bellarmine,
however, uses a threefold distinction here: namely, something is honored,
first, either through itself or incidentally; second, either for its own sake
or for the sake of another; third, either properly or improperly. In chapter
21 he says that images are to be venerated through themselves and properly, so
that they themselves terminate the veneration, and not as though they occupy
the place of the exemplar. In chapter 22 he says that no images are to be
adored with the worship of latria, and in chapter 23 he says it can be admitted
that images are worshiped incidentally with the same kind of worship as the
exemplar: images of God and Christ with the worship of latria, of Mary with
the worship of hyperdulia, and of the saints with the worship of dulia,
analogically and reductively.

We, however, say that it is lawful to have images of Christ as man and of the
saints, but that they are not to be adored with religious worship. We prove
this with the following arguments.

First. If God alone is to be adored with religious

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 31 | printed col. 61 | bsb10175568]

worship, then images of Christ and the saints are not. But the former is true,
Exodus 20:4–5; Deuteronomy 5:9; 6:13; Matthew 4:10; and elsewhere. Therefore.

Second. If nothing in heaven above is to be represented by an image so that we
may worship it, then neither are images of Christ and the saints, who are in
heaven. But the former is true by the second commandment of the Decalogue.
Therefore.

Third. If one must abstain in worship not only from the idols of the Gentiles
but also from likenesses, then likenesses or images of the saints are not to
be made for the sake of worship. But the former is true, 1 Corinthians 10:15;
1 Peter 4:3; 1 John 5:21, where the Vulgate edition uses the word “likeness.”
Therefore Christians are forbidden to worship not only idols but also
likenesses, contrary to what the Papists maintain.

Fourth. If God prohibits the making of a thing itself, its adoration is
unlawful. But the very making of images for religion is forbidden,
Deuteronomy 4:15, 23; Exodus 26:22. Therefore.

Fifth. If the Papists’ images which they worship are idols, then worshiping
them is idolatry and therefore unlawful. But the former is true. Therefore.
The assumption is proved: all images made by human beings, when they are
worshiped and adored, are idols, Genesis 31:34; Judges 18:31; Hosea 4:17;
13:2; 14:9; Acts 7:41. But the Papists’ images are worshiped and adored.
Furthermore, everything said about idols in Psalm 115:4 and elsewhere applies
to the Papists’ images. Therefore.

Sixth. Whatever the Apostle condemns in the Gentiles themselves is much less
lawful for Christians. But he condemns among the Gentiles the adoration of
images which represented God himself, Romans 1:25, where, contrary to the
Pontificians’ distinction, he calls the Gentiles’ idols “images,” verse 23;
Acts 17:23; and elsewhere. Therefore.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 31 | printed col. 62 | bsb10175568]

Seventh. If worship of images made by God’s command was unlawful, much more is
worship of images which he did not command to be made. But the former is true,
2 Kings 18:4.

Eighth. Neither in the Old nor in the New Testament is there an example or
command for the adoration of images. Therefore that adoration occurs without
faith and so is sin, Romans 14, last verse.

Ninth. If even human beings, however holy, are not to be adored, much less are
their images, since human beings are more excellent than images. But the former
is true, Acts 10:26; Revelation 19:10; 22:8.

Tenth. Images erected for the sake of worship are false teachers, teachers of
vanity, and so forth, Jeremiah 10:8; Habakkuk 2:18; Zechariah 10:2. Therefore.

Eleventh. If there are as many mutually different images of Christ and the
saints as there are painters and sculptors—indeed, as many as the times they
paint, as experience teaches—then either those images are not to be adored,
because they are not images of Christ and the saints, or that which is not
truly an image of Christ and the saints is to be adored, unless you say that
Christ and the saints truly can have infinitely many diverse images. But
neither can be said. Therefore.

Twelfth. The councils of Elvira, canon 36; Constantinople under Leo the
Iconoclast; Constantinople under Constantine Copronymus; and Frankfurt
rejected images, while the councils which the Pontificians cite in favor of
images are recent. Likewise, Epiphanius, in his letter to John of Jerusalem,
says that it is contrary to the custom of the Church for an image of Christ or
another saint to be seen hanging in the church; and Jerome, on Daniel,
chapter 3,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 32 | printed col. 63 | bsb10175568]

says, *Worshipers of God ought not adore images.* Pope Gregory, writing to the
bishop of Marseille who had cast down adored images, says, *We approve that
you forbade them to be adored.*

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. There are certain passages which expressly concern images
instituted by God, Exodus 25:10; Numbers 21:8. But if it was lawful to venerate
images of angels, why not also images of the saints? And if it was lawful to
venerate an image of Christ in the form of a serpent, then also in the form of
a man. Therefore.

Response. First, this does not follow: “Certain passages concern images;
therefore they are to be adored with religious worship.” Second, it is denied
that it was lawful to venerate the cherubim or the bronze serpent. Indeed, when
the bronze serpent was received with religious worship, it was demolished, 2
Kings 18:4. Third, this does not follow: “It was lawful to honor images by
God’s command; therefore it is also lawful to honor other images erected by
human beings contrary to God’s command.”

A similar argument would be: “It was lawful for Abraham, who had God’s command,
to sacrifice his son; therefore the same is lawful for others.” It does not
follow.

Instance 1. The images of the cherubim situated over the ark were necessarily
adored by those who adored the ark. Therefore.

Response. There is a twofold falsehood in the antecedent, because it is nowhere
said that the Israelites adored the ark. Furthermore, this does not follow:
“They adored the ark; therefore they adored the cherubim situated over the
ark.” A similar argument would be: “We adore Christ in heaven; therefore we
adore heaven or the other things in heaven.” Third, the ark and cherubim were
removed from the common sight of human beings. Finally, they were types of
future things, but an argument from abolished ceremonies is not valid

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 32 | printed col. 64 | bsb10175568]

for establishing a new worship without God’s command.

Instance 2. Augustine says that useful signs divinely instituted are to be
reverenced because their honor passes to the prototype. Therefore.

Response. Images of Christ and the saints are not divinely instituted.
Furthermore, we deny that honor rendered to images passes to Christ and the
saints. Rather, on the contrary, Christ and the saints do not wish religious
worship to be transferred elsewhere than it ought. Finally, the honor of a
sign pertains to the thing signified when that honor accords with the will of
the one to be honored, because honor depends upon the will of the one to be
honored, not of the one honoring.

Objection 2. Scripture teaches that creatures are to be honored religiously
solely because of their relation to God, Psalm 99:5; Matthew 5:34. Here note
that an oath is an act of religion in which God is honored primarily, and
secondarily that by which the oath is taken. But images have a similar
relation. Therefore they are to be worshiped on the same principle.

Response. This is a false interpretation of the passages. Psalm 99 says,
“Worship at his footstool,” that is, prostrate on the ground in the place of
worship. Furthermore, Matthew 5 forbids all oaths made otherwise than by
interposing the name of God. Since, therefore, the relation is similar, as
Bellarmine maintains, it entirely forbids the religious adoration of images.

Objection 3. Creatures are called sacred or holy because of their relation to
sacred things, as the ground is called holy in Exodus 3, the day of Passover
in Exodus 12, and Scripture is called sacred in 2 Timothy 3. But images of
Christ and the saints are also instituted to signify sacred things. Therefore
it is lawful to honor them.

Response. First, the assumption is denied,

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 33 | printed col. 65 | bsb10175568]

because they are not instituted by God, but by human beings contrary to God’s
Word. Furthermore, even if they were instituted to represent sacred things,
they would nevertheless no more be to be adored than Moses adored the ground,
the Jews the day of Passover, the people their sacrifices, or we the Bible.
Finally, it is one thing to honor and another to worship and adore religiously.

Objection 4. God performs many miracles through images of Christ and the
saints. Therefore it pleases him that images be honored.

Response. First, the antecedent is denied, because what the Papists offer to
confirm it are partly false and partly fabricated miracles, and are the lies
of Antichrist, 2 Thessalonians 2:9. But not even true miracles contrary to
God’s Word are to be believed, Deuteronomy 1:13 and following. Furthermore,
the point in question does not follow. Finally, there are many things through
which God performs miracles which nevertheless are not to be adored with
religious worship: God performed miracles through true prophets, Aaron’s rod,
the waters of Jordan, and even through false prophets.

Objection 5. A human being is worthy of veneration because he is the image of
God. Therefore images of Christ and the saints are also worthy. The inference
is proved: what applies to one image because it is an image necessarily
applies to them all. Or thus: as a human being is related to God, whose image
he is, so a picture is related to Christ, whose image it is. Therefore if a
human being is honorable because he is the image of an honorable thing, then
an image of Christ is also honorable.

Response. The inference is denied, because the case of a human being formed by
God according to God’s image is different from that of images formed by human
beings contrary to God’s command. Second, a human being, insofar as he is the
image of God, cannot be painted; and although he is the image of God, he is not
adored with religious worship, but is honored only civilly.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 33 | printed col. 66 | bsb10175568]

Third, it is false to say that what applies to one image because it is an image
applies to them all, because images instituted by God have one character and
those instituted by human beings contrary to God’s command have another.
Finally, it is false to say that an image of Christ is related to Christ in
the same way that a human being is related to God.

Objection 6. The image of a king is venerable with a certain civil honor.
Therefore images of Christ are venerable with religious worship.

Response. The inference is denied, because we honor the image of a king only
civilly, to attest due obedience, but religious worship of images is
forbidden, nor do we honor the saints by honoring images. Finally, we do not
even bend the knee to the image of a king. Therefore.

Objection 7. An image is capable of receiving injury and insult. Therefore it
is capable of receiving honor and worship.

Response. The inference is denied, because contraries are rightly attributed
to contraries only when the contrariety of the predications depends upon the
very respect in which they are opposed. Thus, in the case of any sign, an
intention to offend God is sufficient; but honor does not follow from honor
shown to a sign unless the sign and its honor are ordained by God, because he
is to be adored only in the manner he instituted. Furthermore, although signs
instituted by God are to be honored, they are nevertheless not to be adored
with worship. Finally, injury done to images which someone does not in reality
approve does not result in insult to him. For example, if someone threw a
counterfeit coin bearing the king’s image into the fire, he would affect the
king with honor rather than disgrace. In the same way, God is honored when
idols are broken.

Objection 8. We rightly break the idols of the Gentiles because they are
images of false gods. Therefore, conversely, images of Christ and the saints
are honorable because they signify good and holy things.

Response. The point in question does not follow.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 34 | printed col. 67 | bsb10175568]

Furthermore, the inference is denied, because they signify not by divine
institution, but rather contrary to God’s institution. Finally, this does not
follow: “They are to be honored; therefore they are to be adored with
worship.”

Objection 9. Those who rejected images were all either Jews, Samaritans,
Muslims, or heretics, while those who defended images were holy men. But it is
not credible that God wished to teach the Church through enemies rather than
friends. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because many Fathers and councils rightly
rejected images. Furthermore, not everything that some good men defended is
true, because even the holiest had their blemishes. Finally, this argument is
invalid: “The Turks and other wicked people reject idols; therefore idols are
good.” For the Turks abhor adultery, drunkenness, and so forth, yet these
things are not good.

Objection 10. If it is lawful to honor the name of Christ, then it is lawful
to honor his image. But the former is true, Philippians 2:10, and those who
honor the name honor not its letters and syllables, but Christ. Therefore.

Response. This is a fallacy arising from the form of expression, because
genuflection at the name of Christ does not signify such submission at the
pronunciation of the name, but spiritual veneration of Christ. Furthermore,
the proposition is denied, because honor of Christ’s name has a command, but
nowhere is it said that every knee is to bend before images of Christ.

Objection 11. Jacob adored the end of his staff, that is, the image situated
at its end, Hebrews 11:21.

Response. This is a false interpretation of the passage, for it does not say
that he adored the staff, but that he adored while leaning upon the end of his
staff, because he was now old.

Objection 12. If those who prostrate themselves before an image do nothing
different from those who pray without an image, then it is lawful to form and
adore images. But the former is true, because both form images—the latter
inwardly, the former

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 34 | printed col. 68 | bsb10175568]

outwardly—and just as it is not idolatry to form an image of Christ in the
mind, neither is it idolatry to form one outwardly. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, and its proof is false, because those who
pray form no image of Christ for themselves, but ask benefits from God only
because of Christ’s merits.

## QUESTION IV: Whether Images Are Rightly Placed in Churches

The fourth difference between us and the Papists concerns the placement of
images in churches. Bellarmine, book 2, chapter 9, *On the Church Triumphant*,
asserts that images of the saints are nowhere better placed than in churches.
We deny this, relying on the following arguments.

Argument 1. If it is entirely forbidden to make images of God and the saints
for the sake of worship, then it is also forbidden to place them in the place
of worship. But the former was proved above. Therefore.

Argument 2. If Scripture teaches that under the New Testament no other images
of Christ are to be possessed than the Gospel itself and the sacraments, then
no others are to be placed in the place of worship for the sake of worship.
But the former is true, Galatians 3:1. Therefore.

Argument 3. Occasions and instruments of idolatry are not to be placed in
churches. But images are such. Thus God commanded them to be removed as
opposed to true worship, Exodus 23:24; 34:13; Numbers 33:52; Deuteronomy 7:5;
and in Scripture godly kings are praised for destroying idols, 1 Kings 15:13;
2 Kings 10:32. Therefore.

Argument 4. If images made by God’s command were broken because of the
occasion for idolatry they furnished, much more should images placed by human
beings in the place of worship be broken, since they are an occasion for
idolatry. But the former is true, as experience makes clear, 2 Kings 18.

Argument 5. That which alienates the enemies of the Church

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 35 | printed col. 69 | bsb10175568]

from the faith and exposes the Gospel to the mockery of its enemies is not to
be placed in churches, because on account of images Jews and Turks abhor
Christianity. Therefore.

Argument 6. If images are to be placed in churches so that they may be the
books of the laity, as Pope Gregory wished, then the primitive Church, which
omitted nothing that could benefit the laity, would have used them. But images
were not introduced into the Church before the year of Christ 400, at which
time many other corruptions were introduced into the Church.

Argument 7. It is not fitting for us to be wiser than God, who wishes his
Church to be instructed not by mute likenesses but by the preaching of his
life-giving Word; indeed, who does not wish to be worshiped in any other way
than he himself prescribed, Luke 16:19 and elsewhere. But he did not prescribe
worship through images. Therefore.

Argument 8. The Council of Elvira, chapter 36, says, *It was resolved that
pictures ought not be in the church, lest that which is worshiped or adored be
depicted on the walls.*

Augustine, book 1, chapters 9–10, *On the Harmony of the Evangelists*, says
that those err who sought Christ and the Apostles not in the Scriptures, as we
say, but on painted walls. And in letter 49: *It is not expedient*, he says,
*indeed, it is not lawful to place images in churches.*

Lactantius, book 2, chapter 3 of the *Divine Institutes*, says, *There is no
doubt that there is no religion wherever there is a likeness.* Epiphanius, in
his letter to John, bishop of Jerusalem, says that it is contrary to the
authority of Scripture—indeed, a dreadful abomination—that there are images in
Christian churches.

### OBJECTIONS

Objection 1. In the Old Testament, when the Jews were most

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 35 | printed col. 70 | bsb10175568]

inclined to idolatry, there were images of cherubim in the temple, Exodus 25.

Response. The inference is denied, because there was a command of God for
those images, but not for these. Furthermore, those images were types and
figures of things that found their fulfillment in Christ, but these are not.
Finally, those were in a place removed from the eyes of the common people and
thus could not be a cause of idolatry, but in the Papacy these are exposed to
everyone’s eyes.

Objection 2. Sacred signs cannot be placed better than in sacred places. But
images are such. Therefore.

Response. The assumption is denied, because they are signs not divinely
instituted, but introduced contrary to God’s institution.

Objection 3. Images ornament a church very well and restrain people’s minds
from wandering in idle thoughts. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because the true ornament of churches is
true worship, not idols, and ornament ought not conflict with God’s Word.
Finally, they neither ornament a church nor restrain people’s minds, but on the
contrary are dangerous to members of the Church and a stumbling block to its
enemies.

Objection 4. A church is an image of heaven, for in Hebrews 9 the Apostle
compares the tabernacle of Moses with heaven. Therefore it is fitting that in
a church there be images of those who are in heaven.

Response. The inference is denied. Furthermore, the case of churches is
different from that of the tabernacle, which was a type and figure, but the
types have now ceased. Finally, the images that are made are only images of
the saints’ bodies, but their bodies are not in heaven.

Objection 5. Images of the saints are nowhere better placed than in their own
houses, that is, in churches. Therefore.

[1620 BSB Tome II | canvas 36 | printed col. 71 | bsb10175568]

Response. Churches are not houses of the dead, but of God and of prayer,
Matthew 21:13.

Objection 6. Images are the books of the laity, by which they are stirred to
adore God and to love God and their neighbor. Therefore.

Response. The antecedent is denied, because Scripture nowhere said, “Learn the
mysteries of salvation by looking at images,” but from Scripture, John 5:39; 2
Timothy 3:14. Therefore books for the laity are to be allowed, but those
instituted by God, not prohibited, and not those which lie about God, as images
do, Jeremiah 10:8; Zechariah 10:2; Habakkuk 2:18.

Objection 7. The use of a thing and the thing itself are not to be abolished
because of an abuse that accidentally attaches to it. But images are dangerous
and furnish a stumbling block only incidentally. Therefore.

Response. The proposition is denied, because in 2 Kings 18 the bronze serpent
was broken because of its abuse. Furthermore, let the proposition be true of
those things whose use God instituted, but he did not institute images.
Finally, one must abstain from every appearance of evil, 1 Thessalonians 5:22.
Therefore much more from evil itself.
