Bellarmine's ninth argument runs as follows: “Through justification the heart
is cleansed so that it may be prepared for the vision of God. Thus Matthew 5
says, ‘Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God,’ and Acts 15
says, ‘purifying their hearts by faith.’ But an eye that is actually unclean
cannot see the sun, even if it is regarded as perfectly clean. Therefore
neither will an unclean heart ever see God, though it is counted clean. True,
not imputed, cleanness is therefore conferred in justification if it must
prepare us for the divine vision.” Thus far Bellarmine.

I answer these points as follows.

First and in general, it is Bellarmine's custom not to reduce his arguments
to formal shape, so that he may more readily create a false appearance by a
loose and confused manner of speaking

[1679 printed p. 300 | IA leaf 308]

and conceal the fallacy latent in them. But if his argument is reduced to
form, it must be constructed in this or a similar fashion: “Through
justification the heart is cleansed so that it may be prepared to see God.
But through imputed righteousness alone the heart is not so cleansed as to
be prepared to see God. Therefore justification does not consist in imputed
righteousness.”

I answer this argument as follows.

First, to the major premise: the heart is said to be cleansed in two ways,
either from the guilt of sin or from its stain and pollution, according to 1
Corinthians 6:11: “But you were washed, but you were sanctified, but you were
justified.” The washing placed first is common to both, and is attributed to
both justification and sanctification: to justification belongs washing
from the guilt of sin, and to sanctification washing from its stain. Given
this, I answer the major by saying that through justification in the former
sense the heart is most properly cleansed—namely, from the guilt of sin by
Christ's imputed righteousness. This relation is not an empty thought, but
is not only real and subsisting; it is also effectual—that is, a true
application of the foundation to its term. Through it a person is so
prepared to see God that he may appear before God with humble confidence;
without his pardon no one can stand in his sight, Psalm 130:3 and Ezra 9:15.
But Bellarmine does not understand here this cleansing of the heart, which
we readily concede to justification. He means the other cleansing, which
washes away not the guilt but the stain of sin, and thus in his customary
fashion confuses justification and sanctification. We indeed concede that
this removal of the stain is conferred together with justification and in
justification; nevertheless, we say that it is formally effected through
sanctification, but efficiently or concomitantly through justification,
insofar as justification always has sanctification joined to it, which
formally works this cleansing.

To the minor premise—“But through imputed righteousness alone the heart is
not so cleansed as to be prepared to see God”—I answer by denying it. Through
justification by imputed righteousness the heart is cleansed in the former
sense, and in the sense already given is also prepared to see God. Faith
apprehends Christ and brings and offers him as a surety to remove guilt and
reconcile God's favor, just as Benjamin was brought to Joseph. Justification
consists in this removal of guilt and conciliation or reconciliation of
divine favor. Since this is acquired through Christ's imputed righteousness
alone, justification certainly consists in the imputation of that
righteousness.

But, if you please, let us follow Bellarmine's loose manner of arguing.

First, he says that through justification the heart is cleansed and prepared
for the vision of God. We have already said in what sense this is true.

Second, to prove this he cites the passages “Blessed are the pure in heart,
for they shall see God,” and “purifying their hearts by faith.” I would like
him to show how these passages prove that this cleansing of the heart, and
the preparation for the vision of God that arises from it, are formally
effected through justification. The former passage says that the pure in
heart will see God, but does not say that through justification the heart is
cleansed in this manner—that is, from the filth of sin inherent within,
which Bellarmine nevertheless means. The passage is satisfied if this
cleansing occurs through sanctification. The latter passage does mention
faith, which concerns justification, but not justification alone: it also
concerns sanctification. Faith justifies by

[1679 printed p. 301 | IA leaf 309]

apprehending Christ's righteousness, and so cleanses the heart from the guilt
of sin that it may see God and appear before him. But faith also sanctifies
by purging the heart from the filth of sin, so that “in righteousness it may
see God's face” all the better and, when it finally awakens, “may be
satisfied with his likeness,” as Psalm 17:15 says.

Third, Bellarmine says: “Just as an eye that is actually unclean cannot see
the sun, though it is regarded as perfectly clean, so neither will an
unclean heart ever see God, though it is counted clean.” We say in response:

1. A heart that is actually unclean is falsely regarded as perfectly clean.
But, as the apostle says, “we know that God's judgment is according to
truth”; consequently, one whom God regards as righteous and clean is truly
regarded as such. Therefore he is not only perfectly clean in justification
through Christ's imputed righteousness; in sanctification, which is always
joined to justification, he is also truly clean in part through the work of
the Holy Spirit and infused grace, though he is not perfectly clean and free
from every stain. He ought to be called clean. The eye in this passage, I
say, is not wholly unclean or blind, since it has now been anointed with
Christ's eye salve. Just as light was the first of created things, so the
beginnings of sanctification start with illumination of the understanding
and renewal of the spirit of the mind, Acts 26:18; Ephesians 4:23; 5:8, 13,
14. The vision of God begins together with this cleansing of the heart,
grows, and finally rises to its maturity. At first the believer is dim of
sight, like the man in the Gospel who “at first saw people walking like
trees.” But afterward, as his cleanness daily increases, his vision becomes
clearer, until at last all the little clouds of sin are dispersed and, with
an eagle's eye now able to gaze at the sun, in the noonday light of the
beatific vision “he will see God face to face.” There is, I say, a twofold
vision of God: one belonging to wayfarers and the other to those who have
attained their end. This second is called direct vision, because the apostle
contrasts walking by sight with walking by faith in 2 Corinthians 5:7. Yet
faith has its own eye and is not wholly blind. Although here it sees God “in
a mirror and in a riddle,” it nevertheless sees something—if not in the
noonday light, then in twilight. And although as long as it remains here it
does not attain that brightest, beatific vision of God, it is nevertheless
prepared to see God, which is the point Bellarmine is discussing here.
