QUESTION XIX.

ON THE EFFICACY OF BAPTISM.

Does baptism so remove sins that they no longer exist, or only so that they do
not reign and are not imputed? Does it remove only past and present sins and
leave future sins to penance? Or does it extend to sins committed not only
before but also after baptism? The former is denied; the latter affirmed,
against the papists.

I. Two things concerning the efficacy of baptism are especially disputed
between us and the papists, which Bellarmine notes in book 1, chapter 12 and
following of *On Baptism*. The first concerns past and present sins: are they
so removed in baptism that they are not only no longer imputed, but no longer
exist at all? The other concerns future sins: does the power of baptism also
extend to future sins, or are they separately blotted out by the sacrament of
penance?

[Marginalia: Are sins entirely blotted out through baptism?]

II. Concerning the former, the papists teach as follows: “In baptism sin, and
whatever has the character of sin in the person baptized, is not only remitted
but also removed.” Concerning the latter, they hold as follows: “The power of
baptism does not reach future sins, but the sacrament of penance is necessary
to expiate them.” Thus speaks the Council of Trent, canon 10 on baptism: “If
anyone says that all sins committed after baptism are either remitted or made
venial by the mere recollection of and faith in the baptism received, let him
be anathema.”

III. The opinion of the orthodox, however, is far different. First, they admit
no blotting out of sin in baptism that occurs from the work performed or from
a power inherent in baptism, as was proved above when the efficacy of the
sacraments was discussed. Second, they say that baptism, taken separately for
the element and its administration, has moral efficacy, as was explained
above; but taken conjointly for the sign and the thing signified, it has real
efficacy with respect to the person receiving baptism, although that efficacy
is not bound to the moment of baptism. Third, they acknowledge in baptism the
attestation and sealing of a twofold grace promised in the gospel: both of
justification, by which sin is removed with respect to guilt so that it is not
imputed, and of sanctification, by which its stain is diminished and corrected
so that it no longer rules in us in this life, but not so that it absolutely
ceases to exist in us. The grace of justification pardons all our sins—
original and actual, past, present, and future. Sanctification, however,
abolishes the depravity inherent in us, not all at once but successively.
Since baptism is the sacrament of each, the effects of each also belong to it
and are sacramentally attributed to it in Scripture. Hence it is gathered that
the efficacy of baptism is rightly extended to all sins whatsoever, future as
well as past and present; and that it declares and seals to us the abolition
of sin in no other way than that true grace of God effects it in us. But they
deny that a sacrament of penance is specially ordained for sins committed
after baptism, as will be proved in its place.

[Marginalia: State of the question.]

IV. To speak distinctly about the two questions proposed, the state of the
first is not whether the efficacy of baptism extends only to preventing sin
from being imputed but not to truly removing it. Bellarmine falsely attributes
this to us, as though we maintained that through baptism sin is merely not
imputed and is in no way

[1847 vol. III printed p. 351 | IA leaf 385 | PDF p. 385]

removed. The declaration of our opinion just given makes it plain that this is
false. Rather, the question is whether it is removed so fully and perfectly
that it not only is not imputed but also no longer exists, as they would have
it. We deny this and say that through baptism sin is removed so that it is not
imputed or does not reign, but not so that it ceases to exist. We therefore
maintain that guilt is indeed fully removed, but the stain only initially and
in part.

[Marginalia: It is proved that sin is not entirely removed through baptism.]

V. The reasons are as follows. (1) Baptism removes sin sacramentally and by
way of sealing in no other manner than do justifying and sanctifying grace,
whose symbol and seal it is. But justifying grace removes sin with respect to
guilt so that it is not imputed to us for punishment, whereas sanctifying
grace removes it not so that it absolutely no longer exists, but only so that
it does not reign, as was seen in its place. Therefore baptism must remove it
in no other manner.

VI. (2) If baptism entirely removes sin with respect both to guilt and to the
stain so that it ceases to exist, everyone baptized will be pure and free of
all sin, both original and actual. For what is totally abolished in a subject
does not remain in any part. That this is false is evident both from Scripture,
which describes for us the condition of the baptized in this life (Rom. 7:14),
and from universal experience in all the baptized. None of them in this life
has been free from the stain of sin (1 Kings 8:46–47; James 3:2; 1 John 1:8),
and there is none who does not need the continual pursuit of sanctification.

VII. (3) Concupiscence, which is sin, always remains in the baptized, as was
proved in its place from Romans 7:7. Therefore sin is not entirely removed
through baptism so that it no longer exists, but so that it is not imputed or
does not reign. In this sense sin is said no longer to rule over us (Rom.
6:14), and believers do not walk according to the flesh—that is, so as to be
subject to its dominion—but according to the Spirit (Rom. 8:1).

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

VIII. When Scripture says that sins are cleansed, purged, blotted out, and
washed away in baptism (as in Eph. 5:26; Titus 5:3 [sic]), this must be
understood not physically but sacramentally, and not absolutely with respect
to all the baptized but only those who believe (Mark 16:16). Cleansing and
blotting out must be understood according to the nature of the matter: with
respect to guilt it is full and perfect, but with respect to the existence of
the inherent stain it is initial and successive.

IX. Baptism is related to sin in the same way that circumcision is related to
the foreskin of the heart, not that of the flesh. For they are compared with
each other in this way (Col. 2:11–12), not as sign and thing signified, but as
different old and new signs signifying the same thing by different figures
and rites. Therefore, just as circumcision of the flesh could not remove sin,
neither can baptism except significatively.

X. Although guilt or a stain still remains in a baptized person, it does not
follow that it cannot be said not to be imputed because God’s judgment is
according to truth. For imputation does not remove the internal desert of
punishment, but only the external obligation to it. And by not imputing, God
does not judge guilt not to exist; rather, he pardons what he knows to be
guilt.

XI. I grant that the grace of sanctification sealed to us through baptism
cannot coexist with reigning sin. But nothing prevents it from remaining
alongside indwelling sin, just as contraries in a diminished degree can subsist
in the same subject. In this sense Paul says that with his mind he serves the
law of God, but with his flesh the law of sin (Rom. 7:23), and believers who
nevertheless are not without sin are said to be in the light (1 John 1:7–8).

[Marginalia: Does baptism extend to future sins?]

XII. The other question concerns the power of baptism with respect to sins
committed after baptism. The papists do not wish baptism to be extended to
these, but want them to be purged by the sacrament of penance and

[1847 vol. III printed p. 352 | IA leaf 386 | PDF p. 386]

left to it. We, however, maintain that through baptism the remission not only
of past and present sins but also of future sins is sealed to us. This is in
such a way, however, that repentance—not sacramental repentance and the kind
they fabricate, but that commanded in the gospel—and especially saving faith
are not excluded, but are coordinated with baptism as a means of our salvation
divinely appointed.

[Marginalia: It is proved, 1. From the covenant.]

XIII. The reasons are as follows. (1) Baptism must be such as the gracious
covenant is, through whose grace baptism is efficacious, for baptism and the
covenant are mutually related as sign and thing signified. But the gracious
covenant is perpetual, extending to the remission not only of past and present
sins but also of future sins (Gen. 17:7; Lev. 26:9; Isa. 53:3; Jer. 32:39;
Hos. 2:19). But if the gracious covenant were not sufficient for the remission
of postbaptismal sins, the covenant itself would have to be multiplied
according to the number of sins.

[Marginalia: 2. Because it is not restricted to certain sins.]

XIV. (2) Scripture nowhere restricts baptism to a certain kind of sins, but
without restriction refers it universally and indefinitely to all sins (Mark
1:4; Luke 3:3; Acts 2:38; 22:16): past (Titus 3:5; Gal. 3:27), present (1 Pet.
3:21), and future (Mark 16:16). Hence we are said to be baptized into Christ’s
death (Rom. 6:3), and Christ’s death expiates not only past but also future
sins.

[Marginalia: 3. From the efficacy of grace.]

XV. (3) The efficacy of God’s grace, Christ’s merit, and baptism must be the
same according to the condition of each person. But God’s grace and Christ’s
merit are efficacious for blotting out all sins, future as well as past and
present, as is evident from Romans 3:24–25; Acts 13:38–39; Matthew 1:21; and 1
John 1:3.

[Marginalia: 4. From circumcision.]

XVI. (4) The power of baptism is not less than that of circumcision. But it is
evident from Genesis 17:11, 13 that circumcision was effective also for the
future. Therefore baptism can be effective as well.

[Marginalia: Sources of solutions.]

XVII. When sins committed after baptism are said to be remitted by the mere
memory of baptism, this does not mean a bare and theoretical memory of the
baptism once received, but a practical memory partly of repentance and partly
of faith: of repentance renewed and restored concerning sins committed after
baptism, and of faith concerning the remission of sins, stirred up and
confirmed by the word of promise and its sacraments. For baptism perpetually
is and remains a sacrament of repentance for the remission of sins (Acts
2:38–39; Luke 3:3).

XVIII. Although baptism passes away physically, it nevertheless remains
morally and so can operate morally, insofar as its power of sealing is not for
a moment but endures perpetually.

XIX. The confession that John requires from believers (1 John 1:9) is not the
papists’ sacramental confession, which is muttered into a priest’s ears, but
evangelical confession. It is opposed neither to Christ’s merit nor to baptism,
but is subordinate to both. Hence it is evident that in adults it is joined
with baptism (Matt. 3:6).

XX. Considered definitely, determinately, and formally, the remission of sins
sealed by baptism is only of past and present sins, but nothing prevents it
from being also of future sins indefinitely and virtually.

XXI. When Paul denies that those who have once been enlightened can, if they
fall away, be renewed to repentance (Heb. 6:4–5), he is not speaking properly
of baptism but of *phōtismos* (φωτισμῷ), enlightenment, which can occur before
and after baptism. Nor is he speaking of just any sin committed after that
enlightenment, but of the sin unto death committed against the Holy Spirit,
which is entirely

[1847 vol. III printed p. 353 | IA leaf 387 | PDF p. 387]

unpardonable. Such persons therefore cannot be renewed to repentance, not
because baptism is ineffectual against future sins, but because by sinning
against the Holy Spirit they have treated him with contempt and have utterly
driven away the Spirit of repentance and renewal.

XXII. Since the remission of sins is none other than that promised and sealed
in baptism—namely, the remission founded on Christ’s blood—and since the Holy
Spirit recalls those who have fallen both before and after baptism to no other
thing than Christ’s satisfaction presented on the altar of the cross, and
nowhere distinguishes between those who sin before or after baptism, another
way of obtaining this remission beyond that sealed to us in baptism is
fabricated in vain. Indeed, since repentance is required together with baptism
even in adults for the remission of prebaptismal sins, it cannot be said that
the way of reconciliation is easier for those sins than for those that follow
baptism.

XXIII. When the fathers call repentance the second plank after shipwreck, they
do not understand this in relation to baptism, as if they were substituting
repentance as a sacrament for it. Rather, they intended to teach that the right
of ecclesiastical communion and of the sacred assembly, which had been lost in
the sight of human beings through an enormous and public sin after baptism,
could be recovered only through public or canonical repentance.

XXIV. It belongs to God’s beneficence to abolish sin and death utterly in a
person and to clothe him with perfect righteousness and immortality, but the
time and degrees in which he effects this must be left to his wisdom. Nor, if
God grants some benefit to his elect, must all of it be attributed to baptism
or thought to occur immediately after baptism or in baptism itself, since it
is evident that in adults baptism follows regeneration, and many are saved
without baptism. Nor is anything thereby taken away from the dignity of
baptism, because it retains the rank in which it is placed in Scripture. But
it is far more truly said that those detract from the dignity of baptism who
make its efficacy depend upon the minister’s intention—without which baptism
is invalid—and who restrict its power to sins committed before baptism.

XXV. The death of sin can be viewed in two ways: either in relation to
justification, in which case sin dies perfectly because it is perfectly
remitted and is in no way imputed; or in relation to sanctification, in which
case sin dies, or rather is put to death, by degrees. According to each of
these relations (*schesis*, σχέσιν), we are said in different ways to die with
Christ in baptism (Rom. 6:3; Col. 2:11).

XXVI. The promises of the cleansing and blotting out of sins do not prove that
baptism possesses the power of fully removing sins. 1. Scripture uses them in
the Old Testament, before baptism was instituted. 2. They can very well refer
to the remission of sins, because through it guilt is taken away before God
and the sinner’s obligation is blotted out of God’s book (Col. 2:14). 3. If
they are referred to sanctification, their fulfillment must occur not all at
once but gradually and successively; otherwise it would follow that
sinlessness (*anamartēsia*, ἀναμαρτησίαν) is promised to the godly, though it
belongs to no one in this life.

XXVII. The rationale of the spiritual effects of baptism must not in every
respect be the same as the rationale of the bodily effects of the preceding
antitypes, such as circumcision, the Red Sea, and the like. For signs can be
similar in some things, not in all, relatively, not absolutely. Therefore,
although circumcision truly cut off the foreskin and the Red Sea entirely
drowned the Egyptians, it does not follow that sins are entirely removed
through baptism so that they no longer exist. It is sufficient that they are
entirely removed with respect to guilt and gradually with respect to the
stain.
