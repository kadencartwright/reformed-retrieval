And how can a bodily substance that is invisible, without location, and wholly everywhere be truly bodily? Let us therefore hear Brenz on page 143 of the *Recognition*: “What Theodoret and others say, that Christ’s human body possesses in the other age the same circumscription it possessed in this age, is said with no correct understanding of the testimony of Holy Scripture and is a childish thought concerning heavenly things.” Augustine says to the contrary in *On the Essence of Divinity*: “Not to believe and confess the Son of God in the assumed humanity to be visible, bodily, and local after the resurrection is profane.” And in Epistle 146: “I believe Christ’s body to be in heaven just as it was on earth when he ascended into heaven.” And just as Christ himself says to the disciples who doubted his resurrection: “Touch and see; a spirit does not have flesh and bones.” And Ambrose, Epistle 22: “The same truth of body is in Christ that is in us.” Shall we instead believe the evidently all-majestic ubiquitarians?

Concerning the hypostatic union, the same Dr. Andreae writes in precisely these words in the same theses: “All things are substantially sustained by God. Likewise, the man Christ has this in common with all the saints: God was personally united with him.” Who would have supposed that Nestorianism, so often slain by the sword of God’s word with the consent of the whole church, could have revived in our times—and indeed could have been combined with the other side of Eutychian impiety, namely the confusion of properties, in company with Eutyches?

“I go to prepare a place for you” (says Christ, John 14:2), that is (according to Brenz’s interpretation on page 144 of the *Recognition*), “I go to death, by which I shall prepare a gracious Father for you, so that I may render you forever happy with me. For he is not speaking of a place but metaphorically of happiness and blessedness.”

Christ says in John 14:3, “I will come.” He therefore truly departed and will then truly come. Dr. Andreae says to the contrary in the same theses, page 63: “Since Christ’s body is no longer physical but spiritual, who will say it moves from place to place?” No, good sir: Paul does not oppose a physical [φυσικόν] body to the spiritual but a natural [ψυχικόν] body. And the angels say in Acts 1:11, “He will come in the same manner as YOU SAW HIM go into heaven.” And Augustine says in the epistle to Dardanus: “Christ gave immortality to his flesh; he did not take away its nature.” What more is needed? If Christ’s body ceased to be physical when it was assumed by the Word, what will be the beginning of the hypostatic union of the two natures in Christ? But if it ceased after the ascension, the ascension has surely dissolved that union. And is this

In the same place Christ says, “I will come so that where I am you too may be.” And in John 14:10, addressing Philip, he says, “Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father is in me?” “Christ is therefore in the Father,” says Dr. Brenz, “and when he leads believers to where he is, it comes about that THEY TOO ARE IN THE FATHER; that is, they possess a gracious Father and enjoy the paternal inheritance together with him.” Behold once more an excellent GLOSS by which the mutual indwelling [τὸ ἐνυπάρχειν] of the three persons is excellently explained as something we evidently possess in common with the Son of God. And is this not to tear up the mystery of the Trinity by the foundations?

“The Son of God did not take hold of angels but the seed of Abraham,” says the apostle, Hebrews 2:16. That is (says Brenz on page 66 of that *Recognition*), “He placed the assumed man in heavenly and divine glory; that is, he equaled him to the deity in glory, power, and majesty,” as Dr. Andreae repeats from Brenz in Thesis 26 of his disputation *On the Majesty of the Man Christ*. Likewise elsewhere: that is, “the Word administers all things through Christ’s humanity.”

The apostle says in Ephesians 3:10, “He ascended above all the heavens that he might fill all things.” According to the ubiquitarians’ interpretation, this means that when the Son of God assumed flesh, he poured all his properties into it, and especially the omnipresence of his essence in all created things without exception, but peculiarly into the bread and wine of the Lord’s Supper—not so that he should be there who already was everywhere, but so that he should there be distributed, to be eaten and drunk essentially by the mouths of all who approach, worthy and unworthy. Thus in descending he would have ascended. And what will that ascension be? “An outward spectacle,” says Brenz on page 167, “as far as the clouds.” And on page 191: “Christ is said to have penetrated heaven with his body or humanity and ascended above all the heavens, not because he deserted this heaven”—but who ever dreamed that?—“or the earth and the things in them”—and what orthodox person ever denied this?—“but because he does not cling to this heaven in such a way that he does not also fill all things.”

As Scripture teaches and the creed of all Christians declares, we believe Christ only in his ascension entered the glory of the heavenly kingdom. As regards Christ’s deity, this glory was never diminished, much less forsaken, but because it lay hidden beneath the veil of weakness during the time of his assumed flesh, it was recovered. As regards the human nature, once the weakness of the cross was swallowed up, it was then at last bestowed upon him in reality, which is declared by his sitting in heaven at the Father’s right hand. But Brenz says on page 247: “Although Christ, because of the OUTWARD SPECTACLE of his ascension into heaven, is said to have sat down at the right hand of his almighty Father, the truth of this matter did not then first come about when Christ visibly ascended into heaven and sent the Holy Spirit to his disciples, but when the Word became flesh and assumed humanity into God,” that is, as he himself explains on page 166, cited by me shortly before, “even then he placed him in heavenly and divine glory.”

### GLOSSES Charged by Dr. Andreae against Theodore Beza in His Treatment of Baptism

1. The apostle in Titus 3 calls baptism “the washing of the regeneration of the Holy Spirit,” that is, according to Beza’s gloss, “a sign of the washing by which the washing of regeneration is signified and represented.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

If Dr. Andreae denies that the outward washing of water represents the inward cleansing of the inner person by the Holy Spirit, then let him show what the sign in baptism is and what the thing signified is. Both must be either established or removed together. And if either is removed, let Dr. Andreae say why he numbers baptism among the sacraments. Moreover, I trust all fair readers will grant me that the apostle there comprehends under the name “washing” both the sign and the thing signified, just as when, speaking of the Lord’s Supper, he said bread and wine are participation [κοινωνία] in Christ’s body and blood. It is no wonder that Andreae falls into these absurdities. Because there is one baptism—that is, one never to be repeated, tending to the one Christ through the one faith—he cannot bear the distinction between the outward baptism of water administered by the minister and that inward power of the Holy Spirit that washes us of every defilement of sins and, mortifying the old man, leads us by spiritual resurrection into new life.

2. “Unless someone is born again of water and the Holy Spirit,” that is, “sealed by water baptism for a regeneration that will be future and follow in its own time, and that only in the elect, who very often are not born again until old age.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

So far am I from denying that adults who approach baptism are regenerated in reality that, on the contrary, since a profession of faith is required from them before baptism and Christ is truly apprehended by true faith, I have said and will say that they are regenerated earlier both in time and order—unless, as God knows, they are hypocrites—than they are baptized. Baptism is applied to them not so that they may then first be regenerated but so that this grace

may be sealed in them, just as Abraham, the father of believers, who had previously been justified in uncircumcision, received circumcision as a sign and seal [σημεῖον καὶ σφραγῖδα] of the righteousness of faith. As for infants, I endeavored to explain, by testimonies and arguments drawn from God’s word, with what fruit they are sealed in the Lord’s covenant by the sacrament of baptism at the very moment they are baptized, if they depart this life before the use of reason. I have affirmed and still affirm that the fruit of baptism must not be restricted to the moment baptism is administered, whether in infants who will reach adolescence or in adults who approach baptism. If Dr. Andreae censures the first of these three things, I say he understands neither the effect of faith nor the distinction by which the simple preaching of the Word is distinguished from the administration of the sacraments—that is, reception into the covenant itself from the sealing of that reception. On the second, I hope I have proved by necessary arguments and authorities that the habit of actual faith neither can nor ought nor needs to exist in the infants of believers, that is, of those professing Christianity. Yet if I should perhaps be found mistaken in this matter, I will gladly hear and follow the judgment of learned men. Dr. Andreae cannot attack the third unless he first demonstrates at least one of these three utterly false positions: either that baptism benefits without the faith of the baptized, that is, *ex opere operato*, as the scholastic sophists speak; or that all who are baptized, whether infants or adults, are truly endowed with faith; or that all who were baptized in infancy but afterward show themselves by fruits contrary to faith and regeneration to be devoid of faith, true religion, and Christianity have entirely fallen away from faith—which, among other things, our adversary teaches most perniciously can happen; or else that those who come to their senses must be rebaptized if the fruit of baptism previously received does not extend throughout life, whenever the faith of the baptized begins.

3. Acts 8:13: “Simon himself also believed,” that is, “he pretended to believe.”

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

If Dr. Andreae maintains that the verb “believe” is never used of feigned faith, let him show why, although all the Holy Spirit’s other gifts can be simulated in remarkable ways, faith must be excepted. And who are those false brothers whom the apostle mentions in 2 Corinthians 11:26 and Galatians 2:4, those most harmful plagues of the churches? And what is that dead faith in James 2:26? Concerning that Simon, I prefer to stand by Peter’s own testimony in Acts 8:21 rather than agree with Dr. Jacob Andreae, to omit what I explained above concerning this same Simon in its place.

4. Acts 23: “The following night the Lord stood by Paul, saying, ‘Take courage, Paul,’” that is, “He appeared to him in a nocturnal vision.”

[Part II | PDF page 250 | logical source scan 249 | printed page 248]

#### Theodore Beza’s Reply

This has nothing to do with baptism. But my adversary was afraid this Gloss too might slip away from him. That this vision was intellectual, as they speak in the schools, not bodily, is first irrefragably confirmed by the article concerning the true ascension, which will last until the final day. Speaking of it, Augustine says, “He is so far NOT HERE that he is also expected to come from heaven.” Then, lest someone perhaps imagine something extraordinary here, comparison with other similar passages manifestly proves it, as in Acts 9:10 and 12, where the term “vision” [ὅραμα] is expressed, which never signifies bodily sight. Indeed, even from the fact that in the same chapter Jesus is said to have been seen by Paul on the road, and that this is repeated in 1 Corinthians 14:8, the presence of Christ’s body on earth should not be inferred. For then, as Augustine aptly says, the head spoke from heaven while the member lay upon the earth. For this reason also, in Acts 22, where Paul says Christ was seen by him in the temple itself, the term “trance” [ἔνστασις] is expressed. Therefore no Gloss but a true interpretation is offered by us for that passage and the similar Acts 27:23, lest truth conflict with itself. But how will our own adversary excuse his gloss here? For if the ubiquitarian invention concerning the true and real presence of Christ’s body in every place is true, what else will the words STOOD BY and WAS SEEN declare than “HE WHO already stood by was made visible from being invisible”? These, then, are the glosses charged against us in the treatment of baptism. In it, our adversary has wandered from the truth in very many ways and corrupted almost everything rightly and orthodoxly said concerning baptism: now denying that, just as circumcision was formerly twofold—one of the flesh and the other of the Spirit—so now baptism, because it is one, is twofold—one outward, of water, and the other inward, of the Spirit—although Christ’s person itself, which is one, is twofold in natures, every human being is composed of body and soul, and even the regenerated soul itself, which certainly is one, is distinguished into the old and new person, or flesh and Spirit; now introducing the proper power of the Holy Spirit into the outward word and element of water; now imagining that the sacrament of baptism is emptied in itself unless we hold with certainty that every baptized person without exception receives the efficacy of baptism in reality; now affirming that all infants are endowed with actual faith at the very moment they are baptized; now teaching that the faith through whose sole intervention Christ is apprehended—which is proper and peculiar to the elect alone and, finally, which, cast as an anchor into God’s immutable will concerning our preservation in his Son, disclosed both in the Word and in the sacraments and made certain in believers’ hearts by the Holy Spirit’s testimony, alone supports our faith and hope in every storm, however violent—can fail and be utterly lost;
