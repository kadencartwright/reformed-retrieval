From these things we see the resolution of that difficulty: whether all
created moral rectitude is from GOD’s free decree. Here it must be noted that
it is one thing for moral equity to be from GOD, and another for it to be from
GOD’s free choice and decree. For the fact that it is right not to sin is from
GOD, but not from GOD’s free choice.

Assertion 1. Simple moral rectitude, which inheres in an entitative act, is one
thing; complex moral rectitude, which results from composition or division, is
another. For the equity inhering in the entitative act of loving GOD is simple
equity, just as the iniquity inhering in the entitative act of hating GOD is
simple lawlessness and illegality. You will say that the entitative act of
hating GOD and the ἀνομία, or malice of the act, are not distinguished. I
answer: perhaps they cannot be separated in reality, but they must be
distinguished. For GOD is the author, the per se efficient, and the cause of
the entitative act in hatred of GOD, just as he is the author of the entitative
act of eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge; for he is the cause and
author of every positive being and every physical action. But GOD is neither
the author nor the cause of the malice in hatred of GOD. 2. Bradwardine
demonstrates that every act is good, in this way: *“Moreover, it seems that no
interior act is evil in itself, because neither blasphemy nor hatred of GOD is
so. This can be shown just as it has been shown concerning exterior acts in
fools and even the insane, and because every such act is a certain natural
perfection of such a natural power. Furthermore, understanding and willing are
different operations. Suppose, therefore, that GOD creates a nature which can
freely will and refuse, and perform the other works of the will, but cannot
judge intellectually or rationally and instead apprehends simply like brute
animals. Such a nature, when blaspheming or hating GOD, would not sin, because
it would not act contrary to the judgment or rule of the act. Such acts,
therefore, are not evil in themselves (intrinsically).”* I acknowledge that I
said GOD could command or forbid people to hate himself. That could perhaps
have been expressed more felicitously in another way, but I take τὸ odisse
DEUM—“the hating of GOD”—in its physical and entitative consideration, not
formally insofar as it includes both the entitative act and its malice. For
this is how I speak: *“All these things, in their physical consideration, are
neither good nor evil; and physically loving GOD is indifferent, though
morally good because GOD commands it.”* But by what law, you will ask, is the
entitative act-*

[Marginal note: The entitative act which underlies or is placed beneath hatred
of GOD, and the lawlessness or malice of the act, are distinguished.]

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 26.]

[Marginal reference: Rutherford, in the exercises, exercise 2, chapter 3, page
338.]

*of loving GOD commanded, and the entitative act of hating GOD forbidden? I
answer: by a law cocreated with rational nature, not by a law given to a person
already created in the natural and complete existence of a rational creature
(as the law against eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge was given to
Adam when he was already created, hearing, and actually understanding the
words of the prohibition, *“You shall not eat,”* and so forth). For when GOD
created humanity—and he created it freely—by the same free act of creating,
not by another but by the same act by which he created an animal participating
in reason, he also created, or rather cocreated, the common practical
principles and the law of nature, with the obligation cocreated with and
inscribed upon the natural constitution of a rational creature: to love GOD
above all things, worship him, and obey him in everything. This also includes
not eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge, on the hypothesis that GOD has
revealed such a legal prohibition; the sacrifice of Isaac, if GOD positively
imposed such a command upon Abraham; subjection to Nebuchadnezzar, if GOD
commanded it through Jeremiah; and keeping all the divine ceremonies which he
would enjoin in both Testaments. Hence, just as GOD was free to create
humanity, he was free in this manner to cocreate GOD’s natural Law and to
command such acts. Thus, if it is from GOD’s free will that the sun was
created, it is also originally from him that an illuminating body was created;
and likewise that fire and a heating element were created. On the supposition
that humanity has been created—or however the matter stands—GOD perhaps cannot
fail, as Bradwardine says, to create together with humanity the natural law
and obligation to love GOD above all things. I also did not wish to determine
or debate whether the obligation to worship GOD is necessarily cocreated with
a rational creature, as on page 360. *“For the question is not whether it was
right from eternity that humanity should worship GOD, and whether GOD willed
that, since it is right—as I now think, from eternity and before every free
decree of GOD. Rather, if τὸ colere DEUM—‘worshiping GOD’—could be separated,
with respect to the equity of the natural obligation, from the nature of a
rational creature, then I say that it could have occurred, if GOD had so
pleased, that it was not right for a rational creature to worship its Creator.
For it is from GOD’s freedom that a rational creature exists, and from the same
freedom that the worship of GOD exists, although that GOD exists is not from
GOD’s freedom.”* Meanwhile, however, this whole necessity of commanding the
entitative act of love or hatred of GOD is on the hypothesis that this
obligation is included in the quiddity of a rational creature, whereas it
depends-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 337; IA PDF page 397; BSB canvas 389]

*entirely upon GOD’s freedom that any rational creature such as humanity has
existed in the nature of things. Therefore, humanity’s existence is from GOD’s
free will through a fitting and concomitant reason. GOD could therefore have
created or not created humanity. Consequently, that such moral rectitude,
which results from the entitative act of love for GOD, existed or did not exist
in the nature of things is originally and causally from GOD’s freedom. 2.
Because hatred of GOD, considered in itself and separated from a rational
creature, is not in its entitative and physical act an evil act in itself, as
Bradwardine proves. It can occur without any malice in another creature which
GOD can create, perhaps one whose nature differs from humanity. Thus, if GOD
were to create a creature endowed with will, whose will was averted from GOD
as the supremely lovable object, yet this creature had only sensory cognition
such as exists in brute animals in the place of intellectual cognition, that
creature would hate GOD and would not sin. For its lack of intellectual
cognition would render that hatred of GOD an invincibly imperfect act, since
it would not be directed toward the supremely lovable object. Nevertheless,
that act would not be contrary to the law cocreated with humanity; therefore
it would not be sin, nor would such a creature be rational. Consequently, the
fact that this simple entitative act of hatred of GOD would be evil depends
upon GOD’s free will. 3. I still desire an answer as to what or what sort of
opposition there is between the entitative act of hatred of GOD and GOD—or,
indeed, between any created being and GOD—or what properly so-called agreement
there is between the entitative act of love for GOD and GOD, or between any
created thing and GOD. Certainly GOD is the Author, Cause, and Creator of every
entitative act, and the entitative act of hatred of GOD, as distinguished from
its malice, is a positive act because it is essentially free. No repugnance can
therefore intervene between Creator and creature, cause and effect. For GOD
created nothing repugnant to himself; and since sin is repugnant to God, GOD is
not the author of sin and is not its cause.

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of God*, book 1, chapter 26.]

Assertion 2. Complex moral rectitude is that rectitude which arises from the
connection of the terms—the predicate with the subject. It is either purely
necessary, when both terms are GOD or something belonging to GOD, as in: *“God
exists. GOD is infinitely good; GOD is independent, infinitely perfect, wise,”*
and so forth; *“GOD is one and three.”* Bradwardine calls these rational truths
antecedent to GOD’s will and natural.-*

[Marginal note: A certain moral rectitude exists antecedently to GOD’s free
will, and of what sort it is.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 338; IA PDF page 398; BSB canvas 390]

*This moral rectitude flows from GOD’s very nature, not from his free will.
Since the foundation and rule for believing propositions of this kind is the
objective truth of the propositions themselves, and GOD’s free will is not
properly the cause for which GOD commands faith to be given to such things,
the moral rectitude in these things believed by us is therefore originally
from GOD. For we believe that GOD is infinitely good more because this is in
fact so than because GOD freely commands us to believe it—or, at least, GOD
commands us to believe that GOD exists,* and so forth. Since GOD is in truth
such in himself, we are not to believe that GOD is such in himself merely
because GOD has commanded it, as if GOD’s command were the original cause of
the moral rectitude in the act of believing. Rather, it is because agreement
really exists between the object or thing believed and faith. For GOD cannot
command us to believe the opposite, or that a matter stands in a certain way
when it does not stand that way. Nor has the question whether GOD can command
hatred of himself or prohibit love of himself ever seemed sufficiently
necessary to this controversy. For it reduces to the question whether moral
adherence to God the Creator is included in the quiddity of a rational
creature. But whether one holds the affirmative or the negative, the question
remains intact: *whether GOD’s free good pleasure is the First Rule of all
created moral Rectitude.*

Assertion 3. There is a mixed moral rectitude resulting from the connection of
terms, when one term is GOD or something belonging to GOD and the other is a
created being. Thus, in the proposition *“it is right for the creature to be
subject to God,”* this moral rectitude, I say, naturally precedes GOD’s free
decree. This is so whether one speaks of the physical subjection by which a
creature has natural dependence upon the Creator, as illumination depends upon
the sun, or of the moral subjection by which it is right that a rational
creature depend upon God in the sphere of moral operations in relation to law,
although the former dependence does not produce the moral rectitude of which
we now speak. Thus it is right for a rational creature to obey God, act well,
and not sin against God. Hence the simple rectitude of love for GOD can depend
upon GOD’s freedom, because here one may distinguish between the entitative act
and goodness in good acts, and between such an act and malice in evil acts.
Nevertheless, the moral rectitude resulting from the connection of the terms
naturally precedes GOD’s free act, because just as the proposition is of
necessary and eternal truth, so moral rectitude is of necessary and-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 339; IA PDF page 399; BSB canvas 391]

*eternal truth. For neither the truth of this proposition—*“it is morally right
for the creature to obey the Creator”*—nor of this one—*“it is morally right
for humanity not to sin against GOD”*—depends upon GOD’s free will. Nor can GOD
decree the opposite here, namely, *“it is not right for the creature to obey the
Creator.”* Just as GOD freely decrees the existence of a rational animal and
can decree its nonexistence, but cannot freely decree that a human is a
rational animal. For no being which is necessarily always such, or is simply
necessary, is the object of a free divine decree. Thus GOD also cannot decree
that adultery is sin, that murder and cruelty are rebellion and sin against
GOD, or that eating the forbidden tree, if it is now forbidden, is
transgression; for all these things are essentially and intrinsically sins.
The connection is essential, not a connection between subject and predicate
made by GOD’s free will. And the ground of the truth in these things which is
everlasting and naturally prior to GOD’s free will is from GOD’s nature rather
than from the creature. For because GOD is infinite, independent, wise, and
just, obedience and moral subjection are owed to GOD by a rational Creature,
not because GOD is a free agent.

Assertion 4. But where the connection between subject and predicate is through
GOD’s free will, and both subject and predicate are created beings which do not
cohere necessarily, then no moral rectitude is good, or is moral rectitude,
except in relation to GOD’s free will. The rectitude in these statements is of
this kind: *“An act of taking life is homicide. To take goods by force from an
unwilling rightful possessor is theft.”* For it is from GOD’s free will alone
that an act of taking life is homicide; it can be a most just act if Saul, when
commanded by the Lord, kills the Amalekites. So says Bradwardine. *“Here,
however, one can answer in every respect about justice or what is just as was
answered above about reason and what is rational. And just as rational things
were divided there, so just things can be divided here. For some just things
are naturally prior to the divine will and do not depend upon it, such as that
GOD exists, that GOD is Eternal and Omnipotent, and similar things; and these
just things and their justice very well can and do move GOD’s will to willing.
But other just things are posterior, while still others are mixed, just as was
said concerning rational things. Legal things and laws are likewise divided.”*-*

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, *On the Cause of GOD*, chapter 21.]

[Marginal summary: Just things too are threefold according to Bradwardine: 1.
Some just things are naturally prior to the divine will. 2. Some are
posterior. 3. Some are mixed.]

*“Perhaps mixed things are such things as that the good are rewarded and the
evil punished, and similar matters. And just as in another genus there is some
proper and first principle by which the rest are regulated, as the preceding
discussion has taught, so in the genus of legal and just things there is
necessarily some first law and justice which is the first rule and first
principle from which, or from which principle, all other subsequent legal and
just things are originally derived and directed in all respects. This proper
principle, moreover, seems to be the infallible truth of the divine essence, on
which all judgments of justice rest as on an immovable foundation. David, that
eminent witness of the truth, seems to attest this when he says, ‘The beginning
of your words is truth; all the judgments of your justice endure forever,’
Psalm 118. Hence the whole law is also called truth from this principle: ‘Your
law is truth,’ above in the same Psalm. This first justice is just through
itself, primarily, necessarily, and altogether absolutely, in no way depending
upon the divine will but naturally prior to it. From this first just thing many
other just things necessarily follow from GOD, or from any human being, whose
opposites also are necessarily and absolutely unjust. This can be shown as
above concerning rational things. Neither GOD nor humanity can dispense in the
least with these just or unjust things, just as neither can alter what is
absolutely necessary or impossible. These things are not subject in any way to
the divine or human will, just as neither the antecedent nor the consequence by
and through which they follow from that first just thing with perfect justice
is subject to it. Of this sort are: that a creature, if it exists, be subject
to the Creator; that one who has received a benefit be bound at least to GOD
his benefactor; that no one ought to sin; and, as is perhaps the case, that no
one ought to do anything against his own conscience. — These are natural laws
simply speaking, immovably founded in nature itself as the source of nature.
But from this first rule of all legal and just things many other legal and just
things follow, not necessarily as the former do, but freely and contingently,
because through a free and contingent means, namely, the divine will; and these
are all legal things instituted in time. Hence Augustine, in book 1, chapter 13
of *On Free Choice*, distinguishing a twofold law, says that nothing is just
and legitimate which people have not derived for themselves from this eternal
law. And therefore Hugh, book 1 of *On the Sacraments*, part 1, chapter 4,
speaking of the divine will, says this: ‘According to it, what is just is just;
and what is just certainly would not be just if according to it the just
thing-*

[Chapter XXII; printed page 341; IA PDF page 401; BSB canvas 393]

*were not just. Therefore, when it is asked why what is just is just, the most
fitting answer is: because it is according to the will of GOD, which is just.
But when it is asked why that will itself is just, the sounder answer is: since
the first cause has no cause, that to which it belongs to be what it is has
this from itself. This alone is the source from which whatever exists arose,
and it did not itself arise but is eternal.’ In the same place: ‘A certain kind
of just thing follows necessarily from a supposition and inseparably attends a
created nature, such as that a human is rational, that all human beings are
alike in species, that the whole is greater than its part, and so with similar
things. This is natural law simply speaking, belonging to created nature and
altogether necessary; neither GOD nor a natural human being can dispense with
it while such a created nature stands. Another kind follows created nature not
altogether necessarily but for the most part, which almost everyone and
everywhere does by the instinct of nature, not by human constitution. Of this
kind are: doing to another what one wills to be done to oneself, and likewise
the negative form; love and education of offspring; requital of benefits;
violent suppression of violence; keeping faith—and this natural law can be
dispensed with for a cause. For such natural things, according to book 2 of the
*Physics*, always or for the most part behave in one way, but sometimes fail in
the lesser part. — There is still another law derived freely, not necessarily,
from the first rule of justice. It is natural in neither of the preceding ways;
before it is enacted there is no stronger reason for it than against it. Such
law is merely positive and legal. — In such matters epieikeia (dispensation)
often has a place.’”*

Assertion 5. Whenever GOD or something belonging to GOD is an immediate term,
this moral rectitude resulting from the connection of terms—as in *“it is right
for the Creature to be subject and obedient to the Creator”*—is not an entirely
Created moral rectitude, nor is it in every respect that created equity
residing in creatures concerning which the present disputation was instituted
and which Calvin and our theologians mean whenever they say that *“something
is just because GOD wills it,”* not the reverse. Thus Calvin speaks piously in
his customary way: *“For GOD’s will is so completely the supreme rule of
justice that whatever he wills must, by the very fact that he wills it, be
regarded as just. Therefore, when it is asked why the Lord did so, the answer
must be: because he willed it. If you go further and ask why he willed it, you
seek something greater and higher-*

*than GOD’s will, and none can be found.”* He takes this from Augustine. Yet he
adds in the same place: *“Nor do we introduce the fiction of absolute power,
which is profane and ought deservedly to be detestable to us. We do not imagine
GOD to be without law, for he is a law to himself; because, as Plato says,
people who struggle with desires have need of law. But GOD’s will is not only
pure from every fault and the supreme rule of perfection; it is also the law
of all laws.”* Our position, like Calvin’s, is not that every moral rectitude,
but—as I expressly said—*every moral justice related to creatures is regulated
by GOD’s good pleasure.* I attacked that statement of Cameron’s, and I still
attack it. *“For if,”* he says, *“GOD is inclined toward love of himself by a
natural and immutable propensity, he will embrace his image with an entirely
similar affection.”* For 1. moral justice, of which we speak, is related to
creatures; that is, it is entirely created justice. But this mixed justice, as
Bradwardine calls it, is not entirely created. For the essential ground of
*“it is right for the creature to be subject and obedient to the Creator,”*
likewise of *“good must be done”* or *“one must act well,”* and of *“one must
not sin against GOD,”* is not entirely in the creature but chiefly in the
Creator. For because GOD is the infinitely good, most wise, and most just
Creator, and so forth, all natural and moral subjection and all obedience is
owed to him as GOD. He must not be offended, nor must one sin against him, not
merely because we are Creatures but rather because he is GOD, who cannot fail
to command every good and forbid every evil. For because GOD is essentially
just and good, it is repugnant for him to be GOD if everything he commands is
not good and everything he forbids evil. Therefore, doing good, refusing to do
evil, refusing to sin, and the creature’s subjection to the Creator do not fall
under GOD’s free decree. GOD commands and forbids these things as GOD, not as a
free legislator. (2.) When we say *“it is right for the creature to be subject
to the Creator,”* either we speak of aptitudinal and fundamental subjection, by
which every creature can and from eternity could be subject to the Creator, or
of actual and formal subjection, by which it is right that the creature
actually obey the Creator. In the former way, aptitudinal subjection is only
the non-repugnance of obeying; and no formal created moral rectitude follows
from it. For on the supposition that GOD had created nothing outside himself
and no creature had ever existed in the nature of things,-*

[Marginal reference: Ibid., page 347, number 13.]

*this objective non-repugnance of obeying GOD would have been due to him,
because he is GOD. Again, it is right for the creature actually to be subject
and obedient to GOD by the very fact that GOD is the first cause and Creator,
while the creature is GOD’s effect and creature. But since this moral rectitude
in general is from GOD insofar as he is GOD, the whole question concerns whence
or from what cause it comes that this entitative act, indifferent in itself, is
good or evil. For all goodness and malice in the moral genus is founded in an
entitative act in the genus of nature, and every moral privation and
lawlessness is founded in an act. Certainly no simple entitative act is evil in
itself, just as no faculty of sight is blind in itself; and every entitative act
seems to me indifferent, and to be good or evil from GOD’s will as creator of
every purely created moral rectitude. Hence it must always be held that moral
good and obedience in general cannot fail to be commanded by GOD, but the
question always remains: whence does it come that this act is good and that one
evil? If we speak of what is naturally just, GOD wills it necessarily, not
freely. He cannot create a rational creature without, by the very fact that he
creates it, also creating it endowed with the moral obligation of rendering
natural obedience to GOD the Creator, as has often been said. But legal justice
is something else, distinguished from natural justice, as Bradwardine
observes; and the ground of legal justice, as also of legal obedience, is
subject to GOD’s free decree. Hence a human being can be necessarily obligated
to render obedience and subjection to GOD in general—for example, to obey GOD,
to act well, to worship, fear, and love above all things the one true GOD, not
to blaspheme and not to hate GOD. Nevertheless, one is not obligated to this or
that species. That is, by the very fact that one is a rational creature, one
does not have a natural law requiring legal obedience, as distinguished from
natural obedience—for example, not eating the fruit of the tree of knowledge,
or repaying a loan to the Egyptians if the Israelites are about to depart from
Egypt. Nor may anyone object that unless some acts are designated in species
and form, through which and in which a person is necessarily bound to display
obedience, it would follow that no obedience is necessary—for a genus is
nothing other in reality than its species—and that whatever acts of obedience
are posited, it could equally be said that GOD comports himself freely toward
them antecedently, just as he does toward others, so that he could will or
refuse them, will to command them or not to command them, or command the-*

*opposite—for example, that a human should not know GOD, should have or retain
no natural conscience, should hate GOD, and should worship the devil. For a
human being is obligated as a rational creature both to obedience in general
and to obedience in a specific form—namely, this natural obedience to be
rendered to God: to love GOD, to fear the one true GOD, and not to worship the
devil—so that GOD could not have commanded the contrary. But as a rational
creature, one was not thus bound to all species of obedience, namely, both the
natural and legal species to be rendered to God. For GOD could command or not
command a rational creature whatever is legally just, or could even command
the contrary. A genus is nothing other in reality than its species.* An animal
is in reality a human and a brute insofar as they actually exist, and obedience
is in reality this particular obedience. But it does not follow that, because
a creature necessarily owes obedience to God, the creature also necessarily
owes any obedience other than that which GOD’s free will commands.

Some object: *“On our position, there is no Atheism, Epicureanism, Diabolism,
magic, hatred and denial of GOD, blasphemy,* and so forth, *which GOD could not
freely will to command, since all these depend solely upon GOD’s free decree and
command, so that they are set forth as determined toward this or that side by
GOD’s will of sign and command. It would consequently follow that GOD, if he
willed, could be the author or approver of disobedience. For there is no
obligation in humanity and no right of obligation in GOD which, necessarily
and antecedently to GOD’s will and law, could prevent this, according to the
Adversaries.”*

I answer: if they proved that such consequences follow from our position, I
would bid it farewell. For we grant that there is some first and essentially
just thing which is naturally prior to GOD’s free decree. *“These just things,”*
says Bradwardine, *“are not subject in any way to either the Divine or the
human will,”* because the natural law in GOD prevents him from being able to
command what is essentially evil. Next, those who seem simply to deny that
something is just because God wills it, and that GOD does not will it because
it is just, would also say that GOD cannot prohibit repaying a loan. Yet he
could prohibit it, and still he would forbid a disobedient obedience, as these
authors will have it. For just as the entitative act which underlies hatred of
God is not in itself sin, but the physical foundation of malice,-*

[Marginal reference: Bradwardine, book 1, chapter 21, letter c, page 231.]

[Chapter XXII; printed page 345; IA PDF page 405; BSB canvas 397]

*malice and not malice itself—two things which not even Vorstius denies to
exist in every sin. 2. It is an act, not a privation which ought to inhere in
an action; for by no logic can an act and a privation be the same. 3. The act
is effectively from God as its author, but the malice in hatred of GOD is in no
way from him.* Therefore, antecedently to GOD’s free action of creating a
rational creature, GOD could have commanded that entitative act which
underlies hatred of GOD. He could, I say, have commanded it to another
creature differing in species and nature from humanity and angels, and in that
case it would have been no more a sin than eating from the tree of knowledge
is sin antecedently to GOD’s free prohibition. I acknowledge that the case of
Atheism is different. For GOD cannot command any creature to believe something
false, that there are many Gods, or that the Devil is God, because such
propositions intrinsically involve a contradiction. For *“there are many
Gods”* and *“the Devil is God”* involve a contradiction. But just as GOD cannot
make contradictories simultaneously true—not from impotence, but because he is
the first truth—so he cannot command any creature to believe that something
which simultaneously does not exist does exist. Nor, in this entire debate, do
I desire anything more than that all praise and glory for created goodness be
rendered to GOD, and that every creature—Human, Angel, and whatever has been
created—lie before the footstool of GOD’s throne.

Assertion 6. But through what has been said, punitive justice in no way inheres
in GOD by necessity of nature, but freely. For among the many arguments which
Dr. Twisse adduces, this one is insoluble to me. GOD by punitive justice gave
his innocent Son, the Lord Jesus Christ, eternally to be adored, over to death;
and he could have refrained from condemning him to death. For GOD did not
condemn his Son to death by any necessity of nature, because then GOD would not
have been GOD if he had not condemned his Son to death, which is absurd. For he
handed his Son over to death from gratuitous love, John 3:16; Romans 8:32. Nor
do those answer who say that *“CHRIST was not innocent, but a sinner by
imputation and made a sinner for us,”* 2 Corinthians 5:21, and that *“a sinner
