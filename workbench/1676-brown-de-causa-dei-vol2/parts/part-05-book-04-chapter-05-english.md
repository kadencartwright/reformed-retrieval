# CHAPTER V

## The law of the Decalogue is a perpetual rule of conduct, even under the New Testament

In the preceding chapter we showed to what extent, or in what sense, the law
of the Decalogue, or moral law, may be said to have been abrogated or
antiquated now under the Gospel. We also denied that this law has been
abrogated insofar as it is the standard of life and conduct. We now confirm
this against the *Antinomians* by arguments drawn from the word of God.

1. When Christ himself interprets this law and vindicates it from the corrupt
glosses of the Pharisees and other false teachers, he declares plainly enough
that this law was by no means abrogated by his coming. He speaks as follows in
Matthew 5:17–19: *“Do not suppose that I came to dissolve the law or the
Prophets. I did not come to dissolve it but to fulfill it. For truly I say to
you, until heaven and earth pass away, not one jot or one point will pass from
the law until all things have been accomplished. Therefore, whoever loosens
one of the least of these commandments and teaches people so will be called
least in the kingdom of heaven.”* The following explanation, in which he
expounds various precepts of the moral law, makes sufficiently clear which law
he is speaking about here. He did not come, therefore, to dissolve
(*katalysai*) or destroy this law. Yet he would destroy it if he antiquated or
abrogated it, for an abrogated law is wholly dissolved and destroyed. Nor does
he wish anyone to loosen this law in either doctrine or practice. He says that
the person who loosens this law in doctrine and violates it in practice will
be called least in the kingdom of heaven, while the person who establishes it
in practice and doctrine will be called great. Who, then, will now teach that
this law has been removed and wholly dissolved and no longer has the force of
a standard binding us to conformity? Consult Calvin and the others cited by
Marlorat on this passage.

2. Paul, following in his Lord's footsteps and foreseeing that many would be
ready to corrupt the teaching of the Gospel and turn it into license—just as
Christ had foreseen that some would dream that by his coming he had removed
this law and exempted his followers from rendering obedience to it—anticipates
this objection and rejects it with indignation in Romans 3:31: *“Do we then
render the law useless through faith?”* Such was the objection. He answers,
*“By no means; rather, we establish the law.”* Since he had previously said
and proved that *“a person is justified by faith apart from the works of the*

[Printed p. 48 | IA PDF 56 | IA leaf 56 | IA decausadei02brow]

*law,”* namely, the moral law—for throughout nearly the whole following fourth
chapter he continues the same teaching by the example of Abraham, who was
justified before the ceremonial law was enacted, and this instance of Abraham
would have contributed nothing to the apostle's purpose if he had been
speaking only of the ceremonial law—he foresaw that his opponents would infer
that by this teaching he made the moral law useless. But he denies that this
absurdity follows from the teaching of the Gospel. Rather, its contrary
follows: the teaching of the Gospel tends more firmly to establish the law.
Therefore, under the Gospel the moral law does not cease or become abolished;
it is instead established. See Marlorat's ecclesiastical exposition of the
passage.

3. The apostle's teaching in Romans 7 proves the same thing. In the *last
verse* he says that with his *mind*—that is, insofar as he was regenerate—he
*“served the law of God.”* This is the law which he calls *“holy, just, and
good”* in verses 12 and 16, through which *“sin becomes exceedingly sinful”*
in verse 13, in which he *“delighted according to the inward man”* in verse
22, through which he *“knew sin,”* especially covetousness, and which says,
*“You shall not covet,”* in verse 7. But if this law were now entirely
ineffectual, wholly abolished and abrogated, this entire struggle of the
apostle would have been vain. How could a dead and abolished law have revived
sin? Yet the apostle says in verse 9 that *“apart from the law sin was dead,
but when the commandment came, sin revived.”* How could a law abolished and
abrogated by Christ's coming have rendered a person who once lived dead? Can
anything of the kind occur when the transgression of an abrogated law becomes
known? How can the things in verses 10 and 11 be applied to any abolished
commandment? Can sin become exceedingly sinful through a commandment wholly
abrogated and possessing no binding force, as the apostle says in verse 13?
Not at all.

4. Further, if this moral law has now been abrogated under the Gospel, why
does the apostle press the precepts of the same law, as may be seen from
Romans 12:9 through the end? Many commands occur there which are imposed upon
the Romans and are nothing other than particular precepts of the moral law,
the same precepts which formerly held God's people bound under the Old
Testament, as is evident to everyone acquainted with the ancient law. He also
presses a moral duty by an argument drawn from the Old Testament in verse 19:
*“Do not avenge yourselves, but give place to wrath, for it is written,
Vengeance is mine; I will repay, says the Lord.”* See Deuteronomy 32:35.

5. We can draw a similar argument from Romans 13:8–10: *“For he who loves
another has fulfilled the law. Indeed, You shall not commit adultery; you
shall not kill; you shall not steal; you shall not bear false witness; you
shall not covet; and if there is any other commandment, it is summarily
comprehended in this saying. Therefore love is the fulfillment of the law.”*
By this argument the apostle urges people to love one another, namely, because
this love is the fulfillment of the law—the moral law, of which he enumerates
five particular precepts and comprehends the rest of the second table under
the general summary and compendium of the whole second table of the
Decalogue: *“You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”* See Matthew 22:39 and
Mark 12:31. But this argument would have no force whatever if this whole moral
law had now been abrogated.

6. To the same end we may also press Christ's words in Matthew 7:12 and Luke
6:31: *“Therefore, whatever you wish people to do to you, do the same to them,
for*

[Printed p. 49 | IA PDF 57 | IA leaf 57 | IA decausadei02brow]

*this is the law and the Prophets.”* Therefore the law which commands this and
the words of the prophets which urge and explain the same thing still bind
us. That law is the moral law, for this is the summary of its second table, as
Christ explains in Matthew 22:39–40.

7. Indeed, whatever was moral in the properly judicial, ceremonial, or mixed
laws binds us even now under the Gospel, as is clear from Paul's words in 1
Corinthians 9:8–10: *“Do I say these things according to man? Does not the law
also say this? For it is written in the law of Moses”*—namely, Deuteronomy
25:4—*“You shall not muzzle the mouth of an ox that treads out grain. Is God
concerned about oxen? Or does he say this entirely for our sake? For it was
written for our sake.”* From this very law, which he affirms was written for
us, he proves that wages should be paid to ministers of the Gospel, as is
clear from the context. Much more, therefore, does the moral law itself still
remain in force. The same apostle uses this same argument to press the same
duty in 1 Timothy 5:17–18: *“Let the elders who rule well be considered worthy
of double honor—for Scripture says, You shall not muzzle an ox that treads out
grain.”*

8. The apostle's statement in Romans 4:15 is a common and accepted maxim:
*“Where there is no law, there is no transgression.”* Romans 5:13 is similar:
*“Sin is not imputed when no law exists.”* Therefore, if the moral law has now
been abolished, no transgression of the moral law exists. The regenerate are
not, and cannot be, guilty of violating the law and consequently are free from
all sin. But this plainly contradicts John's statement in 1 John 1:8, 10: *“If
we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves and the truth is not in us.
If we say that we have not sinned, we make him a liar and his word is not in
us.”* But if our opponents say that the regenerate can sin, I ask: against
what law? For sin is always a violation of law. If they say, against the law
of Christ, I ask again: what is this law of Christ? Is it not the moral law
itself? Compare Galatians 6:2 with Leviticus 19:18. If they say that the
regenerate sin against the love of Christ, which requires something very
different of them, I reply that Christ's love is not properly the standard of
living, but rather a motive for living well, and it presupposes a law
according to which we are to live. Although a motive or inducement, as such,
makes a sin more atrocious, the formal nature of sin consists in its being a
transgression of law, not in its being something done against a displayed
motive. Further, if every sin were now an act or omission against Christ's
love, no sin would now exist other than ingratitude, and absolutely all sins
would belong to the very same lowest species or would be so many individual
acts of ingratitude. Besides, does the moral law not require gratitude, that
we do good to our benefactors, and that we not repay evil for good?

9. The apostle John's statement in 1 John 3:4, *“Sin is the transgression of
the law,”* proves the same thing. Since sin now exists even in the regenerate,
a law whose transgression is sin must also exist. But transgression of an
abrogated law is not sin, for sin is transgression of a law which requires
obedience and binds to compliance and conformity.

10. When the apostle says in Romans 8:7–8 that *“the mind of the flesh is
hostility against God and is not subject to God's law, nor indeed can it be,
and therefore those who are in the flesh*

[Printed p. 50 | IA PDF 58 | IA leaf 58 | IA decausadei02brow]

*cannot please God,”* he presupposes that a law remains in force to which
those who are not in the flesh but in the Spirit—that is, those who are
regenerate and please God—can and actually do submit. This is the same law to
which the unregenerate do not and cannot submit while they remain in that
state, namely, the moral law. Because they neither can nor will observe this
law, they neither please God nor can please him.

11. In the Epistle to the Galatians, where Paul writes so much against the law
considered in a special manner, he nevertheless presses the same law as a
perpetual standard of life and conduct when he urges people to serve one
another through love in chapter 5:13–14 by this argument: *“For the whole law
is fulfilled in one saying, namely, You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”*
This is the moral law, or the second table of that law, as is well known. The
apostle James does the same in James 2:8: *“If you fulfill the royal law
according to Scripture, You shall love your neighbor as yourself, you do
well.”* Those apostles could not have spoken this way if that moral law had
now been antiquated.

12. In verses 9–11 of the chapter just cited, the same apostle James supplies
another argument when he asks them not to show partiality. He uses the
argument that they should not commit sin and be convicted by the law as
transgressors, *“because,”* he says, *“whoever has kept the whole law but
stumbled in one point has become guilty of all.”* What law is this? What
follows explains it: *“For,”* he says, *“he who said, Do not commit adultery,
also said, Do not kill,”* and so forth. This is the moral law. It is therefore
clear that the moral law has not now been abrogated, because if we do not wish
to be regarded as transgressors of this law and become guilty of all its
precepts, we must take serious care not to stumble in any point and so become
transgressors of the law. But an abrogated and wholly abolished law cannot be
violated, for it has been deprived of every binding force.

13. The same apostle's words in James 4:11 prove the same thing: *“Do not
speak against one another. He who speaks against his brother and condemns his
brother speaks against the law and condemns the law. But if you condemn the
law, you are not an observer of the law but a judge.”* He dissuades them from
that evil—from speaking against or condemning their brothers—because whoever
acted this way toward his brother would speak against the law itself and
condemn it. He would thereby constitute himself a judge and usurp God's
throne, rather than being an observer of the law. James presupposes that the
single duty of human beings is to observe God's law. Therefore, God's moral
law still binds us; otherwise, whatever we did against our brothers, we would
not speak against or condemn the law or be held guilty of violating it.

14. All sounder theologians acknowledge that the moral law differs from the
ceremonial and judicial laws in this respect: the latter were enacted only for
a defined period, but the former in perpetuity. The latter concerned a
particular administration of the covenant of grace and constitution of the
Church, but the former was delivered to the Church and people of God as the
rule of life and conduct in every condition and administration. It therefore
had binding force from the beginning of the Church's institution, long before
the Mosaic laws were given, and indeed was prescribed to innocent man as his
standard before the fall. It is thus the standard for man insofar as he is a
creature subject to his Creator.

15. Indeed, when Scripture speaks about the abrogation of the ceremonial law,

[Printed p. 51 | IA PDF 59 | IA leaf 59 | IA decausadei02brow]

it uses words and phrases which it never uses when speaking about the moral
law. Christ *“loosened the barrier of the dividing wall,”* Ephesians 2:14
(*lysas*), but we never read this stated of the moral law. Indeed, we read the
contrary: Christ did not come to dissolve the law (*katalysai*). Christ
*“abolished the law of commandments placed in rites”* (*katargēsas*), but the
apostle nowhere says that the moral law was abolished. Indeed, he expressly
denies that he abolishes the law by his teaching in the final verse of Romans
3: *“Do we therefore abolish the law through faith? By no means; rather, we
establish the law.”* He does say that *we* have been discharged from the law
in Romans 7:6: *“we have been discharged from the law.”* Thus he does not say
that the law has died or been put to death for us, but that *we* have been put
to death to the law, Romans 7:4: *“you also were put to death to the law,”*
and Galatians 2:19: *“For through the law I died to the law.”* He thereby
clearly intimates that no change occurs in the moral law, but rather in us and
our condition. Likewise, when the apostle speaks of the ceremonial law, he
says that a change in it occurred, or necessarily had to occur
(*metathesis*), Hebrews 7:12. But we nowhere read this stated of the moral law.
Similarly, in verse 18 he says that the commandment is annulled because of its
weakness and uselessness (*athetēsis*), but we never read this stated of any
moral commandment. He also says that this law becomes old, grows aged, and
vanishes away in the final verse of Hebrews 7: *“What becomes old and grows
aged is near to vanishing away.”* Where does anything of the kind appear to be
said of the moral law?

16. The daily experience of the godly confirms this truth. They feel their
consciences excuse them when this law is observed and accuse them whenever it
is violated. This activity of conscience sufficiently indicates that this law
still has binding force. For when anything is committed against the ancient
ceremonial law, conscience does not accuse the regenerate in this way, because
that law has now been abolished and has lost all binding force. The
*Antinomians* should not say, with the libertine Porquius of whom Calvin speaks
in *Against the Libertines*, that sin is nothing other than seeing and feeling
sin within us, and consequently that a person sins whenever his conscience
accuses him because this law has been violated. For that paves the way to all
profanity and at last even to atheism itself.

17. If the moral law has now been abolished so that it is no longer the rule
of conduct for the regenerate, the use of Holy Scripture for our direction
and instruction also disappears, for Holy Scripture explains and proposes
this law which ought to be our standard and rule. We will therefore not need
to consult the law and testimony and reject whatever does not conform to this
law and testimony, contrary to Isaiah 8:20. Scripture will not then be useful
for teaching, reproof, correction, and training in righteousness, contrary to
2 Timothy 3:16. The Spirit of God speaking in Scripture will not be the
supreme judge of all controversies. Nor must every teaching proposed by human
beings be examined by this rule. Consequently, the Bereans in Acts 17:11, who
examined the Scriptures to see whether the things spoken by Paul were so,
should not have been praised but rather condemned.

18. [*“If the law,”* says Apollonius in *On the Law of God*, p. 229, *“does
not bind and direct the conscience of a regenerate person, then by doing and
observing the law from conscience he does not render worship pleasing to God
but worship of his own choosing, and does something beyond the duty and
obligation by which he is bound and subjected to God through the law. But
Scripture tells*

[Printed p. 52 | IA PDF 60 | IA leaf 60 | IA decausadei02brow]

*us clearly enough that by observing the law believers fulfill the good,
pleasing, and perfect will of God and render that obedience and worship in
which they please God, doing nothing beyond duty and obligation: Therefore,
when you have done all the things commanded you, say, We are unprofitable
servants, for we have done what we were bound to do,”* Luke 17:10. *“Thus the
law binds the consciences of believers and has that power over them by which
a duty is imposed upon them and they are constrained to do the things which
are just and good and omit those which are unjust and evil.”*]

19. If this law is abolished as our rule and standard, no means will remain
for distinguishing true and lawful worship from false and forbidden worship.
We know that God must be worshiped only in the manner prescribed by him, not
in a manner fashioned and devised by human beings. For he frequently condemns
worship prescribed by human beings simply because it was not commanded by
him, Jeremiah 7:31. The Holy Spirit condemns all *self-chosen worship* and
regards worship prescribed from human commandments and doctrines as mere
superstition, Colossians 2:22–23.

20. If this moral law is removed, the means most suited to disclose the hidden
wickedness and depravity of our heart is removed. This spiritual law
penetrates the innermost recesses of hearts and reveals the deviation of the
very first motions. It thereby discloses our hidden original corruption and
increasingly opens and displays to our sight that fountain of every sin.
Hence the apostle says in Romans 7:7, *“I did not know sin except through the
law, for I would not have known covetousness itself unless the law had said,
You shall not covet.”* The disclosure of this marsh and pool contributes
greatly to humbling the godly themselves and driving them to Christ as the
fountain opened for the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem for
sin and for separation from uncleanness, Zechariah 13:1, so that they may
daily be purified more and more, have their conscience cleansed from dead
works, Hebrews 19:14, and be acquitted of hidden faults, Psalm 19:12.

21. Further, if this law's binding force is removed, believers are deprived
of an effective means for urging and stirring them to perform good works. The
beginnings of new obedience in the regenerate are weak and languid and thus
must be fostered and awakened. The regenerate therefore need the spurs of the
threats annexed to the divine precepts. They also need a bridle to restrain
the untamed corruption still remaining in them, lest it leap beyond all the
bounds of godliness and compel and drive a person to perpetrate crimes.
Apollonius rightly says on p. 222 of the work cited above: [*“Just as an
untamed horse is checked and constrained by a bridle, while a tamed horse is
kept by the same bridle within the bounds of the right path according to the
rider's direction, so intractable people are checked by God's law, while the
regenerate are kept within the bounds of those works in which they ought to
walk according to God's will. For since the regenerate are still partly
carnal, they still need a bridle to restrain the flesh with its lusts, lest it
break out into outward sins and throw off the Spirit as its rider. Hence
Luther on Galatians 3 says,”* because of the flesh the regenerate still need a
tutor who exercises and vexes the strong ass, so that by this tutoring sins
may be diminished and the way prepared for Christ.]

22. Through the law which binds them to obedience, the regenerate themselves
see how great a benefit Christ has bestowed and how greatly they are bound to
love that supreme Redeemer. When they see how wretched they are because of
that body

[Printed p. 53 | IA PDF 61 | IA leaf 61 | IA decausadei02brow]

of death, they most willingly thank God through Jesus Christ, Romans 7:24–25.
They also see how necessary it is to flee to Christ daily, adhere to and rely
upon him, and how impossible it is to attain life by their own righteousness.
But if the moral law is removed or abrogated, the upright will be deprived of
all these and similar benefits.

23. If this moral law has been abrogated, this occurred either because at its
first institution and enactment its duration was limited to some certain and
defined period beyond which it was not to continue, or because the Lawgiver
himself expressly revoked it and abolished it by the interposition of his
authority. But neither can be said. Not the *former*, because nothing of the
kind appears in its first institution. It did not contain a shadow of good
things to come and thus was not to continue only until a time of reformation,
as was the ceremonial law. Nor the *latter*, because God has abolished this
law neither by a simple revocation nor by substituting another, contrary law.
Not the first of these, because this is nowhere stated; if our opponents
perhaps bring forward a testimony of Scripture to this effect, it will be
vindicated later. Nor the second, because no such contrary law can be devised.
The inward law written upon hearts, or the law of Christ about which our
opponents so frequently prattle, does not conflict with this law, but is
rather the same law considered differently, as will appear later when we come
to answer the opponents' objections.

24. If the moral law has been wholly abrogated so that it does not now bind
believers to obedience, the law of nature itself has been removed. But this is
absurd. Therefore, and so forth. The connection of the *major premise* is
clear: the moral law promulgated from Mount Sinai is in reality the same as
the law of nature itself, for it comprises all the precepts of the law of
nature. Therefore, if the moral law has been universally abrogated, the law of
nature has wholly perished as well. But this cannot be said, for it is written
upon human hearts; that is, it is so fixed and engraved upon the human heart
that its knowledge and obligation cannot be erased. Hence the apostle says in
Romans 2:14–15 that *“the Gentiles who do not have the law are a law to
themselves and show the work of the law written upon their hearts, their
conscience bearing witness together with them, and their thoughts mutually
accusing or even defending one another.”*

25. Being under the moral law no more conflicts with the state of grace than
it conflicted with the state of innocence. In the state of innocence Adam had
the moral law written upon his heart, for he was created in the image of God,
endowed with righteousness, knowledge, and holiness, Ecclesiastes 7:29;
Ephesians 4:24; Colossians 2:10. Since man in the state of friendship was
subject to this moral law, who will persuade us that man in the state of
reconciliation is exempt from this subjection?

26. Our opponents admit that this privilege—if it deserves to be called a
privilege—is not common to everyone under the New Testament but peculiar only
to believers. They must therefore concede that it follows from believers'
union with Christ, which occurs through faith. We ask, then, why this
privilege was not common to all believers under the Old Testament. Were they
not truly united to Christ by faith just as believers under the New Testament
are? If this cannot be denied, why did they not share this privilege at least
in part? Does it have any foundation other than faith in Christ? Let them say
what that foundation is. I know that believers under the New Testament share
many privileges which believers under the Old Testament did not possess. But
for the most part this difference concerns different degrees or things which
pertained specifically

[Printed p. 54 | IA PDF 62 | IA leaf 62 | IA decausadei02brow]

to that legal or ceremonial administration of the covenant of grace which
looked toward Christ as still to come. It does not concern things common to
the Church under every administration, as this supposed total abrogation of
the moral law would be—or its abrogation with respect to its power to direct,
reveal God's will, bind to obedience, impose punishment upon the disobedient,
and promise reward to the obedient. How, I ask, can a difference between
believers under the Old and New Testaments be shown in this respect? What some
assert—that God saw sins in believers under the Old Testament but does not see
sins in believers under the New—is a mere fiction. For Balaam's words in
Numbers 23:21, *“He does not behold iniquity in Jacob, nor see perversity in
Israel,”* were pronounced concerning ancient Israel. They are therefore no
less true of believers under the Old Testament than of believers under the
New. Indeed, they were said of that whole people and not only of believers.

27. Does Paul not urge children in Ephesians 6:1–3 to obey their parents in
the Lord by an argument drawn from the moral law itself? *“For this is just,”*
he says. *“Honor your father and mother, which is the first commandment with a
promise, so that it may go well with you and you may live long upon the
earth.”* The apostle therefore acknowledged that the binding force of the
moral law had by no means been diminished.

28. If this privilege flows from the New Testament administration insofar as
it is distinguished from the Old Testament administration, then it is not
peculiar to believers alone but common also to the ungodly. But our opponents
will not concede this. Yet many of their arguments confirm the antecedent and
prove nothing else, as will appear in the next chapter. The *consequence* is
firm: just as the Old Testament administration was in a certain manner common
to the godly and ungodly who lived under it, so also the New Testament
administration is in a certain manner common to the godly and ungodly who live
under it.

29. If holiness does not conflict with the Gospel state, obligation to the
moral law will not conflict with that state. But the former is true.
Therefore, and so forth. No one who considers the following passages will
deny the *minor premise*: Ephesians 1:4 and verse 27; Colossians 1:22; 1 Peter
1:15–16; Revelation 22:11; Hebrews 12:14; 1 Timothy 2:15; Romans 6:19; 2
Corinthians 7:1, with many others. The *consequence* is evident from this:
holiness is nothing other than the conformity of our inclinations, actions,
and words to God's revealed will concerning our duty. This will of God is
signified by the law, for a divine law is simply a sign of the divine will
concerning our duty, as was explained above in Book I, chapter XI.

30. Jonston proves the same thing in *The Socinian Opinion concerning the
Sabbath*, and so forth, p. 68 and following, by many arguments drawn from the
*form* of the law of the Decalogue; its *matter*; its *proximate cause*, or
the proximate foundation of its authority; its *proper, internal, adequate,
and ultimate end*; its *correlative*, or the subject of the obligation; its
*occupied object*; its uses and effects; and its *adjuncts*. There he shows
fully and clearly that all these things still obtain under the New Testament,
and from this he proves that the law of the Decalogue itself still has force
under the New Testament. There he also employs other arguments which the
reader may consult.
