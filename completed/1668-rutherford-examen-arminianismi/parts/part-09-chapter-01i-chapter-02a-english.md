It can be objected: If an erroneous conscience does not oblige, but binds, in
so far as a person cannot act against its dictate, then if an erroneous
conscience dictates that one must not pray, the person is obliged not to pray;
if it dictates to a judge that a murderer must not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 135 | PDF p. 186]

be punished with the sword, then the judge is obliged not to punish the
murderer with the sword. For if a person in such a case does not abstain from
prayer and from punishing the murderer with the sword, then the erroneous
conscience will not even bind, because he will act against the dictate of the
erroneous conscience.

Response. Although an erroneous conscience dictates that one must not pray,
it does not follow that the person is obliged simply not to pray. An Arminian
magistrate believes in his erroneous conscience that it is not lawful to kill
a murderer, and in conscience he does not dare to act against the dictate of
the erroneous conscience, since it binds. Therefore, he is bound to fail in
his office and not kill the murderer. I deny that this follows. He is bound
not to kill with respect to the erring conscience, but he is not bound to kill
him with an erroneous conscience. It must be proved, however, that he is bound
simply not to kill. He is bound to lay aside the erroneous conscience and to
kill.

Although an erroneous conscience binds negatively to a deed not done, in this
manner, namely, to the erroneous element which is in the deed—or does not bind
to the error of the deed—nevertheless it in no way binds positively. Thus the
scholastics say that an erroneous conscience suffices for vice, not for
virtue; and that it ensnares, but does not bind.

One binding concerns the deed; another concerns the erroneous manner of the
deed. The Arminian magistrate is bound to abstain from punishing the murderer
with respect to the erroneous conscience that binds him; that is, he is bound
not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 136 | PDF p. 187]

to punish in an erroneous manner and with an erring conscience. Nevertheless,
he is obliged simply not to abstain, but to punish.

Objection. If an erroneous conscience imposes an obligation and bond, or a
binding, not to act against what the erroneous conscience dictates must be
done, then the knowledge of conscience has the force of law. Romans 14:14: “To
him who believes—although by an erroneous conscience—that anything is
unclean, to him it is unclean.” But what obliges is an immediate rule
established by God. Therefore, an erroneous conscience is a law for me *per
accidens*, though not in itself. Thus Thomas Goodwin in an English treatise,
*On the Aggravation of Sin*, page 45.

Response. I distinguish the major premise. If an erroneous conscience imposes
upon a person an obligation not to act against what it dictates, then it has
the force of a negative law—that is, one obliging to a deed not done, or to
abstinence from the deed in such a manner. This is true. But that it then has
the force of law simply and positively, and of an immediate rule, is false.

Jean Gerson, part 1, *On the Nature and Quality of Conscience*, treatise *On
Three Things Contributing to the Gravity of Sin*: “But when conscience
dictates things which are beside God's law, conscience itself binds and
obliges, yet not simply, but in a qualified respect and under a certain
disjunction, or alternatively. It binds one to do what it dictates, or to lay
itself aside. But in those things which it dictates against

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 137 | PDF p. 188]

the law, it binds, yet in no way to the things it dictates, but simply to be
laid aside.”

Error does not bind and does not oblige, but entangles; or, if you prefer, it
obliges one to lay aside the error. An erroneous conscience binds one to
suspend an act when the act is not necessary. It binds to the negative, not
to the affirmative. It binds in such a way that to oppose an erroneous
conscience is sin, but not in such a way that following it while it errs is
virtue.

Ludovicus Meratius, volume 1, treatise *On the Goodness and Malice of Acts*,
disputation 3, section 2, figure 8, says that it is the common opinion that a
conscience erring vincibly obliges both negatively and positively, and indeed
to sin—not in itself and absolutely, but *per accidens* and upon the
hypothesis that a person was unwilling to lay it aside, although laying it
aside lay within his free power. He proves this from Galatians 5:3: “I testify
to every man who circumcises himself that he is a debtor to do the whole law.”

Response. This opinion is false. For that to which someone is thus obliged is
sin, whether he is obliged to it in itself or *per accidens*; and it is either
sin in itself or sin *per accidens*. But God has placed in conscience no
binding force to sin in any manner. Second, the passage in Galatians 5:3 does
not prove it. Paul does not mean that the circumcised man is obliged to sin
because he is obliged to fulfill the whole law. This is foolish. For to
fulfill the whole law is to obey God, not to sin.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 138 | PDF p. 189]

Meratius's other reason is this: if an erring conscience obliges negatively
not to abstain from a lie, then by an erroneous conscience one is obliged
positively to lie, because not to abstain from a lie is to lie.

Response. Good man, you err by the whole breadth of heaven. An erring
conscience does not oblige one simply, and according to the substance of the
deed, not to abstain from a lie; it obliges one not to abstain in such a
manner, namely, with an erring conscience. Thus one is obliged to abstain from
the manner, not from the deed.

CHAPTER TWO

On God

TITLE ONE

ON THE KNOWLEDGE OF GOD

COLOSSIANS 1:10

That ye might walk worthily of God—growing in all knowledge.

It is asked whether true and right knowledge of God, and conformity of the
mind with God, are not commanded. The Remonstrants, *Against Vedelius*, book
1, part 1, chapter 10, say: “Speculative knowledge is nothing but a passive
reception, which falls under a command no more than

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 139 | PDF p. 190]

sensing, seeing, and hearing do. But the mind is not capable of a command. The
will alone is capable of a command. Knowledge, taken for holy attention, pious
care, and laudable study in seeking divine truth, is commanded to us, because
knowledge is commanded not for its own sake,” says the *Apology*, chapter 2,
folio 40, “but for worship and piety.”

But to the contrary: 1. Knowledge itself, in itself and for its own sake, is
commanded. First Chronicles 28:9: “And thou, Solomon my son, know the God of
thy father and worship him with a whole heart.” Ephesians 4:23: “And be ye
renewed in the spirit of your mind.” Therefore, the mind itself must be
renewed after the image of God.

2. Paul prayed that God would give the Ephesians “the Spirit of wisdom and
revelation in the knowledge of him,” Ephesians 1:17. He gives thanks to God
that the Corinthians had been enriched “in all knowledge,” 1 Corinthians 1:5.

3. Knowledge in the mind is part of God's image, Colossians 3:10. It is a
virtue in a person, pleasing to God, Jeremiah 9:24; Hosea 6:6; and saving to a
person, John 17:3, belonging to the first commandment of the Decalogue. It is
also numbered among the very virtues of Christ our Savior, Isaiah 11:2. And
the law in Matthew 22:37 requires that the whole soul, everything belonging to
the soul, and whatever powers and inclinations are in it be conformed to God.

4. Ignorance of God and a false conception of God—when we think him like wood
or some created thing—is a grave sin. Isaiah 40:18; Acts 17:24, 29. Otherwise,
the man would not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 140 | PDF p. 191]

sin who conceived of God after the manner of an aged king clothed with gold,
purple, and gems.

5. Ignorance of God is the cause of all other sins and of the temporal and
eternal destruction of human beings. First Samuel 2:12; 1 Corinthians 2:8;
Hosea 4:1; Luke 19:42; 2 Thessalonians 1:8.

6. It is Socinian and heretical to think that we can be saved by observing
God's commands even if we conceive of God wrongly in our mind and are ignorant
of God, his nature, attributes, and operations, in so far as they are revealed
in the Scriptures.

7. Otherwise those would not sin who believed in their mind that God delights
in the slaughter of infants.

8. Otherwise the knowledge of Christ himself—of his natures, offices, and
person—and right faith, in so far as it is distinguished from practice, would
not be worship of God. But knowledge itself of the one and triune God is
worship commanded in itself.

9. If only what fell under an act of the will were commanded, then first
thoughts, the inclinations of the soul, and the mind would in vain be required
to conform to God's law.

10. If the mind is not subject to law, and if all the corruption of the will
and affections now after the fall comes from the darkness of the mind and from
a less clear presentation of the object proposed to it—which I shall afterward
demonstrate to be the mind of the Arminians—then a human being appears to me to
be from birth a creature of altogether angelic

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 141 | PDF p. 192]

purity and innocence, in whom only the mind, which can sin against no law of
God, has been somewhat obscured.

TITLE TWO

On God According to His Essence, and on His Unity and Essential Omnipresence

1 CORINTHIANS 8:6

But to us there is only one God.

Where there is perfect unity, there must be perfect simplicity. Hence it is
asked whether God by his nature is most simple and free of all composition
from act and potency, from subject and accidents, and from all multiplicity
and diversity. The Remonstrants deny it in the *Apology*, chapter 2, folios
41–42, following the impious Vorstius, *On God*, in his notes on disputation 3,
theses 1–2, page 146, and the *Apology*, chapter 2, page 4.

We maintain the contrary. 1. God is absolutely perfect and immutable.
Therefore, there are in God no composing parts, no mixture, and no
composition, because parts are less perfect than the whole. He who is Jehovah
in essence—that is, essence simply, first and perfect, possessing being from
himself, absolute and independent—is most simple. But the former is true of
God. Exodus 3:14: “I shall be, who I shall be, hath sent me.” Genesis 15:7: “I
am Jehovah.” Amos 9:6: “Jehovah is

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 142 | PDF p. 193]

his name.”

2. In whom there are no multiplication and dissolubility, there is the highest
simplicity. But these are not in God.

3. Every composition has something of potency and perfectibility mingled with
it. There is nothing which perfects God.

4. Attributes, relations, and modes of subsisting are not in God after the
manner of a form qualifying, actuating, or informing God, as whiteness is in
milk and justice in a person. Rather, they are God himself considered in one
and another manner and presented to our intellect, as a man remaining in the
same position, changed in no way, is on the right of one person and on the
left of another. And the distinction of the attributes and essence from the
modes is not formal, as diverse entities and essences are distinguished; nor
modal, as a being and the existence of a being are distinguished; nor
potential, as parts and whole are distinguished. It is a distinction of
reason, and indeed a reasoned one, as we distinguish Socrates into subject and
attribute. Thus [God] understands by intellect, not by will, and so forth; and
they therefore differ also by the formality of our imperfect and inadequate
conceptions.

5. Something real would be added to God, and an accession of a new real entity
would be made in him, when in time he becomes Creator, Shepherd, Father,
Redeemer, Protector, Savior, and Lord; and if he annihilated creatures, as he
can, new entities would be taken away from God.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 143 | PDF p. 194]

6. If anything real were added to God's essence through the decrees, creation,
redemption, and personal relations, it would be either created or uncreated.
If created, then at least God's decrees will be created entities coeval and
coeternal with God; and God will not be simply eternal. New creations will
accede to God and recede from him, which would be a true change. But if it is
uncreated, then something uncreated is temporal—for being Creator, Redeemer,
and Shepherd accedes to God in time—which implies a contradiction.

“If [something] could come newly to be in God,” Smising rightly says, volume 1,
*On God*, treatise 2, disputation 3, *On God's Immutability*, question 2,
figure 18, “whether some relation or form, there would be some entity which was
not of necessary being. There would also be composition from subjective
potency and formal act, since things altogether really distinct could not
coalesce into one without a composition of act and potency.”

ACTS 17:27

He is not far from every one of us.

It is asked whether God is omnipresent not only in power and operation, but
also according to his essence. For then, as the Remonstrants say, God's
essence would necessarily be diffused and would be everywhere—in sewers,
flies, idols, devils, and hell. The Remonstrants therefore deny it, saying in
the *Apology*, chapter 2, folio 43: “And, good God! Who could define this
placeless and atomical presence of the divine essence and of the three divine
persons, I say not

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 144 | PDF p. 195]

only in heaven and earth, in sewers, in the foulest idols, in devils, and in
hell itself, but outside heaven and earth, and so forth?” Vorstius, *Response
to the Apology*, chapter 6, pages 10–11. Socinus says the same in the fragment
of his *Catechism on the Knowledge of God*, chapter 11, folio 58.

We teach that God's essence is present everywhere. 1. Psalm 139:8: “If I
ascended into the heavens, thou art there; if I made my bed in the grave, thou
art there,” and so forth. Jeremiah 23:23–24: “Am I a God at hand, saith
Jehovah, and not a God afar off? Do I not fill heaven and earth?” Isaiah 66:1:
“Heaven is my throne; the earth, my footstool.” First Kings 8:27: “Behold, the
heavens themselves and the heavens of heavens do not contain thee; how much
less this house which I have built?” Acts 17:27: “Although he is not far from
every one of us.” Ephesians 4:6: “One God and Father of all, who is above all,
and in you all.”

2. If his essence is not everywhere, it is in some certain and determinate
place, and it is here in such a way that it is not elsewhere. I ask about the
place in which God's essence is not: either it can be there, or it cannot be
there. If it can be there, then God's essence can begin anew to be in a place
where it was not before. But this is a true change of place.

If it cannot be there, then, first, there is some creature which excludes
God's essence.

2. It does not even conflict with creatures to be where their power and
operation are. Therefore, it is absurd that God should operate there where he
himself cannot be.

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 145 | PDF p. 196]

3. There is no reason on God's part why he could not be in that creature when
he operates in it; nor on the creature's part does it conflict for him to be
there, because he could have created the heavens in the same *where* in which
the center of the earth is.

4. God would not operate in the elect if he were not in them; nor would he
know the reins and thoughts if his thought and operation were where he himself
cannot be. His essence would be torn from his power.

5. Nothing is more perfect than the divine essence. But an essence in all
things and everywhere is more perfect than an essence which is only here, so
that it is not elsewhere.

6. Christ walking on earth was not true God if God's essence was not wherever
his humanity was.

7. God could not create infinite worlds outside these imaginary spaces,
because it would be necessary for him to begin to be where he had not been
before.

8. It is a gross dream to imagine God's essence polluted by sewers and devils,
when this does not even happen to sunbeams shining in dung. They imagine that
by physical contact with hell God is defiled by its curse, that by contact
with idols and devils he is morally polluted, and that by contact with sewers
he is physically polluted. But sunlight itself, because it has no contrary
except darkness, is not affected by a dunghill.

9. If God is defiled by presence with

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 146 | PDF p. 197]

the vilest things, he would also be defiled by operation in those wicked and
vilest acts. Why, then, would he not thus concur in the entitative acts of
devils, flies, and so forth? Why not?

10. Devils, idols, and so forth are preserved either immediately and
proximately by the divine essence itself, or mediately by God's conserving
power. If the former, the Arminians err shamefully. If the latter, this
conserving power is either God—and thus God's essence, so that what was
maintained still stands—or a creature. If a creature, the same question
returns concerning its preservation. If the creature preserves itself, then
by parity it creates itself, because conservation is continued creation. Or
it is preserved by God, and this either mediately, which is absurd, or
immediately. Why, then, is the same not said equally of devils and idols, which
without doubt are God's creatures?

It is asked whether it is blasphemous that certain of our theologians say God
is freely good. Arminius says so in his *Response to Article 2*. We say that
our theologians do not speak of God's goodness *ad intra*, by which God is
essentially good, but of God's goodness *ad extra*, by which he freely
communicates his goodness to creatures. For God is good in such a way by
creating the world, giving faith to Peter, and communicating entity and
goodness to creatures that he could have been not good in this manner if he
had not created the world or given faith to Peter, and if he annihilated
creatures.

TITLE THREE

ON THE TRINITY

1 CORINTHIANS 8:6

There is only one God.

It is asked whether there are three essences in God, or only one. The wicked
Vorstius says in his *Response to the Apology*, article 1: “Where there are
three truly distinct from one another, and particular actions belong to each
one, there certainly are three distinct beings and therefore three distinct
essences or entities. Add that the divine persons are true hypostases and
substances, not merely modes of subsisting.” So also the impure Smalcius,
*Response to the Refutation of Smiglecius*, chapter 4, folio 39: “Where there
are two, there must be two essences.”

We, on the contrary, maintain with the orthodox only one essence in the
Trinity. 1. There is only one God, with one essence numerically, and besides
him there is no other God. Deuteronomy 6:4; 32:39; 1 Corinthians 8:6.

2. If there are three distinct divine essences, there are three distinct
natures of deity and three gods; and Vorstius is a tritheist. Arminius asserted
this impious dogma in his *Response to the Thirty-One Articles Scattered
Concerning Himself and Borreus* and *Declaration of His Opinion*, page 125:
“The Son, both as Son and as God, has deity from

[1668 KBNL/UvA witness | logical printed p. 148 | PDF p. 199 | stop before ROM. 9.5.]

the Father.” For if the Son as God has deity from the Father, he has another
essence and deity distinct in number. Deity as deity, begetting without
connoting personality, and deity as deity, begotten, are two deities in
number. The same must be said of the Holy Spirit. The Remonstrants impiously
assert this in their *Declaration*, chapter 3, thesis 3: “The Son, therefore,
and the Spirit are of the same deity with the Father; or they are true sharers
in the divine essence and nature considered absolutely and commonly.” Deity
considered commonly is as a genus with respect to species, as the impious
Vorstius taught that God is a genus in relation to the three persons. It is
certain that three men are sharers in animal [nature], but are not the same
animal numerically. But they say that the three persons are sharers in the
divine nature considered absolutely and commonly; they do not say that they
are one essence and deity numerically, but sharers in deity, as though there
were three deities.

3. But if there are three distinct essences here, there are three infinites,
three omnipotents, three intellects, and three wills—not three having the same
infinity, the same omnipotence, and the same intellect and will. Yet although
the persons are not bare modes of subsisting, but connote substance, they do
not connote three substances, each person connoting a separate substance; but
all [connote] one substance numerically.
