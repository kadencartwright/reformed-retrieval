21. It remains for us to prove that the same man was Apollo. Apollonius,
therefore, sings the following about the Argonauts on the deserted island of
Thynias:

[1611 Book I, printed p. 36 | Google Books PA36]

> They established a broad dance,
> beautifully singing “Iepaieon, Iepaieon Phoebus.”

The scholiast comments on this passage: “Apollo is called *Iepaieon* either
because he sends missiles or because the god is the cause of healing.” This
etymology was assigned to him. As for his proper name, Eustathius says,
“Apollo appears to some to be named from destroying.” These explanations, and
any others of the same kind, fit Joshua perfectly and therefore all claim
their origin from him.

Because *Iehosua* means “healer” to the Hebrews—according to Eusebius's words,
though they are not very soundly derived, “Joshua means healing by Iao, that
is, God's deliverance”—Apollo became *Iepaieon*, the god of medicine; for
among the ancients *paio* meant “I heal.” Because the battle against the
Bashanites was fought from a distance, as Josephus relates and the event
itself persuades us—the Israelites, terrified by the Giants' appearance, did
not dare engage at close quarters but pierced them with arrows from afar—the
poets everywhere introduce Apollo shooting arrows. He is also called
*Iepaieon* “because he sends missiles,” perhaps with the accompanying cry *Ie
pas*: “Strike, boy!” Even if the name does not come from that cry, the
Israelites poured out solemn praises to God for the victory finally gained
under Joshua's leadership. Thus “Io Paean” is sung to Apollo, and at least the
ancient paeans, above all, had their origin here. Eustathius describes them:
“A paean is a hymn for the ending of evils, whether present or expected.”

Because the Amorites barred the Israelites' road through their territory as
they journeyed toward Canaan, where they intended to establish the sacred
tabernacle, God's ark, and the divine oracle, the poets said that the dragons
and serpents whom Apollo fights opposed him so that he could not establish his
oracle. Because the Canaanite nation was destroyed through Joshua, the poets
name Apollo from “destroying”—the sense in which *Apolluon* occurs in sacred
literature. A historian appears to support this, saying that in the war of the
gods the offspring of the Giants was utterly destroyed.

Finally, Apollo is famous for nothing more than killing Python. Homer likewise
celebrates him greatly for killing Typhon. What follows? Python and Typhon are
the same, both by an evident anagram and by an evident relationship; Typhon is
Og. It does not prevent this that Homer has *Typhoea* rather than *Typhona*:
that is too slight to count, and every writer later than Homer called the one
whom Homer named Typhoeus “Typhon.” Since, for whatever reason, the wolf is
sacred to Apollo among

One thing remains to be explained in this history concerning Bacchus's
Sileni. From Pausanias's words we may conjecture that Balaam was their leader.
He affirms that although there were many Sileni, all were mortal: “There is a
memorial of Silenus in the country of the Hebrews and another among the people

speak with a human voice, and that this voice terrified the Giants, Bacchus's
enemies. Where did this come from except sacred literature? “Iehova opened the
ass's mouth.” When the report spread far and wide, no one can doubt that it
made the Moabites faint and greatly terrified them.

Finally, to bring the argument to its conclusion, Moses and Bacchus correspond
especially well in their ends. “Moses, the servant of Iehova, died in the land
of the Moabites at Iehova's command. He buried him in the same valley in the
land of the Moabites opposite Beth Peor, and no one knows his grave to this
day.” Hence Diodorus Siculus says that the Egyptians did not know the location
of Osiris's grave, which Strabo confirms: “One of these places keeps Osiris
hidden from everyone.”

22. Why, you ask, have so many things been recounted from so far back? It is so
that, when you saw that the whole adornment of sacred rites penetrated
from the Hebrews into Greece, that every nation of heavenly beings proceeded
from Egypt, and that all the names, acclamations, ceremonies, and deeds of the
gods were taken from the Jews, you would hold it most certain and thoroughly
established that Hades also came from the same source and was consecrated on
the same principle as the rest. What, I ask, prevents it? This is especially
so because Orpheus, who is said to have first introduced the rites of Father
Liber into Greece—the subject we treated most recently—is also said by
Diodorus to have imported the mysteries of Hades at the same time and by the
same work. He followed what he had learned in Egypt from sacred Hebrew
writings, “partly recalling them and partly inventing material of his own.” A
most trustworthy author establishes that the Egyptians and Jews did not hold
the same conviction about Hades and the underworld. Yet Orpheus, who some say
flourished under the Judges around the time of Cadmus, whom he taught,

> wrote rustic cycles and Egyptian Dionysuses,
> revealing the rites of Osiris, the wandering Evius,

Those celebrated victories of Moses, still famous in recent memory, must have
been better known to him than these mysteries concerning Hades. The older
those mysteries were, the farther removed from him they must necessarily have
been. As if the most ancient things had not visited Greece long before! The
Egyptians recorded that the world was divided into seventy-two parts. The
same Plutarch who said that the Egyptians incorporated Jewish affairs into
the history of Bacchus just recalled by us, because they introduced Jerusalem
and Judea into the narrative of Osiris, also mentions—from ancient theology,
of course—the dove released from Noah's ark. From where did Iapetos come but
Japheth, or Vulcan but Tubal-cain?

Indeed, Orpheus himself mentions man's creation from the earth. Eusebius gives
his words: “Orpheus said that the human race was fashioned by the divine one
from the earth and received from him a rational soul.” He drew man's formation
from the earth—that is, from *Adamah*—from sacred literature. Should we think
that he did not also draw from it *Adamah* itself, that is, Hades, and pour it
into Greek? As a capstone add Suidas's testimony that Orpheus posited a certain
“unseen” earth and offered sacrifices resembling those of Moses.

Let us finally leave Orpheus for others. Hesychius, an author rich in Greek
antiquity, gives a most illuminating testimony. In him we read not only
*Adama*, making plain that the word itself was long known to the Greeks, but
also the explanation “earth, and indeed virgin earth.” No ancient writer
except Moses supplies the reason for this interpretation. He alone describes
for us virgin earth, which God had not yet watered and man had not yet
cultivated: that *parthenos* from which Adam was made, and which is deservedly
called both among the Hebrews and among our people “the virgin and true earth.”
Ambrose, *Epistle* 42; Irenaeus 3.3.1, p. 298; Tertullian, *On the Flesh of
Christ*; and Bernard, homily 2 on *Missus est*, all say that the first man was
created from virgin earth.

It was therefore not without reason that Theodotion, the distinguished
translator of the Old Testament, whom many believe to have been Rabbi
Jonathan, translated the other words for earth but appears to have thought
that *Adamah*, whenever it occurred, should not even be touched. I believe
that this most learned man understood first that the word *Adamah*

curse, he has, “You shall not defile your Adama.”

The nearly whole Mosaic doctrine about man consists in these things: the human
body, taken from the earth and fashioned in God's image, is already Earth; it
will someday return to earth and even to dust; and this will happen because of
the curse. These teachings are recorded in so many Greek monuments that
nothing among the ancients is clearer or better attested. Homer's Jupiter
calls Hector's corpse *kophen gaian*, “mute earth.” The Sibyl says, “Naked
from earth, naked you will go to earth.” Epicharmus has, “Clothed with earth,
the spirit is above.” Phocylides says plainly, “We have a body from earth and
are dissolved into ash”—or, to depart from the ancient reading more accurately,
“When dissolved, we are dust.”

Clearest of all is the clearest of all men—I mean Homer. In him Jupiter not
only calls Hector's corpse *kophen gaian*, but Menelaus, when the Greeks shrink
from contest with Hector, also curses them with a return to Earth as a peculiar
and final kind of curse. Why should I claim on my own that Homer sipped these
things from Moses? Justin asserts it, Clement asserts it, and Eusebius asserts
it: all eminent in literature and learning, and one also eminent in martyrdom.
It was not enough for him to affirm that Homer learned these things in Egypt
from ancient divine history; he also pointed out the passage on which this
whole labor depends: “Earth will return to earth.”

Therefore, just as no one doubts that Alpha is Aleph or Delta Daleth, since
they consist of the same elements—perhaps rearranged, but still the same—and
since all who are not entirely stupid both recognize and take offense at the
one and same name *Iehovah* in the Greeks' Iao, the Latins' Iove, the
Phoenicians' Ievo, the Thracians' Evie, the Atticans' Huis, the Aeolians'
Phoibe, the Americans' Hioh, and finally the Egyptians' Bacchus, so when we see
that *Adamah, Mahad,* and *Hadm*—that is, *Admah Mahd Hadm*—agree in the force
and number of their letters and differ only in their arrangement, it cannot be
doubted that they too were once one and must not now be referred to different
origins in different languages.

23. The Greeks teach otherwise, I admit, but by Socrates' testimony they are
poor teachers in this matter. “Do you not know,” he says in Plato, “that those
who wish to teach must themselves first know? In the matters they know there
is no controversy among them but the highest agreement; wherever a dispute
arises, it is the surest argument of ignorance.” Yet how many mutually
conflicting Greek opinions about this very matter present themselves?

Some hold that *ton Haden*, like *ton Aera*, is derived from *apo tou aein*,
which means “to breathe.” Others wish it to be named not from breathing but
from breathing one's last, and they drag in these words from Homer:

> And I said that I would see the dead and the house of Hades
> on the day when life departed from my dear heart.

Some relate that Hades was named as though it were *Heden*, “pleasant,” by
contradiction because death is pleasant to no one. Many mysteries of the
underworld are expressed by this kind of naming: examples include, to mention
no more, the Manes, “Kindly Ones,” and Parcae, “Sparing Ones,” among the
Latins, and Charon, “Joy,” and the Eumenides, “Gracious Ones,” among the
Greeks. Others maintain an opinion verbally opposed to this one but agreeing
in substance. They judge that nothing is disguised here, but that because the
thing itself is unpleasant its name was imposed from unpleasantness, so that
*Hades* is *Aedes*. Plutarch somewhere notes that *ton Haden* was named *apo
tes Aidous*, from shame, and praises Plato as author of this opinion. Yet if
Plato has anything relevant, he appears to derive it instead from *eidesthai*.

By far the largest party holds that *ho Hades* comes *apo tou Aeideous*. The
wording is clear, but the sense quite uncertain. Some say he is so called
because he himself is not seen, others because he causes us not to be seen,
and others refer *ton Haden* not to sight but to deprivation of form, so that
the term seems to describe not so much a place as a condition and to come not
from seeing but from form. Claudian may perhaps have had this in view when,
sublime in a humble subject, he makes Hades himself speak thus:

> I suffer the losses of the third and final lot,
> and the formless regions—

Thus among them there is no stable and certain explanation of this word. Some
seek it from the living, others from the dying; some derive it from acting,
others from suffering; some maintain that it comes from pleasure, others from
pain, others from shame, and others from knowledge. Indeed, they do not even
agree about which letters should write the word or with what aspiration it
should be pronounced. One writes a monophthong alpha, another a diphthong; one
pronounces the thinner *Ades*, another the rougher *Hades*. Therefore, if

to extend the boundaries of their more recent language was so great that they
did not blush to twist into a Greek origin even words they knew to be Hebrew.

Do you believe, for example, that Eustathius was ignorant that the one whom the
poets call Iapetos was the Hebrews' Japheth? He was not ignorant, yet he writes
that the name came from *apo tou iaptein*. Suppose he did not know. Basil
certainly knew the source of “Jesus,” for he answers that it comes from a
Hebrew word. Yet, he says, “I think it better to derive it from *iaomai*, ‘I
heal,’ whose future is *iaso*, whence comes *Iasous* and, with *a* changed to
*e*, *Iesous*.” Lest we think Basil was alone in this heresy, Cyril of Jerusalem
also holds that “Jesus was named by a wordplay from healing.” Epiphanius
likewise derives Jesus both from a Hebrew verb meaning “to save” and from
Greek *iasthai*, “to heal.” The same kind of thing appears when Irenaeus
derives Passover from *apo tou paschein*, “from suffering.” Nazianzen does not
diverge from him: “This great and venerable Passover is called *Phaska* among
the Hebrews. Again, thinking this to be the name of the saving Passion, and
then Hellenizing the sound by changing *ph* to *p* and *k* to *ch*, they
called the feast *Pascha*.”

Thus men neither imprudent nor unlearned seem to jest in a serious matter.
Whether by the custom or the disease of their nation, they were somewhat too
indulgent toward foreign words: Greece was never content merely to give them
citizenship without also arranging their adoption into some family. We should
therefore not marvel
that *ton Haden* received this courtesy, was dressed in a Greek cloak, and was
made agreeable to Greek ears. Yet Adamah itself, the subject of this whole
discussion, still entirely Punic in dress and sound, could not even turn aside
from its road to visit the petty Greeks without being judged a native-born
Greek.

24. “But,” one will say, “Plato is of course an insufficiently religious
witness, plainly talks nonsense, and diminishes his own weight with a
far-fetched derivation when he wishes Hades to have flowed from *eidenai*, ‘to
know.’” No Plato wishes this. Some ignorant people wish it, people carried by
philosophy's evil fortune into Plato. They do not even grasp his words, much
less attain his meaning. He nowhere says that Hades comes from *eidenai*. He
only asserts that the god to whom the souls of the pious migrate was named
much more from the light of knowledge than from the horror of darkness.

This is deservedly so. The former explanation reveals neither the nature of
the word nor of the thing; the latter, if not the nature of the word, certainly
reveals that of the thing. For Plato the highest good was to see God, and he
held that this could happen to no one unless released from the body's bonds.
Nothing undermined this teaching as much as the common fear of the darkness
into which death was believed to lead. The wise man therefore had to seize
every opportunity to tear the innate dread from the minds of philosophers. He
often attempted this elsewhere by ordinary reasoning; here he tried it in his
own—that is, Hebraic and enigmatic—manner of arguing. Thus he teaches that the
condition of death (for this is what he means here by *ton Haden*) is not
fearful or horrifying and not subject to slavery forever, as is plainly known
to have been the common opinion, but peaceful, quiet, and blessed. There God
is not the jailer but the teacher of wisdom, imbuing the minds of the good—the
only ones under discussion—with divine knowledge and filling them with
pleasure, so that they cling to him voluntarily more closely

25. Come then, Plato. We have entered your woods to investigate the origin of
*ton Hadou*. By what steps do you command us to advance toward the
investigation of truth? We must return, you say, to the first names. Quite
right, but what names do you call first? Barbarians are older than Greeks in
this matter. I understand. Yet since the Barbarians are not a single nation,
to which people in particular do you send us? Certain names were instituted
by a power more divine than human. Am I mistaken, or do you direct us to the
place where names bestowed upon things by God himself may be found? I judge
this occurred nowhere except among the Hebrews.

I know that the poets celebrate a certain language of the gods, telling
fables in which heavenly beings call something by one name and humans by
another. Hence Homer's line:

> The heavenly ones called her Chalcis; mortals, Cymindis.

And our Ovid's:

> The gods call him Icelos; mortal people name him Phobetor.

But these matters belong to the Muses' mysteries. We are treating names that
were divinely instituted but nevertheless occur in ordinary use. I therefore
know where to look: among the Hebrews. Yet since Hades is a god among the
Greeks, from what class of things will he be drawn? The ancient Greeks seem to
me to have thought that the only gods were the Sun, Moon, Earth, Stars, and
Heaven. You have now set certain boundaries around this inquiry, but I wish
you to narrow them still further, as much as you can. Hades is the same god as
Pluto, and Pluto is Earth. He is so named because *plutus*, riches, are dug
from the Earth.

O truly divine philosopher! O wonderful genius! O hidden teaching! Plato, as
though doing something else, gradually led us—not merely, I say, to things
named by God, but to the very thing we sought, *Adamah*. Among sacred names for
things it so stands out and rises above the rest that the most Platonic of the
Hebrews appear to affirm that every other name was imposed by man, but this
one alone by God.

We will discuss Plato's whole method more fully elsewhere. For now we merely
note the following. He casts away and tramples the common explanation, *ton
Hadon apo tou Aeideous*. He teaches that Hades is Pluto and Pluto is Earth. He
suggests that the origins of many words the Greeks use, especially those
bearing some divinity before them, must be sought among the Barbarians. These
facts provide the firmest argument that the common people err when they chase
something invisible in this word; that the Platonists talk foolishly when
they suspect their master taught an explanation of *ton Hadon* other than one
expressing Earth's force and power; and finally that we attempt nothing novel
when we call words back to their source—Hades, namely, to *Adamah*. Following
Plato's footprints, we have arrived not only among the Hebrews but at this
very Hebrew name.

I do not wish this to be understood as though, among the Greeks, we owe this
secret of Hebrew theology to Plato alone or first. He too had guides—perhaps
others, but certainly the Sibyl. Since he did not neglect her other material,
who would say that he was ignorant of those oracles containing the explanation
of *ton Hadon*? The Sibyl therefore says of the people of the first age:

> But Hades also concealed them;
> he was called Hades because Adam, the first,
> descended there after death and Earth hid him in burial, etc.

What in these verses could be brought forward—or ought to be desired—that is
older in antiquity, weightier in authority, plainer in words, or fuller in
substance for our subject? The prophetess, inspired by divine power, eminent
in dignity, obscure in her age but most illustrious in prophecy, teaches that
*ton Haden* is *Adamah*; that *Adamah* must be sought from the Hebrews; and
that Earth is expressed and death understood in *Adamah*. Even if the
foundations of languages and letters that we laid down as unshakable did not
exist, and if none of the innumerable arguments we will bring from every kind
of writer existed, this single testimony would give us the question defeated
and the Hebrew origin of *ton Hadon* confirmed. Although this origin may have
been unknown, neglected, or despised by great men in the Church—men whom
learning often makes ignorant and wisdom makes foolish—its well-pressed
footprints loudly proclaim that it was known, received, and honored by the
common people, who generally cling more firmly to antiquity, and not only
among the Greeks but also among Barbarians.

What age or region has not heard the famous title echoing the Sibyl like an
image, by which Adam is said to have fallen into Hades long ago and to have
been called back by Christ's descent? The ancients offer this in sermons to
the people; modern
writers have also placed it in public prayers. Even today many northern
nations use *Adum* for the region of the dead. Although they do not understand
the word, they verbally confess that it declined from *Adam* into *Adum*.

26. I therefore think it has been demonstrated more than sufficiently that
the first proposition is true: Hades is the same word as Adamah. It follows
that we must teach that those which agree in word do not differ in substance.
Yet if Plato is trusted when he holds, as we have just seen, that Hades is the
same as Pluto, what further need is there? Even beginners know that Pluto is
Earth, so Hades is proved to be Earth. But since one Plato has come to count
for nothing among our people—though for men such as Antimachus and Cicero, and
for Augustines of I know not what sort, he used to count as many thousands—let
us join Homer to Plato as his supporter. Lest Homer alone also be despised,
let us join Hesiod to him and, in turn, so many learned people from every
nation, school, and age to Hesiod that whoever does not yield to the weight of
their testimony will be crushed by the multitude of witnesses.

Homer somewhere says of Tartarus:

> As far below Hades as heaven is from earth.

Hesiod expresses this as:

> As far beneath the earth as heaven is from earth;
> as far below the earth as Olympus withdraws from here.

What Homer named “Hades” with the more sacred and secret term, Hesiod called
“Earth” with the more widespread name. Each suggests that this heavy element
on which we are borne is balanced by its own weights and sustained in the
center of the universe, surrounded on every side by air. Tartarus, as everyone
knows, lies an equal distance from the ether. Hesiod explains himself by
adding the fable of the bronze anvil: it takes as many days to fall from
heaven to earth as from Earth into Tartarus—that is, according to Lucian's
interpretation, into the depth of the air. To omit others, Heraclides Ponticus
explains Homer: “In one verse,” he says, “he measured for us the spherical
shape of the world. In its center, like a certain base,”

What of Empedocles, the most acute investigator of nature and, if Lucretius is
believed, a man so divine that he scarcely seems born of human stock? When he
enumerates the four principles of things, *Aidoneus* occupies Earth's place;
that is, Hades:

> First hear the four roots of all things:
> shining Zeus, life-bearing Hera, and Aidoneus,
> and Nestis, who with tears moistens the mortal fountain.
> First hear the four roots of all things:
> ethereal Jupiter, nourishing Juno, Hades,
> and Nestis, who wets mortal eyes with tears.

After citing these lines, Plutarch does not fear to add that the poet of
Agrigent named ether Jupiter, air Juno, Earth Aidoneus, and Water Nestis.

27. The teaching of the Magi hands down nothing different. I do not mean
those impostors from the crossroads, hated equally by God and humanity, but
the truly wise Chaldeans. I would explain at greater length how much must be
granted them if that were as necessary as it is readily available. Among the
Greeks the following is advertised either as an oracle or certainly as
something held for one:

> Only Chaldeans and Hebrews have received wisdom,
> reverently worshiping the self-born God as king;
> Chaldeans and Hebrews alone are wise,
> worshiping the self-born King with pure devotion.

Among the Arabs too, when they flourished most greatly in the glory of the
sciences, the Magi enjoyed such esteem that their decrees were designated not
by the name of philosophy but of religion. To the three laws, as they called
them, of the Hebrews, Christians, and Saracens, they added a fourth, the law
*Al-magus*, that is, of the Magi. Although our people rightly do not grant
this, they nevertheless grant what is enough for me: after Moses, the authors
of magic are the oldest of all, and their logic—which necessarily accompanies
so great an antiquity—comes very close to the first oracles of truth.

What, then, is Hades for the Magi? They establish two principles, Oromasdes and
Areimanius: that is, if we hear the Arabs, Light and Darkness, or, if we
accept Aristotle as interpreter, Zeus and Hades. Since they assign to each of
these one of the two extreme regions of the world,

and in one lies the highest good while in the other the highest evil, they
call the former *Amphiphae*, “bright on every side,” and the latter
*Amphiknephe*, “dark on every side.” The children of the Magi understand by
the “region” of darkness—their own word—the Earth, since it is the lowest of
things. Synesius, a great man and highly practiced in magic, teaches this, to
mention no more. Even if he were silent, *Amphiknephes* itself cries out that
Areimanius is Earth. Because Earth is in herself the spring and origin of
darkness, she imparts from her own force and power the darkness of the other
elements.

Hence some translated Zoroaster's phrase as *Buthon kai Haden*, “Depth and
Hades,” while others, paying less attention to words than things, rendered it
*Kremnon kai Gen*, “Precipice and Earth.” It will be useful to set both verses
in the middle. The first is fuller but obscure in sacred terms:

> Do not incline downward toward the dark-shining world,
> for the ever-faithless depth lies beneath, and Hades,
> dark on every side.

The other is more compressed but clear in ordinary language:

> Do not incline downward; precipice and earth lie below.

What is “Depth” in the first is “Precipice” in the second, and what is “Hades”
there is “Earth” here. In each, one and the same thing is forbidden and one
and the same precipice presented. These are not two Chaldean oracles but one
oracle conveyed to the Greeks in two translations.

28. Why do I linger with poets, philosophers, grammarians, or finally Magi to
prove that Hades is Earth? As though letters had ever breathed in any place
where the most famous fable of the four sons of *Kronos*, that is, *Chronos*,
did not penetrate with them! Everyone who has tried to unfold that fable among
the ancients understood the sons to be the principles of things. Most
unhesitatingly established fire in Jupiter, air in Juno, water in Neptune, and
earth in Pluto. If some held otherwise, as we will soon see, both the nature
of the word and of the things deceived them.

Some used *Heran*, Juno, for Earth, led, it seems, by two reasons—from the name
and from nature. From the name, because *Hera* appeared to be nothing other
than *Era*, while it was agreed that the theology of the ancients especially
regarded the elements and that *Eran* was Earth. Hence “the lower ones are
said to go into Hades,” namely, to be “lying in the earth.” From nature,
because Hera was surnamed *pherespios*, a word by which they wished to signify
the source from which mortals receive sustenance, which the Muses' pupils call
“the grain-bearing, life-sustaining field.” Yet there is nothing solid in
either argument. For even if we grant,

[Marginalia: Synesius, *On Dreams*; Zoroastrian oracles.]

[1611 Book I, printed p. 48 | Google Books PA48]

as we must, that the ancient Greeks consecrated the elements, especially Earth
as the most ancient of the gods, what, I ask, will prevent us from referring
*Eran* among the gods by the method customary among the ancients so that it
becomes Rhea, not Hera? By transposing letters, *Era* becomes *Rea*, just as
*Aer* becomes *Hera*. We should therefore judge that the Greek theologians
used anagramming with domestic as well as foreign words.

We receive this explanation of Hera from Plato, and it is established not so
much by his testimony as by his argument. The lawgiver—the one who assigned
names—used an enigma and called *Aera Eran*, carrying the beginning to the end
(for so I read, not, as before, “the end to the beginning”). This can be known
by repeating the name several times. So Plato. Lest this seem rather obscure,
one must know that frequent repetition of this kind forms a certain circle of
letters. As they return into themselves, *Hera* cannot be pronounced repeatedly
without *Aer* being pronounced repeatedly by the same act. Thus what is
alleged from the name is either nothing or rather supports our opinion,
according to which Hera is not Earth but air.

Nor does nature oppose it. Hera is called *pherespios*, “life-bearing.” She is
indeed. Yet this will not designate Earth more than air, and in fact far less.
Just as earth brings sustenance, which is *bios*, air brings life, which is
also *bios*; and *to ton bion onoma*, “the name of life,” belongs first to
life and then to sustenance.

What of the fable told by the most ancient writers that Juno, bound by Jupiter
with golden chains and weighed down with iron anvils, hung in the air? It
plainly meant that air, more closely joined to heavenly fire, was mixed with
the two elements below, water and Earth. Homer gives the fable. Theopompus,
Hellanicus, and, following them, Fulgentius explain it, to omit others.
Fulgentius himself affirms that Jupiter, Juno, Neptune, and Pluto are called
Saturn's four children—that is, as he says, fire, air, water, and earth.

What of the fact that we read that Juno's temple near Athens had no doors?
Pausanias records that this was for no other reason than to signify that air
is neither shut out nor admitted by doors. In the poet the goddess herself
swears in formal words:

> Let Earth know this, and the broad Heaven above,
> and the downward-flowing water of Styx;
> let Earth know this, and Heaven, and Styx's deep stream.

The grammarians teach that the four elements are indicated here. She named
Earth, they say, water, and ether, while the fourth element was the goddess
herself who swore. The entire nation of the Stoics follows this opinion, to
say nothing of countless others. For them air is

situated between sea and heaven and consecrated under Juno's name, while every

First, so that it may be understood that she is
one of the elements and is not Earth; second, that Hades is numbered in the

especially Christians, were persuaded that he was air, nor ought I marvel.
For when air, by the fault of opinion, occupied Earth's inheritance, who does
not see that by a double error Earth necessarily had to yield in turn to air's
lot?

Added to this were the etymologies already sufficiently refuted: that Hades
was either named from breathing, which belongs to air, or was something that
could not be seen. Since the latter suited neither fire nor water well, and
least of all the density of Earth, only air remained among the elements that
could be called *Aides*. Its rarity and thinness were supposedly so great
that it escaped sight. Yet, as I have sufficiently said, the light of truth
that has now arisen has dispelled this darkness of etymologies.
