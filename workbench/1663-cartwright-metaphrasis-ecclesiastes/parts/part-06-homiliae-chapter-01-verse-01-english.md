HOMILIES

ON

ECCLESIASTES.

CHAPTER I.

VERSE 1. The words of Ecclesiastes, son of David, king of Jerusalem.

Two things must be considered in this Book: 1. Its definition, which is the
Title or inscription of the Book. 2. The division of the Book itself.

The use and purpose of the Title is to win and arouse the attention of the
hearer or reader, and then to sustain and preserve that attention all the way
to the end of the book. This makes known both God's burning and ardent zeal to
gather us to himself and our own slowness and aversion of mind in receiving
salutary doctrine. The fact that this Book was dictated by the Holy Spirit,
whatever writer and amanuensis he employed, ought to be more than sufficient
to keep us in attentive consideration of it. For when he brings us so great a
treasure and so precious a jewel in a vessel of earthenware or clay, it is not
permissible to make light of it—much less reject it—without the greatest
folly. But to disdain it when enclosed in a golden vessel carved with
exquisite workmanship is the height of madness. For although all human beings
may rightly be called vessels of earthenware and clay, nevertheless, in this
lowly condition common to all, some are adorned with God's gifts above others
and so may deservedly be called vessels of silver or gold.

In this inscription, the argument by which he commends this book is drawn from
its efficient instrument. That instrument is described from the charge laid
upon the soul that had been gathered and was gathering with the Church. To
appoint Solomon a preacher in the Church—as the received interpretation
affirms—is plainly ἀθεόλογον, contrary to sound speech about God, especially
after he had fallen away from the truth he once acknowledged. On this matter,
see Ezekiel 44,

[Google Books pthrWjdL0BgC | reader image PA83 | printed pp. 83–84]

verses 10 and 13, and 2 Kings 23:9. Once, indeed, while he was still upright
with God, he prayed publicly in the temple; but it is incongruous that after
so foul a fall he should have acted as a preacher. Further, since it is
certain that he repented—for several testimonies establish that he was chosen
and loved by God—where at last will we find this repentance attested if we
depart from this passage? It was important, moreover, that his repentance be
left attested to posterity, both because his salvation declared through the
Scriptures was established and so that it might also be understood that no
one can be saved unless he repents. From this it is clear that Solomon, after
wandering from the Church because of his crimes, returned to the bosom or
embrace of that same Church through repentance, which he sealed
as attested in this Book. In this matter, both the faith and power of the
Church and Solomon's obedience are all the more illustrious and worthy of
proclamation, insofar as the clearest and best-known voice of God established
for both that Solomon was among the elect and could not fall utterly away
from God. For by God's express command the name “Beloved of the Lord” was
given to him. 2 Samuel 12:25. In addition, he was a Prophet, and Christ
testifies that all the Prophets are in heaven. Luke 13:28. For although some
people who were reprobate, such as Balaam and Judas the betrayer, spoke
θεοπνεύστως—that is, by divine inspiration—nevertheless no one who is not
among the elect has ever written θεοπνεύστως, by divine inspiration.

To the same point belongs what is expressly said in the second Book of
Chronicles, chapter 11: King Rehoboam walked in the ways of his fathers David
and Solomon so long as he persevered uprightly and religiously in the true
worship of God. 2 Chronicles 11:17. Since, therefore, the most exalted and
illustrious man who ever existed or will exist hereafter to the end of the
age—the same man whose salvation it was impious to doubt—was saved only
through repentance, who can exempt himself from this road to salvation
without the greatest pride of mind? It is clearer than daylight that rulers,
not infrequently—and even rulers otherwise pious—have been puffed up by their
own greatness, relied upon their own authority, and entangled themselves in
great crimes. Thus no order of human beings has a greater need than kings for
teaching about repentance. For if there is a danger, as Moses warns in
Deuteronomy 8:10 and 14, that believers enjoying prosperous things flowing
according to their desire may be overtaken by forgetfulness of God, how much
more does this apply to kings? Everything is furnished to them abundantly, as
if by a divine wand, without any labor or care of their own. But to withhold
medicine from those especially susceptible to the disease is the act of
people who counsel kings with too little fidelity.

His presentation of his own gathering into the Church as an ornament lending
grace and esteem to this work is evidence of how highly those who, after
wandering from the Church through obstinacy in crime, are gathered back into
its fellowship should be valued and what honor they should receive. So far
should their falls in their former life be from being cast in their teeth.
See the whole of Luke 15 on this point. It shows likewise how glorious it is
to submit peaceably to the Church, something that the great majority of
people customarily treats as a disgrace. This is still more clearly
established because, when Solomon repeatedly interposes his authority to
confirm the things discussed in this little book, he passes over the glorious
titles of king, son of David, and Solomon, and most frequently employs this
title as the one in which he gloried most. It is as though nothing he had ever
done or accomplished—not even in building the temple or palace, or in the
wisest administration of kingdom or court—could contend in glory with this
submission. All the more execrable, then, is the impiety of those who so
abhor this submission that not only do they themselves refuse to undergo it,
but they also make strenuous efforts to prevent others from submitting as far
as their zeal and exertion can. If they rejected the honor of a kingdom, or
of adoption as a king's

son, when it was conferred upon them, they would pronounce a sentence of
folly upon themselves. But now, when they reject the honor that Solomon
preferred to both of those, how will they escape the mark of insanity? His
speaking of the soul as gathered, rather than of Solomon or his body,
indicates that his repentance was neither feigned nor hypocritical.

Another commendation by which he adorns the doctrine of this Book is drawn
from his relations: he was the son of David, a man renowned for singular
piety both in the Church and in the commonwealth of Christ. So that Luke might
leave John's ministry more pleasing and acceptable both to the present age in
which he lived and to posterity, among the many other arguments he heaps up he
also introduces this one concerning the piety of John's parents. Luke 1:5–6.
For just as impiety is aggravated by the proverb, “from a bad crow, a bad
egg,” so piety shines more brightly if the children's incorrupt life is
supported by their parents' integrity. What is more, the piety of parents
brings children great support against the dangerous temptation of distrust,
as in Psalm 86:16. For this reason alone, therefore, parents ought to devote
their effort to piety, so that their children's industry and diligence may
afterward bear greater fruit in the Church or commonwealth.

The third and final commendation is that he was a King. It was said of old
that those commonwealths are generally excellently ordered in which either
philosophers rule or kings philosophize. Since this was fulfilled in the man
who acted as a Prophet while King and administered his kingdom while
prophesying, Solomon's royal majesty brought no slight weight of authority to
the doctrine he sets forth, lending it both splendor and firmness. A gardener
who speaks suitably is not to be despised, much less a king. This royal
dignity is amplified from the place subject to him where he reigned, which
also includes the people joined to Solomon's kingdom. The point is that all
other monarchs, even the most powerful, are ranked below Solomon by an
immeasurable interval, since the difference between God's people and all
other profane nations is as great as that between a human being and a beast.
It is no ordinary honor to have oxen and the like subject to oneself; it is
still more glorious to have human beings subject to us; but to rule over the
godly is by far the most majestic honor. Psalm 8:8–9. This should admonish
rulers both to make themselves pleasing to God and to treat their subjects
kindly, as Job taught excellently. Job 31:13–15. If Solomon was valued so
highly for civil and ἐξωτερικὴν, external, wisdom that the queen of Sheba,
moved by his fame, came from the farthest coasts and bounds of the earth to
hear him, how much more should these teachings of eternal life be acquired at
expense and labor? Thus far concerning the inscription of this Book.
