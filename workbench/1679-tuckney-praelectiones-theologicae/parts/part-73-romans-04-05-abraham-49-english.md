Bellarmine's eighth argument is drawn from the fact that “in the Song of
Songs the church is compared to a bride who is most beautiful with beauty
inherent in herself, not with the Bridegroom's beauty imputed to her.” He
proves this with two points: “1. The bride is ascribed the beauty proper to
women, and the Bridegroom the beauty proper to men. 2. It would be utterly
absurd if the heavenly and truly most beautiful Bridegroom had a bride who
was exceedingly ugly and merely adorned outwardly with some precious garment
belonging to the man.”

We say the following about the argument in general.

1. In the Song the bride is called beautiful, but it is not explicitly said
whether this is from imputed beauty, inherent beauty, or both. Therefore this
passage can apply to either or to both.

2. Inherent and imputed beauty are also wrongly set against each other, as
though they were so incompatible that both could not belong to the same
bride or person. We regard and affirm this not only as possible but as most
certainly true. I readily acknowledge that the wedding garment in Matthew
22:11 consists both of Christ's infused and of his imputed grace, for we
contend that the bride is adorned with both. So it is with the woman in
Ezekiel 16, who is called beautiful not so much from an innate or inherent
form as from external ornaments, as is clear from verses 10–13—from God's
beauty placed upon her in verse 14. Indeed, inherent beauty is so far from
being opposed to imputed beauty that it depends upon it as an effect upon
its cause.

To the first proof, that “the bride's beauty is that proper to women, as the
Bridegroom's is that proper to men, so that their beauty is not the same,” I
answer:

The beauty of Christ the Bridegroom and of the church his bride is not the
same in every respect. Although it is the same in essence, it differs in
degree and mode. With respect to sanctification and inherence, the bride's
beauty is simply imperfect in this life and, in comparison with Christ, will
always be imperfect even in heaven. Christ's beauty is always most perfect
in both places. With respect to justification, the bride's beauty is also
borrowed and imputed, while Christ's is inherent and his own.

But although they thus differ in degree and mode, it does not follow that
his beauty and hers are altogether different. For although the righteousness
of Christ's person belongs to the Bridegroom, the righteousness of his office
and merit can belong to the bride through imputation, insofar as by his
office he has been made righteousness for us. Just as a wife shines with her
husband's rays, so the church as bride shines with the beauty and
righteousness of Christ her Bridegroom, which is abundantly sufficient both
for him and for his own. Bernard expresses this beautifully and, in his
fashion, very devoutly in Sermon 61: “Lord, I will remember your
righteousness alone, for it is also mine, since you have been made
righteousness for me by God. Must I fear that one righteousness will not
suffice for us both? It is no short cloak

[1679 printed p. 299 | IA leaf 307]

which, according to the prophet, cannot cover two. Your righteousness is
eternal: what is longer than eternity? A righteousness both ample and
eternal will generously cover you and me together.” Nor need we fear that
prohibition in Deuteronomy 22:5: “A woman shall not put on a man's garment.”
Although the same garment is not suitable for bodies of both sexes, the same
grace is most suitable—and, so to speak, uniquely suitable—for souls of
both. Men and women do not have different faith, piety, and charity. The
same image and likeness of God was originally impressed upon Adam and Eve:
“In the image of God he created him; male and female he created them,”
Genesis 1:27, but he created them both in the same image.

To Bellarmine's second proof, which regards it as utterly absurd “for the
most beautiful Bridegroom to have an exceedingly ugly bride merely adorned
outwardly with some precious garment belonging to the man,” we make just two
replies.

1. We are not so blasphemously deranged that we would ever dare to say or
think that Christ's bride, the church, or any faithful soul is exceedingly
ugly. Indeed, insofar as in justification she is clothed with the fleece of
that Lamb “without blemish and without spot,” we do not hesitate to affirm
that she is simply and absolutely most beautiful. With respect to
sanctification and inherent grace, we further say that she is comparatively
most beautiful—not in comparison with God's law and the strict examination
of divine justice, but with her former state of corruption. In that state
she was indeed exceedingly ugly, but now through the infusion of grace she
is adorned with its many gifts and is very beautiful.

Yet,

2. This inherent grace and beauty is imperfect in this life and is sprinkled
with many blemishes and stains from indwelling sin, so that it cannot sustain
the rigor of the law and God's severe judgment or reconcile his favor to
itself. It has this only from Christ, in whom God was well pleased. As for
us, since all our righteousness is like a menstrual rag, let us humbly say
with Bernard: “Since we cannot appear in the clothing of innocence and
righteousness, let us appear clothed with confession. For our beauty does
not consist in the perfection of inherent righteousness, but rather in faith
and repentance.” Thus Gregory comments on the words of the Song, “You are
altogether beautiful”: “Although she sins daily, she nevertheless continually
preserves her purity through continual repentance.” In the same way, the
bride whom the Bridegroom calls altogether beautiful nevertheless calls
herself—being perfectly known to herself—black. I say black, but not for
that reason exceedingly ugly, as Bellarmine blasphemously claims; rather,
she is at once black and beautiful, Song of Songs 1:5.
