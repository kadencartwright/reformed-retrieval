# Anthony Tuckney, *Lecture on Romans 1:17 — Twenty-Second Part*

Certain statements of our own theologians remain which, if not carefully
weighed, may appear to an unwary reader to support Bellarmine. So that they
may not cause anyone to stumble, they must be briefly presented and
explained.

The first is Pareus on Romans 3:20. He explains Paul's words, “Therefore no
flesh will be justified in his sight by works of the law,” as follows: “That
is, by obedience to the law in the state of sin outside Christ.” He thus
appears to mean that justification excludes only works performed by someone
still in the state of sin and outside Christ, not works performed from faith
after being engrafted into Christ.

I answer:

1. This is clearly not Pareus's meaning, for he immediately adds: “Paul
speaks of works of the law as they are now either performed by the
unregenerate—which cannot justify because they are not good at all, being
done without faith—or begun by the regenerate—which likewise cannot justify
because they are not perfect and are performed by those already justified
by faith.” Thus he clearly testifies that Paul excludes from justification
the works of both regenerate and unregenerate people. What he had said in
summary and partially in the words first cited, he develops more fully in
the later words.

2. Anyone who partially expounds Paul's words of works done in the state of
sin outside Christ, to show that they are excluded from justification, must
not immediately be thought to say that works performed by those engrafted
into Christ through faith are included so that we are justified by them.

3. As Pareus himself notes, the regenerate perform these works after already
being justified by faith. Therefore the works follow justification; they do
not constitute it. The moment we are engrafted into Christ by faith we are
justified. It follows that all works performed by such people as such are
effects of their justification, not its cause.

[1679 printed p. 150 | IA leaf 158]

4. Finally—and this most closely fits Pareus's meaning in the passage—after
excluding obedience to the law from justification, he added the words “in
the state of sin outside Christ.” He did not mean that we are justified by
works we perform in the state of grace after being engrafted into Christ,
which he immediately rejects and refutes. He meant that we should not
imagine this inability arises from a defect in the law itself. Although Paul
says the law was weakened in this respect (Romans 8:3), he also says it was
weakened not in itself but through the flesh—by our fault, not its own. Had
we fulfilled it, it would have justified us. But since the fall makes us
fail to fulfill the law, we consequently fail to obtain justification by it.

Pareus's following words clearly establish this as his meaning: “He does not
deny justification to obedience to the law, for before the fall man would
have been justified by works of the law. After the fall the law prescribes
perfect obedience, which, if anyone performed it, would certainly justify
him, just as the man Christ was justified by perfect conformity to the law.
But Paul speaks of works of the law as they are now either performed by the
unregenerate, which cannot justify because they are not good at all, being
done without faith; or begun by the regenerate, which likewise cannot
justify because they are not perfect and are performed by those already
justified by faith.” Pareus's former words may not explain the matter fully
or clearly and perhaps are less precisely expressed, but they contain
nothing of the Roman leaven, at least according to the author's meaning,
which the following words clearly explain in the contrary direction. This
is enough concerning Pareus.

Piscator is the second. Certain expressions fell from him which at least
appear to support the Roman view that only works performed by our own
powers and free choice are excluded from justification. In his analyses of
Romans 3:20 and Galatians 2:16, where Paul says that a person is justified by
faith and not works of the law, Piscator says: “Paul's argument has this
form: We are justified either by faith or by works of the law. But not by
works of the law.” He explains the major premise: “A person is justified
either by his own righteousness procured by his powers and merits—that is,
by perfect observance of God's law—or by the righteousness of another,
given and imputed to him.” According to this explanation, the righteousness
of works Paul excludes appears to be only what rests on our own powers, not
what rests upon Christ's grace and includes works flowing from grace. Such
works therefore appear not to be excluded from justification. Piscator says
nearly the same in *On Justification* 1.8.

I answer:

1. In reading authors we must not agitate minute details of wording so as to
twist them like cords into claims contradicting the constant tenor and
position of their writings. Romanists, Remonstrants, and others behave
dishonestly—indeed, abominably—toward Calvin and other orthodox writers in
this regard. They seize isolated statements that have sometimes been
misunderstood, more often interpolated, and miserably truncated, and drag
them by the neck to prove something from which the whole course of those
authors' writings and sayings recoils.

[1679 printed p. 151 | IA leaf 159]

As regards the present question, Piscator everywhere speaks most correctly
and excludes absolutely every work, whether preceding or following faith,
from justification. Whatever the cited words may seem at first glance to
suggest, anyone who drags them against their author's meaning into a sense
contrary to his consistent position elsewhere and almost everywhere sins no
less against prudence than charity.

2. As to the matter itself, in this analysis Piscator openly identifies the
two covenants God made with us. He represents Paul as arguing that we are
justified either according to the covenant of grace by faith in Jesus
Christ, or according to the covenant of works by works performed by our own
powers and hence by our merits. Paul rejects this latter method of
justifying fallen people and excludes our works from justification, thereby
establishing the former way. This is Paul's meaning and Piscator's analysis.
How does it prove justification by works following faith and performed by
grace?

Perhaps someone will reply that both Paul's argument and Piscator's analysis
exclude only works performed by our powers without faith and grace according
to the covenant of works. Thus they do not deny but imply that works flowing
from grace and faith justify.

On the contrary, they are so far from implying this that they utterly deny
it.

1. Paul nowhere says that any works justify us. James indeed asserts this in
another sense, but in Paul's sense—God's acceptance and justification before
him—Paul always says and invincibly proves the contrary.

2. Paul's denial that works performed by our powers justify us according to
the covenant of works does not imply that works produced by faith justify us
in the covenant of grace. As regards justification, the two covenants do not
oppose one kind of works to another—as though works performed by our own
powers justified in the covenant of works while works performed from faith
and grace justified in the covenant of grace. They oppose works and faith:
works in the former covenant, faith in the new one. When works are simply
excluded from justification, in whatever way and of whatever quality, they
are entirely excluded. Works could justify only in the former covenant;
when that covenant was removed, they were entirely excluded from
justification.

Works are indeed present in the new covenant and, because they flow from
Christ's grace, are far superior to those proceeding from our powers. Yet
the covenant of grace assigns them a very different office from the one
assigned in the covenant of works. Under the covenant of works, people were
to become pleasing and righteous before God through works, to be justified
before him, and by their covenant merit to be accepted as owed for eternal
life. Under the covenant of grace they serve a very different purpose: that
we may walk worthily of God's grace and finally reach eternal life by their
path as by a royal road. They can even, if the Romanists wish, belong to the
“justification” they envision, by which we become truly though imperfectly
inherently righteous and are regarded as such. This is the subject of James's
discussion. But if justification is taken in Paul's sense as forgiveness of
sins and God's gratuitous acceptance to grace and glory,

[1679 printed p. 152 | IA leaf 160]

Christ's merits, not ours, obtain it for us. It comes not from our works but
from his righteousness and obedience, freely imputed to us by God and
received by faith with highest thanksgiving. Our works, even the best, have
no power for this, though they have great power for other ends. Even though
they proceed from grace, if they justified us, the covenants of works and
grace would be confused. Both would use the same means of justification,
namely works; the only difference would be that the covenant of grace
offered a new assistance by which grace enabled us to perform good works.
We would then be justified in precisely the same way as under the covenant
of works.

This directly contradicts the emphasis upon grace in the gospel and Paul's
long, careful arguments in Galatians and especially Romans. The clearest
example is Romans 10. Paul says that the Jews, ignorant of God's
righteousness and seeking to establish their own, did not submit to God's
righteousness. Moses describes the righteousness of the law: the one who
performs it will live by it. The righteousness from faith, by contrast, does
not speak that way but says the word of faith is near, in the mouth and
heart: if you confess the Lord Jesus with your mouth and believe in your
heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. We must note the
following points from this passage.

1. Paul argues against Jews who refused submission to God's righteousness
and sought to establish their own. What was that righteousness? Was it
“their own” only because it consisted in works performed entirely by their
own powers and free choice under a law that merely proposed and commanded
them externally? Who but a Romanist would ever believe that? Did they rely
so entirely on their own powers that they wholly rejected the Spirit's
inward assistance, including grace that both awakened and strengthened them?
The Pharisee—the kind of person most confident in himself and his
works—thanks God that he performed good works and surpassed other people,
especially sinful tax collectors (Luke 18:11–12). Anyone who thanks God for
something thereby ascribes it wholly or partly to God.

Augustine accordingly says while expounding this passage in Sermon 36 on
the Lord's Words: “Let those who impiously prattle and presume upon their own
powers now see and hear this. Let those who say, ‘God made me a man; I make
myself righteous,’ hear it. O one worse and more detestable than the
Pharisee! The Pharisee proudly called himself righteous, yet he thanked God
for it.” Nevertheless, he was rebuked as proud and inflated, not because he
thanked God but because he desired nothing to be added to him. Gregory
likewise says in Homily 7 on Ezekiel, “He rightly thanked God, from whom he
received the good things he had done.” Stella, after identifying three kinds
of proud people—those who do not acknowledge receiving what they have from
God; those who acknowledge it came from God but from their own merit; and
those who acknowledge it came from God and his grace rather than their
merit, yet glory in it as though it were their own—places the Pharisee in
this third class.

Calvin most clearly writes on the passage:

[1679 printed p. 153 | IA leaf 161]

“He is not rebuked because he advertises the powers of free choice, but
because he trusts that the merits of his works have appeased God. His
thanksgiving does not boast in his own virtue, as though he had acquired
righteousness by himself or merited it by his industry; rather, he ascribes
his righteousness to God's grace. Although in thanking God he confesses that
whatever good works he has are purely God's benefit, nevertheless, because
he relies on confidence in works and prefers himself to others, he and his
prayer are rejected.” And again: “We should know, therefore, that although
someone ascribes praise for good works to God, if he imagines their
righteousness to be the cause of his salvation or rests upon it, he is
condemned for perverse arrogance.” How aptly this serves our case.

I concede that Maldonatus thinks otherwise. He claims the Pharisee only
pretended to thank God: “He is so pleased with himself throughout the prayer
that it is perfectly clear he attributed those virtues to himself, as though
he had attained them by his diligence. He did not thank God to give thanks,
but to praise himself.” Brugensis asks whether he thanked God for preserving
him by divine gift from crimes and vices which he could not avoid by the
powers of free choice alone. He answers that it is uncertain, indeed more
likely that the Pharisee acknowledged nothing beyond God's general
cooperation in avoiding such crimes. I am not greatly concerned with these
writers. Theophylact's authority might weigh more, since he brands the
Pharisee with self-confidence. Even if this were true of that Pharisee and
perhaps most Pharisees, it should not be imputed to absolutely every Jew
Paul addresses in Romans 10.

Someone may object that many Jews thankfully acknowledged God's and the
Spirit's help in their good works, but not Christ's grace or gospel faith.
Without these their works were not good, much less justifying. According to
Roman doctrine, only works flowing from Christ's grace and faith in him
justify.

I answer, as we have frequently taught and proved, that those whom Paul
refutes or better instructs in his debate over justification were not, or
certainly not chiefly, bare Jews who expected justification from legal works
done outside Christ and his grace. They were those Jews and other Judaizers
who, although they believed in Christ, wished to join works of the law to
their justification, exactly as the Romanists now do. Paul excludes these
works from justification when he says that those seeking to establish this
righteousness of their own did not submit to God's righteousness in Christ.

2. My second observation from Romans 10 is that Paul, as throughout his
discussion of justification, does not oppose works preceding faith to works
following faith, as though only the former were excluded. He universally
opposes all works to faith, by which alone he shows we are justified. The
passage establishes this in several ways.

1. In verse 2 Paul says the Jews had zeal for God. They thought the gospel
doctrine of justification by faith apart from works intended to abolish all
works and the law itself, and their zeal for the law therefore burned
fiercely against the doctrine of faith. Had they supposed that apostolic and
gospel teaching was what the Romanists teach today—that justification by
faith was so far from abolishing good works that precisely such works
justified—their burning zeal would immediately have cooled at least a
little.

[1679 printed p. 154 | IA leaf 162]

2. In verse 3 Paul opposes God's righteousness to the righteousness of their
own which they sought to establish. Establishing one's own righteousness is
not merely seeking righteousness before God from oneself and one's powers;
it is seeking to merit life by works, whether those works are performed by
one's own powers or by powers of grace. As Pareus rightly notes,
righteousness is called one's “own” insofar as it is opposed to another's.
It is opposed to another's not only with respect to the source from which it
comes—for in that sense the righteousness of works flowing from faith might
rightly be called another's, since it comes from God—but also with respect
to the form by which it exists and the subject in which it inheres. In this
sense only God's righteousness is another's, while the righteousness of
works following faith is properly ours in form and subject.

3. In verse 4 Paul says Christ is the end of the law for righteousness to
everyone who believes. As Piscator rightly observes, this proves Paul's
claim in the previous verse that the Jews had zeal for God and his law, but
not according to knowledge, because they were ignorant of the law's true
end. They thought the law's end was righteousness of works: that God gave
the law so his people might acquire righteousness from works it prescribed.
Paul says, on the contrary, that Christ is the law's true end. God gave the
law to the Jews so that, convicted of sin and subjected to its curse, they
might be led to Christ and his righteousness imputed by God and apprehended
by faith. Thus the Romanists are as ignorant as the Jews of the law's end
when they assign righteousness and justification to works rather than faith,
to the person performing the law rather than believing in Christ.

4. This is still clearer in verses 5, 6, and following. Paul distinguishes
and opposes the righteousness of faith and law. He ascribes everything to
faith in the former, for “with the heart one believes unto righteousness”
(verses 9–10). But righteousness of law consists in this: the person who
performs the law's commandments lives by them. Paul does not say it consists
only in performing them by the powers of free choice, but in performing them
by any means whatever, whether the works occur before or after faith. As
regards justification, even believers' works performed truly as believers
and from their whole heart are opposed to faith throughout Paul's argument,
though they agree excellently with faith insofar as they flow from it and
sanctification is subordinated to justification.

Peter Martyr aptly illustrates this by the logicians' distinction between a
differentia and a property. As a differentia constitutes a nature and
species, so God's righteousness constitutes our justification. As a
property follows a nature already constituted, so the righteousness of good
works follows justification.

5. Finally, Paul says that the righteousness of faith is a word near in the
mouth and heart and is consequently easier. It removes from our minds every
anxious doubt of conscience about attaining eternal life and deliverance
from hell, such as:

[1679 printed p. 155 | IA leaf 163]

“Who will ascend into heaven?” and “Who will descend into the abyss?”
Christ's ascent into heaven and descent to hell assure us of these things.
The righteousness of the law, by contrast, requires its complete
performance. Since that is impossible for us as fallen people, access to
heaven and deliverance from hell by it are also impossible. Hence necessarily
arise the continual torments, terrors, and doubts of an anxious soul and
conscience: “Who will ascend into heaven?” and “Who will descend into the
abyss?”

If, then, this is Paul's characterization of the righteousness of law as
opposed to the righteousness of faith, the righteousness of works produced
by faith undoubtedly belongs to the legal righteousness which Paul excludes
from justification, not to the righteousness of faith. However numerous and
excellent those works of faith may be, the works themselves fail in many
things, and even the most righteous people who perform them sin in many more.
They must therefore perpetually doubt and tremble with anxiety.

This yields a very powerful argument against Roman doctrine. A righteousness
which of itself produces doubts and makes the heart anxious about salvation
is not the righteousness of faith which Paul establishes here. Paul says
this explicitly: “The righteousness which is from faith speaks this way: Do
not say in your heart, Who will ascend into heaven? or Who will descend into
the abyss?” But righteousness from works, even works produced by faith, does
produce these doubts if we rest upon them for justification and meriting
life. Indeed, since the best people know they fall far short of the law, if
they are dealt with under this condition it produces despair of salvation.
By Paul's criterion it therefore belongs not to the righteousness of faith
he seeks to establish, but entirely to the righteousness of law which he
excludes from our justification.

Thus, to speak with Peter Martyr, these works of believers are properties of
those already justified, while faith is the formal differentia of the
justified as justified. The law, insofar as it makes us guilty of sin and
the curse, should lead and drive us as a tutor to Christ to obtain pardon by
grace. Those who seek righteousness from the law and works must therefore
be accused of making a father from a tutor. Righteousness must be expected
from Christ alone and from his righteousness imputed to us by God and
apprehended by faith.

Thus at last we complete the second preliminary question: What works does
Paul exclude from justification in Romans and Galatians? We have shown at
length that they are not merely ceremonial works of the law, or even moral
works preceding faith and produced by powers of nature or free choice, as
the Romanists contend, but universally all believers' works following faith
and proceeding from the regenerate. In their proper order and place these
works are good and pleasing to God. According to Paul's command in Titus
3:14, believers must devote themselves to them in gratitude to God and
Christ their Redeemer, and finally travel by them as by a royal road straight
to heaven. Yet they have no power to justify a sinner before God, since God
did not appoint them to that end and they are imperfect and sinful in
themselves and insofar as we perform them. They themselves need pardon and
therefore justification.
