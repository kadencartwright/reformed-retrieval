### QUESTION IV

#### What Is to Be Thought about These 2,300 Days of Daniel 8:14?

There is much greater ease in this question than in the preceding one.
Therefore we may be much briefer here. I find three principal opinions on this
matter. The first, common to Roman Catholics and Protestants, is that Daniel
speaks of Antiochus Epiphanes and that the days the angel names are natural
days. The second is that of the Jews, who hold that the days are prophetic,
namely 2,300 years, at the end of which they expect the temple’s cleansing
through the Messiah’s advent. The third is that of the excessively Judaizing
modern chiliasts. They likewise hold that the days are prophetic, namely 2,300
years, but cannot satisfactorily work out the limit from which and the limit
to which they run.

We rest in the first, although different people explain it in different ways.
Calvin, modestly and judiciously as usual, resolves these days into six years,
three months, and nearly four. He places their end in the 148th year of the
kingdom of the Greeks, on the twenty-fifth day of the ninth month, Casleu, as
is expressly stated in 1 Maccabees 4:52, and their beginning generally in the
Jews’ first persecution by Antiochus. There was no need to descend into greater
particularity. For if we count backward from the 108th year and the
twenty-fifth day of its ninth month, an arithmetical calculation of a few years
cannot go wrong—especially if we suppose Calvin’s foundation to be correct:
that every difference between the ancient years of the Jews and our modern
Julian years, or indeed any other lunar and solar years, was formerly
reconciled by intercalation at irregular intervals and can easily be made
equal today.

> Marginalia, printed p. 253: *The common opinion is true*.

As for Calvin’s holding, in the passage before us, that the temple was polluted
and cleansed 150 years after Alexander’s death and yet in the 148th year of the
kingdom of the Greeks, the apparent contradiction is easy to resolve. First,
Calvin’s lectures on Daniel were not published by him, but, like various other
works of his, were taken down from his oral dictation and circulated without
his own revision. He expressly professes in this very place that he is speaking
only of the sum of the times παχυλῶς and ἐξωτερικῶς—roughly and
exoterically—not precisely and acroamatically. Furthermore, the epoch of the
kingdom of the Greeks mentioned in Maccabees began neither with Alexander’s
reign nor with his death, as we said in the history, but some time afterward
with Seleucus’s Babylonian kingdom.

> Marginalia, printed p. 253: *Calvin defended*.

Junius, as in most matters, wishes to descend to the smallest details. Thus he
places the end of this period in the 148th year of the Seleucids, on the
twenty-fifth day of the ninth month, and its beginning in their 141st year,
when the high priest Menelaus defected to paganism. He proves this calculation
from 1 Maccabees 1:12. He reduces the 2,300 days to six years, three months,
and eighteen days, supposing two intercalations in each six-year span, or two
leap-year Februaries. For the six hours by which the sun’s course exceeds 365
days in each year make one full day of twenty-four hours in every fourth year.

Rollock follows Junius, as usual, word for word, as does Diodati. Polanus also
does so, but adds that the precise initial limit of the present epoch falls on
the sixth day of the sixth month of the 142nd year.

In this opinion of Junius we disapprove the reduction of the days to six
years, three months, and eighteen days, for when the aforesaid intercalation is
supposed, this calculation will fit the rule of arithmetic exactly. But what
we find wanting is, first, that Junius places the beginning of this number in
the 141st year, one whole year earlier than he should. Polanus rightly corrects
this, though without a refutation, even if other unthinking men follow this
error ἀδοκιμάστως, without examination. Second, as the foundation of this
calculation all the followers of Junius, including Polanus himself, cite 1
Maccabees 1:12. Yet nothing appears there that lends any color to this
reckoning. There we read that Antiochus Epiphanes took the kingdom in the
137th year of the Seleucids, but there is not a word about when he began to
harass the Jews or at what point in time Menelaus the apostate betook himself
to Antiochus.

> Marginalia, printed p. 253: *Junius rejected*.

The first English Annotators cast our days into six years, three months, and a
half. This is wrong and contrary to their own author Calvin, who expressly has
more than a half. The second repeat the first men’s error and neglect to examine
it. The third also repeat the same error without rejecting it, but add Polanus’s
correct calculation without naming its author. They worsen it in this respect:
they sometimes render the eighteen days as nineteen days and sometimes as
three weeks, which make twenty-one days. But—a blunder of far greater
weight—they append here the Jesuits’ very own calculation, which

[Printed p. 254 | IA leaf 284 | access PDF 284 | OCR object 283]

removes both limits of this epoch from their proper place, setting the first
limit at Antiochus’s first advent to Jerusalem in the year 143 and the last at
his death in the year 149. In this they wrongly desert not only the
Protestants, but also the soundest of the Roman Catholics themselves, as we
shall see immediately.

The Roman Catholics labor hard enough here to reconcile the numbers and
histories of the first book of Maccabees with the parallel passages in the
second book and in Josephus, and often to shore up the authority of their
Apocrypha. Both Torniellus and Salianus are less fair to Josephus, but let them
see for themselves to those disputes about the Apocrypha. We note only the
rashness of Pererius. Setting the beginning of this epoch in the 143rd year, he
asks how six years, three months, and twenty days can be counted from that
point to the cleansing of the temple on the twenty-fifth of the month Casleu
in the 148th year, and professes that he does not know what to answer here. He
only hesitantly proposes that the epoch’s end might extend beyond the cleansing
of the temple as far as Antiochus’s death in the 149th year. What Pererius
proposes hesitantly, Torniellus and Cornelius a Lapide assert decisively. The
Englishmen mentioned above even swallow this gross error, contrary to Daniel’s
express text, which explicitly ends the 2,300 days at the cleansing of the
temple by Judas Maccabaeus in the 148th year, many months before Antiochus’s
death. There was no difficulty here at all for which the epoch’s final limit
should be moved from its place. Rather, through the thoughtlessness of
Pererius and his followers, the first limit is wrongly set in the 143rd year,
since long before then the apostate Jews had foully polluted the temple and
God’s worship. Salianus and other Jesuits therefore rightly retain our Polanus’s
calculation, counting backward from the temple’s cleansing six years, three
months, and twenty days, and thus, from the fixed end, establishing the
beginning on the sixth or eighth day of the sixth month of the 142nd year. All
the Roman Catholics differ from our men here by two days, supposing twenty in
place of our men’s eighteen. They do this not so much by following Montanus’s
interlinear text, which, as Salianus notes, reads six years, three months, and
twenty days, as by forgetting the double intercalation Junius rightly observed.

> Marginalia, printed p. 254: *Pererius’s rashness*.

Jerome notes that some of the Latin Fathers read 2,200 days. The Seventy, whom
Ludovicus Capellus very wrongly prefers to the Hebrew text in many places,
certainly read 2,400 days. But the Jesuits themselves embrace the Hebrew
reading of three hundred days as genuine.

As for the Jews’ second opinion, following Calvin’s example we pass over it
here, having spent enough time refuting their similar delusion in the preceding
question. We note only the learned L’Empereur’s gross παρόραμα, oversight. In
his notes on this error of Jacchiades at Daniel 8:14, he insists more than once
that our 1,300 days begin when Antiochus abolished the continual sacrifice on
the twenty-fifth of the month Casleu in the 145th year and end with Judas’s
victory over Nicanor. Certainly no more than half the days before us elapsed
during that time; the years were no more than three. No mortal besides
L’Empereur has affirmed that six years ran between those limits.

> Marginalia, printed p. 254: *L’Empereur’s oversight*.

The third opinion is that of Thomas Parker, the New Englander and son of Robert
the Bishop-Scourge, whose commentaries on Daniel Joseph Caryl, for a long time
the principal London licenser of books by the Independents, commended to the
press. Parker holds that the little horn in Daniel 8 is not Antiochus but the
Roman Antichrist, and that the 2,300 days are not natural but prophetic days.
Although he thinks they may begin from various limits, he nevertheless ends
them in the year of Christ 1650.

> Marginalia, printed p. 254: *Parker’s arguments refuted*.

He brings seven arguments against our common opinion concerning Antiochus.

First. The little horn of chapter 8 is the same as the little horn of chapter
7. But the little horn of chapter 7 is Antichrist, not Antiochus.

Answer. The major premise is a mere begging of the question, which we ought
not to grant without any argument, since we proved above by many arguments and
authorities that the little horn of chapter 7 stood on the head of the fourth
beast, namely the Roman beast, not the third, the Greek beast. And it is no
less manifest that the little horn mentioned in chapter 8 belongs to the third
beast, not the fourth.

Second. The little horn, he says, arises at the end of the kingdom of the
Greeks, when the transgression of sinners had reached its height. But Antiochus
Epiphanes lived in the middle of the kingdom of the Greeks, long before the
sins of either the Jews or the Greeks had reached their summit.

Answer. The words of the text do not denote the absolute end but, as they are
rightly translated, the latter times. Various Greek kings did indeed follow
Antiochus Epiphanes, but from his days onward the power of the Seleucids was so
broken and diminished that his successors were men of very little name and
never afterward possessed any power in Judea. As for the sins, they undoubtedly
reached their highest summit in Epiphanes’s persecution, nor in the Greek
kingdom did the transgressions of the Jewish

[Printed p. 255 | IA leaf 285 | access PDF 285 | OCR object 284]

apostates ever proceed to such a degree of abomination.

Third. The horn discussed in chapter 8 was small at its beginning. But
Antiochus Epiphanes was a great man from his very beginning, the son of
Antiochus the Great.

Answer. Epiphanes was indeed the son of the Great, but he was the younger son.
While his elder brother Seleucus lived, he possessed no power at all, nor any
country or city over which he ruled. Indeed, he lived at Rome in private
condition as a hostage after the manner of a captive, so that few princes had
smaller beginnings.

Fourth. The little horn in chapter 8, verse 9, grew above the former horns. But
Antiochus Epiphanes never equaled the power even of his father Antiochus the
Great.

Answer. Verse 9 speaks of the little horn in itself and in relation to the
South, the East, and Judea. No comparison is made between it and the horns of
verse 8, as though it would be greater either than Alexander or than any one
of his four successors. He did indeed become very great in relation to his
small beginnings. Indeed, none of Alexander’s successors in his age equaled
him either in power or splendor. He certainly surpassed the Ptolemies in Egypt
and Perseus in Macedonia in every glory, and far surpassed his own father, the
Great, in his successes in Egypt and Judea.

Fifth. The little horn had great glory in the South, the East, and Judea. But
Epiphanes suffered great disgrace in all those places. For the Roman envoy
Popilius Laenas drove him out of Egypt with a vine stick; Judas Maccabaeus
broke all his strength in Judea; and the Elymaeans in Persia almost overwhelmed
him with stones.

Answer. Distinguish the times. Epiphanes finally lost everywhere the very
great glory that he had long enjoyed. His first and last periods were wretched,
not the middle one, as Daniel foretold clearly enough in chapter 8, verse 25.

Sixth. The little horn endured, according to verses 17 and 19, until the end of
the world and God’s final wrath; Antiochus Epiphanes did not.

Answer. Daniel does not speak of the end of the age, but of the end of the
kingdom of the Greeks and of this horn’s persecution. Verse 9 expressly says
that it arose from one of Alexander’s four successors, as Ephraim Huit rightly
demonstrates on this passage. Nor does Jacchiades himself, who preceded Parker
in this opinion of his, disagree with us on this point, although the agreement
or disagreement of those rabbis, exceedingly foolish in most matters, appears
to deserve very little weight. Jacchiades at chapter 8, verse 8, certainly
holds that the four successors of Alexander the Great are Romulus at Rome,
Alexander at Alexandria, Herod at Acona, and Antiochus at Antioch. The madness
and stupidity of this delusion cannot be denounced as fully as it deserves.

Parker’s final argument. Pererius, he says, makes the 2,300 days in the passage
correspond to Antiochus’s persecutions. But he begins them in the 143rd year of
the Greeks and ends them in the 148th; in this way the three months and twelve
days expressed by the Prophet are excluded. Therefore calculation in natural
days cannot be admitted.

Answer. What is the authority of one Jesuit to us, especially one who follows
a singular path apart from all his associates? Next, Pererius wrongly placed
the beginning of those days in the 143rd year, as we demonstrated above,
although he has Hugo Grotius as a follower in this error. Third, even though
Pererius wrongly moved the former limits, prolonged the end of the days
beyond the temple’s cleansing, and located their beginning in the 143rd year
rather than, as he should have done with Salianus and others, in the preceding
year, nevertheless from his own beginning—namely, the beginning of the 143rd
year—to Antiochus’s death, he counts 2,300 natural days consistently enough.
Parker attempts to attack this without any reason.

These are Parker’s arguments against us. He brings several on his own behalf
to prove that the little horn of chapter 8 denotes the Roman Antichrist. It is
not expedient to linger over them, for they are rather slight. As far as our
question about the times is concerned, let us see how he explains the days.
He holds that they are prophetic, but in a new and, so far as I judge, wholly
singular way. First, he affirms that a day here signifies not a year but half a
year, because Daniel names days in which there were morning and evening. Since
morning and evening are the beginning and end of a day distinct from night,
the 2,300 days—or the evenings and mornings of 2,300 days distinguished from
their nights—make only 1,150 natural days of twenty-four hours. These years,
beginning from the year of Christ 360, end in 1510, when Luther opposed the
pope.

I would indeed pronounce this calculation ridiculous, had a learned man not
sent it from New England and had Caryl not midwifed it to the press. In any
case, I cannot but note its great absurdity. For in sacred Scripture the
ordinary reckoning of natural days of twenty-four hours is by morning and
evening. Were all the days so numbered in Genesis 1 only halves of natural
days? And indeed, was the world created in six days that must be reduced to

three natural days? Furthermore, if Daniel counts his days as distinct from
nights, how does Parker thereby exclude 1,150 days for us as well, each of
which consists of twenty-four hours and not one of which lacked its own night?

Third. He places the beginning of these days in the year of Christ 360 and so,
without any necessity, makes Daniel leap over a history of many centuries. He
also makes a great epoch begin in the year of Christ 360, which is nevertheless
marked by no one’s deed, and end in 1510, as he himself admits, a full seven
years before Luther uttered any word against the pontiff. Parker wishes to
shift the first part of his epoch, after seven years, from the year 360 to the
pontificate of Damasus, so that he may end it in 1517, when Luther first began
to oppose Roman corruptions. We would call this desultory fickleness, did he
not immediately leap over all these things with a much greater bound. Not
content with cutting one entire half from Daniel’s days, and thus from his
prophetic years, by a new and unheard-of interpretation, he cuts yet another
half from the remainder. From a misunderstanding of Exodus 12:6, he gathers
that every artificial day distinct from night has not merely one but two
evenings, and thus a double morning. Daniel’s 2,300 days must therefore be
reduced not only, as before, to 1,150, but to half of those, 575. Thus each one
of Daniel’s days is contracted not only to half a day, twelve hours, but even
to a quarter of a day, six hours, and so each of his years to a quarter of a
year. This ridiculous and monstrous contraction was invented so that the epoch
of the temple’s great cleansing from antichristianism may end in the year 1650.
To make its beginning agree with this outcome, it is set in the first year of
Pope Hildebrand, the year of Christ 1075. But there has been more than enough
about these insipid trifles.
